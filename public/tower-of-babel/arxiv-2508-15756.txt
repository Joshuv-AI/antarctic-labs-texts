Robust Data Interpretation for Perturbed Nulling Interferometers via Proper Handling of Correlated Errors
Philipp A. Huber, Felix A. Dannert, Romain Laugier, Taro Matsuo, Loes W. Rutten, Adrian M. Glauser, Sascha P. Quanz

Abstract
The detection and atmospheric characterization of potentially habitable, temperate terrestrial exoplanets using a space-based mid-infrared nulling interferometer is a major goal of contemporary astrophysics. A central part of the analysis of such an instrument are correlated errors arising from perturbations in the system. While previous studies have often treated their effects in a limited manner, we aim to treat them comprehensively here and argue that data whitening based on the covariance of these errors is a suitable method to mitigate their impact. We present a framework that quantitatively connects instrumental perturbations to performance metrics and develop two computational tools to support our analysis: PHRINGE , for the generation of synthetic nulling data, and LIFEsimMC , a new Monte Carlo-based end-to-end simulator for the Large Interferometer For Exoplanets (LIFE). Applying our framework to a reference observation of an Earth twin orbiting a Sun twin at 10 pc 10\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c} , we find that whitening is not only essential for a correct interpretation of the detection metric used in hypothesis testing, but also improves the estimates of the planetary properties. Moreover, our approach enables an estimation of the spectral covariance of the extracted planetary spectra, providing valuable additional input for future atmospheric retrievals. We therefore recommend incorporating the framework into performance assessments and requirement derivations for future nulling interferometers.



I
Introduction

A major goal of contemporary astrophysics is the detection and atmospheric characterization of temperate terrestrial exoplanets orbiting in habitable zones of nearby stars through their thermal emission
(
Cockell et al. 2009
;
Quanz et al. 2022a
;
Kammerer et al. 2022
)
. Ultimately, this would pave the way towards an understanding of the habitability and diversity of these worlds, enabling the search for atmospheric signatures indicative of biological activity. Space-based nulling interferometers with their unique combination of high angular resolution, high achievable contrasts, and high mid-infrared (MIR) sensitivity are considered a promising option to tackle this challenging task
(
Bracewell 1978
;
Bracewell & MacPhie 1979
;
Angel & Woolf 1997
;
Mennesson & Mariotti 1997
;
Mennesson et al. 2005
)
. Building on the heritage of NASA’s TPF-I
(
Lawson et al. 2007
)
and ESA’s Darwin
(
Cockell et al. 2009
)
mission concepts, the Large Interferometer For Exoplanets (LIFE) initiative is reevaluating the potential of a MIR nulling interferometer mission leveraging the latest scientific and technological advances. A particular effort of the LIFE community is aimed at deriving detailed technical specifications of the instrument
(
Glauser et al. 2024
, for a recent set of specifications see)
.
End-to-end simulations play a pivotal role in this process
(
Dannert et al. 2022
)
. They link planetary models to instrument models, generate synthetic data of simulated observations, and estimate planet properties from those data through a process called signal extraction. These estimated properties include the planetary radii, effective temperatures, and spectra. The spectra are typically used as input for atmospheric retrieval tools, allowing an estimate of atmospheric structures and compositions
(
Alei et al. 2022
;
Konrad et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Using this forward modeling approach, we can assess whether a given set of technical specifications meets the scientific objectives of the mission. A key aspect of these simulations is the noise sources that are considered. In this work, we focus on so-called
instability noise
, which originates from the perturbed nature of the nulling instrument
(
Lay 2004
;
Lay 2006
)
. In the following, we will also refer to it as
instrumental errors
. Most previous LIFE performance studies assumed that the instrument is built such that it is photon-noise-limited and, hence, did not model instrumental noise explicitly
(
Quanz et al. 2022b
)
. Instead, they implicitly accounted for it by assuming an ideal instrument but requiring a signal-to-noise ratio of
S/N\geqslant 7
instead of the typically used
S/N\geqslant 5
to claim a exoplanet detection. As we will see, in doing so, they may have imposed overly strict instrumental requirements.

Explicitly modeling instrumental errors requires not only more comprehensive instrument models, but, due to the correlated nature of such errors, also suitable data processing techniques
(
Huber et al. 2024
)
.
Serabyn 2000
and
Lay 2004
were the first to tackle this challenge with their efforts to model instrumental errors and connect them to stability requirements for the instrument.
Lay 2004
developed a complex mathematical framework that allowed an analytical calculation of the variance of instrumental errors resulting from perturbations in beam amplitude, phase, and polarization angle. Part of the complexity of the framework is owed to the explicit accounting for temporal correlations in the errors. Emphasis is put on the issue that these correlations can indeed mimic a planetary signal in the data, leading to wrongly claimed detections. In this context,
Dannert et al. 2025
reassess the typical assumption of Gaussian distributed instrumental errors used in hypothesis tests to inform such detection claims. They show that this assumption is invalid and derive an analytical expression for a hypothesis test that accounts for the true distribution of the errors.

While this work is essential for a correct interpretation of the measurements, it only addresses part of the issue of correlated errors: both,
Lay 2004
and
Dannert et al. 2025
, omit a treatment of spectral correlations. First efforts to explicitly model and mitigate the impact of said spectral correlations was done by
Lay 2006
. The author presented an ad hoc approach of first fitting and then subtracting the errors from the data. The fidelity of this approach was increased by
Matsuo et al. 2023
in the context their proposed signal extraction algorithm, called phase-space synthesis decomposition, entailing an extrapolation (from short towards longer wavelengths) and successive subtraction of the errors. Continuing these developments, a more rigorous approach was followed by
Laugier et al. 2023
for the ground-based nulling instrument Asgard/NOTT. They applied a
whitening transformation
proposed by
Ceau et al. 2019
in a slightly different context to mitigate the impact of the correlations on the detection limits of their instrument. This whitening transformation is also the method that is used in this work.

Modeling and correctly handling correlated instrumental errors is essential for a robust interpretation of the generated data, which in turn is an essential prerequisite for a robust derivation of technical specifications. In this study, we thus aim to establish a means to treat spectral correlations by arguing that
(1) data whitening is a suitable method to mitigate their effects and (2) applying this method shows prospects for relaxing technical requirements and providing additional information that can potentially aid subsequent atmospheric retrievals from the estimated spectra
. We first develop a new state-of-the-art synthetic data generation tool, called
PHRINGE
, tailored to the use case of perturbed space-based nulling interferometers. An overview of the instrument model implemented within
PHRINGE
and a description of the correlations in the instrumental errors it produces is given in Section
II
. An explanation of the data whitening transformation, hypothesis testing and signal extraction procedure is outlined in Section
III
. There, we also describe our second tool,
LIFEsimMC
, a new end-to-end simulator for LIFE that incorporates these data processing techniques. In Section
IV
, we apply our framework to the reference case of an Earth twin orbiting a Sun twin at
10\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c}
. We then discuss our findings and limitations of the framework in Section
V
and finally conclude our work in Section
VI
.

(2)

\mathrm{m}

the polarization rotation angle in
\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}

(
Lay 2004
)
. The amplitude

A_{k}=r_{k}\sqrt{\pi\eta_{\text{tot}}}

are the collector radii in
\mathrm{m}

\eta_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}_{k}(t,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=A_{k}\left(1+\delta A_{k}(t)\right)\exp\left(i\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}(t)+\delta\phi_{k}(t,\lambda)\right)\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\cdot\Bigg[\begin{matrix}\cos\left(\theta_{k}+\delta\theta_{k}(t)\right)\\
\sin\left(\theta_{k}+\delta\theta_{k}(t)\right)\end{matrix}\Bigg].

\delta A_{k}

where

II.2
Noise Sources

II.2.1
Fundamental Noise

The fundamental noise sources considered here are of purely astrophysical nature. Specifically, we consider contributions from the host star of an observed system, its exozodi, and the local zodi, as shown in Figure
1
. We model them as additional photon sources within our astrophysical scene and hence describe them through their sky brightness distributions. The total sky brightness
I
, that enters Equation (
1
) can thus be written as

I(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=I_{\text{p}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})+I_{\star}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})+I_{\text{ez}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})+I_{\text{lz}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

(6)

where
I_{\text{p}}
is the sky brightness distribution of the observed planet,
I_{\star}
the one of its host star,
I_{\text{ez}}
the one of the exozodi and
I_{\text{lz}}
the one of the local zodi. Note that we expect contributions from these astrophysical noise sources even in an ideal instrument. Indeed, due to the high resolution of the instrument, the star starts to be resolved, which prevents it from being perfectly
nullled
in the central destructive fringe of the instrument response (see Figure
2
). Since the photon emission from the star is a statistical process that follows a Poisson distribution, photon counts in the outputs of the interferometer may differ, which results in a non-zero contribution in the differential output, often referred to as stellar
leakage
. Similarly, the exozodi leaks into the instrument. The local zodi is a diffuse source that emits throughout the field of view of the instrument, also leaking onto the detector.

The situation is different for the planet: Its angular extent is still too small to be resolved and we can model it as a point source with sky brightness distribution

I_{\text{p}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=F_{\text{p}}(\lambda)\delta\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}\right),

(7)

where
F_{\text{p}}
is the SED of the planet,
\delta
is the Dirac delta function and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}
is the position of the planet in the sky. We use the same models as in
Dannert et al. 2022
for the astrophysical noise terms in Equation (
6
). For completeness, they are given in Appendix
A
.

II.2.2
Instrumental Noise

As described in Equation (
4
), we introduce instrumental errors by adding amplitude, phase, and polarization angle perturbations to the four input beams of the interferometer. This simple approach is justified by the following two assumptions: First, we assume an SMF that acts as a perfect spatial filter in the instrument. This allows us to reduce spatially resolved wavefront errors to an amplitude and phase error. Secondly, we assume that our optical system downstream of the beam combiner entrance is linear. This then allows us to express perturbations downstream of that entrance as perturbations originating upstream of it, i.e., as perturbations in amplitude, phase, and polarization angle of the beams at the instrument inputs. Essentially, this treats everything downstream of the beam combiner entrance as perturbation-free.
Dannert et al. 2025
point out that this assumption is critical, as, in a double Bracewell combiner, perturbations from those downstream optics would affect the input beams in the same manner and thus result in correlated perturbations within them. However, it can be shown that in the double Bracewell combiner, correlations originating downstream of the combiner entrance have no significant effect
(
Dannert et al. 2025
;
Lay 2004
)
.

To create synthetic data for an observation, we generate a random time series for each perturbation term in Equation (
4
). These time series are specified by their root mean square (RMS) and their underlying power spectrum,
S
. In this work, we generate a power spectrum corresponding to pink noise, using

S(\omega)\propto\frac{1}{\omega},

(8)

where
\omega=2\pi f
is the angular frequency (not to be confused with the rotation frequency of the array). The power spectrum is calculated between a low- and a high-frequency cutoff,
f_{\text{low}}
and
f_{\text{high}}
, respectively. In our simulations, we use
f_{\text{low}}=1/P_{\text{rot}}
, where
P_{\text{rot}}
is the rotation period of the array, and
f_{\text{high}}=$10\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
. The definition of the amplitude perturbation,
\delta A_{k}
, in Equation (
4
) allows for values in the range
[-1,1]
. An example of a time series for an amplitude perturbation with an RMS of
0.1\text{\,}\%
based on a pink power spectrum is given in Figure
3
.

Note that we model the amplitude perturbations as wavelength-independent here. We define the phase perturbations,
\delta\phi_{k}
, as

\delta\phi_{k}(t,\lambda)=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\delta p(t),

(9)

where
\delta p
the piston in units of
\mathrm{m}
. Finally, the polarization angle perturbations,
\delta\theta_{k}
, account for mismatches in the linear polarization of the beams and are specified in radians.

As we have anticipated in Section
II.1
, the science signal of the measurement is obtained by subtracting pairs of signals from two dark outputs,

\Delta N_{m}(t,\lambda)\equiv N_{k_{1}}(t,\lambda)-N_{k_{2}}(t,\lambda),

(10)

where
m\in[1,n_{\text{diff}}]
is the index of the differential output and
k_{1}

k_{2}

Hanot et al. 2011

\Delta N_{1}(t,\lambda)=N_{3}(t,\lambda)-N_{4}(t,\lambda).

(11)

Less striking, but also visible in the bottom panel of Figure

are temporal correlations. Contrary to the ideal signal (center panel), where the counts at different time steps are independent of each other (shot noise), the vertical stripes in the bottom panel show a dependence on the values of the neighboring stripes. As mentioned earlier, an explicit treatment of temporal correlations is outside of the scope of this study.

III
Data processing and signal extraction

III.1
Data Whitening

The data recorded in the differential output of the double Bracewell nuller have been illustrated in the three panels of Figure
4
as matrices of shape
n_{\lambda}\times n_{t}
. Here,
n_{\lambda}
is the number of wavelength bins and
n_{t}
the number of time steps. In general, this data is a three dimensional
n_{\text{diff}}\times n_{\lambda}\times n_{t}
data cube,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=(D_{m\tau l})

(14)

\displaystyle\equiv\Delta N_{m}(t_{\tau},\lambda_{l}),

which we obtain by evaluating Equation (
10
) for all
m\in(1,n_{\text{diff}})
differential outputs, discrete time steps
t_{\tau}
with
\tau\in(1,n_{t})
and discrete wavelengths
\lambda_{l}
with
l\in(1,n_{\lambda})
. In this section, we treat this data cube as a one-dimensional vector,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}
, containing
n_{\text{diff}}n_{\lambda}
random variables, of which we acquire
n_{t}
samples during an observation. We can express
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}
also as the sum of the planet signal or model,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}
, and the noise,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}.

(15)

Here, we make the assumption that, when the model,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}
, matches the data,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}})
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
is the covariance of the noise
(
Laugier et al. 2023
)
, in the following referred to as the
instrumental error covariance
. More precisely,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
is an
n_{\text{diff}}n_{\lambda}\times n_{\text{diff}}n_{\lambda}
matrix containing the covariance of the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}
. As we have seen in Figures
4
and
6
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}
generally contains strong noise contributions, which are highly correlated and not independent. This not only complicates signal extraction (see Section
III.3
), but also violates the assumptions used in the hypothesis tests (see Section
III.2
). We resolve this by performing data whitening as suggested by
Ceau et al. 2019
by multiplying the whitening matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}
by the unwhitened vectors,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime},

(16)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}.

(17)

Note that there are different ways to define the whitening matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}
. Common choices include zero-phase component analysis (ZCA) whitening with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and principal component analysis (PCA) whitening with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Lambda}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}^{T}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{U}}^{T}
is the eigendecomposition of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}

(
Kessy et al. 2018
)
. PCA is predominantly useful when a reduction in dimensionality is required. If no dimensionality reduction is performed, PCA and ZCA yield equivalent results in terms of signal extraction and hypothesis testing. Therefore, and to comply with
Ceau et al. 2019
, we choose to continue with ZCA whitening in this work. We thus obtain the whitened data and model by inserting
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
into Equations (
16
) and (
17
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime},

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}.

(19)

This leads to a new model of our data,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(20)

where now
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}})
. Figure
7
illustrates the data and the model after the whitening transformation.

Figure 7:
Illustration of the effect of the whitening transformation on the planetary model (top) and full data (bottom). The correlations visible in the bottom panel of Figure
4
have been removed and the noise appears completely white, whereas the planetary signal is only slightly changed, as expected. Note that the panels now show unitless values and do no longer represent physical counts.

The impact on the planetary signature is minimal, while the total signal has changed significantly. Notably, the spectral correlations have been removed and the noise appears to be white. Indeed, the planetary signature has become slightly visible in the whitened data.

In practice, obtaining
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Cov}}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right)
can be challenging, as a measurement of a planet only provides
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
, that is, the sum of planet signal and noise, and not noise alone. For simplicity, we work here under the hypothesis that acquisitions on a calibration star,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{\text{ref}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{ref}}^{\prime}
, provide us with a relevant estimate of the covariance of errors on the science target,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{ref}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Cov}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{\text{ref}}^{\prime}\right).

(21)

We further discuss this approach and alternatives in Section
V.2
.

III.2
Statistical Hypothesis Testing

Statistical hypothesis testing is used to make informed decisions about whether a signal of interest is present in our data. In particular, given the whitened data and model from above, the goal is to differentiate between the two statistical hypotheses,

\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}},\\
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}},\end{cases}

(22)

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the null hypothesis, under which no planetary signal is present, and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the alternative hypothesis, where a signal is present. We follow the approach of
Laugier et al. 2023
and use the statistical tests that
Ceau et al. 2019
describe for detections in kernel phases for detection tests on our whitened data,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
. We briefly describe the Neyman-Pearson and energy detector tests in Sections
III.2.1
and
III.2.2
, and refer the reader to
Laugier et al. 2023
for a more detailed description thereof.

III.2.1
Neyman-Pearson Test

The likelihood ratio or Neyman-Pearson test
(
Neyman et al. 1933
)
is a suitable test for the problem defined in Equation (
22
) and requires knowledge of the model,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

(
Ceau et al. 2019
)
. It can be regarded as an upper bound to our detection problem. It is based on the likelihood function for Gaussian white noise, which reads
(
Scharf & Friedlander 1994
)

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})=(2\pi)^{-n_{\text{diff}}n_{t}n_{\lambda}/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})^{T}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\right).

(23)

Calculating the ratio of the likelihoods under the alternative and the null hypothesis,
\mathcal{L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}})/\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})
, we can express the test statistic of the Neyman-Pearson test as

T_{\text{NP}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\;\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{H}_{1}\\
\displaystyle\gtrless\\
\mathcal{H}_{0}\end{subarray}\;\xi_{\text{NP}}.

(24)

This test statistic is distributed as
(
Ceau et al. 2019
)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}_{0}:T_{\text{NP}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\right),\\
\mathcal{H}_{1}:T_{\text{NP}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\right).\end{cases}

(25)

The threshold,
\xi_{\text{NP}}
, upon which either the null or alternative hypothesis is accepted, can be determined by

\xi_{\text{NP}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}^{-1}\left(1-P_{\text{FA}}\right),

(26)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}^{-1}
is the inverse cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the normal distribution with
\mu=0
and
\sigma^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
and
P_{\text{FA}}
is the probability of false alarm.
In general, the true model,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
, is not known a priori and we rely on an estimator of the model,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}
. We describe the use of numerical maximum likelihood estimation to obtain this estimator in Section
III.3
.

III.2.2
Energy Detector Test

An alternative test is given by the energy detector test. It assumes no prior knowledge whatsoever, setting
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}

(
Ceau et al. 2019
)
. This represents the
“worst-case”
scenario and can thus be viewed as a lower bound for the detection performance. Its test statistic is defined as

T_{\text{ED}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\equiv\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\;\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{H}_{1}\\
\displaystyle\gtrless\\
\mathcal{H}_{0}\end{subarray}\;\xi_{\text{ED}},

(27)

and distributed as
(
Ceau et al. 2019
)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{H}_{0}:T_{\text{ED}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\sim\chi^{2}_{p}(\delta^{2}=0),\\
\mathcal{H}_{1}:T_{\text{ED}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\sim\chi^{2}_{p}(\delta^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),\end{cases}

(28)

where
\chi^{2}_{p}
is a chi-square distribution with
p=n_{\text{diff}}n_{\lambda}
degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter
\delta
.
The threshold,
\xi_{\text{ED}}
, is given by

\xi_{\text{ED}}=\mathcal{F}_{\chi^{2}_{p}(0)}^{-1}\left(1-P_{\text{FA}}\right),

(29)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\chi^{2}_{p}(0)}^{-1}
is the inverse CDF of
\chi^{2}_{p}(\delta^{2}=0)
.

III.3
Signal Extraction

The goal of signal extraction is to obtain an estimator of the planetary position,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{p}}
and SED,
\hat{F}_{\text{p}}
, which also allows for an estimation of its temperature and radius, e.g., by fitting a black body spectrum to
\hat{F}_{\text{p}}
. This is equivalent to finding an estimator,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}
, which can then also be used for the Neyman-Pearson test.
We can rewrite out Equation (
23
) explicitly in terms of the planetary properties
F_{\text{p}}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}
by using Equations (
13
) and (
14
), to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\right)\propto

(30)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{\text{diff}}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{n_{t}}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\lambda}}\left(F_{\text{p}}(\lambda_{l})T_{\text{p},m}\left(t_{\tau},\lambda_{l},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}\right)-D_{m\tau\ell}\right)^{2}\right).

Note that for convenience, we collect the fluxes
F_{\text{p}}
in a vector
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}}
, of length
n_{\lambda}
. For a given instrument, the planetary signal depends only on the planet’s SED,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}}
, and its position,

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}
.

A common approach to finding
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\text{p}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{p}}
is given by the maximum likelihood estimation
(
Thiébaut & Mugnier 2006
;
Mugnier et al. 2006
;
Dannert et al. 2022
)
. This entails maximizing the likelihood in Equation (
30
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}
or, equivalently minimizing the negative log-likelihood function,

\ell\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{\text{diff}}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{n_{t}}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\lambda}}\left(F_{\text{p}}(\lambda_{l})T_{\text{p},m}\left(t_{\tau},\lambda_{l},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}\right)-D_{m\tau\ell}\right)^{2}

(31)

with respect to these variables. Written out explicitly, the estimators for the SED and position are then given by

\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{p}}\right)=\arg\min_{(\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\right).

(32)

Typically, Equation (
32
) is solved using a matched filter approach
(
Thiébaut & Mugnier 2006
;
Mugnier et al. 2006
;
Dannert et al. 2022
)
. First, the template function that best describes the data is found by evaluating Equation (
31
) on a grid of possible planet positions. The global minimum of the map created in this way indicates the most likely planet position, yielding the corresponding template function. Given this template function, Equation (
32
) can be solved analytically
(
Dannert et al. 2022
)
.

In this study, we refrain from this analytical approach and solve Equation (
32
) numerically by performing a least-squares minimization based on the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, as implemented in the Python package lmfit
(
Newville et al. 2014
)
. This has the inherent benefit of providing us directly with an estimate of the full covariance of the estimated parameters
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\text{p}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{p}}
, an approach that could also be applied to real single-epoch data. Contrary to this, the analytical method relies on repeated simulation of the same observation and repeated extraction of the parameters to calculate the covariance from the statistics of the results
(
Dannert et al. 2022
)
.
To explain the covariance estimation of the numerical approach, we first concatenate our parameter vectors,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{p}}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{p}}
, into a single vector of length
n_{\lambda}+2
,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}\equiv\left[F_{\text{p},1},\ldots,F_{\text{p},n_{\lambda}},\alpha_{\text{p}},\beta_{\text{p}}\right]^{T}.

(33)

We write the negative log-likelihood function in Equation (
31
) also as a function of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, as
\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\right)
. Using the finite difference approximation
(
Newville et al. 2014
)
we calculate the Hessian,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\ell}
, of the negative log-likelihood function, which is a
(n_{\lambda}+2)\times(n_{\lambda}+2)
square matrix with elements

H_{uv}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{D}}\right)}{\partial\Theta_{u}\,\partial\Theta_{v}}.

(34)

Here,
\Theta_{u}
and
\Theta_{v}
are the
u
th and
v
th elements of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, respectively. An estimate of the covariance of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, is then given by the
(n_{\lambda}+2)\times(n_{\lambda}+2)
matrix,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\approx 2\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\ell}^{-1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\right),

(35)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\ell}^{-1}
is the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood function evaluated at
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}}

(
Newville et al. 2014
)
. In the following, we refer to
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
as the
spectral covariance
. An estimate of the standard deviations of the SED,
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\text{p}}
, and position,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{p}}
, can then be extracted from the diagonal elements of Equation (
35
). Note that the spectral covariance,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
, is calculated in a different context and must not be confused with the instrumental error covariance,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, from Equation (
21
), which we have used for the data whitening.

V
Discussion

V.1
Implications for Detection and Characterization

The results presented in Section
IV
suggest that a moderately perturbed interferometer (optimistic scenario) performs similarly to an ideal instrument in both detection and characterization. While this is promising, the significance of this result is tempered by the assumptions about the relatively simple wavelength dependence of the instrumental perturbations (see Equation (
9
)) and the availability of the instrumental error covariance through a calibration observation (see Section
V.2
). The results have also shown that instrumental errors in the form of instability noise predominantly affect the characterization of the planetary atmosphere below
\sim$10\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
, given a more strongly perturbed instrument (pessimistic scenario). This may affect the retrieval of certain atmospheric constituents, whose signatures are predominately expected at those wavelengths, such as methane
(
Konrad et al. 2022
)
.

V.2
Obtaining the Instrumental Error Covariance

As outlined in Section
III.1
, the prerequisite for data whitening is knowledge of the instrumental error covariance,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Cov}}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right)
. In this work, we have limited ourselves to the best-case scenario of having an ideal reference star (in the sense of having properties identical to those of the target star), as we have focused on the exploration of the theoretical implications of the whitening rather than the direct applicability to on-sky observations. In practice, different approaches will be required to obtain a good model of the instrumental errors. One approach that makes use of simulations of non-ideal reference star observations is followed by
Laugier et al. 2023
. They model the part of the noise covariance corresponding to the instrumental noise as

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{instrumental}}\approx\frac{F_{\star}}{F_{\text{ref}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{ref, MC}},

(36)

where
F_{\star}
is the total flux of the star of the current observation,
F_{\text{ref}}
is the total flux of the reference star, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{ref, MC}}
is the noise covariance, which they evaluate for different pointing directions using MC simulations. This approach allows them to build a parametric model of the instrument noise, which can then be used for data whitening. Another step in the direction of using calibrator stars is made by
Ireland 2013
. The author compares several calibration strategies, highlighting their biases, and describes the POISE (phase observationally independent of systematic errors) technique, which may also be relevant in the context of obtaining a suitable instrumental error covariance. In addition to calibrator stars, internal calibrator lasers may also be a viable option that should be explored in future work.

As mentioned earlier in Section
I
, alternative approaches exist that do not rely on calibration observations. For example,
Lay 2006
and
Matsuo et al. 2023
have used data-driven approaches that make assumptions about the shape of the spectral correlations (see bottom panel of Figure
4
) to fit or extrapolate them across the wavelength bins before finally subtracting them from the data. Although both approaches are successful in improving instrument performance, neither ensures that the test statistic follows a controlled distribution, as assumed in signal extraction and hypothesis testing, a property that is, in principle, inherent to data whitening.

Recent progress has been made by
Flasseur et al. 2024
in improving the estimates made from the data with analytical or numerical models. Such numerical models could be derived from simulators such as
PHRINGE
. The incorporation of the ZCA whitening transformation done here into a data-driven approach could be another step toward a suitable treatment of instrumental errors that is applicable to on-sky data. In this regard, it is also interesting to look at methods applied in classical high-contrast imaging of exoplanets, where the estimation and successive subtraction of the stellar point spread function from the data is well established
(
Meshkat et al. 2013
;
Gomez Gonzalez et al. 2016
, e.g.)
. A precise and accurate estimation of the covariance of an observation is ubiquitous in astronomical observations. More work is needed to develop more practical approaches, which we leave for future research.

Previous studies for the retrieval of exoplanet atmospheres in the context of LIFE have assumed that the errors across the wavelength bins of the planetary SED are uncorrelated and independent
(
Konrad et al. 2022
;
Alei et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. This corresponds to considering only the diagonal terms of the spectral covariance matrix, which has been calculated analytically using
LIFEsim

(
Dannert et al. 2022
)
. However, several studies have demonstrated an increase in the quality of retrieved parameters upon consideration of the full spectral covariance
(
Greco & Brandt 2016
;
Nasedkin et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. In this regard, implementing the numerical method for an estimation of the full spectral covariance matrix, as outlined in Section
III
, could contribute to a next step in the development of atmospheric retrievals for the LIFE mission in the presence of instrumental errors. Finally, these spectral covariances will also be interesting in the context of multiplanet systems, as we expect also correlations between the spectra of different planets.

VI
Summary & Conclusion

We have addressed the role of correlated errors in the specification of technical requirements for perturbed space-based nulling interferometers. We have presented a framework that quantitatively connects instrumental perturbations to performance metrics, accounting for the covariance of instrumental errors. Our findings show that data whitening is crucial for improving the sensitivity of detection tests and the quality of the extracted planetary properties.

Previous studies of the performance of such instruments have used assumptions about the independence of their data, which are known to be invalid. With the framework presented here, we can reassess the technical specifications using solid hypothesis testing criteria. Furthermore, our approach allows an estimate of the correlations of the posterior distribution of the astrophysical parameters so that they can be taken into account in atmospheric retrievals. In the context of the LIFE mission,
LIFEsimMC
will serve as an essential tool to support the derivation of requirements.
LIFEsimMC
also demonstrates the potential of
PHRINGE
to integrate into end-to-end simulation pipelines with its state-of-the-art synthetic data generation capabilities and support for the upcoming novel nulling data standard NIFITS.

More research is required to estimate the instrumental error covariance used in the whitening transformation directly from the data, reducing the reliance on observations from calibration stars. This will require efficient means to disentangle the planet from the noise. Further work is also required to account for temporal correlations and non-Gaussian noise in the whitened data to further improve the usability of the test statistics. A first step in this direction could involve integrating the approach of
Dannert et al. 2025
into the framework presented in this work.

Appendix A
Astrophysical Noise Sources

This section contains an overview of the astrophysical noise sources used in
PHRINGE
and
LIFEsimMC
.

A.1
Host Star

We model the star as a time-independent uniform disk of radius
r_{\star}
with the spectral radiance of a blackbody given by the Planck law. In particular, we do not consider effects such as limb darkening. Assuming the small-angle approximation for the angular radius of the star,
r_{\star}/d_{\star}
, its sky brightness is given by

I_{\star}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\Theta\left(\frac{r_{\star}}{d_{\star}}-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\right)B(\lambda,T_{\star}),

(A1)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function accounting for the disk shape of the star,
d_{\star}
the distance between the observatory and the star and
B
is the spectral radiance in units of
\mathrm{p}\mathrm{h}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}\,\mathrm{s}\mathrm{r}^{-2}
as defined by the Planck law:

B(\lambda,T)=\frac{2hc^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\frac{1}{\exp\left(hc/\left(k\lambda T\right)\right)-1}.

(A2)

Here,
h
the Planck constant,
c
the speed of light, and
k
the Boltzmann constant.

A.2
Local Zodiacal Dust

The zodiacal dust found mostly near the ecliptic plane in our Solar system acts as a source of background radiation for our measurement. We use the sky position-dependent model by
Den Hartog & Karlsson 2005
to model this contamination, which, in an ecliptic coordinate system, gives the sky brightness distribution

\displaystyle I_{\text{LZ}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})\;\hat{=}

\displaystyle I_{\text{LZ}}(\lambda,\lambda_{\text{rel}},\beta_{\text{lat}})

(A3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau\left(B(\lambda,T_{\text{eff}})+AB_{\lambda}(T_{\odot})\left(\frac{r_{\odot}}{$1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{U}$}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{\pi/\arccos\left(\cos\lambda_{\text{rel}}\cos\beta_{\text{lat}}\right)}{\sin^{2}\beta_{\text{lat}}+0.6\cdot\left(\lambda/($11\text{$\cdot$}{10}^{-6}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$)\right)^{-0.4}\cos^{2}\beta_{\text{lat}}}\right)^{0.5},

where
\lambda_{\text{rel}}=\lambda_{\text{ecl}}-\lambda_{\text{ecl},\odot}
is the ecliptic longitude relative to the Sun,
\beta_{\text{lat}}
is the ecliptic latitude,
\tau=4\cdot 10^{-8}
is the optical depth toward the ecliptic poles,
T_{\text{eff}}=$265\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
is the effective temperature of the local zodiacal dust at
1\text{\,}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{U}
from the Sun,
A=0.22
is the near-IR dust albedo,
T_{\odot}=$5778\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
is the temperature of the Sun and
r_{\odot}
is the radius of the Sun in AU.

A.3
Exozodiacal Dust

Similar to the zodiacal dust in our own Solar system, exoplanetary systems are expected to contain zodiacal dust. We follow the same approach as
Dannert et al. 2022
and use the model by
Kennedy et al. 2015
to account for the exozodiacal dust in our simulations. We thus model it as a face-on disk surrounding the host star of the planet, using a power-law distribution for its surface density,
\sigma
, and a radius-dependent temperature profile,
T_{\text{EZ}}
. This yields the following sky brightness distribution:

I_{\text{EZ}}(\lambda,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})B(\lambda,T_{EZ}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})).

(A4)

The unitless surface density is defined as

\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=7.12\cdot 10^{-8}z\left(|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\;\frac{d_{\star}}{\text{m}}\left(\frac{L_{\star}}{L_{\odot}}\right)^{0.5}\right)^{-0.34},

(A5)

where
z
is the so-called zodi level, a unitless factor indicating a
z
-times higher surface density than the one of the zodiacal dust in our Solar system and
L_{\star}/L_{\odot}
is the luminosity of the star in units of Solar luminosity. The temperature profile is defined as
