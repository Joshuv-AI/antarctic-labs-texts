Feasibility of using toroidal transceivers for acquiring intraoperative MR images around deep brain stimulation electrodes
Authors: Nur Izzati Huda Zulkarnain, Alireza Sadeghi-Tarakameh, Russell L. Lagore, Dee M. Koski, Gregory J. Metzger, Zuzan Cayci, Noam Harel, Yigitcan Eryaman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides excellent soft tissue contrast for visualizing of deep brain stimulation (DBS) targets, allowing validation of the electrode placement, and assessing complications such as microhemorrhage and edema. However, the presence of the electrodes can introduce challenges such as radiofrequency (RF) induced current artifacts and excessive heating of the electrode contacts. Additionally, extended procedure times are also considered a disadvantage when using MRI as an intraoperative imaging modality following DBS electrode placement.Method:We propose a novel approach of using toroidal resonators to inductively couple the shaft of the electrode to the scanner’s transmit-receive chain thereby utilizing it as a localized imaging antenna. The small extent of the field generated by the electrode antenna allows fast imaging with smaller field-of-views (FOVs) spanning only a few centimeters. Furthermore, we present a fast and accurate safety monitoring strategy that can be used to predict the temperature increase at the electrical contacts of the electrode.Results and Discussion:Imaging with the toroidal transceiver yields a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) efficiency in proximity to the electrodes. This approach reduced the RF induced current artifacts around the electrode which enhanced the visibility of the shaft and improved electrode localization. Moreover, the limited sensitivity around the electrode can be exploited to perform fast scans with small FOVs. The predicted heating around DBS contacts was in quantitative agreement with the experimental heating in swine studies with a normalized root-mean-square error (NRMSE) ≤ 0.09.

Introduction

Intraoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a critical role in deep brain stimulation (DBS) placement surgeries (Succop et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2016; Li et al., 2021; Nishiguchi et al., 2022). Its superior soft tissue contrast compared to computed tomography (CT) allows for the visualization of anatomically defined DBS targets such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN) (Isaacs et al., 2020; Dormont et al., 2004) and the globus pallidus internus (GPi) (Li et al., 2021) subsequently enabling precise verification of the electrode placement (Ostrem et al., 2013; Pollo et al., 2007; Nuzov et al., 2023), in addition to the assessment of post-surgery complications such as hemorrhage and edema (Succop et al., 2023). However, any MRI scan with DBS electrodes in situ presents various challenges (Nuzov et al., 2023; Boutet et al., 2020). Acquiring artifact-free images for DBS electrode localization can be difficult particularly with the commonly used T1- and T2- weighted sequences (Isaacs et al., 2020; Dormont et al., 2004). These sequences are known to produce severe image artifacts due to radiofrequency (RF) induced current on the electrodes (Eryaman et al., 2019). Furthermore, the high RF power required by these sequences increases the risk of thermal injuries due to excessive heating around the electrode contacts (Boutet et al., 2020; Henderson et al., 2005).

Since the RF heating problem was first reported two decades ago, a variety of different approaches have been adopted to address it (Yeung and Atalar, 2001; Vu et al., 2021; Guerin et al., 2019; Eryaman et al., 2015; Acikel, Uslubas and Atalar, 2015). A common strategy used by device manufacturers involves measuring the transfer function model of the electrode to predict local specific absorption rate (SAR) and temperature rise around the electrical contacts (Park, Kamondetdacha and Nyenhuis, 2007). Also adopted in ISO/TS 10974 (International Organization for Standardization Technical Standard (ISO/TS) 2018), this strategy is commonly used to calculate safe incident B1+ RMS and whole-body/head average SAR values to establish safe imaging protocols. However, these safety guidelines (Medtronic 2015; St Jude Medical, 2018) are derived from statistical considerations of heating in patients with either fully implanted systems (electrodes, extension and IPG) or with electrodes and extension cables placed subcutaneously. On the other hand, intraoperative MRI is typically needed when the electrodes are placed at the target, but extension wires have not yet passed under the skin to be connected to the implantable pulse generator (IPG) in the chest or abdomen. Therefore, the guidelines provided by the manufacturer may not directly apply to these patients. Moreover, electrode heating is known to be a highly trajectory-dependent problem (Vu et al., 2021), which underlines the need for a rapid, patient-specific safety assessment workflow to predict electrode heating prior to MR scanning. Another challenge associated with intraoperative MRI is the scan duration (Saleh et al., 2016). MRI acquisition is significantly slower than alternative imaging modalities such as fluoroscopy and CT, which is a disadvantage for intraoperative imaging. Thus, a fast, image-based electrode placement confirmation is desirable for patients and medical staff undergoing long hours of surgery.

Previously, an innovative transceiver approach was developed for interventional MRI using toroidal resonators (Etezadi-Amoli et al., 2015). This approach allowed controlled levels of RF current to flow in a catheter/guidewire for device visualization in the MRI scanner. Temperature increases at the tip of a guidewire were measured and reported for a fixed toroid location at varying power levels, providing valuable estimates for safe power that result in negligible RF heating during device visualization. However, the application of this approach to different devices (e.g., DBS electrodes) and the dependence of temperature increase on toroid location and tissue type have yet to be investigated. Additionally, the work reported a noteworthy finding related to the increased SNR close to the guidewires, which is advantageous for device visualization. Yet, its implications for imaging around DBS electrodes remain unclear. Therefore, understanding the SNR advantages of using a toroidal transceiver compared to using an external receiver array requires further investigation. Due to the needed access to the surgical field, the typical external RF array used for imaging during these interventions is not the standard head coil used for diagnostic imaging but rather a smaller flexible surface coil placed on the head adjacent to the insertion point.

Intraoperative MRI scans are conducted after the electrode contacts are implanted in the brain while the electrode shaft remains partially outside of the skull. Consequently, this exposed part can be accessed and inductively coupled to the scanner’s transmit-receive (T/R) chain using a toroid resonator. This approach allows not only for visualizing the electrode but also to utilize it as a T/R antenna to acquire high SNR images at its immediate vicinity. The extent of the magnetic field generated by the electrode is limited in the radial direction. However, this limited sensitivity pattern can be leveraged to conduct fast scans with small field of views (FOVs), typically a few centimeters. Besides, the temperature increases at the electrical contacts of the electrode can be predicted by adapting the previously proposed workflow (Sadeghi-Tarakameh et al., 2022). If an electrode transimpedance specific to this toroidal excitation problem can be calculated, the workflow can be used to predict the temperature for different MR sequences, toroid excitation locations and electrode insertion lengths, which would enable the transition of the approach to many new clinical applications.

In this work, motivated by the potential advantages summarized above, we investigated the feasibility of using a toroidal transceiver for intraoperative imaging of DBS electrodes (model 6173, Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA). Building upon the previous work that introduced the proposed approach (Etezadi-Amoli et al., 2015), we experimentally investigated the imaging performance of a DBS electrode coupled to the T/R chain of an MR scanner with a toroidal transceiver. For this purpose, we conducted phantom experiments where we compared SNR and RF induced current artifacts around the electrode obtained by a coil array, suitable for intraoperative DBS imaging, against a toroidal transceiver. Furthermore, in order to assess the RF heating risk, we utilized a rapid, image-based, patient-specific safety assessment workflow that predicts electrode heating prior to MR scanning (Sadeghi-Tarakameh et al., 2022). Through phantom and animal studies, we demonstrated the proposed safety assessment workflow.

Methods

Overview

The small flip angle signal intensity (S) in a GRE sequence due to an electrode utilized as a T/R antenna can be written as a proportionality as follows: (1) S(r)~ITxIRxr2

Here, ITx and IRx represent the shaft currents used to calculate transmit (Tx) and receive (Rx) profiles. Because of the 1/r2 term dependency in the signal profiles, the sensitivity of the electrode antenna changes rapidly along the radial direction. Therefore, the SNR obtained with the toroidal resonator, in the immediate vicinity of the electrode can be very high, compared to the SNR obtained by an external coil array. Furthermore, the rapid radial drop of the sensitivity profile has another consequence. Because the overall image intensity vanishes further from the electrode, smaller FOVs can be adopted for imaging around the electrode. When combined with the SNR advantage, this approach can enable higher resolution and/or shorter scan times compared to conventional coils used in intraoperative MRI.

Previously, the heating around the electrical contacts of DBS electrodes fully immersed in a uniform phantom was predicted using a workflow consists of an MR-based current measurement method (Eryaman et al., 2019) and quasi-static electromagnetic (EM) and thermal simulations (Sadeghi-Tarakameh et al., 2022). The workflow requires an initial offline calibration phase, where an equivalent transimpedance (RDBS) value was calculated. During this phase, temperature increase and the shaft current, Is, were experimentally measured in a homogenous phantom. The contact voltage, Vc, was calculated by iteratively matching the time course of the temperature increase from the thermal simulation with the heating experiment. Is was measured experimentally by analyzing the MR artifact generated by the DBS electrode. The ratio of the contact voltage to the shaft current yielded the equivalent transimpedance. Once the transimpedance value was calculated, it was used to predict the temperature increase at the contacts of the DBS electrode for different trajectories and termination conditions.

The workflow summarized above can be adapted to predict temperature increases for partially implanted electrodes as well. The local excitation by the toroidal resonator induces a current distribution on the electrode generating a B1+ field around it. At a certain distance away from the conductor and with a sufficiently large excitation voltage, the shaft current is expected to create a circular dark band around the electrode corresponding to a 180° flip angle region. Knowing the flip angle (i.e., 180°) at the location of the dark band and the RF pulse shape, the B1+ at this location (per Volt) can be calculated as follows: (2) B1,perVolt+=πγ⋅Vexc∫0tprf(t)dt where Vexc is the excitation voltage, rf(t) denotes the time-dependent variation of the RF pulse, tp represents the pulse duration of the sequence, and γ is the gyromagnetic ratio (267.52 rad s−1 μT−1 for proton). Because the B1+ field contributed solely from the current induced along the electrode, the root-mean-square (RMS) shaft current can be calculated as (Etezadi-Amoli et al., 2015): (3) Is=B1,perVolt+4πrdarkbandμ0Vsequence where rdarkband is the radius of the dark band and Vsequence is the RMS voltage applied during the pulse sequence. Once Is is measured, it can be scaled with the RDBS value to assign boundary conditions on the electrode simulation model and conduct temperature simulations similar to previous work (Sadeghi-Tarakameh et al., 2022).

Imaging setup

All experiments were conducted on a 3 T Magnetom Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). A toroidal transceiver was constructed (diameter 17.5 mm, length 60 mm), tuned and matched to 50 Ω at 123 MHz. Fig. 1 shows the toroidal transceiver setup and the electronic system to connect to the scanner TR/RX chain. An in-house 3D-printed glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate (PETG) interface box connected the transceiver to the Siemens patient table. It features a single BNC jack for transceiver RF connection. A 1Tx/8Rx TIM (Total Imaging Matrix, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) patient table plug supplies one transmit and up to eight receive RF connections, alongside a 10 V DC supply for the low noise amplifier (LNA) and PIN diode bias signals (100 mA forward / −30 V reverse). The interface box contains a single T/R switch using three PIN diodes (MA4P7470F-1072T, MACOM, Lowell, MA, USA) for RF switching. In Tx mode, diodes are forward biased completing the circuit to the coil port while in receive mode, they are reverse biased with a PIN diode switching the LC tank circuit (tuned to 123 MHz) to isolate and protect the receive electronics. Passive components include multilayer ceramic capacitors from American Technical Ceramics (100B series, ATC, Huntington Station, NY, USA) and Knowles-Syfer (Itasca, IL, USA) and airwound and ceramic core inductors from Coilcraft (Cary, IL, USA). An integrated LNA (SPF5122Z, Qorvo, Greensboro, NC, USA) with a gain of 25 dB and de-embedded noise figure of 0.5 dB is included on the receive-side of the T/R switch. The interface box maintained an approximate overall noise figure of 1 dB due to additional insertion losses from the RF connection and T/R switch.

SNR efficiency comparison

Imaging using the toroidal transceiver and a 4-channel flex receiver array (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) was conducted in an SNR comparison study. The study was performed with an anthropomorphic head phantom as shown in Fig. 2 (Guérin et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2020). For imaging with the toroidal transceiver, the proximal portion of a DBS electrode, where the extension cable would be connected to, was inserted through the toroidal cavity. For imaging with the flex coil, the toroidal transceiver was removed, and the electrode was looped and taped at the top of the skull. The flex coil was positioned to cover the lateral parts of the skull making sure the superior portion remained accessible, similar to a typical coil positioning in an MR-guided DBS surgery (Gravbrot et al., 2020). The distal 4.5 cm of the electrode, where the electrical contacts are situated, was inserted in the phantom at an oblique angle of 20°

A 2D-T2 turbo spin-echo (T2-TSE) sequence was used to compare the SNR efficiency between toroidal transceiver and flex coil in the proximity of the electrode shaft. SNR was measured on imaging slices orthogonal to the shaft of the electrode roughly 2.5 cm from the distal tip. The imaging parameters for both sequences utilized in both setups are presented in Table 1. To measure noise, the voltages were set to 0 V and the standard deviation of the acquired images was calculated. The signal images were divided by the noise levels to calculate pixel SNR. The SNR maps near the electrode were normalized to the acquisition times and averaged within circular rings with a fixed thickness of 3 voxels at varying radii to evaluate the radial variation of SNR obtained with the two methods. The SNR data at radii smaller than the electrode radius (r ≤ 0.65 mm) were excluded from the analysis.

Estimated transmit SAR efficiency comparison

The transmit SAR efficiency in this study was defined as the total B1,RMS+ around the electrode normalized to the peak SAR. The total B1,RMS+ was derived by calculating the induced current on the electrode, estimated by analyzing the RF artifact around the electrode visible during imaging. The peak SAR was obtained by measuring the initial heating slope during a 3D turbo spin-echo (3D-TSE) sequence (TR/TE = 300/103 ms, echo train length = 15, flip angle = 150°, in-plane resolution = 0.5 mm, slice thickness = 1.25 mm). This comparison study was performed in a rectangular box phantom filled with a HEC gel (conductivity = 0.45 S m−1, relative permittivity = 78, specific heat capacity = 4.25 MJ/m3-K). Fluoroptic temperature probes (Lumasense Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) were placed 2 mm away from the most distal contact to measure the temperature increase.

For the toroidal transceiver, the DBS electrode was partially immersed in the gel (Fig. 3A). An axial 2D-GRE image (TR/TE = 500/3.06 ms, in-plane resolution = 0.34 mm) was acquired 3 cm away from the tip to analyze the electrode-induced, dark band artifact around the electrode shaft. The current flowing on the electrode, Is, was calculated as per Eqs. (2) and 3. To verify the linearity of the measurement, images were acquired with different excitation voltages and the resulting rdarkband values were measured. For each excitation voltage, the rdarkband was measured as follows: Eight different points were selected by visual inspection in a circle within the dark band distributed as uniformly as possible. The center of gravity of these 8 points was calculated and used as the estimate for the center of the electrode. The distance between all 8 sample points to the center of gravity was calculated and averaged to calculate rdarkband. Using the current value derived from this measurements, the total B1,RMS+ map around the electrode can be generated.

For the body coil, the DBS electrode was fully immersed in the gel. Unlike the toroidal transceiver, the total B1+ field is a combination of the incident B1+ field and the B1+ generated by the current induced along the wire. The incident B1+ field was measured in a plane 1 mm away from the electrode tip. The induced B1+ was measured using the Tx-null measurement in a 3D-GRE image, as described by Sadagehi-Tarakameh et al. (Sadeghi-Tarakameh et al., 2022). The fields due to the shaft current and the incident B1+ were superimposed to generate the total B1,RMS+around the shaft.

Phantom heating study

RF heating studies was conducted using an adapted temperature prediction workflow tailored to the toroidal transceiver setup. The current measurement and heating experiment described in Section 2.4 for toroidal transceiver were repeated. The shaft current was scaled with a transimpedance and assigned as a voltage boundary conditions, Vc, in the quasi-static SAR simulation as described in previous work (Etezadi-Amoli et al., 2015). RDBS was iteratively calibrated to minimize the normalized root-mean-square error (NRMSE), thereby obtaining the best match between simulated and measured temperature increase.

The impact of varying tissue EM properties on RDBS value was investigated by using hydroxyethycellulose (HEC) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) gels. For this purpose, gel phantoms with different conductivity and permittivity were prepared (Table 2). The impact of the toroid’s position along the electrode relative to the DBS proximal end was investigated during the PVP phantom studies by varying the DBS insertion length in the toroidal cavity. For all phantom experiments, the aforementioned RDBS calculation was repeated accordingly.

Animal heating study

The adapted workflow was also used to predict temperature increase around electrodes implanted in two swine (Yorkshire-cross, weighing approximately 40 kg each). All experiments were compliant with a protocol approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Minnesota. Surgery procedures and heating experiments were conducted under anesthesia (0.5 – 1.5 % isoflurane). The distal portion of the DBS electrode, 4–5 cm from the distal tip, and a fluoroptic temperature probe, placed near the most distal contact, were surgically implanted into the swine brain. A gap of 1–2 mm between the electrode and temperature probe was intentionally left by inserting, two thin rolls of tape between them. Fig. 3B shows the experimental setup in the animal studies. Six RF heating experiments were conducted using the same 3D-TSE sequences with varying reference voltages to achieve different heating levels. Of these, four in vivo experiments were performed with two animals, and two postmortem experiments were conducted with one animal.

Using the results obtained from the phantom studies described above, a RDBS value specific to the swine brain properties was calculated. For this purpose, the RDBS values measured for the four phantom media were linearly interpolated using the following equation, (4) RDBS=c1σ+c2ε+c3 where σ and ε are the electrical conductivity and relative permittivity of the medium, c1,c2 and c3 are constants determined by solving the system of linear equations constructed from phantom transimpedance measurements. The EM properties of the swine brain were assumed as σ=0.46Sm−1 and ε=63 as described in a previous work (Matković et al., 2022). Similar to the phantom experiments, Is was measured in swine by calculating the rdarkband induced around the electrode in the 2D-GRE images.

Additional EM and thermal simulations were conducted for the animal experiments. Vc for each heating experiment was calculated by multiplying the measured Is with RDBS determined from phantom experiments and used as a voltage boundary condition in the EM simulation. The resulting SAR distribution was used as the input for the thermal simulations (specific heat capacity of 3.60 MJm−3 K−1 and thermal conductivity of 0.5 W m−1 K−1) to predict the temperature increase around the contacts (Shrivastava et al., 2012). For modeling the in vivo temperature increase, a constant blood–tissue heat transfer coefficient of 9000 W m−3 K−1 was used based on previous work by Shrivastava et al. (Shrivastava et al., 2012). For the postmortem experiments, the same coefficient was assumed to be zero. Note that, no fine tuning or readjustment of RDBS was made for the swine studies. The value calculated from the gel phantom studies,100 Ω, was used as it is for the swine experiments with no modification.

Uncertainty analysis

The SAR is proportional to the square of the contact voltage and the conductivity of the medium. On the other hand, the contact voltage is calculated as the shaft current multiplied by the transimpedance. Using the linear approximation of the transimpedance in Eq. (4), the ΔT around the electrodes can be written as a proportionality as follows: (5) ΔT~CσIs2c1σ+c2ε+c32 where C is the specific heat capacity of the brain tissue, σ is the electrical conductivity of the tissue, ε is the relative permittivity and Is is the shaft current. Note that this approximation ignores any non-linear effects, including diffusion and perfusion. Assuming everything else is constant, uncertainty in Is can be calculated as follows: for each individual point used to sample the dark band, there exists an uncertainty of Δr, where r is the distance of the sampling point to the electrode center. For any point on the circle it can be assumed that Δr<Δx2+Δy2 where Δx and Δy denote the image resolution in phase-encoding and frequency-encoding directions. For Δx=Δy the uncertainty of the current measurement would be 2Δx. For a total number of N points, the uncertainty would be reduced by the N. Then the uncertainty with respect to the mean of the RF current measurement would be 2ΔxNrdarkband..

The overall uncertainty in ΔT was calculated by running a Monte-Carlo simulation where the medium was allowed to have tissue properties (C,σ and ε) within bounds determined by the properties of white matter and gray matter in a uniformly distributed manner. Similarly, Is was also allowed to fluctuate around a mean value of 1, within its individual uncertainty determined by a uniform distribution. Each ΔT value, corresponding to different tissue parameters, transimpedance, and Is values, is then divided by a nominal ΔTn value, calculated from the average of the white and gray matter’s tissue properties and a nominal current value of Is=1. The distribution of ΔTΔTn was calculated from 106 different variations. The 99.9th percentile of the distribution represents the overall uncertainty of the temperature results.

Preliminary anatomy and contrast imaging

As a preliminary demonstration of the toroidal coil’s imaging potential, 3D-GRE (TR/TE = 18/2.4 ms, flip angle = 30°, in-plane resolution = 1.0 mm, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, FOV = 200 mm × 200 mm) and 2D-TSE (TR/TE = 6000/107 ms, echo train length = 11, flip angle = 150°, in-plane resolution = 0.39 mm, slice thickness = 3.0 mm) acquisitions were performed in a deceased swine. The surgical procedure to insert the electrode was similar to the animal heating experiment, however, it was inserted deeper to acquire signal from a larger portion of the brain. Additionally, a skull phantom experiment was conducted, where a 1.3 mm catheter filled with HEC (4.5 g/L of NaCl and 0.72 g/L of CuSO4) mimicking the T2 of blood (Thangavel and Saritas, 2017) was imaged using the toroidal transceiver. The extracranial portion of the electrode was looped during imaging with the flex coil, similar to the setup shown in Fig. 2B. The catheter was placed 2 mm away from the electrode in parallel, coinciding within the region with high SNR identified from the studies conducted in Section 2.3. The skull cavity was filled with HEC (2.25 g/L of NaCl, 2.67 g/L of CuSO4) mimicking the average properties of the white and gray matter. Imaging was performed using the same 2D-TSE imaging sequences described in Section 2.3. The distances between the electrode, temperature probe and the catheter were recorded to verify their position in the resulting image.

Results

SNR efficiency comparison

Fig. 4 shows the 2D-TSE images obtained around the shaft of the electrode with the toroidal resonator and the 4-channel flex coil. Fig. 4B shows the SNR efficiency results measured in an axial slice 2.5 cm away from the tip. The SNR analyses showed that the toroid outperformed the flex coil in SNR efficiency significantly up to 4.2 mm away from the center of the electrode for the T2-weighted TSE sequences. Another important finding demonstrated in Fig. 4C is the reduction of the artifact around the electrode, with the electrode shaft clearly seen, when imaged with the toroidal transceiver. On the other hand, due to the complex interference between the RF induced current artifact and the incident field of the body coil, as well as the coupling to the receiver flex coil, it is difficult to identify the true location of the shaft of the electrode, as shown in Fig. 4D. In addition, despite the reduced FOV with the toroid, which shortened maintained an SNR advantage up to 3.0 mm. Note that the TR, TE, resolution and bandwidth per pixel was consistent between the image acquisitions with the two coils.

Transmit SAR efficiency comparison

Fig. 5 shows the dark band that appears around the DBS electrode due to spins experiencing a 180° flip angle during the 2D-GRE sequence. The 8 sampling points visually chosen on the dark band is shown in Fig. 5A. The linear relationship between Vexc and measured rdarkband values is shown in Fig. 5B. Importantly, there was no heating observed during the acquisition.

The comparison of transmit SAR efficiency near and around the electrode in Fig. 6 shows the toroidal transceiver achieved efficiency comparable to the body coil, despite its smaller size. While the body coil exhibited higher average transmit SAR efficiency than the toroidal transceiver, it does not account for both under-flipping and over-flipping of signals caused by interference between the B1 field from the electrode and the incident B1 field. The region where the body coil exhibit low transmit SAR efficiency are affected by various factors, such as electrode trajectory and RF excitation, making the artifact’s severity unpredictable. In contrast, the toroidal transceiver prevents such artifacts, ensuring more reliable imaging and minimizing significant signal loss near the electrode.

Phantom heating study

Fig. 7 shows the result of matching between the measured and simulated temperature increase to subsequently calculate the RDBS for phantom with different EM properties. Note that for each HEC phantom studies with different conductivity, the temperature increase was measured and matched at two locations. Meanwhile, in the PVP phantom study, the matching process was performed for four different DBS insertion lengths.

Fig. 8 shows the variation of the RDBS values for different mediums conductivity (7A), medium permittivity (7B) and electrode insertion lengths in the PVP phantom (7C). The results show a decrease with the conductivity and permittivity. We used these results to approximate the RDBS value for swine brain tissue by solving a linear set of equations using Eq. (4). The constants c1, c2 and c3 was calculated as −93.5, −0.51 and 175.8. On the other hand, the RDBS value for different insertion lengths did not show a significant variation with a maximum variation of 4 %. This result demonstrated that the RDBS is independent of the location of the toroid.

Animal heating study

Fig. 9 shows the results of two animal experiments. Similar to phantoms, the dark band around the DBS electrode due to spins experiencing 180° flip angle was visualized and analyzed in the brain tissue. Fig. 8 also shows the predicted and measured temperature progression curves obtained in the two animals for different shaft currents; in vivo and postmortem. It should be highlighted that the high heating level was deliberately chosen to ensure an adequate temperature increase, allowing us to validate the heating prediction workflow. Note that the RDBS value was not recalculated or fine-tuned to obtain these results. The same value obtained from the phantom studies (100 Ω) was directly used to predict the heating in swine with NRMSE ≤ 0.09.

Uncertainty analysis

Fig. 10 shows the histogram of the ΔTΔTn ratio calculated by using Eq. (5), from 106 different variations. The values of [C,σ,ε] were randomly assigned between properties of the white matter (3583 J kg−1 °C−1, 0.33 S m−1, 53) and gray matter (3696 J kg−1 °C−1, 0.58 S m−1, 74). The uncertainty in Is is dependent on image resolution and the amount of current flowing on the shaft. Here, we adopted the values from the swine studies and calculated the uncertainty as 6.5 % by using the value of rdarkband=2.3mm and number of sample points = 8. ΔTn value was calculated using the average properties of the white and gray matter’s tissue properties and a nominal current value of Is=1. The 99.9th percentile of the distribution was calculated as 1.26 and represents the overall uncertainty of the temperature prediction results. Note that the NRMSE ≤ 0.09 results of the swine studies remained well within the 1–1.26 value. The uncertainty calculation can be repeated with any image resolution and shaft current value. The resulting 99.9th percentile can be used as a safety factor for temperature prediction in human studies.

Preliminary anatomy and contrast imaging

The images shown in Fig. 11 demonstrate the ability of the toroidal transceiver to image anatomy. In the 3D GRE image, nearly the entire swine brain could be visualized, along with the skin, fat layer, and skull near the insertion site. Additionally, the 2D TSE imaging allowed clear visualization of the gyri providing detailed structural information of the brain near the electrode. <0.5 °C temperature increase was observed during both imaging sequences. In the T2-weighted 2D TSE images from the skull phantom in Fig. 12, the toroidal transceiver was able to acquire a higher signal in the catheter filled with blood-like material compared to the background phantom. In contrast, the flex coil image showed the signal from the catheter was obscured by a hypointense artifact. Minimal heating (≤ 0.3 °C) was observed in both cases.

Discussion

Utilizing a toroidal transceiver to excite and receive MR signal with a DBS electrode inserted in tissue was shown to offer advantages over conventional setup. Its limited transmit and receive profile allowed for the selection of smaller FOVs, significantly reducing scan duration for the same image resolution. Although the ability to image the entire brain was lost with this approach, important anatomical landmark locations near the electrode may still be visible. We believe this aspect needs to be further investigated with human studies. If demonstrated to be true, it may allow the registration of intraoperative and preoperative images to validate the electrode location.

Additionally, the increased SNR efficiency achieved with the toroid in the immediate vicinity of the electrode (up to 3.0 mm from the center of the electrode) is advantageous even with the smaller FOV, and offers the added benefit of reduced acquisition time. While a circular ROI with 3.0 mm radius may seem small, it is considerably larger than the apparent size of a standard DBS electrode of radius ~ 0.65 mm in MRI. Therefore, it can potentially allow visualization of edema and hemorrhage that are commonly observed along the path of electrode insertion (Nishiguchi et al., 2022). Furthermore, in sequences less sensitive to the flip angle (e.g., GRE) the high-SNR region is expected to be even larger.

Moreover, in the absence of interference between the DBS electrode and other coils, the RF induced current artifacts were significantly reduced using the toroidal resonator, improving the visibility of the shaft which can be potentially advantageous for electrode localization. Notably, the black band on the shaft of the electrode (Fig. 4A) can be seen clearly in the MR images acquired with the resonator (Fig. 4C), whereas the visibility of the black band as well as the entire shaft of the electrode was reduced in the images acquired with the flex coil. Visualizing the location of such markers on the electrodes (e.g., black band) can be crucial to estimate the position of the electrical contacts in the brain during intraoperative imaging studies. On a different note, the susceptibility related artifacts are expected to remain comparable for both the toroidal transceiver and the surface receiver array.

Temperature prediction was successfully demonstrated with swine experiments. Although the tissue dielectric properties are expected to change after euthanasia, the heating measurements were conducted within an hour, during which the changes are minimal (Schmid et al., 2003). Despite not accounting for these changes, the workflow still yielded accurate heating predictions around the DBS electrode, demonstrating the robustness of the approach. The prediction was conducted 1–2 mm away from the DBS contacts (Delcey et al., 2021). While it is recognized that heating is higher closer to the contacts, distance was introduced to validate the heating prediction without affecting measurement accuracy. The demonstrated approach allows for the accurate prediction of temperatures very close to the contacts which can be used to guide acquisition parameter adjustments.

The transimpedance value obtained from phantom experiments mimicking swine brain tissue can be applied directly for the swine studies. Transimpedance is influenced by various factors, including the geometry of electrical contacts, and dielectric properties of the medium. While we have observed a linear relationship between transimpedance and the medium’s dielectric properties we do not yet have a theoretical explanation for this relationship. Regardless, a suitable transimpedance value for the range of tissue parameters relevant to this study could be approximated by performed phantom experiments. A similar approach can easily be used to ensure RF safety with human subjects using the calculated safety factor.

Furthermore, it was shown that a single transimpedance value was sufficient to predict temperature for different toroid locations. Adjusting the toroid’s position relative to the proximal end of the DBS electrode, or in other words, varying the DBS insertion length within the toroidal cavity, affects the reflection parameter, S11. Measuring S11 variations for different insertion lengths confirmed this influence (Supplemental figure Figure S1). For the electrode-only measurement, S11 improved as the toroid moved further in, reaching an optimum −21 dB at 12 cm. For the electrode connected with an extension cable, the S11 parameter, although less optimal compared to the electrode-only case, showed a similar trend. The S11 gradually improved up to 20 cm before the performance started declining again as the toroid approached the connecting point. Although improved matching can enhance SNR, the transimpedance measurements showed minimal variation, even with DBS insertion length up to 30 cm from the proximal end. These results confirm that the transimpedance is independent of insertion length, and the RF heating prediction remains accurate with the presented workflow. This finding increases confidence in the applicability of the method in clinical scenarios where the toroid location may be randomly chosen by surgeons.

3D-GRE and 2D-TSE images in swine demonstrated the ability of the toroidal transceiver to image anatomy and obtain useful contrast around the electrode. <0.5 °C temperature increase was observed during both imaging sequences. In addition, a phantom experiment with the skull phantom was performed to test the imaging of high T2 signals from blood-like material near the electrode, mimicking conditions of edema or hemorrhage which typically occur along the electrode insertion path. This setup allowed for controlled positioning of the HEC-filled catheter near the electrode. In contrast to the toroidal transceiver, the flex coil failed to accurately visualize the catheter at the expected location. While high T2 signals were observed near it, they did not correspond to the actual catheter position, suggesting that it might be an artifact. Note that the extracranial portion of the electrode was looped, a configuration shown to reduce heating yet the artifact still remained. (Vu et al., 2021) The toroidal transceiver, however, was able to reliably image the catheter without such ambiguity and eliminated the highly trajectory-dependent heating issues, making it a more reliable and safer option for intraoperative imaging during DBS surgery. As this technology can be further developed, its potential to provide improved clinically relevant images can be further improved.

In the thermal simulations, a constant blood–tissue heat transfer (Shrivastava et al., 2012) coefficient model was utilized, resulting in good quantitative agreement with experimental data. For potential MRI studies involving human subjects, more complex perfusion models (Murbach et al., 2014) could be used in the proposed workflow. Alternatively, a more conservative approach could also be adopted by using a perfusion-free model to address the worst-case thermal scenario.

Conclusion

This proof-of-concept study presents preliminary results toward demonstrating the safe and effective use of a toroidal resonator for intraoperative imaging during DBS placement surgery. The RF induced current artifacts around the electrode were significantly reduced and scan duration was shortened without compromising the image resolution. Furthermore, the SNR obtained by the proposed setup outperformed a conventional 4-channel imaging coil at the vicinity of the electrode. Modifying a previously proposed temperature prediction workflow allowed accurate temperature prediction in phantoms and swine experiments, increasing confidence in the safety of the approach.
