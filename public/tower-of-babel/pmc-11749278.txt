Service providers’ perspectives and reproductive (in)justice among Roma women: a qualitative study in Spain
Authors: María Félix Rodríguez-Camacho, María José Sanchís-Ramón, Gaby Ortiz Barreda, Diana Gil-González
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main objective of this study is to identify the barriers that prevent Roma women and girls from accessing reproductive rights derived from reproductive justice by analysing the discourses of service providers as a facilitating source of information. A qualitative study including nine semi-structured in-person interviews with community service providers was conducted in April-June 2019. The interview script addressed intercultural competences, views on gender discrimination against Roma women, resources available to fight against this discrimination, knowledge and use of these resources in reproductive justice, as well as the networks of actors involved. The qualitative analysis involved a discourse analysis. In the analysis, three focal areas were defined around which barriers to access spaces inspired by reproductive justice for Roma women could be identified. In each of the focal areas (stereotypes, discrimination and intercultural competences), discourses and practices have been observed in contrasting ways that can prevent access, as well as others that can facilitate access based on prejudices about Roma culture and people. The relationship models between professionals and users of the Roma population project stereotypes that are an obstacle in terms of accessing reproductive justice. The right of Roma women to preserve their culture and to actively participate in policies that affect them is not included and guaranteed in public policies. The intention to include the Roma population’s situation in an inclusive narrative exists, although without short-term effects. Reproductive justice is an absent framework within professional intervention and the institutional system. Introduction

Women’s reproductive rights are recognised all over the world as a fundamental part of human rights, which in itself constitutes progress in the reconceptualisation of this term.1 These rights protect and guarantee women’s freedom and autonomy, allowing them to make free and responsible decisions about their own bodies and reproduction. Reproductive rights include men’s and women’s rights to obtain information about family planning and safe, effective, affordable and acceptable fertility regulation methods, and the right to receive adequate health services that guarantee safe pregnancies and births, as well as access to quality sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, and information and education on SRH without suffering discrimination, coercion or any type of violence.2 However, there are women in the world whose rights are violated and/or denied in a multidimensional way due to gender inequality in society, which is exercised through policies, culture, or religion. This reality has a serious impact on women’s and girls’ health, social opportunities, access to education, autonomy and dignity.3

Although the European Union (EU) has stated that the health, education and socio-economic conditions of the Roma community are priorities for public policies, current statistics and studies show inequalities and injustices in different areas of society.4 The analytical approach of intersectionality is used to explain how individuals suffer oppression or, in contrast, hold privileges according to social categories. This is a tool that allows us to observe how different forms of oppression interweave and reinforce each other.5 The multiple realities experienced by the Roma population affect an important part of the population in an intersectional way, perpetuating social exclusion, poverty and a low level of education.6,7

The European community has addressed the violation of human rights and ethnic discrimination towards the Roma community. However, other relevant gender issues, such as violence against Roma women, racialised women and gender discrimination, are neglected. The reproductive and health rights of Roma women are closely linked to historical discrimination, such as forced sterilisation practices in countries such as Sweden, Finland or Norway between 1930 and 1970 or more recently in countries such as the Czech Republic or Slovakia. Roma women´s rights are violated in terms of accessing treatments related to reproductive health, such as contraception, or they are exposed to discriminatory behaviour and segregation in health institutions.8–10 For instance, in housing studies, data analysis is based on family units. Gender as a variable is not considered when accessing housing, particularly the needs of women in terms of basic resources, housing equipment, accessible infrastructure or public services.11

In Spain, Roma women’s living situation involves a complex and unequal reality that is reflected in their negative self-perceived health, and many barriers in accessing gynaecology services or reproductive health services, especially when compared to women of the general population.4 The social vulnerability in which Roma women and girls in Spain find themselves is a consequence of stereotypes, social exclusion, institutional racism and its effect on limiting access to education, employment, housing and health services.12,13 Roma women face many barriers and inequalities that prevent them from accessing reproductive justice.14 As part of reproductive rights, the reproductive justice approach involves all women being able to exercise economic, social and political power and resources to make healthy decisions about their bodies, sexuality and reproduction, and includes a holistic view of the individual and their health.15 From a reproductive justice perspective, Roma women’s right to preserve their culture and to actively participate in policies that affect them is not included and guaranteed in public policies in either the European or the national level.16,17

There is evidence that social determinants of health are correlated with women’s empowerment in reproductive decision-making.18,19 Anti-gypsyism is a social determinant that is based on discriminatory stereotypes and prejudices that impact Roma women and girls’ health. Furthermore, there is a lack or poor quality of SRH services adapted to the needs and cultural particularities of Roma women.10

Promoting reproductive justice for Roma women involves introducing an intersectional and participatory approach in order to improve governance and policies aimed at Roma girls and women.12,14,20 The intersectional approach enables us to understand and take action against several barriers and circumstances that affect Roma women, as well as understanding the complexity of their position within the maze of sociocultural relations. Adopting this holistic perspective in policies that guarantee and defend women’s choice to live and reproduce, with freedom, empowerment and autonomy as the main foundations of justice, will help to move forward in terms of eliminating inequalities.20,21

Both the Spanish regulatory and legal framework, as well as professionals in their different fields of expertise, have publicly taken a step forward to promote equality.22,23 However, the institutions where these professionals work have established dynamics of exclusion-inclusion throughout history, for instance, by means of social protection models. These models differ from sociopolitical approaches that are based on remedying or protecting social and personal situations of the Roma population.8,23 In those approaches, professionals working in health, education and other fields provide their perspective and offer resources to address systemic discrimination that affects vulnerable populations and social groups. They have specialised training to understand the needs of populations, as well as the ability to influence public policies and society.24,25 These professionals are the key agents in providing the population with fundamental health, education, social services and care services and thus can contribute to improving Roma girls’ and women’s access to reproductive justice26,27 The principles of reproductive justice are to guarantee that all women can exercise their reproductive freedom without discrimination or violence, as well as access the resources and services necessary to do so. Overcoming structural barriers requires intercultural competence that promotes reproductive justice, focusing on social equity and intersectionality as approaches to address the situation of Roma women.28,29 Furthermore, the intercultural perspective within the competences of professionals is a useful strategy that connects institutions with individuals and involves the acquisition of tools for effective and respectful communication, as well as raising awareness on the values or beliefs that can influence the professional-user relationship, in addition to the quality of services. The cognitive dimension is one of the foundations of intercultural competences.30

Evidence shows that biases and stereotypes among professionals can lead to and perpetuate stigmatisation, discrimination and anti-Roma behaviour towards women and girls, especially in situations of high social vulnerability, such as family planning, breast or cervical cancer prevention or early school dropout.9,31,32 Stereotypes are the deepest and most severe discriminatory expression in social structures towards the Roma population. They are created to project and legitimise desires and feelings that would otherwise be considered socially unacceptable.26 Implicit prejudices and unfair attitudes contribute to health inequalities based on origin: for instance, how professionals manage cultural differences in specific social spaces and how Roma representations are coordinated within everyday institutional contexts.25,33,34 There is little evidence focusing on providing professionals with adequate training, resources and tools to identify and avoid professional biases.35,36 Therefore, the main objective of this study is to understand the barriers to reproductive justice for Roma women and girls from the perspective of service providers as a facilitating source of information.

Methods

This study is part of the European project “RoMOMatteR: Empowering Roma Girls’ Mattering through Reproductive Justice”, carried out in four different locations: Spain (Andalusia and the Region of Valencia), Romania and Bulgaria. The RoMOMatteR project is aimed at transforming sociocultural resources in terms of creating vital and reproductive justice projects for Roma girls (https://institucional.us.es/romomatter/es/inicio/). This study focuses on fieldwork carried out in Alicante (Spain). The RoMOMatteR Alicante (Region of Valencia, Spain) consortium includes the Autonomous Federation of Roma Associations of Alicante (FAGA) and the University of Alicante. One of the goals set for the RoMOMatteR diagnosis phase was mapping Roma girls’ community assets linked to reproductive justice. Therefore, interviews were conducted with service providers from the local community. The discourses of local professionals were collected, which constituted a relevant source of information and resources to address reproductive justice. The value of this information lies in the fact that the available resources were identified at the local level so that the underage girls participating in the project could exercise their right to reproductive justice. The evaluation process, detection, systematisation and transfer of good practices is of relevance.

Nine semi-structured interviews were conducted with professionals from the public education system, as well as health and professional institutions related to social action and associations, with experience of and/or related to working with the Roma population in the city of Alicante, ensuring key criteria of qualitative research such as feasibility, relevance and sample convenience.37 The participants were intentionally selected with the help of key agents in the community using the snowball technique. FAGA advised us throughout the process. The selection of the interviewees was carried out to achieve the maximum possible diversity of the different areas of work (services or resources) of which the Roma girls and women of Alicante are users.

Our sample was sought (work contexts, people, processes) to best respond to the research questions in the project and that would make it possible to know, discover and interpret the services and providers that the young Roma women had in their environment. Contact with the interviewees was via telephone to arrange an in-person meeting. The meetings took place in the interviewees’ workplaces. The interviews took approximately 30–60 minutes.

The interview script contained 27 questions organised into six blocks: (a) intercultural competences aimed at the Roma population; (b) perspectives on gender discrimination towards Roma women and girls; (c) available resources to address gender discrimination in Roma women and girls; (d) knowledge and application of these resources; (e) information and use of resources by the target population and (f) networks of involved agents. The collected information provided relevant qualitative information about the public service and social service providers’ relationships with, perspectives on, and ways of working with Roma service-users in Alicante.

In-person interviews were conducted in April, May and June 2019. All interviews were recorded and transcribed in Spanish once informed consent was signed by the interviewees, then translated into English for reporting. In February 2019, the Ethics Committee of the University of Alicante provided authorisation for participant enrolment and data collection to proceed, following approval by the institutional Data Protection Delegation and in accordance with Spanish and European data protection regulations. Consistent with university policy, formal ethics committee approval was then processed on 6 August 2019 (Exp. UA 2019 07 18).

The qualitative analysis involved a discourse analysis following a mixed strategy, initially deductive, but also taking the inductive emergence of concepts derived from the data into consideration.37

The information from the interview records was used and codes were assigned to sentences or paragraphs identified with the meaning of the codes, which helped to select the fragments of text related to the experiences and work systems carried out by the service providers, who were pseudonymised. By means of a process of segmentation, coding and creation of semantic families of codes, a trajectory was followed from the data to the creation of theoretical categories. In order to approach the first analytical phases, the interview script was taken as a guiding element reference. The analytical framework was refined when ideas were repeated throughout the analysis by discussing with researchers with experience in qualitative research. The research team members verified all codes and contributed to code categorisations and groups.

Analytical triangulation was key in the development of the categories. The coherence of the ideas that supported each category was weighted based on the information expressed by the professionals and the considerations of the research team. In addition, three people (main author and co-authors) were involved in data collection: one belonging to the Roma community and two not. Therefore, an insider-outsider perspective is guaranteed. All people involved in data analysis have previous experience in research and intervention with the Roma people. Finally, a map of meanings was constructed articulating the different categories together with significant links in order to try to answer the research questions. The process followed in this research in terms of validity and reliability has been guided by three aspects: transparency, communication and coherence. In this sense, transparency refers to the fact that the process has been designed where other researchers have been able to verify the data (also part of a European study). Communication refers to the fact that the categories used have been meaningful to other researchers and consultation has been made in each step of the process to guarantee understanding and coherence. Finally, coherence refers to the fact that the categories used have been internally consistent and reflect both individual differences and genuine inconsistencies in the discourses. In accordance with the objective, the analysis has defined the barriers that Roma women face when accessing reproductive justice. In each of these barriers, discourses and practices that can prevent or facilitate access to reproductive justice have been observed in contrasting ways.

Stereotypes

Frustration and conformism

As part of the perspective that service providers offer us about the Roma population, there are simplified models and ideas with a widely shared tendency to attribute generalised characteristics based on popular beliefs and social categorisations, in other words, stereotypes. Normally these mechanisms provoke violent and deep-seated feelings that become displaced towards the Roma population. One of the mechanisms of stereotypes is frustration and conformism.

“Well, about not coming [to school], well we cannot do anything, we can tell them, we do tell them, but … Since I have been here and before, there are families that do not bring their kids to school. They have been fined, they have been to speak to the prosecutor, they have been to court and they do the same thing. […] We have to force them, encourage them, to take them and if they say they do not want to and you accept what they say, because it is convenient, well then in the end the kids do not go if you do not tell them to go. I do not think so, it is not because of the kids, it is mainly because of the family.” (I.7 education)

Roma women as “tools” to achieve changes within the community

The interviewees’ statements do not include aspects related to direct discrimination in their definitions of the Roma population or Roma women. They consider Roma women to be “tools” to achieve changes within the population The stereotypical approach of attributing the situation of women to their culture continues as a response to inequalities in reproductive rights. This would not be the case when respecting the rights and empowerment that the reproductive justice framework offers to achieve health and well-being. Women’s perspective is built from vulnerability and intersectionality with other groups, such as women with disabilities, although this is not a cultural aspect.

“(…) Well, women and, therefore, their community should be responsible for their destiny and empower themselves as individual subjects that must design their personal change processes and allow themselves to receive advice from professionals.” (I.1, health)

“Women, the great caregivers. Yet women today have a mediation role and they are motivated by their family’s health, their group, yes, yes. And women, well we do not want to change her, but she is more impressionable.” (I.3, health)

“All exclusions and all vulnerabilities come together, or many of vulnerabilities can occur. In fact, situations of exclusion can happen, but I could say that they are the same […] in number, relevance and importance that women can suffer, for example, with functional diversity. Regarding Roma women, well obviously adapted to their history, culture, values and their particular way of understanding the world.” (I.5, social action)

Limited abilities of intercultural and institutional professionals

In contrast, other discourses of a passive and contradictory nature emerge. Although a problem is recognised, the interviewees also assume that there is a lot that needs doing to remedy this fact. The interviewees recognise that women’s social situation is complex, as well as their multiple realities, and that it is difficult to intervene to move towards equity. Society owes better treatment to Roma women, and to other groups of women who have experienced multiple discrimination and who have not been recognised and cared for by the system. From this perspective, they acknowledge that Roma women are proactive from within their cultural reality in reducing the inequality gap and empowering women.

“That is the key with this perspective of intersectionality or simultaneity of oppressions, in other words, that the problems are not the same […] because it is not the same when we are talking about a Roma girl, an adult Roma woman, or an Arab woman, or an African woman […]. For me there is a debt, what I call a gender debt, with Roma women in the same way as with other groups of women, with black women, with racialised women in general.” (I.5 social action)

“They have some sort of glass ceiling because they are inside a cultural and social framework where there are rules set over their decisions.” (I.3, health)

“They come from families where the fathers do not have a paid job, the majority of them go out rummaging, which is what they [students] say. There are very few with an income or a job with a contract and everything. Sometimes I wonder how they manage to live. Sometimes I think about it and I wonder how they survive, because it is difficult. The women work in cleaning jobs or … but in general it is the men that go around in vans rummaging.” (I.7 education)

“The groups of women are young women, the majority of them are mothers who are greatly willing to improve their family and social situation. And as I see women now, for example, I see a big change in the groups of Roma women in general and in the Roma women I know who are leading a movement of empowerment of change with tremendous strength, and I am fascinated by it to be honest.” (I.2, health)

Lack of building the Roma identity from the women themselves

The interviewees recall the existence and acceptance of a stereotyped and generalised attitude towards Roma women in society and state that this perspective is wrong as it does not correspond to the reality. Roma women do not construct their own identity, but society generally assigns them contradictory traits that lead to their stigmatisation from both the Roma and non-Roma perspective. This construction of the Roma woman is contradictory to the principles proposed by the reproductive justice framework for the empowerment of women.

“Society is clear about what Roma girls are, they are slaves to their husbands and children; they have no authority, they only stay at home and take care of their children and do the washing up. That image we have of Roma women is not true, right? It is not true at all. It is true that the past has formed that ghost […] society’s expectation of the Roma woman is 100% wrong.” (I.9 education)

“When we meet them, well you know that […] there is no difference, children are children everywhere. Yet we have many prejudices, we have many fears, and that is ignorance. […] this is a normal school ‘what is the gypsy school like?'. No, we are a school like any other school and we do what any other school does because we have to normalise their academic life and their life at school, we cannot also be marginal.” (I.7 education)

“We are particularly talking about socioeconomically more disadvantaged groups, and I think that talking about the Roma community also means there is a variety of very different people, so that will also influence conditions that can affect their health.” (I.2, health)

Discrimination

Lack of interest of the Roma population in their own progress

One of the issues that the interviewees recognise is the discrimination that the Roma population has suffered historically, and which is still ongoing today. Based on this paradigm of discrimination, social spaces and environments are places where discrimination and inequality implicitly coexist.

“They are really used to suffering discrimination […] because they are not treated with respect, because when you go to municipal social services, there are often many derogatory comments […] it continues in the health field and it is supposed to be a very respectful environment where the code of ethics refers to treating everyone well and equally, if you look into other areas it is, well, it is devastating.” (I.4, social action)

“Lack of training and information. Also, the families themselves are not interested in it either (…) most families, except for young people, do not have that proactivist nature to improve these girls’ situation.” (I.1, health)

“On the one hand, there are cultural conditions that can make them have to take on certain roles before they should and then, on the other hand, I believe that the discrimination they suffer and inequalities derived from social conditions mean that they do not have complete freedom to decide about their future.” (I.2, health)

Acceptance of discrimination/self- discrimination

Furthermore, we can observe beliefs in the discourses that the Roma community has not internalised the need to make an effort, or make sacrifices, to obtain social recognition or to achieve goals in life, and demonstrate cultural assimilation.

“They are a group that discriminates against itself, because they do not comply, that is, I mean, discipline is necessary, it is necessary to comply with certain schedules, it is necessary to attend classes, it is necessary to study, it is necessary to learn and move forward. Therefore, I think that discrimination is often present within the group itself at the level of empowerment progress.” (I.3, health)

“At the end of the day, those of us here are not of their, their ethnicity, so the students do not consider it as their school. And they should consider the school as theirs and be part of it, and that become part of the school.” (I.7, education)

However, blaming Roma people for the situation in which they find themselves assumes that they accept discrimination and that this is self-inflicted or caused by the Roma population itself. The Roma community is perceived as a group of people who feel comfortable in a disadvantaged situation that prevents their empowerment. According to this perception, the active participation of women and girls is rare and does not demand reproductive rights, which would require Roma society to defend and guarantee reproductive justice.

“Apart from the discrimination that occurs in administration […] there is also self-discrimination due to the convenience of not moving from where I am […] What is the problem? That half of the women who are given an appointment do not come. That is why I say that there is self-discrimination due to a lack of interest on the part of the population.” (I.1, health)

“Then they go to high school and I know that they no longer stay there, they are looking forward to turning 16 so they can drop out […] not everyone has to study, but they do have to have a project to be able to have other types of jobs and not stay at home taking care of their children. Because that is the greatest dream they have at any given moment, having babies, beautiful babies, which is very good, but everything takes its time. I do not know what could be done, the truth is that I am often pessimistic.” (I.7, education)

The participants highlight there is no discrimination towards the Roma population and do not recognise that discrimination can be a response to be considered when offering services to the Roma population, both regarding the health or education fields. Therefore, these ideas and prejudiced reasoning are used to deny that there are unequal opportunities in different public spheres, such as health, education, housing and employment. There is a dichotomy between the way in which the Roma population is considered a “problem” for the social structure, while discrimination is not.

“I do not think there is discrimination from professionals. I am talking about […] accessing the health system. But I am talking about the health system, I do not think there is any problem whatsoever. Then, regarding the education system[…], I think there is absolutely no discrimination.” (I.3, health)

“Imagine if we personally, as women, have problems, well imagine a Roma woman, they will have many, many problems. Because you also know that the husband is the one in charge in the house, they are not in charge, they do what their husband says and they are used to it, it is very difficult. Empowering them can only happen with education and financial independence. There is no other option.” (I.7 education)

The participants’ discourses are highly familiar, and assume equal distribution of justice and rights. From the paradigm of equality, all existing problems seem to fade away, without perceiving the real need for intersectionality. The need for equity approaches to provide a fair and individual approach to each person according to their needs is not expressed.

“Well, the truth is that I have not done it because I have always thought that children are children everywhere and that a Roma boy or girl does not have to have a different capacity to learn and work. I have always done the same, because I do not think I have to do anything special because they are Roma, they are children.” (I.7 education)

“Here we […] are concerned about them having the same education as everyone else, we do not discriminate here regarding anyone […] everyone works on the same things, everyone according to their abilities, which is why we have therapeutic pedagogy teachers here.” (I.8, education)

Acknowledgment of social disadvantage of the Roma community and the social determinants

In contrast to the discourses based on cultural discrimination, other discourses emerge that interpret the Roma population’s situation of social disadvantage as based on social injustice, both from the community’s unique identity and the social determinants that condition it.

“We are especially talking about socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.” (I.2, health)

“When we work or have to go to neighbourhoods or areas where poverty really exists, it is not so much from a cultural perspective, but rather from an economic perspective or social situations and determinants that generate these gaps.” (I.4, social action)

“[The problem] goes beyond that, stability for a [Roma] family like any other Spanish citizen means having a decent job and decent housing, that is, dignity. The Roma population is asking society for dignity because they do not have a decent job because they do not have a decent home […] it is not about the Roma people not moving forward, the truth is that many opportunities are not given for the [Roma] community to move forward.” (I.9, education)

Intercultural competences

Lack of training for professionals on the issue

Intercultural competences, as part of the dimensions that involve and improve the work system, while enriching and representing diversity, are absent in the discourses. Regarding whether training is received to acquire cultural competences, the following is indicated:

“No […] There is none […] No. For professionals, no.” (I.1, health)

Other discourses discuss some actions that would be necessary to improve the relationship between professionals and the needs of the population.

“Well, I believe, training, training courses with the teachers being Roma professionals or professionals from associations who have higher cultural competences, which is important.” (I.2, health)

It is at this point where we find the greatest contradictions in the discourses as, on the one hand, intercultural competences are an element recognised favourably in the discourses in which the population is recognised as a priority:

“[From a public institution] there is interest, goals as well […] the Roma population is a vulnerable group that we are interested in because they are a priority, they are a priority in public health.” (I.3, health)

Yet, on the other hand, there are no measures to train professionals in this sense. It is left to individuals to learn more voluntarily in order to enhance their professional skills.

“There is no knowledge about the Roma community, nowhere […] the teachers who are teaching the children do not know the history of the Roma population […] it is still very far from being something normal or normalised to have that knowledge and understand it […] it is more about willingness than something more structural.” (I.4, social action)

“I think the professionals in the centres have to learn themselves.” (I.1, health)

“Strength, being consistent and willingness. Because the majority [of courses] are voluntary, out of working hours. Interest and love for what we have […] Limitations. Time, criticism from colleagues who do not understand why we do it, a lack of resources, I need to constantly look for projects, in other words, money from everyone so it does not come from the centre’s resources, otherwise my colleagues will say we are wasting resources.” (I.8, education)

The discourses recognised the importance of acquiring knowledge and valuing cultural dimensions in a system in which, with some exceptions, there is no Roma representation. Furthermore, prejudices and power privileges continue to build social relationships.

“We have many prejudices, we have many fears, and that is ignorance. Well, if there were a subject on interculturality and diversity at university, a mandatory one, then that would prepare us.” (I.7, education)

“They do not have me, I mean, I am not within their culture, I cannot understand it, I cannot have the same sensitivity, although I do have it, but I am not Roma. I mean, we all have to overcome it.” (I.8, education)

Being a Roma as a social detriment

From an institutional and hegemonic perspective, being Roma is a detriment, but it is also more so for the Roma themselves, where their position of social disadvantage and accepted helplessness is recognised.

“Will there be opportunities? No, because I am Roma. Well, that discourages me […] there are people who do not set the goal because they are Roma they know that society will reject them, so they do not have that spirit to say I'm going to give it a go.” (I.9, education)

“But it is also true that this willingness [of attending the workshops] is relative because, of course, they [the Roma women] know that if they come they have more possibilities for other issues within the social centres, for grants, for I don't know, for the children. Of course, they see [the professionals] are more responsible.” (I.6, social action)

Structural weakness of Roma community associations.

Given this contradiction of narratives, the Roma association fabric can be more effective in providing intercultural competences. However, there is a structural weakness and it does not have adequate institutional support. The responsibility for reducing the inequality gap and improving the situation of social disadvantage is understood to belong to the population and not to the system. On the other hand, reliance on community associations should be a disappearing trend in favour of professionals trained in intercultural competences.

“The associations do not have the impact, nor the professional support, that is, at the level of credibility that I know they have to have. Yes, they can work or they have value in what they are doing. Associations have many weaknesses […] everything is up in the air, resources are temporary, from one year to the next you do not know what is going to happen, you are at the mercy of whether the projects receive subsidies or not.” (I.4 social action)

“Well, the truth is that no […] with the Roma population now when we are starting, but above all because the Roma associations are also active and they put these lines of work in place, which I think is what should be done.” (I.2, health)

“It is slower than what is really needed. The informal information that the population has is more important than the formal information that us as health workers can give. […]on the one hand, because we do not know how to get there, and on the other, because it is also difficult for the population. At that moment professionals are mothers-fathers, we tell people what they should do, and that is the mistake. No one likes to be told what to do. Give them information, which is what we were talking about being a mother at 20 or being a mother at 14, you decide, but you have to have the appropriate information, not biased or cultural, that I have believed through my beliefs and my myths.” (I.1, health)

“Associations are now necessary but work should be focused on eliminating that need.” (I.8, education)

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that the relationship between professionals and the Roma population is constrained by stereotypes and prejudices. These prevent professionals from focusing on inequalities and equity and from being able to establish policies that guarantee and defend women’s choice to live and reproduce based on freedom, empowerment and autonomy, which are the foundations of reproductive justice. While the discourses observed in this study show there is agreement regarding the reproductive rights of Roma women and girls, this formula is not based on the principles of reproductive justice or women’s empowerment. Although professionals value the need for training in interculturalism, the public administration does not guarantee this training in a comprehensive and systemic way.

Furthermore, discourses have been found in this study that coincide with literature. Members of the Roma community in Spain are considered to be “comfortable” with a disadvantaged situation, therefore inequality seems to be a “cultural norm”. If they are “not very smart or compliant”,30,38,39 it is not worth them studying; this is what the so-called self-fulfilling prophecy or Pygmalion effect is based on.40 This analysis shows how professionals have a negative preconceived perspective about the future and empowerment of Roma girls and women in their narratives as they make the stereotypical culture the pillar of their intervention, not the Roma person. Some of the narratives propose a social imaginary of what Roma culture is, which responds to the difficulty these professionals described of a lack of participation and commitment of the Roma population to the activities and proposals by institutions and professionals.41

The reproductive justice approach has been described in the reviewed literature. It implies not only access to quality SRH services, but also respect for the autonomy, diversity and dignity of racialised women in the world, as well as recognising their human rights and their active participation in the decisions that affect them. Roma women could be inspired by the example of Sisters Song42 to create their own reproductive justice movement that reflects their specific needs, demands and experiences – for instance, regarding the right to freely decide one’s sexuality and reproduction based on cultural identity, without external impositions or stereotypes. Furthermore, the violence and injustices they suffer due to their status as Roma women, among others, would be exposed, together with a demand for public policies that protect and support them.

In order to promote sexual and reproductive education from an intercultural, feminist and racism-free perspective, the autonomy and diversity of Roma women must be respected.42,43

The results of this research have also been evidenced in other studies related to the Roma population in which the Roma individual is represented from Otherness, as an excluded individual with many problems. This confers on professionals the role of someone who, from a superior position, knows what needs to be done to help the person improve their situation of social exclusion and places the excluded person as the one who must allow themselves to be advised and who must obey.25,26,33

Roma culture provokes fear and ignorance that are expressed in stereotyped discourses and perspectives, which perpetuate the relations between institutions and the Roma population who, although Spanish-born, encounter obstacles to access health or education services. These findings in turn agree with the reviewed literature.4,24,44 Discrimination is structured from the social organisation of power and the way in which the story of the history of Roma culture has been constructed by institutions.45 Relationships between professionals and users are centred on negative perspectives and cultural differences, and although both sides are susceptible to positive change, they are resistant to changing relational structures.33,34 Discrimination increases the Roma population’s fear and distrust towards the institutions that should ensure their well-being and it also hinders their access to and use of quality services.10,29 The limited knowledge about the diversity and historical memory of the Roma culture, as well as the methods and theories used (or not used), are an expression of systemic discrimination that affects inclusion policies and equity. This discriminatory approach includes education, health, and the implementation of anti-Roma policies and practices.6,8,11 Intercultural competences are scarce and fragmented over time, poorly valued in professional practice and are usually associated with volunteering or association networks. This intercultural perspective would help not only to focus on inequalities that originate from Roma girls’ and women’s culture, but also to design policies that guarantee and defend women’s choice to live and reproduce based on freedom, empowerment and autonomy, which are the foundations of reproductive justice.15,30

This study has some limitations, such as the difficulties in involving members of the consortium, who carry out work in different social settings with the Roma population, as well as the changing social conditions and needs during and after the COVID-19 period. The delivery of activities and project schedule have emphasised the aforementioned limitations. These limitations do not diminish the relevance of the results within the specific context studied, although caution is required when applying these conclusions outside the direct study group. The preponderance of women in the sample was unintentional and the perspectives of male professionals may have been under-represented. In addition, the interview script was not devised with the specific objective of this analysis but as already mentioned, had as its objective to collect data on the resources available in the area aimed at the Roma population. Therefore, there will be elements that have not been included in our analysis.

Conclusion

The relationship models between professionals and users of the Roma population project stereotypes that involve obstacles to accessing reproductive justice. Rigid structures that are resistant to Roma cultural diversity and heterogeneity continue to be perpetuated, preventing the gaps of social inequalities from being reduced. The intention to include the situation of the Roma population in an inclusive narrative exists, although the professionals perceive it as a difficult objective to achieve. Reproductive justice as a framework is absent within the intervention of professionals and service providers, who can be one of the access barriers to reproductive and sexual health for Roma women if their specific needs, diversity and culture are not considered, overcoming prejudice and historic stereotypes. These professionals, with responsibilities that affect the health of Roma women in their organisations, have the ability to develop good practices and effective interventions to contribute to moving towards reproductive justice, in favour not only of Roma women but also of other racialised women.
