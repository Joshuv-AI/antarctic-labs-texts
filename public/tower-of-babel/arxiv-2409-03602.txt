A combination theorem for hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups, and application to geometric subgroups of mapping class groups
Giorgio Mangioni

Abstract
. We provide sufficient conditions for two subgroups of a hierarchically hyperbolic group to generate an amalgamated free product over their intersection. The result applies in particular to certain geometric subgroups of mapping class groups of finite-type surfaces, that is, those subgroups coming from the embeddings of closed subsurfaces. In the second half of the paper, we study under which hypotheses our amalgamation procedure preserves several notions of convexity, such as hierarchical quasiconvexity (as introduced by Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto) and strong quasiconvexity (every quasigeodesic with endpoints on the subset lies in a uniform neighbourhood). This answers a question of Russell, Spriano, and Tran.



I will achieve in my life - Heaven grant that it be not long - some gigantic amalgamation between the two discrepancies so hideously apparent to me.

Virginia Woolf

Given a group
G
and two subgroups
A,B\leq G
, it is natural to ask what the subgroup
\langle A,B\rangle_{G}
generated by
A
and
B
looks like, and in particular if it is isomorphic to the
amalgamated free product

A*_{C}B.
In this paper, we prove an amalgamation theorem for when
A
and
B
are subgroups of any group
G
acting “nicely” on a
hierarchical space
(see Definition
1.1
below). This class includes all relatively hyperbolic groups, and all
hierarchically hyperbolic groups
(HHG) in the sense of Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto
[
5
]
(such as mapping class groups of finite-type surfaces, many
3
-manifold groups, many Coxeter and Artin groups, compact special groups…). We give here a special case of the result, postponing the full statement to Section
2
:

Theorem A
.

Let
(G,\mathfrak{S})
be a hierarchically hyperbolic group, let
A,B\leq G
be subgroups and let
C=A\cap B
. Suppose that there exists a constant
M\geq 0
and a domain
Y_{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
for every
a\in(A\cup B)-C
, such that the following hold:

(I)

\max\left\{\mathrm{diam}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0}),\mathrm{diam}_{Y_{a}}(aCx_{0})\right\}\leq M/10
;

(II)

\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0},aCx_{0})\geq M
;

(III)

If
a\in A-C
and
b\in B-C
,
Y_{a}\pitchfork aY_{b}
;

(IV)

In the same setting,
\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0},bCx_{0})\leq M/10
.

There exists
M_{0}\geq 0
, depending only on
(G,\mathfrak{S})
, such that, if
M\geq M_{0}
, then

\langle A,B\rangle_{G}\cong A*_{C}B.

Amalgamation of geometric subgroups

In understanding the above theorem, one should have in mind the following example. Let
G=\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
be the (extended) mapping class groups of a finite-type surface
S
. Let
U
,
V
be two closed, connected, incompressible subsurfaces, such that no connected component of
S-U
(resp.
S-V
) is an annulus. These conditions ensure that
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
naturally embed in
G
, and we denote the image of such embeddings as
geometric embedded
subgroups
†
†

The terminology “geometric” is due to Paris and Rolfsen
[
21
]
and denotes the image of the homomorphism
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(W)\to\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
for any closed subsurface
W
, without further assumptions. Our notation thus denotes the cases when a geometric subgroup is also embedded. If there is a standard name for such subgroups we would be grateful for a reference.
. Let
\Gamma
be the collection of all curves which belong to the boundary of both
U
and
V
; this multicurve might be empty even if
\partial U
and
\partial V
intersect. Suppose
\partial U-\Gamma
and
\partial V-\Gamma
are “sufficiently entangled” in the complement of
\Gamma
, meaning that they are far enough in the curve graph
\mathcal{C}(S-\Gamma)
. This condition ensures that the intersection of
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
is the Dehn Twist flat
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
supported on
\Gamma
. Using the separability of the latter in both
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
, one can find finite-index subgroups
A\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
B\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
, such that every element
a\in A-\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
acts with large translation length on some subsurface
Y_{a}\sqsubseteq U
(Assumption (II)). Furthermore, the entanglement of the boundaries of
U
and
V
ensures that, whenever
a\in A-\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
and
b\in B-\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, the subsurfaces
Y_{a}
and
Y_{b}
must overlap (Assumption (III)). The above example is analysed more thoroughly in Subsection
3
, where we prove the following, slightly more general result:

Theorem B
.

Let
S
be a connected finite-type surface, and let
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
be two geometric embedded subgroups, where each of
U
and
V
is either connected or a multicurve. Let
\Gamma
be the collection of all curves which belong to the boundary of both
U
and
V
(where, with a little abuse of notation, the boundary of a multicurve denotes its support). Suppose that
\partial U-\Gamma
,
\partial V-\Gamma
are both non-empty, and that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{C}(S-\Gamma)}(\partial U-\Gamma,\partial V-\Gamma)\geq 4.

Then there exist finite index subgroups
A\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
,
B\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
, intersecting along the Dehn twist flat
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, such that

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)}\cong A*_{\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}}B.

Comparison with the literature

Our Theorem
B
is similar in spirit to Leininger-Reid’s result for Veech subgroups along a common multitwist
[
16
]
. The main difference is that, while the subgroups there are supported on the whole surface
S
(and indeed every element which does not lie in the intersection is pseudo-Anosov), our result deals with reducible subgroups, and the large translations of the elements are witnessed by pairs of transverse subsurfaces.

Our theorem also covers the case when
U
and
V
are multicurves. In this setting, it should be compared to Loa’s result about free product of multitwists supported on “far enough” multicurves
[
17
]
. While our procedure requires to pass to finite-index subgroups with large translation in the annular domains, theirs applies to the whole Dehn Twist flats supported on the multicurves, but only when the intersection
U\cap V
is empty. We also stress that Loa’s result gives more information about the amalgam, including the fact that it is undistorted in
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
and
parabolically geometrically finite
, in the sense of
[
9
]
.

However, we point out that our result about multicurves is just a very special case of a theorem of Koberda, which can be used to produce more general, undistorted RAAGs in
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
(see
[
15
]
and its quantitative version by Runnels
[
22
]
).

Preserving convexity

If
A
and
B
satisfy some property
P
, it is natural to ask when the subgroup they generate still enjoys
P
. The feature we focus on in the second half of the paper is
hierarchical quasiconvexity
(HQC for short), which is the analogue of quasiconvexity in the world of hierarchically hyperbolic groups. A hierarchically quasiconvex subgroup of a HHG enjoys numerous geometric properties regarding, for example, a coarse median structure and a quadratic isoperimetric function
[
6
,
7
]
, its asymptotic dimension
[
2
]
, and the arrangement patterns of top-dimensional quasiflats
[
4
]
. Therefore, it is relevant to understand when the subgroup generated by two hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups is again hierarchically quasiconvex. We provide sufficient conditions for our amalgamation procedure to preserve hierarchical quasiconvexity (the exact statement is Theorem
4.20
):

Theorem C
.

Let
(G,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHG, let
A,B\leq G
be two hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups, and let
C=A\cap B
. Suppose that:

•

A
and
B
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
A
, for some
M\geq 0
;

•

A
and
B

fill all squares
(Definition
4.11
);

•

A
and
B

have no drift in the orthogonals
(Definition
4.19
).

There exists a constant
M_{0}\geq 0
, depending on
(G,\mathfrak{S})
and the above data, such that, if
M\geq M_{0}
, then
\langle A,B\rangle_{G}\cong A*_{C}B
is hierarchically quasiconvex in
(G,\mathfrak{S})
.

Roughly, two HQC subgroups
A
and
B
fill all squares if, whenever two domains
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
are orthogonal, if
A
has large projection to
\mathcal{C}U
and
B
to
\mathcal{C}V
then the intersection
A\cap B
also has large projection to one of the domains. This property is equivalent to the fact that
A\cup B
is hierarchically quasiconvex (see Lemma
4.13
).

Moreover,
A
and
B
have no drift in the orthogonals if it does
not
happen that both
A
and
B
have bounded projections to some domain
U
, which is orthogonal to the domains
Y_{a}
and
Y_{b}
used to detect the amalgamation. In Subsection
4.4
we provide a counterexample where the lack of this property falsifies the conclusion of Theorem
C
.

In our third and last Theorem, we study when our amalgamation procedure preserves
strong quasiconvexity
. Recall that, given a metric space
X
, a subspace
Y\subseteq X
is
strongly quasiconvex
if every quasigeodesic
\gamma
with endpoints on
Y
lies in a neighbourhood of
Y
, whose radius only depends on
X
and on the quasigeodesic constants of
\gamma
. Such a subset is also called
Morse
, as strongly quasiconvex geodesics are exactly the Morse directions. Most of the properties of quasiconvex subsets of hyperbolic spaces hold for strongly quasiconvex subsets of general metric spaces
[
25
]
. Furthermore, as explored in
[
24
]
, a subspace of a HHG is strongly quasiconvex if and only if it is hierarchically quasiconvex and enjoys a further assumption, the
orthogonal projection dichotomy
. In Theorem
5.4
we prove that the latter property is preserved by our amalgamation procedure. This way, we provide a possible answer to
[
24
, Question 1]
:

Theorem D
.

Let
(G,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHG, let
A,B\leq G
be two strongly quasiconvex subgroups of
G
, and let
C=A\cap B
. Suppose that
A
and
B
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
, for some constant
M\geq 0
.

There exists a constant
M_{0}\geq 0
, depending on
(G,\mathfrak{S})
and the strong quasiconvexity gauge of
A
and
B
, such that if
M\geq M_{0}
then
\langle A,B\rangle_{G}\cong A*_{C}B
is strongly quasiconvex in
G
.

In
[
23
, Theorem G]
, Russell, Spriano, and Tran work in the context of
local-to-global
groups, which include hierarchically hyperbolic groups by combining
[
23
, Theorem 4.18]
and results from
[
1
]
, and prove a combination theorem for
stable
subgroups, i.e. strongly quasiconvex subgroups which are also
hyperbolic
. Our Theorem
D
can therefore be seen as a generalisation of their result in the setting of hierarchically hyperbolic spaces, though the requirements of Theorem
2.1
are stronger and specific to the hierarchical framework.

Towards relative hierarchical quasiconvexity

In a relatively hyperbolic group, the “right” notion of convexity of a subgroup is
relative quasiconvexity
. Indeed, a relatively quasiconvex subgroup inherits a relative hyperbolic structure, and the intersection of two relatively quasiconvex subgroups is again relatively quasiconvex. As both HHG and relatively hyperbolic groups fall into the category of “relative HHG”, in the sense of Definition
1.12
below, one could look for a notion that unifies relative quasiconvexity and hierarchical quasiconvexity.

Problem E
.

Let
(G,\mathfrak{S})
be a relative HHG, and let
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
be a collection of domains which is closed under nesting and contains every
U\in\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathcal{C}U
is not hyperbolic. Formulate a notion of
hierarchical quasiconvexity relative to
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
for subgroups of
G
, such that:

•

If
(G,\mathfrak{S})
is a HHG and
\mathfrak{S}_{0}=\emptyset
, one recovers hierarchical quasiconvexity;

•

If
(G,\mathbb{P})
is relatively hyperbolic and
\mathfrak{S}_{0}=\mathbb{P}
, one recovers relative quasiconvexity;

•

Under suitable conditions on
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
, if a subgroup is HQC relative to
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
then it admits a structure of a
relative hierarchically hyperbolic space
(see Definition
1.4
);

•

The intersection of two HQC subgroups relative to
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
is HQC relative to
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
.

We believe a possible approach would be to generalise the notion of
transition points
on a geodesic in a relatively hyperbolic group, and then try to emulate the characterisation of relative quasiconvexity from
[
14
, Corollary 8.16]
.

After one finds the right definition, one could attempt to extend Theorem
C
to relative HQC subgroups, possibly generalising known combination theorems for relatively quasiconvex subgroups (see, among others,
[
12
,
18
,
26
]
).

Outline

Section
1
provides the background on hierarchical hyperbolicity. In Section
2
we prove the main amalgamation result, Theorem
A
, which we then apply to certain geometric subgroups of mapping class groups in Section
3
.

In Section
4
we strengthen our result to preserve hierarchical quasiconvexity. The proof of Theorem
C
is in three steps. In Subsection
4.1
, we first recall that being HQC coincides with being almost closed under certain quasigeodesics, called
hierarchy paths
. Next, in Subsection
4.2
we determine under which conditions the union
A\cup B
of two HQC subgroups is again HQC (see Lemma
4.13
). Finally, given a hierarchy path connecting two points of
A\cup B
, in Subsection
4.3
we show that it can be decomposed as a union of hierarchy paths with endpoints on cosets of
A\cup B
, and the conclusion follows from the hierarchical quasiconvexity of the latter cosets.

Finally, Section
5
is devoted to the proof of the combination result for strongly quasiconvex subgroups, Theorem
D
.

1.
A crash course in hierarchical hyperbolicity

We start by recalling some notions from the world of
hierarchically hyperbolic spaces and groups
, first introduced by Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto in
[
3
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Hierarchical space)
.

The quasigeodesic space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
is a
hierarchical space
if there exists
E\geq 0
, called the
hierarchical constant
, an index set
\mathfrak{S}
, whose elements will be referred to as
domains
, and a set
\{\mathcal{C}U\mid U\in\mathfrak{S}\}
of geodesic metric spaces
(\mathcal{C}U,\mathrm{d}_{U})
, called
coordinate spaces
, such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

(Projections.)

There is a set
\{\pi_{U}:X\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{C}U}\mid U\in\mathfrak{S}\}
of
projections
mapping points in
X
to sets of diameter bounded by
E
in the various
\mathcal{C}U\in\mathfrak{S}
. Moreover, for all
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, the coarse map
\pi_{U}
is
(E,E)
–coarsely Lipschitz and
\pi_{U}(X)
is
E
–quasiconvex in
\mathcal{C}U
.

(2)

(Nesting.)

\mathfrak{S}
is equipped with a partial order
\sqsubseteq
, and either
\mathfrak{S}=\emptyset
or
\mathfrak{S}
contains a unique
\sqsubseteq
–maximal element, denoted by
S
. When
V\sqsubseteq U
, we say
V
is
nested
in
U
. For each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, we denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{U}
the set of
V\in\mathfrak{S}
such that
V\sqsubseteq U
. Moreover, for all
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V\sqsubsetneq U
there is a specified subset
\rho^{V}_{U}\subset\mathcal{C}U
with
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{C}U}(\rho^{V}_{U})\leq E
. There is also a
projection

\rho^{U}_{V}:\mathcal{C}U\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{C}V}
. (The similarity in notation is justified by viewing
\rho^{V}_{U}
as a coarsely constant map
\mathcal{C}V\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{C}U}
.)

(3)

(Orthogonality.)

\mathfrak{S}
has a symmetric and anti-reflexive relation called
orthogonality
: we write
U\bot V
when
U,V
are orthogonal. Also, whenever
V\sqsubseteq U
and
U\bot W
, we require that
V\bot W
. We require that for each
T\in\mathfrak{S}
and each
U\in\mathfrak{S}_{T}
such that
\{V\in\mathfrak{S}_{T}\mid V\bot U\}\neq\emptyset
, there exists a domain
W\in\mathfrak{S}_{T}-\{T\}
, which we call a
container
for
U
inside
T
, such that whenever
V\bot U
and
V\sqsubseteq T
, we have
V\sqsubseteq W
. Finally, if
U\bot V
, then
U,V
are not
\sqsubseteq
–comparable.

(4)

(Transversality and consistency.)

If
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
are not orthogonal and neither is nested in the other, then we say
U,V
are
transverse
, denoted
U\pitchfork V
. In this case, there are sets
\rho^{V}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{C}U
and
\rho^{U}_{V}\subseteq\mathcal{C}V
, each of diameter at most
E
and satisfying the
Behrstock inequality
:

\min\left\{\mathrm{d}_{U}(\pi_{U}(z),\rho^{V}_{U}),\mathrm{d}_{V}(\pi_{V}(z),\rho^{U}_{V})\right\}\leq E

for all
z\in X
.

For
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
satisfying
V\sqsubseteq U
and for all
z\in X
, we have:

\min\left\{\mathrm{d}_{U}(\pi_{U}(z),\rho^{V}_{U}),\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{C}V}(\pi_{V}(z)\cup\rho^{U}_{V}(\pi_{U}(z)))\right\}\leq E.

The preceding two inequalities are the
consistency inequalities
for points in
X
.

Finally, if
U\sqsubseteq V
, then
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\rho^{U}_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})\leq E
whenever
W\in\mathfrak{S}
satisfies either
V\sqsubsetneq W
or
V\pitchfork W
and
W\not\bot U
.

(5)

(Finite complexity.)

There exists
n\geq 0
, the
complexity
of
X
(with respect to
\mathfrak{S}
), so that any set of pairwise–
\sqsubseteq
–comparable elements has cardinality at most
n
.

(6)

(Large links.)

Let
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, let
z,z^{\prime}\in X
and let
N=\mathrm{d}_{{}_{U}}(\pi_{U}(z),\pi_{U}(z^{\prime}))
. Then there exists
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,\lfloor N\rfloor}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{U}-\{U\}
such that, for any domain
T\in\mathfrak{S}_{U}-\{U\}
, either
T\in\mathfrak{S}_{T_{i}}
for some
i
, or
\mathrm{d}_{T}(\pi_{T}(z),\pi_{T}(z^{\prime}))<E
. Also,
\mathrm{d}_{U}(\pi_{U}(z),\rho^{T_{i}}_{U})\leq N
for each
i
.

(7)

(Bounded geodesic image.)

For all
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, all
V\in\mathfrak{S}_{U}-\{U\}
, and all geodesics
\gamma
of
\mathcal{C}U
, either
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{C}V}(\rho^{U}_{V}(\gamma))\leq E
or
\gamma\cap N_{E}(\rho^{V}_{U})\neq\emptyset
.

(8)

(Partial realisation.)

Let
\{V_{j}\}
be a family of pairwise orthogonal elements of
\mathfrak{S}
, and let
p_{j}\in\pi_{V_{j}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{C}V_{j}
. Then there exists
z\in X
, which we call a
partial realisation point
for the family, so that:

•

\mathrm{d}_{V_{j}}(z,p_{j})\leq E
for all
j
,

•

for each
j
and
each
V\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V_{j}\sqsubseteq V
, we have

\mathrm{d}_{V}(z,\rho^{V_{j}}_{V})\leq E
, and

•

for each
j
and
each
V\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V_{j}\pitchfork V
, we have
\mathrm{d}_{V}(z,\rho^{V_{j}}_{V})\leq E
.

(9)

(Uniqueness.)
For each
\kappa\geq 0
, there exists

\theta_{u}=\theta_{u}(\kappa)
such that if
x,y\in X
and

\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,y)\geq\theta_{u}
, then there exists
V\in\mathfrak{S}
such
that
\mathrm{d}_{V}(x,y)\geq\kappa
.

We often refer to
\mathfrak{S}
, together with the nesting and orthogonality relations, and the projections as a
hierarchical structure
for the space
X
.

Remark 1.2
.

Notice that, if
E
is a hierarchical constant for
(X,\mathfrak{S})
, then so is any
E^{\prime}\geq E
. Hence, throughout the paper we will always implicitly assume that every hierarchical constant is strictly positive.

Remark 1.3
.

Where it will not cause confusion, given
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, we will often suppress the projection map
\pi_{U}
when writing distances in
\mathcal{C}U
, i.e., given
A,B\subseteq X
and
P\subseteq\mathcal{C}U
we shall write
d_{U}(A,B)
for
d_{\mathcal{C}U}(\pi_{U}(A),\pi_{U}(B))
and
d_{U}(A,P)
for
d_{\mathcal{C}U}(\pi_{U}(A),P)
. Furthermore, when
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}
are such that
V_{i}\sqsubsetneq U
or
V_{i}\pitchfork U
for
i=1,2
, we will write
d_{U}(V_{1},V_{2})
for
d_{\mathcal{C}U}(\rho^{V_{1}}_{U},\rho^{V_{2}}_{U})
.

Definition 1.4

((Relative) HHS)
.

A hierarchical space is

•

a
hierarchically hyperbolic space
(HHS) if every coordinate space is
E
-hyperbolic;

•

a
relatively hierarchically hyperbolic space
(relative HHS) if every coordinate space is either
E
-hyperbolic or
\sqsubseteq
-minimal. Notice that this class includes relatively hyperbolic spaces and groups, as explained in
[
5
, Remark 1.15]
.

All properties of hierarchically hyperbolic spaces whose proofs do not involve the hyperbolicity of coordinate spaces also hold for hierarchical spaces in general. In particular, the following is proved as in
[
10
, Lemma 1.5]
, which only uses the partial realisation axiom:

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a hierarchical space. For every
U,V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
such that
U\bot V
and both
\rho^{U}_{W}
,
\rho^{V}_{W}
are defined, then
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\rho^{U}_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})\leq 2E
.

Combining Lemma
1.5
and Axiom
4
we get:

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
U,V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
. Suppose that
\mathrm{d}_{V}(U,W)
is well-defined and strictly greater than
2E
. Then
U\pitchfork W
.

Moreover, possibly after enlarging the hierarchical constant
E
, we get the following variant of the bounded geodesic image axiom, which is proved by combining the consistency inequalities with the original bounded geodesic image axiom, as in
[
5
, Proposition 1.13]
:

Lemma 1.7

(BGI variant)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a hierarchical space. Let
x,y\in X
and let
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
be such that
U\sqsubsetneq V
. Then either
\mathrm{d}_{U}(x,y)\leq E
or every geodesic
[\pi_{V}(x),\pi_{V}(y)]\subseteq\mathcal{C}V
must pass
E
-close to
\rho^{U}_{V}
.

Definition 1.8

(Consistent tuple)
.

Let
R\geq 0
and let
(b_{U})_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}\in\prod_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}2^{\mathcal{C}U}
be a tuple such that for each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, the
U
–coordinate
b_{U}
has diameter
\leq R
. Then
(b_{U})_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}
is
R
–consistent
if for all
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
, we have

\min\{\mathrm{d}_{V}(b_{V},\rho^{W}_{V}),\mathrm{d}_{W}(b_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})\}\leq R

whenever
V\pitchfork W
and

\min\{\mathrm{d}_{W}(b_{W},\rho^{V}_{W}),\mathrm{diam}_{V}(b_{V}\cup\rho^{W}_{V}(b_{W}))\}\leq R

whenever
V\sqsubsetneq W
.

Later we will need the following, which is
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
(notice that it holds for hierarchically
hyperbolic
spaces):

Theorem 1.9

(Realisation)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a hierarchically hyperbolic space. Then for each
R\geq 1
, there exists
\theta=\theta(R)
so that, for any
R
–consistent tuple
(b_{U})_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}
, there exists

.

Observe that the uniqueness axiom (Definition (

.

Definition 1.11
.

A finitely generated group

on a hierarchical space
({X},\mathfrak{S})
by automorphisms if there is a group homomorphism

Definition 1.12

((Relative) HHG)

If a group

2.
Detecting an amalgamated free product in a HHG

We are now ready to prove Theorem
A
from the introduction, in the following extended form.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a hierarchical space
(X,\mathfrak{S})
, and fix a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
. Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\leq G
be subgroups, and let
C
be a subset of the intersection
\bigcap_{l=1}^{n}A_{l}
. Suppose that there exists a constant
M\geq 100E
, where
E
is a hierarchical constant for
X
, and a domain
Y_{a}\in\mathfrak{S}
for every
a\in(\bigcup_{i}A_{i})-C
, such that the following hold:

(I)

\max\left\{\mathrm{diam}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0}),\mathrm{diam}_{Y_{a}}(aCx_{0})\right\}\leq M/10
;

(II)

\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0},aCx_{0})\geq M
;

(III)

If
a\in A_{i}-C
and
b\in A_{j}-C
where
i\neq j
,
Y_{a}\pitchfork aY_{b}
;

(IV)

In the same setting,
\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0},bCx_{0})\leq M/10
.

Then
C
coincides with the pairwise intersections
A_{i}\cap A_{j}
for every
i\neq j
, and in particular
C=\bigcap_{l=1}^{n}A_{l}
. Moreover, the natural map

\Asterisk_{C}A_{\bullet}:=A_{1}*_{C}\ldots*_{C}A_{n}\to\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\rangle_{G}

is an isomorphism.

Remark 2.2

(Outline)
.

Given a word
w=g_{1}\ldots g_{k}c\in\Asterisk_{C}A_{\bullet}-\{1\}
, we shall show that one can find a collection of pairwise transverse domains
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}
, one for every subword of
w
of the form
g_{1}\ldots g_{j}
, such that the projection of
g_{1}\ldots g_{j}Cx_{0}
on
W_{i}
only changes when one passes from
g_{1}\ldots g_{i-1}Cx_{0}
to
g_{1}\ldots g_{i}Cx_{0}
. In a way, the domain
W_{i}
detects “the
i
-th step of
w
”, and as a consequence of assumption
(IV)
all subwords
g_{1}\ldots g_{j}
with
j\geq i+1
cannot undo the translation on
W_{i}
. In particular, one gets that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(Cx_{0},wCx_{0})
is greater than some positive constant, depending on
M
and
E
, and therefore
w
is non-trivial in
G
as it acts non-trivially on
(X,\mathfrak{S})
.

We now move to the proof of Theorem
2.1
. First notice that, whenever
i\neq j
,
C=A_{i}\cap A_{j}
. Indeed
C\subseteq A_{i}\cap A_{j}
, and if
a\in(A_{i}\cap A_{j})-C
then by assumption
(IV)
applied to
a=b
we would get that
\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(Cx_{0},aCx_{0})\leq M/10
, in contrast with assumption
(II)
.

Now, we want to prove that the natural epimorphism
\Asterisk_{C}A_{\bullet}\to\langle A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\rangle_{G}
is injective. In other words, given any non-trivial word
w=g_{1}\ldots g_{k}c\in\Asterisk_{C}A_{\bullet}-\{1\}
, where
c\in C
and every two consecutive
g_{i}
and
g_{i+1}
belong to different factors, we have to show that
w\neq_{G}1
. This is clearly true if
k\leq 2
, so we focus on the case
k\geq 3
.

Notation 2.3
.

For every
i=1,\ldots,k
let
Y_{i}=Y_{g_{i}}
, and set

C_{i}=g_{1}\ldots g_{i-1}Cx_{0},\quad W_{i}=g_{1}\ldots g_{i-1}Y_{i},

so that

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i},C_{i+1})=\mathrm{d}_{Y_{i}}(Cx_{0},g_{i}Cx_{0})\geq M.

We break the rest of the proof of Theorem
2.1
into a series of Claims.

Claim 2.4
.

W_{i}\pitchfork W_{i+1}
for every
i=1,\ldots,k-1
.

Proof of Claim
2.4
.

Notice that
Y_{i}
and
g_{i}Y_{i+1}
are transverse by assumption
(III)
, since
g_{i}
and
g_{i+1}
lie in different factors of the amalgamation. Therefore, the domains
W_{i}=(g_{1}\ldots g_{i-1})Y_{i}
and
W_{i+1}=(g_{1}\ldots g_{i-1})g_{i}Y_{i+1}
must be transverse as well, since the
G
-action preserves transversality.
∎

Claim 2.5
.

For every
i=2,\ldots,k
,
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{i-1},C_{i})\leq M/10+E
.

Symmetrically, for every
i=1,\ldots,k-1
,
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{i+1},C_{i+1})\leq M/10+E
.

Proof.

We only prove the first statement, as the second follows analogously. As
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i-1}}(C_{i-1},C_{i})\geq M\geq 4E
, by the Behrstock inequality one of the following happens:

•

Every point of
\pi_{W_{i}}(C_{i})
is
E
-close to
\rho^{W_{i-1}}_{W_{i}}
. Then the conclusion clearly follows.

•

Every point of
\pi_{W_{i}}(C_{i-1})
is
E
-close to
\rho^{W_{i-1}}_{W_{i}}
. Then we have that

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i},W_{i-1})\leq\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i},C_{i-1})+E\leq M/10+E,

where we used Assumption
(IV)
.

∎

Claim 2.6
.

For every
i=2,\ldots,k-1
,
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{i-1},W_{i+1})\geq 4/5M-4E>6E
.

Proof of Claim
2.6
.

We simply notice that

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{i-1},W_{i+1})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i},C_{i+1})-\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i},W_{i-1})-\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(C_{i+1},W_{i+1})-

-\mathrm{diam}_{W_{i}}(\rho^{W_{i-1}}_{W_{i}})-\mathrm{diam}_{W_{i}}(\rho^{W_{i+1}}_{W_{i}})\geq 4/5M-4E.

∎

We continue with a general statement about families of pairwise transverse domains in a hierarchical space:

Lemma 2.7

(Transverse domains in a row)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a hierarchical space, and let
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}\subset\mathfrak{S}
be a collection of domains such that
W_{i}\pitchfork W_{i+1}
for every
i=1,\ldots,k-1
. If
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j-1},W_{j+1})>6E
for every
j=2,\ldots,k-1
, then
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}
are pairwise transverse, and
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{i},W_{r})>2E
for every
i<j<r
.

Notice that the hypothesis of Lemma
2.7
are satisfied by the
W_{i}
s we are considering, in view of Claims
2.4
and
2.6
and of the fact that
M\geq 100E
.

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We proceed by induction on
k
. If
k=3
we just need to show that
W_{1}\pitchfork W_{3}
. This is true since
\mathrm{d}_{W_{2}}(W_{1},W_{3})>6E
, and we can invoke Corollary
1.6
.

Now assume that the theorem is true for every collection of at most
k-1
elements. In particular, by applying the inductive hypothesis to the collections
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k-1}\}
and to
\{W_{2},\ldots,W_{k}\}
, we get that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{i},W_{r})>2E
whenever
i<j<r
and
(i,r)\neq(1,k)
. Thus, we only need to show that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{1},W_{k})>2E
for every
j=2,\ldots,k-1
, and again Corollary
1.6
will imply that
W_{1}\pitchfork W_{k}
. Now

\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{1},W_{k})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j-1},W_{j+1})-\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j-1},W_{1})-

-\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j+1},W_{k})-\mathrm{diam}_{W_{j}}(\rho^{W_{j-1}}_{W_{j}})-\mathrm{diam}_{W_{j}}(\rho^{W_{j+1}}_{W_{j}}).

Since by Behrstock inequality we have that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j-1},W_{1})\leq E
, and similarly
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j+1},W_{k})\leq E
, we get that

\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{1},W_{k})>6E-4E=2E,

as required.
∎

Claim 2.8
.

For every
i\neq j
and every
x\in C_{j}
,
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,W_{j})\leq E
.

Proof of Claim
2.8
.

We assume that
j<i
, as the case
j>i
is dealt with analogously. If by contradiction
\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,W_{j})>E
, then the Behrstock inequality implies that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(x,W_{i})\leq E
. Moreover
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{i},W_{j+1})\leq E
, either by combining the Behrstock inequality with Lemma
2.7
(if
j\leq i-2
) or because
W_{i}=W_{j+1}
(if
j=i-1
). Thus we get

\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(x,C_{j+1})\leq\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(x,W_{i})+\mathrm{diam}_{W_{j}}(\rho^{W_{i}}_{W_{j}})+\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{i},W_{j+1})+

+\mathrm{diam}_{W_{j}}(\rho^{W_{i+1}}_{W_{j}})+\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j+1},C_{j+1})\leq M/10+5E,

where we used that
\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(W_{j+1},C_{j+1})\leq M/10+5E
by Claim
2.5
. But then

M\leq\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(C_{j},C_{j+1})\leq\mathrm{d}_{W_{j}}(x,C_{j+1})M/10+5E,

giving a contradiction as
M\geq 100E
.
∎

Claim 2.9
.

\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(Cx_{0},wCx_{0})=\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(C_{1},C_{k+1})\geq 9/10M-5E>0.

The Claim concludes the proof of Theorem
2.1
, because it proves that
w
acts non-trivially on
X
, and therefore is non-trivial in
G
.

Proof of Claim
2.9
.

This is just a matter of putting all the above Claims together. Indeed, one has that

\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(C_{1},C_{k+1})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(C_{k},C_{k+1})-\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(C_{k},W_{k-1})-\mathrm{diam}_{W_{k}}\left(\rho^{W_{k-1}}_{W_{k}}\cup\rho^{W_{1}}_{W_{k}}\cup C_{1}\right).

The first term of the right-hand side is at least
M
by assumption
(II)
. The second one is at most
M/10+E
by Claim
2.5
. Regarding the third one, we have that

\mathrm{diam}_{W_{k}}\left(\rho^{W_{k-1}}_{W_{k}}\cup\rho^{W_{1}}_{W_{k}}\cup C_{1}\right)\leq

\leq\mathrm{diam}_{W_{k}}\left(\rho^{W_{k-1}}_{W_{k}}\right)+\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(W_{k-1},W_{1})+\mathrm{diam}_{W_{k}}\left(\rho^{W_{1}}_{W_{k}}\cup C_{1}\right)\leq E+E+(2E)=4E,

where we used Claim
2.8
to bound the last term. Hence

\mathrm{d}_{W_{k}}(C_{1},C_{k+1})\leq 9/10M-5E,

as required.
∎

Before moving forward, we point out the following lemma, which combines some of the above Claims and will be useful later.

Lemma 2.10
.

For every
1\leq j<i\leq k
and every
x\in C_{j}
,

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,C_{i})\leq M/10+5E.

Symmetrically, for every
1\leq i+1<j\leq k
and every
x\in C_{j}
,

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,C_{i+1})\leq M/10+5E.

Proof.

This is just a combination of some inequalities from the above proof. Indeed

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,C_{i})\leq\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,W_{j})+\mathrm{diam}_{W_{i}}(W_{j})+\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{j},W_{i-1})+\mathrm{diam}_{W_{i}}(W_{i-1})+\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(W_{i-1},C_{i}).

The first term is at most
E
by Claim
2.8
; the third term is at most
E
by the Behrstock inequality, combined with Lemma
2.7
; the last term is at most
M/10+E
by Claim
2.5
. Thus

\mathrm{d}_{W_{i}}(x,C_{i})\leq 4E+M/10+E=M/10+5E.

The second inequality follows analogously.
∎

3.
Amalgamation of geometric subgroups along common boundaries

We not describe an application of Theorem
2.1
to mapping class groups of finite-type surfaces. The subgroups we shall amalgam are mapping class groups of embedded subsurfaces, which overlap “sufficiently” away from a collection of common boundary components. The precise result is Theorem
3.2
below, which in turn is Theorem
B
from the introduction.

3.1.
Notation and setting

We gather here the (fairly standard, but sometimes subtle) notation we shall need to prove Theorem
3.2
.

3.1.1.
Curves, surfaces, and mapping classes

In what follows, let
S
be a possibly disconnected surface of finite-type (that is, an oriented, compact surface from which a finite number of points is removed). If
S
is connected, let
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
be the (extended) mapping class group of
S
, that is, the group of isotopy class of self-homeomorphisms of
S
fixing the boundary pointwise. If instead
S
is disconnected, its mapping class group is defined as the direct product of the mapping class groups of its connected components.

By
curve
we denote the isotopy class of an embedding
\mathbb{S}^{1}\hookrightarrow S
which is
essential
, meaning that it does not bound a disk with at most one puncture. A
multicurve
is a collection of pairwise disjoint, non-isotopic curves. We often see a multicurve as a subsurface of
S
, by replacing each curve with a closed annulus whose core is the curve. Given a curve
\gamma
, let
T_{\gamma}
be the Dehn Twist around
\gamma
(see e.g.
[
11
, Section 3.1.1]
).

The
curve graph

\mathcal{C}S
is the simplicial graph whose vertices are all curves on
S
, and where adjacency corresponds to disjointness. This definition does not apply to some surfaces of small complexity:

•

If
S
is either a sphere with four punctures, or a torus with at most two punctures, then two curves are adjacent in
\mathcal{C}S
if and only if their intersection number is minimal among all pairs.

•

If
S
is an annulus then its
annular curve graph
, which we still denote by
\mathcal{C}S
, is a quasiline. We won’t need the actual definition, and we refer to
[
19
, Section 2.4]
for further explanations.

The following is an easy consequence of known facts, but we provide a proof for completeness. Recall that a subgroup
H
of a group
G
is
separable
if, for every
g\in G-H
, there exists a finite quotient
\psi\colon G\to\overline{G}
such that
\psi(g)\not\in\psi(H)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be a finite-type surface, and let
\Gamma\subseteq\partial S
be a collection of boundary components. Then the Dehn twist flat
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
supported on
\Gamma
is a separable subgroup of
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{S}
be the surface obtained from
S
by gluing a once-punctured disk onto each boundary component belonging to
\Gamma
, and let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be the punctures added this way. By e.g.
[
11
, Proposition 3.19]
, the quotient
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)/\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
is isomorphic to the group
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S},\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\})
, which is the subgroup of
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S})
of all mapping classes fixing the punctures
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
pointwise.

Now let
g\in\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)-\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, and let
h
be its image in
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S},\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\})
, which is therefore non-trivial. Now,
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S},\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\})\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S})
, and the latter is residually finite (see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 6.11]
and the discussion below it for punctured surfaces); hence we can find a finite quotient
\overline{G}
of
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S},\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\})
where
h
projects non-trivially. Then, if we take the composition
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)\to\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S},\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\})\to\overline{G}
, we get a finite quotient such that the image of
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
is trivial while the image of
g
is not.
∎

3.1.2.
The marking graph

Let
\mathbb{M}(S)
be the
marking graph
of
S
, as defined in
[
19
, Section 2.5]
. A vertex
x
of
\mathbb{M}(S)
consists of a multicurve of maximal cardinality, called the
support
of
x
and denoted by
\text{supp}(x)
, and for every
\alpha\in\text{supp}(x)
a choice of a set
p\in\mathcal{C}\alpha
of diameter at most
1
, called the
transversal
associated to
\alpha
. By e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 11.1]
, which in turn builds on observations from
[
19
]
,
\mathbb{M}(S)
has the following HHS structure:

•

The domain set
\mathfrak{S}
is the collection of subsurfaces of
S
;

•

\sqsubseteq
is containment and
\bot
is disjointness of subsurfaces (up to isotopy);

•

For every
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, the associated coordinate space
\mathcal{C}U
is the curve graph of
U
, and the projection
\pi_{U}\colon\mathbb{M}(S)\to\mathcal{C}U
is the subsurface projection.

Furthermore,
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
acts geometrically on
\mathbb{M}(S)
, and therefore inherits the HHG structure described above.

3.2.
The result

By
subsurface
we mean the isotopy class of an embedding
U\hookrightarrow S
, where
U
is a finite-type surface whose connected components cannot be pairs of pants. The
boundary

\partial U
of a connected, non-annular subsurface
U
is defined as the closure of
U
minus its interior: if
U
is an annulus, with a little abuse of notation we define its boundary as the core of the annulus. The boundary of a disconnected subsurface is the union of the boundaries of its connected components.

If
U
is a
closed
subsurface, we can extend every mapping class on
U
to the identity on
S-U
, and we get a homomorphism
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)\to\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
. This map is injective if and only if every curve in
\partial U
is essential in
S
, and no two boundary components are isotopic (see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 3.18]
). In this case, we call
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
a
geometric embedded
subgroup of
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
. Our main theorem describes when two such subgroups span a free product, amalgamated along a common boundary Dehn twist flat:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
S
be a connected finite-type surface, and let
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
and
\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
be two geometric embedded subgroups, where each of
U
and
V
is either connected or a multicurve.

Let
\Gamma
be the collection of all curves which belong to the boundary of both
U
and
V
, and let
\Gamma_{U}=\partial U-\Gamma
(resp.
\Gamma_{V}=\partial V-\Gamma
). Suppose that
\Gamma_{U}
,
\Gamma_{V}
are both non-empty, and that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{C}(S-\Gamma)}(\Gamma_{U},\Gamma_{V})\geq 4
.

Then there exist finite index subgroups
A\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)
,
B\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
, intersecting along the Dehn twist flat
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
, such that

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)}\cong A*_{\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}}B.

The proof will highlight some useful techniques to verify the requirements of Theorem
2.1
. Such tools often use only the existence of a HHG structure for the ambient group, and can therefore be exported to more general settings.

Proof.

We start with some considerations on the two subsurfaces and their mapping class groups:

Claim 3.3
.

Given two subsurfaces
W\sqsubseteq U
and
W^{\prime}\sqsubseteq V
, if none of the two subsurfaces is a sub-multicurve of
\Gamma
, then
W\pitchfork W^{\prime}
.

Proof of claim
3.3
.

If by contradiction
W
and
W^{\prime}
were either disjoint, or one contained in the other, then there would be two curves
\delta\subset W
and
\delta^{\prime}\subset W

Claim 3.4
.

\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)\cap\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)=\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}
.

Proof of Claim

.

Clearly
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}\leq\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(U)\cap\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(V)
. Conversely, pick an element

, where each

Now, as

and

. In other words,

.

Now, if one between

as well, and we are done. Otherwise, the above argument shows that

projects to a torsion element of

is the surface obtained by capping every curve in

∎

Next, we produce the subgroups

. Extend

to a multicurve

of maximal cardinality, and let

. Let

(1)

The above quantity is well-defined, as Claim

tells us that, whenever

, so that the projection of

to

where

is a hierarchical constant for

, and we used that subsurface projections are

whose support contains

, and use it to bound the second term of Equation (
1

(2)

C_{0}=\sup\{\mathrm{d}_{W}(x_{0},y_{V})\,|\,W\sqsubseteq U,W\not\sqsubseteq\Gamma\}+\sup\{\mathrm{d}_{W^{\prime}}(x_{0},y_{U})\,|\,W^{\prime}\sqsubseteq V,W^{\prime}\not\sqsubseteq\Gamma\}.

Now, let

be the subset of all elements

\mathrm{d}_{Y}(y_{U},a(y_{U}))\leq 12C_{0}+100E+100

Notice that

. One can define

We are finally ready to show that

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{MCG}^{\pm}(S)}\cong A*_{\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}}B.

We first describe the subsurfaces

we shall use to prove the Theorem. For every

there exists a domain

such that

, by how we chose

We first notice that

and every curve

.

Furthermore, suppose that every

as above was nested in

as above which is nested in

.

Now let

, and choose

, for every

and every

•

(I)
The action of
\mathbb{Z}^{|\Gamma|}

•

(II)

•

(III)

•

(IV)

\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(ax_{0},Y_{b})\geq\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(ax_{0},x_{0})-\mathrm{d}_{Y_{a}}(x_{0},Y_{b})-\mathrm{diam}_{Y_{a}}(\rho^{Y_{b}}_{Y_{a}})\geq M-C_{0}-E\geq 2E.

4.
A combination theorem for hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups

This Section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
C
from the introduction, regarding the hierarchical quasiconvexity of the amalgamated free product of two HQC subgroups
A,B
of a HHG
(G,\mathfrak{S})
.

4.1.
Quasiconvexity and friends

Definition 4.1
.

A subspace
Z
of a geodesic metric space is
R
-
quasiconvex
, for some constant
R\geq 0
, if every geodesic segment with endpoints on
Z
is contained in the
R
-neighbourhood of
Z
.

For the rest of the section, let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHS.

Definition 4.2

(HQC)
.

A subspace
Y\subseteq X
is
\kappa
-hierarchically quasiconvex
, for some
\kappa\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
, if:

•

For every
U\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\pi_{U}(Y)
is
\kappa(0)
-quasiconvex in
\mathcal{C}U
;

•

Realisation:
for every
x\in X
and every
R\in[0,+\infty)
, if
\mathrm{d}_{U}(x,Y)\leq R
for every
U\in\mathfrak{S}
then
\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,Y)\leq\kappa(R)
.

Remark 4.3
.

It follows from the definition, together with the fact that coordinate projections are coarsely Lipschitz, that if
Y
and
Z
are two subspaces of
X
within Hausdorff distance
d
, and if
Y
is
\kappa
-hierarchically quasiconvex, then
Z
is
\kappa^{\prime}
-hierarchically quasiconvex, for some function
\kappa^{\prime}
only depending on
\kappa
,
d
, and
(X,\mathfrak{S})
. This observation will be used repeatedly throughout the section.

An equivalent definition of hierarchical quasiconvexity, which more closely resembles Definition
4.1
, involves being closed under certain quasigeodesic paths, called “hierarchy paths”:

Definition 4.4

(Hierarchy path)
.

For
\lambda\geq 1
, a (not necessarily continuous) path
\gamma\colon[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}\to X
is a
\lambda
–hierarchy path if

•

\gamma
is a
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasigeodesic,

•

for each
W\in\mathfrak{S}
, the path
\pi_{w}(\gamma)
is an unparameterised
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasigeodesic, meaning that it becomes a
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasigeodesic after precomposing it with an increasing function
g\colon[0,l]\to[a,b]
mapping
0
to
a
and
l
to
b
.

Remark 4.5
.

[
3
, Theorem 5.4]
states that any two points of
X
are connected by a
\lambda_{0}
-hierarchy path, for some constant
\lambda_{0}
only depending on
(X,\mathfrak{S})
. From now on, we will assume that the hierarchical constant
E
has been chosen greater than this

.

Lemma 4.6

(
[
24
, Theorem 5.7]
)
.

A subset
Y\subseteq X
is
\kappa
–hierarchically quasiconvex if and only if there exists a function
\eta\colon[1,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
such that every
\lambda
-hierarchy path with endpoints on
Y
is contained in the
\eta(\lambda)
-neighbourhood of
Y
. Moreover,
\kappa
and
\eta
each determine the other.

The following combines
[
5
, Definition 5.4 and Lemma 5.5]
:

Definition 4.7

(Gate)
.

Let
Y\subseteq X
be
\kappa
-hierarchically quasiconvex. There exists a coarsely Lipschitz, coarse retraction
\mathfrak{g}_{Y}\colon X\to Y
, called the

on
Y
, such that, for every
x\in X
and every
W\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\pi_{W}(\mathfrak{g}_{Y}(x))
uniformly coarsely coincides with the coarse closest point projection of
\pi_{W}(x)
to the
\kappa(0)
-quasiconvex subset
\pi_{W}(Y)\subseteq\mathcal{C}W
.

Given two HQC subspaces
A,B
, the gate of one onto the other can be characterised in terms of the
coarse intersection
of
A
and
B
:

Lemma 4.8

(
[
4
, Lemma 4.10]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be
\kappa
-hierarchically quasiconvex subsets of

, and let
\mathrm{d}_{X}(A,B)=r
. There exist non-negative constants

and

, both depending only on

\mathrm{d}_{Haus}(N_{R_{0}}(A)\cap N_{R_{0}}(B),\mathfrak{g}_{B}(A))\leq D_{1}
.

Furthermore, if

is a group and

are

of

there exist

Thus, for HQC subgroups of a HHG, the gate of one onto the other is within finite Hausdorff distance from the actual intersection:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let

and

be

-hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups of a HHG

. There exists a constant

4.2.
Hierarchical quasiconvexity of a union

In hyperbolic spaces, a union of quasiconvex subspaces is again quasiconvex. We provide a proof of this fact for completeness:

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic space, and let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be two
R
-quasiconvex subspaces. Then
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
is
\widehat{R}
-quasiconvex, where

\widehat{R}=R+2\delta+\mathrm{d}_{X}(Y,Y^{\prime})+1.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic segment with endpoints
a,b\in Y\cup Y^{\prime}
. If
a
and
b
are both in
Y
, or both in
Y^{\prime}
, then
\gamma
is in the
R
-neighbourhood of
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
, by quasiconvexity of each subset. Thus, suppose without loss of generality that
a\in Y
and
b\in Y^{\prime}
. Let
p\in Y
and
p^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
be such that
\mathrm{d}_{X}(p,p^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{d}_{X}(Y,Y^{\prime})+1
, and choose geodesics
[a,p]
,
[p,p^{\prime}]
, and
[p^{\prime},b]
. Then
\gamma
lies in the
2\delta
-neighbourhood of
[a,p]\cup[p,p^{\prime}]\cup[p^{\prime},b]
, as geodesic quadrangles are
2\delta
-slim in hyperbolic spaces. In turn,
[a,p]\cup[p,p^{\prime}]\cup[p^{\prime},b]
is contained in the
(R+\mathrm{d}_{X}(Y,Y^{\prime})+1)
-neighbourhood of
Y\cup Y^{\prime}
, and the conclusion follows.
∎

Now we want to establish an analogue of Lemma
4.10
for HQC subspaces of a HHS. More precisely, we shall prove that the necessary and sufficient condition for the union to be HQC is the following. Recall that, given two subspaces
C\subseteq A
of a metric space
X
, we say that
C
is
R
-dense in
A
if
A\subseteq N_{R}(C)
.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHS, and let
A,B\subseteq X
be hierarchically quasiconvex subspaces. We say that
A
and
B

fill all squares
if there exists a constant
T
such that, whenever
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
are orthogonal, either
\pi_{U}(\mathfrak{g}_{A}(B))
is
T
-dense in
\pi_{U}(A)
, or
\pi_{V}(\mathfrak{g}_{B}(A))
is
T
-dense in
\pi_{V}(B)
.

Notice that, if
A
and
B
fill all squares for some constant
T
, then they also fill all squares for any bigger constant
T^{\prime}\geq T
.

Remark 4.12
.

Definition
4.11
roughly forbids the following situation, which gives the name to the property. Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the HHS structure coming from the usual Cartesian coordinates, let
A
be the
x
-axis and
B
the
y
-axis. Both
A
and
B
are hierarchically quasiconvex, as they correspond to factors of the product structure. However,
A\cup B
is not hierarchically quasiconvex, as it does not satisfy the realisation property: any point

has the same
x
-coordinate as some point in
A
and the same
y
-coordinate as some point in
B
, but can be arbitrarily far from
A\cup B
. In other words,
A
and
B
leave some gaps in the square.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHS, and let
A,B\subseteq X
be
\kappa
-hierarchically quasiconvex subspaces. Then
A
and
B
fill all squares (Definition

, if and only if
A\cup B
is
\widetilde{\kappa}
-hierarchically quasiconvex, where

and
\widetilde{\kappa}
each determine the other (together with

).

We split the two implications of Theorem
4.13
into Lemmas

and

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHS, and let

-hierarchically quasiconvex subspaces. If

is
\widetilde{\kappa}
-hierarchically quasiconvex then

and

, and

.

Fix a hierarchical constant

for

From now on, we will say that a quantity is

4.4.
Why no drift?

We now show that the conclusion of Theorem
4.20
might not hold if one removes the hypothesis of having no drift in the orthogonals, Definition
4.19
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{a,b}
be the free group on two generators
a
and
b
, and let
D_{x,y}\coloneq\langle x,y\,|\,x^{2}=y^{2}=1\rangle
be a copy of
D_{\infty}
generated by the involutions
x
and
y
. Let

G=\mathcal{F}_{a,b}\times D_{x_{1},y_{1}}\times D_{x_{2},y_{2}},

and let
X
be the Cayley graph for
G
with respect to the generators
\{a,b,x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\}
. The
G
-action on
X
, which is a direct product of hyperbolic spaces, makes
G
into a HHG; in particular, combining
[
5
, Corollary 8.28 and Theorem 9.1]
, we can find a HHG structure
\mathfrak{S}
whose only domains with unbounded coordinate spaces are the following:

•

For every
g\in\mathcal{F}_{a,b}
, there is a domain
gL_{a}
whose coordinate space is
g\operatorname{Cay}\left(\langle a\rangle,a\right)
, and a domain
gL_{b}
defined analogously;

•

The Bass-Serre tree
T
of the splitting
\mathcal{F}_{a,b}=\langle a\rangle*\langle b\rangle
is a domain, whose coordinate space is the tree itself;

•

Finally, there are two domains
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, whose coordinate spaces are, respectively,
\operatorname{Cay}\left(D_{x_{1},y_{1}},\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\right)
and
\operatorname{Cay}\left(D_{x_{2},y_{2}},\{x_{2},y_{2}\}\right)
.

The relations between the above domains are as follows:
T
,
W_{1}
, and
W_{2}
are pairwise orthogonal; for every
g\in\mathcal{F}_{a,b}
,
gL_{a}
and
gL_{b}
are nested inside
T
; every two domains which are nested inside
T
are transverse.

Now let
A=\langle a^{N}x_{1}x_{2}\rangle
and
B=\langle b^{N}y_{1}y_{2}\rangle
, where
N
is a positive integer to be chosen later. For every
g\in A-\{1\}
let
Y_{g}=L_{a}
, and similarly for every
g\in B-\{1\}
let
Y_{g}=L_{b}
. One can choose
N
large enough that
A
and
B
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
. Moreover, notice that the projection of
A
to every domain which is not
L_{a}
has diameter bounded by some constant
K
, while
\pi_{L_{a}}(A)
is coarsely dense in
\mathcal{C}L_{a}
. In particular,
A
is hierarchically quasiconvex, and similar considerations hold for
B
. We also notice that
A
and
B

(K+1)
-fill all squares, as for every two orthogonal domains
U
and
V
we have that
\min\{\mathrm{diam}_{U}(A),\mathrm{diam}_{V}(B)\}\leq K
. However,
A
and
B
do not satisfy Definition
4.19
, as for example the unbounded domain
W_{1}
is orthogonal to both
L_{a}
and
L_{b}
but both
A
and
B
have bounded projection to
\mathcal{C}W_{1}
.

Finally,
A*B
is not hierarchically quasiconvex. Indeed, the projection of
A*B
to the product
D_{\infty}^{2}\coloneq D_{x_{1},y_{1}}\times D_{x_{2},y_{2}}
is within finite distance from the diagonal
\langle(x_{1}y_{1},x_{2}y_{2})\rangle
; hence the projection of
A*B
to both
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
is coarsely dense, but a point

can be arbitrarily far from the diagonal. Thus

does not satisfy the realisation property from Definition
4.2
.

5.
Combination of strongly quasiconvex subgroups

We conclude the paper by studying when our amalgamation procedure preserves the following notion of quasiconvexity:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
X
be a geodesic metric space. A subspace
Y\subseteq X
is
Q
-
strongly quasiconvex
, for some function
Q\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
called the

of

\lambda\geq 0

(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasigeodesic with endpoints on

Q(\lambda)
-neighbourhood of

The above notion is equivalent to quasiconvexity in hyperbolic spaces (see e.g.
[
8

We also need the following definition from
[

]
:

Definition 5.2

For

\Theta

\mathrm{diam}_{U}(A)\geq\Theta

\pi_{V}(A)
is
\Theta
-dense in
\mathcal{C}V
.

.

\mathrm{diam}_{U}(H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{3})\leq 3\Theta
, which is strictly less than

, Equation (

\mathrm{d}_{U}(H_{j},w)\geq\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,w)-\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{j-1})-\mathrm{diam}_{U}(H_{j-1}\cup H_{j})\geq\mathfrak{T}/4-3\Theta,

where we used that

\mathrm{diam}_{U}(H_{j}\cup H_{j-1})\leq 2\Theta
. Summarising, we found an index
j
such that

\min\{\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{j}),\mathrm{d}_{U}(H_{j},w)\}\geq\mathfrak{T}/4-3\Theta.

Now notice that, whenever
|j-r|\leq 3
, we have that

(7)

\min\{\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{r}),\mathrm{d}_{U}(H_{r},w)\}\geq\mathfrak{T}/4-7\Theta.

Indeed

\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{r})\geq\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{j})-\mathrm{diam}_{U}\left(\bigcup_{t=j}^{r}H_{t}\right),

and as each

\mathrm{diam}_{U}\left(\bigcup_{t=j}^{r}H_{t}\right)\leq\sum_{t=j}^{r}\mathrm{diam}_{U}(H_{r})\leq(|j-r|+1)\Theta\leq 4\Theta.

The same argument shows that
\mathrm{d}_{U}(H_{r},w)\geq\mathfrak{T}/4-7\Theta
, hence the situation in
\mathcal{C}U
is as in Figure (

Suppose first that

, for some

is
\Theta

U
does not contain any
W_{r}
as above.

No transversality.
Next, we argue that
U
cannot be transverse to any
W_{r}
. Indeed
\mathrm{d}_{W_{r}}(C_{r},C_{r+1})\geq M\geq 4E
. Therefore, if
U
were transverse to

C_{r}

E

\rho^{U}_{W_{r}}

\mathrm{d}_{U}(W_{r},H_{r})\leq E

\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,W_{r})\geq\mathrm{d}_{U}(1,H_{r})-\mathrm{diam}_{U}(\rho^{W_{r}}_{U})-\mathrm{d}_{U}(W_{r},H_{r})\geq

\geq\mathfrak{T}/4-7\Theta-2E\geq 2E,

\mathfrak{T}
in Equation (
6

No consecutive orthogonality.
Then we can assume that

No alternated nesting.
We are left with the case when, whenever
