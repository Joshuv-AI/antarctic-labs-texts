Tobacco-free cities: a qualitative evaluation of the tobacco control programmes in Chinese cities, 2009–2018
Authors: Yuchen Zhao, Chenyu Qian, Ling Fang, Pinpin Zheng, Zongshuan Duan, Jeffrey Koplan, Pamela Redmon, Michael Eriksen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveDuring 2009–2018, the Emory Global Health Institute conducted the Tobacco-Free Cities (TFC) programme in 22 cities in mainland China. This study aims to assess the sustained impact of the TFC programme.DesignA qualitative study using semi-structured interviews was adopted, which focused on the leadership and capacity building, city-level smoke-free policies, tobacco control activities, outcomes of tobacco control, sustainability post TFC programme and the accomplishments of tobacco control. The thematic analysis was employed for qualitative data analysis.SettingOnline in-depth personal interviews.ParticipantsThis qualitative study recruited 17 participants from 10 cities which participated in the TFC programme (five with comprehensive and five with partial smoke-free policies). Interview participants included TFC programme leaders, TFC staff and current tobacco control staff.ResultsFirst, TFC members reported enhanced competencies in smoke-free policy effort and leadership. Five cities with comprehensive smoke-free policies had a high degree of public support, while cities with partial bans faced barriers such as lack of leadership support and experiencing tobacco industry influence. Tobacco control activities, including media campaigns, policy enforcement, cessation programmes and monitoring activities, were sustained in almost all cities. Enhancement in smoke-free social norms, decrease in smoking rate and second-hand smoking exposure were perceived. Challenges to sustainability included reduced financial and personnel resources and weakened policy enforcement.ConclusionsThe TFC programme was regarded by participants as an effective model that provided the necessary training and technical support to develop and enforce effective and sustainable tobacco control policies and initiatives at the city level in China. Future tobacco control training should focus more on developing concrete solutions for sustainability challenges.Keywords: Smoking Reduction, Tobacco Use, Health policy, China

Introduction

Tobacco use is one of the major public health disasters of the 20th century, causing over 8 million deaths worldwide annually.1 In 2019, it was estimated that China had 341 million smokers.2 In addition, 68.1% of non-smokers in China are exposed to second-hand smoke.3 If smoking patterns do not change, it is estimated that tobacco will cause one-third of all male cancer deaths in China by 2030.4

To reduce tobacco use and related disease burden, China ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control in 2006, committing to implement the obligations of the treaty.5 WHO proposed the MPOWER toolkits including six effective tobacco control measures to counter the epidemic, including monitoring tobacco use, protecting people from tobacco smoke, enforcing tobacco advertising, promotion and sponsorship bans, raising taxes on tobacco, quitting smoking and warning about the dangers of tobacco.6 The Chinese government has demonstrated its commitment to tobacco control. The national health strategy ‘Healthy China 2030’ established a specific tobacco control goal to reduce the adult smoking rate to 20%.7 International philanthropies, including the World Bank and organisations affiliated with the Bloomberg Initiative and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, have also made significant investments in tobacco control in China.8

In 2008, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation awarded the Emory Global Health Institute a grant to establish the Tobacco-Free Cities (TFC) programme with the goal of supporting the development of effective, accountable and sustainable tobacco prevention and control initiatives that addressed China’s unique needs and challenges. The TFC programme adopted a ‘bottom-up’, city-based approach to tobacco control to address the disease burden of tobacco use in China.9 Given that nationwide policies are difficult to change in the short term, the ‘bottom-up’ approach was adopted to secure the city-level acceptance and enforcement required for effective adherence.10

There are over 142 cities in China with a population over one million residents, and cities are a natural choice to serve as sites for the ‘bottom up’ approach to tobacco control because they are in a unique position due to their authority to adopt and enforce smoke-free policies.11 They also have an infrastructure in place to launch public health campaigns, including mass media. During 2009–2018, the TFC programme selected 22 cities in China based on several factors including strong local government support for tobacco control efforts, commitment of the lead organisation to the expectations of the programme, perceived stage of readiness for change, population and socio-demographics and geography (table 1). Collectively, these 22 cities covered a total population of nearly 220 million people.

The TFC programme was modelled after the Health VII Project of the World Bank (1999–2006) that showed promising city-level results to curb tobacco use and associated disease burdens in China.12 With a total funding of US$18.36 million, the TFC programme provided n-person training in evidence-based tobacco control interventions and intensive technical assistance to 22 cities to support them in determining the focus of their tobacco control efforts based on their unique situation and provided cities with a high level of flexibility in designing, implementing and evaluating their tobacco control interventions.9 13 The city tobacco control teams engaged primarily in tobacco control efforts in a variety of public and private settings including schools, hospitals, worksites, hotels, restaurants and homes. They also played a key role in advocating for city-wide smoke-free policies, legislation and enforcement.9 13 The programme’s target outcomes included enhancing the capacity of tobacco control staff, adoption and enforcement of city-wide smoke-free policies, enhancing the public’s smoke-free social norms, reducing the prevalence of smoking and second-hand smoke exposure. It was also the programme’s goal to ensure that these outcomes would be sustained over the long term, rather than being limited to short-term effects.

Previous research has evaluated the TFC programme through quantitative survey and the annual progress reports from participating cities. Based on the analysis of TFC programme progress reports, previous studies have found that the TFC programme was implemented effectively, with all participating cities carrying out tobacco control activities as scheduled.9 Through quantitative surveys, previous research demonstrated a significant decline in second-hand smoke exposure in public places across five major cities that participated in the TFC programme during its implementation period.13 However, the effectiveness of staff training remains unmeasured, though it was a core component of the programme. Qualitative research with these participants can better evaluate their self-perceived training effects and reveal how reported progress was achieved internally. Moreover, despite these short-term progresses, the long-term effects of the programme remain unknown, especially after the end of training and funding supply. To address these gaps, a qualitative study is needed to assess the effect of the TFC programme.

The purpose of this evaluation is to assess the long-term impact of the TFC programme through qualitative study. Therefore, we selected a post-programme evaluation period to examine its effect, which was approximately 5 years after the final TFC cohort ended in 2018. This evaluation will assess the adoption and implementation of smoke-free policies and the sustainability of related programmes in participating cities. It will also evaluate how these tobacco control programmes can advocate future tobacco control efforts both in China and globally.

Methods

Sampling and Recruitment

To ensure the diversity and representativeness of the samples, different cities in mainland China were selected first, based on the TFC cohort, comprehensive smoke-free legislation or not, location, population and Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The following considerations were taken into account in the selection of cities: (1) All cities must have participated in the TFC programme. In those cities, each cohort of the TFC programme must include three cities at least; (2) Including five cities with comprehensive smoke-free policy and five cities with partial smoke-free policy; (3) The geographical locations of these 10 cities can respectively represent the different location of mainland China; (4) Including cities with populations over 10 million and cities with populations under 10 million; (5) Including cities with a GDP above 1 trillion RMB (Renminbi - currency in China) and cities with a GDP below 1 trillion RMB. Integrating the above conditions, we choose the following 10 cities as the origin cities for the target interview subjects of this research: Anshan, Changchun, Chengdu, Hangzhou, Kelamayi, Ningbo, Qingdao, Tangshan, Wuhan and Xi’an.

A TFC programme leader, a TFC programme staff member and a current staff member from the tobacco control department of each city were invited to participate in the evaluation. No pre-existing relationships were present between the researchers and participants prior to the study. The participants were contacted via text message or email to solicit their participation in the interviews. The total number of participants was 17. The response rate was 57%, which was negatively affected by the retirement of TFC leaders and staff, refusal to participate and TFC staff having dual roles as current staff. While the final sample size was primarily determined by pragmatic constraints, we still assessed the data saturation. The final three interviewees provided no new information, leading us to conclude that data saturation had been achieved.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the School of Public Health, Fudan University (IRB#2023-06-1059). Informed oral consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interviews. The study was reported according to the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guideline (online supplemental material 3).14

Data collection

One-on-one, in-depth online interviews were conducted by Fudan University researchers between July and August 2023 using the software ‘Tencent Meeting’ (a popular online meeting app in mainland China). Two semi-structured interview guides were developed to align with the specific objectives of this study. For the TFC leaders and TFC staff, the guide explored the following topics: capacity and leadership building, city-level smoke-free policies, other tobacco control efforts, outcomes of tobacco control, tobacco control sustainability after the TFC programme and the tobacco control accomplishments. For current tobacco control staff, a separate interview guide was utilised, which deleted the sections on ‘leadership and capacity building’ as these individuals had not participated in the TFC programme before (online supplemental material 1). Since this study was a semi-structured interview, apart from the questions specified in the interview outline, the interviewers would conduct additional follow-up questions based on the participants’ answers.

The interviews were between 1 and 1.5 hours in length. For each interview, one researcher began by explaining the confidentiality (all information would be used solely for scientific research, with no personal identifiers or specific content being disclosed) and requesting permission for audio recording. After obtaining oral informed consent, the researcher conducted the interview while the other audio-recorded and made field notes. No one else was present during the interviews, ensuring a confidential and private environment for the participants. After the completion of transcription, the research team provided the transcript to the interviewee for review and confirmation to address the completeness and accuracy. No repeat interviews were carried out with participants.

Data analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis using the target outcomes of the TFC programme as the main themes, including (1) capacity building, (2) adoption of smoke-free policies, (3) other tobacco control efforts and (4) the outcomes of tobacco control. In addition, we analysed the (5) sustainability of the TFC programme and its (6) accomplishments. The specific coding process integrated both deductive and inductive approaches. First, an inductive approach was employed during data immersion, which involved repeated reading of the transcripts to become familiar with its content, and then identify initial sub-themes related to the main analytical framework (online supplemental material 2). After that, we applied deductive line-by-line coding to systematically analyse all interview content. Throughout this stage, the research team continuously generated, reviewed and refined the codes, ensuring each one accurately represented the meaning of its potential theme.

Because the two guides were the same except for the section on capacity and leadership building, the data from both were analysed in combination. Three researchers took part in the coding process, all of whom hold master’s degrees or higher and are native Chinese speakers. All transcripts were coded independently by two coders with a 91.5% Kappa value. If there were inconsistencies, the two coders were required to re-review the transcripts to confirm them. Once they still failed to reach an agreement, the third coder would be involved in the discussion to reach a consensus. In addition, the results of the study were shown to the participants to confirm the description of their own expression. The contents of this study were translated into English by a bilingual translator in the research team, and all research members reviewed the translated manuscript. The software NVivo 20.0 was employed to facilitate initial sub-theme identification, thematic coding and retrieval of representative quotes during qualitative data analysis.

Regarding capacity and leadership development, in addition to collecting qualitative responses during interviews, this study quantitatively assessed changes across multiple dimensions of tobacco control capacity and leadership. The TFC grantee leaders and staff were asked a series of close-ended questions using a 4-point Likert scale, including ‘not at all’, ‘some’, ‘a great amount’ and ‘already very skilled’ (online supplemental materials 1).

Study neutrality

To maintain neutrality of the findings, the interviews, data analysis and manuscript writing were performed by researchers from Fudan University, an independent institution not involved in the TFC programme’s implementation. All researchers from Fudan University are either faculty members or students. Additionally, they all possess prior experience in conducting interviews and qualitative data analysis. Furthermore, the coding and findings were reviewed by partners from Emory University, providing an additional validation to enhance the credibility of the study. To ensure reflexivity in the research process, the team was composed of three male and five female researchers.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was not applicable to this study as the research focused on the perspectives of tobacco control professionals.

Results

17 participants (eight males, nine females, average age 50.3) from 10 cities with an average work experience of 15.3 years were interviewed for this evaluation. 10 were TFC programme leaders or staff and 7 are current tobacco control staff. Five of the cities represented had adopted comprehensive smoke-free policies either during or after their participation in the TFC programme and five had adopted a partial ban. Four of the cities had populations of over 10 million, and six had over one trillion RMB GDP (US$140.6 billion) (table 2).

Capacity building

The TFC programme provided extensive tobacco control training and technical support to participating cities. Both tobacco control leaders and staff who participated in the TFC programme reported that the programme had enhanced their tobacco control skills and leadership capabilities. Additionally, 13 interviewees specifically emphasised that the programme significantly boosted the engagement of leadership in government and cross-sector staff in tobacco control, thereby establishing a foundation for subsequent tobacco control activities.

Through my involvement in the TFC program and years of tobacco control efforts, I have accumulated substantial experience and now provide training and guidance to others. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC staff)

The TFC program significantly enhanced the engagement of various departments (e.g., the Publicity Department) and government offices (e.g., the Office of Government Administration) in tobacco control efforts. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

As for the specific tobacco control capacity, the most notable capacity advancement was developing and implementing evidence-based tobacco control policies, with 12 participants indicating their skills increased a great amount or were already very skilled. Most of the TFC grantees interviewed stated they were already skilled at developing and launching effective mass media campaigns, conducting monitoring activities and disseminating or communicating evaluation findings. In addition, about 10 TFC grantees reported substantial leadership development through programme participation, particularly in synthesising diverse partner perspectives, resources and expertise and in inspiring and motivating partners inside or outside of their organisation (online supplemental materials 2).

Adoption of smoke-free policies

With the goal of advancing smoke-free legislation, the TFC programme provided training and funding to support cities in creating smoke-free environments, following the identification of their own priority venues. A total of 12 participants from the TFC programme reported on the completion status of the smoke-free environment.

We prioritized smoke-free hotels and restaurants for this project. Previously, a comprehensive smoke-free environment was not achieved in hotels and restaurants. The project’s implementation, coupled with these venues assuming their primary responsibility, has significantly strengthened tobacco control. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

Five cities had adopted comprehensive smoke-free legislation. Smoking is completely banned in indoor public places, indoor workplaces, public transportation, as well as some outdoor areas.

A citywide tobacco control policy have been introduced on September 1, 2013. It primarily covers all indoor public places, workplaces, and public transportation. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

There are also provisions for banning smoking in outdoor public places, such as locations frequented by women and children, and venues hosting large events. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

Regarding facilitating factors in the comprehensive legislative process, beyond the TFC programme, five interviewees highlighted the supportive role of China’s ‘Civilized City Construction’ campaign and the strong backing from residents.

In 2017, with the National Civilized City campaign as an opportunity, we pushed the local government to pass a comprehensive smoke-free policy. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

We organized public hearings and solicited legislative feedback on government websites, receiving strong support from the public. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

Regarding the cities without comprehensive smoke-free policy, their existing smoke-free policies are failing to provide complete coverage of all indoor public areas, workplaces and public transports, because they often permit the establishment of smoking areas within smoke-free areas.

The regulations specify that some smoke-free locations can set up smoking areas. The scope of these smoking areas is quite broad, including the indoor areas of hotels, restaurants and karaoke rooms. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

Compared with interviewees from cities with comprehensive smoke-free policies, those from cities with partial policies more frequently mentioned barriers to adopting comprehensive smoke-free legislation. Five interviewees mentioned tobacco industry interference during the law-making process. Four participants reported insufficient government engagement and support, along with shortages of staff and funding. A few respondents also noted impacts from COVID-19 and influence from bigger cities with weaker tobacco control policies.

During the legislative process, personnel from the tobacco monopoly bureau also participated in the revision and discussion, and they want to propose amendments to almost every clause. (Partial legislation, TFC staff)

Leadership commitment remains insufficient, and tobacco control work is essentially being pushed forward through external pressures rather than internal initiative. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

Other tobacco control efforts

Beyond smoke-free legislation, tobacco control staff in various cities have conducted monitoring, mass media campaigns and smoking cessation programmes. Tobacco control monitoring includes tracking smoking rates among residents and smoke-free environments. Data collection ranges from citywide surveys to targeted groups like government staff or corporate employees.

We have established and refined a citywide tobacco prevalence surveillance system, enabling us to produce an annual report on the city’s smoking rate. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

All interviewees reported tobacco control mass media campaigns in their respective cities. These campaigns extend beyond conventional approaches like distributing tobacco control materials to include more innovative strategies, such as leveraging the influence of key opinion leaders, cultural heritage and trending news topics, to conduct tobacco control publicity with characteristics specific to each city.

We produced a public service announcement titled ‘If Cultural Heritage Could Talk,’ which uses the perspective of historical heritage to against smoking. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

A bus driver was assaulted after advising a smoker to stop smoking on the bus. At that time, we worked with the media to spread this case, which aroused extensive discussion among the citizens. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

To address the growing public demand for smoking cessation, cities are enhancing their cessation services through community-based programmes, cessation competitions, mobile interventions and cessation clinics.

We organized a 100-Day Quit Smoking Competition, established five cessation hotlines across the city, and set up four cessation clinics to provide free counseling throughout the competition. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

Changes in smoking social norms

Accompanied by the establishment of smoke-free legislation and other tobacco control efforts, the smoke-free social norms have been strengthened, reflected in increased public support for smoke-free policies, greater engagement in tobacco control activities and the growing number of tobacco control topics in public discourse.

We also conducted street surveys, and citizens show strong support for smoke-free environments and smoke-free policy. (Partial legislation, current TC staff)

Someone was smoking in the barbecue restaurant before. I advised them to stop smoking, and it turned out that they were quite cooperative. (Comprehensive legislation, current TC staff)

The enhancement of these smoke-free social norms is associated with the implementation of smoke-free legislation and multiple tobacco control measures.

Creating smoke-free environments serves a dual purpose as a public education tool, which have correspondingly increased public knowledge about the harms of tobacco and thus fostered a stronger smoke-free atmosphere. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

Changes in prevalence of smoking and second-hand smoke exposure

A total of 13 interviewees reported a decline in adult smoking rates, observed in cities with both comprehensive and partial smoke-free legislation. Additionally, 10 participants noted a reduction in second-hand smoke exposure.

In our city, the adult smoking rate has been gradually decreasing. The smoking rate was 25.8% in 2015. Last year, the smoking rate had dropped to 20.3% (Comprehensive legislation, TFC leader)

The smoking rate has been gradually declining. Our current smoking rate for ages 15-69 in 2022 is 19.6%. Overall, there has been a steady decrease in recent years, basically maintaining below 20%! (Partial legislation, current TC staff)

Our 2019 survey measured workplace secondhand smoke exposure at 45%, a rate that fell to 37.6% by 2021. (Partial legislation, TFC leader)

Sustainability

Interviewees from cities with comprehensive smoke-free policies confirmed ongoing policy implementation. Nearly all participants reported sustained monitoring and surveillance activities, mass media campaigns and cessation programmes, with particular emphasis on initiatives conducted around World No Tobacco Day.

Smoke-free policy implementation has been strengthened. We have established a unified citywide tobacco control complaint hotline, implemented standardized no-smoking signal, and developed smoke-free environments. (Comprehensive legislation, current TC staff)

Our TV stations provide education related to tobacco control every year. There are also smoking cessation clinics. We have smoking cessation clinics in central hospitals and mental rehabilitation hospitals. (Partial legislation, TFC Staff)

The sustainable development of tobacco control is also reflected in the establishment of a government-led, multi-sectoral collaboration model.

Our subsequent tobacco control work is generally led by the government, and departmental coordination has made great progress. Now the implementation of our tobacco control actions is actually a model in which the leading government departments coordinate with our national participation! (Partial legislation, TFC Staff)

While tobacco control activities have continued in all the cities, challenges were identified. One participant from a city with a comprehensive policy indicated that policy enforcement was weak and more resources were needed for tobacco control. In addition, many participants from cities with partial smoke-free policies cited the lack of personnel and financial resources to support the tobacco control work.

I feel that various departments are not paying too much attention to the policy implementation. The second problem is a severe shortage of both staffing and funding. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

I think what we are most lacking now is people, and there is no financial support. Especially in the current situation, there are no people and no money, and leaders do not pay much attention to it, so the tobacco control work has indeed shrunk in the past two years. (Partial legislation, TFC Staff)

Major accomplishments

The study participants were asked their opinion on their city’s major tobacco control accomplishments in the past 10 years. Participants from all the cities in this study mentioned their smoke-free policy as the greatest achievement. Half of the cities also mentioned social norm changes and the decrease of smoking rate and second-hand smoking exposure as accomplishments.

The biggest achievement is that we issued the smoke-free policy, which is relatively powerful and very effective, because with this policy, the self-awareness and supervision responsibilities of public places have been clarified. the overall smoke-free atmosphere in the city has been improved. The environment in public places is gradually improving, the air is gradually getting cleaner, and people’s participation and awareness of smoking control are increasing. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

The smoking rate in indoor public places has been significantly reduced, and the number of people exposed to second-hand smoke has been reduced. The smoking rate of young people has been significantly reduced, which is full of hope for the future of tobacco control. (Partial legislation, TFC Staff)

Valuable contributions attributed to the TFC programme included training, education and technical support, funding, introductions to other national and international experts and required engagement of city leadership to participate in the programme.

We did receive a lot of support from Emory University, whether it was in terms of funding, ability improvement, or business training and guidance. We also received their affirmation. (Comprehensive legislation, TFC Staff)

Discussion

Through the post-programme interviews with 17 tobacco control staff from cities participating in the TFC programme, we found the TFC programme played an important role in advancing tobacco control across participating Chinese cities, contributing to strengthened local capacity, the adoption and implementation of smoke-free policies from the perspective of interviewees. These efforts were associated with self-reported enhancement in smoke-free social norms and reductions in prevalence of smoking and second-hand smoke exposure, underscoring the programme’s role in fostering an enabling environment for tobacco control. Moreover, the study highlights the critical influence of governmental leadership and multi-sectoral collaboration systems in sustaining tobacco control initiatives, while also revealing persistent challenges such as tobacco industry interference and resource constraints that impede further progress.

Training and leadership development

At the onset of the TFC programme, there were relatively few full-time personnel dedicated to tobacco control, funding and resources were scarce and enforcement and compliance with existing smoking bans was limited.15 The TFC programme addressed this gap by providing capacity building opportunities such as training and technical support for public health professionals and tobacco control advocates. Trainings focused on best practices in tobacco control, including situational analysis, identifying and engaging partners, tobacco control programme planning and implementation, advocacy, policy development, implementation and enforcement, media communications and programme evaluation. The training prepared participants to deliver science-based interventions and implement and evaluate effective policies.

In addition to formal training, the TFC programme developed and delivered a technical assistance model to facilitate continuous monitoring and support through site visits, calls and communication via email or instant messaging. This model allowed for tailored, rapid responses to the challenges faced by each city. This evaluation showed a marked self-perceived improvement in participants’ tobacco control knowledge, skills and leadership abilities. The TFC training and technical assistance model provides a template that can be used to build knowledge and skills of the public health workforce to plan, implement and evaluate evidence-based and sustainable tobacco control programmes in China.16

Achievements in tobacco control

Comprehensive smoke-free policy explicitly prohibits smoking in indoor public places, workplaces and public transportation; does not permit the establishment of smoking areas; and stipulates clear penalties.5 In contrast, a partial smoke-free policy is characterised by incomplete coverage, the permission of smoking rooms or the absence of clear stipulations. Given the diversity of cultural contexts and the difficulty of national smoke-free legislation, implementing tobacco control programmes at the city level may be more targeted and effective. Cities have the autonomy to develop smoke-free policies and other tobacco control measures that align with local needs, making the effort more effective than a one-size-fits-all national policy. The TFC programme enabled cities to focus on tobacco control interventions within their own authority, such as smoke-free policies, cessation programmes and prevention campaigns, while acknowledging that some interventions, like tax policies or product regulations, are regulated by national government.

Nearly all the cities selected some type of smoke-free priority (table 2), either city-wide, public places, schools or hospitals (which were remarkably not smoke-free at the beginning of the TFC programme), laying the foundation for the adoption of smoke-free legislation in these cities.17 Despite interference from the tobacco industry, with the support of the public and local governments,18 19 the first achievement of the TFC programme in advancing tobacco control efforts in China is that it has helped establish comprehensive smoke-free legislation at the city level from the perspective of interviewees. Currently, there are 24 cities in China with comprehensive smoke-free legislation, six of which were among the cities funded by the TFC programme at the time.

With the implementation of smoke-free policies and other tobacco control initiatives, significant changes have been observed across cities in smoke-free social norms, smoking rates and exposure to second-hand smoke. The shift in smoke-free social norms was reflected in public support for smoke-free policies. Consistent with previous quantitative studies, tobacco control policies demonstrate the highest public acceptance among all public health policies in China19, primarily attributable to the continuous improvement in public awareness campaigns about the harms of tobacco and second-hand smoke. Based on this, the smoker’s quitting intention has increased significantly,20 accompanied by a declining trend in smoking rates, particularly in cities with comprehensive smoke-free legislation that participated in the TFC programme. Taking Shanghai as an example, following the implementation of the smoke-free policy in 2017, the smoking rate decreased by 2.2 percentage points.21

Moreover, cities that participated in the TFC programme reported a decrease in second-hand smoke exposure, even without comprehensive smoke-free policy. Previous studies indicated that between 2015 and 2018, five cities participating in the TFC programme demonstrated significant decrease in second-hand smoke exposure across indoor workplaces, restaurants and households. Compared with the overall national second-hand smoke exposure rates reported during the same period, the decline observed in these five TFC cities was substantially greater, suggesting the potential effectiveness of the TFC programme in reducing second-hand smoking exposure in public indoor areas.

Sustainability

This study suggests that working with cities to set their own priorities and providing requisite training and resources generally had a long-term lasting effect on sustaining tobacco control activities. First, the smoke-free policies appeared to have lasting effects, with several cities maintaining and even expanding their smoke-free environments. The establishment of a government-led, multi-sectoral collaboration model supports the implementation of smoke-free policies. Second, the sustained implementation of mass media campaigns and cessation services further reflects the sustainability, contributing to increased cessation rates and reduced smoking prevalence. Tobacco control staff who received TFC training significantly enhanced their tobacco control capacities and leadership skills. Coupled with ongoing exchanges and training among these staff, a sustainable capacity development mechanism has been established, laying a solid foundation for the long-term advancement of tobacco control efforts.16

Challenges on tobacco control

Consistent with previous research, this study identifies tobacco industry interference as a major obstacle to advancing smoke-free policies. China still lacks systematic laws to mitigate such influence, which manifests in strategies ranging from obstructing the formulation of comprehensive smoke-free policies to shaping public opinion through corporate social responsibility activities. While the primary goal of the TFC programme was to promote tobacco control through smoke-free policies, relatively limited attention was given to training tobacco control staff in countering tobacco industry interference. Future capacity-building initiatives should increasingly emphasise skills to identify and resist such interference. Furthermore, it is essential to advocate for the enactment and enforcement of more comprehensive laws against tobacco industry interference, in order to reduce its impact on future tobacco control efforts.

Following the legislation of smoke-free policies, many cities have faced dual constraints in enforcement: insufficient human resources and material support, coupled with decreased engagement from both leadership and staff regarding tobacco control initiatives.18 22 23 This has resulted in the failure to establish consistent and routine enforcement practices. In addition to fostering greater motivation for tobacco control through training, digital enforcement tools can be introduced to monitor compliance. Furthermore, linking strong compliance outcomes to performance appraisal and increasing domestic resource allocation will help ensure the sustainability of smoke-free policy implementation.

While the TFC programme has achievements in participating cities, its limited coverage has hindered the establishment of nationwide smoke-free legislation and curbed its population-level impact on smoking rates across China. Future tobacco control training initiatives should prioritise effective dissemination of successful experiences, positioning participating cities as regional demonstration hubs that can extend their influence to neighbouring areas and amplify tobacco control efforts.

This evaluation has several limitations, including its retrospective design and the inability to involve a wider range of stakeholders. Furthermore, tobacco control staff from higher-performing cities were more likely to accept interview invitations, while respondents may have exaggerated local achievements due to social desirability concerns, thereby introducing both selection bias and social desirability bias into the study. Finally, this study did not account for the potential confounding effects of concurrent national-level policies and broader tobacco control trends. Despite these limitations, it appears that an investment in locally determined tobacco control programmes at the city level can have an important and lasting impact from the perspective of tobacco control staff in China.

Conclusions

Based on in-depth insights from 17 tobacco control staff across 10 Chinese cities involved in the TFC programme, this study offers several key findings. First, those cities can serve as realistic and effective jurisdictions to conduct tobacco control activities, provided that the activities are centred on strategies that fall under the city’s own authority. Second, encouraging the cities to set their own programme priorities tended to assure city ownership of the programme as opposed to having them imposed from outside. In this case, a smoke-free policy was a popular and practical tobacco control strategy that cities prioritised and could implement. Third, modest resources are necessary to support training and technical assistance efforts and provide limited staff support and funds for material development and media programmes, which may help assure programme sustainability. Participants perceived that the TFC programme has advanced tobacco control efforts at city level in China. Future training should place greater emphasis on countering tobacco industry interference, motivating both leadership and staff, and facilitating the dissemination of successful experiences, thereby further ensuring the sustainability of tobacco control work.
