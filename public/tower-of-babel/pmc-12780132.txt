Assessing random forest performance in low resource speech emotion recognition
Authors: Muhammad Adeel, Zhi-Yong Tao, Shu-Ya Jin, Chuan-Jie Guo, Mohammed Alsuhaibani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn human-computer interaction (HCI), speech emotion recognition (SER) is a pivotal technology that enables machines to decipher human emotions. This study delves into the efficacy of employing a random forest (RF) classifier for SER within Urdu speech, a notably low-resource language. By focusing on the three primary emotions—happiness, sadness, and anger, and leveraging Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) for feature extraction, we have meticulously crafted a model with an impressive validation accuracy of 94.53%. This endeavor highlights the robustness of the RF classifier and represents a significant step towards empathetic artificial intelligence, particularly in improving digital user experiences through emotional understanding. Moreover, our examination highlights the key features of MFCCs and concentrating on their crucial role in emotion discrepancy. This research endeavors the viability and realism of RF classifiers in paving the way for emotionally subtle artificial intelligence (AI) systems, even under the constrictions of resource-deprived languages for instance Urdu. In the future, we hope to broaden the scope of our research to include a wider spectrum of emotions and investigate how other datasets affect our model’s functionality, creating new avenues for the advancement of SER technology.Keywords: Speech emotion recognition, Random forest classifier, Mel frequency cepstral coefficients, Urdu low-resource speech analysisSubject terms: Neuroscience, Engineering

Introduction

Human communication, which is closely linked to emotions, goes beyond the mere usage of words and forms the cornerstone of interpersonal interaction1,2. In this area, speech emotion recognition (SER)3,4, a critical interdisciplinary field that integrates speech processing2, machine learning5, and cognitive science6, aims to identify emotions embedded in spoken language and classify them7. This capability is critical for a variety of applications, from improving human-computer interaction (HCI)8 to supporting mental health assessment9,10.

Despite the growing interest in SER, research has predominantly focused on languages with abundant computational resources, leaving languages such as Urdu - a widely used but computationally inadequate language - mainly unexplored11–13. This lapse not only confines the pertinence of SER technologies but also overlooks the rich emotional expressiveness of the Urdu language14–16. To highlight this research gap, our present study makes use of a random forest (RF) classifier to traverse the multifaceted emotional features of the Pakistani Urdu language, which is eminent for its accuracy and robustness in dealing with overfitting17–19. By employing the SEMOUR + dataset18, we evaluate the classifier’s potential in detecting and recognizing the subtleties of emotional states in Urdu usng Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) for feature extraction20–23.

Our contribution lies in exploiting an RF classifier to the Urdu SER field, stressing a novel endeavor to address the resource-deprived language24,25. These attempts are consistent with a wide-ranging measure to make use of an advanced machine learning techniques for emotion recognition and offer perceptions into the steadiness between interpretability and computational demand26–29. Our results demonstrate the classifier’s ability to distinguish emotional states, highlight its potential to improve user experience on digital healthcare platforms and promote equitable development of empathetic artificial intelligence (AI) technologies30–33.

The originality of our study is highlighted by its focus on Urdu, a language spoken by millions of people but significantly underrepresented in SER research. We propose a peculiar approach of the RF classifier to Urdu SER. It comprehensively analyzes its suitability and performance for a language with complex phonetic structure and cultural richness. Thus, this study fills a critical knowledge gap by presenting one of the first comprehensive tests for Urdu SER using an optimized learning approach. This contribution is crucial as an academic activity because of its practical implications in developing culturally sensitive and linguistically inclusive technologies based on AI. Furthermore, comprehensive feature meaning analysis, supported by RF’s innate ability to make such estimates, opens a new perspective for understanding acoustic markers and emotional features in Urdu.

This facilitates the development of SER systems that are fine-tuned to the subtleties of emotional expression specific to Urdu, thus setting a precedent for future research in SER for low-resource languages and diversifying the field beyond the current dominance of high-resource languages.

Related work

The advancement of SER systems is pivotal in bridging the gap between human emotional expression and computational analysis, offering substantial implications across various languages and cultures. This section outlines the current state of SER research, emphasizing available resources for Urdu—a language markedly underrepresented in computational emotion analysis—and compares them with other languages. Furthermore, we review broader advancements in speech processing methodologies for low-resource languages and relevant optimization techniques in SER pipelines, underscoring the innovation of our study in applying these technologies for Urdu SER.

Resources available for the Urdu language

One of the earliest efforts to establish an Urdu emotional speech resource involved the collection of spontaneous utterances from televised Urdu talk shows. This URDU dataset34 comprises 400 emotionally tagged utterances across four basic emotions—angry, happy, sad, and neutral—spoken by 38 speakers (27 males, 11 females). The recordings were extracted from natural, unscripted conversations, capturing spontaneous affective expressions but also introducing limitations such as background noise, music interference, and uncontrolled emotional variability. Annotators from two academic institutions labeled each utterance based on audio-visual cues, achieving consensus through majority agreement. Despite constraints in emotion diversity and recording conditions, this publicly available corpus laid the groundwork for Urdu SER research by providing one of the first emotion-tagged resources in the language.

The paucity of this dataset presents significant challenges for data-intensive machine learning algorithms, creating a considerable research gap in Urdu SER. This stark contrast compared to the extensive datasets available for languages such as English, German, and Chinese highlights the urgent need to enrich Urdu speech emotion datasets to advance SER research and practical applications in HCI and AI.

To address this limitation, a significant advancement has been made with the introduction of SEMOUR+ (Scripted EMOtional Speech Repository for Urdu)—the first large-scale, phonetically balanced, and gender-balanced scripted emotional speech corpus for Urdu18. SEMOUR + comprises 27,640 speech utterances recorded by 24 native speakers representing diverse regional accents, each eliciting syntactically rich and emotionally diverse content across eight basic categories. The dataset spans approximately 14 h of audio and is validated through high inter-rater agreement scores, ensuring consistency in emotional labeling. Its high-quality design, emotional diversity, and reliable baseline prediction scores make SEMOUR + an essential resource for building robust and scalable Urdu SER systems. Furthermore, it supports numerous applications such as personalized robot assistants, remote diagnostics for psychological conditions, and emotion-aware feedback mechanisms for underrepresented or low-tech-enabled populations.

Resources available for other languages

Several languages benefit from robust SER resources that serve as benchmarks for developing and evaluating complex models. For example, the English IEMOCAP database comprises over 10,000 conversation segments covering nine distinct emotions35. Similarly, EmoDB for German36 and CASIA for Chinese37 provide comprehensive emotional speech datasets, facilitating advanced SER system development and validation. These extensive datasets enable effective application and optimization of sophisticated machine learning and deep learning techniques, setting a foundational standard for multilingual SER system comparisons and advancements.

Advances in speech processing for low-resource languages

Recent progress in speech processing for low-resource languages has introduced several promising methodologies that are particularly relevant to Urdu SER. For instance, the fine-tuning of wav2vec-based representations for speaker diarization in low-resource scenarios has demonstrated the adaptability of self-supervised neural models in contexts with limited annotated data38. This is especially pertinent for Urdu SER, where speaker variability remains a key challenge to generalization. Similarly, speaker verification across dialects using identity-mapping strategies has been explored as a means to systematically address dialectal variability—an often overlooked dimension in low-resource corpora39.

A comprehensive survey of machine learning techniques has also reaffirmed the value of traditional ensemble methods such as RFs within contemporary SER pipelines40, thereby supporting the methodological foundation of the present work. In addition, clustering-based preprocessing techniques—including fuzzy C-means, kernel fuzzy C-means, and K-means—have shown potential for enhancing speech signal quality in noisy or variable acoustic environments that are typical of low-resource settings41. These techniques may serve as effective front-end enhancements for improving the fidelity of acoustic feature extraction prior to classification.

Furthermore, insights from other underrepresented languages—particularly Kurdish—offer valuable perspectives for the Urdu context. Transformer-based ASR models applied to Northern and Central Kurdish have demonstrated the effectiveness of end-to-end deep learning approaches in low-resource speech recognition42–44. These findings suggest the feasibility of adapting such architectures for future iterations of Urdu SER pipelines. Similarly, advances in Kurdish text-to-speech synthesis using native corpora and deep generative vocoders (e.g., WaveGlow)45,46 underscore the critical importance of high-quality, representative datasets—an aspect that aligns with our focus on the development and use of SEMOUR+.

Collectively, these studies not only validate our emphasis on interpretability, efficiency, and linguistic inclusivity but also point toward actionable directions for advancing SER in Urdu and other typologically similar low-resource languages.

SER systems: comparative discussion and optimization techniques

Recent advancements in SER have primarily centered around deep learning models, exhibiting superior accuracy and generalization capability compared to traditional approaches47. Models integrating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) have shown considerable effectiveness in recognizing speech emotions across diverse corpora48. Despite their success, these deep learning frameworks typically require substantial computational resources and extensive annotated datasets, making their immediate application challenging in resource-constrained languages such as Urdu. Consequently, the RF classifier emerges as an advantageous alternative, offering interpretability, computational efficiency, and robustness to limited data. Nonetheless, limitations inherent to RF, particularly regarding scalability and capturing complex nonlinear feature interactions intrinsic to deep learning methods, warrant careful consideration.

Methodology

This section describes an RF classifier approach for emotion recognition in Urdu. We collect data, extract features, train models, and evaluate the results.

Data collection and preprocessing

We used the SEMOUR + dataset to focus on happiness, sadness, and anger as the three core emotional states. The collection includes uniformly sampled Urdu speech samples of excellent quality at 44.1 kHz. Part of the pre-processing step was normalizing and standardizing the audio signals to a specified length.

Feature extraction

MFCCs were determined from each audio sample, reflecting important aspects and features of speech. This stage used a time-stepped technique to generate a multi-dimensional feature array for thorough speech representation.

Model training

We chose the RF classifier because of its built-in feature selection and capacity to handle high-dimensional data. StandardScaler was utilized to scale the features during the training process. Furthermore, the amount of training data was gradually increased to check the performance variations and formulate a learning curve.

Evaluation metrics

We assessed the strength of the model using a validation set. The primary metrics mentioned in the classification report were accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The area under the curve (AUC) analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were also employed to estimate the model’s discriminative capability. Comprehending the key MFCCs in emotion classification was made possible by examining the importance of permutation features.

Choice of the classifier

The choice to incorporate an RF classifier for Urdu SER, a less computerized language, was relying on various premeditated considerations. RF is an ensemble machine learning algorithm called Bootstrap Aggregation or Bagging. It is recognized for its robustness and accuracy as it forms numerous decision trees during training and class inference, a classification (or average prediction) of individual trees. RF integrates the output of numerous of multiple decision trees to arrive at a single outcome. This methodology is mostly appropriate for situations such as SER, where the feature space is substantial and perhaps noisy, which is often the case with emotional speech data.

In the case of Urdu speech, a language considered by an extensive multiplicity of tonal progressions and refined disparities, RF’s ensemble method proposes some advantages:

Management of imbalanced data

SER datasets in less privileged languages frequently come across disproportion problems due to the unequal allocating of emotional utterances with exclamations. RF has an inherent capability to address such imbalances by engendering trees trained on different portions of the dataset to guarantee that the range of numerous emotional statuses are embodied.

Feature importance

It is vital to perceive which features contribute most to emotion recognition while dealing with SER. RF comes up with an intrinsic approach for estimating feature importance, predominantly appreciated for distinguishing Urdu’s intricate phonetic and prosodic features that impart emotional statistics.

Resilience to overfitting

Keeping in view the inadequate number of datasets for less studied languages in case of machine learning, there is a high risk of overfitting.

The RF algorithm mostly generalizes better to new data than a single decision tree due to its ensemble nature, making it a reliable choice for small data sets.

Model interpretability

In a field of SER in which understanding the “why” of predictions is as important as the predictions themselves, RF balances interpretability and complexity. This balance is important for SER research, where stakeholders can attempt to understand the factors that influence SER system decisions.

Computational efficiency

Although RF has an ensemble nature of the machine learning model, it is relatively computationally efficient. It is critical when working with low-resource languages where processing computing power and data may be limited. It allows researchers to perform extensive experiments without a substantial burden of computational resources.

Adaptability to multiclass problems

Urdu SER inherently involves multiclass classification to identify emotions. RF classifiers handle multiclass problems effectively without transforming the problem into binary classification tasks, which could simplify the complexity of SER for Urdu.

For these reasons, the RF classifier not only complements the specific challenges presented by the Urdu language but also aligns with the overarching goals of SER—to create accurate, generalizable, and interpretable models that are computationally viable for real-world applications.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates the RF classifier’s accuracy in Urdu SER, effectively capturing sadness and anger through MFCCs. The slightly lower detection rate for happiness invites further research. Our findings align with existing literature, emphasizing the importance of specific MFCCs in emotion detection and showcasing our model’s competitive edge in SER.

The practical applications of this research are significant, particularly in enhancing human-computer interaction and supporting emotional assessments in healthcare. This study also underlines the critical role of targeted feature selection in machine learning for achieving efficient and accurate results. Future directions include integrating broader acoustic features and exploring deep learning models for advanced SER capabilities.

TCN classification pipeline on the SEMOUR + dataset

We implement the complete Temporal CNN –based classification pipeline for the SEMOUR + dataset. After installing and importing all required libraries (librosa for audio loading, keras-tcn for the TCN layer, TensorFlow for model definition and training, and seaborn/matplotlib for visualization), we mount Google Drive and point to the SEMOUR + folder. The Persian low-resource language

The Persian language, part of the Indo-Iranian group within the Indo-European family, is spoken by over 100 million people across Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, and several other countries. Despite its rich linguistic heritage, Persian shares numerous loanwords with Arabic due to geographical proximity, though differences exist, particularly in grammar and syntax. Persian is categorized into formal and colloquial variants, which differ significantly in terms of lexicon and grammatical structure, posing challenges for natural language processing (NLP). Formal Persian is used in academic, scientific, and official contexts, while colloquial Persian appears in everyday conversations, blogs, social media, and informal communications. Persian’s unique features include its right-to-left writing, free word order, pro-drop syntax, and a lack of short vowels in writing, which creates ambiguity in interpretation. Though substantial progress has been made in Persian language processing, it remains underdeveloped compared to languages like English, particularly in NLP and SER tasks, and continues to face challenges due to limited resources and tools49.

ShEMO: Persian speech emotion detection database

The Sharif Emotional Speech (ShEMO) database is a valuable resource for research in Persian speech emotion detection, providing a comprehensive collection of emotion-labeled speech data50. This database consists of 3,000 utterances, totaling approximately 3 h and 25 min of speech. It includes samples from 87 native-Persian speakers, comprising 31 females and 56 males, and covers six emotional states: anger, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutral. The speech samples were sourced from radio plays broadcast online, ensuring a semi-natural environment for the data collection.

To maintain high-quality emotion annotation, twelve contributors labeled each utterance, with the final labels determined through a majority voting system. The inter-annotator agreement, measured using the kappa statistic, yielded a result of 64%, indicating substantial agreement among annotators. Additionally, the ShEMO database is equipped with both orthographic and phonetic transcriptions based on the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), making it ideal for linguistic feature extraction.

Preliminary classification experiments using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier have been conducted, with gender-independent accuracy reaching 58.2%. Gender-dependent accuracies were observed at 59.4% for females and 57.6% for males50. The ShEMO dataset is freely available for academic research and serves as a robust baseline for advancing speech emotion detection in Persian.

Random forest classifier for emotion detection

Building upon the ShEMO dataset, a RF Classifier was employed to classify six emotional states—anger, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutral—using MFCCs extracted from the Persian speech emotion dataset. The dataset consists of 1,737 male and 1,263 female audio files, totaling 3,000 processed audio files. The initial model achieved an overall accuracy of 66.4% on the test set, with significant variability in performance across different emotional categories.

The confusion matrix analysis revealed that the classifier performed well in distinguishing between anger, sadness, and happiness. However, it struggled to accurately identify fear and surprise, as evidenced by low precision and recall values for these emotions. Notably, the recall for the “fear” class was as low as 0.14, indicating significant challenges in recognizing this emotion. In contrast, the “anger” and “sadness” classes achieved higher recall scores of 0.83 and 0.85, respectively, suggesting more reliable classification for these emotions. These findings highlight the model’s strengths in certain categories, while underscoring the need for improvement in handling class imbalances, particularly for the less-represented emotions.

To address these constraints, we have explored a wide array of methodologies, each with specific strengths and limitations. Among these, the RF classifier was selected for its robustness, interpretability, and low computational complexity—features particularly well-suited for low-resource environments.

Expanded emotion range, classifier evaluation, and dataset diversity

ICEEMDAN and multi-class SVM framework

This subsection outlines the core Urdu SER framework developed in our earlier conference work51,52 and expanded herein for comparative evaluation. The approach integrates ICEEMDAN for feature extraction and a Multi-Class SVM classifier for emotion classification in low-resource linguistic settings. ICEEMDAN decomposes non-stationary and nonlinear speech signals into a finite series of Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs), each capturing a specific oscillatory mode. This adaptive decomposition minimizes mode mixing and enhances component separability by injecting controlled noise at each iteration.

Dataset and experimental setup

The experiments utilized the SEMOUR + Urdu emotional speech corpus, a large-scale dataset comprising 15,040 meticulously annotated utterances produced by eight professional actors (male and female) under controlled acoustic conditions. The database neutralizes lexical content effects and provides balanced samples for several emotion categories. In this study, three primary emotions—anger, happiness, and sadness—were selected based on their consistency and clarity in previous exploratory analyses.

Speech signals were preprocessed through pre-emphasis filtering, frame segmentation, and amplitude normalization to emphasize high-frequency content and preserve prosodic contours. Each signal was divided into overlapping frames (30 ms length, 10 ms overlap, 25% frameshift), and amplitude weighting was applied to improve emotional discriminability. These parameter settings yielded the highest recognition performance during optimization trials.

Signal decomposition and feature extraction

Following segmentation, the ICEEMDAN algorithm decomposed each frame into multiple IMFs. For each IMF, two key descriptors were computed:

Center frequency (CF) – derived from the Hilbert–Huang spectrum (HHS) as the energy-weighted mean instantaneous frequency.

Energy intensity (EI) – quantifying the amplitude energy contribution of the IMF.

IMFs were ranked by energy, and the three most energetic components were selected as primary features. These IMFs encode complementary information across temporal and spectral domains, effectively representing emotion-dependent variations such as pitch modulation, amplitude fluctuation, and rhythm. The resulting multidimensional feature set was further normalized using a Self-Organizing Map (SOM) to mitigate scale disparities among feature dimensions.

Dimensionality reduction and visualization

To facilitate interpretation, t-SNE was applied to project the IMF-based feature space into two and three dimensions. Distinct clusters corresponding to anger, happiness, and sadness were observed, reflecting the discriminative power of the ICEEMDAN-derived features. Silhouette analysis confirmed strong intra-class cohesion and inter-class separation.

In the 3D representation, anger exhibited higher amplitude energy and frequency dispersion, happiness formed moderately spaced clusters with balanced spectral characteristics, and sadness appeared tightly grouped with lower energy and subdued prosody.

Classification and performance evaluation

A multi-class SVM classifier, implemented in MATLAB R2023a using a one-versus-one strategy with a radial basis kernel, was trained on the ICEEMDAN-based features. Stratified sampling ensured balanced emotion representation, with an 80:20 train–test split repeated across 1,000 randomized trials for statistical reliability. Performance metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC curves for class-wise discrimination.

The framework achieved an average accuracy of 78%, significantly higher than the 64% recorded without amplitude weighting, confirming the pivotal role of amplitude cues in emotional differentiation. The ROC analysis revealed strong sensitivity for anger and happiness, while sadness exhibited slightly lower discriminability—a common limitation in low-resource emotional corpora.

The ICEEMDAN-SVM configuration effectively overcame the mode-mixing and false-mode challenges inherent to conventional EMD and EEMD methods, producing a stable and precise decomposition. The integration of multiple IMFs and CF-based descriptors yielded a richer emotional representation than single-IMF models. The final system attained consistent recognition accuracy between 72% and 78%, validating its robustness under limited-data conditions.

Discussion and outlook

The findings highlight the strength of combining advanced signal decomposition with machine learning for emotion recognition in under-represented languages. Although SVM delivered reliable results, further improvements can be pursued through expanded datasets, inclusion of additional emotions, and the adoption of deep learning methods. Approaches such as active learning, multiple classifier systems (MCS), and data augmentation could enhance generalization and stability.

Future studies should also integrate multimodal cues (e.g., facial expression, linguistic content, and physiological signals) to enrich affective context. Emerging frameworks—including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for cross-lingual adaptation, meta-learning for real-time emotion transfer, and reinforcement learning for adaptive modeling—offer promising directions. Expanding the SEMOUR + corpus to encompass regional dialects and varied speaking styles will be vital for building inclusive, intelligent HCI systems capable of understanding affective speech in low-resource linguistic environments.

Siamese and convolutional neural network–based architectures

To benchmark deep learning against the ICEEMDAN–SVM baseline, Siamese and CNN models were developed for Urdu SER using MFCC features from the SEMOUR + corpus53. Urdu’s phonetic variability and limited annotated data complicate direct transfer from high-resource languages; thus, these architectures address complementary challenges of representation learning and generalization in low-resource settings.

Model design and training

The CNN framework comprised multiple convolutional–pooling–batch-normalization blocks for hierarchical extraction of localized time–frequency patterns, followed by dense layers and a softmax classifier. Models were trained using categorical cross-entropy loss with Adam optimization and adaptive learning-rate scheduling. After 20 epochs, training accuracy reached ≈ 92%, while validation accuracy remained ≈ 68%. Fine-tuning with batch normalization, dropout, and extended epochs improved test accuracy to ≈ 93%, demonstrating effective feature learning but mild overfitting under data constraints.

The Siamese network adopted a twin-branch configuration with shared weights, each processing separate MFCC feature vectors to produce embeddings compared via a distance function and sigmoid output. Optimization employed binary cross-entropy with Nesterov-accelerated Adaptive Moment Estimation (Nadam) and a reduce-on-plateau schedule, supported by early stopping and checkpointing. Training accuracy reached ≈ 98.9%, whereas validation accuracy stabilized near ≈ 58%. These outcomes indicate strong embedding discrimination but reduced generalization when sample diversity is limited53.

Comparative evaluation

Performance was assessed through accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC metrics. CNNs consistently outperformed the Siamese models in overall classification accuracy, attaining ≈ 93% on unseen subsets, whereas Siamese models achieved ≈ 58–88% depending on configuration and data scaling. Confusion and ROC analyses revealed that happiness–sadness and disgust–fearful categories remained the most confounded. Visualization via 2D/3D t-SNE confirmed clear emotional clustering, with CNN embeddings showing sharper inter-class boundaries53.

Adaptation to sparse data

To emulate low-resource conditions, models were retrained on reduced subsets of SEMOUR+. CNNs retained stable accuracy after weight recalibration, while Siamese performance declined moderately, suggesting CNNs’ superior adaptability to sparse data. Batch MFCC extraction with multiprocessing, stratified sampling, and incremental storage were used for efficient data handling throughout experiments53.

Discussion and outlook

Both architectures complement the ICEEMDAN–SVM framework: CNNs offer robust end-to-end emotion classification through hierarchical feature extraction, while SNs provide a metric-learning perspective valuable for few-shot and cross-lingual verification tasks. Overfitting in SNs and emotion-specific weaknesses in CNNs (particularly disgust and fearful) highlight the need for enhanced augmentation (pitch/time-stretch/specAugment), class rebalancing, and regularization.

Future research should expand emotional coverage, investigate Transformer or CNN–Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) hybrids for long-range prosody modeling, and explore multimodal fusion (acoustic + lexical/visual cues) to improve robustness in real-world HCI contexts. Collectively, these findings affirm CNN and SNs as strong comparative baselines for Urdu SER and underscore their broader potential across low-resource emotional speech domains53.

Acoustic feature engineering for low-resource SER

To situate the ICEEMDAN–SVM and Siamese/CNN results within broader acoustic pipelines, we draw on a comparative study that evaluated classical and deep models for Urdu SER on MFCC features from SEMOUR+—including SVM, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), dense neural networks (DNN), LSTM, CNN, few-shot learning (FSL), and SNs54. Two consistent patterns emerge: (i) the quality and normalization of acoustic features strongly govern downstream performance across model families; and (ii) when data are curated and augmentation is applied, convolutional and recurrent architectures generally outperform shallow classifiers, while metric learning is especially useful in few-shot regimes. These observations complement our findings—where ICEEMDAN-derived features support an interpretable SVM baseline and CNN/Siamese models provide competitive deep benchmarks—by underscoring the central role of time–frequency representations (MFCCs and IMF-based descriptors) and careful train/validation protocols in low-resource settings54.

Overview and methodology

Using SEMOUR+, which contains native Urdu utterances across eight emotions, signals were denoised, normalized, and transformed into MFCC matrices. Models (SVM, KNN, DNN, LSTM, CNN, FSL, SNs) were trained under stratified splits with grid-search/cross-validation, and assessed via accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score to ensure reproducibility and class-aware evaluation54.

Comparative findings

Under a controlled eight-emotion setup, recognition rates were approximately: CNN 90.00%, KNN 88.83%, LSTM 85.73%, and SVM 75.71%54. In a three-emotion protocol (anger, happiness, sadness) with stratified splits, SVM reached about 0.90 overall accuracy and was comparatively strong on sadness; KNN yielded slightly lower but more uniform per-class scores; LSTM trailed CNN overall but benefited emotions with clearer temporal cues; CNN showed balanced PR and a small edge on sadness. ROC–AUC patterns mirrored these trends, with the most common confusion between happiness and sadness, reflecting overlapping prosodic cues in Urdu54.

Implications for this study

Together with our ICEEMDAN–SVM baseline and the Siamese/CNN results, the evidence indicates that (a) robust acoustic front-ends (MFCCs and IMF-based descriptors), (b) stratified, class-aware evaluation, and (c) data-efficient enhancements (noise-aware normalization, pitch/time-stretch/SpecAugment, and post-hoc calibration) materially improve generalization and emotion coverage in low-resource Urdu SER54.

Few-shot emotion recognition (FSL)

To probe extreme data scarcity, we instantiated a few-shot setting on SEMOUR + using MFCC inputs and at most five labeled utterances per emotion (anger, happiness, sadness). A compact, one-hidden-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with 256 units, a 3-way softmax, Adam optimizer, and 20 epochs achieved 100% training accuracy with oscillatory learning curves, indicating a high risk of overfitting —typical when supervision is severely limited. This underscores the need for stronger regularization (dropout, L2, early stopping), augmentation (pitch shift, time-stretch, additive noise, SpecAugment), and episode-aligned objectives or evaluation (repeated runs with variability estimates)54.

Few-shot Siamese metric learning

We also trained a SNs on balanced positive/negative MFCC pairs (≤ 5 labeled utterances per class). Twin shared-weight branches produced embeddings; an absolute-difference layer with sigmoid yielded pairwise similarity. Under a 20-epoch schedule, validation accuracy fluctuated markedly (early spikes followed by collapse), consistent with volatility in tiny-sample regimes. Thresholded evaluation gave precision = 1.00, recall = 0.667, F1 = 0.80, indicating conservative decisions but missed true positives beyond the small support set. Stabilizing the performance of SNs required augmentation, learning-rate scheduling, pair/episode balancing, and stronger regularization. In practice, SNs offer a complementary metric-learning lens alongside end-to-end CNN/LSTM classifiers, particularly when labeled data are scarce and pairwise supervision is inexpensive54.

Summary and outlook

Across acoustic pipelines, CNN/LSTM models employ time–frequency structure for higher end-to-end accuracy, while Siamese metric learning is valuable in few-shot regimes but benefits from augmentation and calibrated thresholds. For low-resource Urdu SER, combining robust acoustic front-ends with class-aware evaluation and data-efficient training remains key; extending toward multimodal SER (e.g., audio + facial cues) and expanding the emotion taxonomy are promising next steps54.

Recurrent GRU architectures with mel-Spectrogram features (Uni-/Bi-GRU)

Recent studies

Building on the ICEEMDAN–SVM and Siamese/CNN baselines, we evaluated gated recurrent units (GRUs) on the URDU corpus to capture longer-range prosodic dynamics. Prior work showed that a Bi-GRU, paired with careful feature engineering (MFCCs, GFCCs, extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS)), normalization, and SHAP-based interpretability, reached ~ 91% peak validation accuracy on four emotions (sadness, neutral, happiness, anger)55. A controlled comparison on Mel-spectrogram inputs further found Bi-GRU outperforming its unidirectional counterpart (Bi-GRU: 98.75% train, 78.75% val, 87.50% test; Uni-GRU: 98.33% train, 75.00% val, 81.25% test), underscoring the benefit of bidirectional temporal context in low-resource Urdu SER56. Together, these results position recurrent modeling as a competitive alternative when temporal dependencies are prominent.

Methodology

Data and preprocessing. We used the URDU corpus of broadcast utterances (talk-show speech) labeled for discrete emotions. Audio was standardized (sampling, channel, loudness) and segmented per utterance, then transformed into three complementary acoustic representations: MFCCs, GFCCs, and eGeMAPS. Features were z-score normalized across speakers/sessions; labels were one-hot encoded. MFCCs followed standard signal-processing pipelines; GFCCs were derived via an ERB-spaced Gammatone filterbank with cubic-root compression and Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT); eGeMAPS was extracted with open-source Speech and Music Interpretation by Large-space feature Extraction (openSMILE) to obtain a compact, theory-informed set of frequency, energy, and temporal descriptors chosen for affective relevance and cross-study comparability55. For direct time–frequency modeling, we also computed log–Mel spectrograms from Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) frames and applied global speaker-normalized scaling56.

Model development and training

Sequence models used stacked GRU layers (unidirectional or bidirectional) with dropout and, where indicated, L2 regularization; dense layers with a softmax head produced class posteriors. Optimization used Adam with categorical cross-entropy; learning-rate reduction on plateaus and early stopping mitigated overfitting. SHAP was applied post hoc to attribute predictions to specific acoustic cues (cepstral contours, energy dynamics, voicing/prosodic markers) for transparency in HCI-oriented deployments55.

Results and discussion

Keeping in mind the engineered-feature settings (MFCC/GFCC/eGeMAPS), the Bi-GRU achieved ~ 100% training accuracy and ~ 91% validation accuracy on the four-class task under standard normalization55. Validation accuracy rose from 0.7143 at epoch 1 to 0.9107 by epoch 20 (val. loss ≈ 0.2632), then stabilized around 0.8750–0.8929 through epochs 30–100 (val. loss ≈ 0.27–0.36). Class-wise metrics on the held-out set were: sad 0.94/0.89/0.92 (n = 37), neutral 0.79/0.92/0.85 (n = 25), happy 0.89/0.83/0.86 (n = 30), angry 1.00/1.00/1.00 (n = 28); macro and weighted averages were both 0.91. (Here, n denotes support, i.e., the number of test samples per class.) The best separability appeared for angry; neutral favored recall over precision; happy and neutral exhibited mild overlap consistent with Urdu prosody55.

Mel-spectrogram setting (Uni-/Bi-GRU)

On identical Mel inputs, Bi-GRU outperformed Uni-GRU: 98.75% train / 78.75% val / 87.50% test (losses 0.0599 / 0.7032 / 0.4070) versus 98.33% / 75.00% / 81.25% (losses 0.5739 / 1.4354 / 1.3742)56. Training dynamics were also favorable to Bi-GRU: early stopping at epoch 35 (best validation loss ≈ epoch 15; best validation and test accuracies ≈ epoch 11) versus Uni-GRU at epoch 59 (best validation loss ≈ epoch 31; best accuracies ≈ epoch 30); average epoch times were ≈ 1.0 s (Bi-GRU) and ≈ 1.8 s (Uni-GRU), with total training ≈ 35 s and ≈ 106 s, respectively. A per-class report on the Bi-GRU test split yielded: sad 1.00/0.70/0.83 (n = 27), neutral 0.73/0.79/0.76 (n = 24), happy 0.50/0.64/0.56 (n = 14), angry 0.71/0.80/0.75 (n = 15); macro precision/recall/F1 were 0.73/0.73/0.72; overall accuracy was 0.74 (weighted 0.78/0.74/0.75). The difference between the 87.50% peak test accuracy and the 0.74 aggregated figure reflects distinct reporting protocols (best-epoch peak vs. fixed held-out fold with per-class aggregation). Reporting both is informative: peak results summarize best-case generalization; per-class summaries expose category-level strengths/weaknesses.

Comparative context and remedies

Against the SVM baseline reported on the same URDU corpus, recurrent models on Mel-spectrograms are consistently stronger, with Bi-GRU > Uni-GRU and both > SVM53,56. Residual weaknesses—most notably for happy in the Mel setting (precision 0.50, recall 0.64)—suggest targeted remedies: (i) label-preserving spectro-temporal augmentation (e.g., SpecAugment time/frequency masking, mild time warping), pitch-shift/time-stretch, and noise injection; (ii) class reweighting or focal losses; (iii) calibration and threshold tuning when PR trade-offs are application-critical (e.g., angry detection). Cross-speaker validation and model selection via validation macro-F1 (rather than accuracy alone) should further stabilize per-class behavior.

In our broader framework, CNNs excel at local time–frequency patterns, SNs are advantageous under few-shot constraints, and GRUs add complementary strength by modeling longer prosodic dependencies. Practically, we recommend standardized Mel/cepstral front-ends, GRU stacks with dropout/L2 and learning-rate scheduling, and routine SHAP/ROC diagnostics to balance accuracy with interpretability in real-world HCI deployments53,55,56.

Hybrid CNN–GRU pipelines with Cross-Corpus validation for Urdu SER

To extend the comparative baselines, we evaluated a hybrid pipeline coupling MFCC front-ends with convolutional and recurrent sequence models, and stress-tested generalization via cross-corpus validation on a second Urdu dataset. Using SEMOUR + for primary development and an external URDU set for out-of-distribution checks, we trained CNNs and GRUs and added a RF comparator to benchmark a non-neural classifier under identical features/splits. The goals were twofold: (i) improve accuracy for Urdu SER in low-resource conditions and (ii) verify robustness beyond a single corpus. Results showed a best validation accuracy of 84.92%, with notably strong discrimination for happiness used as a test case—evidence that MFCCs paired with temporal encoders capture affective cues salient to Urdu prosody. Cross-dataset transfer (train on SEMOUR+, evaluate on URDU, and vice versa) indicated that GRU-based models retain discriminative power off-corpus, while the RF offered a transparent baseline that trailed the neural models yet helped diagnose feature sufficiency and overfitting risk. Together, the findings support a division of labor in which CNN blocks extract local time–frequency structure and GRUs consolidate longer-range dynamics—an effective combination under data scarcity and heterogeneous recording conditions57.

Methodology

We implemented a convolutional–recurrent (CRNN) pipeline in Google Colab (GPU-backed), managing data on Drive and processing with openSMILE, Librosa (LabROSA : Laboratory for the Recognition and Organization of Speech and Audio), Numerical Python (NumPy), and Scikit-learn. Two Urdu corpora were used: SEMOUR+ (> 27,000 utterances; eight emotions; 24 speakers with varied accents) and URDU (400 utterances; four emotions—anger, happiness, neutral, sadness—from 38 speakers with spontaneous, naturalistic speech). Audio integrity and sampling rates were verified/standardized. MFCC features were extracted, z-normalized, and padded to a uniform temporal length while preserving sequence order; labels were numerically encoded. Data were partitioned into train/validation/test sets with stratification where applicable. The CRNN comprised convolutional blocks (ReLU, max-pooling, batch normalization) followed by GRU layers and a softmax classifier. Training used Adam with sparse categorical cross-entropy, early stopping, and checkpointing. We evaluated accuracy and examined confusion patterns and ROC behavior. To probe generalization, we ran cross-corpus transfer in addition to in-corpus splits, yielding a reproducible protocol that balances spectral and temporal cues within a single model family57.

Results and discussion (CRNN, SEMOUR+)

The model progressed from an initial validation accuracy 12.58% (val loss 2.0806) at epoch 1 to 84.92% at epoch 30 (val loss 0.4805). Corresponding training accuracy/loss moved from 12.22%/2.1060 (epoch 1) to 89.51%/0.3088 (epoch 30), with intermediate checkpoints: epoch 5 — train 32.98%/1.6858, val 15.86%/4.6865; epoch 10 — train 60.29%/1.0893, val 46.07%/1.4645; epoch 20 — train 82.48%/0.5119, val 73.33%/0.9000. Early stopping at epoch 45 yielded 94.01% training accuracy (loss 0.1819) and 85.65% validation accuracy (loss 0.4953), indicating a modest improvement over epoch 30 but a widening train–validation gap. Error analyses showed consistently strong recognition of happiness and surprise, with residual confusion between fearful vs. sadness and boredom vs. neutral. The pattern suggests mild overfitting that should respond to stronger regularization and augmentation, while confirming that a convolutional–recurrent design effectively balances spectral and temporal cues and generalizes across both SEMOUR + and URDU57.

Exploratory URDU-only benchmarks (MFCC summaries)

Using the URDU corpus (400 utterances; four balanced classes: sad, neutral, happy, angry), we contrasted a recurrent learner with a conventional non-neural baseline. MFCCs (first 13 coefficients) were averaged over time to form per-utterance vectors; features were standardized and labels one-hot encoded. With a 70/30 train–test split, a GRU sequence model (Bi-GRU → dropout → GRU → dense softmax; Adam) achieved 98.93% training accuracy and 98.33% test accuracy, with precision/recall/F1 all > 96% per class. Class-wise ROC AUCs were 1.00 across sad, neutral, happy, and angry. Five-fold cross-validation under the same setup yielded 94.50% mean accuracy (SD 3.02%), offering a more conservative view of deployment-time performance. As a sanity-check baseline, a RF trained on MFCC summaries provided rapid, interpretable benchmarking but did not match sequence-level modeling on this corpus: in a tuned configuration (GridSearchCV, 100 trees) it reached ~ 88.3% validation accuracy (precision/recall/F1 ≈ 88.8/88.7/88.7) and ~ 91.7% test accuracy (precision/recall/F1 ≈ 92.0/91.6/91.7), with ROC AUCs remaining high (≈ 0.98 sad, 0.97 happy, 1.00 angry) and fast inference (~ 0.02 s on the test set). These URDU-only results align with the CRNN findings: compact MFCC summaries can support near-ceiling accuracy when paired with GRU sequence modeling, while cross-validation provides a robust estimate of generalization57.

Implications

Motivating this line of work is the broader challenge of linguistic diversity and resource constraints—limited labeled data, uneven access to compute, and sociolinguistic variability that complicates affect annotation. By explicitly targeting Urdu and validating across corpora, the present study advances methodological inclusivity in SER and points to practical Emotion-AI applications for Urdu HCI (emotion-aware dialogue systems, assistive screening tools, content analytics). The pipeline also serves as a template for other under-resourced languages: MFCC front-ends with CNN–GRU backbones, sanity-checked against a classical classifier, offer a balanced starting point for accuracy and robustness57.

Practical applications, limitations, and future directions

Impact of Practical Applications

The proposed random forest-basedSER model for Urdu has broad applicability across multiple real-world domains:

Customer service automation

The model can be embedded into Urdu-language customer support systems to detect emotional cues during interactions, enabling emotionally adaptive responses that enhance user satisfaction and service quality.

Mental health assessment

In clinical and telehealth environments, the ability to infer emotional states from speech can aid in remote psychological assessments, where vocal indicators serve as vital proxies for emotional well-being.

Emotion-aware educational platforms

Integration with e-learning systems can enable dynamic content delivery based on a learner’s emotional state, thereby improving engagement, comprehension, and academic outcomes.

Human-computer interaction (HCI)

Incorporating emotion recognition into HCI systems facilitates more empathetic and adaptive user interfaces, enhancing usability in contexts where understanding emotional context is critical.

Media and entertainment analytics

SER can support real-time sentiment analysis of audience reactions, providing valuable feedback for content creators, advertisers, and media strategists.

Beyond these direct applications, the study advances SER research in underrepresented languages and promotes broader goals such as:

Linguistic inclusivity

By focusing on Urdu, a low-resource language, this work contributes toward reducing linguistic disparity in AI and expanding culturally sensitive technologies.

Emotion AI development

The findings support the development of emotion-aware AI systems that are both contextually relevant and ethically grounded.

Study limitations

While the experimental results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged:

Feature representation

MFCCs, although widely used in SER, may not fully capture complex prosodic and paralinguistic features critical for nuanced emotional expression. This can impact the model’s ability to differentiate subtle emotional variations.

Dialectal generalization

The datasets employed (SEMOUR + and URDU) include limited dialectal and accent diversity. As a result, the model’s performance may not generalize well across different Urdu-speaking communities with varying linguistic characteristics. This concern is echoed in recent literature addressing speaker variability and dialectal challenges in related speech verification systems39.

Mixed and ambiguous emotions

Human emotions often occur as blended or overlapping states. The current model is trained for discrete emotion categories and is limited in its ability to recognize compound or ambiguous emotional expressions.

These limitations present key opportunities for methodological refinement in future research.

Future work

To address the above limitations and extend the current work, several research directions are proposed:

Dataset enrichment

Given that the present study relies on publicly available Urdu emotional speech datasets (URDU and SEMOUR+), which are externally developed and not specifically tailored to the goals of this research, future work will prioritize the creation of a dedicated, native Urdu emotional speech corpus. Subject to the availability of institutional resources and funding, this dataset will aim to capture a wider range of dialectal variation, spontaneous speech, emotional intensities, and speaker demographics. This direction is motivated by recent progress in low-resource speech synthesis, particularly the development of high-quality native corpora for Kurdish text-to-speech systems, where carefully curated datasets have significantly improved synthesis fidelity and linguistic representation45. Moreover, the construction of such a corpus could support future multimodal SER pipelines, complementing ongoing advances in end-to-end speech synthesis using deep neural networks46.

Advanced model architectures

Investigating more sophisticated neural models—such as transformers, temporal attention networks, or contextual embeddings—may yield improvements in emotion recognition accuracy and context-awareness. The potential of such models is underscored by recent successes in low-resource automatic speech recognition (ASR) for Kurdish using end-to-end transformer architectures41–43. While ASR and SER address different objectives, these architectures offer promising foundations that could be adapted to enhance SER performance in similarly low-resource languages like Urdu.

Cultural and linguistic embedding

Incorporating sociolinguistic variables and cultural markers into the model design will be a key focus to better capture the nuanced emotional expressions prevalent in diverse Urdu-speaking populations.

Multimodal emotion recognition

Building on this speech-based framework, future work will explore multimodal SER systems that integrate additional input modalities such as facial expressions and textual data. This will enable a richer understanding of emotions and support more comprehensive emotion-aware systems in real-world applications.

Cross-linguistic scalability

The methodologies developed in this study may be adapted for other low-resource languages, facilitating comparative studies in cross-lingual SER and promoting broader inclusivity in AI.

Conclusion

This investigation reaffirms the potential of RF classifiers, in line with MFCCs, as a challenging approach for recognizing emotions in Urdu speech. Achieving a noteworthy accuracy, our study not only highlights the practicality of employing RF classifiers for SER in low-resource contexts but also exemplifies the broader utility of machine learning in enhancing HCI. The significance of this work extends beyond its immediate findings, illustrating the profound implications of integrating emotional intelligence into technology, particularly for languages that have traditionally been underrepresented in computational research. As we look to the future, we are excited about the prospects of integrating our approach with advanced deep-learning techniques and multimodal data to elevate the efficacy of SER systems further. This study not only contributes a high-performing, resource-efficient model to the field of SER but also sets the stage for future innovations that will further integrate emotional intelligence into digital interfaces, thus enhancing the richness of HCI.
