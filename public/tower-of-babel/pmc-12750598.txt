Model experiments on application of oral and maxillofacial surgical robot-assisted mandibular tumor resection and reconstruction
Authors: Zhijie Zhao, Ziwei Zhang, Xiaoyan Jiang, Li Lin, Gang Chai, Jiannan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMandibular tumors significantly impact patient health and quality of life. Surgical resection is the primary treatment, often necessitating reconstruction to restore appearance and function. Traditional surgical methods rely heavily on surgeon experience, posing risks such as excessive blood loss and facial paralysis. The advent of digital and robotic-assisted surgical technologies offers improved precision and outcomes.MethodsThe study included five pairs of models with mandibular tumors, divided into a control group (traditional surgery) and an experimental group (robot-assisted surgery). In the experimental group, procedures were performed using a robot-assisted electromagnetic surgical navigation system, following alignment with the preoperative design under robotic guidance. Conversely, the control group underwent traditional surgery, where osteotomy positions were estimated empirically based on the preoperative design. Postoperative CT scans were used to compare the actual outcomes with the preoperative plans. The osteotomy accuracy and reconstruction outcome were evaluated by measuring the positional and angular errors between the preoperatively designed and actual postoperative data.ResultsThe robot-assisted group demonstrated significantly lower osteotomy distance and angle errors compared to the control group. The reconstruction outcomes in the experimental group also showed superior alignment with preoperative plans, indicating better aesthetic and functional results.ConclusionRobotic-assisted surgery for mandibular tumor resection and reconstruction enhances surgical precision and improves reconstructive outcomes compared to traditional methods. Further research with larger sample sizes and clinical settings is necessary to confirm these findings and expand clinical applications.

Background

Mandibular tumors are a group of common diseases that seriously affect human health and quality of life, and surgical resection is the primary way to treat these tumors [1, 2]. The defects caused by surgical resection can affect the patient’s facial appearance and lead to dysfunctions such as swallowing, chewing, speech, etc. More than 50% of surgical patients need to undergo reconstruction of bone tissues or soft tissues [3, 4]. The fibula has the advantages of a relatively continuous and uniform shape, sufficient length and strength, and few complications, which can match the mandibular characteristics well. The fibular free flap (FFF) has become the preferred donor area for mandibular defect reconstruction [5, 6]. The reconstruction not only needs to restore the facial shape and rebuild the patient's appearance but more importantly, to solve the occlusal relationship between the maxilla and mandible and rebuild the patient's physiological functions, such as swallowing and speech. Both appearance and function reconstruction put forward high demands on the accuracy of bone positional localization and postural adjustment.

In the traditional treatment mode, the surgical plan was made based on preoperative imaging to determine the size of the tumor and its relationship with the surrounding tissues and structures. The surgeon then performs the resection and reconstruction during the operation based on the experience. However, the oral-maxillofacial tissues are complex, with many important blood vessels and nerves. Neurovascular damage may result in excessive blood loss or facial paralysis and other complications. The deformity or trauma that leads to facial disfigurement or weakened function will cause a psychological burden to the patient.

With the development of computer-assisted medical technology, digital surgery has begun to be widely used in the medical field [7, 8], which combines computer imaging technology, graphics processing, precision prototyping, and other technologies [9]. Compared with traditional treatment modes, digital surgical technology has the advantages of improving surgical precision, reducing surgical time, mitigating surgical trauma, and better preserving patient functions, which improves the rate of radical treatment of maxillofacial tumors and improves the quality of patients' postoperative survival [10].

In recent years, the rapid development and successful application of robot-assisted surgical technology in the medical field has provided new possibilities for solving some problems in traditional surgery [11–15]. Especially in cranio-maxillofacial surgery, surgical robots are becoming a hotspot of cutting-edge science and technology in the medical field [16–20]. Combined with the assisted navigation technology in clinical applications, it can accomplish target ontology recognition, high-resolution display, fast and efficient tracking, precise alignment of virtual and real images, and real-time calculating. Since oral-maxillofacial surgery is characterized by complex anatomical structure, small surgical field of view, delicate techniques, and many intraoperative interfering factors, the surgical assistant system's depth information perception and real-time registration ability are more demanding than other body parts. Our research team has completed clinical studies of mandibular angle osteotomy and mandibular distraction osteogenesis for hemifacial microsomia using a similar robotic system, and has validated its accuracy and safety [21, 22]. In contrast to previous studies, tumor surgery requires high-precision border resection and functional reconstruction within complex anatomies, which involves the protection of critical neuro vascularity, occlusal function restoration, and contour restoration, and is more difficult than conventional osteotomies or distraction surgeries. This study is the first to apply a robotic system to the entire process of such complex tumor surgery. This study intends to take mandibular resection, fibular flap preparation, and mandibular reconstruction surgery, which are the mainstream surgical procedures for the treatment of mandibular tumors at present, as the research surgical procedures and develop a craniofacial surgical assisting system with independent intellectual property rights, to realize superimposition of surgical plans into the real surgical environment through the virtual-reality matching based on the fixation of the dental registration piece and to guide the surgeon to complete the mandibular osteotomy, fibular segmentation, and mandibular reconstruction surgeries on models. The postoperative three-dimensional spatial position of the mandible is fitted and analyzed with the preoperative virtual surgical plan to verify the system's accuracy and stability and lay the foundation for the clinical application of robotic-assisted oral-maxillofacial surgery.

Methods

Ethics and imaging data

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital (No. SH9H-2020-T267-3). Three-dimensional computer tomography (CT) of five patients with mandibular tumor were included in the study. Data from each patient were used in the experimental group (robotic-assisted group) and control group (traditional surgery group) of the study. In the control group, no assisted navigation was used, and osteotomies and reconstructions were accomplished based on preoperative CT and clinical experience. Surgical robotic system

The robotic system integrates electromagnetic navigation (Aurora, NDI, Canada) and UR5 robot (Universal Robots, Teradyne, USA) with a detachable end-effector at the robotic arm's end. Surgical navigation software was equipped for visualization and human–robot interaction.

The robot hardware system consists of two core components: the robot controller and the robotic arm.UR5 robotic arm has six degrees of freedom, and each joint is equipped with independent motors for driving, and moves to a predefined surgical position according to the control commands from the computer console, thus realizing precise intraoperative positioning. The surgical navigation software interface displays the real-time position of the end-effector during the entire movement. Once the robotic arm reaches the predetermined position, it stops moving and quickly locks the joint to maintain the current precise position. Based on the differentiated needs of different surgical styles for instrument functionality, the device can be quickly configured with navigation components via a modular interface to provide the operator with an adaptation to different anatomical structures.

Surgical navigation software facilitates preoperative surgical path planning. The platform enables closed-loop control of instrument trajectories by integrating robotic arm motion parameters with electromagnetic localization data. The electromagnetic navigation system employs a magnetic field generator that penetrates soft tissues for dynamic 3D coordinate resolution of electromagnetic markers and maintains high spatial resolution in occluded environments. The robotic navigation system also establishes a spatial mapping relationship between the image and the robotic arm, ensuring the accuracy of each operation during the surgical process.

Registration piece manufacture and preoperative design

The image data of the patient's craniofacial and bilateral fibula 3D CT scans (1 mm thickness) were saved in DICOM format and then imported into the Mimics software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) to reconstruct the mandible and the left fibula three-dimensional virtual model. Three-dimensional printing by rapid prototyping technology. Self-designed registration pieces with four small steel balls as registration points were used. The occlusal splint was fitted on the mandibular dentition, and the fibular part was fixed using a customized clamp. A 3D CT scan was again performed and imported into Mimics software for preoperative design. The preoperative design was done by an experienced surgeon collaborating with a skilled imaging software engineer. The mandibular osteotomy line was designed based on the size and invasion of the mandibular tumor. The reconstructed fibular segment was designed based on the mandibular defect after osteotomy, and the reconstruction was simulated and continuously adjusted (Fig. 2A-C). Robotic-assisted mandibular osteotomy and FFF segment

At the beginning of the operation, the registration piece was installed and the model was secured using a fixtor to maintain its relative position to the robot. After starting the robot system and completing the registration, the software automatically completed the path planning of the end-effector based on the imported preoperative design (Fig. 2D-F).

This study utilized a headgear to simulate an intraoral incision scenario. After proper exposure of the operative area was performed, the robot was commanded to start running, followed by the automatic moving of the end-effector to the designed osteotomy line. Postoperative analysis

After the osteotomy, the models underwent 3D CT scans. The imaging data was imported into Geomagic Control X software (3D Systems, Rock Hills, USA) in STL format and automatically fitted to the preoperative design. Ten points were randomly selected on the osteotomy plane, and the average value of its distance error was defined as the distance error of this osteotomy plane. The preoperative design and the postoperative osteotomy plane were extracted separately, and the angle between the two planes was the angular error of that osteotomy plane (Fig. 4A, B). Both mandibular osteotomy planes and proximal and distal surfaces of the fibular segment were analyzed. The mean of the fibular segment osteotomy surface measurements was used to assess the accuracy of the fibular osteotomy in this case.Fig. 4Post-operative analysis. The mandibular sagittal plane (SP) was defined as the plane passing through the menton, pogonion, and the central incisor embrasure. All measurements were taken three times and averaged for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Inc., Armonk, USA). Levene test was used to test the normality and Shapiro–Wilk test for homogeneity of variance. If the data were normally distributed and with homogeneous variance, data dependence was analyzed. Correlated data were compared between the two groups using paired-t tests; otherwise, independent t-tests were used. A non-parametric test was used if the data were not normally distributed or the variance was not homogeneous. Setting α = 0.05 and p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical differences.

Discussion

In mandibular tumor resection and reconstruction surgery, the accuracy of osteotomy position and angle is crucial to the outcome of the entire surgery. Insufficient resection scope may lead to residual tumor, resulting in postoperative metastasis or recurrence and affecting the patient's survival. Excessive resection may result in a mismatch between the length of the mandibular defect and the reconstructed fibula, causing the reconstruction result to be inconsistent with the expectation and affecting the postoperative facial appearance, occlusal stability, as well as the coordination of the facial nerves and muscles, and the position and functional stability of the temporomandibular joint.

Nowadays, in oral-maxillofacial surgery, digital surgical technology has gradually become routine diagnostic and therapeutic aids, of which surgical navigation and surgical guides are the most frequently used technologies [23–25]. However, there are still some shortcomings in the commonly used digital surgical techniques. 3D surgical guides are usually made by third-party companies, and the production process is time-consuming and unsuitable for acute trauma patients [26]. After the surgical plan is formulated and the guide plate is printed, if there is a change in the condition during the operation, the surgical plan cannot be adjusted according to the actual situation(e.g., if intraoperative tumor border extension or anatomical variations are detected) may result in forced conversion of the procedure to freehand operation, increasing the risk of error [27]. In addition, for the guide to fit in the target area during surgery, it is necessary to do a more extensive range of exposure of the surgical area, indirectly increasing the surgical trauma. The design and manufacture of the surgical guide did not consider the soft tissue interference, and sometimes, the presence of soft tissues on the bone surface during the actual surgery would affect the closeness between the guide and the bone surface, thus affecting the surgical precision.

Surgical robots are presenting promising prospects due to stability and precision. In the field of craniomaxillofacial surgery, this technique has been successfully applied to dental implant placement and orthopaedic surgeries, and its clinical efficacy has been preliminarily demonstrated [28, 29]. Available data show that typical electromagnetic navigation systems can achieve millimeter-level positioning accuracy. Nova et al. [30] and Berger et al. [31] used NDI's Aurora electromagnetic navigation system to accurately localize the condylar region after mandibular distraction surgery. This study demonstrated that the system has excellent performance in controlling the error of condylar translation and rotation, and can accurately determine the spatial orientation of the bone segments without complex intraoperative alignment, which is of great value in improving the accuracy of orthognathic surgery. Another study explored the practical effect of electromagnetic navigation in mandibular osteotomy positioning, which was evaluated by tracking the position of the bone block in real time and comparing it with the deviation of the virtual marker point. In 11 clinical cases, the anatomical marker point error was 3.2 ± 1.1 mm, and the osteotomy navigation point error was 2.6 ± 1.5 mm, which further verified the reliability of the technique [29]. Currently, the application of electromagnetic navigation craniomaxillofacial surgical robots in the field of mandibular tumor osteotomy is still limited. Notably, Tsang et al. successfully applied the technique to the resection of nasopharyngeal tumors and parapharyngeal nerve sheath tumors by innovatively integrating the Fiagon navigation system with the da Vinci surgical robot [32]. Its research focus reveals the core value of this system in the identification of key anatomical structures such as the internal carotid artery. This technology fusion not only effectively circumvents the inherent shortcomings of traditional optical navigation, but also realizes the double enhancement of precision and safety in skull base surgery, which provides a new direction for the innovation of craniomaxillofacial surgical diagnostic and treatment modes. Our research data show that our team's electromagnetic navigation craniomaxillofacial surgical robot can achieve sub-millimeter osteotomy accuracy on at least one side of the osteotomy surface in chin or body/ramus tumor model tests. Compared with similar magnetic navigation devices reported in the literature, this system demonstrated superior positioning performance, which not only validates the technical advantages of electromagnetic navigation in maxillofacial surgery, but also marks a substantial breakthrough in tumor osteotomy navigation technology.

The proposed robotic system is significantly better than the traditional 3D guide plate technology in terms of intraoperative flexibility, real-time adaptability and trauma control. The real-time image fusion and dynamic path replanning algorithms allow for immediate intraoperative correction of the osteotomy range and reconstruction plan, avoiding the limitations of “one-time use” of guides. With high-precision navigation and robotic end effectors, the robot only needs to minimize bone exposure, thus reducing the risk of postoperative infection and scarring. However, the limitations of this technology include higher initial deployment costs than conventional guides, which may limit its popularity in resource-limited areas; extreme anatomical variations (e.g., loss of intraoperative navigation markers due to severe bone destruction) still require manual intervention by the operator, and further optimization of the autonomous error correction capability is needed in the future.

The mean distance deviation and the mean angle deviation of the osteotomy planes in the experimental group were similar to the surgical precision of Chen et al., who studied surgical guide-assisted resection of mandibular lesions and fibular reconstruction [33]. The deviations may be directly related to the osteotomy saw's jittering, the saw blade's bending, and the presence of a blind spot in the field of view that affects the observation of the osteotomy line. In addition, the main reason for the distance deviation of the fibula also stems from the slender, smooth fibula itself having fewer surface features, which is not conducive to the stable and reliable anchoring of the system to the solid after registration. In the results, it can be observed that the reconstruction error was amplified, of which the critical reason may be the lack of guidance for reconstruction. Future studies can further incorporate reconstruction navigation into the experiment to guide the position and angle of FFF placement to achieve more accurate reconstruction [34].

For robotic-assisted surgery, pre-surgical preparation is more complex and time-consuming than for conventional surgery. Preparation and setup of robotic equipment may also increase the overall surgical lead time, requiring larger operating room space to accommodate the robotic equipment and other related technologies. This may place higher space and layout requirements on existing medical facilities. Follow-up studies will continue efforts to further reduce device interference and optimize robotic algorithmic workflows for smoother execution of path-planning solutions and to achieve more user-friendly software operation and a more flexible overall appearance. In this study, the registration was done by non-invasive occlusal fixation, and the mandibular fixation was done by minimally invasive fixation in the vicinity of the tumor margins; to address the anatomical constraints of the mandible, the robotic arm end-tools can access the deep operative field through small incisions to avoid extensive dissection. Nonetheless, for extremely complex tumor cases, extensive exposure is still unavoidable. With the development of technology, especially the advancement of artificial intelligence algorithms and navigation recognition and localization techniques, the future is expected to see the development of alignment and localization techniques based on appropriate soft tissue recognition markers. There are some limitations in this study. For example, the research objects used were models with limited samples. More sufficient evidence is needed in the future to confirm the accuracy and stability of robotic-assisted oral-maxillofacial surgery. In addition, the source and segmentation of preoperative CT data, the possibility of errors in the reconstruction of 3D virtual models, and the failure of surgical planning to meet the actual surgery requirements may all affect our judgment of the accuracy. In addition, for patients with malignant tumors with rapid progression of disease, the plan formulated using preoperative imaging data may differ from the actual situation during surgery, and we hope that this problem can be overcome in the future by formulating a navigation using intraoperative real-time registration.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the feasibility and advantages of electromagnetic navigation robot-assisted technique in mandibular tumor resection and reconstruction surgery. The results showed that the robot-assisted group had significantly better distance and angular accuracy of the osteotomy compared with the conventional surgical approach. The reconstruction results in the experimental group better agreed with the preoperative design. These findings suggest that applying robot-assisted navigation technology to craniomaxillofacial surgery can significantly improve surgical precision and postoperative outcomes, providing new possibilities for clinical utilization. Future studies with larger sample sizes and clinical scenarios are needed to validate these results further and optimize the technique for expanded applications.
