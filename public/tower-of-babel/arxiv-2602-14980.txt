Sub-part-per-trillion test of the Standard Model with atomic hydrogen
Vitaly Wirthl, Arthur Matveev, Alexey Grinin, Randolf Pohl, Theodor W. Hänsch, Thomas Udem

Abstract
Quantum electrodynamics (QED), the first relativistic quantum field theory, describes light–matter interactions at a fundamental level and is one of the pillars of the Standard Model (SM). Through QED’s extraordinary precision, the SM predicts the energy levels of simple systems such as the hydrogen atom with up to 13 significant digits [ 1 ] , making hydrogen spectroscopy an ideal testbed. The consistency of physical constants extracted from different transitions in hydrogen using QED, such as the proton charge radius r p r_{\mathrm{p}} , constitutes a test of the theory. However, values of r p r_{\mathrm{p}} from recent measurements [ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] of atomic hydrogen are partly discrepant with each other and with a more precise value from spectroscopy of muonic hydrogen [ 8 , 9 ] . This prevents a test of QED at the level of experimental uncertainties. Here, we present a measurement of the 2S-6P transition in atomic hydrogen with sufficient precision to distinguish between the discrepant values of r p r_{\mathrm{p}} and enable rigorous testing of QED and the SM overall. Our result ν 2S-6P = 730 , 690 , 248 , 610.79 ​ ( 48 ) ​ kHz \nu_{\text{2S-6P}}=$730,690,248,610.79$($48$)\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$ gives a value of r p = 0.8406 ​ ( 15 ) ​ fm r_{\mathrm{p}}=$0.8406$($15$)\,$\mathrm{f}\mathrm{m}$ at least 2.5 2.5 -fold more precise than from other atomic hydrogen determinations, and in excellent agreement with the muonic value. The SM prediction of the transition frequency ( 730 , 690 , 248 , 610.79 730,690,248,610.79 ( 23 23 ) kHz \mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z} ) is in excellent agreement with our result, testing the SM to 0.7 parts per trillion and, specifically, bound-state QED corrections to 0.5 parts per million, their most precise test to date.

Principle of the measurement

2S-6P transition

We study the 2S-6P transition in a cryogenic beam of hydrogen atoms using Doppler-free one-photon laser spectroscopy.
While the transition has been previously observed with laser spectroscopy
[
19
,
20
]
, this work presents a significant improvement.
Using a linearly-polarized, 410-nm spectroscopy laser, we alternately probe two dipole-allowed transitions from the metastable initial
\mathrm{2S}_{1/2}^{F=0}\text{, }m_{F}\!=\!0
level: the
\text{2S-6P}_{1/2}
transition to the
\mathrm{6P}_{1/2}^{F=1}\text{, }m_{F}\!=\!0
level and the
\text{2S-6P}_{3/2}
transition to the
\mathrm{6P}_{3/2}^{F=1}\text{, }m_{F}\!=\!0
level, as shown in
Fig.
˜
2
a (
F
: total angular momentum quantum number,
m_{F}
: magnetic quantum number).
The excited 6P levels rapidly decay, directly or through cascades, to the 1S and 2S manifolds, resulting in a
\Gamma=$3.90\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
natural transition linewidth.
These decays, predominantly the direct Lyman-
\epsilon
decay to the 1S manifold, are the experimental signal, and the fluorescence line shape is observed in line scans by recording this signal at different spectroscopy laser detunings.
A fraction of
\gamma_{\text{ei}}/\Gamma=$3.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\char 37\relax}\text{/}$
or
7.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\char 37\relax}\text{/}
of decays from the excited level lead back to the initial 2S level for the
\text{2S-6P}_{1/2}
or
\text{2S-6P}_{3/2}
transitions, respectively.
Importantly, quantum interference (QI) between excitation–decay paths that go through either excited level but lead to the same final level can cause substantial distortions of the fluorescence line shape.
The associated line shifts are on the order of
\Gamma^{2}/\Delta\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{FS}}}(\mathrm{6P})\sim\Gamma/100

[
21
,
2
,
22
]
, where
\Delta\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{FS}}}(\mathrm{6P})\approx$405\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
is the 6P fine-structure splitting between the excited levels.
Because the magnitude and sign of the distortions depend on the detection direction (relative to the laser polarization), we here use a large detection solid angle and a magic polarization angle to strongly suppress the shift
[
22
]
.

Light force shift

Just as light waves diffract on a periodic structure, matter waves can diffract on the periodic structure formed by a standing wave of light, as first predicted for electrons by Kapitza and Dirac
[
27
]
.
Here, a similar diffraction can occur as the atoms, which may be understood as matter waves, cross the standing intensity wave formed by the counter-propagating spectroscopy laser beams used to suppress the first-order Doppler shift (see
Fig.
˜
2
b).
This effect, along with scattering on the standing wave, leads to a distortion of the fluorescence line shape and a line shift (LFS).
While such shifts have been observed in other experiments employing standing waves
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, the behavior and size of the shift and the necessary theoretical treatment are highly dependent on the exact experimental conditions.
A traveling wave can be used to avoid the LFS
[
31
]
, but this approach has not yet demonstrated the level of Doppler shift suppression required here.

Diffraction of matter waves, as for light waves, requires some degree of spatial coherence, with diffraction becoming important when the atoms’ transverse coherence length
l_{\mathrm{c,t}}
along the standing wave is comparable to its periodicity of
\lambda/2=$205\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
.
Treating the cryogenic nozzle as a thermal source of atoms, we find
l_{\mathrm{c,t}}
to be
\lambda_{\mathrm{dB,th}}=$0.8\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$

[
32
]
and therefore much smaller than
\lambda/2
, where
\lambda_{\mathrm{dB,th}}=\sqrt{h^{2}k_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{B}}}T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{N}}}/2\pi m_{\mathrm{H}}}
is the thermal de Broglie wavelength (
m_{\mathrm{H}}
: hydrogen mass,
k_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{B}}}
: Boltzmann constant).
However, as is well-known from the van Cittert–Zernike theorem
[
33
]
, the transverse coherence length is enhanced by propagation, as seen in matter wave interference of large molecules from a thermal source
[
34
]
.
At a distance
L
from the nozzle, this results in

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{c,t}}\approx(L/r_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}})\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}/\pi

(2)

for atoms with a speed
v
and de Broglie wavelength of
\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}=h/m_{\mathrm{H}}v
.
For
v=$200\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
, the mean speed of atoms probed in the experiment,
l_{\mathrm{c,t}}
is
129\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
at the standing wave, and hence comparable to its periodicity.
We therefore need to model the atoms as partially coherent matter waves, that is, the atoms are partially delocalized over the standing wave, unlike the localized description
[
35
]
valid for different experimental conditions
[
28
,
29
]
.

We use the Wigner function
[
36
]
to find a quantum mechanical description of the atomic beam, quantizing the motion along the standing wave while treating other directions classically.
This results in a comparable value of
l_{\mathrm{c,t}}
as estimated above
[
23
]
.
The interaction of the atoms with the standing wave can be described in the combined basis of internal energy levels and external momenta along the standing wave (see Supplementary Methods).
The absorption of a photon from the forward- (
+
) or backward-traveling (
-
) beam excites atoms from the 2S to the 6P level and changes their momentum along the beams by
\pm\hbar K_{\mathrm{L}}
(wavenumber
K_{\mathrm{L}}=2\pi/\lambda
;
\hbar=h/2\pi
).
Likewise, stimulated emission into either beam returns atoms to the 2S level and changes their momentum by
\mp\hbar K_{\mathrm{L}}
.
This corresponds to a change in velocity by the recoil velocity,
v_{\mathrm{rec}}=\hbar K_{\mathrm{L}}/m_{\mathrm{H}}\approx$0.97\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
, comparable to the typical transverse velocity
v_{x}
of the atoms.
Since the 6P level predominantly spontaneously decays to the 1S ground level, from which there is no re-excitation while the signal is recorded, the maximum relevant momentum change is here
\pm 4\hbar k
.

The small fraction
\gamma_{\text{ei}}/\Gamma
of the spontaneous decays back to the initial 2S level leads to a random momentum change along the direction of the standing wave, as the direction of the emitted photon is randomly distributed
[
37
]
.
The associated Doppler shift of such a decay (at most twice the recoil shift of
\Delta\nu_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{rec}}}=$1176.03\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
) is below the natural linewidth
\Gamma
, and therefore the atom can be re-excited to the 6P level and ultimately contribute to the signal.
However, since
\gamma_{\text{ei}}/\Gamma\ll 1
, only at most one such back decay is relevant here.

Methods

Data acquisition

Here we give additional relevant details on the experimental scheme and apparatus (both described in detail in
[
23
]
).
The run time of the cryogenic atomic beam is limited to freezing cycles (FCs) of approximately two hours by the accumulation of frozen (molecular) hydrogen inside the nozzle, which is removed by heating the nozzle to room temperature.
The vacuum (
2\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}
, dominated by molecular hydrogen) inside the 2S-6P spectroscopy region is maintained by differential pumping with a cryopump to minimize pressure shifts
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
, with the apparatus’ temperature allowed to equilibrate on each measurement day before collecting data.

The power of the linearly-polarized, 243-nm 1S-2S preparation laser
[
53
,
54
]
is resonantly enhanced in an in-vacuum, standing-wave cavity to
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{1S-2S}}}\approx$1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}$
per direction (
297\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}\text{/}

1/e^{2}
intensity waist radius).
The observed 1S-2S transition linewidth is approximately
3\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
(FWHM; atomic detuning, as opposed to laser detuning), limited by single-photon ionization of the 2S level
[
55
]
.
Therefore, the
\mathrm{2S}_{1/2}^{F=1}
levels are only populated by Doppler-sensitive two-photon excitation, leading to a population of approximately
7\text{\times}{10}^{-7}
in each sublevel relative to the population in the
\mathrm{2S}_{1/2}^{F=0}
level.
The detuning of the preparation laser is set multiple times per FC by observing the 1S-2S transition.
An equal-slit-width optical chopper running at
160\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
periodically blocks the preparation laser (which sets delay time
\tau=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}\text{/}$
) to enable the velocity-resolved detection.
The channel electron multipliers (CEMs) are switched off with a fast high-voltage switch while the preparation laser is unblocked to prevent saturation from scattered 243-nm light.

By using a linearly-polarized 2S-6P spectroscopy laser
[
53
,
54
]
and the
\mathrm{2S}_{1/2}^{F=0}
level as initial level, only transitions to the
\mathrm{6P}_{1/2}^{F=1}
hyperfine level (
\text{2S-6P}_{1/2}
transition) and the
\mathrm{6P}_{3/2}^{F=1}
hyperfine level (
\text{2S-6P}_{3/2}
transition) are dipole-allowed, while the excitation of the
\mathrm{6P}_{1/2}^{F=0}
and
\mathrm{6P}_{3/2}^{F=2}
levels is prevented by angular momentum conservation (see
Fig.
˜
2
a).
The line strengths (
\propto\mu^{2}
, where
\mu
is the dipole moment) of the
\text{2S-6P}_{1/2}
and
\text{2S-6P}_{3/2}
transitions have a 1:2 ratio.
We use spectroscopy laser powers
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{2S-6P}}}
with a ratio of 2:1 for the two transitions to keep their Rabi frequencies

Voigt and Voigt doublet line shapes

A Voigt line shape
[
58
,
23
]
is the convolution of a Lorentzian line shape (with FWHM (full width at half maximum) linewidth
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{L}}}
) and a Gaussian line shape (with FWHM linewidth
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{G}}}
).
It has a combined FWHM linewidth
\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{F}}}\approx 0.5346\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{L}}}+\sqrt{0.2166\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{L}}}^{2}+\Gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{G}}}^{2}}

[
59
]
and amplitude
A
, and is centered on the resonance frequency
\nu_{0}
.
A constant offset
y_{0}
is added to account for the experimentally observed signal background, resulting in
5
free parameters.

The Voigt doublet line shape is here defined as the sum of two Voigt line shapes with generally different resonance frequencies
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
and amplitudes
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, but equal Lorentzian and Gaussian linewidths
[
23
]
(and a constant offset
y_{0}
), resulting in
7
free parameters.
Its resonance frequency is the center of mass of the constituent line shapes, i.e.,
\nu_{0}=(A_{1}\nu_{1}+A_{2}\nu_{2})/(A_{1}+A_{2})
.

Data analysis

The resonance frequency
\nu_{0}
of each velocity group and detector of each line scan is determined by least-square fitting Voigt line shapes to the signal (for data with
\alpha_{0}=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}$

For data with
\alpha_{0}\neq$0\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}$
(data groups G13 and G14), where the line can split into two Doppler components, the Voigt doublet line shape is used instead.
For the fits, we assume the uncertainty on the signal
y_{j}

\sqrt{y_{j}}
(since

We use reduced chi-squared
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}=\chi^{2}/k
(with
k
degrees of freedom) as a measure of the goodness of fit.
The average
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}
is expected to be one if the fitted line shapes exactly match the experimental line shapes and if the signal fluctuations are fully described by shot noise.
We first discuss the Voigt fits (
k=$25$
), where we find
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}
to be substantially larger than one (up to
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}\approx$4.6$
at our highest spectroscopy laser power) for all but the slowest velocity groups.
Only for those velocity groups, which have a comparatively lower signal and Doppler broadening, does
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}
approach one (
\chi^{2}_{\text{red}}\approx$1.1$
for
\tau_{16}

We identify two distinct contributions to the increased

.
First, there are small model deviations between the fitted and experimental line shapes, caused by non-Gaussian (and non-Lorentzian) broadening and saturation effects not included in the former, and clearly observable in the fit residuals (up to
\approx 2\text{\,}\mathrm{\char 37\relax}\text{/}
deviation; see black circles at top of
Fig.
˜
1

Our simulated line shapes (both LFS and QI simulations) include these effects and consequently show much better agreement with the experimental line shapes (see dashed and solid lines in
Fig.

We use this to determine the effect of the deviations on

by repeating the line shape fits using simulated data instead of experimental data.
In this Monte Carlo simulation, the appropriate line shape simulation is scaled and offset to match the experimental data of each line scan, and then shot noise is added to the simulated signal.
The simulated

70\text{\,}\mathrm{\char 37\relax}\text{/}
of the excess (i.e.,

dc-Stark shift

Static stray electric fields in the 2S-6P spectroscopy region can lead to a dc-Stark shift of the observed transition frequency.
Here, the dc-Stark shift
\Delta\nu_{\mathrm{dc}}
is well described as quadratic in strength
E=|\bm{E}|
of the electric field
\bm{E}
within all relevant experimental regimes, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\nu_{\mathrm{dc}}=\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}E^{2},

(21)

where
\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}
is the applicable quadratic dc-Stark shift coefficient.
We distinguish two experimentally relevant field strength regimes: the stray-field regime (
E<$1\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$
), which covers the range of stray electric fields present in the experiment, and the bias-field regime (
E=$10\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}45\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$
), which covers the range of applied bias fields used to determine the stray electric fields.
Importantly, while
Eq.
˜
21
is found to approximately hold in either regime, the coefficient
\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}
may differ, as is the case for the
\text{2S-6P}_{3/2}
transition (see
Sub-part-per-trillion test of the Standard Model

with atomic hydrogen
\endNoHyper
in the Supplementary Methods).
The quadratic behavior arises because the energy levels contributing to the net shift are well-separated in energy from the perturbed level in either of our regimes.
However, the shift of the involved levels between the regimes can lead to substantially different energy separations and thereby different values of
\beta_{\mathrm{dc}}
.

The Faraday cage surrounding the spectroscopy region (see
Fig.
˜
2
b) shields it from external electric fields, including those used to draw the photoelectrons to the channel electron multipliers (whose input surfaces are held at
270\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
; see Section 4.6.2 of
[
23
]
).
The colloidal graphite coating on all surfaces of the Faraday cage suppresses stray electric fields from the surfaces themselves (from charged oxide layers, contact potentials from dissimilar conductors, or local changes in the work function) by, ideally, forming a uniform conductive layer with a uniform work function (see Section 4.6.1 of
[
23
]
).
However, effects such as imperfect shielding of external fields, imperfect graphite coating, or temperature gradients leading to thermoelectric voltages or gradients in the work function can prevent the complete suppression of stray fields.

To address this, we measure the stray electric field by applying voltages to the six electrodes forming the Faraday cage (meshes at the top and bottom and four equal-sized segments of the cylinder wall) and using the atoms themselves as field sensors (see Section 4.6.7 of
[
23
]
), similar to approaches in
[
5
,
69
]
.
Equal and opposite bias voltages are applied to opposing electrodes to create a bias electric field
\bm{E}=E_{i}\hat{\bm{i}}
with strength
|E_{i}|
=
10\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}45\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}
along the given direction (

, as defined in
Fig.
˜
2
b).
By measuring the shifted 2S-6P transition frequency
\nu_{\mathrm{e}}(E_{i})
from a fit to the fluorescence line shape of multiple line scans with opposite-polarity values of
E_{i}
and utilizing the quadratic dependence
\nu_{\mathrm{e}}(E_{i})=\beta_{\mathrm{dc},i}(E_{i}-\Delta E_{i})^{2}+\nu_{\mathrm{e}}(E_{i}=$0\text{\,}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$)

\Delta E_{i}
along the given direction.
This measurement also yields experimental values of
\beta_{\mathrm{dc},i}
at the bias field strengths for each transition.
