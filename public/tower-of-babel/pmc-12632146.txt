Multifunctional nanoagonist enhances photodynamic therapy-driven in situ cancer vaccination by inhibiting tumor thrombosis
Authors: Cheng Liu, Xinyu Wang, Mingzhi Wang, Ran Ji, Shu Xia, Liang Chen, Shao Q Yao, Zhichun Gu, Chao Fang, Xiao Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConverting autologous tumors into therapeutic cancer vaccines represents an attractive strategy for achieving personalized antitumor immunity. However, the antitumor immune response is significantly compromised by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Herein, we developed a polymersomal nanoagonist (cDVPMA) to potentiate photodynamic therapy (PDT)-driven in situ cancer vaccination (ISCV) by inhibiting intratumoral thrombosis. cDVPMA was constructed by encapsulating the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist 2′3′-cGAMP in the aqueous core of a tertiary ammonium group-containing polymersome, while embedding both the photosensitizer verteporfin-phospholipid (VL) and thrombin inhibitor dabigatran etexilate within the hydrophobic layer. Upon tumor accumulation, cDVPMA swells in response to the acidic TME, promoting controlled drug release. VL-mediated PDT not only kills cancer cells but also triggers immunogenic cancer cell death and enhances tumor antigen exposure, thus achieving ISCV by synergizing with 2′3′-cGAMP-mediated STING activation. Dabigatran etexilate effectively inhibits tumor thrombosis, thereby restoring tumor microcirculation, alleviating hypoxia, and reducing the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. In a 4T1 mouse breast cancer model, cDVPMA combined with near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation elicited robust antitumor immunity, significantly suppressing primary tumor growth and metastasis, while establishing durable immune memory that prevented tumor recurrence. Introduction

Therapeutic cancer vaccines (TCVs) capable of stimulating adaptive immune system against tumor-specific antigens offer a promising strategy for the sustained inhibition of tumor growth and metastasis [1, 2]. Typically, TCVs involve the exogenous administration of tumor antigens, often combined with immune adjuvants to activate dendritic cells (DCs), or the direct administration of DCs themselves. In addition to sipuleucel-T, the cell-based cancer vaccine approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for prostate cancer treatment, several protein- and peptide-based TCVs have entered clinical research [3, 4]. However, the development and application of exogenous tumor antigen-based TCVs are hindered by tumor antigen heterogeneity and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [5, 6]. Moreover, the identification and selection of suitable tumor-associated proteins and peptides require complex and costly procedures, involving the collection and processing of cancer cells [7]. Promoting tumor antigen release while stimulating antitumor immune responses within tumor tissues offers an alternative approach for achieving in situ cancer vaccination (ISCV). Compared to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which indiscriminately kill both tumor and normal cells, photodynamic therapy (PDT), a noninvasive treatment regimen, provides several advantages for cancer treatment, including enhanced spatiotemporal selectivity and reduced systemic toxicity [8]. PDT has demonstrated its ability to induce tumor cell apoptosis and trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) in cancer cells [9–11]. This process promotes the release of various tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), calreticulin (CRT), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [12, 13]. However, PDT alone exhibits limited efficacy in eliciting robust antitumor immunity, highlighting the need to incorporate immunoadjuvants to enhance the efficacy of cancer vaccines [14, 15]. Activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including type I interferons (IFN-I) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These cytokines not only facilitate the maturation of DCs and enhance tumor antigen presentation, but also recruit cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) to the TME [16].

In solid tumors, overexpression of tissue factor (TF) by cancer cells, along with increased vascular permeability and plasma extravasation, triggers a hypercoagulable state characterized by both intravascular and extravascular coagulation [17, 18].Additionally, PDT disrupts tumor vasculature and promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines, which further aggravate the coagulation cascade and promote the development of an immunosuppressive TME [19]. Reduced tumor microcirculation caused by tumor thrombus not only impairs drug delivery to tumor tissue but also hinders CTL infiltration [20]. In addition, PDT combined with poor tumor vascular perfusion exacerbates tumor hypoxia, which in turn activates immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby facilitating the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines [18]. It has been demonstrated that thrombin contributes to immune evasion by cleaving glycoprotein A-repeats predominant sequence (GARP) on activated platelets, promoting the release of mature transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) [21].This not only inhibits the proliferation and cytotoxic activity of CTLs but also induces Foxp3 expression in CD4+T cells, promoting their differentiation into immunosuppressive Tregs.

Herein, we developed a pH-responsive polymersomal nanoagonist (cDVPMA) capable of alleviating intratumoral thrombosis to create an immune-supportive TME and enhance PDT-driven ISCV. The polymeric vesicles (PMA), composed of the copolymer methoxyl-poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly (2-(pentamethyleneimino) ethyl methacrylate) (Meo-PEG-b-PPMEMA), were utilized as nanocarriers due to their pH-responsive drug delivery properties. To construct cDVPMA, the photosensitizer verteporfin-phospholipid (VL) and the thrombin inhibitor dabigatran etexilate were co-encapsulated within the hydrophobic layer of PMA, while the STING agonist 2′3′-cGAMP was incorporated into its aqueous core (Scheme 1A). Following intravenous injection, cDVPMA passively accumulates in tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. In addition, the swelling of cDVPMA in response to the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5–6.8) facilitates the release of VL, 2′3′-cGAMP, and dabigatran etexilate. Singlet oxygen (¹O₂) generated by VL under near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation kills cancer cells and simultaneously induces immunogenic cancer cell death, thereby promoting the release of whole tumor antigens and facilitating ISCV through the synergistic activation of the STING pathway by 2′3′-cGAMP (Scheme 1B). Dabigatran etexilate in cDVPMA inhibits tumor thrombosis, thereby restoring tumor vascular perfusion and enhancing intratumoral CTL infiltration. Notably, the improved perfusion also alleviates tumor immune suppression by mitigating hypoxia and reducing thrombin-mediated release of immunosuppressive cytokines (Scheme 1B). In an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model, cDVPMA treatment elicited robust antitumor immune responses that not only induced regression of the primary tumor and prolonged survival in mice, but also established durable immune memory capable of effectively suppressing tumor rechallenge.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of cDVPMA

The amphiphilic block copolymer PEG-b-PPMEMA (PMA, pKa ∼6.89) was synthesized via reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization and characterized by ¹H NMR (Figure S1) [22]. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis showed that the number-average molecular weight (Mn) of PMA was approximately 28.7 kDa, with a polymer dispersity index (Mw/Mn) of 1.2 (Figure S2). Using an emulsion method, PMA self-assembled into polymeric vesicles (cDVPMA), encapsulating VL and dabigatran etexilate in the hydrophobic bilayer, while 2′3′-cGAMP was loaded into the aqueous core. Using the solvent-exchange method, PMA self-assembled into polymeric vesicles (cDVPMA) with a bilayer membrane structure. The lipophilic drugs VL and dabigatran etexilate were preferentially embedded in the hydrophobic bilayer via hydrophobic interactions, further stabilizing the vesicle structure. In contrast, the hydrophilic drug 2′3′-cGAMP was entrapped within the aqueous core during vesicle formation. To confirm the advantages of cDVPMA, the control nanoparticle (cVPMA) was prepared similarly, excluding dabigatran etexilate during the preparation. Monotherapy nanoparticles including cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA, were also prepared by individually encapsulating 2′3′-cGAMP, dabigatran etexilate, and VL in PMA-based polymersomes. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to determine the hydrodynamic diameter of cDVPMA and control nanomedicines (Figure S3A). The average hydrodynamic diameter of cDVPMA was determined to be 65.4 ± 5.3 nm, and the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image showed that cDVPMA exhibits a circular vesicle morphology (Fig. 1A). The polydispersity index (PDI) of cDVPMA was around 0.2, suggesting a narrow size distribution (Figure S3B). cDVPMA demonstrated good colloidal stability, as evidenced by minimal changes in particle size and PDI in PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) over 24 h at both 4 °C and 37 °C (Figure S4). Notably, the hydrodynamic size of cDVPMA increased to 72.3 ± 1.2 nm when the pH shifted from 7.4 to 6.8, likely due to the protonation of the tertiary amine groups (Fig. 1B). Both DLS and TEM analyses showed that cDVPMA disassembled under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) (Fig. 1C). Similarly, the PDI of cDVPMA was also increased with decreasing pH (Figure S5). The zeta potential of cDVPMA in PBS at pH 7.4 was − 9.1 ± 3.9 mV, which increased to −4.4 ± 2.6 mV at pH 6.5 and further to + 15.8 ± 2 mV at pH 5.5 (Fig. 1D). The reversal of surface charge is likely caused by protonation of the tertiary amino groups in the PEG-b-PPMEMA polymer under acidic microenvironments.

The encapsulation efficiencies (EE%) of cDVPMA were determined to be 92.8% for 2′3′-cGAMP, 9.52% for dabigatran etexilate, and 49.7% for VL, with corresponding drug loading capacities of 15.5%, 1.61%, and 4.17%, respectively. The pH-responsive drug release profile of cDVPMA was evaluated under different pH conditions (7.4, 6.8, and 5.5). Approximately 31.4% of dabigatran etexilate was released from cDVPMA at pH 7.4 after 24 h of incubation, increasing to 86.6% at pH 6.8 and 93.0% at pH 5.5 (Fig. 1E). The accelerated release of dabigatran etexilate under acidic conditions is driven by polymer swelling and drug protonation. Similarly, 2′3′-cGAMP exhibited release rates of 34.2%, 69.0%, and 95.9% at pH 7.4, 6.8, and 5.5, respectively (Fig. 1F). The release rate of VL from cDVPMA was 36.5% at pH 7.4, increasing to 61.5% at pH 6.8 and 82.8% at pH 5.5 (Fig. 1G). Singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) was used as a fluorescence probe to examine singlet oxygen (1O₂) generation by cDVPMA. Upon NIR laser irradiation, significant [1]O₂ production was observed in both the cDVPMA and cVPMA groups in an irradiation time- (Fig. 1H) and concentration-dependent manner (Figure S6). To evaluate the in vitro anticoagulant activity, different formulations (cPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, and cDVPMA) were pre-incubated in an acidic environment (PBS, pH 6.8) for 6 h, followed by incubation with fresh mouse blood for 2 h at 37 °C. PBS was used as a negative control, while heparin and free dabigatran etexilate were used as positive controls. Thrombus weight in the cDVPMA-treated group was significantly lower than that in the cVPMA and cPMA groups, and was comparable to that in the heparin and free dabigatran etexilate groups (Fig. 1I).

In vitro ICD induction

The cellular uptake of cDVPMA was evaluated using flow cytometry, based on the fluorescence signal of VL. As expected, a concentration-dependent internalization of cDVPMA by 4T1 cells was observed, as indicated by the progressive increase in VL fluorescence intensity per cell over a 2-hour incubation period (Figure S7). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images (Figure S8) and flow cytometry analysis (Figure S9) revealed no significant difference in the internalization of cVPMA and cDVPMA by 4T1 cells. The generation of 1O₂ in 4T1 cells following different treatments was examined using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a fluorescence probe. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, ROS levels in 4T1 cells treated with cVPMA and cDVPMA were enhanced under (denoted as “+”) NIR laser irradiation. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that both cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) treatment significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels in 4T1 cells (Fig. 2C and D). CCK-8 analysis showed that the viability of 4T1 cells treated with cVPMA and cDVPMA was approximately 76.8% and 75.6%, respectively, at a VL concentration of 1 µM (Fig. 2E). In contrast, treatment with cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) significantly inhibited 4T1 cell growth, resulting in cell viabilities of 19.4% and 23.4%, respectively. The IC₅₀ values of cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) against 4T1 cells were 0.64 µM and 0.68 µM, respectively. Notably, minimal cytotoxicity was observed in 4T1 cells treated with PMA, cPMA, and DPMA (Figure S10). The anticancer effects of cDVPMA were further evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Fig. 2F and G). The flow cytometry gating strategies for analyzing cell apoptosis are shown in Figure S11. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the apoptosis rate in the cDVPMA treatment group were increased from 13.3% to 82.7% upon NIR laser irradiation.

The ability of cDVPMA to induce ICD in 4T1 cells was evaluated by assessing key ICD markers, including the release of HMGB1 and ATP, as well as the translocation of CRT from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the cancer cell membrane. CLSM images showed a significant increase in CRT exposure in the cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) groups compared to control groups (Fig. 3A and C). This observation was further validated by flow cytometry analysis of CRT expression on the surface of 4T1 cells (Figure S12). Similarly, HMGB1 was predominantly localized in the nucleus in the cVPMA and cDVPMA treatment groups, but was released extracellularly upon light irradiation, consistent with published reports (Fig. 3B and D) [23]. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) further demonstrated that cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) treatment significantly increased the extracellular release of HMGB1 (Fig. 3E). In addition, treatment with cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) resulted in approximately 6.4-fold and 6.3-fold increases in ATP release compared to the PBS (+) group, respectively. However, no obvious ICD cascade was induced in the free 2′3′-cGAMP treatment group (Fig. 3A-E and Figure S12). The expression levels of phosphorylated STING (p-STING), TBK1 (p-TBK1), and IRF3 (p-IRF3) in 4T1 cells following different treatments were analyzed by Western blot (Fig. 3G-J). The expression of p-STING was enhanced in the cDVPMA (+) treatment group, with a 2.4-fold and 1.7-fold increase compared with the PBS and free 2′3′-cGAMP treatment groups, respectively. A transwell co-culture system was employed to evaluate the ability of cDVPMA to promote DC maturation in vitro (Fig. 3K). In this system, 4T1 cells placed in the upper chamber were subjected to various treatments, while bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) were placed in the lower chamber. ELISA analysis revealed that the concentration of IFN-β (Fig. 3L) and TNF-α (Fig. 3M) in the supernatant were significantly increased in cVPMA and cDVPMA with or without NIR laser irradiation. Flow cytometry analysis was performed to evaluate DC maturation by measuring the surface markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 3N and O). Flow cytometry gating strategies for analyzing DCs are shown in Figure S13. The proportion of mature DCs in the cVPMA and cDVPMA treatment groups was 26.5% and 27.1%, respectively, and increased to 39.7% and 40.8% following NIR laser irradiation. These findings suggest that cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) treatment effectively induce ICD and activate the STING pathway, thereby stimulating a robust antitumor immune response.

In vivo biodistribution

Before evaluating the in vivo anticancer efficacy, we investigated the biodistribution of cDVPMA in a 4T1 breast cancer mouse model. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to different groups and intravenously administered cVPMA or cDVPMA for a total of four doses, at an equivalent VL dose of 1.0 mg·kg⁻¹. Fluorescence signals of VL in major organs and tumors were monitored using an IVIS imaging system following the final administration. A time-dependent increase in VL fluorescence signals at the tumor sites was observed in both the cVPMA and cDVPMA treatment groups (Fig. 4A and B). Notably, the cDVPMA group exhibited higher tumor accumulation of VL compared to the cVPMA group at all time points, with fluorescence intensity approximately 2-fold greater at 4 h post-injection. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of the tumors and major organs confirmed the preferential biodistribution of both cVPMA and cDVPMA to the tumor site, as evidenced by stronger VL fluorescence signals in the tumor compared to other major organs (Fig. 4C and D). Immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections further demonstrated that cDVPMA-treated tumors exhibited enhanced spatial distribution of VL within the tumor tissue compared to the cVPMA group (Fig. 4E and F). To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we examined remodeling of the TME following different treatments. A significant increase in the proportion of functional blood vessels was observed in cDVPMA-treated tumors, suggesting improved vascular perfusion (Fig. 4G and H). These findings indicate that cDVPMA treatment enhances tumor perfusion, which in turn facilitates more efficient intratumoral drug distribution.

In vivo antitumor effects

The in vivo antitumor efficacy of cDVPMA was evaluated in an orthotopic 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model following a predetermined treatment schedule (Fig. 5A). One week after tumor inoculation, the mice were randomly assigned to receive intravenous injections of different formulations every two days for a total of four doses. In addition to cDVPMA, other control treatments including PBS, cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA were also included. Each group received an equivalent dose of 1 mg/kg VL, and/or 0.2 mg/kg dabigatran etexilate, and/or 1 mg/kg 2′3′-cGAMP. For the NIR laser irradiation (+) groups, mice were irradiated with an NIR laser (500 mW/cm², 2 min) at 6 h post-injection. Compared to the PBS group, cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA (+) treatments modestly induced tumor regression, with tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rates of 23.5%, 4.4%, and 37.3%, respectively (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the cVPMA (+) group demonstrated a TGI rate of 55.4%, which increased to 78.8% in the cDVPMA (+) group. Tumor inhibition was further confirmed by a significant reduction in tumor weight, with cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA (+) treatments decreasing tumor weight by 17.3%, 8.7%, and 30.1%, respectively (Fig. 5C and D). Notably, the cDVPMA (+) group exhibited a 76.4% reduction in average tumor weight compared to the PBS group. cDVPMA exhibited favorable biocompatibility, as indicated by no significant decreases in body weight throughout the experiment (Fig. 5E) and the absence of significant histological abnormalities in major organs, as confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Figure S14). Furthermore, cDVPMA (+) treatment significantly prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice, with 80% of mice remaining alive 40 days post-injection (Fig. 5F). These results underscore the critical role of dabigatran etexilate in enhancing the efficacy of antitumor therapy. Consistent with the PBS group, metastatic foci were detected in the liver and lung tissues of the cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA (+) treatment groups (Fig. 5G and H). In contrast, the cDVPMA (+) group exhibited a marked reduction in pulmonary metastases compared to the cVPMA (+) group, highlighting the critical role of intratumoral thrombosis mitigation in enhancing antitumor immunity induced by combined PDT and immune adjuvants.

H&E staining of tumor tissues revealed a larger necrotic area in the cDVPMA (+) group, characterized by nuclear chromatin pyknosis and cytoplasmic loss, compared to the control group (Fig. 5I). Furthermore, reduced thrombus formation was observed in the cDVPMA (+) group compared to the VPMA (+) and cVPMA (+) groups, likely due to dabigatran etexilate-mediated thrombin inhibition (Fig. 5I). The intratumoral levels of IL-1β and TGF-β1, cytokines associated with thrombotic responses and platelet activation, were significantly elevated in the VPMA (+) and cVPMA (+) treatment groups, showing 1.8-fold and 1.9-fold increases, respectively, compared to the PBS group (Figure S15). This was likely due to PDT-induced exacerbation of intratumoral thrombosis. In contrast, cDVPMA treatment markedly reduced the expression of IL-1β and TGF-β1 compared to the PBS group, likely due to the dabigatran etexilate-mediated inhibition of thrombosis within the TME. The levels of thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂) in tumor tissues, a marker of platelet activation, were significantly reduced in the cDVPMA (+) treatment group compared with the VPMA (+) and cVPMA (+) groups (Figure S16). To evaluate apoptosis and proliferation of tumor cells, tumor tissues were stained for TUNEL (a marker of apoptosis) and Ki67 (a marker of cell proliferation) detection. cDVPMA (+) treatment group exhibited significantly reduced tumor cell proliferation, as evidenced by a lower number of Ki67-positive cells (Fig. 5I and Figure S17A). Consistently, cDVPMA (+) treatment resulted in a higher number of TUNEL-positive cells than other treatment groups (Figure S18). Given that hypoxia is a hallmark of solid tumors and contributes to immunosuppression, we further investigated the effect of cDVPMA treatment on tumor hypoxia by assessing the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). The markedly increased HIF-1α expression was observed in the VPMA (+) and cVPMA (+) groups, likely due to PDT-induced oxygen consumption and impaired tumor microcirculation (Fig. 5I and Figure S17B). In contrast, the DPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) groups exhibited significantly reduced HIF-1α expression, likely due to dabigatran etexilate-mediated inhibition of intratumoral coagulation and the consequent improvement in vascular perfusion.

cDVPMA-mediated regulation of tumor immune microenvironment

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the antitumor effects of cDVPMA, we investigated its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment with different treatments. Innate immune activation was assessed by flow cytometry on day 2 post-treatment. Flow cytometry gating strategies for analyzing immune cell phenotype was shown in Figure S13. The frequency of mature DCs (CD80⁺CD86⁺) in tumor tissues was 9.33% and 8.92% in the PBS and DPMA groups, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). In contrast, the cVPMA (+) and cDVPMA (+) groups exhibited significantly higher proportions of mature DCs, with approximately 7.2-fold and 8.4-fold increases relative to the PBS group, respectively. Additionally, the proportion of immunosuppressive Tregs (CD3⁺CD4⁺Foxp3⁺) was significantly reduced in the cDVPMA (+) group, with approximately 2.4-fold lower than the PBS group (Fig. 6C and D). We further examined the tumor infiltration of CTLs, which are essential for the therapeutic efficacy of ISCV. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the cVPMA (+) treatment group displayed reduced infiltration of CTLs compared to the PBS group, likely due to PDT-induced intratumoral coagulation and impaired vascular perfusion (Fig. 6E and F). In contrast, cDVPMA (+) treatment significantly enhanced tumor infiltration of CTLs, with 2.4-, 4-, and 1.6-fold increases compared to the cPMA, DPMA, and VPMA (+) groups, respectively. This finding was further supported by IHC staining of tumor sections (Fig. 6G). Moreover, compared to the PBS group, cVPMA (+) treatment significantly upregulated the intratumoral levels of proinflammatory cytokines associated with STING pathway activation, including IFN-β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ (Fig. 6H-K). Collectively, these results suggest that cDVPMA treatment establishes an immune-supportive TME, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of PDT-induced ISCV.

Prevention of tumor recurrence

We subsequently evaluated the ability of cDVPMA to inhibit tumor recurrence using a bilateral 4T1 breast tumor model established by injecting 4T1 cells into the left and right mammary fat pads of mice, representing the primary and distant tumors, respectively. Mice bearing the primary tumor were treated with different formulations via i.v. injection before being rechallenged with the distant tumor (Fig. 7A). The growth of distant tumors was monitored at two-day intervals. Notably, VPMA (+) treatment moderately suppressed the growth of both the primary (Fig. 7B) and distant tumors (Fig. 7E) compared to the PBS group. As expected, cDVPMA (+) treatment showed stronger tumor inhibitory effects on both primary and distant tumors than the cVPMA (+) group. This was probably attributed to the reversal of the immunosuppressive TME by dabigatran etexilate-mediated inhibition of tumor coagulation. Consistently, the weights of both primary (Fig. 7C and D) and distant (Fig. 7F and G) tumors were significantly reduced in the cDVPMA (+) group compared to those in the other groups. To investigate the mechanism underlying cDVPMA-mediated inhibition of tumor recurrence, flow cytometry analysis was performed to assess tumor-infiltrating immune cells in distant tumors. The proportion of mature DCs was markedly higher in the cDVPMA (+) group compared to the other groups (Figure S19). Additionally, cDVPMA (+) treatment significantly increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into distant tumors, with approximately 3.2- and 1.6-fold higher levels compared to the PBS and VPMA (+) groups, respectively (Fig. 7H and I). Furthermore, the levels of immunosuppressive Tregs in distant tumor tissues were significantly reduced following cDVPMA (+) treatment, indicating a reversal of the immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 7J and K). The population of memory T cells (Tm) is critical for establishing durable antitumor immune memory and preventing tumor recurrence. To evaluate the durability of the immune response in different treatment groups, Tm in the spleen were analyzed 10 days after the final treatment. Notably, cDVPMA (+) treatment resulted in a 1.4- and 1.3-fold increase in the frequency of Tm than that in the cPMA and VPMA (+) groups, respectively (Fig. 7L and M). The proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6 and IFN-β in distant tumor tissues were significantly increased in cDVPMA (+) groups (Figure S20). Similarly, TGF-β and IL-1β, the immunosuppressive cytokines associated with tumor coagulation were also decreased in distant tumor tissues (Figure S21). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that cDVPMA not only suppresses primary tumor growth through direct cytotoxic effects but also elicit a potent systemic immune response to effectively inhibit the tumor recurrence.

(L) Flow cytometry and (M) quantitative analysis of memory T cells (CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L−) in distant tumors with different treatments. Data are presented as mean values ± SD (n = 3). ns, no significance. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a multifunctional nanoagonist (cDVPMA) designed to mitigate intratumoral coagulation and thereby enhance PDT-triggered ISCV immunotherapy. The cDVPMA was constructed by co-encapsulating the photosensitizer VL, the thrombin inhibitor dabigatran etexilate, and the STING agonist 2′3′-cGAMP within a polymersome containing tertiary ammonium groups. Upon tumor accumulation, the mildly acidic TME triggered the drug release from cDVPMA. VL-mediated PDT not only induced tumor cell death but also initiated an ICD cascade, promoting tumor antigen release. The activation of the STING pathway by 2′3′-cGAMP enhanced DC maturation and antigen presentation, facilitating ISCV-mediated systemic antitumor immunity. Meanwhile, dabigatran etexilate effectively inhibited intratumoral coagulation and restored vascular perfusion. The improved tumor microcirculation enhanced drug distribution, promoted tumor infiltration of CTLs, and reduced the secretion of coagulation-driven immunosuppressive cytokines. Additionally, cDVPMA treatment alleviated tumor hypoxia, a major limitation to effective immune activation. In an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer mouse model, cDVPMA reshaped the TME into an immune-supportive state, characterized by increased CTLs infiltration and decreased Treg infiltration. This triggered a robust antitumor immunity, leading to significant primary tumor regression and prolonged survival. Notably, cDVPMA also elicited durable immunological memory and abscopal effects, suppressing distant tumor growth. Collectively, this study presents a rationally designed, three-in-one nanoagonist to amplify the synergistic efficacy of photo-triggered ISCV immunotherapy by inhibiting the intratumoral coagulation.

Cell culture and animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai University.

Synthesis of Meo-PEG-b-PPMEMA

The amphiphilic block copolymer PEG-b-PPMEMA (PMA, pKa ∼6.89) was synthesized via reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) according to a published method [22]. Briefly, 0.3 mmol Meo-PEG-Br, 24 mmol PMEMA and 0.3 mmol PMDETA were dissolved in 12 ml of a mixed solution (VDMF/V2-propanol=1:1). After deoxygenation, 0.3 mmol CuBr was added under a nitrogen atmosphere to initiate polymerization at 40 °C. after 24 h, the mixture was diluted with tetrahydrofuran (THF), and the catalyst was removed using a neutral Al₂O₃ column. The resulting polymer solution was then dialyzed against THF and deionized water. Finally, the polymer was collected by freeze-drying under vacuum. The chemical structure of Meo-PEG-b-PPMEMA was confirmed by 1HNMR and gel permeation chromatography (GPC).

Preparation and characterization of cDVPMA

cDVPMA were prepared using a controlled solvent exchange method. Briefly, 125 µL of VL solution (4 mg/mL in ethanol), 100 µL of dabigatran etexilate solution (5 mg/mL in ethanol), and 125 µL of PEG-b-PPMEMA solution (20 mg/mL in ethanol) were mixed in a clean glass vial, yielding a total organic phase volume of 500 µL. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, 500 µL of 2′3′-cGAMP aqueous solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added dropwise (~ 50 µL/min) under continuous stirring (600 rpm) and light protection, followed by the gradual addition of 500 µL of ultrapure water to facilitate vesicle self-assembly. The dispersion was stirred for an additional 2 h to stabilize the polymersomal structure. Unencapsulated drugs and residual solvents were removed by dialysis (MWCO 10 kDa) against PBS overnight. The resulting cDVPMA was collected and stored at 4 °C in the dark until use. Particle size and zeta potential were measured using a ZetaSizer Pro (Malvern, U.K.). To evaluate colloidal stability, cDVPMA was dispersed in 0.01 M PBS supplemented with 10% FBS, and particle size was measured at monitored at different time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h) during incubation.

Drug loading efficiency (DLE) and drug encapsulation efficiency (DEE)

To study the DLE of 2′3′-cGAMP, dabigatran etexilate, and VL in cDVPMA, 4 mg of lyophilized cDVPMA was dissolved in 1 mL of ethanol, and the amount of loaded drug was quantified by HPLC using a C18 reverse-phase column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm), based on established calibration curves of the corresponding free drugs. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% aqueous trifluoroacetic acid) and solvent B (methanol). Gradient elution was performed as follows: 0–10 min, 10% B; 10–35 min, linear increase to 80% B; 35–40 min, 80% B; and 40–45 min, re-equilibration to 10% B. The flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min, the column temperature set at 25 °C and the injection volume was 20 µL. Detection wavelengths were set to the maximum absorbance of each analyte: 660 nm for VL, 325 nm for dabigatran etexilate, and 254 nm for 2′,3′-cGAMP. The DLE was calculated using the following formula: DLE (%) = (amount of drug loaded/total weight of nanoparticles) × 100%. The DEE was confirmed by calculating the percentage of drug remained after dialysis. Briefly, 5 mL of the freshly prepared polymersome solution was added to the upper chamber of an ultrafiltration tube (MWCO 100 kDa) and centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min. Polymersomes retained in the upper chamber of the ultrafiltration tube were resuspended in the original volume of PBS. After three repeats, the infiltrates were combined. Drug contents in the combined infiltrates and the purified nanoparticle solution were determined by HPLC. The DEE was calculated using the following formulas: DEE (%) = (amount of drug loaded/amount of drug initially added) × 100%.

In vitro drug release

To evaluate the release profile of cDVPMA, freshly prepared cDVPMA were dispersed in PBS at varying pH conditions (7.4, 6.8, and 5.5) and incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 200 rpm. At predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), 200 µL of the nanoparticle solution was transferred into the upper chamber of an ultrafiltration tube (MWCO 100 kDa) and centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min. The concentrations of 2′3′-cGAMP, dabigatran etexilate, and VL in the filtrates were measured by HPLC.

In vitro photosensitizing activity

The generation of 1O₂ by cDVPMA was assessed using the Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG) probe. Briefly, cPMA, cVPMA, and cDVPMA formulations were first incubated in PBS at pH 5.5 for 6 h to simulate the acidic TME and facilitate drug release. Subsequently, the above solution was diluted with PBS to adjust the pH to 7.4, followed by the addition of SOSG to a final concentration of 2 µM in a 96-well black plate. The mixtures were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and subsequently irradiated with a NIR laser (680 nm, 500 mW) for varying durations (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 min). The fluorescence intensity of SOSG (Ex/Em = 504/525 nm) was measured immediately using a multimode plate reader.

In vitro anticoagulant activity

The anticoagulant activity of cDVPMA was assessed using a whole-blood clot formation assay. The experimental groups included PBS (negative control), free heparin, free dabigatran etexilate, cPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, and cDVPMA. Each formulation was pre-incubated in mildly acidic PBS (pH 6.8) for 6 h, followed by mixing with 200 µL of freshly collected mouse whole blood. The mixtures were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow clot formation. After incubation, the supernatants were gently removed, and the inner walls of the tubes, along with the clots, were rinsed multiple times with PBS to eliminate residual blood. Blood Clots were photographed and gently blotted with filter paper to remove excess moisture before weighing.

Cellular uptake

The cellular uptake of cDVPMA by 4T1 cells was assessed by measuring the fluorescence signal of VL using flow cytometry and CLSM. Briefly, 1 mL of 4T1 cell suspension (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL) was seeded into each well of a 24-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. After that, the cells were treated with cDVPMA and cVPMA at various VL concentrations (0, 0.5, 1, and 2 µM) for 1, 2, and 4 h. After three washes with PBS, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometry and CLSM.

Detection of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS generation was evaluated using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a fluorescent probe. Briefly, 1 mL of 4T1 cell suspension (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL) was seeded into each well of a 24-well plate containing sterile glass coverslips and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The cells were then treated with fresh medium containing PBS, cPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA at a VL-equivalent concentration of 0.5 µM for 4 h. Following treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS to remove excess probe. For the light-treated groups, cells were exposed to laser irradiation (660 nm, 500 mW) for 2 min after DCFH-DA incubation. Intracellular ROS levels were visualized by CLSM after nuclear staining with DAPI, and quantified by flow cytometry.

In vitro cytotoxicity and cell apoptosis

The cytotoxicity of cDVPMA against 4T1 cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The Cells were then treated with various formulations (PBS, PMA, cPMA, DPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, and cDVPMA) at VL-equivalent concentration of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1 µM for 4 h. For the light treatment groups, cells were irradiated with laser (660 nm, 500 mW) for 2 min, followed by incubation for an additional 12 h. Afterward, cells were washed three times with PBS, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (5 mg/mL) as added to each well. After 1 h of incubation, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated accordingly.

For apoptosis analysis, 2 × 10⁵ 4T1 cells were seeded into each well of a 24-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The cells were then treated with various formulations (PBS, PMA, cPMA, DPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, and cDVPMA) at a VL-equivalent concentration of 0.5 µM for 4 h. For the light treatment groups, cells were irradiated with laser (660 nm, 500 mW) for 2 min, followed by an additional 24-hour incubation. After treatment, the cells were washed three times with PBS, digested, and resuspended in PBS. Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry after staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI).

In vivo biodistribution

For in vivo biodistribution analysis, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were i.v. injected with cDVPMA or cVPMA at a VL-equivalent dose of 1 mg/kg and then illuminated with light. The mice were treated every two days for a total of four doses. Live imaging was performed using an IVIS imaging system to monitor nanoparticle biodistribution at various time points (2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h) following the final injection. At the 24-hour time point, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) along with tumors were harvested for ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence intensity in these tissues was analyzed to assess nanoparticle biodistribution. Tumor tissues were subsequently cryosectioned for further evaluation, and CLSM was used to visualize nanoparticle accumulation and intratumoral distribution.

Characterization of vascular perfusion in tumor tissues

To assess vascular perfusion in tumor tissues, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were i.v. injected with cDVPMA or cVPMA at a VL-equivalent dose of 1 mg/kg and then illuminated with light. The mice were treated every two days for a total of four doses. After the final injection, the mice received an i.v. injection of 100 µL FITC-labeled lectin (10 mg/mL) for vascular imaging. Fifteen minutes later, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were immediately harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed tumors were sectioned into 10 μm-thick slices. Tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against CD31 (1:200), followed by a 1-hour incubation with an AF555-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature. Fluorescence images were captured using CLSM. Vascular perfusion was visualized through FITC-labeled lectin, while blood vessels were identified via CD31 staining. Perfusion efficiency was quantified by calculating the ratio of lectin-positive vessels to CD31-positive vessels using ImageJ software.

In vitro ICD induction

4T1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) containing sterile glass coverslips and cultured for 12 h. The cells were then treated with PBS, cPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA at a VL-equivalent concentration of 0.5 µM for 4 h. For the light-treated groups, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (500 mW) for 2 min, followed by an additional 24-hour incubation. For CRT exposure analysis, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and incubated with anti-CRT primary antibodies, followed by AF488-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI for nuclear staining. CRT translocation was visualized using CLSM and analyzed by flow cytometry. To evaluate intracellular HMGB1 localization, cells were fixed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then incubated with anti-HMGB1 primary antibodies, followed by AF488-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI. HMGB1 distribution was observed by CLSM. In addition, extracellular HMGB1 and ATP levels in the culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA and ATP assay kits, respectively, according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

In vitro maturation of DCs

To evaluate the ability of cDVPMA to promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from female BALB/c mice. Briefly, bone marrow cells were harvested by flushing the femurs and tibias and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and 10 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4) for 6 days to induce differentiation into immature DCs. On day 6, the immature BMDCs were collected and seeded into the lower chambers of a Transwell system, while 4T1 cells were seeded into the upper chambers. The 4T1 cells were treated with PBS, cPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA at a VL-equivalent concentration of 0.5 µM for 4 h, followed by laser irradiation (660 nm, 500 mW) for 2 min. After 24 h of co-culture, BMDCs were harvested, washed with PBS, and stained with PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD80, and BV421-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibodies. The proportion of mature DCs (CD11c⁺CD80⁺CD86⁺) was determined by flow cytometry. Additionally, culture supernatants were collected to quantify the secretion of IFN-β and TNF-α using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Western blotting assay

To assess STING pathway activation, 4T1 cells were seeded into six-well plates (1 × 106 cells/well) and cultured for 12 h. The cells were then treated with PBS, cPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA at a VL-equivalent concentration of 0.5 µM for 4 h. For light-treated groups, laser irradiation (660 nm, 500 mW) was applied for 1.5 min. After an additional 24-hour incubation, total protein was extracted using cell lysis buffer and clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 15 min. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (10 µg per sample) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1.5 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phospho-STING and β-actin. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate.

In vivo antitumor activity

An orthotopic breast cancer mouse model was established by injecting 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁶) into the right mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice. One-week post-inoculation, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) and intravenously treated with PBS, cPMA, DPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA. The doses of 2′3′-cGAMP, dabigatran etexilate, and VL were 1 mg/kg, 0.2 mg/kg, and 1 mg/kg, respectively. For light-treated groups, tumors were irradiated with a laser (660 nm, 500 mW) for 2 min at 6 h post-injection. Treatments were administered every 2 days for a total of four doses. Body weight and tumor size were measured every 2 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = (Length × Width²)/2. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Tumor tissues and major organs were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. Tumor sections were stained with H&E, Ki67, HIF-1α, and TUNEL to assess histopathology, proliferation, hypoxia, and apoptosis, respectively. Major organs were stained with H&E to evaluate systemic toxicity.

Antitumor immune response assessment

The immunophenotypes of tumor-infiltrating immune cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Briefly, excised tumor tissues or spleens were cut into 1–2 mm fragments and enzymatically digested in 2.5 mL of digestion buffer (DMEM supplemented with 10% collagenase I and 0.2% DNase) at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 1 h. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, and washed once with PBS. The cell pellets were resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis. For T cell analysis, tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells were stained with FITC anti-mouse CD3, APC anti-mouse CD4, and BV605 anti-mouse CD8 antibodies. Mature DCs were identified using PE anti-mouse CD11c, APC anti-mouse CD80, and BV421 anti-mouse CD86 antibodies. Tregs were analyzed with FITC anti-mouse CD3, APC anti-mouse CD4, and BV421 anti-mouse Foxp3 antibodies. Memory T cells were stained with FITC anti-mouse CD3, BV605 anti-mouse CD8, VioletFluor 450 anti-mouse CD44, and APC anti-mouse CD62L antibodies. The proportions of immune cell populations were quantified using flow cytometry. Additionally, tumor tissues were homogenized, and the concentrations of key cytokines, such as IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and TGF-β1, were measured in the tumor homogenates using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

In vivo antitumor effect in the rechallenged tumor model

A bilateral 4T1 tumor model was established to investigate the antitumor effect of cDVPMA on rechallenged tumors. Briefly, 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁶) were injected into the right mammary fat pad of each mouse to establish the primary tumor. Six days after tumor inoculation, mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) and intravenously injected with PBS, cPMA, DPMA, VPMA, cVPMA, or cDVPMA. The equivalent doses of 2′3′-cGAMP, dabigatran etexilate, and VL were 1 mg/kg, 2 mg/kg, and 1.35 mg/kg, respectively. For the light-treated groups, tumors were irradiated with a 660 nm, 500 mW laser for 2 min at the primary tumor site, 6 h post-injection. Treatments were administered every other day for a total of four doses. Following the final treatment, mice were rechallenged with 4T1 cells (0.5 × 10⁶) injected into the right flank to establish distant tumors. Tumor volume (primary and distant) and body weight were measured every two days. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and both primary and distant tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Distant tumor tissues were then digested into single-cell suspensions, and immune cell infiltration (CD8⁺ T cells, DCs, and Tregs) was analyzed using specific fluorescently labeled antibodies.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between different groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t test (two-tailed). All statistical analyses were performed using Python. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
