Location and capacity optimization of urban sanitation robot base stations using improved NSGA-II
Authors: Jie Ding, Linshen Wang, Kun Pan, Nan Ji, Na Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional sanitation systems are increasingly challenged by contemporary waste collection demands, owing to limited service capacity, high operational costs, and uneven resource distribution. Sanitation robots, capable of high-precision waste identification and multi-robot collaboration, offer a promising alternative to improve collection efficiency. Their performance, however, critically depends on the scientific layout of base stations. This study proposes a multi-objective location and capacity optimization model based on an improved NSGA-II, incorporating multi-source data such as population density and spatiotemporal waste distribution. To strengthen the algorithm’s adaptability to complex urban environments, a self-adaptive crossover operator and a dynamic objective weighting mechanism are introduced. The TOPSIS method is then applied to evaluate and rank Pareto-optimal solutions, yielding an optimal layout and capacity plan for base stations. An empirical analysis in Lixia District, Jinan City, demonstrates that the optimized scheme reduces the number of facilities by one, improves service utility, and decreases the number of uncovered demand points to eight. Compared with existing planning approaches, the proposed model proves effective in dynamic scenarios and provides a collaborative multi-objective optimization framework for smart urban sanitation planning.Keywords: Sanitation facilities, Sanitation robot base stations, Location-allocation, Multi-objective optimizationSubject terms: Energy and society, Sustainability, Computational science, Computer science

Introduction

Currently, the world is facing unprecedented challenges in waste management. The United Nations Environment Programme’s Global Waste Management Outlook 2024 indicates that urban solid waste will increase from 2.1 billion tons in 2023 to 3.8 billion tons by 2050. In 2020, the direct cost of global waste management was estimated at $252 billion. When hidden costs such as pollution and climate change are considered, the cost rises to $361 billion. Without urgent waste management actions, by 2050, the global annual cost will nearly double, reaching a staggering $640.3 billion1 This imbalance has led to a quadruple crisis. Economically, sanitation investments in various cities will increase. Environmentally, open dumping of waste is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions and a major factor contributing to global warming2. Socially, the overall prevalence of occupational injuries among sanitation workers is 32.36%3. The high risk of injury in this industry can easily lead to labor shortages and moral controversies. In terms of urban management, the variety and quantity of sanitation facilities and equipment continue to grow, with automated equipment, mainly automated garbage trucks, being widely used. However, there are challenges such as insufficient parking space, lack of location selection and scale standards, which urgently require academia to seek technological solutions to overcome these obstacles.

In recent years, breakthroughs in sanitation robot technology have provided new pathways for urban waste management. Jinqiang Bai’s team developed a grassland waste recognition robot that achieves a 95% recognition accuracy through deep neural networks4. Guozhen Li’s team integrated tactile sensing with machine learning, achieving a waste classification accuracy of 94%5. Abhishek Gupta’s team optimized multi-robot path planning using collaborative algorithms, significantly reducing the energy consumption and cost of waste collection6. These technologies offer efficient and sustainable solutions for smart city waste management.

The efficient operation of sanitation robots is closely tied to base stations, which act as “energy supply stations” and “data interaction hubs” for these robots, ensuring their continuous operation. The layout of base stations significantly impacts operational costs and resource utilization efficiency. Improper base station layout can result in budget overruns or resource waste. Research by Mario Castro-Gama et al. explored the optimal placement of autonomous robot charging stations within drinking water networks, employing the proposed algorithm to effectively estimate the required number of charging stations and autonomous robots in the distribution network, thereby achieving efficient resource utilization7. The study reveals that base stations significantly affect the operational efficiency of robots. Proper site selection for base stations is crucial for improving the efficiency of sanitation robot systems and reducing operational costs.

In light of this, the paper introduces intelligent enhancements to existing sanitation facilities, transforming them into sanitation robot base station. The study develops a two-stage multi-objective optimization model for resolution. During the site selection phase, a model is established to maximize garbage collection efficiency, minimize economic costs, and cover the most demand points. In the capacity determination phase, a model is constructed to minimize economic costs and reduce garbage collection time. An improved Non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) is employed to obtain the optimal solutions for the multi-objective resolution, with an empirical analysis conducted in Lixia District of Jinan City as the case study. Based on the existing facility layout plan, a spatial optimization layout for base stations was proposed, determining the optimal number of sanitation robots to be deployed at each base station. This ultimately results in a sanitation service system with greater intelligence and improved operational efficiency.

This study builds on the literature related to facility location and multi-objective optimization algorithms (MOOA). Section 2 will systematically review the evolution of facility location theory, the development of multi-objective optimization methods, and their application in sanitation facilities planning. This review will provide the theoretical basis and methodological support for the model construction and algorithm improvement presented in this paper. Section 3 outlines and defines the research problem. Section 4 details the model parameters and develops the model necessary for the study. Section 5 describes the solution process of the NSGA-II. In Sect. 6, a case analysis is conducted using Lixia District, Jinan City as an example. The site selection scheme proposed in this paper is compared with the existing planning scheme to verify the applicability of the proposed model and algorithm. Finally, Sect. 7 summarizes the conclusions of this paper and offers suggestions for future research.

Related work

Over more than half a century, facility location theory has evolved to provide a methodological foundation for selecting location for sanitation robot base stations. Early research focused on single-objective static optimization, such as the Weber problem, which aims to minimize transportation costs8, the P-Median model, which strives to shorten service distances9, and coverage models that seek a balance between the number of facilities and coverage area10. Although these classic models laid the theoretical groundwork, they struggle to meet the complex demands of modern cities. With the rise of sustainable development concepts, multi-objective models have gradually become mainstream.

The goal of multiple objective problems (MOPs) is to find a set of solutions approximating the Pareto Front (PF) with good convergence and diversity11. For example, the study by Ling Shen et al. explored the optimal locations for centralized and decentralized construction waste treatment facilities to achieve low-cost, low-transportation carbon emissions, and low social negative impact utilization12. Eun-Kyeong Kim et al. developed an advanced multi-objective location optimization model, optimizing three objectives: maximizing the utilization rate of candidate sites for establishing urban parks, maximizing the number of vulnerable groups covered by urban parks, and minimizing the total construction cost of urban parks13. Similar to the above studies, location-capacity problems usually also involve multiple conflicting objectives, meaning that improvement in one objective may lead to the deterioration of another objective14. For instance, increasing economic costs can allow for the deployment of more sanitation robots, thereby reducing waste collection time. However, since it is unlikely to find a single solution that optimizes both cost and time in reality, the Pareto optimal set is, in a sense, the best trade-off solution15. It contains various solutions that balance all objectives, where an improvement in one objective cannot be achieved without deteriorating at least one other objective16.

Multiple objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) are currently popular for multi objective optimization17,18, utilizing population evolution to search the PF. Mainstream algorithms can be categorized into three types19. (1) Pareto-dominance-based algorithms. These algorithms directly approach the PF by employing fast non-dominated sorting to filter the solution set. NSGA-II20,21 is a classic example of this category. It balances solution diversity and convergence through fast non-dominated sorting, crowding comparison, and elitist strategies, and is widely applied in multi-objective optimization problems. Strength pareto evolutionary algorithm II (SPEA2)22 introduces an environmental selection mechanism and refined archive management, significantly enhancing the uniformity of the solution set distribution, making it particularly suitable for optimization problems in high-dimensional objective spaces. (2) Performance indicator-based algorithms. These algorithms are guided by indicators such as hypervolume to direct the search. Indicator-based evolutionary algorithm (IBEA)23 assesses the quality of the solution set using custom indicators, allowing flexible adaptation to various optimization needs, though its computational complexity rises significantly with the number of objectives. Fast hypervolume indicator-based MOEA (FV-EMOA)24 integrates hypervolume indicators into the selection mechanism, using gradient information to guide the search, improving convergence speed while maintaining diversity. (3) Decomposition-based algorithms. These algorithms decompose multi-objective problems into multiple single-objective subproblems, approaching the PF through collaborative optimization. MOEA based on decomposition (MOEA/D)25 uses weight vectors to partition the objective space, combined with neighborhood update strategies, excelling under complex constraints. MOEA/D with dynamical resource allocation (MOEAD-DRA)26 introduces a dynamic resource allocation mechanism that adaptively adjusts the allocation of computational resources for subproblems, significantly enhancing the algorithm’s efficiency and robustness. This research provides methodological support for multi-objective facility location; however, its applicability in the context of sanitation robot base station layout still needs to be tested.

Currently, there is limited research on location and capacity determination of sanitation robot base station, and numerous challenges are encountered. Firstly, the amount and distribution of urban waste are influenced by factors such as seasons, holidays, and city activities, resulting in significant spatiotemporal variations (Firman Firmansyah; Bruno Ribas Alzamora)27,28. Traditional location models struggle to respond in real-time to these dynamic changes, leading to base station layouts that fail to meet actual demands, causing resource waste or insufficient service. Secondly, the selection of base station locations must balance multiple objectives, including construction cost, operational cost, service coverage, and the convenience of charging and maintenance (Dongqing Jia; Guokai Li; Vinay Yadav; Constantinos Chatzouridis)29–32. These objectives are interrelated and constrain each other. Pursuing extensive coverage may increase costs, while overly compressing costs may reduce service quality. Existing MOOA have weak capabilities when handling complex constraints and goal conflicts (Ines Mehouachi; Farhad Habibi)33,34, making it difficult to find a global optimal solution. Finally, the location of base station faces coordination issues with existing urban infrastructure (Zhonglin Sheng)35. Urban space is limited and complexly laid out, requiring base station planning to consider compatibility with surrounding facilities to avoid disrupting urban operations. Notably, emerging research in the fields of drones and unmanned vehicles shows unique value: Balaji Kirubakaran and others have proposed an innovative perspective of integrating drone base station into urban infrastructure, providing new insights into the layout of drone base station and offering technical inspiration for this study36. Daria Matkovic and others integrated a location-based charging station saliency prediction model with the lion optimization algorithm to optimize the placement of additional charging stations within the established charging infrastructure, thereby improving the utilization efficiency of the charging station37. This method can be applied to the location of sanitation robot base stations to balance the conflict between service coverage and construction investment.

To address the MOPs of location and capacity determination for sanitation robot base stations, this study proposes a three-tier “data-model-validation” system. At the data layer, a multi-source heterogeneous database is constructed by integrating data such as population density, satellite remote sensing images, and the number of tourists in scenic spots during holidays. The improvements at the model layer are demonstrated in two aspects: firstly, an adaptive crossover operator is designed to maintain population diversity in the later stages of NSGA-II evolution; secondly, a dynamic adjustment mechanism for objective weights is introduced. This mechanism computes different time weight vectors for weekdays, weekends, and holidays, dynamically adjusting the weights of service utility objectives to better align the optimization process with actual demand changes. At the validation layer, the improved NSGA-II is applied to the location and capacity determination study of sanitation robot base stations in Lixia District, Jinan City, assessing the distribution of existing facilities and determining the optimal location and capacity scheme.

The breakthrough of this study lies not only in algorithmic improvements but also in the transformation of the research paradigm. Unlike traditional studies on location and capacity determination that rely on static empirical models, this research incorporates the spatiotemporal fluctuation characteristics of waste volume into the optimization model by constructing a “time-demand” dynamic coupling mechanism, achieving a shift from a “predict-respond” mode to a “perceive-adapt” mode. These advancements provide new theoretical tools for smart city infrastructure planning, and the methodological framework can be extended to areas such as emergency station deployment and shared charging station layout, demonstrating significant potential for multi-domain application.

Problem description

As urban waste continues to increase, traditional labor-intensive sanitation models are increasingly exposed to issues such as insufficient service resilience, rising labor costs, and delayed dynamic responses. These challenges are particularly pronounced during holidays when population surges lead to significant fluctuations in waste generation. Such conditions pose severe challenges to the traditional sanitation model, which relies on fixed scheduling and static resource allocation. In this context, sanitation robots, as the core components of intelligent sanitation systems, are redefining the paradigm of urban sanitation management with their advantages of round-the-clock service and intelligent scheduling. The deployment efficiency of sanitation robot systems is highly dependent on the strategic layout of the base stations. Base stations not only provide energy supply and data transmission support for the robots but also directly influence the feasibility of their service radius, operational scheduling, and route planning. Therefore, determining how to scientifically select locations and configure capacities for sanitation robot base stations in complex urban environments has become a core issue urgently needing resolution in the current field of smart sanitation.

The location and capacity determination for sanitation robot base stations involve optimizing multiple objectives. Firstly, the layout of existing sanitation facilities needs to be optimized with the goals of maximizing service utility, minimizing economic costs, and covering the most demand points, based on the dynamic changes in waste generation over time. Secondly, when the existing facilities cannot cover additional demand points, new base stations should be added appropriately, with economic cost as a constraint. Finally, the number of sanitation robots should be determined by minimizing waste collection time and economic costs. This issue is a typical multi-objective location and capacity problem with spatiotemporal dynamic characteristics, which traditional static models struggle to address due to the dynamic service demands caused by temporal heterogeneity. Therefore, the study established a dynamic response, multi objective oriented optimization model for the location and capacity determination of sanitation robot base stations to address this problem.

The location and capacity determination problem discussed in this paper primarily involves two types of sites: base stations that provide charging and maintenance for sanitation robots, and demand points that receive waste collection services. The base stations include existing, candidate, and newly added stations. The demand points are served by sanitation robots. Given the spatial heterogeneity of garbage distribution, such as the need for high-load continuous operation in large open areas and the requirement for flexible mobility and precise interactive services in backstreets and commercial streets—the study adopts a heterogeneous robot collaborative operation system. Medium-sized cleaning robots, equipped with large-capacity cargo boxes, handle garbage collection and transportation on main roads, while small-sized cleaning robots, with compact bodies and intelligent sensing modules, perform detailed operations in narrow spaces. The medium-sized cleaning robot utilizes the research findings of Qiaokang Liang et al.38. The electric unmanned sanitation vehicle (EUSV) integrates multiple sensor technologies and can operate continuously at high intensity. The small-sized cleaning sanitation robot employs the SOLIS S200 multifunctional cleaning robot launched by Candela39, which can autonomously plan cleaning paths to perform precise outdoor operations and comprehensive cleaning. Algorithm solutions

Improved NSGA-II

The location-capacity model proposed in this research is a multi-objective problem characterized by spatiotemporal dynamics. The NSGA-II, with its strengths in fast non-dominated sorting, elitist preservation strategy, and crowding comparison operator, effectively balances convergence and diversity, making it well-suited for multi-objective optimization problems. To further enhance the performance of the NSGA-II and ensure its capability to handle the spatiotemporal dynamic changes in urban waste distribution, the research has implemented the following improvements: (1) An adaptive crossover operator is designed to maintain population diversity in the later stages of evolution, preventing premature convergence; (2) A dynamic adjustment mechanism for objective weights is introduced by setting weight factors to balance sanitation services at different times. Execution steps of the elitism preservation strategy. The process comprises two key stages: (1) merging the parent and offspring populations, and performing fast non-dominated sorting and crowding distance calculation on the merged population; (2) iteratively selecting individuals with higher non-dominated ranks, and then employing crowding distance to maintain population diversity while removing individuals with lower crowding distances. This strategy effectively preserves high-quality solutions while promoting diversity and convergence towards the Pareto optimal front.

Parameter study

In the design and application of MOEAs, parameter settings have a significant impact on the algorithm’s convergence speed, solution set quality, and computational efficiency. To further optimize the performance of improved NSGA-II, this study conducted a systematic analysis of two key parameters: population size and the number of generations. Through a comparative analysis of the algorithm’s performance under various parameter settings, the most suitable configuration for this problem was identified, balancing both convergence and diversity.

Population size directly affects the algorithm’s global search capability and computational complexity. A larger population helps maintain diversity and avoid premature convergence but significantly increases the computational burden. Conversely, a smaller population, while improving efficiency, may reduce the comprehensiveness and robustness of the solution set. To scientifically determine the optimal population size, 50 independent experiments were conducted for each of four configurations (population sizes of 25, 50, 75, and 100) under a fixed number of generations. This configuration not only ensures high HV values and stability but also prevents inefficient computations associated with an excessively large population, thereby achieving an effective balance between convergence and diversity.

The number of generations is another key parameter affecting algorithm convergence. A sufficient number of generations is a necessary condition for ensuring the algorithm thoroughly explores the solution space and approximates the true PF. To determine an appropriate number of generations, this study tested the performance of the improved NSGA-II on the ZDT1 and ZDT2 functions under 100, 200, and 300 generations, with a fixed population size of 50. The study revealed that the impact of the number of generations on population diversity varied depending on the characteristics of the function. On ZDT1, diversity was observed to gradually decrease with an increasing number of generations, suggesting a gradual transition of the algorithm from global exploration to refined exploitation. Conversely, on ZDT2, diversity showed an initial increase followed by a decrease, achieving its optimal distribution state at 200 generations. A comprehensive analysis of both convergence and diversity performance led to the selection of 200 as the optimal number of generations for the algorithm. With this parameter, the solution set was able to sufficiently approach the theoretical PF on both ZDT1 and ZDT2, thereby satisfying the convergence requirements. Simultaneously, good diversity was maintained on ZDT1, and optimal diversity was achieved on ZDT2. While a slight improvement in convergence on ZDT1 was observed with 300 generations, the effect was not significant, and the computational cost increased. Furthermore, the diversity on ZDT2 even decreased. Therefore, 200 generations were determined to achieve the optimal balance among computational efficiency, convergence, and diversity. Consequently, the algorithm parameters were set as follows: a population size of 50 and 200 generations.

Case study

Overview of the study area

Lixia District in Jinan City is located in the core area of Jinan, covering a total area of 100.89 square kilometers, with a permanent population of 819,100. It is a typical high-density urbanized area. The district is home to scenic spots such as Baotu Spring, Daming Lake, and Qianfo Mountain. During holidays, the tourist flow is dense, resulting in a distinct heterogeneity in waste generation. This characteristic closely aligns with the previously mentioned research framework, providing a typical scenario for verifying the collaborative model of medium-sized cleaning robots and small interactive robots under the dynamic partition operation mechanism.

According to research data, there are currently six sanitation facilities in Lixia District, with plans to construct 13 more, as shown in Fig. 15a. The large number of tourists in scenic spots like Baotu Spring and Daming Lake during holidays also leads to a very noticeable spatiotemporal variation in population density and waste output in Lixia District, as shown in Fig. 15b–d. Since scenic spots like Baotu Spring and Daming Lake are located on the west side of Lixia District, most sanitation facilities are concentrated in the western part of the study area, with fewer facilities on the east side, resulting in an uneven distribution of sanitation facilities throughout the area. Furthermore, the entire sanitation service model is static and cannot meet the needs of the study area.

The spatial distribution of sanitation facilities and the population density at different periods in Lixia District.

Data sources and processing

The population density data used in the study is sourced from World Pop40 (https://hub.worldpop.org/) and the Lixia District Tourism Public Service Center. The location data of sanitation facilities is sourced from field research. Given the extensive land use of the study area, the dense population distribution, and the comprehensive coverage of base stations, the waste clearance demand in the study area is discretized. The study area was divided into 800 m × 800 m grids using the Fishnet tool in ArcGIS Desktop 10.8.141 (URL: https://www.esri.com), with the centroid of each grid representing a waste clearance demand point. After excluding invalid demand points, 181 uniformly distributed waste collection demand points are generated within the study area, as shown in Fig. 16. The waste generated in each divided area is allocated to these demand points, determining the waste amount at each demand point.

Conclusions and recommendations

This paper achieves scientific decision-making for the location and capacity optimization of sanitation robot base stations. This was accomplished by constructing a multi-objective optimization model that integrates spatio-temporal dynamic features, combined with an improved NSGA-II. The conclusions are as follows:

The improved NSGA-II demonstrated excellent performance in the case study of Lixia District, Jinan. The optimized solution resulted in one less facility compared to the original plan, with only 8 demand points remaining uncovered, thereby exhibiting strong adaptability to spatio-temporal fluctuations in waste volume.
