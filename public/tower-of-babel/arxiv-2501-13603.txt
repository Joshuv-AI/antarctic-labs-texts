Abstract
. Specifying and verifying graph-manipulating programs is a well-known and persistent challenge in separation logic. We show that the obstacles in dealing with graphs are removed if one represents graphs as partial commutative monoids, and admits applying over them structure-preserving functions (i.e., morphisms), including higher-order ones (i.e., combinators). By distributing over the monoid operations, morphisms work in tandem with framing to propagate the low-level pointer manipulation to the more abstract level of mathematical graphs, and to reduce the reasoning about the whole graph to reasoning about sub-components. We illustrate the approach with a novel and concise proof of a well-known graph benchmark, the Schorr-Waite graph marking algorithm.



1.
Introduction

Two characteristic properties make separation
logic
(
O’Hearn et al. 2001
;
Ishtiaq and O’Hearn 2001
;
Reynolds 2002
)
an effective
formalism for program verification.
The first property is that specifications tightly circumscribe the
pointers that a program accesses. Then
framing
, with the
associated frame rule of inference, allows extending the specification
with a
disjoint
set of pointers, deducing that the program
doesn’t change the extension. This makes the
verification
local

(
Calcagno et al. 2007
)
, in the sense that
the verification can focus on the relevant heap by framing out the
unnecessary pointers.

The second property is that the heap layout of a data structure is
typically defined in relation to the structure’s contents, so that
clients can reason about the contents and abstract from the heap as
much as possible.
A common example is the predicate
\mathit{list}\ \alpha\ (i,j)

(
Reynolds 2002
)
illustrated in Fig.
1
, which
describes the layout of a singly-linked list between pointers
i
and

j
, abstracting the other internal pointers, with list’s contents

\alpha
represented as an inductive mathematical sequence. This
way,
spatial
(i.e., about heaps and pointers) reasoning gives
rise to
non-spatial
(i.e., pure, mathematical, heap-
and pointer-free, about contents) reasoning for the clients.

Figure 1.
Spatial predicate
\mathit{list}\ \alpha\ (i,j)
describes the
layout of a linked list, whose contents is sequence
\alpha
,
between pointers
i
and
j

\alpha

The interaction of the two characteristic properties imparts an
important requirement on non-spatial reasoning. Since framing involves
decomposition of heaps into disjoint sub-heaps,
locality mandates that the mathematical structure representing
contents
must also
support some form of disjoint
decomposition. For example, in Fig.
1
, the heap
divides into two contiguous but disjoint parts, between pointers
i

and
k
, and
k
and
j
, respectively. Correspondingly at the
non-spatial level, the sequence
[a,b,c,d]
decomposes into
sub-sequences
[a,b]
and
[c,d]
so that
\mathit{list}\ [a,b]\ (i,k)

and
\mathit{list}\ [c,d]\ (k,j)
hold of disjoint sub-heaps.

While mathematical sequences (along with sets, mathematical trees, and
many other structures) admit useful forms of decomposition, graphs
generally don’t. Indeed, traversing a list or a tree reduces to
traversing disjoint sub-lists or disjoint sub-trees from different
children of a common root; thus lists and trees naturally divide into
disjoint sub-components. But in a graph, the sub-graphs reachable from
the different children of a common root need not be disjoint,
preventing an easy division.
Even if a graph is divided into sub-graphs with disjoint nodes,
one must still somehow keep track of the edges crossing the divide, in
order to eventually re-attach the pieces. Keeping track of the
crossing edges is simple in the case of lists, as there’s at most one
such edge (e.g.,
k
in Fig.
1
),
but graphs generally don’t exhibit such regularity.

The lack of natural decomposition is why graphs have traditionally
posed a challenge for separation logic. For example, the Schorr-Waite
graph marking algorithm
(
Schorr and Waite 1967
)
is a well-known benchmark
that has been verified in separation logic early on
by
Yang 2001a
;
Yang 2001b
, and in many other logics and tools
such as Isabelle
(
Mehta and Nipkow 2003
)
and
Dafny
(
Leino 2010
)
. While these proofs share conceptually
similar mathematical notions (e.g., reachability in a graph by paths
of unmarked nodes), they are formally encoded differently, with Yang’s
proof exhibiting by far the largest formalization overhead.

Yang’s proof is

Notably, Yang’s proof avoids using graphs in non-spatial reasoning.
In other words, the mathematical representation of the traversed graph
isn’t an explicit parameter to the various spatial
predicates. Instead, these are parametrized by abstractions of the
graph that do decompose, such as: sequences of nodes encoding certain
paths, sets of marked and unmarked nodes, the graph’s spanning tree,
etc.
The inability to directly access the graph mathematically, and having
to reason about it by proxy abstractions, even ones that support
decomposition, is responsible for most of the complexity in Yang’s
proof. More recent

sought to avoid the indirection
by explicitly parametrizing the spatial predicates with the full
mathematical graph, but none considered graph decomposition, nor
tackled Schorr-Waite to improve on Yang’s proof.

In this paper, we start from the observation that having graphs as
explicit non-spatial arguments is essential for avoiding the
complexity of reasoning by proxy. However, to work effectively with
graphs, one must: (1) Generalize graphs to
partial graphs
by
admitting
dangling edges
. These are edges whose source node is
in the graph, but whose sink node isn’t. As we shall see, partial
graphs admit natural decomposition, because an edge crossing the
divide of the decomposition is preserved as dangling in the component
of its source. (2) Utilize the decomposition by
introducing
morphisms
over partial graphs. These are functions
and predicates that, when passed a decomposed graph as
input,
distribute
over the sub-components.
Notions similar to partial graphs have been defined
before
(
Wang 2019
;
Costa et al. [n. d.]
)
, but without exploiting
the induced distributivity and morphisms in verification.

Specifically, we note that partial graphs form a
partial
commutative monoid (PCM)
. A PCM is a triple
(U,\bullet,e)
, where

\bullet
(pronounced “join”) is a partial, commutative and
associative binary operation over

, with unit

. If
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\bullet\gamma_{2}
, we say that
\gamma
decomposes into
\gamma_{1}
and

\gamma_{2}
. In general, PCMs
provide a fundamental framework for considering data structure
decomposition, which explains their widespread use in separation
logic, with instances including heaps, permissions, and
histories
(
Bornat et al. 2005
;
Calcagno et al. 2007
;
Jensen and Birkedal 2012
;
Krishnaswami et al. 2012
;
Ley-Wild and Nanevski 2013
;
Dinsdale-Young et al. 2013
;
Turon et al. 2014
;
Jung et al. 2015
;
Sergey et al. 2015b
;
Nanevski et al. 2019
;
Krishna et al. 2020
;
Farka et al. 2021

PCMs induce a notion of structure-preserving function (i.e., PCM
morphism). Bringing morphisms front-and-center when reasoning about
graphs is our first contribution, justifying the adjective
“algebraic” in the title of the paper.
Given PCMs
A
and
B
, the function
f:A\rightarrow B
is a
morphism if the following equations hold.

(1)

\displaystyle f\ e_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{B}

(2)

\displaystyle f\ (\gamma_{1}\bullet_{A}\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\ \gamma_{1}\bullet_{B}f\ \gamma_{2}

Focusing on equation (
2
), it says that
f

distributes over the join operation of the domain PCM, and explains
the ways in which morphisms apply to verification.
2
2

2

This paper
only considers morphisms that distribute unconditionally. Partial
morphisms that distribute only if a certain condition on
\gamma_{1}
and

\gamma_{2}
is satisfied are useful and have been developed to an extent
by
Farka et al. 2021
.

First, morphisms mediate between PCMs, as the join operation of
A
is
mapped to the join operation of
B
. In this paper we will use
morphisms between various PCMs, but an important example is to morph
the PCM of graphs to the PCM of propositions of separation logic
(equivalently, to the PCM of sets of heaps). Connecting graphs to
heaps allows a suitable heap modification to be abstracted as a
modification to the graph, thereby elevating low-level separation
logic reasoning about pointers into higher-level reasoning about
graphs.
For example, if a node in a graph is represented as a heap storing the
node’s adjacency list, mutating the pointers in this heap corresponds
at the graph level to modifying the node’s edges.

Second, computing a value of
f
over a graph that is divided into two
components (
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in equation (
2
)) can
be achieved by applying
f
over
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
individually, and
joining the results. The left-to-right direction of this equation is
used in proofs to localize the consideration to the part of the graph
that’s being modified, say
\gamma_{1}
, while the remaining part
\gamma_{2}
is
propagated and re-attached later to the modified version of
\gamma_{1}
by
using the right-to-left direction of the equation.
Therefore, in this particular aspect, morphisms serve the same purpose
as framing, but act in a different and complementary manner.

As an illustration of morphisms, consider
filtering
a graph to
obtain a sub-graph containing only the nodes from a specific set.
Filtering differs from taking a sub-graph in standard graph theory, in
that the filtered sub-graph retains—as dangling—the edges into the
part avoided by the filter. This enables re-attaching the two parts
later on, after either has been processed. Clearly, to filter a
composite graph it suffices to filter the components and join the
results, making filtering a morphism from the PCM of graphs to itself.

Not all useful graph abstractions are morphisms, but even those that
aren’t gain from their interaction with morphisms. For
example,
reachability
between the nodes in a graph is often
characterized as a
global
property
(
Krishna et al. 2020
)
,
precisely because it isn’t a morphism, as it can’t be computed from
graph’s sub-components as per equation (
2
). A
common verification pattern for graph algorithms is to consider
reachability via paths consisting of a certain subset of nodes (e.g.,
unmarked ones). In our setting, such properties obtain
by
composing
reachability with filtering; that is by
considering reachability in the filtered sub-graph consisting of the
desired nodes only. More generally, we will be able to state intricate
specifications, such as that for Schorr-Waite, by similarly composing
generic graph primitives drawn from a small vocabulary.

In the presence of morphisms, the specifications can utilize still
higher levels of abstraction. Our second contribution notes that
useful graph transformations are encodable as instances of
a
higher-order combinator
(like map over sequences in
functional programming), itself also a morphism from the graph PCM to
itself.
Morphisms thus relate different levels of the abstraction stack that
has heaps and framing at the bottom, and localize the reasoning across
all levels.

We illustrate the approach with a novel specification and proof of the
Schorr-Waite algorithm, which is our third contribution. From the
high-level point of view, the key challenge of a Schorr-Waite
verification is dealing with two structures: a linked list that’s
embedded, in place, among the nodes of the graph. We utilize PCMs,
morphisms, and combinators in an essential way to formalize how these
two structures interact with each other, with graph decomposition,
with the underlying heap, and with framing. We also minimize the
formal development that’s specific to Schorr-Waite, in favor of
increased reliance on generic graph primitives and their theory. This
results in a specification and proof of some conceptual simplicity and
conciseness, extending to the mechanization in Coq which required just
110 lines of explicit proofs. The line count includes the development
specific to Schorr-Waite, and excludes the Coq library of general
graph and morphism lemmas—that we also developed—precisely because
that library is general and reusable.

3.
Partial Graphs

Dangling edges.

The example in Section
2

pointers
link disjoint parts of a list.
Similarly, dangling
edges
are needed to connect disjoint parts
of a graph. However, unlike lists, which have a single dangling
pointer, graphs generally have multiple dangling edges bridging the
divide (Fig

To parallel Section
2

was treated as a parameter of
\mathit{list}
, one might consider grouping the
dandling edges of a graph into a set that parametrizes the predicate

\mathit{graph}
, the graph analogue to
\mathit{list}
.

Bornat et al. 2004
explored this approach, but it
resulted in an unsatisfactory definition of the predicate that relied
on a program-specific traversal order in addition to the graph itself.

In this paper, we instead adopt the approach that
embeds
the
information about dangling edges directly into the graph, leading to

partial graphs
.
We focus on directed partial graphs, but the approach applies to the
undirected variant as well. Specifically, we choose a graph
representation where each node is associated with its adjacency list,
but we allow for the possibility that a node in the adjacency list

need not be present in the graph itself
. More formally, a
partial graph (hereafter, simply graph) of type

Schorr-Waite Algorithm

The goal of a graph-marking algorithm is to traverse a graph starting
from some root node
r
and mark the reachable nodes. The obvious way
to implement this functionality is as a recursive function that
traverses the graph in a depth-first, left-to-right manner. However,
as graph marking is typically employed in garbage collection—when
space is sparse—recursive implementation isn’t optimal, as it
expends space on the stack to keep track of the recursive calls and
execute backtracking. The idea of Schorr-Waite is that the information
about backtracking can be maintained within the graph itself, while
the graph is traversed iteratively in a loop.

We consider the variant of Schorr-Waite that operates over binary
graphs. We also have the contents of a node record the node’s status
in the traversal by setting the contents to:

O

if the node is
unmarked, i.e., the traversal hasn’t encountered the node;

L

if
the node has been traversed once towards the left sub-graph;

R

if the traversal of the left sub-graph has completed, and the
traversal of the right sub-graph began;

X

if both sub-graphs
have been traversed.
Thus, we use graphs of type
\textit{binary{-}graph}\ \{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle{\textsf{{O}}}$},\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle{\textsf{{L}}}$},\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle{\textsf{{R}}}$},\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle{\textsf{{X}}}$}\}
, and the predicate
\mathit{graph}
to describe their heap layout.

7.
Related work

Proofs of Schorr-Waite in separation logic.

The starting point of our paper has been Yang’s
proof
(
Yang 2001a
;
Yang 2001b
)
, which is an early work on
separation logic generally, and the first work on graphs in separation
logic specifically. The staring point of distinction with our proof is
that Yang doesn’t use mathematical graphs as an explicit argument to
the
\mathit{graph}
predicate, but rather relies on proxy abstractions, such
as the spanning tree of the graph, and various subsets of nodes. These
proxy abstractions aren’t independent, and thus a significant formal
overhead must be devoted to keeping them syncronized.

Considering his first proof too complex, Yang tackled Schorr-Waite
algorithm again using
relational
separation
logic
(
Yang 2007
)
. Relational logic establishes a
contextual refinement between two programs; in the case of
Schorr-Waite, a refinement with the obvious DFS implementation of
graph marking.
While relational reasoning is powerful and useful in its own right,
it’s clear that proving refinement between two programs is much more
demanding than establishing a pre- and postcondition for a single
program, as we do in this paper.
Further optimization for the relational proof is given by

Crespo and Kunz 2011
, who show that Schorr-Waite is equivalent to
DFS, and mechanize the proof in around 3000 lines in Coq.

Non-separation proofs of Schorr-Waite.

Being a standard verification benchmark for graph algorithms, Schorr-Waite has
been proved numerous times, using a variety of different
approaches. These ranged from automated
ones
(
Suzuki 1976
;
Loginov et al. 2006
;
Roever 1977
;
Leino 2010
)
, to studies of
the algorithm’s mathematical properties
(
Bubel 2007
;
Mehta and Nipkow 2003
;
Topor 1979
;
Griffiths 1979
;
Morris 1982
;
Dershowitz 1980
;
Gries 1979
;
Bornat 2000
;
Abrial 2003
;
Hubert and Marché 2005
)
, to proofs
based on program
transformation
(
Dufourd 2014
;
Preoteasa and Back 2010
;
Gerhart 1979
;
Ward 1996
;
Crespo and Kunz 2011
;
Giorgino et al. 2010
;
Preoteasa and Back 2012
;
Broy and Pepper 1982
)
.
The important difference from this work is that we explicitly wanted
to support and utilize framing in our proof, as framing is the key feauture of separation logic.

Graphs in separation logic, without PCMs.

In the search for a
\mathit{graph}
predicate that enables decomposition,

Bornat et al. 2004
define a partial graph as a
recursive tree-shaped term, encoding the following strategy. Given
some default traversal order, the first time a node is encountered,
it’s explicitly recorded in the term. Every later occurence is
recorded as a reference (i.e., pointer) to the first one. This enables
that closed graphs can be encoded in a way that facilitates
decomposition, but is dependent on the traversal order, which is
problematic, as it prevents developing general libraries of lemmas
about graphs. We used the PCM of partial graphs to remove the
restriction to closed graphs, which also removes the traversal-order
dependence.

Wang 2019
and
Wang et al. 2019

parametrize their
\mathit{graph}
predicate with a closed mathematical graph,
and express program invariant in terms of it. Because a closed graph
doesn’t necessarily decompose into closed sub-graphs, these invariants
aren’t considered under distribution. Thus, to prove that a state
modification maintains the graph invariant, one typically must resort
to reasoning about the whole graph. This global reasoning can be
ameliorated by introducing additional logical connectives and rules,
such as e.g., overlapping conjunction and ramified frame
rules
(
Hobor and Villard 2013
)
. In contrast, by relying on PCMs and
morphisms, we were able to stay within standard separation logic over
heaps.

Graphs in separation logic, with PCMs.

Sergey et al. 2015a
and
Nanevski 2016

parametrized their
\mathit{graph}
predicate by a heap (itself a PCM), that
serves as a representation of a partial graph. Heaps obviously
decompose under disjoint union. However, since the
\mathit{graph}
predicate
includes the restriction that the implemented graph must be closed,
the predicate itself doesn’t distribute. This is worked around by
introducing helper relations for when one graph is a sub-graph of
another, which our formulation provides for free (e.g.,
\gamma_{1}
is a
subgraph of
\gamma
iff
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\bullet\gamma_{2}
). These works don’t
consider PCM morphisms or Schorr-Waite.

More recently,
Krishna et al. 2020

and
Meyer et al. 2023
introduced a theory of
flows
,
as a framework to study the decomposition of graph properties. Flows
evoke Yang’s first proof, in that they serve as
decomposition-supporting proxies for the specification of the graph.
In contrast, we parametrize the reasoning by the whole graph, and compute proxies by
morphisms, when needed. That said, flows are intented for automated
reasoning, which we haven’t considered.

Finally,
Costa et al. [n. d.]
define the PCM of

pregraphs
. A pregraph is like our partial graph, but it may
contain incoming dangling edges, not just outgoing ones that sufficed
for us. The paper proceeds to define a separation logic over
pregraphs, much as separation logic over heaps is classically defined
using local actions
(
Calcagno et al. 2007
)
. In contrast, we use a
standard separation logic over heaps, where (partial) graphs are
merely a secondary user-level PCM. Having heaps and graphs coexist is
necessary for verifying pointer-based graph algorithms such as
Schorr-Waite, but it requires PCM morphisms to mediate between heaps and graphs.

Graphs algebraically.

Recent works in functional programming, theorem proving and category
theory have proposed treating graphs
algebraically
(
Master 2021
;
Master 2022
;
Liell-Cock and Schrijvers 2024
;
Mokhov 2017
;
Mokhov 2022
)
,
though so far without application to separation logic. Most
recently,
Kidney and Wu 2025
share with us the representation of
graphs as maps, which they further endow with the algebraic structure
of rings, comprising distinct monoids for vertices and for edges. They
also propose several different algebraic constructions along with the
associated morphisms. Their application is in implementing graph
algorithms as state-free coinductive programs in Agda and Haskell.
In contrast, we use the algebra of graphs for specification and
verification of programs, not for their implementation, as our
programs in general, and our variant of Schorr-Waite in particular,
are implemented in an imperative pointer-based language customary for
separation logic. Our focus on separation logic also gives rise to
somewhat different monoidal structures. In particular, as we need to
relate graphs to pointers and framing, we focus on partiality and,
correspondingly, use a monoid of graphs whose join operation is very
different from the constructions considered in the above work.

8.
Conclusion

The starting observations of our paper is that graphs form a PCM if
one admits dangling edges, thus deriving partial graphs.
PCM representation is essential to define how graphs compose, and
gives rise to associated algebraic notions of distributivity and
morphisms.
Decomposition, distributivity and morphisms enable that: (1)
mathematical graphs can be related to, and abstract, the low-level
pointer manipulation of separation logic, and (2) reasoning about the
whole graph can be reduced to reasoning about sub-graphs, and carried
out abstractly, without manipulating pointers.

Specifically, using PCM morphisms (e.g.,
erasure
,
sinks
,

\mathit{nodes}
,
map
, etc.) enabled us to reduce various proofs
about the graph to proofs about sub-graphs. For example, the proof of
POP in Fig.
12
uses distributivity of
morphisms in most of the lines, to exploit that the graphs
\gamma
and

\gamma^{\prime}
share the sub-graph
\gamma{\setminus p_{0}}
.
Even when the graph properties aren’t morphisms (e.g.,
closed
,

reach
, etc.), they benefit from morphisms. Form example, while

\textit{closed}\ (\gamma{\setminus p_{0}})
doesn’t follow from
\textit{closed}\ \gamma
, the
proof of case
(b)
in Fig.
12
exploits
that
closed
is defined in terms of morphisms
sinks
and
\mathit{nodes}
,
to exploit the facts about
\mathit{nodes}\ (\gamma{\setminus p_{0}})
and

\textit{sinks}\ (\gamma{\setminus p_{0}})
that are given by the assumptions of the
proof.

Utility of morphisms isn’t limited to distributivity, as interaction
between different morphisms is also of essence. For example, while the
distributivity of
inset
and
restore
is used in
Fig.
12
, arguably an even more
important application of
inset
and
restore
is in stating the key
invariants of Schorr-Waite in Fig.
9
, where they
interact with the erasure morphism in the equations

\textit{inset}\ {\alpha}\ {\gamma}=|{\gamma}|
and
\textit{restore}\ {\alpha}\ {t}\ {\gamma}=|{\gamma_{0}}|
.

We also introduced the higher-order combinator
map
and
employed it to define
inset
and
restore
, where the function

if-mark
is mapped over the graph, but with two different
arguments, to capture two different properties. Mapping the same
function lets us prove general lemmas about
if-mark
once, and
use them several times. For example, Lemma
5.1
is used
to prove both equations (
16
) and (
17
) of
Lemma
5.2
, which are in turn both used in the proofs of
POP in Section
6.2
and of PUSH in the appendix.

Finally, the whole development of this paper is mechanized
in
Nanevski et al. 2006
;
Nanevski et al. 2010
;
Hoare Type Theory HTT
, an
extension of Coq for reasoning in separation logic via types. The
encoding of Schorr-Waite in the mechanization differs somewhat from the
one shown here, in that it uses functional (i.e., non-mutable)
variables, but this technicality has no bearing on the results.
