Associations between the completion rate of the unsupervised community swallowing training and swallowing function in older adults with mild dysphagia: a quasi-experimental pre-post study without control
Authors: Yu Wang, Hongli Liu, Quan Liu, Bingbing Li, Mingming Wang, Ying Yang, Li Heping, Wang Liugen, Xi Zeng, Hongji Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the associations between the unsupervised Simple Swallowing Training Program (SSTP) and swallowing function, and factors influencing the completion rate and functional improvement.MethodsThis quasi-experimental pre-post study included community-dwelling older adults with mild dysphagia in China between 2024 and 2025. Each participant performed the SSTP twice daily without supervision for 15 consecutive days and provided online feedback on their progress. Pre- and post-study swallowing function and quality of life were assessed using the Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP) and Dysphagia Handicap Index (DHI). Participants were divided into 31 groups according to their SSTP completion status. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests were used to explore the minimum training frequency of the SSTP. Univariate and multivariate linear regression models were used to explore the factors influencing the MBSImP improvement and the unsupervised SSTP completion rate.ResultsThere were 741 participants. The pre- and post-study MBSImP differences were statistically significant when the SSTP completion sessions were ≥20. Therefore, and the recommended minimum SSTP training frequency was 20 sessions. There was significant improvement in the oral, pharyngeal, and total MBSImP and DHI (p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in the esophageal MBSImP (p > 0.05). Age, previous stroke, neurodegenerative diseases, and previous head and neck cancer were factors only influencing the MBSImP improvement (p < 0.05).ConclusionsCompleting ≥20 SSTP sessions was associated with significant improvements in oropharyngeal swallowing and quality of life. Age and swallowing-related medical history were associated with functional improvement, but not with the completion rate of the SSTP.Keywords: Presbyphagia, geriatrics, quality of life, rehabilitation

Introduction

Dysphagia is the medical term for swallowing difficulties which refer to problems with any stage of the swallowing process [1]. It affects approximately 20% of community-dwelling older adults [2,3]. In this population, the etiology of dysphagia can be broadly classified into pathological and physiological factors [4]. Many cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative disorders can affect swallowing efficiency, safety, control, and coordination [5,6]. Mild dysphagia is often unresponsive to rehabilitation interventions, with swallowing function typically remaining impaired despite therapy [7]. Physiologically, ageing is associated with degenerative changes in the oropharyngeal region. This can lead to presbyphagia [8], characterized by limited oral intake, slowed eating, reduced food intake, choking during meals, and difficulty swallowing pills [9]. Long-term lack of exercise and excessive reliance on liquid or semi-solid diets can lead to disuse atrophy of swallowing function [10]. Studies have shown that most swallowing problems are physiological and mild in community-dwelling older adults [11]. In such cases, timely intervention and training can effectively prevent further functional deterioration and break the vicious cycle between swallowing and nutrition or emotion [12].

Entirely relying on clinical departments to address community swallowing issues can lead to wastage and strain on medical resources [13]. Community intervention is expected to help address this concern and has gained attention [14]. There are some challenges with community-based swallowing interventions, such as cost and benefit balance, diversification of residents’ demands, and patient adherence [15,16]. To address these problems, a general and user-friendly community-based swallowing training program has been developed by a multidisciplinary team [17]. It was named the Simple Swallowing Training Program (SSTP) and was primarily designed for community-dwelling older adults with mild dysphagia. The SSTP has incorporated many practical clinical rehabilitation techniques and is equipment-independent. It consists of a warm-up and 11 formal behavioral sections, with each session lasting <20 min. The original study reported that training twice daily for 15 days significantly improved swallowing function and quality of life [17].

However, there are still many unresolved issues regarding the SSTP. In the original study, swallowing function was assessed using the Gugging Swallowing Screen and 10-item Eating Assessment Tool [18,19]. Although these tools quantified swallowing function from both subjective and objective perspectives, there was lack of accuracy compared to the gold standard of videofluoroscopy [20]. Additionally, it is still unknown how many sessions of SSTP are needed at a minimum to be associated with significant improvements in swallowing function. The SSTP was conceptualized as an unsupervised training program. However, it remains unclear whether an entirely participant-driven SSTP without supervision can still be effective. The factors associated with the SSTP completion rate and the degree of functional improvement are not fully understood. Furthermore, the randomized controlled trial in community setting was at risk of intervention contamination. For example, such studies typically cannot manage participants within hospital settings, and participants may engage in training on their own privately. Some physical activities, inter-participant communication, and medication use may also contaminate interventions. Addressing these issues can help provide reliable evidence for training effects, improve cost-effectiveness for users, and offer references for community promotion, protocol optimization, and individualized rehabilitation [21]. Based on these factors, the current study was conducted with the following objectives: First, we aimed to investigate the minimum training frequency associated with significant functional improvement. Second, we identified factors influencing the functional improvement and the SSTP completion rate. This helps strengthen program design, implement targeted interventions, and optimize resource allocation, which might ultimately benefit the effectiveness and feasibility of the unsupervised SSTP.

Methods

Procedures and participants

This study followed the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University First Affiliated Hospital on 27th May 2024 (Record No.: 2024-KY-0115). This study has obtained the approval from the original developers of the SSTP. All the participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted from June 2024 to May 2025. There were three Chinese hospitals involved as research centers. For each research center, two cooperative neighborhoods were randomly selected in each county-level administrative district of the city. Posters were used in each neighborhood to publish a brief introduction to this study. Interested individuals could contact us for details and be included through voluntary response sampling. Interested individuals first described their swallowing conditions to the doctors, who then assessed whether there was suspected dysphagia. Those with suspected dysphagia were invited to undergo the modified barium swallow test for further diagnosis, which was part of the inclusion criteria. The staff responsible for recruiting participants were required not to deal with their acquaintances.

The participants were community-dwelling older adults with mild dysphagia. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥60 years. (2) Mild dysphagia: In the 17-item Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP), there were 1–5 impaired items, with little or no residue, and occasional or no aspiration/penetration [22]. (3) No history of hospitalization in the past six months. (4) Clear consciousness. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Severe liver and kidney failure, tumors, blood or other systemic diseases. (2) Physical dysfunction that leads to being unsuitable for or unable to perform the SSTP. (3) Acute or chronic physical pain. (4) Individuals who have recently received treatment that might affect swallowing function. (5) Gastrostomy tube placement. (6) Structural abnormalities or pain in the mouth, pharynx, or esophagus. Participants first underwent baseline assessments including the MBSImP and demographic data, lifestyle, and disease information, and then received a reference video and manual for the SSTP. 2119 interested individuals contacted this study. Among them, 1433 were suspected of dysphagia and were screened for participation. 1118 cases met the criteria and were included in the study. Among the 315 excluded participants, 11, 59, 48, 142, 21, and 34 cases met the exclusion criteria items 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively.

There were 11 behavior-training sections in the SSTP [17], as shown in Appendix S1. The study lasted 15 days for each participant. Participants performed the SSTP twice a day without any supervision from the study, for a total of 30 sessions (duration = 15 days, frequency = twice a day). All sessions were self-directed. Participants were informed that they should avoid significant changes in their daily routines and exercise intensity during the study. At noon and in the evening, participants reported their completion of the SSTP through an online questionnaire using WenJuanXing (https://www.wjx.cn/). The statement was: ‘Do you think you completed today’s first/second SSTP?’ with options of ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. When participants copied the SSTP video, we informed them of the criteria for completing a single SSTP: (1) Completing all sections; (2) Pausing for less than 10 min during the training; (3) Completing each section correctly according to the video’s pace; and (4) Ensuring that the amplitude and intensity of the movements matched the video. The researchers explained the key points of each section and demonstrated both incorrect and correct examples to ensure the participants’ understanding. Participants were informed that the completion of the SSTP would not result in any rewards or punishments, and the first priority was to report the truth and integrity. Participants were not required to record and upload their training videos after each training session because this could lead to potential response bias. The researchers called participants who had not reported the previous day to inquire about the results. If contact was unavailable, participants were allowed to make up for the missed report within three days. Those who could not be contacted for three consecutive days were excluded. Participants had the right to voluntarily withdraw from the study. On the sixteenth day, participants were instructed to visit the research center for outcome assessments. During the study, the staff were not allowed to interact unnecessarily with the participants.

In the data analysis, the SSTP completion rate was used as a regression-dependent variable. The completion rate was calculated as follows: (the count an individual considered himself or herself to complete the SSTP)/30. For example, if someone stated that they completed it 27 out of 30 times, their completion rate would be 0.9 (90%). There were at most 19 independent variables, and the estimated sample size was 20 times the number of independent variables [23]. Assuming a sample loss rate of 20%, at least 475 cases were required. An increase in the sample size was considered to enhance the validity of the conclusions. Within the budget limit, we recruited as many participants as possible for this study.

Assessments

The assessors were unaware of the SSTP completion rate of each participant. All assessments were conducted face to face. The baseline and outcome assessments were completed within a single day, respectively.

Swallowing function

The MBSImP was used to assess swallowing function before and after the study [22]. It included oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal aspects, with a total of 17 items. Participants underwent modified barium swallow tests under 30-frame Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Studies for the MBSImP. Various consistencies of barium meal were prepared using thickening agents: thin liquid, nectar liquid, thin honey liquid, pudding/semi-solid, and biscuits [24]. For thin and nectar liquid, participants first swallowed 5 ml, followed by a small cup, and then continuous swallowing. For the rest, 5 ml was swallowed each time. The first swallow was a thin liquid and was not scored. Subsequent swallows were scored by the assessors based on their overall impressions, which reflected the worst condition. The assessment included both lateral and anterior views. The assessors were experienced certified language speech therapists. They completed the entire course and passed the exams under the copyright holder of the MBSImP (https://www.mbsimp.com/). Each rating was scored independently by two assessors. When the inter-rater reliability was higher than 90%, the final score was the average of the two scores and rounded up. The MBSImP total scores could range from 0 to 55, with a higher score indicating worse swallowing function.

Swallowing-related quality of life

The Dysphagia Handicap Index (DHI) was used to assess swallowing-related quality of life before and after the study. It is a self-reported questionnaire that has been cross-culturally adapted into Chinese Mandarin and includes the impact of swallowing on physiological, psychological, and functional aspects [25]. The DHI consists of 25 items, and the total score could range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating worse quality of life.

General information

Variables that might affect the SSTP completion rate were evaluated using prior knowledge. The following variables were included at baseline and used as independent variables for subsequent regression analyses: (1) Sex. (2) Age. (3) Educational level. (4) Body mass index. (5) Smoking. (6) Alcohol intake. (7) Exercise habits. (8) Hypertension. (9) Type 2 diabetes. (10) Hyperlipidemia. (11) Previous stroke. (12) Neurodegenerative diseases. (13) Sarcopenia. (14) Previous head and neck cancer. (15) Anxiety. (16) Exercise self-efficacy. The reasons for choosing each variable are shown in Appendix S2.

Disease-related variables were based on medical records. Body mass index was measured onsite, and lifestyle and general demographic characteristics were based on self-report. Smoking/drinking at least once a week in the past six months was defined as Yes in smoking/drinking. Exercising for at least one hour daily for at least four days per week over the past six months was defended as Yes in exercise habits. The exercise intensity here was at least brisk walking, meaning that strolls were not included. Sarcopenia records were first based on the most recent annual physical examinations or medical records. Participants who had not been diagnosed with sarcopenia were assessed on-site, which was based on the diagnostic criteria recommended by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019, including muscle strength, appendicular skeletal muscle mass, and physical performance [26]. Details regarding the assessment and cut-off values are shown in Appendix S2. Only participants who were not medically diagnosed and passed the on-site assessment were defined as No in sarcopenia. Anxiety was assessed using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder [27]. Exercise self-efficacy was assessed using the Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale [28].

Statistical analysis

Normality and homogeneity of variance tests were conducted for continuous variables. Normal continuous data are presented as means and standard deviations (x ± s). Categorical data are presented as counts and proportions [n (%)]. Skewed continuous data or ordinal data are presented as medians and quartiles [M (Q25, Q75)]. Participants were grouped according to their completion rates. The minimum recommended training frequency of the SSTP was defined as the point at which the pre- and post-study MBSImP differences started to be significant in each group. To explore the minimum recommended training frequency, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests were used to compare the pre- and post-study MBSImP in each group. To explore the association between the unsupervised SSTP and swallowing function and quality of life, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests were used to compare the MBSImP and DHI scores before and after the study as the skewed distribution. This analysis was conducted respectively for the total sample and those who reached the minimum recommended training frequency. In the total sample, univariate and multiple linear regression models were used to explore the factors influencing the SSTP completion rate and the MBSImP improvement. In the model where the SSTP completion rate was the dependent variable, the baseline MBSImP was additionally considered. In the model where the outcome MBSImP was the dependent variable, the SSTP completion rate was additionally included. Variables with significance in either univariate regression were included in multivariate regression analysis. In the multiple linear regression, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among variables. Variables with VIFs > 10 were considered to have severe multicollinearity issues and were excluded from the regression [29]. Missing values in covariates were imputed using multiple imputations. Cases with missing values of the DHI or MBSImP were excluded. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. SPSS AU (https://spssau.com/) was used for statistical analysis [30].

In total, 248 participants voluntarily withdrew from the study. Among them, 94 cited lack of motivation, 53 had other living arrangements, 26 were unwilling to undergo the outcome swallowing fluoroscopy assessment, and 75 cited other reasons or did not specify their reasons. Another 129 participants missed the report within three days and could not be contacted for three consecutive days. A flowchart is shown in Figure 1. Finally, 741 participants were included in the data analysis. No major illness or death occurred during the study. There were 39 items with missing values and were handled using multiple imputations. Minimum recommended training frequency and pre- versus post-study differences

The participants were divided into 31 groups based on their completion sessions of the SSTP, and the differences in the MBSImP before and after the study were compared within each group. The results showed that, as the number of completion sessions increased, the trend of significance generally increased, as shown in Figure 2. Due to the use of multiple simple comparisons, the Bonferroni method adjusted the p value to 0.0016, resulting in the finding that the difference became statistically significant when the SSTP completion sessions were ≥20, as shown in Table 2. This indicated that completing ≥20 sessions of the SSTP was associated with significant improvement in swallowing function. In addition, we found that in all subgroups that completed ≥25 SSTP sessions, the proportion of participants with an improvement in MBSImP ≥3 exceeded 50%. This might indicate that at least 25 SSTP sessions have clinical significance (Table 2).

Influencing factors of the SSTP completion rate and functional improvement

The VIFs of all the independent variables in the multiple linear regression were <1.5. After adjusting for confounding factors, exercise habits, sarcopenia, and exercise self-efficacy were identified as factors influencing both the SSTP completion rate and the MBSImP improvement (p < 0.05). Age, previous stroke, neurodegenerative diseases, and previous head and neck cancer were factors only influencing the MBSImP improvement (p < 0.05), as shown in Table 4. This indicated differences between the factors influencing the SSTP completion rate and swallowing functional improvement. Additionally, the baseline MBSImP was not significantly associated with the SSTP completion rate (p = 0.079), whereas the completion rate was significantly associated with the MBSImP Improvement (p < 0.001). The similar results were obtained using logistic regression models, as seen in Appendices S3 and S4.

Discussion

Swallowing problems in older adults have raised public health concerns [31]. The SSTP has been developed to provide a general, user-friendly, and easy-to-promote swallowing intervention in community settings [17]. The current study showed that the unsupervised SSTP was associated with significant improvement in swallowing function and quality of life, consistent with the original SSTP study [17]. The current study included the gold standard of Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Studies to assess swallowing function [20], preliminarily explored the recommended SSTP training frequency, and investigated the factors influencing the completion rate and functional outcomes. These findings can provide references for the promotion and application of the SSTP and are considered practically significant.

This study showed that the unsupervised SSTP was associated with significant improvement in oropharyngeal swallowing function. The sample was randomly selected, so the participants’ compliance was considered statistically representative of the population to some extend. This indicated that, although the SSTP completion rate could be varied among individuals, the population was expected to show significant functional improvement. These findings highlighted the value of SSTP in population health and community applications. However, the unsupervised SSTP did not appear to significantly affect esophageal swallowing. The reason might be that the MBSImP focuses on oropharyngeal swallowing and only includes one esophageal item [22]. Moreover, SSTP was not originally designed with the primary goal of improving esophageal swallowing [17]. Lastly, swallowing problems in community-dwelling older adults can be partly physiological, and most issues initially manifest in the oropharyngeal phase [8]. The median score for the esophageal MBSImP was 0. This indicated an overall non-to-mild impairment, which might suggest limited room for improvement.

Based on the intensity and frequency of clinical rehabilitation in China, the SSTP was originally designed as a 15-day training program with 30 sessions [17]. Exploring the minimum recommended training frequency can help improve adherence and cost-effectiveness. The pre- and post-study differences in swallowing function and health-related quality of life became steadily significant in the participants who competed at least 66% of the SSTP sessions. This finding highlighted the critical role of consistent participation in rehabilitation programs. It might indicate that completing two-thirds of the sessions may be essential for realizing the intended benefits of the SSTP. In addition, this study showed that approximately half of the participants reached this minimum recommended training frequency without supervision. This raises considerations for future designs and modifications of the SSTP to achieve higher participant adherence. It should also be noted that, while we determined the minimum recommended SSTP training frequency, the observational design limited the ability to analyze the optimal training arrangement. The average item scores of the MBSImP are three points, thus this study considered a score of 3 as the threshold of clinical significance. Based on this stricter standard, the recommended training sessions were 25. This finding might provide additional guidance for clinical practice, ensuring that patients can achieve substantial progress in the recovery of swallowing function.

Exercise habits, sarcopenia, and Exercise Self-Efficacy were identified as factors influencing the SSTP completion rate and the MBSImP improvement. Subsequent analysis revealed a significant correlation between the completion rate and the MBSImP improvement (β = 0.678, p < 0.001). Therefore, the association between these factors and the functional improvement might be mediated by the completion rate. Individuals with exercise habits have typically integrated physical activities into their daily lives [32]. This can help them follow the structured SSTP plan. The insufficient physical strength caused by sarcopenia can increase the difficulty to complete the training and lead to lack of motivation to continue participating [33]. Exercise Self-Efficacy refers to an individual’s confidence to successfully complete a physical task [28]. People with higher Exercise Self-Efficacy typically believe that they can complete the training and are less likely to be defeated by difficulties or fatigue during training [34]. This confidence can motivate them to engage more actively in the training. It should be noted that age, previous stroke, neurodegenerative diseases, and previous head and neck cancer can only influence the functional improvement but not the SSTP completion rate. Age and dysphagia-related diseases typically affect physiological conditions and physical resilience [35,36]. This can directly influence the effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions. Older participants or those with dysphagia-related medical histories might have encountered difficulties during training or experienced slow recovery [35,36]. These physiological limitations can affect outcomes and typically not influence psychological factors such as motivation and confidence.

This study had several limitations. First, the completion of the unsupervised SSTP and some baseline variables were based on self-report, which might lead to underreporting, overreporting, or inaccurate responses. These issues might lead to social desirability bias, which in turn affects the accuracy and generalizability of the result [37]. Second, without a control group, it was challenging to determine whether the observed effects on swallowing function and quality of life were solely due to the SSTP or if they could be attributed to other external factors. A control group could help to identify the effects of the intervention. Third, the study experienced a considerable dropout rate. This loss of participants could introduce bias, as the dropout group was not entirely statistically comparable with those who completed the study. This might indicate that the adherence to SSTP was lower in the real world. Fourth, the observation period was set to 15 days to reduce potential dropout, but this limited this study to explore long-term effects of the SSTP. Fifth, the sample size in each group was relatively small in analyzing the minimum recommended training frequency. Multi-group-controlled studies could be designed to validate the current conclusions. Additionally, using screening questionnaires, such as the Eating Assessment Tool [38], instead of doctor checks might have facilitated the participant selection process. Lastly, we did not consider older adults with cognitive issues, which led to the limited applicability of our conclusions.
