An integrated single-cell transcriptomic and microbiome dataset of orbital adipose tissue in thyroid eye disease
Authors: Xiang Xu, Fei Wang, Weiqi Liu, Haowen Li, Yingyu Wang, Yuan Li, Bing Wang, Mingming Liu, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid eye disease (TED), also historically referred to as thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) or Graves’ orbitopathy (GO), is an autoimmune ophthalmopathy characterized by inflammation and adipogenesis within orbital adipose (OA) tissue. While the contribution of immune cell infiltration and fibroblast activation is recognized, the cellular heterogeneity and the potential role of local microbial dysbiosis in the orbital microenvironment remain poorly defined. Here, we present a multi-omics dataset derived from OA of patients with TED and healthy controls collected at Beijing Tongren Hospital. The dataset comprises single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data generated from human orbital adipose tissue and subcutaneous fat. Following quality control filtering and doublet removal, a total of 61,302 high-quality singlet cells were retained for downstream analyses, capturing the transcriptomic landscape of orbital fibroblasts, immune subsets, and vascular cells. In parallel, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on orbital adipose tissue samples collected from the integrated study cohort, alongside environmental samples for contamination assessment, to characterize the low-biomass orbital microbiome DNA signatures. Raw sequencing reads, quality-controlled gene-expression matrices, and contamination-corrected microbial feature tables are provided. Our dataset provides an opportunity to investigate the crosstalk between host cellular immunity and potential local microbiome signatures, facilitating hypothesis generation regarding host-microbiome crosstalk and the exploration of novel therapeutic targets for TED.

Background & Summary

Thyroid eye disease (TED)1, also historically referred to as thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO) or Graves’ orbitopathy (GO), represents the most common extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease2,3. The pathology is driven by an autoimmune response targeting the orbital connective tissue, leading to orbital fibroblast proliferation, adipogenesis, and hyaluronan accumulation4. Despite recent advances, the precise mechanisms initiating this local autoimmune cascade remain elusive5. Current research suggests a gut-eye axis6,7 where intestinal dysbiosis modulates immune responses8, however, the existence of locally resident microbial DNA in orbital tissue and its potential functional relevance remains a subject of intense debate and high clinical interest.

OA is distinct from other fat depots due to its neuroectodermal origin and complex cellular composition9. To date, an atlas integrating high-resolution cellular landscape with the local microbial environment of OA is lacking10. Understanding the microenvironment is important, as the interaction between resident fibroblasts and infiltrating T cells may be modulated by microbial antigens or metabolites11, potentially perpetuating chronic inflammation.

We established a cohort of TED patients undergoing orbital decompression and healthy controls undergoing cosmetic blepharoplasty at Beijing Tongren Hospital. We employed an experimental design to generate a parallel dataset of scRNA-seq (10x Genomics) and 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq). Recognizing the low-biomass nature of orbital tissue12, we implemented sterile collection protocols and included matched environmental negative controls (air and saline swabs) to computationally filter contaminants using SourceTracker (0.9.1) and Decontam algorithms.

High-resolution transcriptomic profiling reveals different orbital fibroblast subpopulations and their gene expression programs associated with differentiation into adipocytes13. Concurrently, the data support the characterization of the local immune repertoire, with a focus on uncovering T-cell exhaustion signatures and defining macrophage polarization states within the orbital tissue14,15. Integrative analyses further provide a means to interrogate the existence of a different orbital microbiome and examine its statistical associations with specific host transcriptional programs. Our resources are essential for bridging the historical divide between cellular immunology and microbiology, thereby advancing the understanding of ocular autoimmunity.

It is important to note that the cellular and microbial profiles reported here derive from a Chinese patient cohort, and their interpretation should consider known ethnic and sociodemographic differences in TED presentation. A large multicenter study of 4,157 Chinese TED patients reported a mean age of 46.0 ± 16.4 years, a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.8:1, and a predominance of moderate-to-severe disease (82.5%)16. These demographic features contrast with those of predominantly Western cohorts, which typically exhibit a higher female-to-male ratio (~6:1) and a greater proportion of mild disease. Furthermore, Chinese patients possess anatomically shallower orbits, resulting in lower mean Hertel exophthalmometry readings compared with Caucasian populations. These differences highlight the value of this Chinese dataset as a complement to existing Western cohorts and underscore the importance of population-specific reference ranges in TED research. Cross-ethnic comparative analyses leveraging both this and other publicly available datasets may yield insights into the genetic, environmental, and sociocultural determinants of TED pathogenesis and progression.

Materials and Methods

Sample collection and ethics

Orbital adipose tissue samples were collected from patients with TED and healthy controls at Beijing Tongren Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. TRECKY2018-056-GZ (2022)-07) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The consent form covered voluntary participation, the scope of biological sample collection (orbital adipose tissue, conjunctival sac swabs, deep nasal swabs, and stool samples), the intended use for multi-omics research on thyroid eye disease, data storage for 10 years, and data sharing. Participants were informed that: (i) de-identified 16S rRNA sequencing data (bacterial sequences) would be deposited in a public repository (NCBI SRA); (ii) de-identified human single-cell RNA sequencing data (raw reads) would be deposited in a controlled-access repository (GSA-Human at CNCB); (iii) processed expression matrices would be deposited in an open-access repository (Zenodo); and (iv) no individually identifiable information would be shared. All participants provided consent for data sharing under these terms. Participants were enrolled at Beijing Tongren Hospital from January 2021 to December 2024. Patients with TED (diagnosed according to the Bartley diagnostic criteria) undergoing orbital decompression surgery were recruited as the case group. Control orbital and subcutaneous adipose tissue samples were collected from patients without thyroid disorders undergoing ocular plastic surgeries (for ptosis, single eyelids, or baggy eyelids). Enrollment was conducted by attending clinicians during routine preoperative consultations. Exclusion criteria included previous ocular surgery, concurrent metabolic diseases, and pregnancy or lactation. No participants from vulnerable populations were recruited, and no coercion was involved in the enrollment process. To minimize contamination for microbiome analysis, all surgeries were performed under aseptic conditions.

OA and additional subcutaneous adipose (SA) samples, including abdominal and upper-arm fat, were collected and enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions for downstream scRNA-seq. For microbiome analysis, multiple sample types were collected from TED patients and controls, including OA, conjunctival sac swabs (CS), deep nasal swabs (NS), and stool samples (ST) (Fig. 1).

The integration of these previously published sequences with our newly generated datasets provides a resource for elucidating TED pathogenesis, with all reused data annotated under the GSA for human BioProject accession PRJCA067648. In addition, a separate cohort comprising 25 TED patients and 10 negative controls was used for multi-site microbiome sampling (OA, CS, NS, and stool) (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

To minimize potential contamination in microbiome analyses, all sampling and processing procedures were conducted under strict aseptic conditions. In parallel, multiple types of environmental and procedural controls were collected, including operating room air samples, surgical instruments, sampling swabs, collection tubes, and sterile buffer solutions, to monitor and account for potential contamination introduced during sampling and processing. These environmental controls were further used in downstream analyses to identify and remove potential contaminant signals.

We note that the clinical landscape for TED has shifted substantially since sample collection. The approval of the IGF-1 receptor antibody SYCUME® (teprotumumab N01) by China’s NMPA in March 2025 has led to a marked reduction in orbital decompression surgeries at our institution18. The specimens described in this study were collected prior to this transition and represent a pre-biologic-treatment surgical cohort.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Fresh tissue was enzymatically digested to generate single-cell suspensions. Cell viability and concentration were assessed prior to loading. Libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit (10x Genomics)19, targeting a recovery of 9,000–10,000 cells per sample. Sequencing reads were demultiplexed to generate FASTQ files which were then aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using Cell Ranger (v10.0.0) to generate feature-barcode matrices.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Biological samples, including orbital adipose tissue, conjunctival sac swabs, deep nasal swabs, stool samples, and negative controls, were collected under sterile conditions and immediately transferred to sterile containers. All samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. To minimize potential contamination, low-biomass samples and high-biomass samples were processed separately, and DNA extraction and PCR were performed using independent plates. Sample positions were randomized, and sample identifiers were scrambled during sequencing.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from frozen tissue samples, swab samples, stool samples, and negative controls using a sterile DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′)20. Amplicons were purified and pooled at equimolar concentrations to construct sequencing libraries. Library concentration and fragment size distribution were assessed using Qubit 4.0 fluorometry and Qsep400 capillary electrophoresis prior to sequencing. Multi-omics-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were generated in multi-omics-end FASTQ format and subjected to initial quality control by the sequencing provider.

Data processing

For scRNA-seq, downstream analysis was performed using the Seurat (v5.3.1) R package21. Quality control involved filtering cells based on mitochondrial gene content (>15% excluded) and gene counts (<200 or >8,000 detected genes excluded). Putative doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder (v2.0.4). Dimensionality reduction (PCA, UMAP) and clustering were performed to identify cell types. Cell-type annotation was performed using canonical marker genes. To provide comprehensive marker gene information, differential expression analysis was performed using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function with the following parameters: Wilcoxon rank-sum test, only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25. The top 20 marker genes ranked by average log2 fold-change were selected as representative markers for each cluster. Annotation confidence was assessed manually based on the specificity and established nature of marker gene expression.

For microbiome data, raw reads were processed using the QIIME 2 (version 2025.10) pipeline22. Denoising and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) calling were performed using DADA223. Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA database24. Potential contaminants were identified and removed using the Decontam25 R package and SourceTracker26, utilizing the environmental negative controls as the source of background noise.

Data Overview

Using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform, we generated single-cell RNA sequencing data from human orbital adipose tissue samples collected from patients with TED and healthy controls. After quality filtering and aggregation, a total of 61,302 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analyses. The single-cell transcriptomes exhibited robust sequencing depth, with consistent numbers of detected genes and unique molecular identifiers per cell across samples, supporting reliable characterization of major stromal and immune cell populations.

In parallel, 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable regions was performed on matched orbital adipose tissue samples and environmental negative controls. Sequencing data showed high retention of effective reads following quality filtering and denoising, and a substantial proportion of reads could be assigned at the phylum level after contamination-aware processing. Together, these datasets constitute a high-quality multi-omics resource for investigating the cellular landscape and potential microbial signatures of the orbital microenvironment in thyroid eye disease (Fig. 3).

Hierarchical structure of the data deposited in https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18983916 labeled Orbital_Adipose_processed_data.zip. The dataset contains 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) sequencing data and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, organized into processed data, quality control reports, and sample-specific folders.

Technical Validation

scRNA-seq quality control

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were generated using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform with the Single Cell 3′ v3 chemistry. Cell quality and concentration were assessed prior to library construction, and libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads, with sufficient sequencing depth for downstream analyses. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v10.0.0) with the GRCh38 reference transcriptome, and samples were aggregated to normalize sequencing depth across samples.

According to the aggregated Cell Ranger report, a total of 72,728 cells were captured across all samples, with an average of 86.8% of sequencing reads assigned to cell-associated barcodes, indicating efficient cell capture and low ambient RNA background. The median number of detected genes per cell was approximately 3,700, with values ranging from 2,644 to 6,906, reflecting expected variability across different cell types. The median UMI counts per cell were approximately 13,700, ranging from 6,146 to 28,966, indicating sufficient sequencing depth and transcriptomic complexity. (Table 6) Quality metrics of single cells, including the relationship between detected genes and UMI counts, as well as the distributions of total UMI counts (nCount_RNA) and detected genes (nFeature_RNA) per cell, demonstrated overall data quality and consistency across samples (Fig. 4). Downstream quality control was performed using the Seurat (v5.3.1) R package, during which cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, cells with more than 8,000 detected genes, or cells with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 15% were removed. Potential doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder (v2.0.4) (Table 7). After stringent quality filtering, a total of 61,302 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analyses. Cell clustering and annotation

To further evaluate the biological structure and reliability of the dataset, dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses were performed. Cells were well distributed in the UMAP space and formed distinct clusters, indicating pronounced cellular heterogeneity within orbital adipose tissue. Based on canonical marker gene expression, major cell populations were identified and annotated. Stromal fibroblasts were characterized by high expression of extracellular matrix-associated genes such as COL1A2, DCN, and FBLN1, forming several prominent clusters. Vascular-associated cells, including endothelial cells, were identified by the expression of PECAM1, VWF, and ECSCR. Immune cell populations were also clearly resolved, with T cells expressing markers such as TRBC2 and SKAP1, and myeloid cells exhibiting expression of genes including CD52 and C3. Additional cell populations, including epithelial or gland-associated cells, were supported by marker genes such as AQP1 and SERPINF1. These cell types exhibited different and cluster-specific expression patterns, supporting the robustness of the broad cell-type annotations while preserving the continuous transcriptomic states inherent to tissue remodeling in TED. The distribution of clusters was consistent across samples, and the observed transcriptional signatures aligned with known biological identities of orbital tissue-resident and infiltrating cells. Together, these results demonstrate that the dataset captures the complex cellular composition of orbital adipose tissue and provides a robust foundation for downstream analysis of stromal, vascular, and immune compartments (Fig. 5).

16S rRNA microbiome quality control

In the multi-site microbiome cohort, a total of 110 samples were included for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. After quality filtering and chimera removal, the average number of raw reads per sample was 100,951, with a median of 115,331 and a range from 2,764 to 126,692. The sequencing data were of high quality, with a mean Q30 score of 95.50%, a median of 95.60%, and values ranging from 94.00% to 96.60% across all samples. Following denoising and chimera filtering using the DADA2 pipeline, an average of 86.30% of reads per sample were retained as high-quality non-chimeric sequences, with a median of 91.56% and a range from 45.45% to 95.08%. Samples from conjunctival sac swabs exhibited relatively lower sequencing depth compared to other sample types, which is consistent with the low microbial biomass characteristic of the ocular surface33. This variation in sequencing depth across anatomical sites reflects inherent biological differences in microbial load rather than technical bias. To minimize potential bias arising from differences in sequencing depth, all downstream analyses were performed on normalized data. Inclusion of all samples did not affect overall diversity patterns or the robustness of the results.

To ensure the reliability of microbiome data and minimize the impact of potential contamination, multiple types of negative controls were included throughout sample collection and processing, including sterile water controls and environmental samples such as air exposure, surgical instruments, and sampling materials. DNA extraction and library preparation were performed in parallel for biological samples and negative controls. Sterile water controls consistently exhibited markedly lower alpha diversity compared with biological samples, reflecting minimal background contamination. In contrast, environmental controls displayed relatively higher diversity, which is expected given their exposure to ambient microbial sources. Importantly, beta diversity analysis based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity demonstrated that microbial community structures of environmental controls were clearly distinct from those of biological samples, indicating that environmental signals did not overlap with or dominate the microbial profiles observed in biological specimens. Together, these results support effective discrimination between background contamination and putative tissue-associated microbial DNA signals. The inclusion of multiple negative control types enabled systematic monitoring of potential contamination introduced during sample handling and sequencing, following established practices for low-biomass microbiome studies, thereby strengthening the reliability and interpretability of the microbiome dataset. Microbiome contamination assessment

Given the inherently low bacterial biomass of OA, technical validation was performed to evaluate and control potential environmental and reagent-derived contamination in 16S rRNA gene sequencing data, following established principles for low-biomass microbiome studies35. Environmental negative controls, including sterile water and blank samples, were processed in parallel with biological samples across DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing to monitor background signals.

All samples were sequenced targeting the bacterial 16S rRNA V3–V4 region using Illumina platforms. Across two sequencing batches, more than 11 million raw reads were generated. Only samples meeting predefined sequencing depth and quality thresholds were retained, with qualified OA samples typically yielding approximately 10⁴–10⁵ reads per sample, sufficient for downstream microbial community analysis.

Sequence data were processed using the QIIME 2 (version 2025.10) pipeline with DADA2 for denoising, quality filtering, and chimera removal, resulting in a high-confidence amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table. To assess contamination, microbial profiles of OA samples were compared with those of negative controls using alpha diversity metrics and Bray–Curtis-based beta diversity analyses. Principal Coordinate Analysis demonstrated clear separation between OA samples and negative controls, indicating that tissue-associated microbial profiles were distinct from background contamination.

Taxa consistently enriched in negative controls were identified as potential contaminants and removed using statistical decontamination approaches. After contaminant filtering, the retained dataset was considered to enrich for putative tissue-associated microbial signatures while mitigating background noise. Collectively, these validation procedures support the robustness of the sequencing data and the reliability of downstream microbiome analyses.

Given the inherently low microbial biomass of orbital adipose tissue, computational quality control was essential to isolate putative tissue-associated microbial DNA signals from environmental noise. Aseptic sampling procedures were paired with an extensive suite of negative controls, including sterile buffers, sampling swabs, and surgical instruments. To systematically partition endogenous microbiota from exogenous artifacts, we employed a dual-algorithm decontamination strategy. Initially, the Bayesian framework SourceTracker (v0.9.1) was applied to quantify the proportion of environmentally derived sequences within the orbital samples36. Subsequently, we utilized the decontam R package (v1.31.0) to statistically prune spurious taxa based on their prevalence profiles in the negative controls37. We evaluated a gradient of probability thresholds (ranging from 0.01 to 0.9). Crucially, even under the most conservative stringency parameter (0.01), different bacterial signatures remained stable, supporting their consistency with a tissue-associated origin, though viability cannot be confirmed by amplicon sequencing alone rather than being procedural artifacts. Furthermore, cross-referencing these orbital profiles with diverse anatomical sites, including fecal, deep nasal, and conjunctival samples, enabled the precise categorization of taxa into locally resident, gut-translocated, or contaminant populations, thereby enhancing the reliability and interpretability of the dataset.

Usage Notes

Researchers should exercise caution when processing microbiome data. We recommend utilizing the environmental control data provided when reanalyzing raw 16S rRNA reads to establish custom filtering thresholds and mitigate batch effects. Regarding the single-cell RNA sequencing data, the provided object includes annotations for major cell types. However, sub-clustering of fibroblasts and lymphocytes, is highly encouraged to fully explore different cellular states. Finally, researchers analyzing the dataset should account for the cross-sectional nature of the cohort. Analytical models should be designed to evaluate correlative associations rather than drawing definitive causal conclusions regarding disease initiation or tissue remodeling.

The key cell-state observations reported in this study require independent experimental validation through orthogonal approaches, for example quantitative RT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, or flow cytometry, conducted in larger and independent surgical cohorts. The present data descriptor is centered on data generation and technical quality control. Future investigations employing these complementary methodologies represent a necessary step toward translating the present transcriptomic findings into an improved biological understanding of TED pathogenesis.

Several limitations govern the interpretation of the provided dataset. Orbital adipose tissue harbors a low microbial biomass, and while computational decontamination protocols were applied. 16S rRNA gene sequencing detects nucleic acids rather than viable microorganisms and does not differentiate among DNA originating from viable bacteria, non-viable bacteria, or circulating cell-free DNA fragments. Accordingly, terms such as microbiome, microbial community, and colonization as used throughout the dataset refer to DNA-based taxonomic profiles and should not be interpreted as evidence of active bacterial colonization. Demonstration of microbial viability within orbital tissues will require complementary approaches, including microbial culture, metatranscriptomic profiling.

Furthermore, tissue dissociation required for single-cell RNA sequencing disrupts the native architectural integrity of the orbital microenvironment. Cellular interactions, particularly the localized crosstalk among infiltrating immune populations, resident fibroblasts, and putative microbial antigens, occur within organized spatial domains. The absence of spatial transcriptomic mapping prevents the precise anatomical localization of specific cellular niches driving disease pathogenesis. Additionally, the observational study design captures a singular temporal snapshot of a dynamic autoimmune process. Thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy progresses through distinct clinical phases, transitioning from acute inflammation to chronic fibrosis. The provided dataset cannot resolve whether the observed transcriptomic shifts and microbial alterations initiate the disease cascade or represent secondary responses to orbital tissue remodeling. Longitudinal sampling is required to establish causal trajectories. Finally, amplicon sequencing targeting the V3 and V4 hypervariable regions provides restricted taxonomic resolution, typically failing to identify microbes at the species or strain level. Amplicon data provides no direct evidence of microbial metabolic activity or functional capacity, meaning transitioning toward shotgun metagenomics will be essential to elucidate the precise biochemical pathways mediating interactions between the host and microbes in the orbit.

In addition, the cell-state observations reported in our dataset would benefit from independent experimental validation using orthogonal methods such as quantitative RT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, or flow cytometry in independent surgical cohorts. We explicitly encourage and look forward to these efforts as the next logical step toward translating these transcriptomic findings into a deeper biological understanding of TED pathogenesis. We note that the clinical landscape for TED has shifted substantially since sample collection: the approval of teprotumumab N0138 / IBI31139 in China (March 2025) has markedly reduced the availability of orbital decompression specimens, and post-treatment tissues may not be directly comparable to pre-treatment samples. The present dataset therefore represents a pre-biologic-treatment reference for future validation studies.
