Interband contributions to nonlinear transport in semiconductor nanostructures
Kazuki Nakazawa 1 , Henry F. Legg 2 , Jelena Klinovaja 2 , and Daniel Loss 1,2

Abstract
Spin-orbit interaction (SOI) is a crucial ingredient for many potential applications of quantum devices, such as the use of semiconductor nanostructures for quantum computing. It is known that nonlinear conductivities are sensitive to the strength and type of SOI, however, many calculations of nonlinear transport coefficients are based on the semiclassical Boltzmann theory and make simplifying assumptions about scattering effects due to disorder. In this paper, we develop and employ a microscopic theory based on the Keldysh formalism that goes beyond simple semiclassical approximations. This approach, for instance, naturally takes into account the effects of interband transitions, Berry curvature, and allows for a more precise treatment of impurity scattering. As a test of this formalism, we consider the nonlinear transport properties in an effective two-band model of one-dimensional nanowires (1DNWs) and two-dimensional hole gases (2DHGs) in the presence of a magnetic field causing Zeeman splitting of the spin states. We find that the small energy scales in nanostructures can result in interband contributions that are relevant, especially in the dirty limit, and therefore could modify qualitative features found using a purely semiclassical approach. Nonetheless, we find that different types of SOI (linear or cubic) still result in remarkably pronounced different in-plane field angle dependences, which survive even when interband effects are relevant. Our results provide a detailed understanding of when interband effects become important for nonlinear transport and can serve as the basis for a microscopic description to predict other nonlinear transport effects in materials and devices.

In addition, this semiclassical approach often employs simple phenomenological assumptions about the details of the system, such as the momentum relaxation of charge carriers. A microscopic description of nonlinear transport effects, going beyond a simple phenomenological description, would therefore enable a fuller characterization of, e.g., the SOI in nanostructures used to realise hole qubits.

In this paper, we investigate the effects of interband transitions in nonlinear transport and employ a detailed description of momentum relaxation due to impurity scattering. We start from the ground up by establishing a microscopic description of nonlinear effects based on the Keldysh formalism. We then apply our results to semiconductor nanostructures such as one-dimensional nanowires (1DNWs) and two-dimensional hole gases (2DHGs). First, we demonstrate that the corresponding intraband terms in the microscopic expression are consistent with Boltzmann theory. Based on the band representation of the energy dispersion, we also discuss the various contributions to nonlinear transport behavior. Following this, we apply our microscopic formalism to low-dimensional nanostructures as a testbed for the new effects incorporated in this description. In the case of 1DNWs and 2DHGs, we show that interband contributions increase near the band crossing and discuss their relevance for characterising SOI in these nanostructures. Furthermore, we also show that, when the self-energy is treated within the Born approximation or self-consistent Born approximation, the van Hove singularities in 1DNWs and the off-diagonal (non-spin conserving) component of the self-energy can, in certain circumstances, significantly affect the behavior of second-order nonlinear conductivity. Overall, our microscopic analysis provides a detailed understanding of when interband and impurity scattering effects can have an influence on nonlinear transport properties beyond simple semiclassical approaches. Furthermore, our results provide the basis for a full microscopic description that can be used to predict a variety of nonlinear transport effects in materials and devices.

II.1
General formula for nonlinear conductivities

To derive the DC second-order nonlinear conductivity, we here employ the Keldysh Green functions based on the path integral formalism
[
74
]
. Throughout we will set
\hbar=1
, unless otherwise stated. The electric current
j_{i}
can be calculated using the current operator
\hat{j}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{j}_{i}\rangle=-\frac{i}{2}{\rm tr}[\hat{j}_{i}\hat{G}^{\rm K}],

(1)

where
\hat{G}^{{\rm K}}
is a Keldysh Green function, and
\rm tr
is the trace taken over all degrees of freedom (e.g., momentum, frequency, spin, and orbital).

To discuss the response to the electric field, let us assume a continuum model described by the Hamiltonian
\hat{\cal{H}}=\hat{H}({{\bm{p}}}-e{{\bm{A}}})+\hat{H}_{\rm i}
with
{{{\bm{p}}}=-i\nabla}
being the momentum operator,
{{\bm{A}}}\equiv{\bm{A}}(t)
the time-dependent
U

\hat{H}_{\rm i}
the impurity scattering term. The hat on the Hamiltonian indicates that it is a matrix with respect to discrete degrees of freedom (e.g., for spin and orbital band index). Let us consider a Fourier component
\hat{H}({{\bm{k}}}-e{{\bm{A}}})
where
{{\bm{k}}}
is the wave vector, and expand it with respect to
{{\bm{A}}}
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}({{\bm{k}}}-e{\bm{A}})\simeq\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle-eA_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}+\frac{e^{2}}{2}A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{3}}{6}A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}A_{\gamma}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}},

(2)

leading to a current operator of the form,

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{\alpha}({{\bm{k}}})=\frac{\delta\hat{H}({{\bm{k}}}-e{\bm{A}})}{\delta A_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\simeq-e\partial_{\alpha}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}+e^{2}A_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}-\frac{e^{3}}{2}A_{\beta}A_{\gamma}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}},

(3)

where
e
is a charge and
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial k_{i}
. From here on we define
\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\alpha}_{1}{\alpha}_{2}\cdots{\alpha}_{n}}\equiv\partial_{{\alpha}_{1}}\partial_{{\alpha}_{2}}\cdots\partial_{{\alpha}_{n}}\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}
. Here,
\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}\equiv\hat{H}({{\bm{k}}}-e{\bm{A}})|_{{\bm{A}}=0}
is the momentum space representation of the Hamiltonian without the coupling to the external field, and we define the retarded and advanced Green functions
\hat{G}_{{{\bm{k}}}{{\bm{k}}}^{\prime}}^{{{\rm R}}}({\varepsilon})=({\varepsilon}-\hat{\cal H}|_{{{\bm{A}}}=0}+i0^{+})_{{{\bm{k}}}{{\bm{k}}}^{\prime}}^{-1}=(\hat{G}_{{{\bm{k}}}{{\bm{k}}}^{\prime}}^{{\rm A}})^{\dagger}
, which takes the full account of the system including the impurity scattering which we discuss in Sec.
II.2
. Thanks to this expansion we can obtain the response to the general order of the vector potential
{\bm{A}}
. In particular, we are interested in the second-order response of the electric field, given in frequency space

by
{\bm{E}}({\omega})=i{\omega}{\bm{A}}({\omega})
. After first calculating the nonlinear conductivity within the Kelydsh framework, we will then check the consistency with previous studies
[

]
(see Appendix

\displaystyle\langle\hat{j}_{\alpha}^{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}E_{\beta}E_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRR}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RR})E_{\beta}E_{\gamma},

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}

\displaystyle=-e^{3}{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}\Biggl[\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\right)\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}\Biggr],

\displaystyle\quad+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})

(5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}

\displaystyle=-e^{3}{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}\Biggl[\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\right)\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}\Biggr],

(6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRR}

\displaystyle=-e^{3}{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}\Biggl[f({\varepsilon})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}}{\partial{\varepsilon}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\right)\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\Biggr],

\displaystyle\quad+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})

(7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RR}

\displaystyle=-e^{3}{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}\Biggl[f({\varepsilon})\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}}{\partial{\varepsilon}^{2}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\Biggr].

II.2
Self-energy

The effect of elastic scattering due to the impurities is incorporated by considering the self-energy
\Sigma
of the impurity-averaged Green function
\hat{G}_{{\bm{k}}}^{{\rm R}}({\varepsilon})=({\varepsilon}-\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}-\hat{\Sigma}^{{\rm R}}({\varepsilon}))^{-1}
. The simplest approximation is to put the constant damping rate
\gamma
as the self-energy
\Sigma^{{\rm R}}=-i{\gamma}\equiv-\frac{i\hbar}{2\tau}
, where
\tau
is the momentum relaxation time. This is an especially good approximation in the non-interacting two-dimensional electron (hole) gases as the density of states are approximately constant in energy in 2D, see below.

To go further we can also include a more microscopic description of the electron damping. For instance, the potential from point like static impurities can be described as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm i}=u\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm i}}\delta({\bm{r}}-{\bm{X}}_{j}),

(9)

where
u
,
N_{\rm i}
, and
{\bm{X}}_{j}
are the strength of the impurity scattering, the number of impurities, and the random position of the
j
th impurity, respectively.
The self-energy with respect to the impurity scattering can be treated within the Born approximation,

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{{\rm R}}({\varepsilon})=\frac{n_{\rm i}u^{2}}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{g}_{{\bm{k}}}^{{\rm R}}({\varepsilon}),

(10)

where
\hat{g}_{{\bm{k}}}^{\rm R}({\varepsilon})=({\varepsilon}-\hat{H}_{\bm{k}}+i0^{+})^{-1}
is the bare retarded Green function and
n_{\rm i}=N_{\rm i}/V
is the impurity concentration with the system volume
V
. A more accurate treatment can be achieved by considering the self-consistent treatment of the self-energy within the Born approximation, referred to as self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA):

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\rm SCBA}^{{{\rm R}}}({\varepsilon})=\frac{n_{\rm i}u^{2}}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\hat{G}_{\bm{k}}^{{\rm R}}({\varepsilon}),

(11)

where the self-energy is self-consistently defined, such that one has to perform the summation iteratively until convergence.

II.3
Intraband and Interband terms

The simplest way to investigate the nonlinear transport properties is to apply Boltzmann transport theory. For this we use the eigenvalue equation for
\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}
,
\hat{H}_{{\bm{k}}}|n{{\bm{k}}}\rangle={\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}|n{{\bm{k}}}\rangle
, with eigenenergy
{\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}
and eigenket
|n{{\bm{k}}}\rangle
, where
n
denotes the band index. Expanding up to the second order of electric field within the relaxation time approximation and at zero temperature, we get
[
25
,
26
,
37
]

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm B}=-e^{3}\tau^{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}v_{{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(n)}\delta(\mu-{\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}),

(12)

where, again,
\tau
represents the momentum relaxation time, and
v_{\alpha}^{(n)}=\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}
and
v_{{\alpha}{\beta}}^{(n)}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}
are the group velocity and band curvature, respectively. Note that
{\sigma}_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm B}
only contains the intraband contributions.

We now derive the band representation from the microscopic formula Eq. (
4
) for comparison to the Boltzmann formalism and the physical descriptions. For this, let us, for simplicity, consider a constant self-energy. Also, as the eigenvalues, eigenstates, and the Green functions are characterized in each wave number
{{\bm{k}}}
and the Green functions are evaluated at
{\varepsilon}=\mu
, we hereafter use the notations
{\varepsilon}_{n}\equiv{\varepsilon}_{n{{\bm{k}}}}
,
|n\rangle\equiv|n{{\bm{k}}}\rangle
, and
G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}\equiv G_{{{\bm{k}}}n}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}(\mu)
in the diagonal basis. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}\equiv\langle n|\hat{G}_{{\bm{k}}}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}|n\rangle.

(13)

Introducing the notation
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\equiv{\varepsilon}_{n}-{\varepsilon}_{m}
, the current operator and its derivative are modified such that

\displaystyle{\langle n}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}|{m\rangle}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n}\delta_{nm}+i{\varepsilon}_{mn}A_{\alpha}^{nm},

(14)

\displaystyle{\langle n}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\alpha}{\beta}}|{m\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{n}\delta_{nm}+\frac{i}{2}{\varepsilon}_{nm}\partial_{\beta}A_{\alpha}^{mn}

\displaystyle+i\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{nm}A_{\alpha}^{mn}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell}{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}A_{\alpha}^{m\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}+({\alpha}\leftrightarrow{\beta}),

(15)

where
A_{\alpha}^{nm}=-i{\langle n}|{\partial_{\alpha}m\rangle}
. The first terms in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) are the intraband components, and all other terms express the interband processes. Hence we can decompose
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}
into the interband and intraband contributions,

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter},

(16)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter},

(17)

and define
{\sigma}_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm intra}=\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}=\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter}
, where the explicit definitions of these terms are given in Appendix
B
.

Let us first comment on
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm intra}
. This term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm intra}=\frac{e^{3}}{\pi}\tau^{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}v_{{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(n)}{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}.

(18)

We see that this intraband term is proportional to
\tau^{2}
and
{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}\to-\pi\delta(\mu-{\varepsilon}_{n})
in the
{\gamma}\to 0
limit, this intraband contribution is analogous to Eq. (
12
) obtained from the Boltzmann theory.

Next, we consider the interband term
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}
. The band expression of
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}
is quite complicated, as it contains multifarious interband processes involving a large number of terms (see Appendix
B
). To get some insights on the disorder dependence, let us consider the dirty limit, such that
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau\ll 1
for all wave numbers. In this limit we can write
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}\equiv\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm BCD}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm QM}
to leading order in
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm BCD}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{e^{3}}{\pi}\tau\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}v_{\beta}^{(n)}{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}{\gamma}\delta}\Omega_{\delta}^{(n)}{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}+{\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma},

(19)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm QM}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{e^{3}}{\pi}\tau^{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}g_{{\alpha};{\beta}{\gamma}}^{n\ell}{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}.

(20)

Here,
\Omega_{\delta}^{(n)}
is the Berry curvature and
g_{{\alpha};{\beta}{\gamma}}^{n\ell}
is a symmetric tensor with respect to the band indices
n
and
m
with
{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}{\gamma}\delta}
the Levi-Civita symbol (see Appendix
B
for details). The first term Eq. (
19

\tau
and responsible for the nonlinear Hall effect
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. We can also obtain this expression without requiring the criterion
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau\ll 1
(see Appendix
B
). The second term, Eq. (
20
), contains the rest of the interband term, which is related to the quantum metric
[
36
,
35
]
specified by
g_{{\alpha};{\beta}{\gamma}}^{n\ell}
. This term has a
\tau^{2}
-dependence in the leading order if we assume
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau\ll 1
. This limit provides insight into the origin of the various interband contributions, in later sections, we will show that this approximation works particularly well for dirty systems with small scattering times

V
Discussion

V.1
Detecting SOI

We have investigated how interband contributions modify nonlinear transport coefficients, going beyond simple semiclassic approximations. We found that the interband contributions are largest when the energy difference between the bands,
{\varepsilon}_{+-}
, is small but nonzero. As a result, according to our findings, there are two optimal scenarios where the interband terms are suppressed: i) in a clean system with weak disorder and therefore large
\tau
, and/or ii) in the presence of a large magnetic field resulting in a large
\varepsilon_{nm}
. The first point is based on the fact that the intraband term is proportional to
\tau^{2}
and interband contributions are lower order in
\tau
(higher order of
\gamma
) in clean systems. As such, in a sufficiently clean system with a large
\tau
, the intraband effect will exceed the interband contribution. The second point is based on the fact that the interband term is more pronounced when the difference in band energies is small.

To summarize, the criterion for neglecting the interband terms is determined by the relationship between the band energy difference
{\varepsilon}_{+-}
and the damping rate
\gamma=\frac{\hbar}{2\tau}
, such that it is important to make the region which satisfies
{\varepsilon}_{+-}/\gamma\gg 1
as large as possible. Given that the mean free path in Ge can reach several 10s of
\mu
m, this should be satisfied in clean Ge samples
[
76
]
. Note, however, that the interband contribution is important, even in a clean system, if the chemical potential is located sufficiently close to a nodal point (see Appendix
C
).

V.2
Vertex corrections

We emphasize that our analysis neglects the contribution from vertex corrections arising from impurity scattering. Although we do not expect such corrections to strongly alter the qualitative results in the cases considered here, it should be noted that, for instance, previous studies have shown that side-jump and skew-scattering process can give considerable contributions in the general two band system with finite Berry curvature dipole
[
22
]
. Furthermore, since our formalism can treat the effects of impurity scattering in a fully microscopic way it should be possible to implement vertex corrections in a gauge invariant way via Ward identities. The implementation of vertex corrections could, therefore, be of considerable interest for future studies, especially for systems with a finite Berry curvature dipole.

VI
Summary

We have developed a microscopic formulation to analyze nonlinear transport including both intraband and interband contributions in the presence of a magnetic field inducing Zeeman splittings (but without orbital effects). First, we derived a microscopic formulation to calculate nonlinear transport coefficients using the Keldysh formalism and confirmed its consistency with the results of previous studies based on the Boltzmann equation approach. Then, we defined the intraband and interband terms using the band representation and investigated the dependence of each term on the relaxation time at zero temperature, confirming that the interband term is important when the band energy difference is comparable to (or smaller than) the damping rate. In particular, within this framework, we find that the interband contributions arise due to the band (quantum) geometry and Berry curvature dipole. As a test for this formulation, we considered transport in a 1DNW and found that interband terms are the largest close to the nodal point and in the case of weak magnetic fields. Furthermore, we found that a full treatment of the self-energy by Born approximation can significantly alter the nonlinear transport properties in a 1DNW. As a second example, we considered the 2DHG and, in particular, models corresponding to Ge [100] and Ge [110]. In both cases, the interband term is found to increase around the nodal point, similar to what was observed for the 1DNW. The magnetic field angle dependences in the
xy
-plane are significantly different between Ge [100] and Ge [110], reflecting the nature of SOI. Our results provide a microscopic framework to calculate nonlinear transport coefficients beyond standard Boltzmann descriptions. In addition, we found that a simple interpretation of SOI contributions to nonlinear transport signatures in semiconductor nanostructures necessitates the use of clean samples, where interband terms can be neglected.

A.3
Validity check for DC limit

Let us quickly check that the DC limit is uniquely determined. It is sufficient to show that the term proportional to
{\omega}_{i}^{n}/{\omega}_{1}{\omega}_{2}
in
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(2)}
is zero. Namely, if we write

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(2)}({\omega}_{1},{\omega}_{2})=\frac{ie^{3}}{2}{\rm tr}\Biggl[\frac{A_{0}}{{\omega}_{1}{\omega}_{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{A_{n}{\omega}_{1}^{n}+A^{\prime}_{n}{\omega}_{2}^{n}}{{\omega}_{1}{\omega}_{2}}+({\rm Regular\ part})\Biggr],

(34)

we need to show that
{\rm tr}A_{n}={\rm tr}A^{\prime}_{n}=0

[
34
]
. Generalizing this result, we now demonstrate that
{\rm tr}A_{n}
,
{\rm tr}A^{\prime}_{n}

. Indeed, collecting all the terms proportional to
\omega_{i}^{n}
, we get

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=f({\varepsilon})\frac{1}{n!}\left[\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\alpha}{\beta}}(\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}+\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}{}_{n}C_{\ell}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{n-\ell}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{\ell}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}+\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{n}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\right]

\displaystyle-f({\varepsilon})\frac{1}{n!}\left[\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\alpha}{\beta}}(\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}+\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}{}_{n}C_{\ell}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{n-\ell}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{\ell}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}+\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{n}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}})\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}\right]

Appendix B
Band representation

In this appendix, we derive the band representation for the physical description. Let us put here
-e^{3}/(2\pi)=1
to simplify the equations. First, we note that

\displaystyle i\partial_{\alpha}({\langle n}|{m\rangle})

\displaystyle=i{\langle\partial_{\alpha}n}|{m\rangle}+i{\langle n}|{\partial_{\alpha}m\rangle}=[-i{\langle m}|{\partial_{\alpha}n\rangle}]^{*}-[-i{\langle n}|{\partial_{\alpha}m\rangle}]=(A_{\alpha}^{mn})^{*}-A_{\alpha}^{nm}=0,

(36)

\displaystyle-i\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}({\langle n}|{m\rangle})

\displaystyle=-i\left[\langle\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}n|{m\rangle}+{\langle\partial_{\beta}n}|{\partial_{\alpha}m\rangle}+{\langle\partial_{\alpha}n}|{\partial_{\beta}m\rangle}+{\langle n}|\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}m\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=-i\partial_{\alpha}({\langle n}|{\partial_{\beta}m\rangle})-i\partial_{\beta}({\langle\partial_{\alpha}n}|{m\rangle})=\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}^{nm}-\partial_{\beta}(A_{\alpha}^{mn})^{*}=\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}^{nm}-\partial_{\beta}A_{\alpha}^{nm}=0,

(37)

from which we see the useful relations
A_{i}^{nm}=(A_{i}^{mn})^{*}
and
\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}^{nm}=\partial_{\beta}A_{\alpha}^{nm}
.
Using Eqs. (
13
), (
14
), and (
15
), the extrinsic terms
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}
are expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA}

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}[\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})^{2}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}\hat{G}^{{\rm R}}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})]

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{nm\ell}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}[{\langle n}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}|{m\rangle}(G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}{\langle m}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\beta}|{\ell\rangle}G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}{\langle\ell}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\gamma}|{n\rangle}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})]

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{nm\ell}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\biggl[(\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n})(\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{m})(\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{\ell})\delta_{nm}\delta_{m\ell}\delta_{\ell n}-{\varepsilon}_{m\ell}{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}(\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n})\delta_{nm}A_{\beta}^{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}-{\varepsilon}_{nm}{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}(\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{m})A_{\alpha}^{nm}\delta_{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-{\varepsilon}_{nm}{\varepsilon}_{m\ell}(\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{\ell})A_{\alpha}^{nm}A_{\beta}^{m\ell}\delta_{\ell n}+i{\varepsilon}_{nm}{\varepsilon}_{m\ell}{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}A_{\alpha}^{nm}A_{\beta}^{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})\biggr](G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

(38)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}\biggl[2v_{\alpha}^{(n)}v_{\beta}^{(n)}v_{\gamma}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{3}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}+\sum_{\ell}{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}^{2}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}(A_{\beta}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+A_{\gamma}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n})G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{\ell}{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}^{2}(G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}\left\{v_{\beta}^{(n)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+v_{\gamma}^{(n)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{m\ell}i{\varepsilon}_{nm}{\varepsilon}_{m\ell}{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}A_{\alpha}^{nm}(A_{\beta}^{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+A_{\gamma}^{m\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n})(G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}\biggr]

(39)

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(3)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(4)}

(40)

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA}

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm Im}\ {\rm tr}[\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}(\hat{G}^{{\rm R}})^{2}\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\hat{G}^{{\rm A}}]={\rm Im}\sum_{nm}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}[{\langle n}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{\alpha}|{m\rangle}(G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}{\langle m}|\hat{{{\cal V}}}_{{\beta}{\gamma}}|{n\rangle}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}]

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{nm}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}(G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}(\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n}\delta_{nm}+i{\varepsilon}_{mn}A_{\alpha}^{nm})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{n}\delta_{nm}+i[\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{nm}A_{\beta}^{mn}+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})]+i{\varepsilon}_{nm}\partial_{\gamma}A_{\beta}^{mn}-\sum_{\ell}[{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+{\varepsilon}_{m\ell}A_{\gamma}^{m\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}]\right)

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}\biggl[(\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n})(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{n})-\sum_{\ell}{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}(\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n})(A_{\beta}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+A_{\gamma}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n})(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{m}{\varepsilon}_{nm}[\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{nm}A_{\alpha}^{nm}A_{\gamma}^{mn}+\partial_{\gamma}{\varepsilon}_{nm}A_{\alpha}^{nm}A_{\beta}^{mn}](G_{m}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(3)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(4)}

(42)

In metals, terms
\propto\tau^{2}
will be dominant in most cases. To check that the leading terms are
\tau^{2}
, just use
G_{n}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}=i\tau(G_{n}^{{\rm R}}-G_{n}^{{\rm A}})=-2\tau{\rm Im}G^{{\rm R}}
which leads to
(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}\simeq 2i\tau^{2}{\rm Im}G^{{\rm R}}
, i.e. only imaginary parts are left.

Let us first comment on the relation between the intraband terms
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}
. Using integration by parts, we can show

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}v_{{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{\beta}^{(n)}v_{{\gamma}{\alpha}}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}-2{\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{{\alpha}}^{(n)}v_{\beta}^{(n)}v_{\gamma}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{3}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle={\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{{\gamma}}^{(n)}v_{{\alpha}{\beta}}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}={\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{{\beta}}^{(n)}v_{{\gamma}{\alpha}}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}.

(43)

Here, we put
v_{\alpha}^{(n)}\equiv\partial_{\alpha}{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
v_{{\alpha}{\beta}}^{(n)}\equiv\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}{\varepsilon}_{n}
. This yields

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}=2{\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}v_{{\beta}{\gamma}}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}=-2{\rm Im}\sum_{n{{\bm{k}}}}v_{{\alpha}}^{(n)}v_{\beta}^{(n)}v_{\gamma}^{(n)}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}=-\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}.

(44)

Actually, these are the only intraband terms, namely
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm intra}=(\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra})_{\tau^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,intra}=-\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,intra}
. Note also that these terms reproduce those from the Boltzmann equation approach.

Next, let us discuss the interband terms. Using
G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}G_{m}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}=({\varepsilon}_{n}-{\varepsilon}_{m})^{-1}(G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})}-G_{m}^{{{\rm R}}({{\rm A}})})
and
G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}}G_{m}^{{{\rm A}}}=({\varepsilon}_{n}-{\varepsilon}_{m}-2i\gamma)^{-1}(G_{n}^{{{\rm R}}}-G_{m}^{{{\rm A}}})
,
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)}
are calculated further as

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)}={\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}v_{\alpha}^{(n)}(A_{\beta}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+A_{\gamma}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n})G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}.

(45)

Thus, we can verify that the interband contributions
\propto\tau^{2}
, which are contained in
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)}
, cancel each other:
(\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)})_{\tau^{2}}=0
. The remaining term is the interband term in higher order of disorder. Similar calculations for
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(2)}
,
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(2)}
, and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(3)}
lead to

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(3)}

\displaystyle=2\tau\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n}[v_{\beta}^{(n)}{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}{\gamma}\delta}\Omega_{\delta}^{(n)}+v_{\gamma}^{(n)}{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}{\beta}\delta}\Omega_{\delta}^{(n)}]{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}[v_{\beta}^{(n)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+v_{\gamma}^{(n)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}]G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}[v_{\beta}^{(\ell)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+v_{\gamma}^{(\ell)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}]{\varepsilon}_{n\ell}(G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}

\displaystyle+{\rm Im}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}[A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}{\partial}_{\beta}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}]{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}^{2}(G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}})^{2}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}\,.

(46)

The first term of the last equality is the so-called Berry curvature dipole term, which will be responsible for the nonlinear Hall effect (not considered here). Note that, for the two band system, two of the velocities
{\varepsilon}_{\ell}
,
{\varepsilon}_{m}
, and
{\varepsilon}_{n}
should be the same, leading to
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(4)}=0

Let us derive Eq. (
20

{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau\ll 1

{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}
as
G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}\simeq G_{n}^{{\rm R}}+{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}
.
Noting that
|{\rm Re}[G_{n}^{{\rm R}}]|\gamma<1
, and

\displaystyle G_{\ell}^{{\rm R}}G_{n}^{{\rm A}}\simeq-2\tau{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}+i{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}\tau\{(G_{n}^{{\rm R}})^{2}+2\tau{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}\},

(47)

we can safely neglect
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(3)}
and
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm R(R)A,inter(4)}
under the condition
{\varepsilon}_{nm}\tau\ll 1
. If the Berry curvature dipole term is negligible,
\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm inter}

\displaystyle\simeq\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RRA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(1)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(2)}+\sigma_{{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}}^{\rm RA,inter(3)}

\displaystyle=2\tau^{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\sum_{n\ell}{\rm Re}\left[v_{\alpha}^{(n)}A_{\beta}^{n\ell}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}-v_{\beta}^{(n)}A_{\gamma}^{n\ell}A_{\alpha}^{\ell n}-v_{\gamma}^{(n)}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}A_{\beta}^{\ell n}+\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}A_{\alpha}^{n\ell}\partial_{\beta}A_{\gamma}^{\ell n}+({\beta}\leftrightarrow{\gamma})\right]{\varepsilon}_{\ell n}{\rm Im}G_{n}^{{\rm R}}.

Defining the symmetric tensor
