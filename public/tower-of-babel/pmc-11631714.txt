Giant Primary Choledocholithiasis: A Rare Case Report and Comprehensive Review of Literature
Authors: Samiksha Lamichhane, Suraj KC, Nishant Mishra, Shritik Devkota, Abhijeet Kumar, Rakesh Kumar Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCholedocholithiasis is the second most common complication of gallstone disease. Giant primary choledocholithiasis is a rare occurrence. Ultrasonography is the initial mode of imaging, and endoscopic ultrasound is considered superior to other modalities. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography can be used for stone extraction. Other treatment modalities include laparoscopic or open common bile duct (CBD) exploration. Many institutions in underdeveloped countries still practice open CBD exploration. Factors such as larger stone diameter, edema of the CBD, and the presence of multiple lithiasis can influence the treatment approach. In this article we are presenting a case of 62 years‐old‐female with no any known comorbidities had presented to emergency with severe upper quadrant pain and giant choledocholithiasis was diagnosed in ultrasound and computed tomography scan with no other features of complications and patient successfully underwent open CBD exploration with intraoperative choledochoscopy and primary closure.

Introduction

Choledocholithiasis refers to the presence of calculi within the common bile duct (CBD), either with or without concurrent gallstones in the gallbladder, making it the second most common complication of gallstone disease [1]. Risk factors for developing gallstones include female sex, age over 40 years, obesity, and rapid weight loss [2]. Most cases of choledocholithiasis are secondary, where gallstones migrate from the gallbladder into the CBD. However, primary choledocholithiasis can also occur, where stones form directly within the bile ducts [3]. The management of cholelithiasis with choledocholithiasis must be conducted appropriately to prevent increased morbidity and mortality. Unlike other diseases with clear diagnoses, the presence of stones in the CBD can sometimes only be suspected. Historically, diagnosis relied on a combination of clinical symptoms, and radiological imaging. However, advancements in radiological imaging, interventional endoscopy, and laparoscopy have led to faster and more accurate diagnoses. The management of cholelithiasis with choledocholithiasis now involves a multidisciplinary approach, with radiologists, gastroenterologists, endoscopists, and surgeons working together to provide tailored care based on individual patient needs and available resources. Endoscopy and surgery continue to play a central role in both diagnosis and treatment [2]. We here by present a case of a 62 years female with giant primary choledocholithiasis managed with open CBD exploration and cholecystectomy, managed without any complications. Open CBD exploration can be performed safely with experienced hand in a setting with limited laparoscopic and endoscopic facility.

Case History

A 62‐year‐old female presented to the emergency department with complaints of severe pain in the right upper quadrant with nausea and vomiting. The pain was severe in intensity and was aggravated after intake of fatty food. Patient did not give history of yellowish discoloration of the sclera or skin, fever, abdominal distension, or shortness of breath. She has no other comorbidities such as anemia, hypertension or diabetes. She is a nonsmoker and does not consume alcohol. On examination, she appeared ill but hemodynamically stable.

General Physical Examination

She was conscious and well oriented to time place and person. She had normal blood pressure of 110/80 mmHg with heartbeats of 110 beats/min and a respiratory rate of 19 breaths/min. She was afebrile with a temperature of 36.5°C.

Systemic Examination

Abdominal examination revealed mild tenderness in the right hypochondrium.

Methods and Differential Diagnosis

After thorough history taking and clinical examination, we had kept pancreatitis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, CBD stricture, choledochocele, and periampullary carcinoma as our differential diagnosis.

Investigations

Her baseline investigations showed raised normal total blood count (~9000 cell/mm) with normal neutrophil and leukocytes count. Renal function tests and liver function tests were normal except for the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) which was only slightly raised (150 U/L) (normal range: 35–130 U/L).

Imaging

Transabdominal ultrasonography showed a calculus of size ~7.3 cm in distal CBD causing gross dilatation of proximal CBD and tortuous intrahepatic biliary radicals in both lobes of liver, also there was nonobstructive calculus of size ~3.9 cm within dilated proximal biliary radicals (Figure 1). She was admitted to the ward and managed with intravenous antibiotics, analgesics, and fluids. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) revealed a large hyperdense calculus measuring approximately 6.8 × 4.7 cm in the distal CBD, causing its obstruction and proximal dilatation (maximum dilatation measures approximately 4.5 cm) with dilated biliary radicals in both lobes of the liver. Additionally, a nonobstructive calculus measuring approximately 3.8 × 3.2 cm was seen in the dilated common hepatic duct (Figure 2). There was no features of cholangitis or pancreatitis in imaging studies.

Treatment

She underwent open CBD exploration with cholecystectomy with intraoperative choledochoscopy and primary closure. Intraoperative findings showed a dilated CBD with a single calculus measuring 7 × 5 cm and another calculus measuring 3 cm in the right hepatic duct (Figure 3).

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Following surgery, she was shifted to the surgery ward on second postoperative day and discharged on the fourth postoperative day. Abdominal drain was removed on sixth postoperative day. The patient was followed up after 2 weeks for stiches removal and was recovering well.

Discussion

Patients with choledocholithiasis may present to either the outpatient department or the emergency room with a range of symptoms, from abdominal pain to severe complications such as pancreatitis and cholangitis [4]. Choledocholithiasis may come in various sizes, with large stones, known as large choledocholithiasis, typically measuring ≥ 15 mm. Rare cases involve giant CBD stones measuring 5 cm or more [5]. In this case, the patient had presented with severe right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and vomiting, without any other local or systemic complications and calculi measured approximately 7 × 5 cm, categorizing it as a giant calculi. It is uncommon for a large stone to present without jaundice as in our case as there was no jaundice and raised liver enzymes [6]. Ultrasonography is the initial diagnostic tool for suspected choledocholithiasis, with a sensitivity ranging from 7% to 63% and a specificity of approximately 95% [7, 8]. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is highly sensitive (90%–95%) and specific (92%–98%) in detecting CBD stones [8]. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is even more sensitive than ultrasound and comparable to diagnostic ERCP which has major advantage of decreased morbidity compared to ERCP [9, 10]. ERCP with endoscopic sphincterotomy and stone extraction was first described in 1974 and has been a first‐line treatment ever since [11]. Although a computed tomography (CT) scan is not the preferred modality for investigating and diagnosing choledocholithiasis, as soft tissue density stones are challenging to identify as they are isodense to surrounding tissue, however, heavily calcified stones can be easily identified [12]. In our case, we were able to diagnose choledocholithiasis easily on the CT scan. The sensitivity for diagnosing choledocholithiasis on a CT scan in a study by Anderson et al. [12] is 69%–87%, with a specificity of 83%–92% and an accuracy of 84%–88%. Contrast‐enhanced CT scans can also be used to diagnose remnant or recurrent stones after cholecystectomy and to differentiate between calculi or malignant causes of CBD obstruction, as malignant masses enhance after contrast administration. Another essential investigation for choledocholithiasis is magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), which is a highly specific test for biliary pathology. It can detect the location of the obstruction, the distance of the calculus to the papilla, and even very small calculi as small as 1 mm, as well as the number of calculi. However, it cannot determine the composition of the biliary stones. MRCP has largely replaced ERCP for the diagnosis of choledocholithiasis, reducing the need for unnecessary invasive procedures. Additionally, MRCP provides important anatomical details of the biliary tree [13].

The available treatment options for choledocholithiasis include preoperative ERCP followed by laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), which is followed by postoperative ERCP, and intraoperative laparoendoscopic “rendezvous” technique. Other procedures such as laparoscopic antegrade sphincterotomy, laparoscopic transcystic CBD exploration, laparoscopic choledochotomy, and open CBD exploration with or without transduodenal papillotomy are also performed [14]. In patients with large bile duct stones, ASGE recommends performing endoscopic sphincterotomy followed by large balloon dilation (ES‐LBD) rather than endoscopic sphincterotomy (ES) alone [15]. A two‐stage approach of ERCP followed by LC (ERCP + LC) is the most commonly used strategy worldwide [16]. Laparoscopic bile duct exploration is a safe, efficient, and cost‐effective treatment, associated with a high stone clearance rate ranging from 84% to 97%, a postoperative morbidity rate of 4%–16%, and a mortality rate of up to approximately 0.8% [17]. The first open CBD exploration, was successfully done by Courvoisier in 1980 [18]. In limited resource settings open biliary surgery, CBD exploration, and drainage with primary closure of the CBD can be a safe and preferred option in experienced hands [19]. Because of limited availability of resources and trained hands, we opted for open surgery. In a study, it was found that laparoscopic CBD exploration was converted to open CBD exploration when the stone diameter was ≥ 0.9 or 1 cm, edema of the CBD, and the presence of multiple lithiasis [20]. Since no study demonstrated that laparoscopic CBD exploration is superior to open CBD exploration for treating CBD stones, many institutions in underdeveloped countries still practice open CBD exploration [21].

Conclusion

The incidence of giant primary CBD calculus is rare. Although ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis is the initial investigation of choice, recent literature suggests that endoscopic ultrasonography is a superior imaging modality. Various laparoscopic and endoscopic procedures have been developed for stone extraction with technological advancements. However, in limited resource settings, open biliary surgery, CBD exploration, and drainage with primary closure of the CBD can be a safe and preferred option in experienced hands, as in our center.
