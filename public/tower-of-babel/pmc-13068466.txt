Flatback Deformity and Stiff Pelvic Mobility Alter the Optimal Cup Anteversion After Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Toshiki Konishi, Satoshi Hamai, Hidehiko Higaki, Shinya Kawahara, Ryosuke Yamaguchi, Goro Motomura, Takeshi Utsunomiya, Kenji Kitamura, Soichiro Yoshino, Satoru Ikebe, Yuki Nakao, Takahiro Inoue, Wataru Uehara, Yasuharu Nakashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Flatback deformity and stiff spinopelvic mobility (SPM) are recognized risk factors for dislocation after total hip arthroplasty. This study aimed to determine patient-specific optimal cup anteversion ranges associated with spinopelvic abnormalities.Methods:We analyzed postoperative sit-to-stand motion in 108 hips using three-dimensional-to-two-dimensional model-image registration. Implanted components were virtually positioned at their actual positions with inclination at 40°. Using pelvic tilt at maximal anterior tilt and standing, the range of combined anteversion (CA) satisfying required range of motion was evaluated. CA midpoint (CA_mid) and range (CA_range) were compared across spinopelvic alignment groups.Results:Flatback hips demonstrated a significantly lower CA_mid than normal (41.1° vs. 46.8°), whereas stiff SPM hips showed a similar CA_mid but a narrower CA_range (14.5° vs. 19.4°). Significant correlations were observed between spinopelvic alignment and CA parameters.Conclusions:Spinopelvic alignment influences both the optimal cup orientation and its tolerance range. Incorporating these factors may allow patient-specific optimization of acetabular component positioning.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective cohort study. See Instructions for Authors for a complete description of levels of evidence.Clinical Relevance:Personalized component positioning based on dynamic spinopelvic alignment may help reduce dislocation risk and improve implant stability in high-risk THA patients.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a highly effective procedure for relieving pain and improving function in patients with hip disorders1,2. However, postoperative dislocation remains a major complication and is one of the leading causes of revision surgery3. Prosthetic impingement, often resulting from suboptimal component positioning, is a mechanical trigger for dislocation4,5. Several studies have reported that many dislocated hips have acetabular components positioned within traditional safe zones, indicating that ideal cup orientation varies among patients6. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of spinopelvic alignment abnormalities—such as flatback deformity and stiff spinopelvic mobility (SPM)—as risk factors for dislocation after THA7-11.

Although classification-based target cup orientations have been proposed based on static spinopelvic alignment10,12,13, such static methods may not fully capture functional variability among patients. In our previous study, we for the first time investigated dynamic three-dimensional (3D) kinematics of the pelvis and hip joint during chair-rising using model-image registration techniques14. We demonstrated persistently posterior pelvic tilt in patients with flatback deformity and reduced pelvic motion with compensatory increases in hip range of motion (ROM) in patients with stiff SPM, suggesting an increased risk of implant impingement.

The purpose of this follow-up study was to determine patient-specific optimal cup anteversion by incorporating spinopelvic alignment characteristics into ROM analysis. Using our prior dynamic kinematic data, we calculated the appropriate cup anteversion needed to maintain impingement-free motion and investigated how flatback deformity and stiff SPM affect the safe zone to support individualized alignment strategies for high-risk patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective study included 108 hips from 99 patients who had undergone primary THA and dynamic radiographic analysis in our previous investigation14. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines15 and was approved by our institutional review board.

Spinopelvic Alignment Assessment

Spinopelvic alignment was classified using pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), and dynamic pelvic tilt. Flatback deformity was defined as a PI–LL mismatch >10°, and stiff SPM was defined as a change in anterior pelvic plane angle (ΔAPPa) of <10° between seated and standing7 (Appendix Figure). Patients without flatback deformity, stiff SPM, hyperlordosis (PI–LL < –10°), or hypermobility (ΔAPPa >30°) were classified as normal14.

Image Acquisition

All patients underwent postoperative standing lateral spinopelvic radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans (Aquilion, Canon Medical Systems) covering the pelvis to below the knee. Standing lateral radiographs were obtained at 1 year postoperatively, CT scans at 6 months, and dynamic anteroposterior radiographs during sit-to-stand motion at a mean of 13.7 ± 12.8 months postoperatively14. Pelvic tilt angles at maximal anterior tilt and standing were quantified via 3D-to-2D model-image registration (Fig. 1). In addition, femoral stem anteversion was measured from postoperative CT images using ZedHip (LEXI Co., Ltd.)16,17.

Acceptable Range of Cup Anteversion

To determine the acceptable range of acetabular cup anteversion, postoperative ROM evaluations were performed using ZedHip16,17. The implanted components were virtually placed in actual postoperative positions. Radiographic inclination (RI) and radiographic anteversion (RA)—defined relative to the supine functional pelvic plane (FPP)18—were used to represent cup orientation. Supine FPP was selected because acetabular orientation is typically planned using a supine pelvic reference, including navigation and robotic systems19,20. RI was fixed at 40° to isolate the effects of anteversion and spinopelvic parameters on impingement-free motion, while RA was incrementally adjusted in 1° steps across a wide range. ROM evaluations were conducted under 2 pelvic orientations derived from kinematic analysis14: (1) maximal anterior tilt (representing high-risk hip flexion/internal rotation) and (2) standing tilt (representing high-risk hip extension/external rotation). The acceptable range of RA was defined as the span of angles that satisfied all required ROM thresholds—≥110° flexion, ≥30° extension, ≥30° internal rotation at 90° flexion, and ≥30° external rotation21,22—without prosthetic impingement in both pelvic positions. CA was then calculated by adding femoral stem anteversion to each acceptable RA value23. We selected CA rather than cup anteversion alone because stem version is also a major determinant of impingement. The minimum (CA_min), maximum (CA_max), midpoint (CA_mid), and tolerance width (CA_range = CA_max − CA_min) were recorded.

Oscillation Angle Evaluation

To account for implant-specific ROM limits, we evaluated the oscillation angle for each implant24. In the absence of a standardized definition24,25, it was defined as the total arc of flexion and extension without impingement with the cup set at 40° RI and 20° RA and the stem at 20° anteversion.

Statistical Analysis

Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations between spinopelvic parameters (PI–LL mismatch and ΔAPPa) and CA metrics were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. To identify independent predictors of RA_mid and RA_range, multivariable regression analyses with the stepwise selection method (entry/removal p < 0.20) were conducted. Candidate variables included age at surgery, sex, BMI, follow-up duration, PI–LL mismatch, ΔAPPa, oscillation angle, and stem anteversion. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro software (version 16.0; SAS Institute Inc.).

Ethical Review Statement

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (23113-00). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

By contrast, patients with stiff spinopelvic mobility showed no significant difference in the CA_mid compared with normal subjects (44.2° vs. 46.8°, p = 0.37). However, the CA_range was significantly narrower in the stiff SPM group (mean 14.5° vs. 19.4°, p = 0.047) (Table I).

There was a significant negative correlation between PI–LL mismatch and the CA_mid (R = –0.46, p < 0.0001; regression: CA_mid = 46.62 − 0.22 × PI–LL), indicating that greater sagittal imbalance was associated with a lower required CA. Discussion

Spinopelvic abnormalities influenced both the target CA and the range width, based on patient-specific kinematic analysis using 3D-to-2D model-image registration data from 108 hips. Unlike prior studies that relied on static alignment parameters or simplified assumptions, this approach incorporated actual postoperative implant positioning and dynamic pelvic tilt angles observed during functional movement. For every 10° increase in PI–LL mismatch, the optimal CA decreased by 2.2°, and a 10° decrease in ΔAPPa—reflecting stiffer pelvic mobility—reduced the impingement-free CA range by 2.4°, indicating that sagittal imbalance and pelvic stiffness, respectively, lower and narrow the safe zone for cup orientation.

Patients with flatback deformity exhibited significantly reduced CA midpoints compared with those with normal spinopelvic alignment, consistent with the negative correlation between PI–LL mismatch and CA_mid. This finding aligns with prior studies indicating persistent posterior pelvic tilt in flatback patients, which may shift the functional safe zone posteriorly and increase the risk of posterior impingement26.

By contrast, stiff spinopelvic mobility did not alter the target CA midpoint but significantly narrowed the impingement-free safe zone, reducing the margin for error and emphasizing the need for accurate cup positioning. The positive correlation between pelvic mobility (ΔAPPa) and CA tolerance suggests that dynamic pelvic motion widens the safe zone. Accordingly, technologies and implant designs reported to increase impingement-free ROM—such as CT-based navigation, computer-assisted surgical systems, large femoral heads, or dual mobility liners16,27,28—may be particularly beneficial.

Multivariable analysis revealed that the midpoint of impingement-free radiographic anteversion was significantly associated with PI–LL mismatch and stem anteversion, underscoring the relevance of sagittal alignment and supporting the concept of combined anteversion in reducing instability23. The range of radiographic anteversion was independently associated with pelvic mobility (ΔAPPa), implant oscillation angle, and PI–LL mismatch. Smaller oscillation angles reduced impingement tolerance and require greater placement accuracy. Although PI–LL mismatch was not linearly correlated with the anteversion range, its independent association may reflect interactions with pelvic mobility, as larger PI–LL mismatch is often accompanied by reduced ΔAPPa.

There are several limitations to this study. First, spinopelvic alignment was assessed only postoperatively, and therefore, changes from the preoperative state could not be evaluated. Second, the simulated motion was limited to chair-rising from the seated to standing position. However, both seated and standing postures are known to be common positions at the time of dislocation29 and are therefore considered clinically relevant. Third, the ROM evaluation model used a simplified impingement algorithm and did not account for soft tissue or bone-to-bone contact; moreover, RI was fixed at 40°, and the effects of varying inclination were not evaluated. Fourth, patient-reported outcomes were unavailable, and further studies incorporating clinical outcomes are necessary to clarify the clinical relevance of these functional safe zones. Clinically, spinopelvic alignment parameters may help identify patients in whom conventional target orientations are inadequate and support individualized acetabular positioning. By incorporating dynamic pelvic motion, this study complements prior static patient-specific models30 when defining functional acetabular targets.

In conclusion, spinopelvic abnormalities significantly influence both the optimal target and the tolerance range of combined anteversion after THA. Flatback deformity was associated with a lower required CA, whereas stiff spinopelvic mobility reduced the impingement-free margin. These findings support individualized cup positioning strategies that incorporate patient-specific spinopelvic mechanics to enhance implant stability and reduce dislocation risk.
