On the connection between zero-free regions and the error term in the prime number theorem Thanks: F. Broucke was supported by a postdoctoral fellowship (grant number 12ZZH23N) of the Research Foundation – Flanders
Frederik Broucke

Abstract
. We provide for a wide class of zero-free regions an upper bound for the error term in the Prime Number Theorem, refining works of Pintz (1980), Johnston (2024), and Révész (2024). Our method does not only apply to the Riemann zeta function, but to general Beurling zeta functions. Next we construct Beurling zeta functions having infinitely many zeros on a prescribed contour, and none to the right, for a wide class of such contours. We also deduce an oscillation result for the corresponding error term in the Prime Number Theorem, showing that our aforementioned refinement is close to being sharp.

1.
Introduction

In his 1859 paper, Riemann communicated his revolutionary insight that the distribution of prime numbers is intimately connected with the distribution of zeros of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
. His ideas led to the first proofs of the Prime Number Theorem (PNT) by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, which crucially make use of the fact that
\zeta(s)
does not vanish for
\operatorname{Re}s\geq 1
. Later, de la Vallée Poussin proved that
\zeta(s)
does not vanish in the region
\sigma>1-c/\log(\lvert t\rvert+2)
for some
c>0
(where we write
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
). From this he deduced the following form of the PNT with remainder:

\psi(x)\coloneqq\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)=x+O\Bigl(x\exp\bigl(-c^{\prime}\sqrt{\log x}\bigr)\Bigr),\quad\text{for some }c^{\prime}>0.

In his 1932 monograph
[
11
]
, Ingham posed the question of what error term would follow from a general zero-free region
\sigma>1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)
for a certain function
\eta
. His initial result was greatly improved by János Pintz in 1980
[
18
]
. Before stating Pintz’s theorem we define, for a given function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,1/2]
, the function

\omega_{\eta}(x)\coloneqq\inf_{t\geq 1}\bigl(\eta(t)\log x+\log t\bigr).

When
\eta
is clear from the context, we will often omit the subscript and simply write
\omega(x)
.

Theorem 1.1

(Pintz)
.

Suppose that
\eta:[0,\infty)\to(0,1/2]
is continuously decreasing, and suppose that
\zeta(s)
has no zeros in the domain
\sigma>1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\psi(x)-x\ll_{\varepsilon}x\exp\bigl(-(1-\varepsilon)\omega(x)\bigr).

In the same paper, Pintz also showed the following converse.

Theorem 1.2

(Pintz)
.

Suppose that
\zeta(s)
has infinitely many zeros in the domain
\sigma\geq 1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)
, where
f(u)\coloneqq\eta(\mathrm{e}^{u})
is such that
f^{\prime}(u)
is non-decreasing with limit
0
and strictly increasing to
0
if
f\to 0
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\psi(x)-x=\Omega_{\pm}\Bigl(x\exp\bigl(-(1+\varepsilon)\omega(x)\bigr)\Bigr).

These two theorems provide a close to definitive answer to Ingham’s question. Note that the Riemann hypothesis (RH) implies that
\psi(x)-x\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{1/2+\varepsilon}
, so that both theorems follow trivially from RH: the first because the conclusion is always true, the second because the premise is always false (at least if
\eta<1/2
).

A natural broader context to study Ingham’s question is that of
Beurling zeta functions
attached to
Beurling generalized number systems
. Such zeta functions need not satisfy RH, and it is conceivable that a wide range of functions
\eta
can occur as describing the asymptotic best zero-free region

1-\eta(t)+\mathrm{i}t
as connecting the extreme points of the convex hull of the set of all zeros. Instead of using a function

[

]
, in particular to Section 8 regarding some subtleties for the converse Pintz theorem.

\eta_{\mathrm{RZ}}
is a function asymptotically describing this zero-distribution, the Pintz theorems for functions
\eta
which are asymptotically larger than or smaller than
\eta_{\mathrm{RZ}}
are trivial consequences of the one for
\eta=\eta_{\mathrm{RZ}}
.

Recently, Révész
[

generalized Pintz’s theorems to the Beurling context (see Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
below), further confirming that
1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)\longleftrightarrow x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\eta}(x)}
is the “true connection” between zero-free regions and error terms in the PNT.

The aim of this paper is twofold. First, we prove a refinement of the first Pintz–Révész theorem replacing
\varepsilon
by a function tending to
0
. We note that in the case of the Riemann zeta function, such a refinement was recently obtained by Johnston
[
13
]
. Our refinement is slightly better and applies to general Beurling zeta functions, while Johnston’s result applies to a wider class of zero-free regions. Nonetheless, our theorem is sufficient to capture any “reasonable” choice of
\eta
.

Second, we show that our obtained refinement is sharp, up to the value of the constant of the secondary term
\varepsilon(x)\omega(x)
in the exponential. We do this by constructing for every “reasonable” choice of
\eta
, a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
whose zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has infinitely many zeros on the contour
\sigma=1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)
and none to the right, and for which we also obtain an oscillation result for
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-x
, matching the error term predicted by the refined Pintz–Révész theorem (up to the value of the aforementioned constant).

1.1.
Beurling generalized number systems

Beurling generalized number systems were introduced in
[
3
]
in order to investigate the minimal assumptions needed to prove the PNT. They form an abstraction of the multiplicative structure of the primes and integers, and typically lack any additive structure.

A Beurling generalized number system is a pair of sequences
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
where
\mathcal{P}
, the sequence of
generalized primes
, consists of real numbers
p_{k}
satisfying
1<p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\dotso
and
p_{k}\to\infty
. The sequence of
generalized primes

\mathcal{N}
consists of all possible products of generalized primes
n_{j}=p_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\dotsm p_{k}^{\nu_{k}}
(including the empty product
n_{0}=1
) ordered in a non-decreasing fashion. We allow repeated elements in both sequences; if a generalized integer has multiple factorizations into generalized primes, its multiplicity in the sequences
\mathcal{N}
equals the number of such distinct factorizations. It can be useful to think of these as different generalized integers with the same numerical value. (Alternatively, one may define a generalized number system as an abstract multiplicative semigroup with a multiplicative norm function.)

Given a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
, we set for
x\geq 1

\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\coloneqq\#\{p_{k}\leq x\},\quad N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\coloneqq\#\{n_{j}\leq x\}.

Note that
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(1)=0
and
N_{\mathcal{P}}(1)=1
. In this article, we will assume that

(1.1)

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O(x^{\theta}),

for some
A>0
and
\theta\in[0,1)
. This assumption is referred to as the integers being “
\theta
-well-behaved”; sometimes it is also called Axiom A. It guarantees that the associated
Beurling zeta function
, defined as

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{s}}=\prod_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-p_{k}^{-s}}

and initially holomorphic on
\operatorname{Re}s>1
due to the absolute convergence, is such that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)-\frac{A}{s-1}
admits analytic continuation to the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}s>\theta
. Finally we define the weighted prime-counting functions

\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{p_{k}^{\nu}\leq x}\frac{1}{\nu},\quad\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{p_{k}^{\nu}\leq x}\log p_{k}=\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(u),

which have Mellin–Stieltjes transform

\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\log\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s),\quad\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=-\frac{\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)}.

More background on Beurling generalized number systems can be found in the book
[
9
]
.

We mention that (
1.1
) implies the existence of an absolute constant
c>0
such that
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has no zeros in the region

(1.2)

\sigma>1-\frac{c(1-\theta)}{\log\lvert t\rvert},\quad\lvert t\rvert\geq t_{0}(\mathcal{P}),

and consequently

(1.3)

\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-x\ll_{\varepsilon}x\exp\bigl(-(2-\varepsilon)\sqrt{c(1-\theta)\log x}\bigr).

This follows from Landau’s method
[
15
]
(see also Theorem
6.1
below) and the bound

(1.4)

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\ll\frac{\lvert t\rvert^{\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\theta}}\log\lvert t\rvert}{\sigma-\theta},\quad\theta<\sigma\leq 1,\quad\lvert t\rvert\geq\mathrm{e}

in the critical strip (see e.g.
[
21
, Lemma 2.6]
).

In
[
23
]
, Révész generalized Pintz’s theorems to Beurling generalized number systems
2

1.3
does not account for a possible zeros
\rho_{0}=\beta_{0}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{0}
with

and

2.
Some remarks on the assumptions

The assumption that
f(u)
is eventually (meaning for sufficiently large
u
) decreasing,
C^{1}
, and strictly convex is not so restrictive. A general
f
can always be replaced by its
lower convex envelope

\breve{f}(u)\coloneqq\sup\{F(u):F\text{ convex with }\forall v\,F(v)\leq f(v)\}
without altering the function
\omega(x)
:

\inf_{u\geq 0}\bigl(\breve{f}(u)\log x+u\bigr)=\inf_{u\geq 0}\bigl(f(u)\log x+u\bigr),

see
[
23
, Lemma 10]
. Also, for any
f
converging to
0
,
\breve{f}
is always (strictly) decreasing, convex, and hence differentiable at all but countably many points. The strict convexity of
f
and the continuity of
f^{\prime}
we require for technical convenience: it guarantees that for every
x\geq 1
,
\inf_{u\geq 0}\bigl(f(u)\log x+u\bigr)
is attained at a unique point, namely
u_{0}(x)
.

Clearly
\breve{f}(u)\leq f(u)
, so if
\sigma>1-f(\log t)
is a zero-free region, then so is
\sigma>1-\breve{f}(\log t)
. Upon replacing
f
by
\breve{f}
, the Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}
from Theorem
1.6
will not necessarily have infinitely many zeros
on
the contour
\sigma\geq 1-f(\log t)
, but it will have infinitely many zeros
on or to the right
of this contour, namely on the contour
\sigma=1-\breve{f}(\log t)
.

The assumption
f(u)\succ 1/u
in Theorem
1.6
is also not so restrictive. In view of (
1.2
), we may in any case assume that
f(u)\gg 1/u
, while the case
f(u)=c/u
is the content of the paper
[
8
]
.

The most restrictive assumption we make is that
f
is of regular or slow variation. This need not be the case in general. Yet, most of the zero-free regions one typically has in mind satisfy this restriction, such as those given by
f(u)=u^{-\alpha}(\log u)^{\beta}
with
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
(
\beta\leq 0
if
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=1
). This assumption could potentially be weakened at the cost of more technical proofs. We deemed this not worth pursuing, as this assumption covers all practical cases, and the proofs are already quite technical at certain points.

The study of functions of slow and regular variation is the subject of a well-developed theory, see for instance the treatise
[
4
]
. Karamata’s characterisation theorem states that a regularly varying

(positive and measurable) of index
-\alpha
is of the form
f(u)=u^{-\alpha}L(u)
for some slowly varying function

, and Karamata’s representation theorem states that a slowly varying

(positive and measurable) is of the form

L(u)=\exp\biggl(g(u)+\int_{u_{0}}^{u}\frac{\varepsilon(v)}{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\biggr),\quad u\geq u_{0},

for certain bounded measurable functions
g
and
\varepsilon
, where

converges to a finite limit, and

converges to

; see e.g.
[
4

. In particular,
f(u)=u^{-\alpha+o(1)}
. Also, the asymptotic relation

holds uniformly for

If

\epsilon=\epsilon(x)=o(1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\bigl(f(u)\log x+(1-\epsilon)u\bigr)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u)\log x+1-\epsilon\geq f^{\prime}\bigl((1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x+1-\epsilon

\displaystyle=1-\epsilon-\frac{\lvert f^{\prime}\bigl((1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr)\rvert}{\lvert f^{\prime}\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\rvert}\geq 0,

if
x
is sufficiently large. Hence

S_{2,b,ii}\ll\sum_{j=0}^{\log x}(2^{j}\tau^{1+\delta})^{-\delta f(u_{0}(x))^{B}}\exp\Bigl(-f\bigl((1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x-(1-\epsilon)(1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\Bigr)\omega(x)^{C}.

Using that
\frac{1}{f(u)^{B}}\log\frac{1}{f(u)}=o(u)
we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{\log x}(2^{j}\tau^{1+\delta})^{-\delta f(u_{0}(x))^{B}}\ll\frac{1}{\delta f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)^{B}}\exp\bigl(-\delta f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)^{B}(1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr)\ll_{\delta}1.

Next, from the mean value theorem and the fact that
f^{\prime}
is increasing, we see that

f\bigl((1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x\geq f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x+\delta u_{0}(x)f^{\prime}\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x=f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x-\delta u_{0}(x),

whence

\displaystyle S_{2,b,ii}

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta}\exp\Bigl(-\omega(x)+\epsilon(1+\delta)u_{0}(x)\Bigr)\omega(x)^{C}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigl(-\omega(x)+(1+\delta)[A(1+\delta^{\prime})^{B}+\delta]f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)^{B}u_{0}(x)\Bigr)\omega(x)^{C}.

If
\tilde{\delta}>0
is a fixed positive number, we conclude that upon choosing
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
\delta^{\prime}
sufficiently small,

\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-x\ll_{\varepsilon,\delta,\delta^{\prime}}x\exp\Bigl(-\omega(x)+(A+\tilde{\delta})f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)^{B}u_{0}(x)\Bigr)\omega(x)^{C}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

For the Riemann zeta function, the asymptotically best known zero-free region and zero-density estimate near the
1
-line take the shape

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sigma>1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)=1-\frac{c}{(\log\lvert t\rvert)^{2/3}(\log_{2}\lvert t\rvert)^{1/3}},\quad\lvert t\rvert\geq t_{0}

(3.5)

\displaystyle N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{A(1-\sigma)^{3/2}}(\log T)^{C}.

The main tool in proving these estimates is the Vinogradov–Korobov estimate
\zeta(s)\ll\lvert t\rvert^{a(1-\sigma)^{3/2}}(\log\lvert t\rvert)^{2/3}
for the Riemann zeta function near the
1
-line. At the time of writing this paper, the best known constants in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) seem to be (see
[
2
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
respectively)

c=\frac{1}{48.0718},\quad A=57.8875,\text{ with }C\approx 10.95.

The zero-free region (
3.4
) yields

\omega(x)\asymp u_{0}(x)\asymp\frac{(\log x)^{3/5}}{(\log_{2}x)^{1/5}},

so that, with
B=3/2
,
f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)^{B}u_{0}(x)\asymp(\log_{2}x)^{-1/2}\ll 1
. Hence in this situation, the effect of the constant
C
is more important than that of the constant
A
. As far as the author is aware, the current lowest value of
C
is due to Pintz (
[
19
, Theorem 1]
), who showed
N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{86(1-\sigma)^{3/2}}(\log T)^{6}
, yielding

\psi(x)-x\ll x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\eta}(x)}\frac{(\log x)^{18/5}}{(\log_{2}x)^{6/5}},\quad\text{with $\eta$ as in \eqref{Riemann zero-free}}.

It is an interesting question whether one can obtain a “log-free
B=3/2
-type” zero-density estimate. The original
B=3/2
-type estimate is due to Halász and Turán (
[
10
, Theorem 3]
), who showed that

N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{(1-\sigma)^{3/2}(\log\frac{1}{1-\sigma})^{3}}.

They said “We laid no stress on obtaining the smallest possible exponent of
\log 1/(1-\sigma)
”. Interestingly, if one could lower this exponent
D
of
\log 1/(1-\sigma)
from
D=3
to any value below
3/2
, it would outperform in this application any estimate of the form (
3.5
) with
B=3/2
,
C>0
, yielding (along the same lines as the proof of Theorem
3.1
)

\psi(x)-x\ll x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\eta}(x)}\exp\bigl(O((\log_{2}x)^{D-1/2})\bigr).

Finally, note that

N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{(1-\sigma)^{3/2}(\log\frac{1}{1-\sigma})^{D}}\implies N\biggl(1-\frac{1}{(\log T)^{2/3}(\log_{2}T)^{2D/3}},T\biggr)\ll 1,

so that
D<1/2
would improve the Vinogradov–Korobov zero-free region (
3.4
) asymptotically. Therefore, a mere optimization of the Halász–Turán argument will likely not yield improvements beyond
D=1/2
.

4.
The Diamond–Montgomery–Vorhauer function

We now turn our attention to the construction of Beurling zeta functions having infinitely many zeros on a prescribed contour. A common strategy for constructing Beurling number systems satisfying certain properties, is to first construct a system
in the extended sense
satisfying those properties, and to then approximate it by an actual (discrete) system. By a Beurling number system in the extended sense we mean a pair of right-continuous non-decreasing functions
\mathcal{B}=(\Pi(x),N(x))
, supported on
[1,\infty)
, and satisfying

(4.1)

\Pi(1)=0,\quad N(1)=1,\quad\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N=\exp^{\ast}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi^{\ast n}}{n!}.

Here
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi^{\ast n}
denotes the
n
-th convolution power with respect to multiplicative convolution:

(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F\ast\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}G)(E)\coloneqq\iint_{uv\in E}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}G(v),

and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F^{\ast 0}=\delta_{1}
, the unit point mass at
x=1
, which is the unit for convolution.
If
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F=\sum_{n}f(n)\delta_{n}
,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}G(x)=\sum_{n}g(n)\delta_{n}
, then
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F\ast\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}G=\sum_{n}h(n)\delta_{n}
, where
h=f\ast g
is the usual Dirichlet convolution.

The last equation in (
4.1
) should be thought of as the “fundamental theorem of arithmetic” for the system
\mathcal{B}
, connecting the “primes” with the “integers”. On the Mellin transform side this translates to the “Euler product formula”

\zeta_{\mathcal{B}}(s)\coloneqq\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N(x)=\exp\biggl(\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi(x)\biggr).

We also define the Chebyshev function of the system as
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi(u)
, and note that
\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=-\frac{\zeta_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta_{\mathcal{B}}(s)}
.
We refer to
[
9
, Chapters 2–3]
for more background on these notions. A simple but fundamental example of a system in the extended sense is given by

\Pi(x)=\Li(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\frac{1-u^{-1}}{\log u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,\quad N(x)=x,\quad(x\geq 1).

Its associated Chebyshev and zeta functions are
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=x-\log x-1
and
\zeta_{\mathcal{B}}(s)=\frac{s}{s-1}
.

One way to introduce zeros to the zeta function is by considering, for fixed
\rho\in\mathbb{C}
, the function

\Li(x^{\rho})\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\frac{u^{\rho-1}-u^{-1}}{\log u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

One may check that

\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Li(x^{\rho})=\log\frac{s}{s-\rho}\quad\text{for }\operatorname{Re}s>\operatorname{Re}\rho

(
\log
denoting the principal branch of the logarithm), so that subtracting
\Li(x^{\rho})
from
\Pi(x)
introduces a zero at
s=\rho
for the zeta function. Note also that

\Li(x^{\rho})\sim\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho\log x},\quad\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Li(u^{\rho})=\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho}-\log x\sim\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho},\quad x\to\infty,

which aligns with the idea that a zeta zero at
s=\rho
should roughly correspond to a perturbation to
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
of the form
-\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}
via the Riemann–von Mangoldt explicit formula (
3.1
). The terms
\Li(x^{\rho})
were used in
[
6
]
to construct Beurling zeta functions having zeros and poles precisely at prescribed
finite
sets. This construction seems not feasible if one wants to have
infinitely many
zeros. The issue is guaranteeing that
\Pi(x)
, or equivalently,
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
, is non-decreasing. Indeed, an infinite sequence of zeros
(\rho_{k})_{k}
introduced in this way contributes to the derivative
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(x)
as
-\sum_{k}(x^{\rho_{k}-1}-x^{-1})
, and it is unclear how to handle this in general divergent series
6
6

6

For the ordinary primes one has the exact formula

\frac{\psi(x^{+})+\psi(x^{-})}{2}=x-\sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho}-\log 2\pi-\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\Bigr),

the sum being over the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Hence, at least as distributions one has
-\sum_{\rho}x^{\rho-1}=\sum_{n}\Lambda(n)\delta_{n}(x)+\frac{1}{x^{3}-x^{2}}-1
, so one expects positive divergence in
-\sum_{\rho}x^{\rho-1}
when
x
is a prime power and massive cancellation otherwise. However, such a behavior seems very hard to “cook up” for a general well-chosen sequence of zeros
(\rho_{k})_{k}
, without knowing in advance that they come from a positive prime measure!
.

In the construction of Diamond, Montgomery, and Vorhauer
[
8
]
, these issues are dealt with in a very ingenious manner. Instead of the simple
\frac{s-\rho}{s}
, another function is used to introduce a zero at
s=\rho
, namely

(4.2)

G(z)\coloneqq 1-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}-\mathrm{e}^{-2z}}{z}.

This is an entire function with zeros at
z=0
and
z=z_{\pm j}=x_{j}\pm\mathrm{i}y_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, where (see
[
8
, Lemmas 1–2]
)

(4.3)

x_{j}<-\frac{\log(\pi j/2)}{2},\quad x_{j}=-\frac{\log(\pi j)}{2}+O(j^{-1/2}),\quad\pi j<y_{j}<\pi(j+1).

Hence for
a>0
,
G(a(s-\rho))
has a zero at
s=\rho
(and infinitely many to the left of
\rho
).

In
[
8
]
, it is shown that
\log G(z)
can be expressed as a Mellin transform:

(4.4)

\log G(z)=-\int_{1}^{\infty}g(x)x^{-z-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where
g
is a function supported on
[\mathrm{e},\infty)
. As will become evident later on, the fact that
g
is supported away from
x=1
is crucial.
Below we collect the other necessary properties of
g
required for our analysis. They are proved in
[
8
, Section 4]
; see also
[
9
, Section 17.6]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
g
is nonnegative, continuous on
(\mathrm{e}^{2},\infty)
and continuously differentiable on
(\mathrm{e}^{4},\infty)
. On the interval
(\mathrm{e}^{m},\mathrm{e}^{m+1})
(
m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
),
g(x)
is a polynomial in
\log x
of degree at most
m-1
. Furthermore,
g(x)
satisfies the estimates

\displaystyle g(x)\log x

\displaystyle=1+O(x^{-1/2}),\quad

\displaystyle x\geq\mathrm{e}^{2}

\displaystyle\bigl(g(x)\log x\bigr)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ll x^{-3/2},\quad

\displaystyle x\geq\mathrm{e}^{5}.

Let us now consider arbitrary
\rho\in\mathbb{C}
and
a>0
. Multiplying a zeta function with the factor
G(a(s-\rho))
introduces a zero at
s=\rho
, and corresponds with adding to
\Pi(x)
the term

-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a})}{a}u^{\rho-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Note that this is nonzero only if
x>\mathrm{e}^{a}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\rho=\beta+\mathrm{i}\gamma
with
\beta\in[1/2,1)
and
\gamma\geq 1
and let
a\geq 4
. Then for
x\geq\mathrm{e}^{5a}
we have

-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a})}{a}(\log u)u^{\beta-1}\cos(\gamma\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=-\frac{x^{\beta}\sin(\gamma\log x)}{\gamma}+O\biggl(\frac{x^{\beta-1/(2a)}}{\gamma}+\frac{x^{\beta}}{\gamma^{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{5a\beta}\biggr),

while for
\mathrm{e}^{a}\leq x<\mathrm{e}^{5a}
,

-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a})}{a}(\log u)u^{\beta-1}\cos(\gamma\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=O\biggl(\frac{x^{\beta}}{\gamma}\biggr).

Here, the implicit
O
-constants are absolute; they do not depend on
\rho
or
a
.

Proof.

If
x\geq\mathrm{e}^{5a}
, we split the integral into the part from
1
tot
\mathrm{e}^{5a}
and the part from
\mathrm{e}^{5a}
to
x
. The first integral is
O(\mathrm{e}^{5a\beta})
, while the second can be estimated via integrating by parts and using the estimates from Lemma
4.1
.

If
x<\mathrm{e}^{5a}
, we split the integral in integrals over the intervals
[\mathrm{e}^{ma},\min(\mathrm{e}^{(m+1)a},x)]
,
1\leq m\leq 4
. On each such interval, we write
g(u^{1/a})\log u^{1/a}=P_{m}(\log u^{1/a})
, where
P_{m}
is a polynomial of degree at most
m
(cfr. Lemma
4.1
), and integrate by parts.
∎

If we now consider an infinite sequence of zeros
(\rho_{k})_{k}
and a corresponding sequence
(a_{k})_{k}
increasing to infinity, then we have no issues of convergence in the contributions to
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
and
\psi_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(x)
: in the series

-\sum_{k}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a_{k}})}{a_{k}}(\log u)u^{\rho_{k}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\quad\text{and}\quad-\sum_{k}\frac{g(x^{1/a_{k}})}{a_{k}}(\log x)x^{\rho_{k}-1},

only the terms with
\mathrm{e}^{a_{k}}\leq x
are nonzero, owing to the support of
g
. So, for each fixed
x
, only finitely many terms contribute to the series.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

5.1.
The setup

We recall that we assume that
f
is an (eventually)
C^{1}
, decreasing, and strictly convex function satisfying
f(u)\succ 1/u
, which is either regularly varying of index
-\alpha
(
0<\alpha\leq 1
) or slowly varying, in which case it also satisfies (
1.10
). We introduce the following parameters:

(5.1)

\gamma_{k}\coloneqq\exp(4^{k}),\quad\ell_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{f(\log\gamma_{k})},\quad\beta_{k}\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}},\quad a_{k}\coloneqq\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}\Bigr)\log\gamma_{k}.

We will define a zeta function having a bunch of zeros near
\beta_{k}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{k}
, for every integer
k\geq k_{0}
with
k_{0}
sufficiently large. We set

(5.2)

M_{k}\coloneqq\biggl\lfloor\exp\biggl(\frac{a_{k}}{\ell_{k}}-\sqrt{\frac{a_{k}}{\ell_{k}}}\biggr)\biggr\rfloor,\quad\gamma_{k,m}\coloneqq\gamma_{k}+c_{k,m}\frac{m\gamma_{k}}{M_{k}},\quad\rho_{k,m}\coloneqq\beta_{k}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{k,m},

for
m=0,1,\dotsc,M_{k}
. Here,
c_{k,m}
are real numbers with
c_{k,m}=1+o(1)
which will be determined later. From the outset we note that
a_{k}/\ell_{k}\sim\log\gamma_{k}f(\log\gamma_{k})\to\infty
and
M_{k}\leq\gamma_{k}^{1/\ell_{k}}
.

Finally we define

(5.3)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{s}{s-1}\prod_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\prod_{m=0}^{M_{k}}G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\rho_{k,m})\bigr)G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\overline{\rho_{k,m}})\bigr);

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Li(x)-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k}}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a_{k}})}{a_{k}}u^{-1/\ell_{k}}\cos(\gamma_{k,m}\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Note that in view of (
4.4
),

\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)=\exp\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x).

5.2.
The template Beurling number system

First of all we show that
\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)
is non-decreasing. For this it suffices to show that
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
, where
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(u)
. Let
x\geq\mathrm{e}^{a_{k_{0}}}
, and let
K
be such that
\mathrm{e}^{a_{K}}\leq x<\mathrm{e}^{a_{K+1}}
. Then

\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}(x)=1-\frac{1}{x}-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k}}\bigl(g(x^{1/a_{k}})\log x^{1/a_{k}}\bigr)\cos(\gamma_{k,m}\log x)x^{-1/\ell_{k}},

so that from
g(u)\log u\ll 1
(see Lemma
4.1
),

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{x}+O\Biggl(\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K}M_{k}x^{-1/\ell_{k}}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{x}+O\Biggl(\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K}\exp\biggl\{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{k}/\ell_{k}}}\biggr)\frac{a_{k}}{\ell_{k}}-\frac{a_{K}}{\ell_{k}}\biggr\}\Biggr).

The term corresponding with
k=K
is
\exp(-\sqrt{a_{K}/\ell_{K}})=o(1)
. For the terms with
k_{0}\leq k<K
, we note that
a_{k}\sim 4^{k}
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K}\exp\biggl\{\biggl(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_{k}/\ell_{k}}}\biggr)\frac{a_{k}}{\ell_{k}}-\frac{a_{K}}{\ell_{k}}\biggr\}

\displaystyle\leq o(1)+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{4^{K}}{2\ell_{k}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq o(1)+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-1}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{4^{K-k}}{2}\cdot 4^{k}f(4^{k})\Bigr)=o(1),

as
4^{k}f(4^{k})\to\infty
.
Hence,
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq 1
if
k_{0}
is sufficiently large.

If we now set
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{\mathrm{c}}(x)\coloneqq\exp^{\ast}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x))
,
N_{\mathrm{c}}(x)=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{\mathrm{c}}
, we have that
(\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x),N_{\mathrm{c}}(x))
is a Beurling number system in the extended sense with associated Beurling zeta function
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)
.

5.3.
Bounding
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)

Next we consider the infinite product for
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}
. Let
s=\sigma+\mathrm{i}t
with
\sigma\geq 1/2
and
t\geq 0
. Recalling the definition of
G
(
4.2
) we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\rho_{k,m})\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll 1,\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{k}\lvert s-\rho_{k,m}\rvert\leq 1;

(5.6)

\displaystyle G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\rho_{k,m})\bigr)

\displaystyle=1+O\biggl(\frac{1+\exp\bigl(2a_{k}(1-\sigma-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}})\bigr)}{a_{k}\lvert s-\rho_{k,m}\rvert}\biggr);

Let
t>0
be fixed. By the rapid increase of
\gamma_{k}
there is at most one
k\geq k_{0}
with
\gamma_{k}/2\leq t\leq 3\gamma_{k}
. For all other values of
k
one has
\lvert s-\rho_{k,m}\rvert\gg\gamma_{k}
.

Suppose now
\gamma_{k^{\prime}}/2\leq t\leq 3\gamma_{k^{\prime}}
for some (unique)

. There is at most one value of
m
,
0\leq m\leq M_{k^{\prime}}
with
\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+(m-1/2)\gamma_{k^{\prime}}/M_{k^{\prime}}\leq t<\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+(m+1/2)\gamma_{k^{\prime}}/M_{k^{\prime}}
, which we denote by
m^{\prime}
. If there is no such

, we set

or

depending on whether

or

. Recalling (

) with

\lvert\gamma_{m,k^{\prime}}-t\rvert\gg\lvert m-m^{\prime}\rvert\gamma_{k^{\prime}}/M_{k^{\prime}}
. Since

Hence the product (

) converges locally uniformly on

, and we obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)

\displaystyle\ll 1+\frac{1}{|s-1|},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }\forall k,m:\lvert t-\gamma_{k,m}\rvert>\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2M_{k}};

(5.8)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)

\displaystyle\ll\Bigl(1+t^{2(1-1/\ell_{k})(1-\sigma)}\Bigr)\min\biggl\{1,\frac{1}{\lvert t-\gamma_{k,m}\rvert\log t}\biggr\},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }\lvert t-\gamma_{k,m}\rvert\leq\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2M_{k}}.

In particular, if
T\geq 1
,

(5.9)

\int_{T}^{2T}\lvert\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\ll T,\quad\text{uniformly for }\sigma\geq 1/2.

5.4.
The location of the zeros

We now consider the zeros of
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}
. By construction, the zeros
\rho_{k,0}
and
\overline{\rho_{k,0}}
lie on the contour
\sigma=1-\eta(\lvert t\rvert)
. As
\eta
is decreasing, the zeros
\rho_{k,m}
and
\overline{\rho_{k,m}}
with
m>0
lie to the left of this contour. We claim that all others lie also to the left of this contour. In view of (
4.3
), (
5.1
), (
5.2
), and (
5.3
), every such zero in the region
\{s:\operatorname{Re}s\geq 1/2,\operatorname{Im}s\geq 0\}
is of the form

\rho_{k,m,\pm j}\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}+\frac{x_{j}}{a_{k}}+\mathrm{i}\Bigl(\gamma_{k,m}\pm\frac{y_{j}}{a_{k}}\Bigr),\quad k\geq k_{0},\quad 0\leq m\leq M_{k},\quad 1\leq j\leq J_{k},

with
J_{k}\sim(1/\pi)\gamma_{k}^{(1-2/\ell_{k})(1-1/\ell_{k})}
.
As
\eta
is decreasing, it suffices to check that
\operatorname{Re}\rho_{k,0,-j}<1-f(\log(\operatorname{Im}\rho_{k,0,-j}))
. For
j\leq J_{k}
we have

\log\Bigl(\gamma_{k}-\frac{y_{j}}{a_{k}}\Bigr)>\log\Bigl(\gamma_{k}-\frac{\pi j}{a_{k}}\Bigr)\geq\lambda\log\gamma_{k}

say, where
\lambda=1-\frac{1}{a_{k}\log\gamma_{k}}
.
From the assumption
f(\lambda u)\sim\lambda^{-\alpha}f(u)
with
0<\alpha\leq 1
or
f(\lambda u)\sim f(u)
, and the fact that this relation holds uniformly for
\lambda
in compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
, we see that, if
k_{0}
is sufficiently large in terms of
f
and
k\geq k_{0}
,

f(\log(\operatorname{Im}\rho_{k,-j}))<f(\lambda\log\gamma_{k})<\lambda^{-2}f(\log\gamma_{k})\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{4}{a_{k}\log\gamma_{k}}\Bigr)\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}.

It now indeed follows that

\operatorname{Re}\rho_{k,0,-j}\leq 1-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}-\frac{\log(\pi j/2)}{2a_{k}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}-\frac{4}{a_{k}\ell_{k}\log\gamma_{k}}<1-f(\log(\operatorname{Im}\rho_{k,0,-j})).

Finally we note the following concerning the number of zeros of
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}
. For
1/2<\sigma<1-1/\ell_{k}
, the number of zeros in the rectangle
[\sigma,1]\times\mathrm{i}[\gamma_{k}/2,3\gamma_{k}]
is

\sim\frac{M_{k}}{\pi}\gamma_{k}^{2(1-\sigma-1/\ell_{k})(1-1/\ell_{k})}

for
k
sufficiently large. In particular, for every fixed
\delta>0
,

(5.10)

N(\sigma,T)=\Omega\bigl(T^{2(1-\sigma)-\delta}\bigr),\quad\text{uniformly for }\sigma\in[1/2,1-\delta/2].

5.5.
The asymptotics of
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)=\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(u)
. Suppose
x
is large and fixed, and let
K\geq k_{0}
be such that
\mathrm{e}^{a_{K}}\leq x<\mathrm{e}^{a_{K+1}}
. From (
5.4
) and the fact that
\supp g\subseteq[\mathrm{e},\infty)
, we get that

\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)=x-1-\log x-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k}}\int_{\mathrm{e}^{a_{k}}}^{x}\bigl(g(u^{1/a_{k}})\log u^{1/a_{k}}\bigr)u^{\beta_{k}-1}\cos(\gamma_{k,m}\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

If
k\leq K-2
then
\mathrm{e}^{5a_{k}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{5a_{K}/16}\leq x^{5/16}
and
M_{k}\leq\mathrm{e}^{a_{k}/\ell_{k}}\leq x^{1/16}
, so that by Lemma
4.2

(5.11)

\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)=x-1-\log x-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{K-2}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k}}\Biggl(\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}\sin(\gamma_{k,m}\log x)}{\gamma_{k,m}}+O\biggl\{\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}\biggl(x^{-1/(2a_{k})}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\biggr)\biggr\}\Biggr)\\
+O\biggl(Kx^{6/16}+M_{K-1}\frac{x^{\beta_{K-1}}}{\gamma_{K-1}}+M_{K}\frac{x^{\beta_{K}}}{\gamma_{K}}\biggr).

At first, note that
K\ll\log_{2}x
,
\log\gamma_{K-1}=(1/16)\log\gamma_{K+1}\geq(1/17)\log x
say,
\log\gamma_{K}\geq(1/5)\log x
, and
M_{K}\leq\mathrm{e}^{a_{K}/\ell_{K}}\leq x^{1/\ell_{K}}
. Hence the final error term above is
O(x^{16/17})
.

Next, recalling (
1.5
) and (
5.1
),

\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}=x\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\ell_{k}}\log x-\log\gamma_{k}\Bigr)=x\exp\bigl(-h(\log\gamma_{k},x)\bigr).

The function
h(u,x)
attains its minimum
\omega(x)
when
u=u_{0}(x)
. Let

(5.12)

\kappa_{x}=\frac{\log u_{0}(x)}{\log 4},\quad k_{x}=\begin{cases}\lfloor\kappa_{x}\rfloor&\text{if }\kappa_{x}-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor\leq 1/2,\\
\lceil\kappa_{x}\rceil&\text{if }\kappa_{x}-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor>1/2.\end{cases}

In view of (
2.3
) or (
2.4
) we obtain
u_{0}(x)\ll f(u_{0}(x))\log x=o(\log x)
, so that in particular,
k_{x}<K-2
if
x
is sufficiently large. Note that
\log\gamma_{\kappa_{x}}=u_{0}(x)
(with the obvious definition of
\kappa_{x}
is not an integer) and

\displaystyle k>k_{x}

\displaystyle\implies\log\gamma_{k}\geq 2u_{0}(x),

\displaystyle k<k_{x}

\displaystyle\implies\log\gamma_{k}\leq\frac{u_{0}(x)}{2}.

With this information, we can estimate the terms in (
5.11
) with
k\neq k_{x}
: from Lemma
2.1
(with
\lambda=2
) it follows that there exists some
\mu>0
so that

M_{k}\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}\leq x\exp\bigl(-h(\log\gamma_{k},\log x)+f(\log\gamma_{k})\log\gamma_{k}\bigr)\ll x\exp\bigl(-\omega(x)-\mu u_{0}(x)\bigr),\quad k\neq k_{x}.

Next, as
a_{k_{x}}\asymp\log\gamma_{k_{x}}\asymp u_{0}(x)=o(\log x)
,
x^{-1/(2a_{k_{x}})}\to 0
. Hence it follows that

(5.13)

\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)=x-2\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k_{x}}}\Biggl(\frac{x^{\beta_{k_{x}}}\sin(\gamma_{k_{x},m}\log x)}{\gamma_{k_{x},m}}+o\Bigl(\frac{x^{\beta_{k_{x}}}}{\gamma_{k_{x}}}\Bigr)\Biggr)+o\bigl(x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}\bigr).

We now show that the above som has the desired order from Theorem
1.6
, for suitably chosen arbitrarily large values of
x
.

Let
k\geq k_{0}
be large and fixed and set

(5.14)

x_{k}\coloneqq u_{0}^{-1}(\log\gamma_{k})=u_{0}^{-1}(4^{k}),

where
u_{0}^{-1}
denotes the inverse function of the increasing function
u_{0}
. With this definition we obtain
u_{0}(x_{k})=\log\gamma_{k}
and
k_{x_{k}}=\kappa_{x_{k}}=k
. Next we set
\tilde{x}_{k}=\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{k}}x_{k}
, where
0\leq\epsilon_{k}<2\pi/\gamma_{k}
is chosen so that
\sin(\gamma_{k}\log\tilde{x}_{k})=(-1)^{k}
. Finally, recalling
\gamma_{k,m}=\gamma_{k}+c_{k,m}m\gamma_{k}/M_{k}
, we choose
c_{k,m}=1+o(1)
so that
\gamma_{k,m}\log\tilde{x}_{k}-\gamma_{k}\log\tilde{x}_{k}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. With these choices we obtain from (
5.13
) that

\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(\tilde{x}_{k})=\tilde{x}_{k}+2(-1)^{k}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{k}}\frac{(\tilde{x}_{k})^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k,m}}(1+o(1))+o\bigl(\tilde{x}_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(\tilde{x}_{k})}\bigr).

The error we make by replacing
x_{k}
with
\tilde{x}_{k}
is negligible:

\displaystyle\frac{(\tilde{x}_{k})^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k,m}}\asymp\frac{(x_{k})^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}=x_{k}\exp\bigl(-\omega(x_{k})\bigr)\asymp\tilde{x}_{k}\exp\bigl(-\omega(x_{k})\bigr);

\displaystyle\lvert\omega(\tilde{x}_{k})-\omega(x_{k})\rvert\leq\left\lvert\log\frac{\tilde{x}_{k}}{x_{k}}\right\rvert\ll\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}},\quad\text{see \eqref{omega slow variation}};

\displaystyle f\bigl(u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{k})\bigr)\sim(1+\alpha)\frac{\omega(\tilde{x}_{k})}{\log\tilde{x}_{k}}\sim(1+\alpha)\frac{\omega(x_{k})}{\log x_{k}}\sim f\bigl(u_{0}(x_{k})\bigr);

where in the last equation we again used (
2.3
) if
f
is regularly varying of index
-\alpha
and (
2.4
) if
f
is slowly varying (in which case we set
\alpha=0
above).
Finally, for every
\delta>0
it holds for sufficiently large
k
that

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Bigl((1-\delta)\frac{\log\gamma_{k}}{\ell_{k}}\Bigr)=\exp\bigl((1-\delta)u_{0}(x_{k})f\bigl(u_{0}(x_{k})\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\bigl((1-\delta)u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{k})f\bigl(u_{0}(x_{k})\bigr)\bigr)\geq\exp\bigl((1-2\delta)u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{k})f\bigl(u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{k})\bigr)\bigr).

Putting all this together we obtain for every
\delta>0
:

(5.15)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(\tilde{x}_{2k})-\tilde{x}_{2k}}{\tilde{x}_{2k}\exp\bigl\{-\omega(\tilde{x}_{2k})+(1-\delta)f\bigl(u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{2k})\bigr)u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{2k})\bigr\}}=\infty,

(5.16)

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(\tilde{x}_{2k+1})-\tilde{x}_{2k+1}}{\tilde{x}_{2k+1}\exp\bigl\{-\omega(\tilde{x}_{2k+1})+(1-\delta)f\bigl(u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{2k+1})\bigr)u_{0}(\tilde{x}_{2k+1})\bigr\}}=-\infty.

5.6.
The discrete approximation and conclusion of the proof

To conclude the proof, we will approximate the system in the extended sense
(\Pi_{\mathrm{c}},N_{\mathrm{c}})
by an actual discrete system, by which we mean a sequence of Beurling generalized primes
\mathcal{P}=(p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc)
for which
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
,
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
, and
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
are sufficiently close to
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)
,
N_{\mathrm{c}}(x)
, and
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)
respectively. For it, we employ the random approximation procedure from
[
7
]
, summarized in the following

Theorem 5.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
F
be a non-decreasing right-continuous function tending to
\infty
with
F(1)=0
and
F(x)\ll x/\log x
. Then there exists a sequence of generalized primes
\mathcal{P}=(p_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that
\lvert\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-F(x)\rvert\leq 2
and such that for any real
t
and any
x\geq 1

(5.17)

\Bigg\lvert\sum_{p_{j}\leq x}p_{j}^{-\mathrm{i}t}-\int_{1}^{x}u^{-\mathrm{i}t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F(u)\Bigg\rvert\ll\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{\frac{x\log(\lvert t\rvert+1)}{\log(x+1)}}.

We apply this theorem with
F(x)=\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)
. Hence we obtain a sequence of Beurling primes
\mathcal{P}
with
\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)+O(1)
. From this we deduce

(5.18)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)+O(\sqrt{x})=\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)+O(\sqrt{x}),

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(u)=\psi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)+O(\sqrt{x}\log x),

so that the second assertion of Theorem
1.6
follows immediately from (
5.15
) and (
5.16
).

Next we write

\displaystyle\log\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)-\log\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\bigl(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathrm{c}}(x)\bigr)+\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\bigl(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon Z_{1}(s)+Z_{2}(s).

The estimate (
5.17
) and a short calculation show that
Z_{1}(s)
defines an analytic function in the region
\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1/2
, where it satisfies the estimate

Z_{1}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll\frac{1}{\sigma-1/2}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\lvert t\rvert+1)}{\sigma-1/2}}.

Similarly, as
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
is a positive measure and in view of (
5.18
), also
Z_{2}(s)
is analytic for
\sigma>1/2
and satisfies
Z_{2}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll(\sigma-1/2)^{-1}
. Combining both estimates yields

(5.19)

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\mathrm{e}^{Z_{1}(s)+Z_{2}(s)}=\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\exp\biggl(O\biggl(\frac{1}{\sigma-1/2}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\lvert t\rvert+1)}{\sigma-1/2}}\biggr)\biggr),\quad\sigma>1/2.

We conclude that, apart from the pole at
s=1
,
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has analytic continuation to the half-plane
\sigma>1/2
, where it has the same zeros as
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)
, so that also the third assertion of Theorem
1.6
is proved.

It remains to show that
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
, for some
A>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
. To show this we will use Perron inversion as in
[
9
, Section 17.8]
:

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\leq\int_{x}^{x+1}N_{\mathcal{P}}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{2-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{2+\mathrm{i}\infty}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)\frac{(x+1)^{s+1}-x^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

We shift the contour of integration to
\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma_{0}\coloneqq 1/2+(\log x)^{-1/3}
. Letting
A\coloneqq\res_{s=1}\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
, we obtain

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\leq Ax+\frac{A}{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\sigma_{0}-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma_{0}+\mathrm{i}\infty}\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)\mathrm{e}^{Z_{1}(s)+Z_{2}(s)}\frac{(x+1)^{s+1}-x^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

We split the integral in dyadic intervals. Employing (
5.9
) and the bounds

\displaystyle\big\lvert\mathrm{e}^{Z_{1}(s)+Z_{2}(s)}\big\rvert\leq\exp\Bigl\{c\bigl((\log x)^{1/3}+(\log T)(\log x)^{1/6}\bigr)\Bigr\},\quad s=\sigma_{0}+\mathrm{i}t,\quad T\leq\lvert t\rvert\leq 2T

\displaystyle\big\lvert(x+1)^{s+1}-x^{s+1}\big\rvert\ll\begin{cases}Tx^{1/2}\exp((\log x)^{2/3})&\text{if }T\leq x,\\
x^{3/2}\exp((\log x)^{2/3})&\text{if }T>x,\end{cases}

a short calculation yields

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\leq Ax+O\Bigl(x^{1/2}\exp\bigl(c^{\prime}(\log x)^{2/3}\bigr)\Bigr),\quad\text{for some }c^{\prime}>0.

The other inequality follows similarly upon writing
N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)\geq\int_{x-1}^{x}N_{\mathcal{P}}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

5.7.
Variant 1: slower growth of
\zeta

In the above example, the additional factor
\exp\bigl((1-\delta)f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)u_{0}(x)\bigr)
was obtained by, roughly speaking, putting as much zeros as possible in the rectangle
[1/2,\beta_{k}]\times\mathrm{i}[\gamma_{k},2\gamma_{k}]
. The cost for doing this is the large growth of the resulting zeta function: in bounding
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(s)
pointwise in (
5.8
), we could only barely improve upon the “trivial” convexity bound (
1.4
). In particular, the analogue of the Lindelöf hypothesis fails for these zeta functions.

If one is content with
\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)
oscillating of size
x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}
only, then a simpler construction with fewer zeros suffices, resulting in a zeta function with much better pointwise bounds. In the next section we will employ this simpler construction to shed some light on the relationship between zero-free regions on the one hand, and pointwise bounds and the size of “clusters” of zeros of the zeta function on the other hand.

Instead of (
5.1
), (
5.3
), (
5.4
), we now set

(5.20)

\displaystyle\ell_{k}\coloneqq 4^{k},\quad\beta_{k}\coloneqq 1-1/\ell_{k},\quad\gamma_{k}\coloneqq\exp\bigl(f^{-1}(1/\ell_{k})\bigr),\quad\rho_{k}\coloneqq\beta_{k}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{k},\quad a_{k}\coloneqq\ell_{k};

(5.21)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(s)\coloneqq\frac{s}{s-1}\prod_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\rho_{k})\bigr)G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\overline{\rho_{k}})\bigr);

(5.22)

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}(x)\coloneqq\Li(x)-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a_{k}})}{a_{k}}u^{-1/\ell_{k}}\cos(\gamma_{k}\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

The method of Subsection
5.2
no longer applies to see that
\Pi_{1}(x)
is non-decreasing. However, the non-decreasingness can be proven in exactly the same way as
[
9
, Lemmas 17.20–17.21]
, the only difference being the value of the
\gamma_{k}
. However, they only occur in the cosine, which is estimated trivially by
1
. Letting
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{1}(x)\coloneqq\exp^{\ast}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{1}(x))
,
N_{1}(x)=\int_{1^{-}}^{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{1}
, we have that
(\Pi_{1}(x),N_{1}(x))
is a Beurling number system in the extended sense with associated zeta function

\exp\biggl(\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{1}(x)\biggr)=\zeta_{1}(s).

Similarly as in Subsection
5.3
one has

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\ll 1+\frac{1}{|s-1|},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }\forall k:\lvert t-\gamma_{k}\rvert>\gamma_{k}/2;

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\ll\biggl(1+\exp\biggl(\frac{2(1-\sigma)}{f(\log\gamma_{k})}\biggr)\biggr)\min\biggl\{1,\frac{f(\log\gamma_{k})}{\lvert s-\rho_{k}\rvert}\biggr\},

(5.23)

\displaystyle\ll\biggl(1+\exp\biggl(\frac{2(1+o(1))(1-\sigma)}{f(\log t)}\biggr)\biggr)\min\biggl\{1,\frac{f(\log t)}{\lvert s-\rho_{k}\rvert}\biggr\},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }\lvert t-\gamma_{k}\rvert\leq\gamma_{k}/2.

In particular, as
1/f(u)=o(u)
, we have
\zeta_{1}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll_{\varepsilon}\lvert t\rvert^{\varepsilon}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, so the analogue of the Lindelöf hypothesis holds for
\zeta_{1}
.

The analysis of the zeros is similar to Subsection
5.4
. Regarding the oscillation of
\psi_{1}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{1}(u)
, one can in this case actually show that

(5.24)

\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{1}(x)-x}{x\exp\bigl(-\omega(x)\bigr)}=2,\quad\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{1}(x)-x}{x\exp\bigl(-\omega(x)\bigr)}=-2.

This is done analogously as in Subsection
5.5
, except that one sets

\kappa_{x}=\frac{\log\frac{1}{f(u_{0}(x))}}{\log 4},\quad k_{x}=\begin{cases}\lfloor\kappa_{x}\rfloor&\text{if }\kappa-\lfloor\kappa_{x}\rfloor\leq 1/2,\\
\lceil\kappa_{x}\rceil&\text{if }\kappa-\lfloor\kappa_{x}\rfloor>1/2.\end{cases}

Now one has
u_{0}(x)=f^{-1}(4^{-\kappa_{x}})
, and

\displaystyle k>k_{x}

\displaystyle\implies\log\gamma_{k}\geq f^{-1}\biggl[\frac{1}{2}f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\biggr]\geq(2+o(1))u_{0}(x),

\displaystyle k<k_{x}

\displaystyle\implies\log\gamma_{k}\leq f^{-1}\Bigl[2f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\Bigr]\leq\biggl(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\biggr)u_{0}(x);

where the right-most equalities follow from the regular or slow variation of
f
and the fact that
f^{-1}
is decreasing. Hence one can similarly invoke Lemma
2.1
to deduce (
5.24
).

Finally, the fact that
N_{1}(x)=Ax+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
for some
A>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
can be shown similarly as in Subsection
5.6
, or alternatively from applying a Schnee–Landau-type theorem using the bound
\zeta_{1}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}t)\ll_{\varepsilon}\lvert t\rvert^{\varepsilon}
.

5.8.
Variant 2:
o(x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)})
remainder

In the first construction, the location of the zeros
\rho_{k,m}=\beta_{k}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{k,m}
was chosen in such a way that the different oscillations
x^{\rho_{k,m}}/\rho_{k,m}
(
m=0,\dotsc,M_{k}
,
k
fixed) in the explicit Riemann–von Mangoldt formula (
3.1
) exhibit constructive interference, producing the additional factor
\exp\bigl((1-\delta)f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)u_{0}(x)\bigr)
. It is also possible to introduce destructive interference, and actually obtain a non-trivial saving over
x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}
in the error term of the PNT.

We will show this when
f(u)=u^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The parameters
\gamma_{k},\ell_{k},\beta_{k},a_{k}
remain the same as in (
5.1
). Next we set
M_{k}=1
and

(5.25)

\gamma_{k,0}=\gamma_{k},\quad\gamma_{k,1}=\gamma_{k}+\frac{\pi}{\log x_{k}},

where
x_{k}
is defined as in (
5.14
), so

x_{k}=u_{0}^{-1}(4^{k})=\exp\biggl(\frac{4^{(1+\alpha)k}}{\alpha}\biggr),

as
u_{0}(x)=(\alpha\log x)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
in this case. The Beurling number system is then defined via

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{s}{s-1}\prod_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\prod_{m=0}^{1}G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\rho_{k,m})\bigr)G\bigl(a_{k}(s-\overline{\rho_{k,m}})\bigr);

\displaystyle\Pi_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Li(x)-2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{1}\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(u^{1/a_{k}})}{a_{k}}u^{-1/\ell_{k}}\cos(\gamma_{k,m}\log u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

The analysis of the Subsections
5.2
,
5.3
,
5.4
, and
5.6
goes through without difficulties. The key difference with the first construction lies only in the analysis if
\psi_{2}(x)\coloneqq\int_{1}^{x}\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{2}(u)
.

Let
x
be large, and set again
k\coloneqq k_{x}
as in (
5.12
). The analysis in Subsection
5.5
can be followed to obtain

\psi_{2}(x)=x-2x^{\beta_{k}}\biggl(\frac{\sin(\gamma_{k,0}\log x)}{\gamma_{k,0}}+\frac{\sin(\gamma_{k,1}\log x)}{\gamma_{k,1}}\biggr)\\
+O\biggl(\frac{x^{\beta_{k}-1/(2a_{k})}}{\gamma_{k}}+\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{(\gamma_{k})^{2}}+x\exp\bigl(-\omega(x)-\mu u_{0}(x)\bigr)\biggr),

for some
\mu>0
. Recall from (
5.12
) that
k=k_{x}=\kappa_{x}+\vartheta_{x}
with
\lvert\vartheta_{x}\rvert\leq 1/2
, so
\log\gamma_{k}=4^{\vartheta_{x}}4^{\kappa_{x}}\asymp u_{0}(x)\asymp(\log x)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
. Also
\log x/a_{k}\asymp\log x/\log\gamma_{k}\asymp(\log x)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}
, and in any case
x^{\beta_{k}}/\gamma_{k}\ll x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}
. Hence we see that

\psi_{2}(x)=x-\frac{2x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}\bigl(\sin(\gamma_{k,0}\log x)+\sin(\gamma_{k,1}\log x)\bigr)+O\Bigl\{x\exp\Bigl(-\omega(x)-c(\log x)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\Bigr)\Bigr\},

for some
c>0
. We will show that the second term above is
\ll x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}(\log x)^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)}}
. When
x
is close to
x_{k}=u_{0}^{-1}(\log\gamma_{k})
,
x^{\beta_{k}}/\gamma_{k}
is close to
x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}
, and we will obtain cancellation from the two sines, which are exactly out of phase for
x=x_{k}
, see (
5.25
). When
x
is further removed from
x_{k}
, we cannot extract cancellation from the sum of sines anymore, and we will instead use that
x^{\beta_{k}}/\log\gamma_{k}=x\exp(-h(\log\gamma_{k},x))
becomes smaller compared to
x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}
, as
\log\gamma_{k}=u_{0}(x_{k})
is then further removed from
u_{0}(x)
.

We start by writing
x=(x_{k})^{1+\nu}
. As
u_{0}(x)=4^{\kappa_{x}}=4^{k-\vartheta_{x}}=4^{-\vartheta_{x}}u_{0}(x_{k})
, we have that

x=\exp\biggl(\frac{1}{\alpha}\bigl(u_{0}(x_{k})4^{-\vartheta_{x}}\bigr)^{1+\alpha}\biggr)=(x_{k})^{4^{-(1+\alpha)\vartheta_{x}}},

so
4^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}-1\leq\nu\leq 4^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}-1
. We have

(5.26)

u_{0}\bigl((x_{k})^{1+\nu}\bigr)=(1+\nu)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}u_{0}(x_{k})=(1+\nu)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\log\gamma_{k},\quad f\bigl[u_{0}\bigl((x_{k})^{1+\nu}\bigr)\bigr]=(1+\nu)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}f\bigl[u_{0}(x_{k})\bigr].

If
\lvert\nu\rvert
is larger than some fixed
\delta>0
, then we can invoke Lemma
2.1
with
\lambda=(1+\delta)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
or
\lambda=(1-\delta)^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
to see that there exists a constant
\mu(\delta)>0
for which

\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}=x\exp\bigl(-h(\log\gamma_{k},x)\bigr)\leq x\exp\bigl(-\omega(x)-\mu(\delta)u_{0}(x)\bigr).

Hence we may assume that
\lvert\nu\rvert\leq\delta
for some small fixed
\delta>0
.

With (
5.25
) and some trigonometry one finds

\sin(\gamma_{k,0}\log x)+\sin(\gamma_{k,1}\log x)=\bigl(1-\cos(\pi\nu)\bigr)\sin(\gamma_{k}\log x)-\sin(\pi\nu)\cos(\gamma_{k}\log x)\ll\lvert\nu\rvert.

On the other hand, for small
\nu
, in view of (
5.26
) and the fact that
f\bigl(u_{0}(x)\bigr)\log x=u_{0}(x)/\alpha
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\beta_{k}}}{\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle=x\exp\biggl(-\omega(x)-\biggl(\frac{(1+\nu)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}-1}{\alpha}+(1+\nu)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}-1\biggr)u_{0}(x)\biggr)

\displaystyle=x\exp\biggl(-\omega(x)-\biggl(\frac{\nu^{2}}{2(1+\alpha)}+O(\nu^{3})\biggr)u_{0}(x)\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq x\exp\biggl(-\omega(x)-\frac{\nu^{2}u_{0}(x_{k})}{4(1+\alpha)}\biggr),

provided that
\delta
is sufficiently small, as
u_{0}(x)=\bigl(1+O(\lvert\nu\rvert)\bigr)u_{0}(x_{k})
in view of (
5.26
).
Combining both we find that

\frac{\psi_{2}(x)-x}{x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}}\ll\exp\biggl(\log\lvert\nu\rvert-\frac{u_{0}(x_{k})\nu^{2}}{4(1+\alpha)}\biggr).

The function
\log\lvert\nu\rvert-u_{0}(x_{k})\nu^{2}/(4(1+\alpha))
is maximal when
\lvert\nu\rvert=\sqrt{2(1+\alpha)/u_{0}(x_{k})}
, at which it takes the value
\log\sqrt{2(1+\alpha)/u_{0}(x_{k})}-1/2
, so that

\frac{\psi_{2}(x)-x}{x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}}\ll\exp\Biggl(\log\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{u_{0}(x_{k})}}\Biggr)\asymp(\log x_{k})^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)}}\asymp(\log x)^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)}}.

Summarizing we obtain

Proposition 5.2
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
there exists a Beurling number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
satisfying

(1)

N_{\mathcal{P}}(x)=Ax+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
for some
A>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
;

(2)

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
has infinitely many zeros on the contour
\sigma=1-(\log\lvert t\rvert)^{-\alpha}
, and none to the right of this contour;

(3)

the error term in the PNT satisfies

\psi_{\mathcal{P}}(x)-x\ll x\mathrm{e}^{-\omega(x)}(\log x)^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)}}.

In particular, in Theorem
1.4

\varepsilon
cannot be taken to be zero.

6.
Some remarks on zero-distribution and growth of Beurling zeta functions

Finally we discuss some connections between zero-free regions on the one hand, and growth, zero-density estimates, and the size of zero clusters of the Beurling zeta function on the other hand.

6.1.
Landau’s method for deducing zero-free regions

Currently, the best technology available for producing zero-free regions is the century-old method of Landau
[
15
]
. Given an upper bound for
\lvert\zeta\rvert
near the
1
-line, one uses the Borel–Carathéodory lemma to upper bound
-\operatorname{Re}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}
in terms of the
\lvert\zeta\rvert
-bound and the real part of a hypothetical nearby zero. Juxtaposing this with the familiar “3-4-1-inequality” yields an upper bound for the real part of the hypothetical zero, i.e. a zero-free region.

Theorem 6.1

(Hauptsatz of
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\varphi(t)
and
\psi(t)
be positive non-decreasing functions for
t\geq t_{0}
satisfying
\varphi(t)\to\infty
,
\psi(t)\geq 1
, and

(6.1)

\psi(t)\ll\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(t)}.

Let
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}
be a positive measure on
[1,\infty)
for which
\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-\sigma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}(x)
converges for
\sigma>1
. Suppose
F(s)=\exp\int_{1^{-}}^{\infty}x^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}(x)
has analytic continuation to
\sigma\geq 1-1/\psi(\lvert t\rvert)
, with the exception of a simple pole at
s=1
, and suppose

(6.2)

F(s)\ll\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(t)}\quad\text{for}\quad\sigma\geq 1-\frac{1}{\psi(t)},\quad t\geq t_{0}.

Then there is a constant
c>0
so that

(6.3)

F(s)\neq 0\quad\text{for}\quad\sigma\geq 1-\frac{c}{\psi(2t+1)\varphi(2t+1)},\quad t\geq t_{0}.

Remark 6.2
.

•

Landau formulated his Hauptsatz for functions
F
of the form
F(s)=\exp\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-s}
with
a_{n}\geq 1
, but the proof readily applies to the more general case. Also he considered the point
3t
instead of
2t+1
. Typically
\varphi(t)
and
\psi(t)
are slowly varying, so the specific constant in front of
t
is not so important.

•

The standard works of Titchmarsh and Ivić also contain this result for the Riemann zeta function specifically (see
[
24
, Theorem 3.10]
and
[
12
, Lemma 6.8]
respectively), but interestingly, they both require that
\varphi(t)\psi(t)=o(\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(t)})
, instead of Landau’s slightly more general condition (
6.1
) which is sufficient.

•

The proof hinges on a clever use of a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial such as
3+4\cos t+\cos(2t)
. Landau investigated the use of other polynomials. Setting

V\coloneqq\inf\biggl\{\frac{a_{1}+\dotsb+a_{n}}{(\sqrt{a_{1}}-\sqrt{a_{0}})^{2}}:a_{k}\geq 0,\quad a_{1}>a_{0},\quad a_{0}+a_{1}\cos t+\dotsb+a_{n}\cos(nt)\geq 0\biggr\},

he showed

7

7

This work predates his discovery using the “local method”
[

]
and employs the Hadamard factorization of

yielding the better constant
2/V
instead of

in the de la Vallée Poussin zero-free region.

14

for the Riemann zeta function that it has no zeros for
\sigma>1-\frac{2/V-\varepsilon}{\log t}
,
t\geq t_{0}(\varepsilon)
, for every

. Determining the value of

Tracking the best constants in Landau’s proof of his Hauptsatz, one may see that any

In this context it is interesting to consider the zeta function
\zeta_{1}(s)

Landau’s theorem then yields that

6.2.
Zero-density and clustering

For the Riemann zeta function, the Vinogradov–Korobov type estimate

\zeta(s)\ll\lvert t\rvert^{a(1-\sigma)^{3/2}}(\log\lvert t\rvert)^{2/3}

In

[

[
16

T\geq 0

n(T,r)\coloneqq\#\bigl\{\rho:\lvert 1+\mathrm{i}T-\rho\rvert\leq r\text{ and }\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho)=0\bigr\}\quad\text{(counted with multiplicity)}.

Corollary 6.4

(Révész)
.

Let
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
be the Beurling zeta function of a number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
satisfying (
1.1
). Suppose
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{0})=0
, where
\rho_{0}=\beta_{0}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{0}
with
\gamma_{0}
sufficiently large and
1-\beta_{0}<\frac{1-\theta}{40\log_{2}\gamma_{0}}
. There exist positive absolute constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for every
\delta>0
and every
\kappa\geq 1

1-\beta_{0}>C_{1}\delta
, or there exists an

for which

(6.5)

n(\gamma_{0},r)+n(2\gamma_{0},r)\geq\frac{C_{2}}{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\cdot\frac{r^{\kappa}}{\delta^{\kappa-1}(1-\beta_{0})}.

It is worth pointing out that such clustering results can also be used to obtain zero-free regions from upper bounds on
\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)
by Jensen’s inequality, see the discussion in
[
16
, Chapter 11]

Again, the examples in the style of Diamond, Montgomery, and Vorhauer show that this clustering result is sharp. Indeed, consider
\zeta_{1}(s)
(

) (or a discrete random approximation of it). It has a zero
\rho_{k}=\beta_{k}+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{k}
with

Comparing with (

\frac{C_{2}}{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\cdot\frac{r^{\kappa}\ell_{k}}{\delta^{\kappa-1}}\leq n(\gamma_{k},r)+n(2\gamma_{k},r)\ll\ell_{k}r,

showing that

cannot be chosen arbitrarily large in (

.
