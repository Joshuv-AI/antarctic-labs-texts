Postmortem Demonstration of Tau Pathology in a Korean Patient With WDR45-Related β-Propeller Protein–Associated Neurodegeneration
Authors: Jae Eun Sohn, Kyoungyul Lee, YeShin Kim, Seung-Hwan Lee
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We previously reported a Korean patient with β-propeller protein–associated neurodegeneration (BPAN) caused by a heterozygous WDR45 mutation (c.345-1G>A).1 The patient was a 38-year-old female with lifelong developmental delay in whom adult-onset progressive parkinsonism and chorea had developed at the time of initial report. She also had intellectual disability since childhood. Formal neuropsychological assessments were not available; however, her cognitive abilities during the last 10 years of her life in a long-term care facility were limited to simple verbal communication. She died at the of 53 years old, and a postmortem neuropathological examination was performed through the Kangwon National University Hospital Brain Bank (Autopsy No. A25-1).

Gross examinations revealed marked frontotemporal and hippocampal atrophy, moderate hypopigmentation of the substantia nigra, and a brain weight of 931 g, which is well below the typical range for adult females (approximately 1,200 g), indicating severe cerebral atrophy in the context of BPAN.2 Microscopy revealed severe neuronal loss in the substantia nigra and hippocampal sclerosis. Iron staining confirmed iron deposition in the substantia nigra and globus pallidus, which was consistent with neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA).

Premortem MRI performed at age 38 (at the time of the initial report) and postmortem pathological findings are summarized in Fig. 1. Gross examination of the left midbrain demonstrated brownish discoloration of the substantia nigra but no gross abnormality in the globus pallidus (Fig. 1A and B). Premortem MRI revealed bilateral hypointense lesions in the substantia nigra on T1-weighted images that were surrounded by a characteristic hyperintense halo, with corresponding hypointense signals on gradient-echo images (Fig. 1C). Although no abnormal signal change was evident in the globus pallidus on T1-weighted images, bilateral hypointense lesions consistent with iron accumulation were noted on gradient-echo images (Fig. 1D).

Histological examinations demonstrated differential vulnerability between the globus pallidus and substantia nigra. While only mild iron and tau deposition were observed in the globus pallidus (Fig. 1E-G), the substantia nigra showed marked iron accumulation and prominent hyperphosphorylated tau deposition (Fig. 1H-J). Immunohistochemical analyses revealed widespread AT8 positive hyperphosphorylated tau pathology involving the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, and brainstem, with prominent tau-positive neurofibrillary tangles, pretangles, and neuropil threads, but no astrocytic or oligodendroglial tau inclusions (e.g., tufted astrocytes, astrocytic plaques, or coiled bodies). In contrast, immunostaining amyloid-β (4G8), α-synuclein, and TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) immunostaining produced negative findings. Neuropathological change associated with Alzheimer’s disease was absent (A0B0C0 according to the National Institute on Aging - Alzheimer’s Association [NIA-AA] criteria), and there was no evidence of other neurodegenerative proteinopathies.

These findings indicate that WDR45-related BPAN can progress to a distinct tauopathy, supporting the hypothesis that impaired autophagy due to WDR45 dysfunction leads to defective tau clearance and promotes neuronal degeneration. The observed tau pathology was characterized by prominent neuronal involvement, including neurofibrillary tangles, pretangles, and neuropil threads, without astrocytic or oligodendroglial tau inclusions, consistent with frontotemporal lobar degeneration-tau–like features. The coexistence of iron accumulation and tau deposition further suggests that impaired autophagic flux contributes to proteostatic failure in BPAN and links NBIA-related neurodegeneration to secondary tauopathy.34567

While axonal spheroids have been reported in some NBIA disorders, they were not systematically evaluated in the present case.7 In addition, AT100 phosphorylated tau antibody immunostaining was not performed, which may have resulted in insufficient characterization of the fibrillar nature of the tau deposits. Furthermore, tau immunostaining was performed using the AT8-positive hyperphosphorylated tau antibody, which detects phosphorylated tau but does not distinguish between the 3R and 4R isoforms, and so the isoform composition of the tau deposits could not be determined.

To our knowledge, this represents the first autopsy-confirmed BPAN case with tau pathology in South Korea, which extends the neuropathological spectrum of NBIA and reinforces the mechanistic link between defective autophagy and tau-mediated neurodegeneration.
