Structural basis for double-stranded DNA cytosine deamination by BaDTF3 and its application in mitochondrial genome editing
Authors: Lulu Yin, Chae Jin Lim, Ke Shi, Jae Hee Yoon, Bong-Kook Ko, Seungmin Ryou, Jin-Soo Kim, Hideki Aihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial deaminase toxin family (BaDTF) proteins are weapons used in bacterial warfare, and they are useful tools in base editing, epigenetics analyses, and genomic footprinting applications. Our previous studies revealed the mechanisms of 5’-TC-specific cytosine deamination in double-stranded (ds)DNA by DddA from BaDTF1 and sequence context-independent single-stranded (ss)DNA cytosine deamination by SsdA from BaDTF2. Here, we show that a representative member of BaDTF3, DddB, deaminates cytosines specifically in dsDNA, but with a broad sequence context preference. Our crystal structure of DddB bound to dsDNA reveals a distinct mechanism of substrate engagement, in which a helix-hairpin-helix motif inserted into the minor groove of dsDNA promotes flipping of the target cytosine into the enzyme active site. Based on the structural information, we generate both monomeric and split DddB-derived cytosine base editors (BdCBE) and demonstrate that they can perform CRISPR-free mitochondrial base editing in human cells, with an expanded targeting scope compared to the DddA-derived DdCBEs. Our studies highlight the mechanistic diversity among BaDTF proteins and expand the repertoire of dsDNA deaminase enzymes for genome editing and other applications.

Introduction

Bacteria use various strategies to attack their competitors to gain a growth advantage and thrive in natural environments. Interbacterial toxins delivered to contacting cells via the type 6 secretion system (T6SS) are used as weapons in such bacterial warfare and are widespread in Gram-negative bacteria. A variety of T6SS toxins have been identified and characterized, including peptidoglycan hydrolases, lipases, membrane pore-forming proteins, nucleases, (p)ppApp synthetases, metallopeptidases, ADP-ribosylases, and NAD(P)+ glycohydrolases1–3. Recent studies have also identified a large group of T6SS toxins that have DNA deaminase activity4. These bacterial deaminase toxin family (BaDTF) proteins catalyze the deamination of cytosines to uracils in the genomic DNA of recipient cells and thereby cause chromosomal degradation or arrest of DNA replication5. Furthermore, the activity of BaDTFs facilitates the evolution of surviving target bacterial populations and contributes to the development of antibiotic resistance via sub-lethal levels of mutations5.

Based on the phylogenetic relationship, at least three BaDTFs have been identified5, and structure prediction-based analyses of large genomic data suggest that there are many more classes of bacterial deaminase toxins6–8. The best-characterized family (BaDTF1) includes a double-stranded DNA deaminase, DddA, from Burkholderia cenocepacia. DddA selectively deaminates cytosines in the TC sequence motif in double-stranded DNA, and this unique activity has been adapted for CRISPR-free base editing of mitochondrial DNA, genome-wide protein–DNA interaction site mapping, and chromatin accessibility profiling applications4,9–12. The second family (BaDTF2) is represented by a single-stranded DNA deaminase, SsdA, from Pseudomonas syringae. SsdA uses a distinct mechanism of target DNA engagement to deaminate cytosines in ssDNA without strong sequence context dependence, which can be utilized in base editing in human cells13–15. The third family (Pput_2613-like, BaDTF3; Supplementary Fig. 1) has been less well characterized, but it has been adapted for genome-wide high-resolution transcription factor footprinting applications16.

We show in this study that a representative BaDTF3 member, designated as DddB, selectively deaminates cytosines in dsDNA. However, unlike DddA from BaDTF1, DddB does not have a strict DNA sequence context requirement, and it shows distinct preferences on the identity of bases flanking the target cytosine. Crystallographic analyses show that the observed substrate selectivity of DddB is conferred by unique structural elements appended to the basic deaminase fold. We also report the structure of DddB in complex with the cognate Immunity protein (anti-toxin), which reveals how it blocks the active site of DddB via DNA mimicry. Furthermore, we show that the activity of DddB can be harnessed in CRISPR-free base editing and allows efficient editing of mitochondrial DNA, with a different targeting scope from the DddA-derived DdCBEs. These studies highlight the mechanistic diversity of BaDTF proteins and suggest that BaDTF3/DddB can serve as a valuable tool in genome engineering and potential gene therapy applications.

Results

BaDTF3 preferentially deaminates dsDNA with no sequence context requirement

Earlier studies showed that a representative BaDTF3 member from Taylorella equigenitalis (GenBank sequence ID: NZ_LR134346; locus tag: EL142_RS06975) deaminates E. coli genomic DNA when expressed at a sub-lethal level in a strain lacking uracil DNA glycosylase5. The same protein also deaminates the genomic DNA in human cells when expressed as a fusion protein to various transcription factors16. To further investigate target DNA preferences of the BaDTF3 toxin, we expressed a C-terminal fragment spanning residues 1928 to 2060 of the T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 toxin containing the deaminase domain by in vitro translation and examined its biochemical activity (Fig. 1a–c). T. equigenitalis BaDTF3(1928-2060) efficiently deaminated cytosine in the middle of a 14-bp dsDNA substrate (5’-GCAAGTXCGGGTAC-3’) preceded on 5’ by any of the four bases (X = T, C, A, or G), with preference for the 5’-GC and 5’-CC dinucleotide targets (Fig. 1b). In contrast, it showed little activity on the same oligonucleotide substrates in the absence of the bottom strand (i.e., as ssDNA). The protein also deaminated the single cytosine in an 18-nt substrate (5’-AAAAAAAAXCGAAAAAAA-3’, X = T, C, A, or G) as dsDNA but not as ssDNA, with modest preference for 5’-TC and 5’-CC targets (Fig. 1c). Thus, the BaDTF3 toxin selectively targets cytosines in dsDNA with a broad sequence context preference. This contrasts with the specific targeting of 5’-TC motifs in dsDNA by a representative BaDTF1 member, B. cenocepacia DddA4,17. The lack of deaminase activity of T. equigenitalis BaDTF3(1928-2060) on the ssDNA substrates is consistent with an earlier study, in which BaDTF3 members failed to function as ssDNA deaminase in the Cas9-based cytosine base editor (CBE) platform in mammalian cells6. Adopting the recent proposal by He et al., who showed the utility of BaDTF3 in high-resolution genome-wide mapping of transcription factor-binding sites (FOODIE)16, we will hereafter refer to the T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 deaminase as DddB.Fig. 1DddB specifically deaminates cytosines in dsDNA with a unique mode of substrate engagement.a Domain organization of the T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 deaminase toxin. Crystal structure of DddB bound to dsDNA

To understand the molecular basis underlying the highly dsDNA-selective, but relatively sequence non-selective cytosine deamination by DddB, we determined the crystal structure of DddB in complex with a dsDNA substrate (Fig. 1d–f). The best diffracting crystals were obtained with a DddB construct spanning residues 1938–2060 of the full-length T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 toxin and a 14 bp dsDNA (5’-GCAAGTTCGGCTAC-3’ annealed with its complement), similar to that used in the activity studies mentioned above and designed based on the preferred target sequence of DddB in E. coli5. The crystal structure was refined to 2.4 Å-resolution with excellent model quality and fit to experimental data (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Another crystal form of DddB-dsDNA complex was obtained with a further truncated DddB construct spanning residues 1952–2060 and the same 14 bp dsDNA, which yielded essentially the same structure but without the N-terminal region, refined to 2.9 Å-resolution (Table 1). DddB with a deaminase-inactivating E1995A mutation was used to capture the enzyme-substrate DNA complex in both crystals. DddB is bound primarily across the minor groove of the dsDNA, with N-terminal residues preceding the first β-strand of the deaminase domain reaching into the adjacent major groove (Fig. 1d). The core of DddB has the canonical deaminase fold consisting of a single layer of β-sheet flanked by a pair of α-helices on one side, with the catalytically essential zinc ion coordinated by the side chains of two cysteines (Cys2019 and Cys2022) and a histidine (His1993) positioned near the N-terminal ends of the α-helices. In addition, the DddB structure features a unique insertion following the second β-strand at the edge of the core β-sheet and immediately preceding His1993, which includes a pair of short α-helices connected by a short loop comprising Gly1984 and Phe1985 (Supplementary Fig. 3). This compact ‘helix-hairpin-helix’ (HhH)-like element plays a critical role in the dsDNA substrate engagement as described below.

Target cytosine engagement

The dsDNA substrate bound to DddB shows a distorted conformation around the target deoxycytidine nucleotide, which is flipped out of the double helix and engaged in the zinc-bound deaminase active site. Several base-pair steps surrounding the target cytosine (C0) are underwound (Twist angle = 19.3° for T–2/T–1, 25.3° for G+1/G+2), and the DNA helical axis is bent by ~50° away from the HhH motif inserted into the minor groove (Fig. 1e). The DNA strand containing the target cytosine fits in a narrow cleft centered on the active site of DddB, sandwiched between the HhH motif bound in the minor groove and the N-terminal residues containing a characteristic RRPP sequence bound in the major groove (Fig. 1f). The cytosine base is slotted in a pocket lined by the zinc ion and zinc-coordinating His1993 on one side, and the His1952 and Tyr2016 aromatic side chains on the other (Fig. 2a). The O2, N3, and N4 atoms of the cytosine base are hydrogen-bonded, respectively, to the Thr1994 backbone amide nitrogen, Thr1994 side chain hydroxyl group, and the Arg2017 backbone carbonyl and Tyr2039 sidechain hydroxyl groups, within the active site pocket. Although mutated to Ala in our structure, Glu1995 is at the bottom of the pocket and is positioned to activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the C4 position of cytosine (Fig. 2a). Mutation analysis confirmed the importance of the DddB residues surrounding the target cytosine (Fig. 2b). DddB H1952A and Y2016A mutations severely compromised the DNA deaminase activity. For Thr1994, T1994A had only a modest effect, but T1994G almost completely abolished the activity, suggesting that structural integrity is more important than the direct polar contact with the cytosine base at this position. For Tyr2039, which is another residue positioned deep within the active site pocket, Y2039F diminished the activity of DddB, indicating the importance of its side chain hydroxyl group. Modeling suggests that the tetrahedral deamination intermediate of cytosine would be stabilized by direct interaction of the added oxygen atom with the zinc ion and, additionally, a possible polar interaction between the leaving amino group and the hydroxy group of Tyr2039 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Tyr2039 in addition donates a hydrogen bond to the Tyr2016 backbone carbonyl group to stabilize the active site pocket, which could also explain the compromised activity of DddB Y2039F.

Mechanism of dsDNA binding

The distorted conformation of dsDNA is stabilized by extensive contact made by DddB through the minor groove, including insertion of the HhH motif into a significantly widened minor groove (~11 Å compared to 6 Å in B-DNA) (Fig. 3a). Phe1985 at the tip of the HhH motif is inserted deep into the groove and π-stacked on the G-C base pair at the +1 position (G+1-C+1’), displacing the target cytosine (C0) and the complementary guanine (G0’) into extra helical positions (Figs. 3a and 4b). The following residue Pro1986, at the N-terminus of the second short α-helix of the HhH motif, also supports this configuration through van der Waals interaction with the deoxyribose moiety of G0’ and the adjacent adenine base (A–1’) from the non-deaminated strand complementary to T–1. Leu1990 one helical turn away in the same α-helix makes van der Waals interaction with the T–1 base. The positioning of Phe1985 and Pro1986 is supported by hydrogen bonds between the preceding Gly1984 and both of the flanking short α-helices, enabled by a conformation of Gly1984 uniquely allowed for Gly (Backbone dihedral angles: ϕ = +74°, Ψ = +160°) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Thus, the 1984GFP1986 sequence connecting the two short helices in the HhH module is important for dsDNA substrate engagement, consistent with its high conservation among BaDTF3 orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Pro2020 and Arg2021, which are between the Zn-coordinating Cys2019 and Cys2022 and at the N-terminus of one of the longer α-helices comprising the core deaminase fold, also make DNA base contacts through the minor groove (Fig. 3a). DddB also makes many contacts with the sugar-phosphate backbone of both strands of the dsDNA. Ser1973 and Ser1989 side chains and the backbone of Asn1975 and Met1976 interact with the deaminated strand downstream of the target cytosine, whereas the side chains of Lys2023, Arg2027, and Arg2050 interact with the backbone of the non-deaminated strand on the other side of the target C0-G0’ pair (complementary to the upstream region of the deaminated strand) (Fig. 4). While DddB primarily engages the minor groove side of dsDNA, an N-terminal segment 1948RRPP1951 extends over to the compressed DNA major groove near the site of base-flipping. Arg1948 from this stretch is inserted deep into the major groove and forms bidentate hydrogen bonds with G+2, whereas Arg1949, Pro1950, and Pro1951 track along the DNA backbone and interact extensively with Tyr2016 through cation-π stacking and hydrogen bonding, adjacent to the target cytosine (Fig. 3b). These interactions extend the wall of the active site pocket, allowing DddB to wrap around the deaminated DNA strand. A comparison of the DNA deaminase activity for a series of N-terminally truncated DddB constructs showed that DddB(1952–2060) was less active compared to all other constructs with longer N-terminus: DddB(1948-2060), DddB(1938-2060), and DddB(1928–2060) (Fig. 3d). Thus, the N-terminal 1948RRPP1951 motif contributes to optimal dsDNA substrate binding, which explains the superior crystal quality of DddB(1938–2060) in complex with dsDNA compared to DddB(1952–2060) bound to the same DNA (Table 1). Although we did not observe interpretable electron density for residues 1944 to 1946, most likely due to high flexibility, a strong electron density, best attributable to a further upstream residue Phe1943 intercalated in dsDNA, was observed (Supplementary Fig. 2). Phe1943 in this position is sandwiched between the T–1 base and Phe1985, filling the void generated after flipping of the target cytosine (Figs. 1f and 3b).

A fluorescence anisotropy assay showed that DddB(1938–2060) bound to dsDNA substrates harboring either a central TC or GC motif with comparable affinities, with a KD of ~1.0 μM, consistent with the broad sequence context preference (Fig. 3f, g, and Supplementary Table 1). A double mutation, F1985A/P1986A, in the HhH motif compromised binding to both substrates by ~70-fold. DddB(1952–2060), missing the N-terminal 1948RRPP1951 motif, similarly showed more than an order of magnitude weaker binding than DddB(1938–2060). Thus, both the minor and major groove interactions play important roles in the dsDNA-binding of DddB.

Mutation analysis

To further validate the functional relevance of our structural observations, we examined the activities of wild-type and various mutants of DddB using the 14 bp dsDNA substrate with T-1C0 target sequence (Fig. 3c). For a key DNA-intercalating residue Phe1985 from the HhH motif, an alanine substitution (F1985A) diminished, though not abrogated, the DNA deaminase activity. On the other hand, replacing Phe1985 with a bulkier aromatic amino acid Trp (F1985W) did not compromise the activity. An alanine substitution for Pro1986 caused a partial loss of the activity. These results underscore the importance of the HhH motif, particularly an aromatic moiety that stabilizes the flipped-out conformation of the target cytosine. P2020A or R2021A mutation did not notably compromise the activity, suggesting that the minor groove interaction made by these residues is not essential. However, R2021D or R2021E mutation caused an essentially complete loss of the activity, suggesting that, although the hydrogen bond to A–1’ by Arg2021 is not critical, the introduction of a negative charge at this position severely compromises target engagement. Similarly, whereas replacing Leu1990 by Asn (L1990N), Gln (L1990Q), or Cys (L1990C) did not notably diminish the DddB DNA deaminase activity and Thr substitution (L1990T) had only a modest effect, Asp (L1990D) or Glu (L1990E) at this position led to a more severely compromised activity. The L1990D mutation weakened the DNA-binding affinity of DddB(1938-2060) by more than an order of magnitude, which likely underlies the compromised activity (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Table 1). Interestingly, the L1990 variants showed altered sequence preference for –1 position compared to the wild-type, consistent with its proximity to the base at –1 position (Supplementary Fig. 7). A mutation of Phe1943 (F1943A) from the most N-terminal stretch caused a modest loss of the activity, suggesting that the intercalation of Phe1943 in the dsDNA major groove, as seen in the crystal structure, is not essential but contributes to target DNA engagement. In addition, consistent with the observed hydrogen bonds between Arg1948 and G+2, changing the +2 base to A, C, or T led to reduced deamination efficiency, whereas changing G+1 had no apparent effect, demonstrating that DddB has a modest sequence preference for guanine at the +2 position due to the interaction of the N-terminal segment (Fig. 3e).

Immunity protein blocks the DNA-binding surface

Like other BaDTF toxins, the T. equigenitalis DddB gene is flanked by that encoding an immunity protein (DddBI), which inhibits the DNA deaminase activity of the cognate toxin to protect the producer and/or its sister cells. We determined the crystal structure of the DddB-DddBI complex to understand the mechanism of inhibition (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 8). The structure at 1.61 Å-resolution shows that DddBI has a variant of jellyroll fold consisting of two layers of 4-stranded β-sheets embracing a C-terminal α-helix (Fig. 5a). DddBI engages the HhH motif of DddB via its concave negatively charged β-sheet surface and the flanking extended loops, completely blocking the DNA-binding surface of DddB (Fig. 5b, c). A superposition of the DddB-dsDNA and DddB-DddBI complexes highlights similarity in shape between the dsDNA and DddBI, indicating DNA mimicry by DddBI as previously reported for DddA and SsdA4,14,17 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 9). The three families (BaDTF1 ~ 3) share a general strategy of competitively blocking DNA-binding of the deaminase by an acidic surface of the immunity protein. Although the interfaces are not conserved at the residue level, DddAI and DddBI exhibit similar folds and share some (albeit low) sequence homology, suggesting a possible evolutionary relationship (Supplementary Fig. 9). DddB structure between the dsDNA and DddBI-bound states shows the largest deviation for the HhH motif, with the position of Phe1985 shifted by 5.7 Å. The main chain carbonyl group of Phe1985 is hydrogen-bonded to Lys16 of DddBI, whereas hydrophobic residues of DddBI surround the side chain of Phe1985; Pro36 of DddBI stacks on the Phe side chain while Tyr39 of DddBI forms an edge-to-face π-stacking with the aromatic ring of Phe1985 (Fig. 5b). The active site of DddB bound to DddBI does not show a zinc ion, with the Zn-coordinating His1993 being re-oriented and stabilized by a hydrogen bond to Glu61 of DddBI. Thus, DddBI not only physically blocks DNA binding but also stabilizes an inactive conformation of DddB.

Mitochondrial base editing by BdCBE

DddA-derived cytosine base editors (DdCBEs) enable CRISPR-free base editing in mitochondrial DNA but are constrained by the inherent sequence preference for TC motifs4. To overcome this limitation, variants of DddA with a relaxed 5’-TC requirement have been generated through phage-assisted evolution18. DddA orthologs that naturally have distinct context compatibilities have also been identified19–22. We hypothesized that DddB, as a cytosine deaminase that selectively acts on double-stranded DNA without strong sequence specificity, could provide an alternative and complementary strategy to overcome the limitation. To test this, we explored whether the DddB deaminase could be used in a TALE-based CBE to enable C-to-T editing beyond the TC context. We designed both split and monomeric DddB-derived CBEs (BdCBEs) using TALE proteins customized to target five sites in the human mitochondrial genome: COX3.1, ND1, ND1.3, ND6, and ND6.24,23,24.

Based on the structural features of BaDTF3, the deaminase was split, to avoid its cytotoxicity, at multiple loop regions into two inactive halves, and each pair was fused to two TALE proteins to create split BdCBEs. Among the five split sites tested, only the Gly2045 split version, which separates the last β-strand from the rest of DddB and is structurally analogous to the functional Gly1397 site of DddA4, exhibited detectable C-to-T editing activity (Fig. 6; Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Targeted deep sequencing revealed that BdCBEs displayed distinct C-to-T editing patterns compared to DdCBEs across the five mitochondrial targets in HEK293T cells (Fig. 6a, c and Supplementary Fig. 10). The split version of BdCBE demonstrated editing not only at TC contexts but also at broader sequence contexts compared to the conventional DdCBE using identical TALE pairs. Context preference analysis using both maximum and average editing efficiencies in the spacer region further confirmed strong CC context preference of BdCBE at the ND1.3 site. In addition to CC preference, split BdCBE also exhibited editing activity in the GC context at the ND6.2 target site. The highest editing efficiency reached 2.55%, while the average efficiency was 1.30%, representing approximately 1.9-fold increase compared to DdCBE, which showed only 1.35% and 0.70%, respectively (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 12b). Furthermore, BdCBE demonstrated markedly higher editing efficiency than DdCBE at cytosine C3 at ND6.2 and C5 at COX3.1 within the TC context, indicating distinct editing pattern diversity even within TC targets (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 10a). The observed editing activity at ND6.2 cytosine C4 is likely due to the preceding cytosine C3 being converted to thymine (T), resulting in the heteroplasmic formation of a TC motif at the C4 site (Fig. 6c). TCC context-dependent editing pattern was also observed at the COX3.1 and ND6 target sites, particularly at cytosines C13 and C14, and C7 and C8, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10a, c). Whole mitochondrial genome-wide off-target analysis showed that DdCBEs and BdCBEs against ND1.3 and ND6.2 sites have comparable off-target editing activity, but the majority of off-target sites were different (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Next, we also created monomeric BdCBEs (mBdCBEs) using three activity-attenuated mutants (F1985A, P1986A, or L1990D) of DddB. To evaluate the editing performance of these variants, we compared their editing efficiencies, patterns, and context preferences at five target sites using the same left and right TALEs employed in the split BdCBE system. These results were analyzed alongside those of control editors, including DdCBEs and DddA E1347A variant monomeric DdCBEs (mDdCBEs)11. The heatmap results revealed variable editing efficiencies and patterns of mBdCBEs (Fig. 6e, g and Supplementary Fig. 13a, c, e). Interestingly, mBdCBEs maintained editing efficiency comparable to the split version, whereas most mDdCBEs exhibited significantly lower editing activity compared to DdCBEs (Fig. 6f, h, Supplementary Figs. 13b, d, f and 14). Notably, the L1990D variant showed a distinct preference for the GC context, particularly at the ND6.2 and ND6 target sites. Thus, at the ND6.2 target site, the mBdCBE L1990D variant achieved a C-to-T editing efficiency of 21.06%, which is approximately 7-fold and 13-fold higher than those of split BdCBE and DdCBE, which showed efficiencies of 2.87% and 1.58%, respectively. (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Figs. 13f and 14d, e). Taken together, these results suggest that the CC and GC context preferences of BdCBEs could expand editing scope and that mBdCBEs provide additional flexibility in the design of mitochondrial DNA editing enzymes.

Discussion

A comparison of the DddB-dsDNA structure with our previously determined DddA-dsDNA structure17 shows similarities in the protein fold and general mode of dsDNA-binding (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 16). Both enzymes primarily engage the minor groove of dsDNA with a pair of α-helices from the conserved deaminase fold, promoting flipping of the target cytosine into the Zn-bound active site. However, the mechanisms of base flipping employed by the two enzymes are distinct (Fig. 7). DddA uses the ‘tandem displacement’ mechanism, where Phe1375, positioned between the two Zn-coordinating Cys residues, intercalates in dsDNA and displaces the T–1 base, which in turn forms a non-canonical T–1-G0’ base-pair in a shifted register to displace the target C0 base (Fig. 7a). In contrast, DddB uses a unique HhH insertion in the deaminase fold containing the 1984GFP1986 motif to directly displace the target C-G pair (Fig. 7b). The difference in the mechanism of target cytosine flipping explains why DddA has the strict TC target preference, whereas DddB can deaminate cytosines preceded by any base (Fig. 1b, c). The distinct mechanisms are also reflected in different conformations of the bound dsDNA; the dsDNA bound to DddA is sharply bent away from the core of the deaminase fold, whereas the dsDNA bound to DddB is bent away from the HhH motif (Supplementary Fig. 16). It is notable that the HhH motif of DddB, which follows the second β-strand at the edge of the core β-sheet of the conserved deaminase fold, corresponds to structural elements that support unique substrate selectivities of distinct DNA deaminase families (Supplementary Fig. 3a). DddA has a loop at this position that includes Ala1341, which makes key DNA minor groove contact with the noncanonical T-G base pair17. SsdA has a particularly long insertion at this position, which starts with the ‘SGW’ motif and harbors a cluster of aromatic residues important in ssDNA-binding, including His332, Tyr334, and Tyr34214 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). APOBEC3A has a flexible loop3 at this position, involved in ssDNA-binding25. The active site of DddB has a Zn ion coordinated by two Cys and one His, along with Glu (mutated to Ala in our structures) at the bottom of the pocket, which are highly conserved among the diverse BaDTFs and APOBEC-family DNA deaminase enzymes. However, beyond these most fundamental catalytic residues, enzymes from different BaDTF classes show considerable diversity in the residues forming the active site pocket (Supplementary Fig. 4). The DddB active site features a set of aromatic amino acid residues that contribute to positioning the target cytosine: His1952 takes the place of Thr found in most other deaminase families (e.g., Thr1311 in DddA, Thr290 in SsdA, Thr214 in APOBEC3B)14,17,25 and stacks on the cytosine base together with Tyr2016. Tyr2039, deeper in the active site, is important for the deaminase activity, with a potential catalytic role through direct interaction with the amino group of cytosine. A sequence alignment shows perfect conservation of His1952 and Tyr2039 among BaDTF3 orthologs, consistent with their importance (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, another highly conserved residue in the active site pocket, Thr1994, was not found to be essential for the DddB deaminase activity (Fig. 2b). Conversely, the N-terminal stretch 1948RRPP1951 that contributes to the DddB activity (Fig. 3d) is not conserved among the BaDTF3 orthologs, suggesting that its importance is DddB-specific (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We demonstrated that DddB-derived cytosine base editor (BdCBE) can perform targeted mitochondrial DNA editing in human cells, exhibiting similar efficiency but with expanded targeting scopes compared to the DddA-derived base editors (DdCBEs). Efforts to overcome context constraints of DddA in mitochondrial base editing have yielded DddA variants, including DddA11, which offered an elevated activity and a broadened HC (H = A, C or T) sequence compatibility18,26. DddA orthologs that enable the editing of GC targets have also been identified19–22. Thus, BdCBE offers a useful alternative to complement the existing DdCBE platforms, further enriching the mitochondrial DNA base editing toolbox. In particular, the utility of BdCBE in mitochondrial DNA editing was highlighted by efficient editing of the ND1.3 site containing consecutive CC motifs, which was not accessible by the prototypic DdCBE. BdCBE showed up to 27-fold higher CC editing efficiency at this site, compared to DdCBE (Fig. 6b). We also found that some mBdCBEs with DddB variants were as efficient as split BdCBEs, whereas mDdCBEs were less efficient than split DdCBEs (Fig. 6f, h, Supplementary Figs. 13b, d, f and 14) and that some mBdCBEs were able to perform base editing at sites with a GC motif (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Figs. 13f and 14d, e). The observed preference of mBdCBE L1990D for GC context is particularly interesting and could be enabled by hydrogen bonds formed between the substituted Asp side chain and guanine base at the –1 position (Supplementary Fig. 17), although L1990D compromised the binding to both TC and GC-containing dsDNA substrates (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Table 1) and did not enhance the preference to deaminate a GC target biochemically (Supplementary Fig. 7). While further investigation is required to precisely define the context preferences and editing patterns, our results suggest that DddB-derived CBEs are promising tools that broaden the scope of mitochondrial DNA editing.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

DddB constructs with two different lengths, corresponding to residues 1938-2060 or 1952-2060 of T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 toxin (GenBank sequence ID: NZ_LR134346; locus tag: EL142_RS06975) and with a deaminase-inactivating mutation E1995A, were used in crystallographic studies. The proteins were expressed from a codon-optimized synthetic gene cloned in the pET-24a expression vector with a C-terminal His6-tag in the E. coli strain BL21(DE3). Transformed bacteria were grown in 4 liters of LB medium to an OD600 of 0.8, at which point the culture was supplemented with 100 μM ZnCl2 and induced by the addition of Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at a final concentration of 0.5 mM. After 20 h of shaking and incubation at 18 °C, the bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 30 min. The bacterial pellets were resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.5 M NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 5 mM imidazole, lysed by the addition of lysozyme (0.4 mg/ml) and sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 64,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C to separate the supernatant from cell debris. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.2 μm asymmetric polyethersulfone membrane and applied to a 5 mL Ni-NTA Superflow cartridge (QIAGEN). His-tagged DddB protein was eluted with a linear concentration gradient of imidazole and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 column operating with 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.2 M NaCl, 0.5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP). Purified proteins were concentrated by ultrafiltration using Amicon centrifugal filters (3 kDa MWCO). Protein concentrations were determined based on UV absorbance at 280 nm measured on a Nanodrop 8000 spectrophotometer and theoretical extinction coefficients calculated from the amino acid sequences. The immunity protein/anti-toxin DddBI (locus tag: EL142_RS06970) was expressed with a C-terminal His6-tag and purified similarly to DddB.

The deaminase-active DddB of various lengths used in biochemical assays, corresponding to residues 1928-2060, 1938-2060, 1948-2060, or 1952-2060 of T. equigenitalis BaDTF3 toxin, were expressed using NEB Express cell-free E. coli protein synthesis system (New England Biolabs) with Sso7d (W24A/R43E) protein fused to the N-terminus and a His6-tag on the C-terminus. Various amino acid substitutions were tested in the background of the longest construct (1928-2060). The DNA-binding-defective double mutant of Sso7d helped to improve the solubility and stability of the expressed proteins27,28. Linear dsDNA templates used for expression were chemically synthesized as gBlock DNA fragments by Integrated DNA Technologies. The sequences of the gene fragments were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The cell-free expression mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2.5 h and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to stop the reaction. The in vitro expressed Sso7d-DddB-His6 proteins were affinity-purified with Dynabeads His-Tag Isolation and Pulldown kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Captured proteins were eluted in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.5 M NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 200 mM imidazole, confirmed by SDS-PAGE, and their concentrations quantified based on band intensities in SDS-PAGE using Image J29 against the purified inactive DddB protein as a standard. A representative set of in vitro expressed Sso7d-DddB-His6 proteins is shown in Supplementary Fig. 18.

Biochemical DNA deaminase assay

DNA oligonucleotides used in the biochemical deaminase assays to test the –1 base preference (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Fig. 7) were 5’-fluorescein-labeled 14-nt (FAM-5’- GCAAGTXCGGGTAC-3’) or 18-nt (FAM-5’-AAAAAAAAXCGAAAAAAA-3’) with different –1 nucleobases (X = T, C, A, G), with or without an unlabeled complementary strand. The former sequence was designed based on the preferred target sequence of DddB in E. coli5. In experiments to investigate the +1 or +2 base preference (Fig. 3e), the G bases at these positions in the 14-nt substrate were changed to T, C, or A. The substrate in Figs. 2b and 3d was FAM-5’-GCAAGTTCGGGTAC-3’ annealed with the unlabeled complementary strand. The substrate in Fig. 3c was FAM-5’-GCAAGTTCGGCTAC-3’ annealed with the unlabeled complementary strand. The reactions contained 200 nM DNA substrate, DddB at 5–100 nM (stated in the figure legend for each experiment), 40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 50 mM KCl, 1.0 mM MgCl2, and 1.0 mM dithiothreitol. After incubating at 37 °C for 50 min, pfuEndoQ was added to a final concentration of 1.0 μM, and the samples were further incubated at 60 °C for 30 min to specifically cleave deaminated products30,31. The reactions were stopped by the addition of formamide to 67% and heating to 95 °C for 10 min. The products were separated by 15% denaturing PAGE and scanned on a Typhoon FLA 7000 imager (GE Healthcare). For each experiment, a control substrate representing the deaminated product (dU oligo) was treated with pfuEndoQ in parallel to confirm complete cleavage. In addition, the DNA substrate (dC oligo) without deamination was confirmed not to be cleaved by EndoQ.

X-ray crystallography

DddB(1938-2060) E1995A was mixed with a 1.5-fold molar excess of 14 nt dsDNA (5’- GCAAGTTCGGCTAC-3’ annealed with the complementary strand) in 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP at the final protein concentration of 12.4 mg mL−1. The DddB-dsDNA complex was crystallized in the sitting drop vapor diffusion method by mixing the protein-DNA complex with an equal volume of the reservoir solution containing 0.1 M sodium chloride, 25% v/v pentaerythritol propoxylate (5/4 PO/OH), and 10% v/v dimethyl sulfoxide. DddB(1952-2060) E1995A was co-crystallized with the same 14 nt dsDNA in a similar fashion, with the reservoir solution containing 0.15 M magnesium acetate, 18% w/v polyethylene glycol 3350, pH 7.9. The DddBI-bound DddB(1952-2060) E1995A crystals were also obtained similarly, by mixing a SEC-purified protein complex at 22.2 mg mL−1 with a reservoir solution containing 0.1 M citric acid, pH 3.5, 10% w/v polyethylene glycol 6000, and 6% v/v ethylene glycol. The crystals were cryo-protected by brief soaking in the respective reservoir solution supplemented with 20% ethylene glycol and flash-cooled by plunging into liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the AMX beamline of NSLS-II and the BL12-2 beamline of SSRL. All datasets were processed with XDS32 for integration, followed by POINTLESS33, AIMLESS, and TRUNCATE (10.1107/S0567739478001114) from the CCP4 Suite34 for reduction, scaling, and structure factor calculation, respectively. Anisotropic diffraction analysis and truncation were done with STARANISO. The structures were determined by molecular replacement with PHASER35 using the DddB structure predicted by AlphaFold236 as the search model. Iterative model building and refinement were conducted using COOT37 and PHENIX38. Optimal TLS group definitions were determined using the TLS Motion Determination (TLSMD) server39 by submitting the refined atomic coordinates with isotropic B-factors. The grouping schemes recommended by TLSMD, comprising 12 and 11 TLS groups for 9PW0 and 9PW1, respectively, were selected based on minimization of the residual sum of squares and the physical plausibility of the resulting segments. Final refinement cycles were performed with phenix.refine, incorporating simultaneous optimization of TLS parameters, individual isotropic B-factors, and atomic coordinates. The summary of crystallographic data statistics is shown in Table 1. Representative composite omit 2mFo-DFc maps are shown in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 8. Figures were generated using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/).

Fluorescence polarization assay

The dsDNA-binding affinities of DddB(1938-2060) E1995A, F1985A/P1986A/E1995A, and L1990D/E1995A, and the N-terminally truncated DddB(1952-2060) E1995A were determined by a fluorescence polarization/anisotropy assay. Two 5’-fluorescein-labeled 18-nt DNA probes (FAM-5’-AAAAAAAAXCGAAAAAAA-3’) with alternative –1 nucleobases (X = T or G) were annealed with a 1.5-fold molar excess of respective unlabeled complementary strand to ensure dsDNA formation. A two-fold serial dilution series of each protein was mixed with pre-annealed probes to achieve the final dsDNA concentration of 18 nM and the protein concentrations ranging from 97.7 nM to 100 μM in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 0.1 mg ml−1 bovine serum albumin, with a final volume of 100 μL in a black flat-bottom half-area 96-well plate. Fluorescence polarization was measured on a Tecan Spark 10 M microplate reader at 26 °C, with the excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively, and the bandwidth of 20 nm. Changes in fluorescence polarization (mP) from the control sample containing no DddB were plotted against the total DddB concentration in the reaction. Triplicated data were fit to the one-site specific binding model in GraphPad Prism 10 to determine the KD values and 95% confidence intervals, listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Plasmid construction

The plasmids encoding CBEs were constructed to insert custom-designed TALE array sequences encoding specific target sites9,11,23,40. We first created master vectors for the various split versions of BdCBE and monomeric BdCBE (mBdCBE), which were constructed with the same composition as DdCBE except by fusing DddB instead of DddA4. Each split BdCBE consisted of two plasmid components: CMV promoter-SOD2(MTS)-3X HA tag-TALE NTD (N-terminal domain)-TALE repeat modules (Stuffer)-TALE CTD (C-terminal domain)-DddB N-terminal half-UGI (Uracil glycosylase inhibitor), and the other plasmid containing CMV promoter-COX8A(MTS)-3X FLAG tag-TALE NTD-TALE repeat modules (Stuffer)-TALE CTD-DddB C-terminal half-UGI. The architecture of mBdCBE plasmids was as follows: CMV promoter-SOD2(MTS)-3X HA tag-TALE NTD-TALE repeat modules (Stuffer)-TALE CTD-DddB including F1985A, L1990D, or P1986A mutation (the sequences shown in Supplementary Information).

To utilize various BaDTF3 split and monomeric CBEs, human codon-optimized DNA fragments were synthesized (GenScript), and master vectors capable of inserting TALE array sequences were generated using the Gibson assembly method (NEB). TALE array sequences targeting specific sites in the mitochondrial DNA were inserted into the master vectors using six TALE array module vectors and the Golden Gate assembly method40. The resulting plasmid constructs were chemically transformed into E. coli DH5α, and plasmids from selected antibiotic-resistant colonies were subjected to Sanger sequencing to analyze the identity of the constructs.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% antibiotic-anti-mycotic solution (Gibco), at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the lipofection, cells (5 × 104) were seeded on 48-well plates (Corning). After 24 h, the cells were transfected with 500 ng each of left and right split CBE plasmids or 500 ng of monomeric CBE plasmids, using 1.5 µl of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). After 3 days, the transfected cells were harvested and lysed by incubating at 50 °C for 1 h, followed by 80 °C for 20 min, in 100 µL of cell lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 (Welgene) 1 mM EDTA (Welgene), and 0.05% sodium dodecyl sulfate (Welgene) supplemented with 5 µL of Proteinase K (Qiagen).

Targeted deep sequencing

To create NGS libraries for targeted deep-sequencing, target sites were amplified using nested PCR, and adapter and final index sequences were incorporated with PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara). The final PCR products were purified using a PCR purification kit (GeneAll) and sequenced with Illumina MiniSeq paired-end sequencing equipment. Base editing frequencies from the targeted deep sequencing data were measured using source code (https://github.com/ ibs-cge2/prime_editor_analysis, created by BotBot Inc.), as previously described41. The PCR primer sequences are provided in Source data.

Whole mitochondrial genome sequencing

To create NGS libraries for whole mitochondrial genome sequencing, mtDNA was amplified from cell lysate using PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara) with overlapping primers to cover the entire mitochondrial genome. The resulting PCR products were purified using a PCR purification kit (GeneAll). The purified PCR products were subjected to tagmentation using the Illumina DNA prep kit, and final index sequences were incorporated using Nextera DNA CD indexes (Illumina) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The final libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina MiniSeq platform with paired-end reads to generate FASTQ files.

Raw sequencing reads were first processed using the Trimmomatic program (v0.39) to remove Illumina adapters and low-quality bases. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using BWA (v0.7.17-r1198), and then BAM files were generated with BAMtools program (version 1.21). Base calling of mapped reads was conducted using the IGVtools program (version 2.3.98).

For editing site analysis, cytosine-to-thymine (C-to-T) conversion sites with a frequency ≥1% were considered potential off-target edits and included in the analysis, along with identified on-target editing sites (spacer region). To eliminate false positives, single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) found in untreated control samples were excluded from the final editing site list.
