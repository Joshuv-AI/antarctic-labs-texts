Association between Healthy Eating Index 2015 and albuminuria in US adults with hypertension: a cross-sectional analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2007–2020
Authors: Yejia Chen, Hongshan Chen, Minyan Li, Hongbin Liang, Yutian Wang, Xiao Chen, Xinlu Zhang, Yumiao Liu, Qiuxia Zhang, Jiancheng Xiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundAlbuminuria is a critical prognostic indicator for adverse outcomes (eg, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular events) in adults with hypertension. While healthy dietary patterns are linked to improved renal and cardiovascular health, the association between overall healthy eating (assessed by a comprehensive index) and albuminuria in this population remains insufficiently investigated.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the association between the Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI-2015) and albuminuria in US adults with hypertension using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2020 data.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional analysis of data from the NHANES 2007–2020. Participants aged ≥20 years with hypertension defined in accordance with the American Heart Association (AHA) clinical guidelines were included. HEI-2015 score was evaluated from dietary intake data collected by conducting 24-hour dietary recall interviews. Albuminuria was defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥30 mg/g. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analysis were used to evaluate the association between HEI-2015 and albuminuria.ResultsA total of 12 829 US adults with hypertension were included, with 2163 (16.86%) having albuminuria. The weighted mean HEI-2015 score was 53.9, and the weighted mean UACR was 60.6 mg/g. In the fully adjusted logistic regression model (model 3), compared with Q1 (reference), the OR of albuminuria for Q4 was 0.75 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.95, p=0.02). For each 13-point increase in HEI-2015, the OR of albuminuria was 0.91 (95% CI 0.83 to 0.99, p=0.04) in model 3. Restricted cubic spline analysis showed a linear association between HEI-2015 and albuminuria (P for non-linearity=0.239, P overall <0.001). Stratified analyses (by age, sex, race, body mass index, etc) showed no significant interaction (all P for interaction >0.05).ConclusionHigher HEI-2015 scores (indicating healthier dietary patterns) are significantly associated with a lower prevalence of albuminuria in US adults with hypertension. These findings support healthy eating as a potential strategy for preventing albuminuria in hypertensive populations.Keywords: Hypertension, Healthy diet index, Albuminuria, NHANES, Association

Introduction

Hypertension, a pervasive chronic condition, significantly contributes to the global disease burden.1 It stands as a principal risk factor for the development of chronic kidney disease.2 In the USA, a concerning 9% of hypertensive patients present with comorbid stage 3–5 chronic kidney disease.3 Albuminuria, recognised for its predictive value in nephropathy progression and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, is a key indicator of glomerular damage in hypertensive individuals.4

There has been a growing emphasis on the dietary influences on health outcomes, including renal function. It is well established that a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and low-fat dairy is correlated with a diminished risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders.5 6 Specific dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet and the Nordic diet, have demonstrated beneficial effects on blood pressure regulation and renal health.7,9 Prior studies have further established robust links between these pattern-specific dietary strategies and improved blood pressure management in hypertensive patients,10 11 and some have extended this work to explore associations with albuminuria: for instance, adherence to the DASH diet has been linked to lower Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) risk (a condition closely tied to albuminuria),7 while the Nordic diet has shown promise for preserving renal function.9

Previous investigations have illuminated the association between specific dietary components and albuminuria. For instance, heightened sodium intake has been correlated with increased albuminuria,12 13 while elevated potassium intake, abundant in fruits and vegetables, has been inversely related to albuminuria.14 15 Furthermore, a diet replete with antioxidants like vitamins E and C exerts a protective effect against albuminuria formation.16,19 However, although specific dietary patterns and certain dietary components have been proven to be potentially associated with albuminuria in patients with hypertension, there is still a lack of research on the impact of overall healthy dietary patterns on albuminuria in these populations in real-world settings. Given that overall dietary patterns are more in line with the eating habits of the general population, it is imperative to clarify the relationship between overall healthy dietary patterns and albuminuria in these patients.

The Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI-2015) is widely used to assess and quantify overall healthy dietary patterns. The HEI-2015 was developed by the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) to reflect adherence to the US Dietary Guidelines, making it highly tailored to the dietary structure and public health priorities of US adults.20 The HEI-2015 includes 13 food groups and nutrients, encompassing both ‘adequacy components’ (eg, total vegetables, whole fruits, whole grains, seafood/plant proteins) that reflect recommended nutrient intake and ‘moderation components’ (eg, sodium, refined grains, saturated fats, added sugars) that address dietary factors linked to chronic disease risk.20 This design captures the cumulative impact of multiple dietary factors that may be biologically relevant to albuminuria in hypertension and its association with the outcome. In this study, utilising data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES),21 we assessed the association between healthy dietary patterns and albuminuria among hypertensive US adults.

Methods

Study Population

NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design to select the representative, non-institutionalised population in the USA. All participants provided written informed consent. The survey protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.21

There were a total of 38 433 (≥20 years) participants in NHANES 2007–2020. We excluded individuals who were non-hypertension (n=18 052), missing dietary data (n=3097), extreme values of energy intake (<500 kcal/day and >8000 kcal/day for men; <500 kcal/day and >5000 kcal/day for women, n=1093) and missing covariates data (n=3362) (online supplemental figure S1). Finally, 12 829 participants were included in the current study (figure 1).

Flowchart showing the number of NHANES participants included in the current analyses. BMI, body mass index; NHANES, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

HEI-2015

The HEI-2015 is a dietary quality index designed by the USDA researchers to assess the extent to which Americans are adhering to the Dietary Guidelines.22 The index consists of 13 food groups or nutrients, including nine adequate components (eg, total vegetables, vegetables and legumes, total fruits, whole fruits, whole grains, dairy products, total protein foods, seafood and plant proteins, and fatty acids) and four moderate components (ie, sodium, refined grains, saturated fats and added sugars) (online supplemental table S1).20 HEI-2015 ranges from 0 to 100, with a higher score implying a better quality diet.

In the NHANES survey, information on dietary intake from Americans was collected by conducting 24-hour dietary recall interviews. This approach was used to estimate the types and amounts of food consumed by participants in the 24 hours before the interviews.23

Hypertension

The NHANES survey used a standardised blood pressure measurement protocol recommended by the American Heart Association (AHA). Adults with hypertension were defined in accordance with the AHA clinical guidelines: (1) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mm Hg, (2) diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mm Hg or (3) self-reported use of antihypertensive medications. Blood pressure measurements were obtained by trained clinicians using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer, following NHANES’ standardised protocol (up to three measurements per participant, with 1-min rest between readings); the final SBP/DBP values were calculated as the average of all valid measurements. Participants meeting any of the three criteria above were classified as having hypertension and included in the analysis.24

Albuminuria

The participants’ urinary albumin and creatinine were evaluated. The urine albumin was quantified using a solid-phase fluorescent immunoassay, using random spot samples. For the urine creatinine test, the Jaffe reaction was employed. Albuminuria was defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥30 mg/g.

Covariates

Family questionnaires collected information on age, race, sex, education level, smoking status, presence of comorbid diabetes and coronary heart disease, stroke and use of diabetes and antihypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, haemoglobin A1c ≥6.5% or current glucose-lowering therapy. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m2). The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation (2021).25 Before conducting the main analyses, we evaluated the missing value status of all covariates. As shown in online supplemental figure S1, the highest missing rate was observed for family poverty status (10.39%), followed by alcohol consumption (10.26%) and BMI (1.41%), while other key covariates (eg, sedentary time, sex, diabetes, smoking status, education level) had missing rates ≤0.75%.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc) or R (V.4.3.0), and two-tailed p≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were described as mean±SE, and categorical variables were expressed as proportions (%). Variance and χ² tests were used to analyse differences between groups. OR and 95% CI for HEI-2015 and albuminuria were calculated using logistic regression models. To systematically account for potential confounders, three models were constructed: model 1 was adjusted for age, sex and race/ethnicity; model 2 was adjusted for model 1 plus education levels, family poverty, marital status, smoking status and alcohol consumption; and model 3 was further adjusted for model 2 plus diabetes, coronary heart disease, BMI and sedentary time. The median of each quartile was used as a continuous variable to test for linear trends. In addition, the continuous exposure variable (HEI-2015) was modelled using restricted cubic spline regression to test for a possible linear relationship between HEI-2015 and albuminuria. The relationship between HEI-2015 and albuminuria was further analysed in age (<60 and ≥60 years), race/ethnicity, sex, BMI, education levels, smoking status and alcohol consumption.

Patient and public Involvement

None.

Results

A total of 12 829 participants were included in the analysis, including 2163 individuals with albuminuria. The weighted UACR was 60.6 mg/g, and the weighted HEI-2015 score was 53.9. Participants were grouped into quartiles by HEI-2015 score (Q1 −44; Q2 44–53; Q3 53–63; and Q4 >63.2). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the association between the HEI-2015 and albuminuria in adults with hypertension. This cross-sectional study showed that a higher healthy eating index was associated with a lower prevalence of albuminuria in US adults with hypertension. Dose-response analysis showed a linear relationship between HEI-2015 and albuminuria. Our study provides new evidence for the relationship between healthy dietary patterns and the prevalence of albuminuria in US adults with hypertension.

Our results align with prior research elucidating the ties between dietary patterns and albuminuria. For instance, adherence to a diet abundant in vegetables and fruits has been shown to diminish albuminuria in hypertensive individuals.5 26 Additionally, augmented dietary potassium intake and restricted sodium intake have been correlated with a decreased risk of albuminuria.15 A recent cross-sectional study in Taiwan corroborated these findings, indicating that a vegan dietary pattern was associated with a lower prevalence of albuminuria. Conversely, a study by Lin et al identified a positive association between excessive dietary intake of animal protein, animal fat and cholesterol with increased albuminuria.27 28 Nonetheless, some studies, such as one by Hsin-Fang Chung et al, reported no association between high-fat meat and fish consumption, as well as traditional Chinese dietary patterns and albuminuria.29 This divergence may stem from the fact that individual foods or nutrients cannot wholly account for the dietary impact on albuminuria. The HEI-2015, in contrast, facilitates a more holistic appraisal of an individual’s dietary habits, rather than concentrating on specific nutrients or foods alone.

The following may be the potential mechanisms underlying the association of the Healthy Eating Index with the incidence of albuminuria in people with hypertension. Poor blood pressure control in patients with hypertension is an important cause of albuminuria.30 Healthy dietary patterns such as the DASH diet can help to lower blood pressure and reduce arterial wall pressure, which in turn reduces glomerular pressure and reduces the risk of protein leakage.31 32 A healthy diet is rich in antioxidants, fibre and many vitamins and minerals that have anti-inflammatory properties. These nutrients reduce the inflammatory response in the kidneys and lower the risk of albuminuria.33 Additionally, some studies have shown that a healthy diet improves lipid metabolism, lowers blood cholesterol and triglyceride levels and reduces atherosclerosis.34,36 Improved vascular function helps to reduce glomerular damage, thereby reducing the risk of albuminuria.37 38 Furthermore, the consumption of dietary fibre-rich foods aids in weight management and blood pressure reduction, alleviating renal strain and the incidence of albuminuria.39

Our study sheds novel light on the correlation between HEI-2015 and albuminuria, a significant marker in nephrology. Future large-scale cohort studies are essential to substantiate this correlation, which could significantly enhance our comprehension of how healthy dietary patterns impact kidney disease. Such insights would lay the groundwork for more precise preventive and therapeutic strategies, specifically aimed at improving renal health in individuals with hypertension.

Regarding the limitations of our study, several points should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES precludes any definitive conclusions about causality. Second, the HEI-2015, which is based on 24-hour dietary recall data, may not accurately capture an individual’s habitual dietary patterns over an extended period. Finally, the single measurement of urinary albumin and creatinine in the NHANES could introduce bias into the diagnosis of chronic albuminuria.

Conclusion

In summary, a healthier dietary pattern, as estimated by a higher HEI-2015, is significantly associated with a lower incidence of albuminuria in adults with hypertension. The results of this study emphasise the importance of healthy eating in the management of hypertensive patients and may have far-reaching implications for clinical practice and public health strategies.
