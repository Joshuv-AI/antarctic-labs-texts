Fibers of phase tropicalizations
Andrei Bengu s , -Lasnier and Mikhail Shkolnikov

Abstract
. The subject of the present paper is phase tropicalization, which was used crucially in the context of Mikhalkin’s correspondence theorem for curve counting in the complex coefficient case. The subject can be traced back to Viro’s patchworking for constructing topological types of real algebraic curves. These two instances correspond to complex and real phases. Both fall into the category of what can be called "abelian" or classical tropicalization, referring to degenerations of varieties within an algebraic torus (or its compactification). In contrast, in "non-abelian" tropicalizations the ambient torus is replaced by a non-commutative group such as the special linear group. This is the beginning of a general theory valid for a wide array of coefficient systems and dimensions. As an application, the paper settles the question of phase tropicalization for the special linear group SL 2 \mathrm{SL}_{2} . It also gives an algebraic explanation and phase extension of the case of curves, previously studied in the purely geometric framework. To accomplish these tasks we introduce valuative tools that allow us to prove an affine version of Kapranov’s theorem on tropical hypersurfaces and its generalization to arbitrary tropical varieties. Most notably, we show the functorial properties of the graded ring of a valuation and exhibit the polynomial structure of the graded ring of monomial valuations.

Introduction

Kapranov’s tropical geometric theorem is a foundational result relating tropicalizations
of toric varieties to their defining ideals (see for instance
[
MS15
, 3.1.3 and 3.2.3]
).
In fact the theorem is stronger and gives the defining ideal of phase tropicalizations.
In this paper we extend these results to affine varieties in the following way. Consider an
algebraic variety
X
defined over the field of (reversed) Hahn series
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
, where
\nu

is its associated valuation. Elements in this field can be written as
ct^{\alpha}+o(t^{\alpha})
,
where
c\in\mathbb{C},\>\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and the term
o(t^{\alpha})
designates lower order terms. Any vector

A\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
can be written as
t^{\alpha}B+o(t^{\alpha}),\>B\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
by
taking the entries of highest order. Define
\mathrm{In}_{\nu}
as the map that associates to this vector
its leading part
(\alpha,B)
. Our main result states that these leading terms form explicit
algebraic sets.

For any
f=\sum_{u}c_{u}x^{u}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, set
c_{u}=\lambda_{u}t^{\alpha_{u}}+o(t^{\alpha_{u}})
and
write
\nu_{\alpha}(f):=\max\{\nu(c_{u})+\alpha|u|\>:\>c_{u}\neq 0\}
, where

|(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})|=u_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}
. Define then

\mathrm{IN}_{\alpha}(f):=\sum_{\nu(c_{u})+\alpha|u|=\nu_{\alpha}(f)}\lambda_{u}X^{u}\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}].

For any ideal
I
in
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, let
\mathrm{IN}_{\alpha}(I)
be the ideal in

\mathbb{C}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
generated by the
\mathrm{IN}_{\alpha}(f),\,f\in I
. Recall that the disjoint union
of a family of sets
(S_{j})_{j\in J}
is constructed as
\bigsqcup_{j\in J}S_{j}:=\bigcup_{j\in J}\{j\}\times S_{j}
. The following result is the main contribution of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider an algebraic variety
X\subset\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, given by an ideal

I\subset\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

(1)

The set of initial parts can be described as a fibration of algebraic sets

\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(X)=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{V}(\mathrm{IN}_{\alpha}(I))\setminus\{0\}.

(2)

There exists a set of critical values
\beta_{0}<\ldots<\beta_{r}
such that, for

i=0,\ldots,r+1
the function
\alpha\in(\beta_{i-1},\beta_{i})\mapsto\mathrm{IN}_{\alpha}(I)
is
constant and gives a homogeneous ideal. The ideals
\mathrm{IN}_{\beta_{i}}(I)
are not homogeneous
(we set
\beta_{-1}=-\infty
and
\beta_{r+1}=+\infty
).

Our main application of this result concerns
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
(or
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}
)
tropicalizations as they were introduced in
[
MS22
]
. Simplifying, they associate
to a family of varieties
X=(X_{t})_{t>0}
with
X_{t}\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
a limit degeneration
that we denote
\mathrm{VAL}(X)\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Consider the polar decomposition of

\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{H}^{3}\times\mathrm{PSU}(2)
. The set
\mathbb{H}^{3}
of positive
definite, Hermitian and unimodular matrices is a (Hermitian) model of the hyperbolic space.
We write
O
for its center, which is the identity matrix. The natural metric gives rise to a
distance function that can be calculated explicitly:
\mathrm{dist}(O,B)=|\ln(\lambda(B))|
,
where
\lambda(B)
is one of the eigenvalues of
B
. Focusing on the Hermitian parts
of
\mathrm{VAL}(X)
, we obtain the hyperbolic tropicalization of
X
. The authors of
[
MS22
]

obtain that the hyperbolic tropicalizations of families of complex algebraic curves are unions of
concentric spheres around
O
and segments extending to geodesics passing through
O
. In

[
PS25a
]
, it is shown that the hyperbolic tropicalization of a family of surfaces
(X_{t})_{t>0}

in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a complement to an open ball in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
centered at the image of
the identity matrix denoted by
O
.

In this work,
1.1
completes
[

, Thm. 3, 4]

\mathrm{SL}_{2}
version of Kapranov’s theorem at the algebraic level for complex surfaces, and
reprove and refine the analogous theorem for curves. Write
\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}(X)
for the

tropicalization of

. When restricting our varieties to convergent Hahn series,

as a family of varieties

To summarize, this work settles the question of how phase tropical limits of surfaces in

, which, in essence, is a real phase toric tropicalization. The second instance

. The

2.
Fibers of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}

phase tropicalization

Before diving into the principal algebraic narrative of this article, we would like to detail
our main geometric application. We start by defining the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}

degenerations. Consider a family of matrices
(A_{t})_{t>0},\>A_{t}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We see it
as a function
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, and we assume it has a first asymptotic

t^{\alpha}B+o(t^{\alpha}),\>B\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
for large
t
. For
h>0
fix the operator

R_{h}:\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
that acts in the following way: for

A\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
consider its polar decomposition
A=PU
where
P=P^{*}
and

UU^{*}=U^{*}U=1
; then
R_{h}(A)=P^{h}U
. Our geometric degeneration of
(A_{t})_{t}
is the limit

\lim_{t\to+\infty}R_{\log(t)^{-1}}(A_{t})\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}).

We write this limit as
\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}(A)
. For a family of varieties
(X_{t})_{t>0},\>X_{t}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
one defines its
\mathrm{SL}_{2}

tropicalization
as

\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}(X)=\{\lim_{t\to\infty}R_{\log(t)^{-1}}(A_{t}):\>A_{t}\in X_{t},\>t>0\}
.

In
Appendix

B
we prove the following formula.

(1)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}R_{\log(t)^{-1}}(A_{t})=[\![e^{\alpha}B+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}]\!],

where
[\![C]\!]=(\det(C))^{-1/2}C\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is the unique normalization assuming

\det(C)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. A similar formula for
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
appears first in

[
PS24
]
, with the proof sketched in
[
PS25b
]
. This expression is hard to work with directly,
nevertheless one first remarks that it only depends on the first asymptotics of the entries in

(A_{t})_{t}
. It is thus well-defined, regardless of whether the entries of
(A_{t})_{t}
are convergent
Hahn series or general formal power series. Thus, instead of considering families of points

(A_{t})_{t}
or families of varieties
(X_{t})_{t}
, where the entries of the matrices or coefficients
of the defining polynomials vary with a parameter
t
, one can consider points and varieties with
entries and coefficients in a valued field
\mathbb{K}
. The typical prototype for
\mathbb{K}
is the field of
Hahn series, converging or not. Thus from now on, we assume
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
and

X\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
is a subvariety. We can still define
\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}(X)

via the right-hand side expression of (
1
).

We give a geometric picture of this process.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the total space of a circle bundle over the quadric surface

Q_{2}(\mathbb{C})=\{[B]_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}:\>\det(B)=0\}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{3}
, with the fiber over a point

[B]_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
being
\{[cB]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}:c\in U(1)\}
. In
Appendix

B
we show
there is a diffeomorphism
(0,+\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(2)

given by
(\alpha,[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})\mapsto[\![e^{\alpha}B+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]
.
This allows us to see
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
as a disjoint union

\big(\{0\}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)\big)\cup\big((0,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\big)
.
Our situation is thus dramatically simplified, since we can now focus on

(\alpha,[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})
instead of
\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}
.

We abbreviate
\{B\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\{0\}:\>\det(B)=0\}
with
\{\det=0\}
.
The
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
tropicalization is dominated via a surjection

\pi_{\mathbb{R}}:\Big(\{0\}\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\Big)\cup\Big((0,\infty)\times\{\det=0\}\Big)\to\Big(\{0\}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)\Big)\cup\Big((0,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\Big),

that is defined as follows. Consider the polar decomposition of the special
linear group:
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{H}^{3}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
.
For any matrix
B\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
consider its polar decomposition
B=PU

where
P\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
U\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
. Set

\pi_{\mathbb{R}}(0,B)=(0,U)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{\mathbb{R}}(\alpha,B)=(\alpha,[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})\quad\text{for }\alpha>0.

We can thus shift our focus to this new set

\Big(\{0\}\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\Big)\cup\Big((0,\infty)\times\{\det=0\}\Big)\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}^{4}.

This can be seen as the first instance of the new type of phase tropicalization of

\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})

being a complex-coefficient real-powered series.
We will henceforth call it
valuative tropicalization

It was mentioned in the introduction, that

Remark 2.1
.

We enumerate several facts about the fibers
X_{P}:=\varkappa^{-1}(P),\>P\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
for
a general surface
X
.

(1)

First of all, the fibers
X_{P}
are real semi-algebraic sets, thus we may speak about
the dimension of their irreducible components. One striking fact is that zero-dimensional
fibers never appear.

(2)

For a generic point
P
the fiber
X_{P}
is a generalized Hopf link,
i.e.,
a union of
pairwise linked circles in
\varkappa^{-1}(P)\cong\mathbb{S}^{3}
. More specifically,
X_{P}

is a preimage of a finite set under a Hopf fibration defined by taking the quotient by
the stabilizer of
P
under the action by
\mathrm{SU}(2)
,
i.e.,

\mathbb{S}^{3}=\mathrm{SU}(2)\to\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{Stab}(P)\cong\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
this finite subset does not locally depend on the level
\mathrm{dist}(O,P)
while it is non-critical.

(3)

There is a finite number of
critical levels
, the first of which is
r>0
,
the radius of the open ball

B_{r}(O)=\mathbb{H}^{3}\setminus\varkappa(\widetilde{\mathrm{VAL}}(X))
.
For each critical level
\beta>0
, a generic point
P\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
at distance
\beta

from
O
has a two-dimensional fiber
X_{P}
. More precisely,
X_{P}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}

may be seen as the radial projection of a complex algebraic curve in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with Newton
polygon having vertices
(n,0),(m,0),(0,n),(0,m)
, for some natural
n
and
m

depending only on the critical level.

(4)

For some phase tropicalizations
X
there might be also three-dimensional fibers
X_{P}
.
This can happen, for instance, if the first critical level
r
is
0
,
i.e.,
the phase-forgetful tropicalization is the whole
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Then
X_{O}
is a spherical
coamoeba of some complex surface in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, which is often, but not always,
three-dimensional. Otherwise, the locus of
P
with
X_{P}
three-dimensional (and actually being the whole three-sphere) is a
finite union of segments extending to geodesics passing through
O
, which corresponds to the case when the above curve
C_{i}
has an irreducible component of bi-degree
(0,1).

Similarly, tropicalization of curves in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
admit a floor diagram

3.
Preliminaries

In this section we present the basics of valuation theory necessary to define
a phase space and prove a generalized version of Kapranov’s theorem.
We adopt the convention that the set
\mathbb{N}
contains

0
:
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
. We use the
(\max,+)
convention for valuations,

i.e.,
we adopt the following notations. Let
R
be a commutative, unitary ring and

\Gamma
a totally ordered abelian group. We write
+
for the addition law
of this group and we add an element not in
\Gamma
, that we denote
-\infty
.
We write
\Gamma_{\infty}=\Gamma\cup\{-\infty\}
and extend the addition operation
and order relation so that
-\infty
plays the role of the smallest element.

Definition 3.1
.

A
valuation

\nu
on
R
is a map
\nu:R\longrightarrow\Gamma_{\infty}
satisfying

(V1)

\forall a,b\in R,\>\nu(a\cdot b)=\nu(a)+\nu(b)
.

(V2)

\forall a,b\in R,\>\nu(a+b)\leqslant\max\{\nu(a),\nu(b)\}
. This
is called the ultrametric inequality.

(V3)

\nu(1_{R})=0
and
\nu(0)=-\infty
.

(V4)

\nu^{-1}(-\infty)=(0)
.

We often write
(R,\nu)
for a
valuative pair
,
i.e.,
a ring equipped with a valuation.
The mapping
\nu:R\setminus\{0\}\to\Gamma
may not be surjective.
We write
\Gamma_{\nu}
the abelian group generated by
\nu(R\setminus\{0\})
, called
the
group of values
. The valuation
\nu
is called
trivial
if

\Gamma_{\nu}=\{0\}
. We only consider nontrivial valuations in this paper.

Remark 3.2
.

(1)

When we talk of embedding a pair
(R,\nu)
into

\nu

(2)

(V4) implies that

(3)

For any integer

. Indeed

\nu(n\cdot 1_{R})=\nu(\underbrace{1_{R}+\ldots+1_{R}}_{n\text{ times}})\leqslant\nu(1_{R})=0.

Furthermore, we have
\nu(-1_{R})=\nu(1_{R})=0
as

(4)

By (V2) one can prove the following: for any

For the special case when

The

\displaystyle\kappa_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\nu}/\mathfrak{m}_{\nu}.

We will write

By definition, any homogeneous element

4.
Composing initial forms

A key ingredient in proving our Kapranov theorem is functoriality. One could see this
result as a means to substantiate a change of variables on the level of the graded
algebra, mirroring a change of variables on the level of the polynomial algebra.

4.1.
Functoriality

We show how the construction of the graded algebra is functorial.
We extend
[
BL21
, Thm. 1.8, p. 9]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider two valuative pairs
(R_{1},\nu_{1}),\>(R_{2},\nu_{2})
. We assume
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}

take values inside a common ordered subgroup
\Gamma
. Let
\Phi:R_{1}\to R_{2}

be a ring morphism such that
\nu_{1}\geqslant\nu_{2}\circ\Phi
,

i.e.,

\forall a\in R_{1},\>\nu_{1}(a)\geqslant\nu_{2}(\Phi(a))
.

(1)

There is a canonical homogeneous map of graded rings
(
i.e.,
sending homogeneous elements to either
0
or homogeneous of same degree)

\mathrm{gr}(\Phi):\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{1}}(R_{1})\longrightarrow\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{2}}(R_{2}),

sending
\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(a),\>a\in R_{1}
to
\Phi(a)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\gamma}(R_{2},\nu_{2})
, where
\gamma=\nu_{1}(a)
.

(2)

The kernel of
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
is the homogeneous ideal

\mathrm{Ker}(\mathrm{gr}(\Phi))=\Big\langle\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(I)\Big\rangle,\text{ where }I=\{a\in R_{1}\,:\>\nu_{1}(a)>\nu_{2}(\Phi(a))\}.

(3)

The above construction
\mathrm{gr}(-)
is functorial in the following sense.
Let
(R_{3},\nu_{3})
be another valued pair and let
\Psi:R_{2}\to R_{3}
be another
ring morphism such that
\nu_{2}\geqslant\nu_{3}\circ\Psi
. Then one has

\mathrm{gr}(\Psi\circ\Phi)=\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)\circ\mathrm{gr}(\Phi).

Proof.

By hypothesis we have for every
\gamma\in\Gamma

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}(R_{1},\nu_{1}))

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}(R_{2},\nu_{2})

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\gamma}(R_{1},\nu_{1}))

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\gamma}(R_{2},\nu_{2}).

This induces a mapping of abelian groups

\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}(R_{1},\nu_{1})}{\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\gamma}(R_{1},\nu_{1})}\longrightarrow\frac{\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}(R_{2},\nu_{2})}{\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\gamma}(R_{2},\nu_{2})},\quad\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(a)\longmapsto\Phi(a)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(a)}(R_{2},\nu_{2}).

These maps put together generate a global mapping of abelian groups

\mathrm{gr}(\Phi):\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{1}}(R_{1})\to\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{2}}(R_{2})
.

Since
\Phi
is a ring morphism, it sends units to units, so
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
clearly sends the

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{1}}(R_{1})
to the unit of
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{2}}(R_{2})
. By (V1) and the fact that

\Phi
is multiplicative,
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
is also multiplicative. It is enough to show it for two
homogeneous elements
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(a),\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(b),\,a,b\in R_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(a)\cdot\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(b))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(ab))

\displaystyle=\Phi(ab)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(ab)}(R_{2},\nu_{2})

\displaystyle=\Phi(a)\Phi(b)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(a)+\nu_{1}(b)}(R_{2},\nu_{2})

\displaystyle=\Big(\Phi(a)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(a)}(R_{2},\nu_{2})\Big)\cdot\Big(\Phi(b)\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(b)}(R_{2},\nu_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(a))\cdot\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(b)).

The kernel of
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
is the abelian subgroup of
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{1}}(R_{1})

generated by the homogeneous elements
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(a)
that are sent to
0

in
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{2}}R_{2}
. In other words, these homogeneous elements are the

\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(a)
such that
\Phi(a)\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\nu_{1}(a)}(R_{2},\nu_{2})
.
This last condition is equivalent to

.

Lastly, functoriality is a consequence of the fact that the morphism

In order to give a better picture of phase tropicalizations, we will need to define a set

. We frequently use multi-index notation:

and the elements

These valuations on

can have an excessively complicated structure or

We introduce some additional notations. Write

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in U_{0}}c_{u}x^{u}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

Proposition 4.2

Let

Third step:
we show that
\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(fg)=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}}[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})
.
Observe that

\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}})<\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}})\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(g-[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})<\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(g)=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([g]_{\underline{\gamma}}).

By the first step we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(fg-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}}[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f(g-[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})+[g]_{\underline{\gamma}}(f-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\left\{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f(g-[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})),\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([g]_{\underline{\gamma}}(f-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}}))\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\left\{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)+\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(g-[g]_{\underline{\gamma}}),\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([g]_{\underline{\gamma}})+\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}})\right\}

\displaystyle<\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}})+\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([g]_{\underline{\gamma}})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}}[g]_{\underline{\gamma}}).

By using, in succession, steps three, two and one, we obtain

\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(fg)=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}}[g]_{\underline{\gamma}})=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}})+\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([g]_{\underline{\gamma}})=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)+\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(g),

which concludes our proof.
∎

We grade the
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
with respect to
\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}
:
any element of
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
can be uniquely decomposed as sum of terms

\theta X^{u},\>u\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
\theta\in\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
homogeneous. We associate to it the degree

\deg(\theta)+u\cdot\underline{\gamma}
. It should come as no surprise that

\deg(\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(c)X^{u})=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(cx^{u})
.

Observe finally that
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
is a natural

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
-algebra. Indeed, by functoriality
4.1
, there is a clear
injective ring map
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})\to\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
.
Thus we define the algebra structure as follows: for any
c\in\mathbb{K},f\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(c)\cdot\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(cf)
.

Monomial valuations have graded algebras that can be described explicitly as is
demonstrated by the next proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

For a monomial valuation
\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}
, the graded ring

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
is isomorphic
(as graded rings) to
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
(with
X_{i}
of weight
\gamma_{i}
).
Namely

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])=\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x_{n})],

where the
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x_{i})
are algebraically independent over

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
. More precisely, the initial form of
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)

can be written as the polynomial

\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{n}))
.

Proof.

We fix
f=\sum_{u\in U}c_{u}x^{u}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. By definition

\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f-[f]_{\underline{\gamma}})<\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}})
. Thus

(2)

\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}}).

Furthermore, each term
c_{u}x^{u}
of
[f]_{\underline{\gamma}}
have equal valuation to that of

[f]_{\underline{\gamma}}
. By
3.5
(5) we have

(3)

\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}([f]_{\underline{\gamma}})=\sum_{u\in U_{0}}\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(c_{u}x^{u})=\sum_{u\in U_{0}}\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(c_{u})\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x)^{u}=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x)).

By combining (
2
) and (
3
) we conclude that indeed

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
is a polynomial algebra over

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
. We show that the elements
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x_{n})
are algebraically independent over
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
.
Let
F\in\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
be non-zero. We can decompose it into homogeneous
components
F=F_{1}+\ldots+F_{N},\>F_{i}\neq 0
, according to the grading of

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
. For each
i=1,\ldots,N
it is clear that

F_{i}(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x))
is homogeneous of degree
\deg(F_{i})
.
Without loss of generality, we can thus suppose that
F
is homogeneous and non-zero
and we show that
F(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x))\neq 0
. Indeed, since
F
is
homogeneous, we can write
F=\sum_{u\in U}\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(c_{u})X^{u}
. Since
F
is homogeneous,
we clearly have
F=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)
, where
f=\sum_{u\in U}c_{u}x^{u}
. Thus, by a
computation that is similar to that in the first part of the proof

F(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x))=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(x))=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)

and
\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)\neq 0
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

We deduce that
\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}
is multiplicative: for any
f,g\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(fg)=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(g).

Proof.

We apply
4.3
and use the fact that
\mathrm{in}_{\nu}
is multiplicative:

\displaystyle\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(fg)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(fg)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(f)\cdot\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{n}))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(g)(\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{n}))

and we conclude as the elements
\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(x_{n})
are algebraically
independent.
∎

4.3.
Changing variables for initial forms

Throughout this subsection,
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
is a fixed valued field.

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider a morphism of polynomial algebras
\Psi:\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\to\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]

given by
\Psi(x_{i})=g_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Equip
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
with a
monomial valuation
\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}
and
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
with a monomial
valuation
\nu_{\underline{\delta}}
. We suppose that
\forall i=1,\ldots,n,\,\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(g_{i})\leqslant\gamma_{i}
.
If we identify
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{1}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
and
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{2}}(\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}])

with their respective polynomial algebras
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
and

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}]
. Then
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)
is determined by the images of

X_{i}=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{1}}(x_{i})
.

(1)

If
\nu_{2}(g_{i})<\gamma_{i}
, then
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)(X_{i})=0
.

(2)

If
\nu_{2}(g_{i})=\gamma_{i}
, then
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)(X_{i})=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{2}}(g_{i})
.

Proof.

We show that
\Psi
verifies the condition of
4.1
. Take
f\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

and write
f=\sum_{u\in U}c_{u}x^{u}
. By the ultrametric inequality we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(\Psi(f))

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\delta}}\left(\sum_{u\in U}c_{u}g_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots g_{n}^{u_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{u\in U}\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(c_{u}g_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots g_{n}^{u_{n}})

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in U}\nu(c_{u})+u_{1}\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(g_{1})+\cdots+u_{n}\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(g_{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{u\in U}\nu(c_{u})+u_{1}\gamma_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in U}\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(c_{u}x^{u})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f).

Whether
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)(X_{i})=0
or
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)(X_{i})=\mathrm{in}_{\nu_{2}}(g_{i})
comes from the point (2) of

4.1
and the construction of
\mathrm{gr}(\Psi)
.
∎

We are now ready to explain how to change variables for initial forms.

Lemma 4.6
.

Consider an isomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\to\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
. Fix

\underline{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})
and

\underline{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n})
such that
\forall i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(\Phi(y_{i}))

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(y_{i})

\displaystyle\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(\Phi^{-1}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(x_{i}).

Then
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi):\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\delta}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])\to\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}])
is an isomorphism.

Upon identifying
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\delta}}}(\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}])
and

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
with their respective
polynomial algebras
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]
and
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
,
one can see
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
as an isomorphism between them. Thus

\forall f\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}],\,\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(\Phi(f))=\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)
and

\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)(\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\gamma}}(f))=\mathrm{IN}_{\underline{\delta}}(\Phi(f)).

Situations as the ones described above arise naturally in the following way: consider

\gamma\in\Gamma
and consider
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
, independent linear forms
on
\mathbb{K}^{n}
with
\varphi_{i}=\sum_{j}a_{i,j}x_{j}
and
a_{i,j}=0
or
\nu(a_{i,j})=0
.
Then the isomorphism defined by
\Phi(x_{i})=\varphi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
verifies the
conditions of
4.6
.

Proof.

Both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
satisfy the conditions of
4.5
, thus both
maps
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
and
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi^{-1})
can be defined. By
4.1
(3), we have

\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)\circ\mathrm{gr}(\Phi^{-1})=\mathrm{gr}(\Phi\circ\Phi^{-1})=\mathrm{gr}(\mathrm{id})=\mathrm{id}

and likewise
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi^{-1})\circ\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)=\mathrm{id}
. Thus
\mathrm{gr}(\Phi)
is an
isomorphism. By
4.1
(2), we see that there are no

f\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
\nu_{\underline{\delta}}(\Phi(f))<\nu_{\underline{\gamma}}(f)
. The last statements are a consequence of

4.3
.
∎

5.
Lifting phase tropicalizations

5.1.
Phases

For any natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set a norm on
\mathbb{K}^{n}
as follows

z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n},\>V_{\nu}(z)=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\nu(z_{i}).

Just as we defined the graded algebra
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
for the valued field, we can define the
graded abelian groups. For any
\alpha\in\Gamma_{\nu}
set

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\mathbb{K}^{n}:\,V_{\nu}(x)\leqslant\alpha\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\mathbb{K}^{n}:\,V_{\nu}(x)<\alpha\}.

and set

\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n}):=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma_{\nu}}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}{\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}\quad,\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{Gr}}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n}):=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Gamma_{\nu}}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}{\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}.

There is a clear injection
\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\hookrightarrow\widetilde{\mathrm{Gr}}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
.
If the context is clear, we abbreviate the
\alpha
homogeneous component
as
G_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})/\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
. Just like
g_{\alpha}
,
the abelian group
G_{\alpha}
is a
\kappa_{\nu}
vector space.
We have an initial form mapping

\mathrm{In}_{\nu}:\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n}),\>z\mapsto z\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{K}^{n}),\text{ where }\alpha=V_{\nu}(x).

Definition 5.1
.

We call
\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
the
phase space
for
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
.
We call its elements
forms
. A form is thus a couple
(\alpha,\theta)

where
\theta\in\kappa_{\nu}^{n}
. The associated degree
\alpha
is the
value

of the form and
\theta
is its
phase
.

Let
X=\mathbb{V}(I)\subset\mathbb{K}^{n}
be an affine algebraic set.
We define the
phase tropicalization of

X
as

\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(X):=\{\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z):\,z\in X\}\subset\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n}).

This assignment is clearly functorial: any (set-theoretic) map
u:X\to Y
gives
rise to a map
\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(u):\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(X)\to\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(Y)
, the phase forms of degree
\alpha
.

We also set
\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(X)_{\alpha}:=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(X)\cap G_{\alpha}

Example 5.2
.

We have
\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\})=\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\setminus(\Gamma_{\nu}\times\{0\})
.

Similarly to the graded algebra construction,
\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}
is a functorial construction.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
and
(\mathbb{L},\mu)
are two valued fields with values in a
common valued group
\Gamma
. They induce the norms
V_{\nu}
and
V_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{K}^{n}
and

\mathbb{L}
respectively. Let
\phi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{L}^{m}
be a mapping (any kind),
such that
V_{\nu}\circ\phi\leqslant V_{\mu}
. Then there exists a map
\mathrm{Gr}(\phi):\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\to\mathrm{Gr}_{\mu}(\mathbb{L}^{m})
, sending
\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z)
to

z\>\mathrm{mod}\>\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{L}^{m})
where
\alpha=V_{\nu}(z)
.
The association
\phi\mapsto\mathrm{Gr}(\phi)
is functorial.

The proof is identical to that of
4.1
. One needs to replace the groups

I_{\alpha},I^{+}_{\alpha}
with the corresponding
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha},\mathcal{P}^{+}_{\alpha}
.
The following result mirrors the analog result for valued rings,
i.e.,

4.6
.
Its proof stems from the definitions.

Lemma 5.4
.

Consider a bijection
\phi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
, such that
V_{\nu}\circ\phi=V_{\nu}
. Then both

\mathrm{Gr}(\phi)
and
\mathrm{Gr}(\phi^{-1})
are defined and are inverse to each other. Hence

\forall z\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\},\>\mathrm{Gr}(\phi)(\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z))=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(\phi(z)).

Example 5.5
.

Consider the bijective linear mapping
\phi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
defined by

\phi(z)=(\phi_{1}(z),\ldots,\phi_{n}(z))
, where

\phi_{i}(z)=z_{i}+\sum_{j>i}a_{i,j}z_{j},\quad a_{i,j}\in\mathcal{O}_{\nu}.

We show that
\phi
satisfies
V_{\nu}\circ\phi=V_{\nu}
. It is clearly invertible.
For any index
i
and all
z\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

\nu(\phi_{i}(z))\leqslant\max\Big(\{\nu(z_{i})\}\cup\{\nu(a_{i,j}z_{j}:j>i\}\Big)\leqslant\max\{\nu(z_{j}):j\geqslant i\}\leqslant V_{\nu}(z).

Thus
V_{\nu}\circ\phi\leqslant V_{\nu}
.
For the converse inequality, fix
z\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and let
i
be the highest index
for which
V_{\nu}(z)=\nu(z_{i})
. Thus, for all
j>i,\,\nu(a_{i,j}z_{j})\leqslant\nu(z_{j})<\nu(z_{i})
. By
3.2
(4) we have
\nu(\phi_{i}(z))=V_{\nu}(z)
.

Remark 5.6
.

Observe how
\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
is constructed as a disjoint union, whereas
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})

is constructed as a direct sum. In
[
Duc
]
, the author defines a notion of

annélloïde
(which could be roughly translated to
ringoid
), which is
a disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{m\in M}A_{m}
, where
M
is an abelian monoid, the
A_{m}
are
abelian groups and there is a well-defined notion of homogeneous multiplication,
i.e.,
compatible morphisms
A_{m}\times A_{m^{\prime}}\to A_{m+m^{\prime}}
. In such structures, one can multiply
homogeneous elements and add homogeneous elements of same degree.

We choose to keep
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
as a graded algebra for the results concerning
substitutions of initial forms (
cf.

4.6
).
We also wish to keep
\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
as a disjoint union as to give a more geometric
nature to this object, this is illustrated in
5.10
.

We end this paragraph by relating the graded module over
\mathbb{K}^{n}
to the graded algebra

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
.

Proposition 5.7
.

For any
\alpha\in\Gamma
there is a canonical isomorphism of abelian groups

\Theta_{\alpha}:G_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\simeq}g_{\alpha}^{n}.

They combine in canonical isomorphisms of
\kappa_{\nu}
vector spaces

\widetilde{\mathrm{Gr}}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\simeq\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Gamma}(g_{\alpha})^{n}\simeq(\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}))^{n}.

We also have a homogeneous bijection between ringoids

\mathrm{Gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\simeq\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}g_{\alpha}^{n}
.

Proof.

Consider
Z=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z)\in G_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
and

V_{\nu}(z)=\max_{i}\nu(z_{i})=\alpha
. Define

\Theta_{\alpha}(Z)=\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\quad\text{where}\quad\theta_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(z_{i})&\text{if }\nu(z_{i})=\alpha\\
0&\text{if }\nu(z_{i})<\alpha.\end{array}\right.

The map
\Theta_{\alpha}
is a group morphism. From the above construction, it is clear that
for any
a\in\mathcal{O}_{\nu}^{\times}
we have
\Theta(\overline{a}\cdot Z)=\overline{a}\cdot\Theta_{\alpha}(Z)
. Let
Z,Z^{\prime}\in G_{\alpha}
. Suppose
Z=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z)
and

Z^{\prime}=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z^{\prime})
. Define
w\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
as follows

w_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z_{i}+z_{i}^{\prime}&\text{if }\nu(z_{i})=\nu(z_{i}^{\prime})=\nu(z_{i}+z_{i}^{\prime})=\alpha\\
z_{i}&\text{if }\nu(z_{i}^{\prime})<\nu(z_{i})=\alpha\\
z_{i}^{\prime}&\text{if }\nu(z_{i})<\nu(z_{i}^{\prime})=\alpha\\
0&\text{if }\nu(z_{i}+z_{i}^{\prime})<\alpha.\end{array}\right.

Observe that
y+y^{\prime}-w\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{+}(\mathbb{K}^{n})
, so that
Z+Z^{\prime}=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(w)
.
By the properties and case work in
3.5
, we have that

\Theta_{\alpha}(\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(w))=\Theta_{\alpha}(Z)+\Theta_{\alpha}(Z^{\prime})
.

The morphism
\Theta_{\alpha}
is surjective. Consider
\theta\in g_{\alpha}^{n}
. Without loss
of generality, suppose
\theta_{i}=\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(z_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,m
and
\theta_{i}=0
for

i=m+1,\ldots,n
. Set the vector
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
. We have

\theta=\Theta_{\alpha}(Z)
for
Z=\mathrm{In}_{\nu}(z)
.

The morphism
\Theta_{\alpha}
is injective. Indeed, by construction, if
Z\neq 0
, then

\Theta_{\alpha}(Z)\neq 0
.
∎

We henceforth identify the phase space with
\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}g_{\alpha}^{n}
. Thus
a phase will be an element of type
(\alpha,\theta)
where
\alpha\in\Gamma
and

\theta\in g_{\alpha}^{n}
.

5.2.
Splittings

Abstract graded algebras can have a very complicated structure. We present our objects
in the particular case of valuations admitting a splitting or cross-section.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
be a valued field with value group
\Gamma_{\nu}
. A

multiplicative splitting
or
cross-section
is a family of elements

(t^{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\nu}}
such that the mapping
\Gamma_{\nu}\to\mathbb{K}^{\times},\>\gamma\mapsto t^{\gamma}
is a group morphism and
\nu(t^{\gamma})=\gamma
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\nu}
.

Cross-sections are hard to find. For fields
\mathbb{K}
which are
\aleph_{1}
-saturated (in the
model-theoretic sense), one can construct a splitting (
cf.

[
ADH17
, 3.3.39, 3.3.40]
and

[
PC21
, Thm. A.10, Variant A.11]
for more ample details).

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\nu}^{\times}
, the equation
X^{n}=x
has a solution
in
\mathbb{K}
(
e.g.,

\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, or perfect and henselian).
Then
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
admits a section.

Proof.

The abelian group
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}^{\times}
is divisible, thus it is an injective
\mathbb{Z}
-module.
Consequently the short exact sequence

0\to\mathcal{O}_{\nu}^{\times}\to\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\Gamma_{\nu}\to 0

is split. This yields a section in a direct manner.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
(\mathbb{K},\nu)
be a valued field with splitting
(t^{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\nu}}
.

(1)

The graded algebra
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K})
has a presentation as a group algebra.
More precisely, the mapping

\varphi\,:\>\kappa_{\nu}\left[t^{\Gamma_{\nu}}\right]\to\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(\mathbb{K}),\quad\overline{a}\cdot[\gamma]\mapsto\mathrm{in}_{\nu}(at^{\gamma})

is an isomorphism.

(2)

One has a natural bijection

6.

In this section we restrict to rank one valuations. We can thus assume that

For any vector

Appendix B
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
valuation and
the diffeomorphism

Let
\tilde{R}_{h}\colon\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
be a diffeomorphism defined in terms
of the polar decomposition by
PU\mapsto P^{h}U
. Consider an
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-valued series
A_{t}

converging for large real
t
. We want to compute the limit of
\tilde{R}_{(\log(t))^{-1}}(A_{t})

as
t\to+\infty
.

First, we will find the unitary part of the limit. For this, we need to know the
expression of the spherical coamoeba map,
i.e.,
of
U
for a matrix

C=PU\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Note that
P^{2}=CC^{*}
and
\varkappa^{\circ}{C}=U=P^{-1}C
,
thus we are left with expressing the inverse of the square root of
P^{2}
.
This can be done by the Cayley-Hamilton formula:

P^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(P)P+1=0.

Thus,
P^{-1}=(P^{-2}+1)/\mathrm{tr}(P)=[\![(C^{*})^{-1}C^{-1}+1]\!]
.
Therefore,
\varkappa^{\circ}(C)=[\![C+(C^{*})^{-1}]\!]
.

To compute the limit of the unitary part of
A_{t}
, we need to rethink the operation
of taking the inverse of a unimodular matrix – namely,
C^{-1}=\mathrm{tr}(C)-C

for
C
with
\det(C)=1
, again by Cayley-Hamilton formula. The expression

C^{adj}=\mathrm{tr}(C)-C
extends to all two-by-two matrices as a linear map known as

adjugate
, satisfying the core property
CC^{adj}=C^{adj}C=\det(C)C
.

Thus, the limit of the unitary part of
A_{t}
expanded asymptotically as

t^{\alpha}B+o(t^{\alpha})
, with
B
being a complex matrix, is easy to compute:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\varkappa^{\circ}(A_{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}[\![t^{\alpha}(B+(B^{*})^{adj})+o(t^{\alpha})]\!]=[\![B+(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]\in\mathrm{SU}(2).

Note that in the asymptotic expansion
A_{t}=t^{\alpha}B+o(t^{\alpha})
the exponent

\alpha
cannot be negative since
1=det(A_{t})=t^{2\alpha}det(B)+o(t^{2\alpha})
.
Moreover, if
\alpha=0
then
\det(B)=1
and
\det(B)=0
otherwise.
In the case
\alpha=0
, we see that
A_{t}
itself converges to
B
,
and thus the limit of
R_{(\log(t)))^{-1}}(A_{t})
is the same as of

R_{(\log(t)))^{-1}}(B)
, which is again
[\![B+(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]
.

Therefore, we are left with the case
\alpha>0
, when
A_{t}
itself does not converge
in the vector space of two-by-two matrices. To compute the limit of the

(\log(t))^{-1}
power of the Hermitian part
P(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
of
A_{t}
,
what we do is polar decomposing
\mathbb{H}^{3}
itself with respect to the matrix

1
being the center of the decomposition. Namely, first we compute the limit of the
distance from
(P(t))^{(log(t))^{-1}}
to the identity matrix. Note that such a distance
is computed as the logarithm of the top eigenvalue of
A_{t}A^{*}(t)
divided by
2\log(t)
.
Since
A_{t}A^{*}(t)
is unimodular and diverges, this eigenvalue is asymptotically equal
to
\mathrm{tr}(A_{t}A^{*}(t))=t^{2\alpha}\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})+o(t^{\alpha})
. Thus,
the distance from the Hermitian part of
R_{(\log(t))^{-1}}(A_{t})
to
I_{2}
converges
to
\alpha>0
.

This was the radial component of the limit in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. To find the angular component
in
S^{2}=\partial\mathbb{H}^{3}
consisting of rank one hermitian matrices considered
up to a scalar multiple, we simply compute the limit of
A_{t}A^{*}(t)
normalized
by its trace (which is necessarily non-vanishing). This gives the point

BB^{*}/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})\in S^{2}
as the limit of the angular part.

Now we have to combine this angular part
BB^{*}/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})
with the radial part

\alpha>0
to get a point in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. What we need to find is such a Hermitian
unimodular matrix which has the same eigenvectors as
BB^{*}
, and the eigenvalue of
the vector spanning the image of
BB^{*}
should be
e^{\alpha}
(this is due to the distance

\alpha
to the identity matrix). The desired expression is

P=e^{-\alpha}I_{2}+(e^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha})BB^{*}/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})
.

Combining it with the unitary part, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}(R_{(\log(t))^{-1}})(A_{t})

\displaystyle=(e^{-\alpha}I_{2}+(e^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha})BB^{*}/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*}))[\![B+(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]

\displaystyle=[\![e^{-\alpha}B+(e^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha})BB^{*}B/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})

\displaystyle+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}+(e^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha})BB^{*}(B^{*})^{adj}/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})]\!].

This expression can be simplified since
B
is a rank-one matrix,
i.e.,
the last term is
vanishing due to
B^{*}(B^{*})^{adj}=0
, and
BB^{*}B/\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})=B
because if we write

B=vw
where
v
is a column vector and
w
is a row vector,
\mathrm{tr}(BB^{*})=tr(vww^{*}v^{*})=\mathrm{tr}(v^{*}vww^{*})=\mathrm{tr}(|v|^{2}|w|^{2})=|v|^{2}|w|^{2}
and

BB^{*}B=vww^{*}v^{*}vw=|v|^{2}|w|^{2}vw
. Therefore, the final formula for

A_{t}=t^{\alpha}B+o(t^{\alpha})
is

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(R_{(\log(t))^{-1}})(A_{t})=[\![e^{\alpha}B+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),

which is also compatible with the case
\alpha=0
that we considered separately.

The final remark here is that the expression
[\![e^{\alpha}B+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}]\!]

for
\alpha>0
(and so
\det(B)=0
) contains exactly the same information as the pair

(\alpha,[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})
, where
[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
denotes the class of matrices
equal to
B
up to a positive real multiple. More formally, there is a diffeomorphism

\Psi:(0,+\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(2)
given by

(\alpha,[B]_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}})\mapsto[\![e^{\alpha}B+e^{-\alpha}(B^{*})^{adj}]\!].

It is possible to write an explicit (and quite cumbersome) formula for its inverse.
However, from the geometric perspective, the invertibility of the above map is not hard
to see. Namely, a matrix
C\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\backslash\mathrm{SU}(2)
, is distinct from its
inverse-conjugate
(C^{*})^{-1}
. Take a 2-plane
H
in the space of matrices passing through

0,\,C
and
(C^{*})^{-1}
. The projectivisation of this plane intersects the quadric surface

\det=0
in two conjugate points
[B]_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
and
[(B^{*})^{adj}]_{\mathbb{C}^{*}}
.
Thus, we have an alternative basis in the plane
H

i.e.,
the two matrices
B_{0}
and

(B_{0}^{*})^{adj}
, and our initial point

can be written uniquely as

C=aB_{0}+b(B_{0}^{*})^{adj}
for a pair of non-zero complex numbers
a
and
b
.
Note that since
\det(C)=1
the arguments of
a
and
b
are opposite.
Now we simply solve for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{*},\,c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, where
B=cB_{0}

and

aB_{0}+b(B_{0}^{*})^{adj}=r(e^{\alpha}cB+e^{-\alpha}\overline{c}(B^{*})^{adj}),

which gives the inverse of

B

and
(B^{*})^{adj}
if
\alpha
