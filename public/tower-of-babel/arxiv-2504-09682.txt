Conservative projection-based data-driven model order reduction of a fluid-kinetic spectral solver
Opal Issan, Oleksandr Koshkarov, Federico D. Halpern, Gian Luca Delzanno, Boris Kramer

Abstract
Kinetic simulations are computationally intensive due to six-dimensional phase space discretization. Many kinetic spectral solvers use the asymmetrically weighted Hermite expansion due to its conservation and fluid-kinetic coupling properties, i.e., the lower-order Hermite moments capture and describe the macroscopic fluid dynamics and higher-order Hermite moments describe the microscopic kinetic dynamics. We leverage this structure by developing a parametric data-driven reduced-order model based on the proper orthogonal decomposition, which projects the higher-order kinetic moments while retaining the fluid moments intact. We demonstrate analytically and numerically that the method ensures local and global mass, momentum, and energy conservation. The numerical results show that the proposed method effectively replicates the high-dimensional spectral simulations at a fraction of the computational cost and memory, as validated on the weak Landau damping and two-stream instability benchmark problems.



1
Introduction

A kinetic description is essential to accurately capture the behavior of many collisionless plasma phenomena, including magnetic reconnection, sheaths, shocks, and wave-particle interactions. The kinetic equations evolve the particle distribution function in a six-dimensional 3D3V phase space. Solving these equations via an Eulerian discretization on a grid approach is computationally demanding. The memory costs scale as
\mathcal{O}(N^{6})
, where the dimension
N
represents the number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) in each spatial or velocity dimension (although, in practice, the number of DOFs might differ in each dimension). Such unfavorable scaling is commonly known as the
curse of dimensionality
. Moreover, the often enormous scale separation between microscopic and macroscopic processes renders kinetic simulations computationally intractable at large scales, especially for many-query parametric studies, including uncertainty quantification, sensitivity analysis, inverse problems, and design optimization.

A promising avenue to reduce these computational costs is to develop kinetic reduced-order models (ROMs), most commonly done via modal decomposition techniques. Within this class of methods, there are two main reduction approaches for the kinetic equations: proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and dynamic low rank (DLR).
The latter approximates the high-dimensional particle distribution function by a low-dimensional modal decomposition that results in storage requirements that scale as
\mathcal{O}(N_{r}N^{3})
and computational efforts that scale as
\mathcal{O}(N_{r}^{2}N^{3})
, where
N_{r}
is the rank of the approximation
Cassini and Einkemmer [2022]
,
Koch and Lubich [2007]
. DLR evolves not only the reduced basis coefficients but also the reduced basis over time, such that this method does not rely on an offline phase, in contrast to POD.
Here, the offline phase refers to expensive pre-computations (e.g., basis generation), while the online phase leverages this pre-computed information for fast and efficient evaluations.
A comprehensive review of DLR for the kinetic equations is provided in
Einkemmer et al. [2024]
, including recent advancements that enabled 3D3V simulations on a single workstation
Cassini and Einkemmer [2022]
.
Alternatively, POD relies on
a priori
training simulation data to construct a global parametric basis using the singular value decomposition, i.e., the offline phase
Lumley [1967]
,
Berkooz et al. [1993]
. The system dynamics are then projected onto the POD basis and evolved in the reduced linear subspace.
POD was independently rediscovered several times by
Pearson [1901]
,
Hotelling [1933]
,
Loève [1955]
,
Karhunen [1946]
, and is also referred to as the Karhunen-Loève expansion or Principal Component Analysis.
This method evolves the global basis coefficients, trading higher offline computational costs for a more efficient reduced model during the online phase. Therefore, POD is very useful for parametric studies, where similar problems are solved sequentially.
The POD (without hyper-reduction) online storage requirements scale as
\mathcal{O}(N_{r})
, while the computational efforts scale as
\mathcal{O}(N_{r}^{2}N^{3})
due to the Vlasov nonlinearity, where
N_{r}
represents the rank of the reduced POD basis. The memory footprint is significantly lower for POD in comparison to DLR. Additionally, although the POD and DLR computational efforts scale similarly,
Koellermeier et al. [2024]
show that for the shallow water equations, POD provides a speed-up factor of 50, while DLR achieves a speed-up factor of around 8 at the same error level. This is anticipated because POD relies on a fixed basis, whereas DLR requires solving additional equations to update the reduced basis.
In several works, coherent structures and limit cycle identification in plasma simulations have been explored by analyzing the POD basis
Sasaki et al. [2019]
,
Kaptanoglu et al. [2021]
,
Gahr et al. [2024]
. POD has also been applied to particle-in-cell kinetic simulations in
Nicolini et al. [2019]
and its symplectic formulation in
Tyranowski and Kraus [2022]
. POD is also employed in the design of preconditioners to enhance the convergence rate of iterative solvers in high-dimensional simulations
Peng [2024]
. Additionally,
Tsai et al. [2023]
use POD to reduce a conservative Eulerian finite difference discretization of the 1D1V collisionless Vlasov-Poisson equations. However, the resulting ROM fails to preserve mass, momentum, and energy conservation laws, which are essential for accurately predicting macroscopic dynamics. This paper addresses this challenge.

We propose a novel strategy for constructing data-driven conservative parametric kinetic ROMs. The method is based on the asymmetrically weighted Hermite spectral expansion in velocity
Grant and Feix [1967]
,
Schumer and Holloway [1998]
,
Camporeale et al. [2016]
. The so-called
fluid-kinetic
coupling in this formulation arises from the lower-order Hermite expansion coefficients representing macroscopic fluid dynamics, while the higher-order coefficients account for microscopic kinetic effects. Leveraging this fluid-kinetic coupling property, we only build a ROM for the higher-order moment equations, as most memory and computational resources are spent on resolving the kinetic moments. As a result, the fluid moments remain intact, ensuring local and global conservation of mass, momentum, and energy.
The concept of selectively reducing only the higher-order moments of the dynamics was introduced in
Peng and McClarren [2021]
using DLR for the radiation transport equations and extended to the kinetic equations in
Coughlin et al. [2024]
using DLR.
Furthermore,
Koellermeier et al. [2024]
applied POD and DLR reduction to higher-order moments in the shallow water equations, demonstrating both local and global mass conservation.
We build on this idea by employing POD for the higher-order moments of the kinetic plasma equations, an approach that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been explored before.
We test the ROM on 1D1V Vlasov-Poisson benchmark problems, including weak Landau damping and two-stream instability, demonstrating a significant reduction in the number of DOFs.
We anticipate the ROM will be particularly useful in reducing 3D3V simulations due to POD’s favorable memory and computational scaling.

2.3
Artificial Collisions

We add an artificial collisional operator to solve the problem of
filamentation

These highly localized and rapidly varying small-scale structures in velocity space can surpass the resolution capabilities of any method, leading to potential numerical instabilities and recurrence phenomena (artificial temporal periodicity)

We employ the collisional operator introduced in
Camporeale et al. [2016]
, which is a normalized higher-order Lenard-Bernstein collisional operator
Lenard and Bernstein [1958]

4

\mathcal{C}\left(C_{s\mathchar 59\relax n}(x\mathchar 59\relax t)\right)\coloneqq-\nu\eta_{n}C_{s\mathchar 59\relax n}(x\mathchar 59\relax t)\qquad\mathrm{with}\qquad\eta_{n}\coloneqq\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{(N_{v}-1)(N_{v}-2)(N_{v}-3)}\mathchar 59\relax

(6)

where the tunable parameter
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the artificial collisional rate of the last Hermite coefficient
C_{s\mathchar 59\relax N_{v}-1}(x\mathchar 59\relax t)
. The artificial collisional operator does not act directly on the first three moments (

) of the Hermite expansion to maintain the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy

,
Camporeale et al. [2016]

is chosen as the minimal value that effectively damps unresolved fine-scale velocity structures that lead to recurrence or numerical instabilities while preserving the essential collisionless dynamics.

3
Conservative Model Order Reduction of Kinetic Moments via Proper Orthogonal Decomposition and Galerkin Projection

The proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) is a technique to compute the optimal basis for representing an experimental or simulated dataset by minimizing the mean squared error between the snapshot matrix (where each column is the simulation results at different timesteps and parameter realizations) and its reduced rank
N_{r}
representation
Berkooz et al. [1993]
,
Lumley [1967]
; see section
3.1
for further details.
POD requires performing a series of simulations
a priori
, known as the
training dataset
, by varying some of the tunable parameters.
Next, we apply Galerkin projection to the semi-discrete kinetic equations (
8
), projecting them onto the POD basis.
We take advantage of the fluid-kinetic coupling property of the AW Hermite spectral expansion and only project the kinetic moments, thereby preserving mass, momentum, and energy conservation and retaining the fluid moments (macroscopic quantities) intact; see section
3.2
.
In section
3.3
, we highlight that the spectral operators have an affine (linear) dependence on the model’s tunable parameters, enabling efficient computation of the reduced operators for varying solver parameters (i.e.,
\nu\mathchar 59\relax\alpha_{s}\mathchar 59\relax u_{s}
) and physical parameters (i.e.,
q_{s}\mathchar 59\relax m_{s}
).
Lastly, we prove analytically in section
3.4
that the conservation properties are not impacted by projecting the kinetic moments.

\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{t}N_{\mu}}
defined in Eq. (
11
), and
N_{r}
is the number of POD basis vectors used to approximate the problem of size
N_{K}
.
Therefore, the POD basis offers a reduced set of basis vectors that minimizes the mean squared error between the simulation dataset and its rank
N_{r}
approximation.
In principle, the POD basis
\{\mathbf{p}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{p}_{N_{r}}\}
is computed by an eigenvalue decomposition of the matrix
\mathbf{\bar{\Psi}}\mathbf{\bar{\Psi}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{K}}
, or more efficiently when
N_{K}\gg N_{t}N_{\mu}
via the
method of snapshots

Sirovich [1987]
which uses
\mathbf{\bar{\Psi}}^{\top}\mathbf{\bar{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{\mu}\times N_{t}N_{\mu}}
, or also directly via the singular value decomposition of the snapshot matrix
\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, such that
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{K}}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}N_{\mu}\times N_{t}N_{\mu}}
are unitary matrices and
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{t}N_{\mu}}
is a diagonal matrix with the singular values
\sigma_{i}
arranged in decreasing order on its diagonal. The POD basis is the first
N_{r}
columns of the left singular vectors matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{K}}
, which we denote by
\hat{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{r}}
. The number of reduced dimensions
N_{r}
is typically chosen based on where the singular values plateau or reach a certain threshold.

3.2
Galerkin Projection for ROM Construction of the Kinetic Part of the Spectral Expansion

We assume there is a low-dimensional representation of the kinetic state vector
\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}}
, such that

\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\approx\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{r}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\qquad N_{r}\ll N_{K}\mathchar 59\relax

where
\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times N_{r}}
are the reduced kinetic state and POD basis of species
s
, respectively.
The Galerkin projection of Eq. (
8
) yields the following reduced equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}}_{N_{r}\times N_{K}N_{x}}\underbrace{\left[\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)\right]}_{N_{K}N_{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)

(12)

\displaystyle+\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\left[\mathbf{\Xi}_{F}^{\top}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)\odot\mathbf{E}(t)\right].

Currently, evolving Eq. (
12
) computationally scales with the high-dimensional kinetic discretization
N_{K}
instead of the low-dimensional
N_{r}
due to the nonlinear acceleration term. This is often referred to as the
nonlinear bottleneck
in model reduction, where the linear low-dimensional projection of nonlinear high-dimensional equations still scales computationally with the dimensions of the original high-dimensional equations. We overcome the nonlinear bottleneck via a simple Kronecker product identity:

\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)=\left[\hat{\mathbf{P}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{x}}\right]\left[\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)\right].

By employing the identity above and the Kronecker identity to simplify the nonlinearity in Eq. (
12
), we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)+\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}}_{N_{r}\times N_{r}N_{x}}\underbrace{\left[\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)\right]}_{N_{r}N_{x}}+\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\left[\mathbf{\Xi}_{F}^{\top}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)\odot\mathbf{E}(t)\right].

Therefore, evolving the reduced kinetic equation scales with the reduced dimension
N_{r}\ll N_{K}
,
where the reduced advection matrix is
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{r}}
, the reduced acceleration matrix is

\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}[\hat{\mathbf{P}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{x}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{r}N_{x}}
,
and the fluid-kinetic coupling reduced matrices are
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{F}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times N_{x}}
. To summarize, the semi-discrete
reduced-order model
(ROM) equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)+\mathbf{B}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}\left[\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)\right]+\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\left[\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}(t)\otimes\mathbf{E}(t)\right]+\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)+\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\left[\mathbf{\Xi}_{F}^{\top}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}(t)\odot\mathbf{E}(t)\right]\mathchar 59\relax

(14)

coupled with the semi-discrete Poisson equation (
9
) and with
\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}\coloneqq\mathbf{G}_{s\mathchar 59\relax F}\hat{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{F}\times N_{r}}
. While the overall structure of the equations remains unchanged, the FOM (
8
) consists of high-dimensional sparse operations evolving
N_{K}
DOFs, whereas the ROM (
14
) consists of low-dimensional dense operations evolving
N_{r}\ll N_{K}
DOFs.

3.3
Affine Parametric Dependence

The kinetic FOM Eq. (
8
) depends on a set of tunable model parameters: the Hermite parameters
u_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and the artificial collisional parameter
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The same holds for mass
m_{s}
and charge
q_{s}
. The kinetic FOM operators in Eq. (
8
), denoted by
\mathbf{A}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{B}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{G}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}
and
\mathbf{J}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}

\mathbf{A}(\mu)=\mu\mathbf{A}
, where
\mu
is the parameter and
\mathbf{A}
is the operator; see section
3.2
for the kinetic FOM operators’ definition.
This structure allows us to efficiently construct the parametric reduced operators
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathchar 59\relax\hat{\mathbf{B}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathchar 59\relax\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}
and
\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{s\mathchar 59\relax K}

a priori
computation.

3.4
Conservation Properties: Mass, Momentum, and Energy

The fluid FOM Eq. (
7
) is identical to the fluid ROM Eq. (
13
) besides the coupling term to the kinetic state. However, as shown below, the difference in the fluid-kinetic coupling term does not influence the ROM’s conservation of mass, momentum, and energy.

3.4.1
Mass Conservation

The mass of species
s
is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{s}(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{s}(x\mathchar 59\relax v\mathchar 59\relax t)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x=\Delta x\alpha_{s}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\|_{1}.

The FOM (
7
) and ROM (
13
) evolution of
\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)
are identical and lead to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbf{D}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)+u_{s}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\right].

Since the central finite difference derivative operator
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}}
defined in Eq. (
10
) is skew-symmetric, i.e.
\mathbf{D}=-\mathbf{D}^{\top}
, we get
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}=[\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{1}]^{\top}=-[\mathbf{D}\mathbf{1}]^{\top}=\mathbf{0}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}}
is a column vector of all ones and
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}}
is a column vector of all zeros. Thus,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\cancelto{\mathbf{0}^{\top}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)+u_{s}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\right]=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}_{s}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=0.

3.4.2
Momentum Conservation

The total momentum is defined as

\mathcal{P}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{s}m_{s}\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf_{s}(x\mathchar 59\relax v\mathchar 59\relax t)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x=\Delta x\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}m_{s}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)\|_{1}+u_{s}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\|_{1}\right]\mathchar 59\relax

The time derivative of the total momentum is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Delta x\sum_{s}m_{s}\alpha_{s}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}+u_{s}\cancelto{0}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\right].

(15)

The FOM (
7
) and ROM (
13
) evolution of
\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)
are identical and lead to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\cancelto{\mathbf{0}^{\top}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)+\alpha_{s}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 2}(t)+u_{s}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)\right]+\frac{\sqrt{2}q_{s}}{\alpha_{s}m_{s}}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\odot\mathbf{E}(t)\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}q_{s}}{\alpha_{s}m_{s}}\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t).

Inserting Eq. (
16

15
) and employing the semi-discrete Poisson equation (

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Delta x\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\underbrace{\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}q_{s}\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)}_{\text{insert Eq.}\penalty\ \eqref{poisson-ode}}=\Delta x\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}(t)=0.

In the above, we use the skew-symmetric property of
\mathbf{D}

\mathbf{D}=-\mathbf{D}^{\top}
, which leads to
\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}(t)=[\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}(t)]^{\top}=-\mathbf{E}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}(t)=0

Thus, total momentum is conserved at the semi-discrete level.

3.4.3
Energy Conservation

The total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energies denoted as
\mathcal{E}(t)\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pot}}(t)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\Delta x}{2}\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}m_{s}\left[\frac{\alpha_{s}^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 2}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\sqrt{2}u_{s}\alpha_{s}\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 1}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}^{2}}{2}+u_{s}^{2}\right)\cancelto{0}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{C}_{s\mathchar 59\relax 0}(t)\|_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{E}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

5
Conclusions

We have derived a conservative data-driven parametric ROM for the kinetic equations treated with an expansion in Hermite functions in velocity space, where the first three Hermite moments are solved exactly and the higher-order Hermite moments are approximated via POD. Due to the affine parametric dependence of the FOM, the reduced operators are constructed once
a priori
, which significantly reduces the method’s online computational cost.
We examined the proposed ROM performance on 1D1V electrostatic problems: weak Landau damping and two-stream instability.
In the weak Landau damping case, we show that the ROM successfully extrapolates both temporally and across parameters. For the more challenging two-stream instability test, which features strongly nonlinear dynamics, the ROM with
N_{r}=150
reduces the FOM CPU simulation time by approximately a factor of
5
while maintaining a density mean relative error below 0.5% for both parametric tests and achieving a memory reduction factor of
97
. This is an important result as it highlights the potential of complexity reduction techniques even for problems where complex structures form in the distribution function.
We anticipate that the developed ROM will be advantageous for 3D3V simulations due to its computational and memory scaling, which may enable performing multi-query parametric simulations, perhaps for design or uncertainty quantification, on laptop computers or similar devices instead of requiring supercomputer resources.
There are several other aspects worth investigating to further improve the performance of the ROM. These include hyperreduction techniques to improve the computational scaling of the nonlinear term
Chaturantabut and Sorensen [2010]
,
Pagliantini and Vismara [2025]
and the use of local bases in time to reduce
N_{r}
for long-time nonlinear simulations
San and Borggaard [2015]
,
Tsai et al. [2023]
.
Furthermore, the number of preserved fluid moments in the ROM framework can be increased; however, for the problems analyzed in this manuscript, we found no advantage in preserving more than three moments.
The ROM can be constructed using the Legendre basis instead of the AW Hermite discretization in velocity, as both spectral expansions exhibit the fluid-kinetic coupling property
Manzini et al. [2016]
. Lastly, in high-dimensional parameter spaces, full-grid sampling scales exponentially with the number of parameters, while adaptive sampling via greedy search or local sensitivity analysis can help reduce the offline computational cost
Benner et al. [2015]
.
