Efficacy and safety of radiotherapy in first-line treatment for de novo advanced esophageal cancer in the era of immunochemotherapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hai Zeng, Yan-Ling Wu, Xiaofeng Wang, Hui Bai, Cihui Yan, Wencheng Zhang, Qifeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of systemic treatment combined with radiotherapy (RT) as the first-line treatment for de novo advanced esophageal cancer (EC).MethodsA meta-analysis was conducted, and it followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline. A literature search was performed systematically in PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science on February 1, 2025. The protocol of this meta-analysis was published in PROSPERO with the registration number CRD42025650118.ResultsEight studies involving a total of 11,356 patients were finally included. Systemic treatment combined with RT improved OS (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.70–0.74, P < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.62–0.78, P < 0.001) compared with systemic treatment alone. The grade ≥ 3 treatment-related lymphopenia (OR = 5.52, P < 0.001), leukopenia (OR = 1.56, P < 0.001), and esophagitis (OR = 13.11, P < 0.001) were more frequent in the RT-combined group; no significant differences were observed in other severe toxicities. Subgroup analysis on systemic treatment type, ESCC, and TNM stage edition also demonstrated that this RT-combined treatment could provide significant survival benefits. Exploratory analysis showed that maximal survival benefit emerged in patients who received systemic therapy, especially immunochemotherapy, combined with radical (≥ 50 Gy) primary tumor RT.ConclusionsBy synthesizing data from both the pre-immunotherapy and immunotherapy eras involving 11,356 patients, we found that the incorporation of radical radiotherapy into first-line systemic treatment regimens improves survival outcomes while maintaining acceptable toxicity profiles in selected patients with advanced EC.Further randomized clinical trials are needed to verify our conclusions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15420-1.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, First-line treatment, Radiotherapy, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Despite therapeutic advances, esophageal carcinoma (EC) remains a leading cause of global cancer mortality, with emerging immunotherapies showing promising antitumor efficacy in advanced stages [1, 2]. The most common histology is esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) in East Asia and in South and East Africa, whereas it is esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) in North America and Europe [3–5]. Limited screening options and absence of early symptoms result in approximately 40% of EC patients presenting with advanced disease at initial diagnosis [4, 5].

Historically, platinum-based regimens constituted first-line therapy for advanced ESCC, yielding median OS below 13 months—a suboptimal outcome necessitating novel strategies [4, 6]. Recently, several randomized clinical trials (RCTs) [7–13] have demonstrated that adding PD-1 inhibitors to chemotherapy can significantly improve the survival, resulting in a median OS of 13.2 to 17.2 months, thereby contemporary guidelines endorse immunochemotherapy as first-line standard care, superseding chemotherapy-alone approaches. While immunochemotherapy has reshaped first-line management, intrinsic resistance limits its universal efficacy [7–13], many studies [14, 15] support radiotherapy as a synergistic modality to overcome these limitations, particularly in locally progressive disease. Moreover, significant primary tumor progression has been reported to occur in 30% to 40% of patients with advanced EC treated with systemic treatment alone [14–17]. Furthermore, uncontrolled primary disease drives significant morbidity in advanced ESCC, frequently manifesting as dysphagia or hemorrhage that compromises survival [6, 14–16, 18].

In clinical practice, radiotherapy (RT) is frequently administered to patients with advanced EC to alleviate local symptoms such as dysphagia, pain, and dyspnea [17, 19]. Over the years, the combination of RT and chemotherapy has shown excellent synergistic anti-tumor efficacy in the comprehensive treatment of advanced EC [6, 20–23]. In the era of pre-immunotherapy, a large number of retrospective studies and small-sample prospective phase 2 studies have demonstrated that in the first-line treatment of de novo advanced EC, the combination of radical radiotherapy (≥ 50 Gy) targeting the primary tumor with chemotherapy can promptly alleviate local symptoms (e.g., dysphagia) and significantly improve OS [17, 24–28], highlighting the value of aggressive primary tumor radiotherapy. In the current era of immunotherapy, a growing body of evidence suggests that survival benefits may be associated with aggressive RT in patients with advanced EC [14, 18, 19, 29, 30]. Preclinical studies [31–35] showed that RT can induce adaptive and innate immune responses, PD-1 inhibitors can promote vascular normalization and improve radiosensitivity, and the combination of RT and immunotherapy exhibits synergistic effects. Recently, the results of multi-center real-world studies [14, 18] and a prospective phase 2 clinical trial [29] showed that integration of radiotherapy with systemic therapy significantly enhanced survival outcomes and was well-tolerated.

Given the lack of pooled analysis of published data on this topic, for the first time, we summarized the available clinical evidence on the value of RT in the first-line treatment of de novo advanced EC. In this meta-analysis, we aimed to determine the most effective first-line treatment regimen for clinical practice while providing high-level evidence to assist clinicians in making informed treatment decisions.

Materials and methods

Study design and literature search

This meta-analysis adhered to PRISMA standards [36], and was prospectively registered (PROSPERO: CRD42025650118). Literature searches spanned PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The Population, Intervention, Control, Outcomes and Study Design method was used to define literature inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table 1). A literature search was performed systematically in PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science on February 1, 2025. The detailed Queries are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

To be included in this meta-analysis, studies were required to have administered a combination of systemic treatment (i.e., chemotherapy or immunochemotherapy) and RT as the first-line treatment to patients diagnosed with de novo advanced EC. The studies included should meet the following criteria: (1) patients with metastatic EC at initial diagnosis and no prior anticancer treatment before diagnosis; (2) dual-arm or single-arm studies including patients who received a combination of systemic treatment and external RT as first-line treatment; (3) the language of studies was limited to English; (4) data on primary and secondary endpoints had to be reported, or could be acquired by contacting the corresponding author of related trials, or could be extracted from the Kaplan-Meier curve. When results of the same trial were reported in different papers, we included the most recent and informative one. Exclusion criteria: (1) duplicate studies; (2) review, meta-analysis, and case report. The workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram. Out of 6259 studies were screened, and a total of 8 articles were included

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was OS; and the secondary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential variations in treatment effects based on the systemic treatment type (chemotherapy or immunochemotherapy), pathological type, and edition of the TNM stage. Additionally, an exploratory analysis of the optimal combination mode of systemic treatment and RT was performed.

Methodological quality assessment

The quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers, using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria [37].

Data extraction and synthesis

Two reviewers reviewed the full text of each study, irrelevant or overlapping studies were removed, thus creating a set of qualified publications (Fig. 1). In this meta-analysis, data were acquired by contacting the corresponding author of related trials, or extracted from the Kaplan-Meier curve of each study using Engauge Digitizer version 4.1 (available from: https://digitizer.sourceforge.net/), or directly extracted from the text of each study. For each study, the following data were extracted: first author’s name, year of publication, the author’s country, study period, study type, sample size (systemic treatment arm / combined radiotherapy arm), TNM stage version, histology type, systemic treatment arm, combined radiotherapy group, radiation site, radiation dose, radiation technology, and follow-up time. Detailed information of all included studies is presented in Table 1.

Statistical analyses

This meta-analysis was performed using Stata 16.0‌ and ‌R 4.1.2‌. For single-arm trial, we combined the proportion of each endpoint with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) and visualized results using forest plots. For two-arm trial, hazard ratios (HRs), risk ratios (RRs), or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Pooled RRs for 1- and 2-year OS, 1- and 2-year PFS, DCR, and ORR, along with ORs for treatment-related adverse events, were derived using the ‌Mantel-Haenszel method under a fixed-effect model‌. HRs for time-to-event outcomes were synthesized via the ‌generic inverse-variance method‌ with log-transformed estimates and standard errors. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with ‌I² ≤ 50% and P ≥ 0.05‌ indicating low heterogeneity (fixed-effect model). If ‌I² >50%‌, a random-effects model was applied. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by iteratively excluding individual studies. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s linear regression test [39] and visual inspection of funnel plots. Notably, when the number of included studies is small (e.g., fewer than 10), the statistical power of Egger’s test is substantially diminished, thereby increasing the risk of a false-negative result—specifically, the failure to detect existing publication bias. Consequently, Egger’s test was employed as an exploratory method in conjunction with funnel plots in this meta-analysis. A Galbraith radial plot was generated to visually identify outlier studies that might disproportionately influence heterogeneity. All tests were two-sided, with ‌P < 0.05‌ considered statistically significant.

Results

The electronic search initially yielded 8,825 potentially relevant publications. After deduplication and screening, 8 studies [14, 17, 18, 26, 27, 29, 38] were included in this meta-analysis. Notably, individual patient data (IPD) from the AEC-ICR-1ST trial [14] and TR-EAT trial [29] were acquired by contacting the corresponding authors of the trials. The flowchart of study selection was shown in Fig. 1. The characteristics of all included studies were detailedly presented in Table 1. Among these studies, seven were two-arm cohort studies [14, 17, 18, 26, 27, 38]; one [29] was a prospective single-arm phase 2 trial. Among these studies, five studies used chemotherapy as systemic treatment [17, 26, 27, 38], while three studies [14, 18, 29] used immunochemotherapy as systemic treatment. The details of the assessment regarding the included studies are shown in Supplementary Table 3. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the MINORS criteria. For the single-arm study (non-comparative design), the score was 13 (maximum possible score: 16), indicating high quality according to Slim et al. [37]. The remaining seven retrospective comparative studies scored 18 (maximum possible score: 24), which also met the threshold for high quality (≥ 18/24). Therefore, the overall quality of the included studies was considered high.

A total of 11,356 patients were included, with a median follow-up time ranging from 6.68 to 37 months (as shown in Table 1). All patients had confirmed histological diagnoses; the majority of them had EAC (61.0%) and ESCC (34.1%), except for a very small proportion of patients (4.9%) who had other histological types. Notably, all patients with EAC and other histological types included in this meta-analysis (Table 1) were from the study by Guttmann et al. [17].

All included studies reported OS, but the TR-EAT trial [29] was a single-arm trial. In pooled analysis, when comparing the combined treatment of systemic therapy and RT with systemic therapy alone, the combined treatment group had a significantly higher 1-year and 2-year OS rate compared with the systemic therapy-alone group (RR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.76–0.82, P < 0.001; RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80–0.92, P < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 2A-B). Consistent with the above results, the HR for OS was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C), indicating a substantial benefit of RT. No publication bias was detected for HR-OS using Egger’s test; however, there was significant publication bias in the 1- and 2-year OS (Supplementary Table 4). Visual inspection of funnel plots revealed asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). To investigate potential sources of publication bias, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies. The exclusion of Guttmann et al.‘s study [17] resulted in a substantial reduction in heterogeneity for both time points (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). At the same time, the Galbraith radial plot identified the study by Guttmann et al. [17]. as a prominent outlier (Supplementary Fig. 1E-F). The above results show that this study might be a key contributor to the observed bias. After removal of this study the funnel plot symmetry was restored (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B), and Egger’s test no longer indicated publication bias (Supplementary Table 4). Sensitivity analyses show none of these exclusions led to notable changes in the pooled effect estimates or reintroduced significant heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 2C-D). The Galbraith radial plot demonstrated that all studies clustered within the 95% confidence region, with no individual study exhibiting significant outliers (Supplementary Fig. 2E-F). And following the exclusion of this study, the forest plots for 1-year and 2-year OS in the updated meta-analysis both demonstrated I² values of 0, indicating absence of heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 2G-H).

PFS was reported in 7 studies, but the TR-EAT trial [29] was a single-arm trial. The combined treatment group had a significantly higher 1-year and 2-year PFS rate compared with the systemic therapy-alone group (RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80–0.93, P < 0.001; RR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.93, P < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 2D-E). Consistent with the above results, the HR for PFS was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.62–0.78, P < 0.001; Fig. 2F), indicating a substantial benefit of RT. No publication bias was detected (Supplementary Table 4).

The DCR data was reported in four studies, but the TR-EAT trial [29] was a single-arm trial. The combined treatment group had a significantly higher DCR rate compared with the systemic therapy-alone group (RR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.17–1.33, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 3A). No publication bias was detected (Supplementary Table 4).

The ORR data was reported in four studies, but the TR-EAT trial [29] was a single-arm trial. The combined treatment group had a significantly higher ORR rate compared with the systemic therapy-alone group (RR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.24–1.56, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 3B). No publication bias was detected (Supplementary Table 4).

The rate of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs was reported in five studies, but the TR-EAT trial [29] was a single-arm trial. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4A-H, there was no significant difference in the rate of grade ≥ 3 treatment-related pneumonia (OR = 3.63, 95% CI: 0.61–21.80, P = 0.158), neutropenia (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.57–1.42, P = 0.648), thrombopenia (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.62–1.96, P = 0.739), abnormal liver function (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.60–1.80, P = 0.884), and digestive reaction (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.66–1.63, P = 0.886) between the two groups; however, the combined treatment group had a significantly higher rate of grade ≥ 3 treatment-related lymphopenia (OR = 12.71, 95% CI: 8.09–19.96, P < 0.001), esophagitis (OR = 14.36, 95% CI: 5.22–39.50, P < 0.001), and leukopenia (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.34–2.36, P < 0.001) compared with the systemic therapy-alone group.

During the subgroup analysis of the systemic treatment type, both in the subgroup that received immunochemotherapy as the systemic treatment and in the subgroup that underwent chemotherapy as the systemic treatment, the combined treatment group had a significantly higher OS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Fig. 3A-B) and a significantly higher PFS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B) compared with the systemic therapy-alone group. Consistent with the above results, the HR for OS was 0.67 (95% CI: 0.54–0.83, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C), and the HR for PFS was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.64–0.90, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 5C) and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.57–0.76, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 5C), indicating a substantial benefit of RT. Notably, among the studies that provided specific survival data for patients who received immunochemotherapy combined with RT as the first-line treatment, the pooled results indicated favorable outcomes. The pooled 1-year OS rate reached 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69–0.77, P < 0.001; Fig. 3D), and 2-year OS rate reached 0.51 (95% CI: 0.42–0.59, P < 0.001; Fig. 3E). The pooled 1-year PFS rate reached 0.41 (95% CI: 0.07–0.74, P = 0.019; Supplementary Fig. 6A), and 2-year PFS rate reached 0.30 (95% CI: 0.21–0.39, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 6B).

In the ESCC subgroup, the combined treatment group had a significantly higher OS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Fig. 3F-G) and a significantly higher PFS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Supplementary Fig. 6C-D) compared with the systemic therapy-alone group. Consistent with the above results, the HR for OS was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.61–0.79, P < 0.001; Fig. 3H), and the HR for PFS was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.62–0.78, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 6E), indicating a substantial benefit of RT. It is worth noting that all patients with EAC and other histological types included in this meta-analysis (Table 1) were from the study by Guttmann et al. [17]., and we were unable to specifically perform a subgroup analysis of patients with EAC or other pathological types.

To investigate the impact of aggressive primary tumor RT on treatment outcomes, in an effort to explore the optimal combination model of systemic treatment and RT, we conducted a pooled analysis of patients who received radical RT (≥ 50 Gy) targeting the primary tumor from the study by Guttmann et al. [17]., the study by Lyu et al. [26]., the study by Li et at [27]., and the IPD of AEC-ICR-1ST trial [14]. In this subgroup analysis, the combined treatment group of systemic therapy and radical RT (≥ 50 Gy) targeting the primary tumor had a significantly higher OS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Fig. 4A-B) and a significantly higher PFS rate at 1 year and 2 years (Supplementary Fig. 7A-B) compared with the systemic therapy-alone group. Consistent with the above results, the HR for OS was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74, P < 0.001; Fig. 4C), and the HR for PFS was 0.64 (95% CI: 0.52–0.79, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 7C), indicating that the combination model of systemic therapy and radical RT (≥ 50 Gy) targeting the primary tumor yields the most significant survival benefits for patients. It is worth noting that in the other three studies [18, 29, 38], patients received RT targeting both the primary tumor and metastatic lesions. Therefore, we were unable to specifically conduct a subgroup analysis to investigate the impact of aggressive metastases RT on treatment effects. Additionally, we conducted an exploratory analysis using IPD from the AEC-ICR-1ST trial [14] to compare the efficacy of combined treatment involving immunochemotherapy and radical (≥ 50 Gy) primary tumor RT with that of immunochemotherapy alone. To mitigate confounding bias, we implemented propensity score matching in clinical characteristics of the patients in the two groups (Supplementary Table 5). As shown in Fig. 4D-E, after PSM, the combined treatment group had a significantly longer median OS (24.90 versus 16.90 months, P < 0.001) and median PFS (11.20 versus 10.60 months, P = 0.030) than the immunochemotherapy-alone group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the present meta-analysis represents the first comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of RT in the first-line treatment of de novo advanced EC, encompassing both the chemotherapy and immunotherapy eras. By synthesizing data from eight studies involving 11,356 patients with de novo advanced EC, our findings demonstrate that the addition of aggressive RT to systemic therapy as a first-line combined treatment strategy significantly improves OS, PFS, ORR, and DCR, with survival gains offsetting manageable toxicity increments. Moreover, the survival advantages of the combination regimen were further confirmed by subgroup analyses, implying the stability and robustness of the results. Besides, an exploratory analysis of the optimal combination mode of systemic therapy and RT suggested that the survival benefits were most pronounced in the subgroup that received systemic therapy combined with definitive dose (≥ 50 Gy) RT targeting the primary tumor. These results underscore the clinical value of integrating RT, especially aggressive primary tumor RT, into first-line treatment regimens for de novo advanced EC, particularly in the context of evolving therapeutic paradigms. More importantly, these findings may provide evidence for redefining the role of RT from a purely palliative modality to a key component in a multimodal treatment strategy.

Overall, the meta-analysis indicated that integration of radiotherapy with systemic therapy significantly enhanced survival outcomes, reducing mortality risk by 28% (OS HR = 0.72) and progression risk by 30% (PFS HR = 0.70). These survival benefits may stem from multiple mechanisms. First, as RT is an effective local treatment that can reduce tumor burden by directly eliminating tumor lesions within the radiation field, patients who received RT were more likely to achieve a clinical response than those who did not receive RT [14, 15, 17, 19, 20, 40]. Meanwhile, RT can rapidly alleviate local symptoms (e.g., dysphagia, dyspnea, and bleeding), which are independently associated with poor prognosis [17, 41]. In the AEC-ICR-1ST trial [14], the rates of dysphagia relief, ORR, and DCR were significantly higher in the immunochemotherapy combined with RT cohort than in the immunochemotherapy-alone cohort. Second, by delaying local progression, RT may prolong the duration during which patients remain eligible for subsequent systemic therapy [4, 19]. Third, the combination of RT and chemotherapy or immunotherapy can produce excellent synergistic anti-tumor efficacy in the comprehensive treatment of malignancies. For example, preclinical and clinical studies [31–34] suggest that radiation-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) can lead to the release of tumor neoantigens, recruit and activate immune cells, promote T-cell infiltration, and enhance the immunogenicity of the tumor microenvironment (TME), while PD-1 inhibitors can promote vascular normalization, thereby overcoming hypoxia and improving radiosensitivity. This hypothesis is also supported by preclinical models showing enhanced abscopal effects with RT-immunochemotherapy combinations and clinical case reports in humans [42–45]. Ai et al. [34]. confirmed that the combination of RT, chemotherapy, and PD-1 inhibitors brought a higher local control rate (LCR) for patients with locally advanced ESCC by promoting vascular normalization, overcoming hypoxia, and improving radiosensitivity, with a 2-year LCR of 81.7% (95% CI: 72.7%–90.7%).

This meta-analysis revealed that combining RT with systemic therapy—whether chemotherapy or immunochemotherapy—yielded superior survival benefits compared to systemic therapy alone, and the benefits were most pronounced in patients who received systemic therapy combined with radical (≥ 50 Gy) primary tumor RT, exhibiting a 36% reduction in the risk of progression (HR = 0.64) and a 28% reduction in the risk of death (HR = 0.72). Our results align with historical evidence supporting the role of aggressive RT in the first-line treatment for advanced EC. In the era of chemotherapy, significant primary tumor progression has been reported to occur in 30% to 40% of patients with advanced EC treated by chemotherapy alone [16, 17]. As shown in Table 1, prior studies [17, 26, 27, 38] in the chemotherapy era demonstrated that radical RT (≥ 50 Gy) targeting the primary tumor could alleviate symptoms and improve survival by mitigating local progression, a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in this population. For instance, a large-sample cohort study by Guttmann et al. [17]. showed that in the first-line treatment of newly diagnosed metastatic EC, compared with chemotherapy alone, chemotherapy plus definitive dose (≥ 50.4 Gy) primary tumor RT was associated with improved survival (median OS: 8.3 versus 11.3 months, P ≤ 0.001), whereas chemotherapy plus palliative dose (< 50.4 Gy) primary tumor RT was significantly associated with inferior OS (median OS: 8.3 versus 7.5 months, P ≤ 0.001). Lyu et al. [26]. conducted a comparative analysis comparing the treatment outcomes of newly diagnosed stage IV ESCC patients treated with chemotherapy alone or in combination with radical (≥ 50 Gy) primary tumor RT. They found that radical primary tumor RT was associated with a better median OS, prolonging from 11 months to 14 months [26]. However, it is worth noting that these studies were all published before the era of immunotherapy and only evaluated the role of radical primary tumor RT plus chemotherapy in de novo advanced EC, without involving immunotherapy.

In the immunotherapy era, a growing body of evidence highlights the immunomodulatory effects of RT, which can synergize with PD-1 inhibitors to enhance systemic antitumor responses in the first-line treatment of de novo advanced ESCC. This synergistic effect was best exemplified in the TR-EAT trial [29]. This phase 2 trial [29] included a total of 33 patients with de novo advanced ESCC, all of whom received concurrent chemoradiotherapy with toripalimab, and the RT target encompassed both the primary tumor (50–50.4 Gy/25–28 fractions) and all metastatic lesions (30–40 Gy/3–5 fractions). The results of this trial were promising, with a median PFS of 9.8 months and a manageable safety profile [29]. Notably, in this meta-analysis, an exploratory analysis based IPD showed that for patients who underwent immunochemotherapy combined with radical (≥ 50 Gy) RT targeting only the primary tumor, the median OS reached 24.90 months (Fig. 4D), and the median PFS reached 11.20 months (Fig. 4E). The most pronounced survival benefits in these patients may be explained from the following two aspects. First, RT is a two-edged sword as it has both immune stimulatory and suppressive impacts. Although the synergistic anti-tumor efficacy between RT and immunochemotherapy is complex, the larger the RT range, the greater damage it may cause to the immune system (e.g., radiation-induced lymphopenia, RIL) [14, 46]. Li et al. [14]. compared the treatment outcomes between immunochemotherapy combined with RT targeting the primary tumor only (Group A) and immunochemotherapy combined with RT targeting both the primary tumor and metastases (Group B) in the first-line treatment for de novo advanced ESCC. The results showed that Group B had inferior survival and a significantly higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 lymphopenia compared with Group A (54.8% versus 33.8%, P = 0.029) [14]. Lymphocytes were generally considered to be the primary effectors of anti-tumor immunity in the host immune system, and several studies have confirmed that RIL impairs the effectiveness of immunotherapy and adversely affects the OS of ESCC patients [47–51]. Second, throughout the years, numerous studies [17, 26, 27, 38] have demonstrated that radical RT (≥ 50 Gy) for the primary tumor is significantly associated with improved survival in patients with advanced EC. Recently, a prospective phase 2 trial [52] showed that the combination of local and systemic therapy was associated with significantly better OS compared to systemic therapy alone (median OS: not reached vs. 18.6 months, P = 0.002). Notably, all patients with advanced ESCC in this study had previously undergone radical RT or surgery for their primary tumor prior to enrollment [52], which highlights the importance of prioritizing the control of the primary tumor before addressing metastatic lesions in advanced ESCC.

It is crucial to interpret these findings in the context of histology. The vast majority of patients in our pooled analysis, particularly those from studies conducted during the immunotherapy era, had ESCC. Although the study by Guttmann et al. included a considerable proportion of patients with EAC [17], it is based on a single retrospective dataset derived from the chemotherapy era. Therefore, the compelling survival benefits demonstrated here are most applicable to ESCC, and definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of this combined approach for EAC cannot be drawn due to limited data and potential biases. The distinct clinical features and tumor microenvironments of ESCC and EAC may lead to different responses to combined modality therapy [3, 4], necessitating histology-specific investigations in the future.

The safety of novel treatment strategies is also a frequently addressed concern in clinical practice. In this meta-analysis, while grade ≥ 3 treatment-related lymphopenia (OR = 12.71) and esophagitis (OR = 14.36) were substantially more frequent in the RT-combined group, no significant differences were observed in other severe toxicities such as pneumonia, digestive reaction, or abnormal liver function. This indicates that although the addition of RT is associated with a predictable and significant increase in the likelihood of specific local or hematological toxicities, it does not substantially impair the overall tolerability of the first-line treatment approach, and these risks can be effectively managed through proactive clinical strategies. Moreover, the safety profile observed in the current study was consistent with that previously reported in patients with advanced EC who received PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy as first-line treatment [7, 9, 10, 53].

Limitations and future directions

Despite rigorous methodology, several limitations in this meta-analysis warrant consideration. First, although this study is a large-sample meta-analysis including 11,356 patients, most included studies were retrospective. Second, heterogeneity in RT protocols (e.g., dose, target volume) complicates the standardization of recommendations. Last but not the least, it is well known that the clinical features and biological behaviors are distinctly different between ESCC and EAC. However, as no subgroup analysis was performed in the included studies, it was difficult to conduct a subgroup analysis of patients with EAC.

To address these limitations, and the following issues deserve in-depth study in the future: (1) The impact of different RT ranges on prognosis, whether RT targets only the primary tumor, metastatic lesions, or both. The study by Shi et al. [38]. showed that RT targeting all lesions brought significant survival benefits to patients with oligometastatic ESCC. Increasing evidence suggests that patients with oligometastatic ESCC can attain longer-term survival using active comprehensive treatment compared to those with multiple metastases [38, 52], thus, oligometastatic ESCC may be suitable for more aggressive RT. (2) The impact of different timings of RT intervention on prognosis. Although exploratory analysis in the AEC-ICR-1st trial [14] suggests that concurrent RT can significantly improve survival while subsequent RT cannot, it remains to be confirmed by future RCTs whether the concurrent use of immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and RT will improve survival without significantly increasing the incidence of severe TRAEs. (3) The impact of different radiation doses on prognosis. Evidence from the chemotherapy era suggested that a radiotherapy dose of ≥ 50 Gy for the primary tumor was necessary to improve OS [17]. In the current era of immunotherapy, when immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy are used concurrently, could a lower RT dose benefit patients and achieve the effect of “enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity”? In the study by Chen et al. [18], RT of ≥ 40 Gy radiation dose to the primary tumor also brought survival benefits to de novo advanced ESCC patients. (4) Determining the optimal beneficiary population from the combined treatment of RT and immunochemotherapy. In the future, concrete efforts should be made to screen for biomarkers predicting efficacy and to develop prognosis prediction models to guide individualized treatment. Notably, the results from running RCTs are awaited (Supplementary Table 6).

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that incorporating radical RT into first-line systemic regimens for patients with ESCC improves survival outcomes with acceptable toxicity profiles. Further studies are needed to verify our conclusions.
