Monomial ideals whose all matching powers are Cohen-Macaulay
Antonino Ficarra, Somayeh Moradi

Abstract
. In the present paper, we aim to classify monomial ideals whose all matching powers are Cohen-Macaulay. We especially focus our attention on edge ideals. The Cohen-Macaulayness of the last matching power of an edge ideal is characterized, providing an algebraic analogue of the famous Tutte theorem regarding graphs having a perfect matching. For chordal graphs, very well-covered graphs and Cameron-Walker graphs, we completely solve our problem.

Introduction

Let
S
be either a regular local ring, or the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
over a field
K
, and let
I\subset S
be an ideal. We say that
I
is Cohen-Macaulay if
S/I
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring. Assume, moreover, that
I
is radical. Then, a classical result in commutative algebra guarantees that
I
is a complete intersection if and only if
I^{k}
is Cohen-Macaulay for infinitely many
k
. This result was proved in the late seventies, more or less independently, by Achilles and Vogel in
[
1
]
, by Cowsik and Nori in
[
7
]
, and by Waldi in
[
42
]
.

In particular, let
I=I(G)
be the edge ideal of a finite simple graph
G
on the vertex set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, that is, the ideal in the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
generated by those monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
for which
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
is an edge. Hereafter, we will say that
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay graph, if
I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay. We have that
I(G)
is a radical ideal, and the aforementioned result implies that all powers
I(G)^{k}
are Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
E(G)
consists of disjoint edges.

The situation becomes more interesting if one turns its attention to other kind of powers. The first paper in this direction
[
33
]
, written by Minh and Trung, characterizes those unmixed 2-dimensional monomial ideals
I\subset S
whose (almost) all symbolic powers
I^{(k)}
are Cohen-Macaulay. After the appearance of this paper, many researchers tried to characterize all those unmixed monomial ideals
I\subset S
whose (almost) all symbolic powers are Cohen-Macaulay, culminating with the beautiful solution due, independently, to Minh and Trung
[
34
]
, and Varbaro
[
41
]
. Writing any squarefree monomial ideal
I
as the Stanley-Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta}
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
, the aforementioned authors proved, independently, that
I_{\Delta}^{(k)}
is Cohen-Macaulay for all
k\gg 0
if and only if
\Delta
is a matroid.

Very recently, in
[
3
]
and
[
18
]
, the concept of squarefree power was introduced. Subsequently, this concept was extended to all monomial ideals with the introduction of
matching powers

[
15
]
, see also
[
11
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
21
,
22
,
30
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Hereafter, we always refer to this generalized concept. Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal and let
\mathcal{G}(I)
be its minimal monomial generating set. The
k
th matching power of
I
is the monomial ideal generated by the products
u_{1}\cdots u_{k}
, where the
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}(I)
form a regular sequence. In other words,
\textup{supp}(u_{i})\cap\textup{supp}(u_{j})=\emptyset
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, where the
support
of a monomial
w\in S
is defined as the set
\textup{supp}(w)=\{x_{i}:x_{i}\ \textup{divides}\ w\}
. Moreover, the
monomial grade
of
I
is defined as
\nu(I)=\max\{k:I^{[k]}\neq 0\}
. A
matching
of
G
is a family of pairwise disjoint edges of
G
. Let
\nu(G)
be the maximum size of a matching of
G
. Then, it is easy to see that
\nu(I(G))=\nu(G)
. Furthermore, the generators of
I(G)^{[k]}
correspond to the vertex sets of the
k
-matchings of
G
, which motivates the choice to name the ideals
I^{[k]}
the matching powers of
I
. Our goal is to identify and classify those monomial ideals
I\subset S
whose matching powers
I^{[k]}
are Cohen-Macaulay, for all
1\leq k\leq\nu(I)
.

Recently, in
[
13
]
, Das, Roy and Saha proved that
I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay for all
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
, if
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay forest. Moreover, it is proven in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is a polymatroidal ideal for any graph
G
. By a result due to Herzog and Hibi
[
26
]
, the Cohen-Macaulay polymatroidal ideals are of very special nature. These two facts suggest that there should be a pleasant theory regarding the Cohen-Macaulayness of the matching powers of an edge ideal. This is what we consider in this paper.
More precisely, we study when all matching powers of
I(G)
are Cohen-Macaulay. We characterize such graphs
G
when
G
has a perfect matching and the
(\nu(G)-1)
th matching power of
I(G)
has height
2
. These conditions are fulfilled when
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay very well-covered graph. Chordal graphs and Cameron-Walker graphs are other families for which we give such characterization. We will also discuss the situation for the graphs having up to
7
vertices.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Section
1
, we investigate the hereditary nature of the Cohen-Macaulay property of
I(G)^{[k]}
.
It is shown that if
I(G)^{[k]}
possesses the Cohen-Macaulay property, then so does

, where

is either the subgraph

is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if either
G
has a perfect matching, or
G\setminus\{i\}
has a perfect matching of size
|V(G)|-1
for all
i\in V(G)
. This result can be seen as an algebraic analogue of the famous Tutte theorem in classical graph theory
[
40

. This characterization plays an important role in the study of edge ideals of graphs whose all matching powers are Cohen-Macaulay.

In Section

we concentrate our attention on the family of graphs which admit a perfect matching. In Theorem

are Cohen-Macaulay, provided that

and

, as is shown in Lemma

we use the Hilbert-Burch Theorem and a well-known result from graph theory on the characterization of trees. An interesting family of graphs which meets the conditions of Theorem

is the family of the Cohen-Macaulay very well-covered graphs. Using a characterization of this family due to Crupi, Rinaldo and Terai
[
12

, in Proposition

we give a formula for the dimension of

for all

. In particular, when

, this dimension is
|V(G)|-2
. So, applying our main theorem to this family we get Corollary
2.8
, which shows that the only very well-covered graphs for which all matching powers of their edge ideals are Cohen-Macaulay, are only the Cohen-Macaulay forests. Bipartite graphs and whisker graphs are subfamilies of very well-covered graphs. Applying our result to these families we obtain the analogous characterizations, see Corollaries
2.9
,
2.10
. We close this section by obtaining the normalized depth function of edge ideals of very well-covered graphs

such that

is Cohen-Macaulay, for all

, see Corollary
2.11
.

In Section

, we answer our question on Cohen-Macaulay matching powers for the family of chordal graphs and Cameron-Walker graphs. For chordal graphs we use a result by Herzog, Hibi and Zheng
[
28

which characterizes Cohen-Macaulay chordal graphs. In Theorem

we prove that all matching powers of the edge ideal of a chordal graph are Cohen-Macaulay if and only if

is either a complete graph or a Cohen-Macaulay forest. Using the characterizations given for chordal graphs and bipartite graphs, in Theorem

In the final Section

. Our experiments would indicate that among the graphs on

non-isolated vertices, besides the complete graphs and the Cohen-Macaulay forests, there are only two extra graphs, up to isomorphism, for which the matching powers of their edge ideal is Cohen-Macaulay for all
k

1.
Cohen-Macaulayness of matching powers of edge ideals

Throughout this paper,
G
is a finite simple graph. Since the edge ideal of
G
does not change by removing isolated vertices, we may extra assume that
G
has no isolated vertices. We denote the vertex set of
G
by
V(G)
and the edge set by
E(G)
.
In this section we show that the Cohen-Macaulayness of a matching power of
I(G)
is inherited by its connected components and by its subgraphs
G\setminus N_{G}[x]
for any vertex
x
of
G
. Moreover, we characterize those edge ideals whose last non-vanishing matching power is Cohen-Macaulay.

Recall that for a vertex
x_{i}\in V(G)
, the sets

\displaystyle N_{G}(x_{i})\

\displaystyle=\ \{x_{j}\in V(G)\ :\ \{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G)\},

\displaystyle N_{G}[x_{i}]\

\displaystyle=\ N_{G}(x_{i})\cup\{x_{i}\},

are called the
open neighbourhood
and the
closed neighbourhood
of
x_{i}
in
G
.

The following lemma is needed for the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal and
u\in S
be a monomial with
u\notin I
. If
S/I
is Cohen-Macaulay, then
S/(I:u)
is Cohen-Macaulay and moreover
\textup{depth\,}S/I=\textup{depth\,}S/(I:u)
.

Proof.

By
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
,
\textup{depth\,}S/I\leq\textup{depth\,}S/(I:u)
. Combining this with the inequalities
\textup{depth\,}S/(I:u)\leq\dim S/(I:u)\leq\dim S/I
implies the assertion.

∎

For what follows, we need to recall some concepts and results from
[
14
,
23
]
. Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal. By
\mathcal{G}(I)
we denote the unique minimal set of monomial generators of
I
. Let
J,L\subset S
be monomial ideals such that
\mathcal{G}(I)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}(J)
and
\mathcal{G}(L)
. Following
[
23
]
, we say that
I=J+L
is a
Betti splitting
if

\beta_{i,j}(I)=\beta_{i,j}(J)+\beta_{i,j}(L)+\beta_{i-1,j}(J\cap L),\ \ \ \text{for all}\,\ i,j\geq 0.

(1)

Consider the natural short exact sequence

0\rightarrow J\cap L\rightarrow J\oplus L\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0.

(2)

By
[
23
, Proposition 2.1]
, equation (
1
) holds if and only if the induced maps

\textup{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,J\cap L)\rightarrow\textup{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,J)\oplus\textup{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,L)

in the
Tor
exact sequence arising from the above sequence are zero for all
i\geq 0
. In this case, we say that the inclusion map
J\cap L\rightarrow J\oplus L
is
Tor
-vanishing
. The next result appeared implicitly firstly in
[
14
, Proposition 3.1]
(see also
[
35
, Lemma 4.2]
).

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
J,L\subset S
be non-zero monomial ideals with
J\subset L
. Suppose there exists a map
\varphi:\mathcal{G}(J)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(L)
such that for any
\emptyset\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{G}(J)
we have

\textup{lcm}(u:u\in\Omega)\in\mathfrak{m}(\textup{lcm}(\varphi(u):u\in\Omega)),

where
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
. Then the inclusion map
J\rightarrow L
is
Tor
-vanishing.

For a monomial ideal
I\subset S
, we denote by
\partial^{*}I
the ideal generated by the elements of the form
u/x_{i}
, where
u\in\mathcal{G}(I)
, and
x_{i}
is a variable dividing
u
. The following result was proved in
[
35
, Proposition 4.4]
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
J,L\subset S
be non-zero monomial ideals. Suppose that
\partial^{*}J\subseteq L
. Then
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}L
and there exists a map
\varphi:\mathcal{G}(J)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(L)
satisfying the assumption of Proposition
1.2
. In particular, the inclusion map
J\rightarrow L
is
Tor
-vanishing.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
G
be a graph,
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
be an integer, and
x\in V(G)
be a vertex. Then,

(I(G)^{[k]}:x)=\sum_{y\in N_{G}(x)}yI(G\setminus\{x,y\})^{[k-1]}+I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}.

(3)

If
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\neq 0
, then this decomposition is a Betti splitting, and in particular,

\textup{depth\,}S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}-|N_{G}(x)|\ \geq\ \textup{depth\,}S/(I(G)^{[k]}:x).

Proof.

The equation (
3
) was proved in
[
13
, Lemma 2.9(ii)]
. To simplify our discussion, we set
I=(I(G)^{[k]}:x)
,
J=\sum_{y\in N_{G}(x)}yI(G\setminus\{x,y\})^{[k-1]}
,
L=I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}
, and we assume, up to a relabeling, that
N_{G}(x)=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
.

Firstly, we notice that
\mathcal{G}(I)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}(J)
and
\mathcal{G}(L)
. Indeed, all the minimal generators of
J
are monomials of degree
2k-1
and are divided by some variable
y_{i}
, whereas each minimal generator of
L
is a monomial of degree
2k
and is not divided by
y_{i}
, for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Now, we compute
J\cap L
. We have

\displaystyle J\cap L\

\displaystyle=\ (\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}I(G\setminus\{x,y_{i}\})^{[k-1]})\cap I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}[I(G\setminus\{x,y_{i}\})^{[k-1]}\cap I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}]

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\ =\ (y_{1},\dots,y_{m})I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}.

Here, in the second equality we used that
y_{i}
does not divide any generator of
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}
, and in the third equality we used, for
1\leq i\leq m
, the chain of inclusions
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\subset I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k-1]}\subseteq I(G\setminus\{x,y_{i}\})^{[k-1]}
.

To prove that the inclusion map
J\cap L\rightarrow J\oplus L
is
Tor
-vanishing, it is enough to show that the inclusion maps
J\cap L\rightarrow J
and
J\cap L\rightarrow L
are
Tor
-vanishing.

Notice that
\partial^{*}I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\subset I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k-1]}\subseteq\sum_{i=1}^{m}I(G\setminus\{x,y_{i}\})^{[k-1]}
. Therefore, by Lemma
1.3
, there exists a map

\varphi\ :\ \mathcal{G}(I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}I(G\setminus\{x,y_{i}\})^{[k-1]})

such that for every non-empty subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{G}(I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]})
we have

\textup{lcm}(u:u\in\Omega)\in\mathfrak{m}(\textup{lcm}(\varphi(u):u\in\Omega)),

where
\mathfrak{m}
is the graded maximal ideal of
S
.

Each minimal generator of
J\cap L=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})L
is of the form
y_{i}u
with
1\leq i\leq m
and
u\in\mathcal{G}(I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]})
. Now we define a map
\Phi:\mathcal{G}(J\cap L)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(J)
by setting
\Phi(y_{i}u)=y_{i}\varphi(u)
. It is clear that
\Phi(y_{i}u)\in\mathcal{G}(J)
. Let
\Omega=\{y_{i_{\ell}}u_{\ell}\}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{G}(J\cap L)
be non-empty. Then

\displaystyle\textup{lcm}(\{y_{i_{\ell}}u_{\ell}\}_{\ell})=\textup{lcm}(\{y_{i_{\ell}}\}_{\ell})\textup{lcm}(\{u_{\ell}\}_{\ell})\

\displaystyle\in\ \textup{lcm}(\{y_{i_{\ell}}\}_{\ell})\cdot\mathfrak{m}(\textup{lcm}(\{\varphi(u_{\ell})\}_{\ell}))

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{m}(\textup{lcm}(\{\Phi(y_{i_{\ell}}u_{\ell})\}_{\ell})).

Thus, Proposition
1.2
implies that
J\cap L\rightarrow J
is
Tor
-vanishing.

To prove that
J\cap L\rightarrow L
is
Tor
-vanishing, notice that
J\cap L=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})L
and that the variables
y_{i}
do not divide any minimal generator of
L
. Therefore, we can define a map
\Psi:\mathcal{G}(J\cap L)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(L)
by setting
\Psi(y_{i}u)=u
for all
1\leq i\leq m
and all
u\in\mathcal{G}(L)
. It is clear that this map satisfies the condition in Proposition
1.2
, and so the map
J\cap L\rightarrow L
is
Tor
-vanishing.

Hence, equation (
3
) is indeed a Betti splitting. It follows from equation (
1
) that

\textup{depth\,}S/I\ =\ \min\{\textup{depth\,}S/J,\textup{depth\,}S/L,\textup{depth\,}S/(J\cap L)-1\}.

Since
J\cap L=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})L
, we have
\textup{depth\,}S/(J\cap L)=\textup{depth\,}S/L-(m-1)
. Therefore, it follows that

\textup{depth\,}S/L-m=\textup{depth\,}S/(J\cap L)-1\geq\textup{depth\,}S/I.

Since
m=|N_{G}(x)|
, the proof is complete.

∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following useful permanence property.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a graph and
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
be an integer. If
I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, then
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, for any vertex
x\in V(G)
.

Proof.

Consider a vertex
x\in V(G)
. We may assume that
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\neq 0
. Then by Lemma
1.4
we have

\textup{depth\,}S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}-|N_{G}(x)|\geq\textup{depth\,}S/(I(G)^{[k]}:x).

Since
I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, by Lemma
1.1
we have

\textup{depth\,}S/(I(G)^{[k]}:x)=\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[k]}.

From this fact and the assumption that
I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, we get that
\dim S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}-|N_{G}(x)|\geq\dim S/I(G)^{[k]}
. We show that indeed

\dim S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}-|N_{G}(x)|=\dim S/I(G)^{[k]}.

(4)

Let
N_{G}(x)=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
, and let
P\subset S
be a monomial prime ideal such that
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\subseteq P
. Then one can see that
I(G)^{[k]}\subset(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},P)
. Since any minimal prime ideal of
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}
is a monomial ideal generated by variables, it follows that
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[k]}\leq\textup{height}\,I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}+|N_{G}(x)|
. This shows the equality (
4
).
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\textup{depth\,}S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\

\displaystyle\geq\ \textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[k]}+|N_{G}(x)|\ =\ \dim S/I(G)^{[k]}+|N_{G}(x)|

\displaystyle=\ \dim S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}\ \geq\ \textup{depth\,}S/I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}.

Hence
I(G\setminus N_{G}[x])^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, as desired.

∎

The following result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.5
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
G
be a graph with the connected components
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
, and let
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
be an integer. If
I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, then
I(G_{i})^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay for all
i
.

The converse of Corollary
1.6
does not hold in general.

Example 1.7
.

Let
G
be a graph with the connected components
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
, where
K_{n}
denotes the complete graph on
n
vertices. Then
I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay, but
I(G)^{[2]}
is not Cohen-Macaulay, since
\dim S/I(G)^{[2]}=4
and
\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[2]}=3
.

Next, we characterize those edge ideals
I(G)
having the property that their last non-vanishing matching power is Cohen-Macaulay.

It is clear that for any graph
G
on
n
non-isolated vertices we have
\nu(G)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.

A
perfect matching
in
G
is a matching
M
of
G
such that each vertex of
G
is an endpoint of some edge in
M
. So
G
has a perfect matching if and only if
n=2\nu(G)
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
non-isolated vertices. The following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is Cohen-Macaulay.

(b)

I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2\nu(G)]}
and
\nu(G)=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
.

(c)

Either
G
has a perfect matching, or
G\setminus\{i\}
has a perfect matching of size
|V(G)|-1
for all
i\in V(G)
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b) By
[
15
, Theorem 1.7]
(or also
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
),
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is a squarefree polymatroidal ideal. By Herzog and Hibi
[
26
, Theorem 4.2]
(see also
[
10
, Corollary 3]
) the only squarefree Cohen-Macaulay polymatroidal ideals are the squarefree principal ideals and the squarefree Veronese ideals
\mathfrak{m}^{[s]}
for
1\leq s\leq n
. Then, our assumption implies that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is either a squarefree principal ideal, or a squarefree Veronese ideal generated in degree
2\nu(G)
.

Let
k=\nu(G)
. We claim that
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and
I(G)^{[k]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2k]}
.

To this end, we first show that any variable divides some generator of
I(G)^{[k]}
. Up to relabeling, we may assume that
M=\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}
, with
e_{i}=\{2i-1,2i\}
for all
i
, is a
k
-matching of
G
. If
2k=n
, the assertion is proved. Suppose
2k<n
. Thus it remains to show that
x_{v}
divides some generator of
I(G)^{[k]}
for all
2k<v\leq n
. There exists
w\in V(G)
with
\{v,w\}\in E(G)
. We have
w\in V(M)
, otherwise
M\cup\{\{v,w\}\}
would be a
(k+1)
-matching of
G
, which is absurd because
\nu(G)=k
. Thus
w\in e_{i}
for some
i
. Hence
M^{\prime}=(M\setminus e_{i})\cup\{\{v,w\}\}
is a
k
-matching of
G
, and the generator of
I(G)^{[k]}
corresponding to this matching is divided by
x_{v}
.

Now, if
2k=n
then
I(G)^{[k]}=(x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n})=\mathfrak{m}^{[n]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2k]}
and
k=\frac{n}{2}=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Suppose now
2k<n
. Then, since all variables divide some generator of
I(G)^{[k]}
, it follows that
I(G)^{[k]}
is not principal. Thus, by the first paragraph of the proof,
I(G)^{[k]}
is the squarefree Veronese ideal
\mathfrak{m}^{[2k]}
of degree
2k
in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. It remains to show that
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Let
K_{n}
be the complete graph on
n
vertices. Then
I(G)\subseteq I(K_{n})
and
I(G)^{[k]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2k]}=I(K_{n})^{[k]}
. Thus,
[
19
, Proposition 1.3]
implies that
I(G)^{[\ell]}=I(K_{n})^{[\ell]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2\ell]}
for all
\ell\geq k
. Since
k=\nu(G)
, we have
I(G)^{[\ell]}=0
for all
\ell>k
. Hence
\mathfrak{m}^{[2\ell]}=0
for all
\ell>k
, and so
k=\lfloor\frac{\nu(\mathfrak{m})}{2}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a) Conversely, if
\nu(G)=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2\nu(G)]}
, then
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is Cohen-Macaulay by the result of Herzog and Hibi
[
26
, Theorem 4.2]
.

(b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) Let
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Then either
n=2k
or
n=2k+1
. Depending on these cases, we see that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2k]}
if and only if statement (c) holds.

∎

2.
Graphs with perfect matchings and very well-covered graphs

To study our question on Cohen-Macaulay matching powers and taking Theorem
1.8
into account, it makes sense to consider the family of graphs which have a perfect matching. This is our focus in this section. It turns out that the Cohen-Macaulayness of the
(\nu(G)-1)
th matching power for this class of graphs is crucial in order to get the answer for all matching powers.
Theorem
2.2
shows this fact. In order to prove our result we use the Hilbert-Burch Theorem and the next elementary result of graph theory.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
non-isolated vertices. Then

|E(G)|\geq n-1,

and equality holds if and only if
G
is a tree.

We now state and prove our main result in the section.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with a perfect matching of size
n\geq 2
and such that
\dim S/I(G)^{[n-1]}=2n-2
. Then, the following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, for all
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)=n
.

(b)

G
is a Cohen-Macaulay forest.

Proof.

The implication (b)
\Rightarrow
(a) is a particular case of
[
13
, Corollary 3.6]
.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b) First we prove the assertion for the case that
G
is a connected graph.
By assumption on the dimension we get
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[n-1]}=2
. Thus,
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is a perfect ideal of grade 2, and by the Hilbert-Burch Theorem
[
4
, Theorem 1.4.17]
, the minimal (multi)graded free resolution of
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is of the form

\mathbb{F}\ \ :\ \ 0\rightarrow S^{m-1}\xrightarrow{X}S^{m}\rightarrow I(G)^{[n-1]}\rightarrow 0,

where
m
is the minimal number of generators of
I(G)^{[n-1]}
, the matrix
X
represents the homomorphism
S^{m-1}\rightarrow S^{m}
, and
I_{m-1}(X)=I(G)^{[n-1]}
. Here, for an integer
t
and a matrix
A
, we denote by
I_{t}(A)
the ideal generated by all
t
-minors of
A
.

We claim that
m\geq 2n-1
. To this end, we show that there is an injective map

\Phi\ :\ E(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[n-1]}).

By Lemma
2.1
,
|E(G)|\geq|V(G)|-1=2n-1
. Thus if we show the existence of the map
\Phi
, then
m\geq|E(G)|\geq 2n-1
as desired.

Let
{\bf x}=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
and
{\bf y}=y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n}
.
We define
\Phi
as follows:

(i)

\displaystyle\Phi(\{x_{i},y_{i}\})\

\displaystyle=\ ({\bf xy})/(x_{i}y_{i})\ =\ \,\prod_{\ell\neq i}(x_{\ell}y_{\ell})\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[n-1]}),

(ii)

\displaystyle\Phi(\{x_{i},y_{j}\})\

\displaystyle=\ ({\bf xy})/(y_{i}x_{j})\ =\ (x_{i}y_{j})\prod_{\ell\neq i,j}(x_{\ell}y_{\ell})\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[n-1]}),

(iii)

\displaystyle\Phi(\{x_{i},x_{j}\})\

\displaystyle=\ ({\bf xy})/(y_{i}y_{j})\ =\ (x_{i}x_{j})\prod_{\ell\neq i,j}(x_{\ell}y_{\ell})\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[n-1]}),

(iv)

\displaystyle\Phi(\{y_{i},y_{j}\})\

\displaystyle=\ ({\bf xy})/(x_{i}x_{j})\ =\ (y_{i}y_{j})\prod_{\ell\neq i,j}(x_{\ell}y_{\ell})\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[n-1]}).

It is clear that the above monomials are pairwise distinct. Hence
\Phi
is injective.

Since
m\geq 2n-1
and each non-zero entry of
X
is an homogeneous element of degree at least one, we see that each non-zero
(m-1)
-minor of
X
has degree at least
m-1\geq 2n-2
. Since
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is equigenerated in degree
2(n-1)
, we then see that the equality
I_{m-1}(X)=I(G)^{[n-1]}
implies that
m=2n-1
and that each entry of
X
is linear (that is, of degree one). By the inequalities
m\geq|E(G)|\geq 2n-1
we obtain
|E(G)|=2n-1
. So, Lemma
2.1
implies that
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay tree.

Now, suppose that
G
has connected components
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
. We show that each
G_{i}
is a tree. Since
G
has a perfect matching, each
G_{i}
has a perfect matching. Let
|V(G_{i})|=2n_{i}
for all
i
.
We fix an integer
1\leq i\leq r
. If
n_{i}=1
, then
G_{i}
is a tree, and we are done. So we may assume that
n_{i}\geq 2
.
Set
J=\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{r}I(G_{j})^{[n_{j}]}
. Then
J=(u)
for some monomial
u
of degree
2n-2n_{i}
and we have the inclusion
uI(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}\subseteq I(G)^{[n-1]}
. Since
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is Cohen-Macaulay with
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[n-1]}=2
, we conclude that
I(G)^{[n-1]}=\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}P_{\ell}
, where each
P_{\ell}
is a monomial prime ideal of height
2
. If
u\in P_{\ell}
for all
\ell
, then
u\in I(G)^{[n-1]}
. This is not possible, since
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is generated in degree
2n-2
and
\deg u=2n-2n_{i}<2n-2
. So
u\notin P_{\ell}
for some
\ell
. Since
uI(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}\subseteq P_{\ell}
, we obtain
I(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}\subseteq P_{\ell}
. This shows that

\textup{height}\,I(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}\leq 2
,
which implies that

\dim S_{i}/I(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}\geq 2n_{i}-2
, where
S_{i}=K[V(G_{i})]
. Since
n_{i}\geq 2
, by Lemma
2.4
it follows that
\dim S_{i}/I(G_{i})^{[n_{i}-1]}=2n_{i}-2
. Moreover, by Corollary
1.6
we conclude that
I(G_{i})^{[k]}

is Cohen-Macaulay for all
1\leq k\leq n_{i}
. Therefore, by the first part of the proof on the connected case, it follows that
G_{i}
is a Cohen-Macaulay tree. Hence,
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay forest.

∎

In the proof of Theorem
2.2
it is shown that for a connected graph
G
with a perfect matching such that
S/I(G)^{[n-1]}
is Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
2n-2
, the Hilbert-Burch matrix
X
has only variables as non-zero entries. This gives the following

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with a perfect matching of size
n
such that
S/I(G)^{[n-1]}
is Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
2n-2
. Then
I(G)^{[n-1]}
has a linear resolution.

The dimension of
S/I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}
which is referred in the statement of Theorem
2.2
could get only two values as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a graph with a perfect matching on
2n
vertices. Then

2k-1\leq\dim S/I(G)^{[k]}\leq n+k-1,

for all
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)=n
.
In particular, if
n\geq 2
, then

\dim S/I(G)^{[n-1]}\in\{2n-3,2n-2\}.

Proof.

Let
M=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be a perfect matching of
G
with
e_{i}=\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
for all
i
. For each non-empty
subset
F\subseteq[n]
, we set
u_{F}=\prod_{i\in F}(x_{i}y_{i})
. Let
1\leq k\leq n
be an integer and let
J=(u_{F}:F\subseteq[n],\,|F|=k)
.
Since
J\subseteq I(G)^{[k]}
, to prove the right-hand side inequality, it is enough to show that
\textup{height}\,J\geq n-k+1
. If this was not the case, there would exists a monomial prime ideal
P
such that
J\subseteq P
and

\textup{height}\,P\leq n-k
. Set
A=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\in P\textup{ or }y_{i}\in P\}
. Without loss of generality assume that
A\subseteq[n-k]
. Then
u_{F}\notin P
for
F=[n]\setminus[n-k]
, which is a contradiction. Finally, by
[
19
, Proposition 1.1]
we have
\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[k]}\geq 2k-1
.

∎

Remark 2.5
.

The inequality
2n-3\leq\dim S/I(G)^{[n-1]}
in the previous lemma, can also be obtained as follows. Recall that the
big cosize
of a monomial ideal
J\subset S
with minimal generating set
\mathcal{G}(J)=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
, is denoted by
\textup{big-cosize}\,J
, and it is defined as the smallest integer
s
such that

\textup{lcm}(u_{j_{1}},u_{j_{2}},\dots,u_{j_{s}})\ =\ \textup{lcm}(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m}),

for all integers
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{s}\leq m
. It is shown in
[
2
, Theorem 3.3(a)]
that
\textup{proj\,dim}\,S/J\leq\textup{big-cosize}\,J
. Notice that
I(G)^{[n-1]}
is a monomial ideal equigenerated in degree
2n-2
in the polynomial ring
S
having
2n
variables. From this observation it follows immediately that
\textup{big-cosize}\,I(G)^{[n-1]}\leq 3
. Hence
\textup{proj\,dim}\,S/I(G)^{[n-1]}\leq 3
. Then, the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula implies that
2n-3\leq\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[n-1]}\leq\dim S/I(G)^{[n-1]}
, as desired.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
G
be a Cohen-Macaulay very well-covered graph. Then,

\dim S/I(G)^{[k]}=|V(G)|/2+k-1\,\,\,\textit{for all}\,\,\,\,1\leq k\leq\nu(G).

Proof.

We have
|V(G)|=2n
and
\nu(G)=n
, for some
n\geq 1
. By Lemma
2.4
, the result follows, once we show that
\dim S/I(G)^{[k]}\geq n+k-1
for all

\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[k]}\leq n-k+1
for all

. Consider the prime ideal
P=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-k+1})
for all
1\leq k\leq n

It is enough to show that
I(G)^{[k]}\subseteq P
.
Suppose the contrary. Then we could find a monomial generator
u\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{[k]})
which is not divided by any of the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-k+1}
. Such an element

, if it exists, would be a squarefree monomial of degree

in the set of variables

\{y_{i}:\ 1\leq i\leq n-k+1\}\cup\{x_{j},y_{j}:\ n-k+2\leq j\leq n\}.

Note that none of the variables

, with
1\leq i\leq n-k+1
, can divide

, because by Theorem

(iii) any edge containing
y_{i}
must contain also a vertex from the set

\{x_{i}:\ 1\leq i\leq n-k+1\},

and then the corresponding variable

would divide

, which is forbidden. Hence,
u
would be a squarefree monomial in the set of variables

This would imply that

.

3.
Chordal graphs and Cameron-Walker graphs

We characterize chordal graphs and Cameron-Walker graphs whose matching powers of edge ideals are all Cohen-Macaulay. For chordal graphs we use a result by Herzog, Hibi and Zheng
[
28
]
which characterizes the Cohen-Macaulay chordal graphs. A
clique
of a graph
G
is a subset
F\subseteq V(G)
such that the induced subgraph of
G
on
F
is a complete graph. The
clique complex
of
G

is the simplicial complex on the vertex set
V(G)
whose faces are the cliques of
G
, and we denote it by
\Delta(G)
. A
free vertex
of
\Delta(G)
is a vertex which belongs to precisely one facet of
\Delta(G)
.
The main theorem in
[
28
]
shows that
G
is a Cohen-Macaulay chordal graph if and only if
V(G)
is the disjoint union of the facets of
\Delta(G)
which admit a free vertex.

We set
r_{G}
to be the number of facets of
\Delta(G)
which admit a free vertex.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a chordal graph.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

I(G)^{[k]}
is Cohen-Macaulay, for all
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
.

(b)

G
is a Cohen-Macaulay forest or a complete graph.

For the proof of this theorem, we need some auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and let
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be the maximal homogeneous ideal. Then
\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[k]}=\dim S/\mathfrak{m}^{[k]}=k-1
for all
1\leq k\leq n
.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathfrak{m}^{[k]}
is the so-called squarefree Veronese ideal in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
generated in degree
k
. This ideal is also denoted by
I_{n,k}
in
[
10
]
. The assertion follows from
[
10
, Lemma 2]
and the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
R=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
,
T=K[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
and
S=R\otimes_{K}T=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
. Let
I_{1}\subset R
be the edge ideal of the complete graph on the vertex set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
,
I_{2}\subset T
be the edge ideal of the complete graph on the vertex set
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
, and set
I(G)=I_{1}+I_{2}
. Suppose that
n+m\geq 5
. Then, there exists an integer
k\in\{\nu(G)-1,\nu(G)\}
such that
I(G)^{[k]}
is not Cohen-Macaulay.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\mathfrak{n}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
. Then
I_{1}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2]}
and
I_{2}=\mathfrak{n}^{[2]}
.

If at least one of
n
and
m
is an odd integer, say
n
, then
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is not Cohen-Macaulay. Indeed, let
x_{i}\in V(K_{m})
. Then neither
G
nor
G\setminus\{x_{i}\}
have perfect matchings, because
K_{n}
is a component of both graphs with an odd number of vertices. Then, by Theorem
1.8
(c),
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is not Cohen-Macaulay.

Suppose now that both
n
and
m
are even integers, say
n=2r
and
m=2t
. Then
n+m\geq 6
and we have

I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}=\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}+\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]},

(5)

with the convention that
J^{[0]}=S
for a monomial ideal
J\subset S
. We claim that (
5
) is a Betti splitting. Indeed,

(\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]})\cap(\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]})=\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}.

Since
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
are monomial ideals in disjoint sets of variables, it is clear that the inclusion maps
\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}
are
Tor
-vanishing, if and only if, the inclusion maps
\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}\rightarrow\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}
are
Tor
-vanishing, which is clearly the case. Therefore, equation (
1
) yields

\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}\ =\ \min\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\displaystyle\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]},\,\,\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}-1\end{subarray}\right\}.

(6)

Again, because
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
are monomial ideals in disjoint sets of variables, by using Lemma
3.2
, we obtain that

\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}=\begin{cases}\textup{depth\,}R/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}+\textup{depth\,}T/\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}+1=n+m-3&\textup{if}\ t>1,\\
\textup{depth\,}R/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}+\textup{depth\,}T=n+m-1&\textup{if}\ t=1.\end{cases}

Similarly,

\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}=\begin{cases}n+m-3&\textup{if}\ r>1,\\
n+m-1&\textup{if}\ r=1.\end{cases}

and

\textup{depth\,}S/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}\mathfrak{n}^{[2t]}=\textup{depth\,}R/\mathfrak{m}^{[2r]}+\textup{depth\,}T/\mathfrak{n}^{[2s]}+1=n+m-1.

Since
n+m\geq 6
, we have
r+t\geq 3
. Thus, at least one of the integers
r
or
t
is strictly bigger than one, and so, the previous three formulas combined with (
6
) yield that

\textup{depth\,}S/I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}=n+m-3.

We claim that
\dim S/I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}=n+m-2
, which finishes the proof. For this aim, it is enough to show that
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}=2
. We claim that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}
is contained in
(x_{1},y_{1})
. This is clear, as the following computation shows

I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}\ =\ \begin{cases}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}+(y_{1}\cdots y_{m})\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}&\textup{if}\ r>1\ \textup{and}\ t>1,\\
(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})+(y_{1}y_{2})\mathfrak{m}^{[2r-2]}&\textup{if}\ r>1\ \textup{and}\ t=1,\\
(x_{1}x_{2})\mathfrak{n}^{[2t-2]}+(y_{1}\cdots y_{m})&\textup{if}\ r=1\ \textup{and}\ t>1.\end{cases}

Since at least one of
r
and
t
is strictly bigger than one, the previous computation also shows that no principal monomial prime ideal can be an associated prime of
I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}
. Hence
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}>1
. Consequently
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\textup{Ass}\,I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}
. This shows that
\textup{height}\,I(G)^{[\nu(G)-1]}=2
, which concludes the proof.

∎

Next, we study the class of Cameron-Walker graphs. We recall some concepts which are used in the sequel. An
induced matching
of
G
is a matching
M
of
G
such that for any two edges
e=\{a,b\}
and
e^{\prime}=\{c,d\}
belonging to
M
the induced subgraph of
G
on
\{a,b,c,d\}
consists of two disjoint edges
e
and
e^{\prime}
. We denote by
\textup{im}(G)
the maximum size of an induced matching of
G
.

A graph
G
is called a
Cameron-Walker graph
if
G
is connected and
\nu(G)=\textup{im}(G)
. Cameron-Walker graphs are classified in
[
5
, Theorem 1]
(see also
[
29
]
), where it is shown that
G
is a Cameron-Walker graph if and only if
G
is either a star graph, or a star triangle, or it consists of a connected bipartite graph with vertex partition
[n]\sqcup[m]
such that there is at least one leaf edge attached to each vertex
i\in[n]
and that there may be possibly some pendant triangles attached to each vertex
j\in[m]
. By a star triangle we mean a graph joining some triangles at one common vertex.

The following result is crucial for the characterization of Cameron-Walker graphs whose all matching powers are Cohen-Macaulay.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be a Cameron-Walker graph such that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is Cohen-Macaulay. Then
G
is either a star triangle or a bipartite graph.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is neither a star triangle nor a bipartite graph. Then
G
is a graph consisting of a connected bipartite graph with vertex partition
[n]\sqcup[m]
such that there is at least one leaf attached to each vertex in
[n]
and that there exists at least one pendant triangle
T
attached to some vertex in
[m]
. Suppose that
G
has a perfect matching. Since
\nu(G)=\textup{im}(G)
, we conclude that
G
has a perfect matching which is an induced matching
M
. Thus any vertex of the triangle
T
is an endpoint of an edge in the matching
M
, which is not possible since
M
is an induced matching of
G
. So,
G
does not have a perfect matching. Consider some vertex
i\in[n]
. Then the pendant vertices of
G
which are adjacent to
i
are isolated vertices in the graph
G\setminus\{i\}
. Since there exists at least one of them, we conclude that
G\setminus\{i\}
does not have a perfect matching. So, it follows from Theorem
1.8
that
I(G)^{[\nu(G)]}
is not Cohen-Macaulay.
