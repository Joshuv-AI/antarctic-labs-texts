Existence, scaling, and spectral gap for travelling fronts in the 2D renormalized Allen–Cahn equation
Gideon Chiusole, Christian Kuehn

Abstract
We study the deterministic skeleton of the renormalized stochastic Allen–Cahn equation in spatial dimension 2 2 . For all sufficiently small regularization parameters δ > 0 \delta>0 , we construct monotone travelling wave front solutions connecting the renormalized equilibria, derive a small- δ \delta asymptotic description of their profile and speed, and identify the leading-order contributions. Linearizing about the wave and working in a naturally chosen weighted space, we show that there exists a spectral gap between the symmetry induced eigenvalue 0 0 and the rest of the spectrum. The spectral gap grows linearly in the renormalization constant as δ ↓ 0 \delta\downarrow 0 .

traveling wave fronts,

spectral gap,
Schrödinger operators,

1  Introduction

This paper is part of a larger effort to understand the stability of patterns in singular stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). As a model problem, we consider the stochastic Allen–Cahn equation with additive spacetime white noise
\xi
, which is formally given by

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u\underbrace{-u(u-\alpha)(u-1)}_{=:f(u)}+\sigma\xi,\quad\alpha\in(0,1/2),\sigma>0,

(1.1)

where
u=u(t,\bx)
is (formally) an unknown time-dependent random scalar field on the full space
\bbR^{d}
which we split as
\bx=(x_{1},x_{\perp})
into a longitudinal direction
x_{1}\in\bbR
and a transversal direction
x_{\perp}\in\bbR^{d-1}
.
1
1

1

The content of this paper concerns the case of spatial dimension
d=2
, but we keep
d
general in the following preliminary discussion. While the choice of
\bbR
in the longitudinal direction is fixed by the shape of the wave profile, the choice of
\bbR^{d-1}
in the transversal direction is rather arbitrary. There are other reasonable choices, like the torus
\bbT^{d-1}
. As far as the results of this paper are concerned, the choice of transversal direction is irrelevant though.

The following two facts about this equation are well known:

1.

The deterministic version of (
1.1
), given by formally setting
\sigma=0
, admits planar monotone travelling wave front solutions connecting the two locally asymptotically stable homogeneous equilibria
0
and
1
of the non-linearity
f
. That is, there exists a smooth function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with

\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}\Phi(x)=1,\quad\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}\Phi(x)=0,\quad\Phi^{\prime}(x)<0\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.2)

and
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u(t,\bx)=\Phi(x_{1}-st)
is a solution of (
1.1
) with
\sigma=0
- see e.g.
[
21
, Sec. 8]
,
[
12
, Sec. 2.3.3.2]
. In the comoving frame
(\eta,x_{\perp}):=(x_{1}-st,x_{\perp})\in\bbR^{d}
, the planar wave profile
(\eta,x_{\perp})\mapsto\Phi(\langle\eta,\cdot\rangle)
is a stationary solution, and the linearization around it is given by

\underline{\sL}=\Delta+s\partial_{\eta}+f^{\prime}(\Phi).

(1.3)

It can be split into a longitudinal part and a transversal part as

\underline{\sL}=\sL\otimes I+I\otimes\Delta_{\perp},\quad\text{with}\quad\sL=\partial_{\eta}^{2}+s\partial_{\eta}+f^{\prime}(\Phi),\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{\perp}=\sum_{i=2}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}.

(1.4)

The spectral analysis of the operator
\underline{\sL}
can thus be reduced to the analysis of the one-dimensional operator
\sL
. By abstract principles, due to the translational symmetry of the equation in the direction of propagation
x_{1}
, the operator
\sL
has a neutral mode given by
\partial_{\eta}\Phi
, which spans the kernel of
\sL
. However, modulo this neutral mode, the travelling wave solutions described above are stable (in an appropriate sense - see
[
13
, Thm. 1.2]
). Crucial for the proof of this statement is the existence of a spectral gap between
0
and the rest of the spectrum of
\sL
(see
[
13
, Hyp. 1.1]
). That is, there exists
\beta>0
such that

\sigma(\sL)\cap\left\{z\in\bbC:\Real(z)\geq-\beta\right\}=\{0\}.

(1.5)

2.

In dimension
d\geq 2
, the stochastic Allen–Cahn Equation (
1.1
) is ill-posed due to the interplay of the irregularity of the white noise
\xi
and the non-linearity
f
. However, in dimension
d\leq 3
it can be renormalized to restore well-posedness. More specifically, let
\rho_{\delta}
be a mollifier at scale
\delta>0
and let
\xi_{\delta}=\rho_{\delta}\ast\xi
denote the regularization of the noise at scale
\delta
via convolution. Then, for each
d\in\{2,3\}
, there exists a polynomial
g_{\delta}
, with coefficients depending on the regularization scale
\delta>0
, such that solutions to the renormalized equation

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-u(u-\alpha)(u-1)+g_{\delta,\sigma}(u)+\sigma\xi_{\delta},

(1.6)

converge as
\delta\downarrow 0
to a limit which is independent of the choice of mollifier
\rho_{\delta}
- see
[
4
,
9
]
. Solutions to Equation (
1.1
) should then be understood as the limit of the solutions to the renormalized equation. In the case of dimension
d=2
, the renormalization polynomial
g_{\delta}
can be obtained via Wick renormalization and is given by

g_{\delta}(u)=3C_{\delta,\sigma}u-(1+\alpha)C_{\delta,\sigma},

(1.7)

where
C_{\delta,\sigma}\simeq\sigma^{2}\log(\delta^{-1})
as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

These two facts naturally lead us to study the deterministic skeleton

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u+f(u)+g_{\delta,\sigma}(u)=\partial_{x}^{2}u-u^{3}+(1+\alpha)u^{2}+(3C_{\delta,\sigma}-\alpha)u-(1+\alpha)C_{\delta,\sigma},

(1.8)

of the renormalized Equation (
1.6
) in the direction of propagation, the existence of travelling wave solutions therein, and the existence a spectral gap for the associated linearization around these waves. With a view
[
15
,
6
]
towards stability results for the stochastic problem (
1.6
) in the renormalized limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, we are not only interested in the qualitative properties of the wave and spectral gap, but also quantitative dependence in terms of the regularization parameter
\delta>0
and
\sigma>0
.

Strategy, organization and main results

In order to study the singular limit
\delta\downarrow 0
and hence
C_{\delta,\sigma}\rightarrow\infty
, we begin by extracting the relevant scales of the travelling wave problem of Equation (
1.8
), which turn out to be
\varepsilon:=C_{\delta,\sigma}^{-1/2}
and its powers. In particular, in Section
2
we compute an asymptotic expansion of the renormalized equilibria
(z^{-}_{\varepsilon},z^{0}_{\varepsilon},z^{+}_{\varepsilon})
in Lemma
2.1
and show that there exists a travelling wave solution
({\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}},{s^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}})
of Equation (
1.8
) connecting these equilibria satisfying

\sup_{x\in\bbR}|{\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(x)|\simeq\varepsilon^{-2},\quad{s^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}\simeq\varepsilon^{2},

(1.9)

in Theorem
2.2
. From there, in Section
3
, we study the spectral problem as a meromorphic perturbation problem around
\varepsilon=0
. We introduce the
\varepsilon
-dependent linearized operator
{\sL^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
around the renormalized wave
({\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}},{s^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}})
in an
\varepsilon
-dependent comoving frame on an appropriately weighted space and show in Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.6
that it has a spectral gap
\beta(\varepsilon)
, between
0
and the rest of the spectrum, which behaves like

\beta(\varepsilon)\simeq\varepsilon^{-2}=C_{\delta,\sigma}.

(1.10)

The proof is split into two parts - see Figure
1
. In Subsection
3.1
we compute the essential spectrum by conjugating
{\sL^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
to an asymptotically constant operator
{\sM^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
on an unweighted
L^{2}
-space and then decomposing
{\sM^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
into a sum of an asymptotic operator
\sM^{\infty}_{\varepsilon}
and a relatively compact perturbation
\sK
. By Weyl’s theorem, the contribution of
\sK
does not affect the essential spectrum of
\sM^{\infty}_{\varepsilon}
, which can be computed via Fredholm theory and the existence or absence of an exponential dichotomy for an ODE associated to
\sM^{\infty}_{\varepsilon}
. In Subsection
3.2
we compute the discrete spectrum. We firstly reduce the spectral gap problem for the Sturm–Liouville operator
{\sL^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
on a weighted space to a problem for a Schrödinger operator
{\sH^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
, by using the strong decay properties of Schrödinger eigenfunctions in classically forbidden regions (Agmon decay). We then extend the domain of the parameter
\varepsilon
to a small punctured ball around
0
, leading to a meromorphic perturbation problem. Finally, we use dilation to reduce further to a regular perturbation problem of Schrödinger operators.

2  Renormalized equilibria and waves

Throughout the paper, we will work around the limiting case
C_{\delta,\sigma}=\infty
. In the later sections, we want to consider this as a meromorphic perturbation problem and thus immediately switch scales to a small parameter
\varepsilon:=C_{\delta,\sigma}^{-1/2}
. The reason for the choice of exponent will become apparent in the following section. The non-linearity in (
1.8
) thus takes the form

{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(u):=-u^{3}+(1+\alpha)u^{2}+(3\varepsilon^{-2}-\alpha)u-(1+\alpha)\varepsilon^{-2},

(2.1)

where
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
is fixed throughout the paper and thus suppressed in the notation. In this section we study the roots of
{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
in terms of
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in order to compute the asymptotic states of the travelling wave solutions of (
1.8
) which will be derived in Theorem
2.2
. Because in Section
3.2
we will rely on holomorphic perturbation theory, we will immediately allow
\varepsilon\in\bbC
in the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and holomorphic functions
z^{-},z^{0},z^{+}:B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0})\rightarrow\bbC
such that

{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(z^{-}_{\varepsilon})={f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(z^{0}_{\varepsilon})={f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(z^{+}_{\varepsilon})=0,\quad\forall\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0}),

(2.2)

which are real-valued for
\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0})\cap(0,\infty)
and satisfy

z^{-}_{\varepsilon}<z^{0}_{\varepsilon}<z^{+}_{\varepsilon},\quad\text{for }\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0})\cap(0,\infty).

(2.3)

The leading parts of their Laurent series around
\varepsilon=0
are

\displaystyle z^{0}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\alpha}{3}+\calO(\varepsilon^{2}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle z^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{3}\varepsilon^{-1}+\frac{1+\alpha}{3}+\calO(\varepsilon).

(2.5)

In particular, the expansion of
z^{0}_{\varepsilon}
has no linear contribution.

Proof.

In order to see that
{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
has three distinct real solutions for
\varepsilon
small enough and real, recall that
{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
can be brought into the standard form of a depressed cubic
{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(z)=y^{3}+b_{1}y+b_{0}
via the variable/parameter change

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle:=-\left(z-\frac{1+\alpha}{3}\right),

(2.6)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=-3\varepsilon^{-2}+\frac{3\alpha-(1+\alpha)^{2}}{3},

(2.7)

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2(1+\alpha)^{3}-9\alpha(1+\alpha)}{27},

(2.8)

see
[
5
, Sec. 14.6, p. 611]

\varepsilon_{0}^{(1)}>0
small enough, the discriminant
\Disc(p_{\varepsilon}(y))=-4b_{1}^{3}-27b_{0}^{2}

. Hence by
[
3

has three distinct complex solutions for every
\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0}^{(1)})
and three distinct real solutions for every
\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0}^{(1)})\cap(0,\infty)

{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}
.

For the bounded solution branch
\varepsilon\mapsto z^{0}_{\varepsilon}
, consider the polynomial function

g^{0}_{\varepsilon}(z):=\varepsilon^{2}{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(z)=-\varepsilon^{2}z^{3}+\varepsilon^{2}(1+\alpha)z^{2}+(3-\varepsilon^{2}\alpha)z-(1+\alpha),

(2.9)

for
\varepsilon\in\bbC
and

and note that for
\varepsilon=0

there exists

\varepsilon

\varepsilon=0

z^{0}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1+\alpha}{3}+\calO(\varepsilon^{2}).

(2.10)

Since the solutions of

, the restriction of

to

For the two unbounded branches

(2.11)

For
\varepsilon=0

\varepsilon_{0}^{(3)}>0

\tilde{z}_{1},\tilde{z}_{2},\tilde{z}_{3}:B(\varepsilon_{0}^{(3)})\rightarrow\bbC

z^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}=\pm\sqrt{3}\varepsilon^{-1}+c_{0}\varepsilon^{0}+\calO(\varepsilon).

(2.12)

To obtain the coefficient at order

Finally, set
\varepsilon_{0}:=\min\{\varepsilon_{0}^{(1)},\varepsilon_{0}^{(2)},\varepsilon_{0}^{(3)}\}

Recall that the unrenormalized Allen–Cahn equation admits travelling wave solutions in dimension
1
(and thus any dimension). More precisely, for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u-u(u-\alpha)(u-1)

{\overline{\Phi}}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}x\right)\right)=\left(1+\exp\left(-x/\sqrt{2}\right)\right)^{-1},\quad{\overline{s}_{\alpha}}=\sqrt{2}\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right).

see e.g.
[
21

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
z^{-}_{\varepsilon},z^{0}_{\varepsilon},z^{+}_{\varepsilon}
be as in Lemma
2.1
and let
\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0})\cap(0,\infty)

\fa_{\varepsilon}:=z^{+}_{\varepsilon}-z^{-}_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{12}\varepsilon^{-1}+\calO(\varepsilon).

Then Equation (

{\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(x)=\fa_{\varepsilon}{\overline{\Phi}}(\fa_{\varepsilon}x)+z^{-}_{\varepsilon},\quad{s^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}=\calO(\varepsilon^{2}),

\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}{\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(x)=z^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}

Proof.

We will transform Equation (
1.8

2.14

\varepsilon\in B^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{0})\cap(0,\infty)
and

\displaystyle{\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{z^{0}_{\varepsilon}-z^{-}_{\varepsilon}}{z^{+}_{\varepsilon}-z^{-}_{\varepsilon}}

(2.18)

\displaystyle{\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\fa_{\varepsilon}^{-3}\left({f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}(\fa_{\varepsilon}x+z^{-}_{\varepsilon})\right).

(2.19)

Notice that
{\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}}
is a polynomial of order

with leading coefficient

which has roots at

, and

(corresponding to the roots

). Therefore

{\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}}(u)=-u(u-{\overline{\alpha}_{\varepsilon}})(u-1),

which has the form of Equation (

(2.21)

define

(2.22)

and observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\fa_{\varepsilon}^{3}(\partial_{t}v)(\fa_{\varepsilon}^{2}t,\fa_{\varepsilon}x)

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\fa_{\varepsilon}^{3}(\partial_{x}^{2}v)(\fa_{\varepsilon}^{2}t,\fa_{\varepsilon}x)+\fa_{\varepsilon}^{3}{\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}}(v(\fa_{\varepsilon}^{2}t,\fa_{\varepsilon}x))

(2.24)

\displaystyle=\fa_{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}^{2}(v(\fa_{\varepsilon}^{2}t,\fa_{\varepsilon}x)+z^{-}_{\varepsilon})+\fa_{\varepsilon}^{3}{\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}}(v(\fa_{\varepsilon}^{2}t,\fa_{\varepsilon}x))

3  Spectral analysis

We now turn to the analysis of the spectrum of the family of ordinary differential operators, formally given by

{\sL^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u+{s^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{x}u+{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}({\Phi^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}})u.

(3.1)

{f^{\Ren}_{\varepsilon}}

) and (
2.17

\bbR^{2}

7

w_{\beta}(x):=\langle x\rangle^{-\beta}=(1+|x|^{2})^{-\beta/2},\quad\beta>2,

Its product with the same weight in the transversal direction

Decomposition of the spectrum

We follow the definition of
[
14
, Ch. IV, §5]
, according to which a closed linear operator
\sL
on a Banach space

is Fredholm if its range is closed and both its kernel and cokernel are finite-dimensional. We denote the Fredholm index by
\ind(\cdot)
and split the spectrum of
\sL
into the following three parts:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\not F}(\sL)

\displaystyle:=\{\lambda\in\bbC:\sL-\lambda I\text{ is not Fredholm}\},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\sL)

\displaystyle:=\{\lambda\in\bbC:\sL-\lambda I\text{ is Fredholm and }\ind(\sL-\lambda I)\neq k\},\quad k\geq 1,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(\sL)

\displaystyle:=\{\lambda\in\bbC:\sL-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible, Fredholm and }\ind(\sL-\lambda I)=0\}.

(3.15)

The three sets are clearly disjoint. To see that they comprise the entire spectrum of
\sL
, recall that invertibility of a closed linear operator implies it being Fredholm with index
0
. Multiple (in general) non-equivalent definitions of the essential spectrum exist in the literature - see
[

,
11
]
. We follow the definition of what is called the

essential spectrum
\sigma_{e5}
in
[
11
, Sec. 2]
,

\sigma_{\ess}(\sL):=\sigma_{\not F}(\sL)\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\sigma_{k}(\sL),

(3.16)

which is the standard choice in the analysis of travelling waves - see e.g.
[
12
, Def. 2.2.3]
,
[
20
, Def. 3.2]
. This definition has the advantage that 1) Weyl’s theorem
[
14
, Thm. VI.5.35.]
on the invariance of the essential spectrum under relatively compact perturbations holds true,
2
2

2

[
14
, Thm. IV.5.26.]
ensures that relatively compact perturbations preserve not only the Fredholm property, but also the Fredholm index of a closed linear operator.
and 2) with this definition
\sigma_{\ess}
is large enough to ensure that the complement is contained in the set of eigenvalues.

1/2
unit ball around
0
, is lower bounded by
1/2
.

Thus, the term in the outer brackets is lower bounded by
1/2
, and hence the entire right-hand side is upper bounded by
2\exp\left(\Real(z)/\sqrt{2}\right)
.

Let
z\in\Sigma^{+}
be arbitrary and recall the definition of
{\overline{\Phi}}
from (
2.15
). Then

\displaystyle|{\overline{\Phi}}(z)-1|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)^{-1}-1\right|=\left|\frac{-\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}{1+\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}\right|

(A.12)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\underbrace{\Bigg|\Bigg(1+\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Bigg)^{-1}\Bigg|}_{={\overline{\Phi}}(z)}

(A.13)

On the one hand, since
{\overline{\Phi}}
is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\calN
, it is bounded on
\Sigma^{+}\cap\{\Real(z)\leq 1\}
. On the other hand, on the set
\Sigma^{+}\cap\{\Real(z)\geq 1\}
, by the reverse triangle inequality

\left|1+\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right|\geq\left|1-\left|\exp\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right|\right|\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\approx 0.51>0.

(A.14)

Hence
{\overline{\Phi}}
is bounded uniformly on all of
\Sigma^{+}
and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\calU
be open,
\varepsilon_{0}\in\calU
, and
b_{0},\ldots,b_{n}:\calU\rightarrow\bbC
be holomorphic such that the polynomial
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(\varepsilon_{0})z^{k}
has
l
simple solutions. Then there exist an open neighborhood
\calU_{0}\subseteq\calU
containing
\varepsilon_{0}
and holomorphic functions
z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}:\calU_{0}\rightarrow\bbC
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\calU_{0}

f_{\varepsilon}(z_{k}(\varepsilon))=0\quad\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,l\}.

(A.15)

Proof.

Label the
l
distinct elements in the set
f^{-1}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(0)
as
\{s_{1}(\varepsilon_{0}),\ldots,s_{l}(\varepsilon_{0})\}
and let
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
be arbitrary. Then the map

f:\calU\times\bbC\rightarrow\bbC;\quad(\varepsilon,z)\mapsto f_{\varepsilon}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(\varepsilon)z^{k}

(A.16)

is holomorphic, satisfies
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}(s_{i}(\varepsilon_{0}))=0
, and, since
s_{i}(\varepsilon_{0})
is a simple root,
\partial_{z}f_{\varepsilon_{0}}(s_{i}(\varepsilon_{0}))\neq 0
. Thus, by the (complex analytic) implicit function theorem
[
23
, App. II, Lem. 3B(f)]
, there exists an open neighborhood
\calU_{0}^{i}\subseteq\calU
of
\varepsilon_{0}
and a unique holomorphic function
z_{i}:\calU_{0}^{i}\rightarrow\bbC
such that

f_{\varepsilon}(z_{i}(\varepsilon))=0\quad\forall\varepsilon\in\calU_{0}^{i}.

(A.17)

A common domain for all
z_{i}
can be obtained by taking
\calU_{0}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{l}\calU_{0}^{i}
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

If
a_{0},a_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\bbR)
, then the differential operator

\sA=\partial_{x}^{2}+a_{1}(x)\partial_{x}+a_{0}(x).

(A.18)

with domain
H^{2}(\bbR)
is a closed linear operator on
L^{2}(\bbR)
with non-empty resolvent set.

Proof.

Conjugating via the Fourier transform to a multiplication operator shows that
(\partial^{2},H^{2}(\bbR))
is a closed linear operator on
L^{2}(\bbR)
. By the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality
[
1
, Thm. 5.2]
, we have

\left\|a_{1}\partial_{x}u+a_{0}u\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\max_{k=0,1}\|a_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}(\varepsilon\|\partial_{x}^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}+C_{\varepsilon,k}\|u\|_{L^{2}})

(A.19)

Thus, choosing
\varepsilon>0
small enough gives a relative bound
\tilde{\varepsilon}<1
(and corresponding
\tilde{C}_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}
) of
a_{1}\partial_{x}+a_{0}
with respect to
\partial^{2}
. Hence by the Kato–Rellich theorem
[
14
, Thm. IV.1.1.]

\sA
is closed on
H^{2}(\bbR)
.

Let
\calR(\partial_{x}^{2},\lambda)
denote the resolvent of
\partial_{x}^{2}
. Since
\sigma(\partial_{x}^{2})=[0,\infty)
, we may choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}<0
large enough such that

\tilde{C}_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\|\calR(\partial_{x}^{2},\lambda_{\varepsilon})\|+\tilde{\varepsilon}\underbrace{\|\partial_{x}^{2}\calR(\partial_{x}^{2},\lambda_{\varepsilon})\|}_{<\infty}<1.

(A.20)

Then by
[
14
, Thm. IV.3.17.]
the resolvent set of
\sA
contains
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
and is thus non-empty.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}(\bbR)
such that
\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{|x|>R}|a(x)|=0
. Then the multiplication operator
M_{a}:H^{1}(\bbR)\rightarrow L^{2}(\bbR);u\mapsto au
is compact.

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\bbN}\subseteq H^{1}(\bbR)
be a bounded sequence and let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. By choice of
a
and because
(u_{n})_{n\in\bbN}
is also bounded in
L^{2}(\bbR)
we may choose
R>0
large enough such that

\int_{|x|>R}|a(x)u_{n}(x)|^{2}dx\leq\sup_{|x|>R}|a(x)|^{2}\underbrace{\int_{|x|>R}|u_{n}(x)|^{2}dx}_{\leq\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\bbR)}^{2}\lesssim 1}<(\varepsilon/2)^{1/2},\quad\forall n\in\bbN.

(A.21)

The restriction
(u_{n}|_{(-R,R)})_{n\in\bbN}
is also a bounded sequence in
W^{1,2}((-R,R))
. By the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem
[
1
, Thm. 6.3.II]
there exists a subsequence
(u_{n_{k}})_{k\in\bbN}
such that the sequence
(u_{n_{k}}|_{(-R,R)})_{k\in\bbN}
is Cauchy. Thus, we may choose
N
large enough such that for every
k,l\geq N

\|M_{a}u_{n_{k}}-M_{a}u_{n_{l}}\|_{L^{2}(\bbR)}\leq\underbrace{\sup_{x\in\bbR}|a(x)|}_{\lesssim 1}\|u_{n_{k}}-u_{n_{l}}\|_{L^{2}((-R,R))}+\|au_{n_{k}}-au_{n_{l}}\|_{L^{2}(\bbR\setminus[-R,R])}<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.

(A.22)

Hence
(M_{a}u_{n_{k}})_{k\in\bbN}\subseteq(M_{a}u_{n})_{n\in\bbN}
is a Cauchy subsequence in
L^{2}(\bbR)
, which thus converges in
L^{2}(\bbR)
.
∎
