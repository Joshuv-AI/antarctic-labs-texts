Temporal associations between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality: a US state-level ecological study
Authors: Kristiaan Proesmans, Adam Edward Lang, Lies Lahousse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSmoking is the leading cause of preventable deaths worldwide. Consequently, smoking reduction interventions have major long-term public health benefits. However, the temporal association of smoking-related mortality remains ill-defined. Therefore, this study describes the lag-response of smoking prevalence reduction on smoking-related mortality.MethodsOur study included smoking prevalence and mortality for the US states. Generalised additive models (GAM) with random intercepts were used to model the cause-specific mortality as a function of the smoking prevalence history. The lag-response of an absolute 5% smoking prevalence reduction was estimated using a distributed lag non-linear model. Covariables were included accounting for demographics, socioeconomic status and general trends.ResultsBased on smoking prevalence data from 8 354 095 people, the relationship between smoking prevalence history and smoking-related mortality rates appeared non-linear. The largest association with mortality (per 100 000 person-years) caused by ischaemic heart disease was observed at 8 years (−3.02 (95% CI −4.31 to −1.73)), 10 years for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (−1.35 (95% CI −1.89 to −0.82)), 11 years for lung cancer (−1.68 (95% CI −2.44 to −0.91)) and for other smoking-related cancers at 20 years (−0.61 (95% CI −0.81 to −0.40)).Notably, COPD mortality showed a rapid decline. Our results estimated an absolute 5% reduction in smoking prevalence from 1999 onwards is associated with a reduced mortality in 2019 by 23.91% (95% CI 15.36% to 32.46%) for ischaemic heart diseases, 30.82% (95% CI 22.81% to 38.83%) for COPD, 25.20% (95% CI 18.85% to 31.55%) for lung cancer and 7.88% (95% CI 5.24% to 10.52%) for other smoking-related cancers.ConclusionImpact and timing of smoking reduction vary across causes of death. Notably, COPD-related deaths decrease rapidly. The largest association of smoking prevalence on COPD and lung cancer mortality occurred after 10 years and 11 years, while for ischaemic heart diseases, it was at 8 years.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, Risk Assessment

Introduction

Smoking is the leading cause of preventable deaths worldwide. Yearly, it is the cause of death for approximately 8 million people.1 Consequently, reducing smoking prevalence is a public health priority. Between 1965 and 2022, specific health and prevention measures have reduced smoking rates by 73% among US adults.2 This reduction prevented many non-communicable disease-related deaths. Despite this reduction, still 11.6% of US adults smoked in 2022.2 Because of current smoking prevalence, the increasing popularity of e-cigarettes, and the link between vaping and smoking initiation becoming increasingly evident, smoking will remain a considerable health challenge for the foreseeable future.3

The main preventable deaths related to smoking are ischaemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer and other smoking-related cancers.4 Nonetheless, the effects of tobacco on these diseases are not immediate. For example, on an individual level, the lag between cigarette smoking and lung cancer development is circa 15–30 years.5 Therefore, current smoking prevalence is likely associated with public health consequences for decades to come. The timing and magnitude of these associations may depend on the evolution of smoking prevalence as well as demographic, socioeconomic and health characteristics.6 This could be defined by the lag-response curve, which shows the population-level temporal association between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality. Currently, the lag-response of smoking remains remarkably ill-defined.7 Several projection models have been used to estimate the future burden of smoking-related mortality, but these were mainly focused on predictive accuracy rather than lag-response.7 8 Knowledge of the lag-response relationships is essential to derive how historical changes in smoking prevalence relate to current smoking-related mortality.7 By focusing on mortality, the combined influence of disease occurrence and survival over time can be captured, providing a broad assessment of the long-term population burden attributable to smoking. This provides insight for policymakers into the magnitude of health returns and the time at which these measures become evident. Therefore, our study aims to describe, based on US nationwide data, the temporal lag-response relationship between smoking prevalence and major causes of smoking-related deaths.

Materials and methods

Data source

The estimated effect of smoking prevalence on smoking-related mortality is based on cross-sectional data of smoking prevalence, demographics and mortality for the 50 states and the District of Columbia.

Smoking prevalence data was obtained from the prevalence and trends data obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS).9 A national state-based system, using telephone interviews, for health surveys to collect detailed information on health-related behaviours, chronic conditions and access to healthcare, reaching more than 100 000 people each year.10 The mortality data were extracted from the CDC WONDER database, a database derived from the death certificates of US residents.11 The population of each state was extracted from the US Census Bureau.12 Demographic variables, including the proportion of adult people older than 65 years of age, and the percentage of black and Hispanic inhabitants, were derived from the Kaiser Family Foundation state health facts.13 Medicaid expenditure was derived from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and the state average income was derived from the Bureau of Economic Analysis.14 15 The inflation was derived from the Consumer Price Index.16

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of our research.

Outcomes and exposure

The outcome of interest was the yearly mortality rate of lung cancer (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10): C34), COPD (ICD-10: J40–J44), ischaemic heart disease (ICD-10: I20–I25) and smoking-related cancer mortality (without lung cancer) (ICD-10: C00–C16, C18–C22, C25, C32–C33, C53, C64, C65, C67, C92.0), from 2015 to 2019. The exposure of interest was the prevalence of people who were active smoking in the specific year of interest and the past 20 years before the outcome year of interest. The smoking prevalence at a specific year was estimated based on the BRFSS interviews that year. This was defined as people who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their life, and at the moment of the interview reported to smoke cigarettes every day or some days. In case an invalid smoking status was reported (refused, do not know or missing), it was considered uninformative and not included. A full description of the data collection, survey design and weighting can be found on the BRFSS website.9 Survey weights were used to ensure representativeness of state populations. Since 2011, the weighting methodology has shifted from poststratification weighting to iterative proportional fitting (raking) weighting.9 17 The BRFSS stability requirements were unmet,18 in 5 of the 1326 state-year-survey smoking prevalence numbers, being the District of Columbia in 1995, Hawaii in 2004, New Jersey in 2019 and Utah in 1995 and 1996. For these state-years mean imputations of the past and consecutive year were used for simplicity.

An absolute sustained reduction of 5% of the smoking prevalence was used as a counterfactual scenario. While this is ambitious, it has been empirically plausible in the past and could be an important benchmark toward a tobacco endgame.19,21

Covariables

Statewide demographic factors were used as covariables. This included the percentage of people with a black and a Hispanic ethnicity. To account for differences in age distribution, the proportion of adults over 65 years old was included. Socioeconomic status was approximated by the average income. Finally, the average Medicaid expenditure in the year of interest was included in our analyses. Income and expenditures were corrected for inflation. To account for deterministic time trends, the year of outcome was used as a proxy.

Statistical analysis

The mortality rates were expressed by the incidence of deaths per 100 000 persons per year (person-years). The average outcomes of each year were calculated and reported with 95% CIs. Geospatial visualisations were used to visualise smoking prevalence. The Pearson correlation coefficient was estimated between smoking prevalence and the outcomes of interest. The lag-exposure of smoking prevalence on the outcomes of interest was estimated by a linear mixed generalized additive model (mixed-GAM) model, allowing non-linear relationships between variables. Temporal dependency between smoking prevalence and the outcome of interest was estimated by a distributed lag non-linear model.22 The lag-exposure was approximated by a P-splines (penalised splines).23 The penalisation ensures stability and reduces the risk of multicollinearity among the temporal smoking prevalences. Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated with classical variance inflation diagnostics. The optimal smoothing parameters were estimated by restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML).23 Smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality were considered to be linear. This linear approach provides a clear comparison of the relative importance of smoking prevalence on the mortality outcome, which is useful in studies predicting smoking events.24 A random intercept was used to account for differences between states. A counterfactual scenario was created to estimate the impact of an absolute reduction of 5% smoking prevalence. The discrete lag response for smoking prevalence at each prior year is reported, together with the cumulative lag-response, defined as the sum of the discrete lag-responses over preceding years. Integration of the lag-response function will give the eventual cumulative impact of total historic prevalence. It is advisable to interpret the lag structure in its entirety rather than overemphasising individual lag estimates. Additionally, the analyses were repeated with state-specific smooth time trends using factor-smooth interactions. These state-specific time trends account for potential unmeasured differences in treatment, prevention strategies and policy environments.

Finally, these results were used to estimate the reduction in mortality in 2019, under the counterfactual scenario in which the absolute smoking prevalence was reduced by 5% in previous years. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, additional counterfactual scenarios assuming absolute reductions in smoking prevalence at 3% and 7% were evaluated. The impact of relaxing the linear assumption was evaluated using p-splines to model the impact between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality, in case there was a baseline smoking prevalence of 20%. Second, the 20-year look-back window could lead to left truncation. To investigate the impact of the look-back window on the outcome, the models were re-estimated using alternative look-back periods of 10 years, 15 years, 17 years and 19 years. Third, analyses were repeated for an age-standardised smoking-related mortality outcome, with the 2019 national population as the reference. Fourth, additional analyses restricted the exposure to the percentage of people who smoke daily. Fifth, to account for the 2011 BRFSS methodological redesign, pre-2011 smoking prevalence estimates were recalibrated. The mean annual change in smoking prevalence across the study period for each state, excluding 2010–2011, was estimated. The difference between the change in prevalence from 2010 to 2011 and the mean annual change was used to approximate the lack of coherence due to the redesign. This difference was applied to pre-2011 smoking prevalence estimates to improve comparability with post-2011. Sixth, the robustness was checked in case the smoothing parameter was estimated based on generalised cross-validation, optimising Mallows’ Cp instead of REML. Seventh, as a robustness check, models were estimated using natural and polynomial splines, selected based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). All statistical analyses were performed in R V.4.5.0 using the packages dlnm and mgcv.

Exposure lag response association

The single-year lag-response, in case 5% of the population ceased smoking, is shown in online supplemental eFigure 3. A non-linear lag-response was observed between smoking prevalence and the mortality rate of ischaemic heart disease, COPD, lung cancer and other smoking-related cancers. The largest single-year impact of smoking prevalence reduction on cause-specific mortality rates (per 100 000 person-years) occurred as follows: (1) ischaemic heart disease mortality at 8 years (−3.02 (95% CI −4.31 to −1.73)), (2) COPD mortality at 10 years (−1.35 (95% CI −1.89 to −0.82)), (3) lung cancer mortality at year 11 years (−1.68 (95% CI −2.44 to −0.91)) and (4) mortality associated with other smoking-related cancers, it was at 20 years (−0.61 (95% CI −0.81 to −0.40)).

The cumulative impact on mortality rate (per 100 000 person-years), in case 5% of the population ceased smoking and it is sustained over time, is shown in figure 2. A significant reduction in ischaemic heart disease mortality was found at 4 years (−6.60 (95% CI −12.35 to −0.85)). For COPD and other cancer-related mortality, there was an immediate, limited but significant effect, respectively −0.28 (95% CI −0.52 to −0.04) and −0.03 (95% CI −0.04 to −0.02). Finally, for lung cancer, it was respectively at 9 years (−4.64 (95% CI −8.74 to −0.55)). After 20 years of sustained reduction, the largest impact was observed in the mortality caused by ischaemic heart diseases (−34.11 (95% CI −46.26 to −21.97)). For COPD, lung cancer and other smoking-related cancer mortalities, the reductions were respectively −20.09 (95% CI −25.24 to −14.94), −14.55 (95% CI −18.18 to −10.92) and −6.74 (95% CI −9.00 to −4.49).

Sensitivity analysis

Counterfactual scenarios assuming an absolute reduction of 3% and 7% in smoking prevalence were evaluated for both linear and non-linear assumptions between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality, as shown in online supplemental eFigure 4. With a 7% reduction in smoking prevalence, the linear reduction in smoking-related mortality for ischaemic heart disease was −47.76 (95% CI −64.76 to −30.76), for COPD was it −28.13 (95% CI −35.34 to −20.91), for lung cancer −20.37 (95% CI −25.45 to −15.29) and for other smoking-related cancers: −9.44 (95% CI −12.59 to −6.28). When using a non-linear relation between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality, the results were −44.29 (95% CI −70.98 to −17.60), −27.94 (95% CI −36.38 to −19.51), −17.23 (95% CI −25.63 to −8.84) and −9.44 (95% CI −12.59 to −6.28), respectively. The influence of the look-back window on lag-response is shown in online supplemental eFigure 5. A 10-year window yielded a larger main effect, especially in COPD-mortality. While windows between 15 and 20 years showed broadly consistent lag-response curves. Additionally, online supplemental eFigure 6 and online supplemental eFigure 7 show the impact of age-standardised mortality and restrict the exposure to daily smoking. With this exposure, a stronger estimated effect on ischaemic heart disease was observed, compared with the effect when not restricting to daily smoking. In online supplemental eFigure 8, the lag-response is shown when accounting for the 2011 BRFSS methodological redesign. Online supplemental eFigure 9 shows the lag response in case different methods are used to derive the smoothing parameter and online supplemental eFigure 10 shows the robustness of the choice of spline used to predict the lag-response. Remarkably, the lag-response in ischaemic heart mortality showed a more delayed, but stronger decreasing trend depending on the choice of spline.

Discussion

This study showed a non-linear and delayed association between smoking prevalence and major smoking-related causes of death across the US.

COPD and lung cancer-related mortality showed the strongest correlations with smoking prevalence. In a counterfactual scenario of a 5% absolute reduction in smoking prevalence, the largest single-year association with COPD mortality was seen at year 10, while the largest association for lung cancer was seen at year 11. Particularly, COPD mortality experienced a rapid and significant reduction. Despite the lower correlation of smoking prevalence with ischaemic heart disease mortality, sustained reduction in smoking prevalence was associated with the largest cumulative effect at the population level. Mainly, the smoking prevalence 8 years earlier showed a strong association with the current ischaemic heart disease mortality. Additionally, it was estimated that a 20-year smoking prevalence reduction was associated with a substantial impact on mortality, with the greatest reduction seen in COPD mortality at 30.82%, and both ischaemic heart disease and lung cancer showed more than 20% reduction in mortality.

The delayed reduction in lung cancer mortality became apparent after 9 years, and the maximum lag effect occurred at 11 years. A time delay of risk reduction in lung cancer up to a decade after smoking cessation has previously been observed at the individual level,25 26 and at a population level, for example, in Spain.27 However, lag periods of around 20–30 years for lung cancer have been reported as well, suggesting the underlying maximal direct impact of smoking may reach beyond 20 years, while we observed a plateau after 14 years.28 29 A longer follow-up period could further reveal this association, as studies have suggested that those who start smoking as teenagers have an elevated risk after 60 years.30 A plateau in the association of smoking prevalence and lung cancer during follow-up has previously been described for lung cancer incidence.7 This could be driven by the harvesting hypothesis.31 Here, the initial risk is suggested to drop after early lung cancer deaths of more susceptible people, causing a gap with a rise again at older age due to inflammation-related smoking deaths. However, the observed pattern may also reflect methodological factors, such as left truncation of smoking exposure history, which can redistribute the effects of earlier smoking prevalence into the lag-response function within the observed time window.

A 5% absolute reduction in smoking among the population was associated with an immediate significant decrease in COPD mortality, with a fast reduction after 5 years, suggesting potentially rapid public health benefits from smoking cessation programmes, with additional returns for up to 17 years. At an individual level, people who quit smoking experienced a risk reduction of 11% in COPD mortality after 2–7 years of abstinence. After 8 years of abstention, the risk is even further decreased by 39%, compared with people who remain smoking.32 A possible pathway for these rapid reductions could be explained by the direct impact of smoking cessation on the reduced risk of COPD hospitalisation, which may lead to lower COPD mortality.33 The correlation with smoking prevalence was weaker in ischaemic heart disease mortality compared with the other outcomes, suggesting that a larger share of variation may be explained by other risk factors, potentially contributing to greater uncertainty in the estimated relationship. Major factors correlated to smoking prevalence could have an underlying impact, such as socioeconomic status or healthcare access. Furthermore, we cannot exclude the possibility that some COPD exacerbations were being classified as myocardial infarction. The estimated reduction in mortality was markedly larger compared with the other outcomes in the first decade. Additionally, the model with ischaemic heart disease outcome showed the highest susceptibility to different model specifications. On an individual level, active smoking is known to double the risk of several cardiovascular diseases, including acute myocardial infarction and heart failure.34 In prior studies, individual smoking cessation has been shown to reduce the risk of cardiovascular diseases within the first decade after abstinence.35 36 Although people who never smoke have a lower risk compared with people with a past of heavy smoking who quit smoking, the gap slowly diminishes over decades.26

Finally, smoking-related cancers apart from lung cancer were investigated. The correlation of smoking prevalence with other smoking-related cancers was stronger than that of smoking prevalence and ischaemic heart disease. However, these cancers were rarer compared with ischaemic heart diseases at a population level, resulting in a more limited incidence reduction association over time, in case a scenario occurred where the absolute percentage of people who smoke was reduced by 5%. Consistent with evidence from individual-level studies, we found a slow decrease in risk. The association of smoking cessation with other smoking-related cancers was smaller and the delay longer compared with lung cancer.25 Importantly, smoking-related inflammatory markers remain present in the body even after 20 years of smoking cessation, increasing the direct risk of smoking on cancer potentially beyond our long-term observation period.37 Since the relative risk of several smoking-related cancers continues to decrease over time after smoking cessation, the long-term carcinogenic effects of smoking may continue to influence population mortality patterns.26 38

This framework provides insight into the temporal effect of smoking prevalence on the public health burden, capturing the combined influence of disease occurrence and survival over time. The results could provide information on the potential timing and magnitude of population-level mortality changes that may follow reductions in smoking prevalence. In combination with short-term effects of smoking, including sick leaves, mid-term effects include the onset of COPD, osteoporosis and cardiovascular diseases. This could provide a valuable tool for policymakers to get the global and temporal impact of smoking on public health.

Our study had several strengths. First, it focused on the lag-response on a population level, which remains understudied. The estimated lag-response, therefore, represents associations on a macro level rather than effects of individual smoking behaviour or smoking cessation. Second, our study comprised a large study population. Furthermore, the analysed CDC data are credible and have undergone several validation processes.39 Third, several covariables and a random effect of the state of interest were taken into account to reduce confounding effects. Fourth, several sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of the results. Finally, a penalised framework of Distributed Lag Non-Linear Modelss was used to reduce the risk of overfitting, while also accounting for more complex non-linear effects.23

However, our study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the ecological design does not allow causality at the individual level. The analysis uses population-level smoking prevalence as an indicator of tobacco exposure and does not capture variation in individual smoking histories. Therefore individual-level characteristics such as medical history, genetic susceptibility, smoking history, smoking intensity and environmental exposures could not be accounted for. Consequently, the estimated lag-response functions should be interpreted as population-level temporal associations between smoking prevalence and mortality rather than causal estimates of the effects of individual smoking cessation. Second, our model accounted for non-linear temporal dependency between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality, but a linear relation between smoking prevalence and lung cancer risk was assumed. This linear link is used as a parsimonious approximation within the observed smoking prevalence ranges, as it is known that smoking is a major causal pathway to this disease-related mortality, and it could capture dominant association patterns.40 Smoking prevalence represents a population-level exposure indicator reflecting the proportion of smokers in the population, independent of individual smoking intensity, duration and secondhand exposure.41 While this specification restricts more flexible lag-response shapes, it reduces the risk of overfitting. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the impact of a non-linear relation between smoking prevalence and lung cancer risk. Although this specification offered greater flexibility in capturing the underlying association, it was accompanied by increased estimation uncertainty. Third, the mortality was derived from death certificates, which only contain one underlying cause of death. This may lead to an underestimation of specific mortality causes.42 Especially, COPD could be under-reported when the primary cause of death is not pulmonary.43 Improvement in cause of death classification could affect observed mortality trends independently of smoking prevalence. Fourth, there might be an under-reporting of smoking, though an update of the BRFSS in 2011 led to improved representation.17 However, as stigma continues to surround smoking, under-reporting remains likely. This may attenuate the estimated association between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality, potentially biasing the lag-response of smoking prevalence on smoking-related mortality, leading it towards the null. Fifth, the 20-year look-back period of smoking prevalence may not fully capture the latency periods associated with certain causes of mortality, potentially leading to an overestimation of the cumulative lag-response by redistributing the effects of earlier periods within the observed time window.44 A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the possible impact of the lag period choice. At lag windows over 15 years, the estimated lag-response curves showed broadly similar results to those obtained with the 20-year specification. Sixth, our study modelled counterfactual scenarios. Therefore, prevention policies were interpreted as contextual factors, instead of an adjustment. These policies generally influence mortality indirectly by reducing smoking prevalence and intensity. Their inclusion in the model could result in overadjustment or mediation of smoking prevalence. However, secondary prevention measures like lung cancer screening independently reduce smoking-related mortality.45 Additional analyses were performed to account for state-specific deviations in the time trend in the model. This allowed us to adjust for unmeasured state-level factors that evolve and differ across states (eg, changes in healthcare access, enhancement of diagnosis). However, this could also increase the risk of overadjustment and potentially attenuate the association between smoking prevalence and smoking-related mortality. Additional studies could further elucidate the impact of specific policies. Seventh, our predictions are based on US data. Different countries could have different smoking histories, tobacco control policies, healthcare systems, treatment advances and coding practices for causes of death. Therefore, additional studies are required to ensure generalisability to other countries or healthcare systems. Eighth, this study did not account for the impact of e-cigarettes. While randomised trials suggest that e-cigarettes could influence smoking behaviour, real-world evidence does not show an increase in smoking cessation.46 Furthermore, dual usage of combustible cigarettes and e-cigarettes may increase the risk of adverse smoking outcomes.46 Therefore, it is currently unclear what the impact of e-cigarettes would be on smoking-related mortality.

Conclusion

Based on US state-specific empirical data, the impact and timing of smoking reduction vary across causes of death. Notably, COPD-related deaths decrease rapidly when the smoking prevalence is reduced. The largest effect of smoking prevalence on lung cancer and COPD mortality occurred at 11 and 10 years, while for ischaemic heart diseases, it took 8 years. Future studies are required to evaluate generalisability to other countries.
