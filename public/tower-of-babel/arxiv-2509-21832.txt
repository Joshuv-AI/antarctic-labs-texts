Micro-macro kinetic flux-vector splitting schemes for the multidimensional Boltzmann-ES-BGK equation
James A. Rossmanith, Preeti Sar

Abstract
The kinetic Boltzmann equation models gas dynamics over a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. Simplified versions of the full Boltzmann collision operator, such as the classical Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) and the closely related Ellipsoidal-Statistical-BGK (ES-BGK) operators, can dramatically decrease the computational costs of numerical solving kinetic equations. Classical BGK yields incorrect transport coefficients (relative to the full Boltzmann collision operator) at low Knudsen numbers, whereas ES-BGK captures them correctly. In this work, we develop a finite volume method using a micro-macro decomposition of the distribution function, which requires a smaller velocity mesh relative to direct kinetic methods for low and intermediate Knudsen numbers. The macro portion of the model is a fluid model with a moment closure provided from the heat flux tensor calculated from the micro portion. The micro portion is obtained by applying to the original kinetic equation a projector into the orthogonal complement of the null space of the collision operator – this projector depends on the macro portion. In particular, we extend the technique of Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens [ Uniformly stable schemes for the Boltzmann equation preserving the compressible Navier-Stokes asymptotics, J. Comput. Phys. (2008) ] to two-space dimensions, the ES-BGK collision operator, and problems with reflecting wall boundary conditions. As it appears in both the micro and macro equations, the collision operator is handled via L-stable implicit time discretizations. At the same time, the remaining transport terms are computed via kinetic flux vector splitting (for the macro equations) and upwind differencing (for the micro equation). The resulting scheme is applied to various test cases in 1D and 2D. The 2D version of the code is parallelized via MPI, and we present weak and strong scaling studies with varying numbers of processors.

Introduction

A rarefied gas is a collection of unbound molecules in which the mean free path (i.e., the average distance a molecule will travel before colliding with another molecule) is comparable or larger than some characteristic length scale in the problem; the ratio of these two lengths (i.e., the mean free path divided by the characteristic length scale) is called the
Knudsen number
, which we denote by
\varepsilon>0
. Different flow regimes can be enumerated through the size of the Knudsen number; a common approach is to define the following four flow regimes: (1) continuum
\left(\varepsilon<10^{-2}\right)
, (2) slip
\left(10^{-2}<\varepsilon<10^{-1}\right)
, (3) transition
\left(10^{-1}<\varepsilon<10\right)
, and (4) free molecular
\left(\varepsilon>10\right)
. A gas is typically considered
rarefied
in all but the continuum regime. Applications in which rarefied gases are essential in the overall dynamics include flow around high-altitude aircraft, various propulsion mechanisms for spacecraft, and flow through microfluidic and nanofluidic devices
book:karniadakis2005microflows
.

To make accurate predictions of rarefied gas dynamics (RGD), continuum fluid models such as the Navier-Stokes equations are insufficient, and instead kinetic transport models such as the Boltzmann equation are typically required. Kinetic Boltzmann equations are ordinarily more challenging to solve computationally than continuum fluid models, since they require the solution of integro-differential equations in full phase space and the discretization of potentially complicated collision operators. We briefly review some standard approaches for solving kinetic equations below.

Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC).
DSMC (e.g., see Bird
(
Bird1970DirectEquation
;
Bird1994MolecularEdition
)
, Nanbu
article:Nanbu1983
, and
Pareschi and Russo
Pareschi2001AnEquation
) is a classical approach for solving kinetic Boltzmann equations that uses a Lagrangian description of gas particles and models collisions between particles using a probabilistic Monte-Carlo approach. DSMC is efficient for large Knudsen numbers (i.e., small mean-free path), but the computational cost increases with decreasing Knudsen number. Another disadvantage of DSMC is that the numerical results will contain statistical noise resulting from the Monte Carlo treatment of the collision operator, which decreases relatively slowly with the
number of particles,
N
:
{\mathcal{O}}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)
.

Direct Kinetic Methods.
An alternative to the DSMC approach is to
directly
discretize the kinetic equation, meaning that all terms in the equations are approximated deterministically using a variety of finite difference, finite volume, finite element, and spectral methods. Several such approaches exist in the literature, including Chorin
article:Chorin1972
, Pareschi and Russo
(
Pareschi2000NumericalOperator
)
,
Pieraccini and Puppo
Pieraccini2007Implicit-explicitEquations
, Xu and Huang
article:XuHuang2010
, Filbet and Jin
Filbet2011AnEquation
,
Güçlü and Hitchon
article:Guclu2012
, Kitzler and Schöberl
article:Kitzler2015
, Su et al.
Su2020ImplicitEquation
, Li et al.
article:LiFang2022
, as well as many others. Most direct kinetic methods are applied to simplified collision operators like BGK
Bhatnagar1954ASystems
or ES-BGK
article:Holway1965
. The advantage of direct kinetic models over DSMC is that they do not suffer from the statistical noise of Monte Carlo, and they can remain relatively efficient even for small Knudsen numbers through appropriate implicit or semi-implicit time-stepping schemes. However, the main drawback of such methods is that they require a discretization of the full phase space, which, through the curse of dimensionality, can result in huge computational costs.

Moment-Closure Methods.
An alternative to direct kinetic methods is to replace the full particle density function (PDF) by a small number of moments of the PDF (e.g., mass, momentum, and energy). The dynamics of these moments occur on a lower-dimensional manifold than the full PDF (e.g., 3D instead of 6D), significantly reducing the computational complexity. Furthermore, moments of the collision operator are typically simpler than the full collision operator. The difficulty with this approach is determining an accurate and efficient moment-closure that allows one to replace the full PDF with a small number of moments. The first systematic moment-closure approach was developed by Grad
Grad1949OnGases
in 1949; in subsequent decades, many alternative moment-closures have been developed, including maximum entropy closures
article:Dreyer87
;
Levermore1996MomentTheories
;
article:MuRu93
, quadrature-based moment closures
article:desjardins2008
;
article:Fox08
, the R13 closure
Struchtrup2003RegularizationAnalysis
, and many others. Torrilhon
article:Torrilhon2009
and the references therein review many of these approaches.
The advantages of these methods are that they greatly reduce the computational complexity and directly capture the small Knudsen number limit. The disadvantage of these approaches is that they often only produce accurate solutions near thermodynamic equilibrium, and therefore, may not provide accurate solutions if the Knudsen number becomes too large.

1.1
Asymptotic Preserving Property

The so-called
fluid limit
of the kinetic Boltzmann occurs when the Knudsen number tends to zero (from the right):
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. This limit is of vital importance in gas dynamics, since it represents the instantaneous relaxation to thermodynamic equilibrium. The difficulty is that this limit is also a
singular limit
of the kinetic Boltzmann equation, since certain high-order derivative terms (i.e., viscosity and thermal diffusion) vanish in that limit. In complex flows, parts of the domain may be in or very near thermodynamic equilibrium, while other portions may not; thus, accurately and efficiently handling the full range of Knudsen numbers is critical.

The key property that allows a numerical scheme to remain stable and accurate in the singular limit,
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, is commonly referred to as the
asymptotic preserving
(AP) property (e.g., see

Caflisch1997UniformlyRelaxation
;
Coron1991NumericalEquations
;
Gabetta1997RelaxationEquations
;
article:Jin1995
;
Jin1999EfficientEquations
;
Jin2012AsymptoticReview
;
Jin1996NumericalTerms
). The basic idea is this: given a stable and consistent numerical method for the kinetic equation, it should be possible to fix the mesh parameters (i.e., the time-step and mesh spacing), and then take the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in the numerical method and arrive at a new stable and consistent numerical method for the system in thermodynamic equilibrium (i.e., compressible Euler equations). If this is possible, we refer to the original numerical method for the kinetic equation as asymptotic preserving (AP). The key mathematical property that must be met is this: the limit of
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and the limit of the mesh parameters tending to zero (again, from the right) must
commute
. A commutative diagram illustrating this point is shown in Figure
1
. To achieve the AP property in practice, one must use an implicit or semi-implicit time discretization for the collision operator.

1.2
Scope of this work

An alternative to the methods described above (i.e., DSMC, direct kinetic methods, and moment-closure methods) is the so-called
micro-macro decomposition
approach first developed by Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
, with additional modifications developed by several authors, including
Crestetto, Crouseilles, and Lemou
article:Crestetto2012
, Xiong, Jang, Li and Qiu
XIONG201570
, Gamba, Jin, and Liu
GAMBA2019264
, and Endeve and Hauck
ENDEVE2022111227
.
The basic idea is to write the full particle distribution function (PDF) as a sum of its thermodynamic equilibrium (i.e., the macro portion) plus everything else (i.e., the micro portion). The micro-macro decomposition approach can be thought of as a kind of direct kinetic method in the sense that ultimately it does solve for the entire distribution on a mesh; however, it also borrows from moment-closure methods in that it directly evolves fluid moments (i.e., macro portion). Unlike “standard” direct kinetic methods, micro-macro only discretizes the non-equilibrium portion of the PDF on the full phase space mesh, which, in practice, allows for velocity grids that have smaller velocity extents and lower resolutions than in a comparable direct kinetic method. The benefit of the micro-macro over moment-closure methods is that one can accurately simulate flows for any Knudsen number, arbitrarily close or far away from thermodynamic equilibrium.

This work aims to extend the micro-macro decomposition method of Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
in several key directions. We show how to extend the micro-macro decomposition to multiple dimensions and to accurately handle the Ellipsoidal-Statistical-Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (ES-BGK)
article:Holway1965
collision operator. One of the disadvantages of the micro-macro decomposition approach is that boundary conditions can be far more difficult to derive and implement than in direct kinetic methods. In this work, we show how to implement reflecting wall boundary conditions in both the macro and micro portions of the numerical update to recover solutions comparable to those computed with direct kinetic methods. In the proposed numerical scheme, the collision operator, as it appears in both the micro and macro equations, is handled via L-stable implicit time discretizations. At the same time, the remaining transport terms are computed via kinetic flux vector splitting (for the macro equations) and upwind differencing (for the micro equation). The semi-implicit nature of the full update allows the scheme to be asymptotic preserving (AP). The resulting scheme is applied to various test cases in 1D and 2D. The 2D version of the code is parallelized via MPI, and we present weak and strong scaling studies with varying numbers of processors. The resulting methods are written in an open-source C++ code with MPI for parallelization and Python plotting routines; this code can be freely downloaded as a
bitbucket.org
repository
code:RossmanithSar2025
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In §
2
we describe the kinetic Boltzmann-BGK and Boltzmann-ES-BGK models and review their properties. We develop in §
3
the one-dimensional version of the proposed micro-macro decomposition numerical method; we provide careful details of the numerical scheme, both in the macro and micro portions of the method. Numerical examples of the resulting scheme are presented in §
4
, including a convergence study, a shock-tube problem, and a boundary-driven flow that shows the robustness of the scheme across a wide range of Knudsen numbers. In §
5
we extend the proposed method to the 2D Boltzmann-ES-BGK model; we again provide careful details of the numerical scheme’s macro and micro portions. Numerical examples of the resulting scheme are presented in §
6
, including a convergence study and the results of a lid-driven cavity example in the slip/transition regime that clearly shows dynamics beyond the Navier-Stokes-Fourier approximation. We also briefly describe a parallel MPI implementation in the 2D case, and provide both a weak and strong scaling analysis. Finally, we summarize our findings in §
7
.

2
Boltzmann Equation and Approximate Collision Operators

We begin with a brief review of the kinetic Boltzmann equation for a monoatomic gas, the BGK and ES-BGK approximate
collision operators, as well as the fluid equations that result in the strongly collisional limit.

2.1
Boltzmann Equation

The dimensionless form of the single-species Boltzmann equation can be written as:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+{\underline{v}}\cdot\nabla_{{\underline{x}}}f=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathcal{Q}(f),

(1)

where
f(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}}):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the particle density function,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is time,
{\underline{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is the spatial coordinate, and
{\underline{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is the velocity coordinate, and
d_{x},d_{v}\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
are the spatial and velocity dimensions, respectively. The parameter
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the Knudsen number, a nondimensional number that compares the mean free path of particles to a characteristic length scale; small (large) Knudsen numbers mean high (low) rates of collisions. The collision operator,
\mathcal{Q}(f):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, models inter-particle collisions.

We use the following shorthand to denote integration over velocity:

\left\langle g\right\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}g\left({\underline{v}}\right)\,d{\underline{v}}.

(2)

The first
d_{v}+2
moments of the distribution function,
f
, are then given by

{\underline{U}}:=\left(\rho,\,\rho{\underline{u}}\,,\,{\mathcal{E}}\right):=\left\langle{\underline{m}}\left({\underline{v}}\right)f\right\rangle\qquad\text{where}\qquad{\underline{m}}\left({\underline{v}}\right):=\left(1,\,{\underline{v}}\,,\,\frac{\|{\underline{v}}\|^{2}}{2}\right),

(3)

and
\rho\left(t,{\underline{x}}\right):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the mass density,

\rho{\underline{u}}\left(t,{\underline{x}}\right):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is the momentum density,
and
{\mathcal{E}}\left(t,{\underline{x}}\right):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the scalar energy density.
The following variables can be directly computed from the first
d_{v}+2
moments:

macroscopic velocity:

\displaystyle\quad{\underline{u}}=\frac{\rho{\underline{u}}}{\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}{\underline{v}}\,f\,d{\underline{v}}\,,

(4)

scalar pressure:

\displaystyle\quad p=\frac{2{\mathcal{E}}-\rho\|{\underline{u}}\|^{2}}{d_{v}}=\frac{1}{d_{v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\|{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\|^{2}\,f\,d{\underline{v}}\,,

(5)

scalar temperature:

\displaystyle\quad T=\frac{p}{\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho d_{v}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\|{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\|^{2}\,f\,d{\underline{v}}\,.

(6)

The collision operator,
\mathcal{Q}(f)
, can take different forms (e.g., see Villani
article:Villani2002
), but in each version
should obey the following key properties:

•

Conservation of mass, momentum, and energy:

\left\langle{\underline{m}}\left({\underline{v}}\right)\,\mathcal{Q}(f)\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall f\geq 0;

(7)

•

Entropy inequality:

\left\langle\mathcal{Q}(f)\,\log(f)\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall f\geq 0;

(8)

•

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is the equilibrium distribution:

\mathcal{Q}(f)\equiv 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\equiv\mathcal{M}\left[f\right]:=\frac{\rho}{\left({2\pi T}\right)^{d_{v}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\|^{2}}{2T}\right),

(9)

where
\rho,{\underline{u}}
, and
T
are the density, macroscopic velocity, and scalar temperature associated with
f
:

\left\langle{\underline{m}}\left({\underline{v}}\right)\,\mathcal{M}[f]\right\rangle\equiv\left\langle{\underline{m}}\left({\underline{v}}\right)\,f\right\rangle.

(10)

2.2
Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) Collision Operator

To avoid the complexity of the full Boltzmann collision operator, approximate collision operators are often employed in numerical simulations that mimic the behavior of the Boltzmann operator, but that are much less computationally expensive to evaluate. While there exist multiple possible approximations, the simplest approximate collision operator that satisfies all of the properties outlined above in (

), (
8
), and
(

\mathcal{Q}(f):={\tau}\bigl(\mathcal{M}\left[f\right]-f\bigr),

(11)

where
\mathcal{M}\left[f\right]
is the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution (

\varepsilon
is the constant Knudsen number,
and
\tau
is a collision parameter that allows us to make the collision frequency solution-dependent

Mieussens2000DiscreteDynamics

We apply a power series expansion for

f=\mathcal{M}\left[f\right]\left(1+\varepsilon g_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}g_{2}+\cdots\right),

(12)

which results to
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
in the Navier-Stokes-Fourier system:

\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
\rho{\underline{u}}\\
{\mathcal{E}}\end{pmatrix}+\nabla_{\underline{x}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\rho{\underline{u}}\\
\rho{\underline{u}}\otimes{\underline{u}}+p{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}\\
\left({\mathcal{E}}+p\right){\underline{u}}\end{pmatrix}=\varepsilon\nabla_{\underline{x}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\mu\,{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}\\
\,\mu\,{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}\;{\underline{u}}-{\underline{h_{1}}}\,\end{pmatrix}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right),

(13)

where
{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}

\displaystyle{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}=\left(\nabla_{\underline{x}}\,{\underline{u}}+\left(\nabla_{\underline{x}}\,{\underline{u}}\right)^{T}\right)-\frac{2}{d_{v}}\left(\,\nabla_{\underline{x}}\cdot{\underline{u}}\,\right){\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}\,\,\,\,\left(\text{stress tensor}\right),\quad{\underline{h_{1}}}=-\kappa\nabla_{{\underline{x}}}T\,\,\,\,\left({\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon\right)\text{heat flux vector}\right).

(14)

In (

\mu
and

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{p}{\tau}\,\,\,\,\left(\text{viscosity}\right),\quad\kappa=\left(\frac{d_{v}+2}{2}\right)\frac{p}{\tau}\,\,\,\,\left(\text{heat conductivity}\right),\quad\text{Pr}=\left(\frac{d_{v}+2}{2}\right)\frac{\mu}{\kappa}=1\,\,\,\,\left(\text{Prandtl \#}\right).

(15)

Note that the Prandtl number, which is proportional to the ratio of the viscosity to the heat conductivity, is always one in the BGK model.

ES-BGK Collision Operator

The BGK model has the correct compressible Euler limit when

To overcome this problem, Holway

For ES-BGK, conservation property (

\frac{d_{v}-1}{d_{v}}\leq\text{Pr}<\infty.

To describe the ES-BGK collision operator, we first define the temperature tensor:

{\underline{\underline{\mathbb{T}}}}=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\left({\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\right)\otimes\left({\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\right)\,f\,dv\,,\quad T=\frac{1}{d_{v}}\,\text{tr}\left({\underline{\underline{\mathbb{T}}}}\right),

(17)

where the trace of the temperature tensor is proportional to the scalar temperature
T

Next we introduce the

:

{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}=\left(1-\nu\right)\,T\,{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}+\nu\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\frac{1}{1-d_{v}}\leq\nu<1\,.

(18)

Note that in the case

, the range on
\nu
is given by
-\infty<\nu<1

\text{tr}\left({\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}\right)=\text{tr}\left({\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}\right)

Using the modified temperature tensor, the ES-BGK collision operator can be written as

\mathcal{Q}(f)={\tau}\left(\mathcal{G}[f]-f\right)\,,

(19)

where the Gaussian distribution,

\mathcal{G}[f]=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\text{det}\left(2\,\pi\,{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}\,\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\left(\,{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\,\right)\cdot{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}^{-1}\left(\,{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\,\right)}\right).

:

\nu=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau=\frac{p}{2\mu_{B}(T)}=\left(\frac{3A_{2}(5)\,\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\rho\approx\left(0.4625\,\pi\right)\rho.

(29)

3
1D Micro-Macro Decomposition

The micro-macro decomposition method proposed in this work is based on the original approach of Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens
(
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
)
for solving the one-dimensional (i.e.,
d_{v}=1
and
d_{x}=1
) BGK model. In this section, we review the approach of

(
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
)
, although we introduce small changes that will be propagated to the multi-dimensional extension (i.e.,
d_{v}=2
and
d_{x}=2
) for the ES-BGK model in Section
5
.

3.1
Mathematical Formulation

The one-dimensional (
d_{v}=1
and
d_{x}=1
) Boltzmann-BGK equation can be written as:

f_{,t}+{v}f_{,x}=\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]-f\right)\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(v-u\right)^{2}}{2T}\right],

(30)

and

\rho=\left\langle f\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]\right\rangle,\quad u=\frac{1}{\rho}\left\langle v\,f\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\rho}\left\langle v\,{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]\right\rangle,\quad T=\frac{1}{\rho}\left\langle\left(v-u\right)^{2}\,f\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\rho}\left\langle\left(v-u\right)^{2}\,{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]\right\rangle.

(31)

One of the key steps in the micro-macro approach
(
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
)
is to decompose
the full distribution function,
f
, into a macroscopic Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution,
\mathcal{M}[f]
,
plus whatever is left over, which is called the microscopic portion and denoted by
g
:

f={\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]+\varepsilon\,g\qquad\text{where}\qquad\varepsilon:=\text{Knudsen number}.

(32)

We note that although this looks like a perturbation expansion, it is, in fact, not a perturbation expansion, because we do not assume that
\varepsilon
is small, nor is anything being truncated. Also note that because
f
and
{\mathcal{M}}[f]

have the same first three moments, the first three moments of the microscopic portion,
g
, must be zero:

\left\langle{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)g\right\rangle=0\qquad\text{where}\qquad{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)=\left(1,\,v,\,\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\right).

(33)

Inserting the micro–macro decomposition (
32
) into the Boltzmann-BGK equation yields:

{\mathcal{M}}_{,t}+{v}{\mathcal{M}}_{,x}+\varepsilon g_{,t}+\varepsilon\,vg_{,x}=-\tau\,g.

(34)

Computing the first three moments of (
34
) as defined by (
3
) with
d_{v}=1
, yields the following fluid system:

\left(\text{1D Macro}\right):\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
{\mathcal{E}}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+\rho T\\
u\left({\mathcal{E}}+\rho T\right)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
-h\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{1}{2}\rho T+\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2},\quad h=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left\langle v^{3}g\right\rangle,

(35)

which is not closed since the heat flux,
h
, must still be supplied via the microscopic portion
g
.

To close fluid system (
35
), we need to obtain an evolution equation for the microscopic portion,
g
, from equation (
34
). Noting that the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is in the nullspace of the collision operator (Property (
9
)), Bennoune et al.
(
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
)
introduced an orthogonal projector that projects any distribution into the null space of the BGK collision operator. An orthogonal basis for the nullspace (
11
) is given by

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\,\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M},\quad\left(\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M},\quad\left(\frac{\left(v-u\right)^{2}}{2T}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M}\,\right\}.

(36)

An orthogonal projection onto this basis for a generic particle density function,
F
, is given by:

\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[F\right]=\left\{A_{1}\left[F\right]+\left(\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{T}}\right)A_{2}\left[F\right]+\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\left({v}-{u}\right)^{2}}{2T}-\frac{1}{2}\right)A_{3}\left[F\right]\right\}{\mathcal{M}}[f],

(37)

where

\displaystyle\Bigl(A_{1},\,A_{2},\,A_{3}\Bigr)\left[F\right]:=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1,\,\left(\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{T}}\right),\,\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{(v-u)^{2}}{2T}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\,F\left(v\right)\,dv.

(38)

This projection operator has the property that:

\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\mathcal{M}}\right]={\mathcal{M}},\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\mathcal{M}}_{,t}\right]={\mathcal{M}}_{,t},\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[g_{,t}\right]=0,\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[g\right]=0,

(39)

and when applied to (
34
) yields:

{\mathcal{M}}_{,t}+\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{v}{\mathcal{M}}_{,x}\right]+\varepsilon\,\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[vg_{,x}\right]=0.

(40)

Subtracting (
40
) from (
34
), dividing by
\varepsilon
, and rearranging terms yields an evolution equation for the microscopic portion
g
:

\displaystyle g_{,t}+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left[{v}g_{,x}\right]=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\biggl(\tau\,g+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left[{v}{\mathcal{M}}_{,x}\right]\biggr),

(41)

where
{\mathcal{I}}
is the identity operator.

Equation (
41
) can be further simplified, since it turns out that it is possible
to analytically compute the orthogonal projection of the Maxwellian transport term:

\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left[v{\mathcal{M}}_{,x}\right]=\left\{\left(\frac{(v-u)^{3}}{2T}-\frac{3}{2}\left(v-u\right)\right)\,\frac{T_{,x}}{T}\right\}{\mathcal{M}}.

(42)

Equation (
41
) can now be rewritten as follows:

\left(\text{1D Micro}\right):\quad g_{,t}+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left[{v}g_{,x}\right]=-\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\biggl(g-\widehat{g}\biggr),\qquad\widehat{g}:=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{(v-u)^{3}}{2T}-\frac{3}{2}\left(v-u\right)\right)\,\frac{T_{,x}}{{T}}{\mathcal{M}}.

(43)

The final 1D micro-macro evolution equations are then given by (
35
) and (
43
).

Remark 2
.

For
\varepsilon\ll 1
,
g
will be driven towards
\hat{g}
, and the full distribution function,
f
,
will have the following form:

f={\mathcal{M}}[f]+\varepsilon\,\hat{g}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right).

(44)

Therefore, the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution,
{\mathcal{M}}[f]
, is the compressible Euler limit, while

\varepsilon\,\hat{g}
is the Navier-Stokes-Fourier modification, and all other terms
represent higher-order corrections beyond Navier-Stokes-Fourier.

3.2
Setup for 1D Micro-Macro Finite Volume Method

Now that the micro-macro formulation for
d_{v}=1
has been established via (
35
) and (
43
),
we turn our attention to the numerical discretization. Although our version is similar to the approach of
Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens
(
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
)
, we introduce few small changes, including
a version that does not use a staggered grid for the
g
, ultimately allowing us to extend the method to higher dimensions (see Section
5
).

We introduce a uniform, cell-centered, finite volume mesh in the domain
\left(x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}\right)\times\left(v_{\text{min}},v_{\text{max}}\right)
:

x_{i}:=x_{\text{min}}+\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta x}\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\left[1,N_{x}\right],\quad v_{k}:=v_{\text{min}}+\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta v}\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\left[1,N_{v}\right],

(45)

where

{\Delta x}:=\frac{x_{\text{max}}-x_{\text{min}}}{N_{x}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\Delta v}:=\frac{v_{\text{max}}-v_{\text{min}}}{N_{v}}.

(46)

At each time level,
t^{n}=n\Delta t
, the macroscopic and microscopic portions of the distribution functions are given:

{\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}:=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{i},\,\rho u^{n}_{i},\,{\mathcal{E}}^{n}_{i}\,\right)\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\left[1,N_{x}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad G^{n}_{ik}\quad\text{for}\quad\left(i,k\right)\in\left[1,N_{x}\right]\times\left[1,N_{v}\right].

(47)

The solution is initialized at time
t=0
and run forward until
t=T_{\text{final}}
.
The time step is chosen as a global constant and is defined as follows:

\text{Initialize}:\quad\Delta t=\frac{\text{CFL}\cdot\Delta x}{\mathcal{V}},\quad N_{\text{steps}}=\left\lceil\frac{T_{\text{final}}}{\Delta t}\right\rceil\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Reset}:\quad\Delta t\leftarrow\frac{T_{\text{final}}}{N_{\text{steps}}},\quad\text{CFL}\leftarrow\frac{\mathcal{V}\,\Delta t}{\Delta x},

(48)

where
N_{\text{steps}}
is the total number of timesteps needed to advance from
t=0
to
t=T_{\text{final}}
and

\left(\text{CFL number}\right):\quad 0<\text{CFL}<1,\qquad\left(\text{max speed}\right):\quad\mathcal{V}:=\max\left\{\left|v_{\text{min}}\right|,\left|v_{\text{max}}\right|\right\}.

(49)

3.3
1D Micro Finite Volume Method

In this section, we demonstrate how to update the current value of the micro portion,
G^{n}
, to its value at the next time step,
G^{n+1}
:

\text{Input:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{i},\,\rho u^{n}_{i},\,{\mathcal{E}}^{n}_{i}\,\right)\quad\text{and}\quad G^{n}_{ik}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Output:}\quad G^{n+1}_{ik}.

(50)

First, we construct an approximate
\hat{g}
from (
43
) on the numerical grid:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{ik}^{n}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{\tau^{n}_{i}}\left(\frac{(v_{k}-u^{n}_{i})^{3}}{2T^{n}_{i}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(v_{k}-u^{n}_{i}\right)\right)\,\left(\frac{T^{n}_{i+1/2}-T^{n}_{i-1/2}}{\Delta x\,{T^{n}_{i}}}\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ik},\quad T^{n}_{i+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{n}_{i+1}+T^{n}_{i}\right),

(51)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ik}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\rho^{n}_{i}}{\sqrt{2\pi T^{n}_{i}}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(v_{k}-u^{n}_{i}\right)^{2}}{2T^{n}_{i}}\right].

(52)

We approximate the term
vg_{,x}
in (
43
) via an upwind gradient:

\displaystyle Z^{n}_{ik}

\displaystyle:=v_{k}^{-}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{i+1k}-G^{n}_{ik}}{\Delta x}\right)+v_{k}^{+}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{ik}-G^{n}_{i-1k}}{\Delta x}\right),\quad v_{k}^{-}:=\min\left(0,v_{k}\right),\quad v_{k}^{+}:=\max\left(0,v_{k}\right),

(53)

and then compute its orthogonal projection onto the nullspace of the collision operator (see
(
37
)–(
38
)):

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}^{n}_{ik}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left[A_{1}\right]^{n}_{i}+\left(\frac{v_{k}-u^{n}_{i}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{i}}}\right)\left[A_{2}\right]^{n}_{i}+\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\left({v_{k}}-{u^{n}_{i}}\right)^{2}}{2T^{n}_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left[A_{3}\right]^{n}_{i}\right\}{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ik},

(54)

where the coefficients are computed via the following approximations:

\displaystyle\biggl\{\left[A_{1}\right]^{n}_{i},\quad\left[A_{2}\right]^{n}_{i},\quad\left[A_{3}\right]^{n}_{i}\biggr\}:=\frac{\Delta v}{\rho^{n}_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{v}}\left\{Z^{n}_{ik},\quad\left(\frac{v_{k}-u^{n}_{i}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{i}}}\right)\,Z^{n}_{ik},\quad\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{(v_{k}-u^{n}_{i})^{2}}{2T^{n}_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)Z^{n}_{ik}\right\}.

(55)

Lastly, the numerical micro solution,
G^{n}
, is updated to its new value,
G^{n+1}
,
by applying to the micro equation (
43
) a mixed forward Euler (for the transport term) and
backward Euler (for the collision term) time discretization:

\displaystyle G^{n+1}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{i}}\right)\biggl\{G^{n}_{ik}-\Delta t\left(Z^{n}_{ik}-\widehat{Z}^{n}_{ik}\right)\biggr\}+\left(\frac{\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{i}}{\varepsilon+\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{i}}\right)\widehat{G}^{n}_{ik}.

(56)

Note that this update is
asymptotic-preserving

Jin2012AsymptoticReview
, since we can take the limit

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
of this expression for fixed mesh parameters,
\Delta t
,
\Delta x
, and
\Delta v
,
and arrive at the following result:

\displaystyle G^{n+1}_{ik}\rightarrow\widehat{G}^{n}_{ik}\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

(57)

3.4
1D Macro Finite Volume Method

In this section, we demonstrate how to update the current value of the macro variables at
t=t^{n}

to the new values at
t=t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t
:

\text{Input:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{i},\,\rho u^{n}_{i},\,{\mathcal{E}}^{n}_{i}\,\right)\quad\text{and}\quad H^{n+1}_{i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Output:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n+1}_{i},\,\rho u^{n+1}_{i},\,{\mathcal{E}}^{n+1}_{i}\,\right),

(58)

where the micro portion has already been updated to
t=t^{n+1}
via (
56
), and the heat flux is computed via

\displaystyle H^{n+1}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\Delta v\sum_{k=1}^{N_{v}}\left(v_{k}\right)^{3}\,G^{n+1}_{ik}\,.

(59)

The macro equation given by (
35
) is discretized using the following finite volume method:

\displaystyle{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i}}}

\displaystyle={\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\,{\underline{\mathcal{F}^{\,n}_{i+1/2}}}-{\underline{\mathcal{F}^{\,n}_{i-1/2}}}\,\right)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
H^{n+1}_{i+1/2}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
H^{n+1}_{i-1/2}\end{bmatrix}\right),\quad H^{n+1}_{i+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(H^{n+1}_{i+1}+H^{n+1}_{i}\right),

(60)

where the numerical fluxes,
\mathcal{F}
, are computed via kinetic flux vector splitting (KFVS)
(
Mandal1994KineticEquations
)
. In 1D, KFVS produces the following numerical flux at cell interface

x_{i-1/2}
:

\begin{split}{\underline{\mathcal{F}^{\,n}_{i-1/2}}}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{i-1}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{i}\,dv\\
&=\alpha^{n}_{i-1}\,\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{n}_{i-1}\\
\rho^{n}_{i-1}u^{n}_{i-1}\\
\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n}_{i-1}\left(2T^{n}_{i-1}+\left(u^{n}_{i-1}\right)^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}+\beta^{n+}_{i-1}\,{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i-1}}}\right)-\alpha^{n}_{i}\,\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{n}_{i}\\
\rho^{n}_{i}u^{n}_{i}\\
\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n}_{i}\left(2T^{n}_{i}+\left(u^{n}_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}+\beta^{n-}_{i}\,{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}\right),\end{split}

(61)

where

\displaystyle{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i}}}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{n}_{i}u^{n}_{i}\\
\rho^{n}_{i}\left(T^{n}_{i}+\left(u^{n}_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\\
\frac{1}{2}\rho^{n}_{i}u^{n}_{i}\left(3T^{n}_{i}+\left(u^{n}_{i}\right)^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix},\quad\alpha^{n}_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{T^{n}_{i}}{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(u^{n}_{i}\right)^{2}}{2T^{n}_{i}}\right),\quad\beta_{i}^{n\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{u^{n}_{i}}{\sqrt{2T^{n}_{i}}}\right)\right).

(62)

4.3
Heat Transfer Problem

The two previous examples had simple boundary conditions (periodic and constant extrapolation). To test the micro-macro approach on a problem with more complex boundary conditions, we consider the heat transfer problem involving a gas on the domain
0\leq x\leq 1
between two walls at fixed temperatures (diffusively reflecting). This problem has been considered by several papers in the literature (e.g., Gamba et al.
article:Gamba2010
, Abdelmalik et al.
Abdelmalik2017ErrorEquation
, and van der Woude et al.
article:vanderWoude2024
).
At the kinetic level, the boundary conditions are

\displaystyle f(t,x=0,v)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{M}}_{C}(t,v)=\frac{\rho_{C}(t)}{\sqrt{2\pi T_{C}}}\exp\left[-\frac{v^{2}}{2T_{C}}\right]\quad

\displaystyle v>0

,

(72)

\displaystyle f(t,x=1,v)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{M}}_{H}(t,v)=\frac{\rho_{H}(t)}{\sqrt{2\pi T_{H}}}\exp\left[-\frac{v^{2}}{2T_{H}}\right]\quad

\displaystyle v<0

.

(73)

Following
Abdelmalik2017ErrorEquation
, we take
T_{C}=1.0
and
T_{H}=1.2
; the wall densities,
\rho_{C}(t)
and
\rho_{H}(t)
, are set to ensure that there is no mass flow through the walls:

\int_{0}^{\infty}v{\mathcal{M}}_{C}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{n}\,dv=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}v{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{x}}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v{\mathcal{M}}_{H}^{n}\,dv=0.

(74)

Several boundary values need to be specified to implement the above boundary conditions in the micro-macro approach. In the micro update as described in Section
3.3
, we need boundary values in (
51
) and (
53
). The boundary temperature values at the wall boundaries in (
51
) are computed via kinetic flux vector splitting:

\displaystyle T^{n}_{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{2}{\mathcal{M}}_{C}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v^{2}{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{n}\,dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{C}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{n}\,dv}=\left[\frac{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi T_{C}}{2}}-u\right)\rho\alpha+\left(T-u\sqrt{\frac{\pi T_{C}}{2}}+u^{2}\right)\rho\beta^{-}}{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T_{C}}}\rho\left(\alpha-u\beta^{-}\right)}\right]_{\,i=1}^{\,n},

(75)

\displaystyle T^{n}_{N_{x}+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{2}{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{x}}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v^{2}{\mathcal{M}}_{H}^{n}\,dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{x}}^{n}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathcal{M}}_{H}^{n}\,dv}=\left[\frac{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi T_{H}}{2}}+u\right)\rho\alpha+\left(T+u\sqrt{\frac{\pi T_{H}}{2}}+u^{2}\right)\rho\beta^{+}}{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T_{H}}}\rho\left(\alpha+\beta^{+}\right)}\right]_{\,i=N_{x}}^{\,n}.

(76)

where
\alpha
and
\beta^{\pm}
are defined in (
62
).
In the micro update equation (
53
), all inflow fluxes are set to zero:

\displaystyle Z^{n}_{1k}=v_{k}^{-}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{2k}-G^{n}_{1k}}{\Delta x}\right)+v_{k}^{+}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{1k}-\mbox{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} 0}}{\Delta x}\right),\quad Z^{n}_{N_{x}k}=v_{k}^{-}\left(\frac{\mbox{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} 0}-G^{n}_{N_{x}k}}{\Delta x}\right)+v_{k}^{+}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{N_{x}k}-G^{n}_{N_{x}-1k}}{\Delta x}\right).

(77)

In the macro update equation (
60
), we need to specify fluxes at the wall; we accomplish this via kinetic flux vector splitting between fluid (interior) and wall (exterior) Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions:

\displaystyle{\underline{\mathcal{F}^{\,n}_{1/2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{C}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{1}\,dv,\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{C}^{n}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T_{C}}}\left(\rho^{n}_{1}\alpha^{n}_{1}-\rho^{n}_{1}u^{n}_{1}\beta^{n-}_{1}\right),

(78)

\displaystyle{\underline{\mathcal{F}^{\,n}_{N_{x}+1/2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{N_{x}}\,dv+\int_{-\infty}^{0}v\,{\underline{m}}\left(v\right)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{H}\,dv,\quad

\displaystyle\rho_{H}^{n}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T_{H}}}\left(\rho^{n}_{N_{x}}\alpha^{n}_{N_{x}}+\rho^{n}_{N_{x}}u^{n}_{N_{x}}\beta^{n+}_{N_{x}}\right),

(79)

where
\alpha
and
\beta^{\pm}
are defined in (
62
).
Finally, we also need to specify the heat flux at the boundaries; for that, we take:

H^{n+1}_{0}=0\quad\text{and}\quad H^{n+1}_{N_{x}+1}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H^{n+1}_{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}H^{n+1}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad H^{n+1}_{N_{x}+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}H^{n+1}_{N_{x}}.

(80)

We run a series of numerical simulations all on
(x,v)\in\left[0,1\right]\times\left[-6,6\right]

\left(\rho,\,u,\,T\right)(t=0,x)=\left(1,\,0,\,1\right),\quad g(t=0,x,v)=0.

t=100

0.95

5
2D Micro-Macro Decomposition Scheme

In this section, we extend the 1D micro-macro scheme from §
3
to 2D (i.e.,
d_{x}=d_{v}=2
) and to the ES-BGK collision operator. To handle the ES-BGK collision operator, we solve the extended compressible Euler equations (i.e., 6-moment model when
d_{v}=2
) in the macro step so that we have access to the full pressure tensor.

5.1
Mathematical Formulation

The two-dimensional (
d_{v}=2
and
d_{x}=2
) Boltzmann-ES-BGK equation can be written as:

f_{,t}+{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}f=\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]-f\right),\quad{\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]:=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\text{det}\left(2\,\pi\,{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}\,\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\left(\,{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\,\right)\cdot{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}^{-1}\left(\,{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\,\right)}\right),

(87)

where
{\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]
is a Gaussian distribution with the same mass, momentum, and scalar energy as
f
, but with a different temperature tensor given by (
18
). The temperature tensor of
f
is
{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}
(see (
17
)). The trace of
{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}
and
{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}
are equal and proportional to the scalar temperature:
T
(see definitions in Equation (
17
)). The pressure tensors for the Gaussian,
{\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]
, and
f
are also different:

{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{P}}}}=\left(1-\nu\right)\,p\,{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}+\nu\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{P}}}}}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad-1\leq\nu<1\,,

(88)

where
{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{P}}}}
and
{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{P}}}}}
are defined in (
23
), and
the scalar pressure is defined in (
5
).

Remark 3
.

From (
87
) it appears at first glance that the nullspace of the ES-BGK collision operator is
f\equiv{\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]
; however, by construction
f
and
{\mathcal{G}}\left[f\right]
are not equivalent, because they have different temperature tensors:
{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}\not\equiv{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}
for
-1\leq\nu<1
. The only exception to this non-equivalence is when
{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{T}}}}}\equiv T{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}
, in which case
{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{T}}}}\equiv T{\underline{\underline{\mathcal{I}}}}
, which follows from (
17
); this exception is precisely the case when
f\equiv{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]
, which means the nullspace of (
87
) is again the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution (see (
89
) below).

Similar to the 1D case, in the 2D micro-macro approach, the distribution function
f
is written as the sum of the macroscopic Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution and the microscopic portion denoted by
g
:

f={\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]+\varepsilon\,g,\qquad{\mathcal{M}}\left[f\right]:=\frac{\rho}{2\pi T}\exp\left[-\frac{\left\|{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\right\|^{2}}{2T}\right].

(89)

Inserting the micro-macro decomposition (
89
) into the Boltzmann-ES-BGK equation (
87
) yields:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{,t}+{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}{\mathcal{M}}+\varepsilon g_{,t}+\varepsilon\,{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}g=\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{M}}\right)-\tau\,g.

(90)

Computing the first six moments of (
90
), namely
\left(1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{2}^{2},v_{1}v_{2},v_{2}^{2}\right)
, yields the following fluid system:

\displaystyle\left(\text{2D Macro}\right):\quad\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}_{,t}+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}+{\underline{g}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,y}=\frac{\tau\left(1-\nu\right)}{2\varepsilon}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{22}-{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
-2{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{11}-{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

(91)

where

\begin{gathered}{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{1}^{3}+3u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+{\mathbb{H}}_{111}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+2u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}+{\mathbb{H}}_{112}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}^{2}+2u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}+u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+{\mathbb{H}}_{122}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\underline{g}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+2u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}+{\mathbb{H}}_{112}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}^{2}+2u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}+u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+{\mathbb{H}}_{122}\\
\rho u_{2}^{3}+3u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+{\mathbb{H}}_{222}\end{bmatrix},\end{gathered}

(92)

which is not closed since the heat flux tensor must still be supplied via the microscopic portion
g
:

\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{H}}_{111}\\
{\mathbb{H}}_{112}\\
{\mathbb{H}}_{122}\\
{\mathbb{H}}_{222}\end{pmatrix}=\varepsilon\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{3}\\
\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{2}\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)\\
\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{2}\\
\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{3}\end{pmatrix}\,g\,dv_{1}\,dv_{2}.

(93)

In (
91
),
\varepsilon
is the Knudsen number,
\nu
is the Prandtl number, and
\tau
is the collision parameter.

To close fluid system (
91
)–(
92
), we need to obtain an evolution equation for the microscopic portion,
g
, from equation (
90
). An orthogonal basis for the nullspace (
19
) is given by

Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
:

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\,\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M},\quad\left(\frac{v_{1}-u_{1}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M},\quad\left(\frac{v_{2}-u_{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M},\quad\left(\frac{\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{2}}{2T}-1\right)\frac{1}{\rho}\mathcal{M}\,\right\}.

(94)

An orthogonal projection onto this basis for a generic particle density function,
F
, is given by:

\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[F\right]=\left\{A_{1}+\left(\frac{v_{1}-u_{1}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)A_{2}+\left(\frac{v_{2}-u_{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)A_{3}+\left(\frac{\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{2}}{2T}-1\right)A_{4}\right\}{\mathcal{M}}[f],

(95)

where

\displaystyle\left(\,A_{1},\,A_{2},\,A_{3},\,A_{4}\right)\left[F\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\,1,\,\frac{v_{1}-u_{1}}{\sqrt{T}},\,\frac{v_{2}-u_{2}}{\sqrt{T}},\,\frac{\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{2}}{2T}-1\right)F\,dv_{1}\,dv_{2}.

(96)

This projector has the property that

\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)={\mathcal{M}},\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left({\mathcal{G}}\right)={\mathcal{M}},\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left({\mathcal{M}}_{,t}\right)={\mathcal{M}}_{,t},\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(g_{,t}\right)=0,\quad\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(g\right)=0,

(97)

and when applied to (
90
) yields:

{\mathcal{M}}_{,t}+\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}{\mathcal{M}}\right]+\varepsilon\,\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}g\right]=0.

(98)

Subtracting (
98
) from (
90
), dividing by
\varepsilon
, and re-arranging terms yields an evolution equation for the microscopic portion
g
:

\displaystyle g_{,t}+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left({\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}g\right)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\biggl(\tau\,g+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left({\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}{\mathcal{M}}\right)-\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{M}}\right)\biggr),

(99)

where
{\mathcal{I}}
is the identity operator. Note that for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
,
{\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon\right)
, which means the last term in the parenthesis on the right-hand side of (
99
) is

{\mathcal{O}}\left(1\right)
for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.

Just as in 1D, equation (
99
) can be further simplified, since it turns out that it is possible
to analytically compute the orthogonal projection of the Maxwellian transport term:

\begin{gathered}\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left(\,{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}{\mathcal{M}}\,\right)=\Bigr\{\,{\underline{\underline{B}}}:{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}+{\underline{C}}\cdot{{\underline{\nabla}}T}\,\Bigl\}{\mathcal{M}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y}&\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x}&-\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix},\\
{\underline{C}}=\left(\frac{\|{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}\|^{2}}{2T}-2\right)\frac{{\underline{v}}-{\underline{u}}}{T},\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\underline{\underline{B}}}=\frac{1}{2T}\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]-\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)^{2}&\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)\\
\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)\left(v_{2}-u_{2}\right)&-\left(v_{1}-u_{1}\right)^{2}\end{bmatrix}.\end{gathered}

(100)

Substituting (
100
) into (
99
) results in the following micro evolution equation:

\left(\text{2D Micro}\right):\quad g_{,t}+\left({\mathcal{I}}-\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\left({\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}g\right)=-\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\biggl(g-\hat{g}\biggr),\quad\hat{g}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\Bigr\{\,{\underline{\underline{B}}}:{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}+{\underline{C}}\cdot{{\underline{\nabla}}T}\,\Bigl\}{\mathcal{M}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}-{\mathcal{M}}\right).

(101)

For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
,
\hat{g}={\mathcal{O}}\left(1\right)
, and the conclusion of Remark
2

again applies in this 2D case.

5.2
Setup for 2D Micro-Macro Finite Volume Method

Now that the micro-macro formulation for
d_{v}=2
has been established via (
91
) and (
101
),
we turn our attention to the numerical discretization. We introduce a uniform, cell-centered, finite volume mesh in the domain
\left(x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}\right)\times\left(y_{\text{min}},y_{\text{max}}\right)\times\left(\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{min}},\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{max}}\right)\times\left(\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{min}},\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{max}}\right)
:

\begin{gathered}x_{i}:=x_{\text{min}}+\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta x}\quad\forall i\in\left[1,N_{x}\right],\quad y_{j}:=y_{\text{min}}+\left(j-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta y}\quad\forall j=\in\left[1,N_{y}\right],\\
\left[v_{1}\right]_{k}:=\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{min}}+\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta v_{1}}\quad\forall k\in\left[1,N_{v_{1}}\right],\quad\left[v_{2}\right]_{\ell}:=\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{min}}+\left(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta v_{2}}\quad\forall\ell\in\left[1,N_{v_{2}}\right],\end{gathered}

(102)

where

{\Delta x}:=\frac{x_{\text{max}}-x_{\text{min}}}{N_{x}},\quad{\Delta y}:=\frac{y_{\text{max}}-y_{\text{min}}}{N_{y}},\quad{\Delta v_{1}}:=\frac{\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{max}}-\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{min}}}{N_{v_{1}}},\quad{\Delta v_{2}}:=\frac{\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{max}}-\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{min}}}{N_{v_{2}}}.

(103)

At each time level,
t^{n}=n\Delta t
, the macroscopic and microscopic portions of the distribution functions are given:

{\underline{Q^{n}_{ij}}}:=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{ij},\,\rho{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}},\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{E}}^{n}_{ij}}}}\,\right)\quad\text{and}\quad G^{n}_{ijk\ell}\quad\forall i\in\left[1,N_{x}\right],\,\,\,\forall j\in\left[1,N_{y}\right],\,\,\,\forall k\in\left[1,N_{v_{1}}\right],\,\,\,\forall\ell\in\left[1,N_{v_{2}}\right].

(104)

The solution is initialized at time
t=0
and run forward until
t=T_{\text{final}}
.
The time step is chosen as a global constant and is defined as follows:

\Delta t=\text{CFL}\cdot\text{min}\left\{\frac{\Delta x}{\mathcal{V}_{1}},\,\frac{\Delta y}{\mathcal{V}_{2}}\right\},\quad N_{\text{steps}}=\left\lceil\frac{T_{\text{final}}}{\Delta t}\right\rceil\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta t\leftarrow\frac{T_{\text{final}}}{N_{\text{steps}}},\quad\text{CFL}\leftarrow\max\left\{\frac{\mathcal{V}_{1}\,\Delta t}{\Delta x},\,\frac{\mathcal{V}_{2}\,\Delta t}{\Delta y}\right\},

(105)

where
N_{\text{steps}}
is the total number of timesteps needed to advance from
t=0
to
t=T_{\text{final}}
and

\left(\text{CFL number}\right):\quad 0<\text{CFL}<1,\qquad\left(\text{max speed}\right):\quad\begin{cases}\mathcal{V}_{1}:=\max\left\{\bigl|\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{min}}\bigr|,\bigl|\left[v_{1}\right]_{\text{max}}\bigr|\right\},\\
\mathcal{V}_{2}:=\max\left\{\bigl|\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{min}}\bigr|,\bigl|\left[v_{2}\right]_{\text{max}}\bigr|\right\}.\end{cases}

5.3
2D Micro Finite Volume Method

In this section, we demonstrate how to update the current value of the micro portion,
G^{n}
, to its value at the next time step,
G^{n+1}
:

\text{Input:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n}_{ij}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{ij},\,\rho{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}},\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{E}}^{n}_{ij}}}}\,\right)\quad\text{and}\quad G^{n}_{ijk\ell}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Output:}\quad G^{n+1}_{ijk\ell}.

(107)

To guarantee stability up to a CFL number of one as defined in (
105
), we elect
to solve the micro equation (
101
) using operator splitting:

\displaystyle\Delta t:

\displaystyle\quad g_{,t}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(v_{1}\,g_{,x}\right)-v_{1}\,g_{,x}

\displaystyle\left(\,G^{n}\rightarrow G^{\star}\,\right),

(108)

\displaystyle\Delta t:

\displaystyle\quad g_{,t}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(v_{2}\,g_{,y}\right)-v_{2}\,g_{,y}

\displaystyle\left(\,G^{\star}\rightarrow G^{\star\star}\,\right),

(109)

\displaystyle\Delta t:

\displaystyle\quad g_{,t}=-\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}\biggl(g-\hat{g}\biggr)

\displaystyle\left(\,G^{\star\star}\rightarrow G^{n+1}\,\right).

(110)

For the first part of the operator splitting as described by (
108
), we first approximate the term
v\,g_{,x}
via simple upwinding:

Z^{n}_{{ijk\ell}}=\left[v_{1}\right]_{k}^{-}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{\left(i+1,j,k,\ell\right)}-G^{n}_{\left(i,j,k,\ell\right)}}{\Delta x}\right)+\left[v_{1}\right]_{k}^{+}\left(\frac{G^{n}_{\left(i,j,k,\ell\right)}-G^{n}_{\left(i-1,j,k,\ell\right)}}{\Delta x}\right).

(111)

We also compute the projection of this term on the nullspace of the collision operator via

\widehat{Z}^{n}_{ijk\ell}=\left\{\left[A_{1}\right]^{n}_{ij}+\left(\frac{{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{ij}}}\right)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}\\
A_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{n}_{ij}+\left(\frac{\Bigl\|{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}\Bigr\|^{2}}{2T^{n}_{ij}}-1\right)\left[A_{4}\right]^{n}_{ij}\right\}{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ijk\ell},

(112)

where

\left(\,\left[A_{1}\right]^{n}_{ij},\,\,\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}\\
A_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{n}_{ij},\,\,\left[A_{4}\right]^{n}_{ij}\,\right)=\frac{\Delta v_{1}\,\Delta v_{2}}{\rho^{n}_{ij}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{v_{1}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{v_{2}}}\left(\,1,\,\,\frac{{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{ij}}},\,\,\frac{\Bigl\|{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}\Bigr\|^{2}}{2T^{n}_{ij}}-1\,\right)Z^{n}_{ijk\ell}.

(113)

Using forward Euler in time yields the following approximation to (
108
):

G^{\star}_{ijk\ell}=G^{n}_{ijk\ell}+\Delta t\left(\widehat{Z}^{n}_{ijk\ell}-Z^{n}_{ijk\ell}\right).

(114)

For the second part of the operator splitting as described by (
109
), we first approximate the term
v\,g_{,y}
via simple upwinding:

Z^{\star}_{ijk\ell}=\min\left(0,\,\left[v_{2}\right]_{\ell}\right)\left(\frac{G^{\star}_{\left(i,j+1,k,\ell\right)}-G^{\star}_{\left(i,j,k,\ell\right)}}{\Delta y}\right)+\max\left(0,\,\left[v_{2}\right]_{\ell}\right)\left(\frac{G^{\star}_{\left(i,j,k,\ell\right)}-G^{\star}_{\left(i,j-1,k,\ell\right)}}{\Delta y}\right).

(115)

We also compute the projection of this term on the nullspace of the collision operator via

\widehat{Z}^{\star}_{ijk\ell}=\left\{\left[A_{1}\right]^{\star}_{ij}+\left(\frac{{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{ij}}}\right)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}\\
A_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\star}_{ij}+\left(\frac{\Bigl\|{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}\Bigr\|^{2}}{2T^{n}_{ij}}-1\right)\left[A_{4}\right]^{\star}_{ij}\right\}{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ijk\ell},

(116)

where

\left(\,\left[A_{1}\right]^{\star}_{ij},\,\,\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}\\
A_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\star}_{ij},\,\,\left[A_{4}\right]^{\star}_{ij}\,\right)=\frac{\Delta v_{1}\,\Delta v_{2}}{\rho^{n}_{ij}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{v_{1}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{v_{2}}}\left(\,1,\,\,\frac{{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}}{\sqrt{T^{n}_{ij}}},\,\,\frac{\Bigl\|{\underline{v_{k\ell}}}-{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}}\Bigr\|^{2}}{2T^{n}_{ij}}-1\,\right)Z^{\star}_{ijk\ell}.

(117)

Using forward Euler in time yields the following approximation to (
109
):

G^{\star\star}_{ijk\ell}=G^{\star}_{ijk\ell}+\Delta t\left(\widehat{Z}^{\star}_{ijk\ell}-Z^{\star}_{ijk\ell}\right).

(118)

To complete the final part of the operator splitting as described by (
110
), we first construct an approximate
\hat{g}
from (
101
) on the numerical grid:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{G}_{ijk\ell}^{n}&=-\frac{1}{\tau^{n}_{ij}}\Bigg({\underline{\underline{B^{n}_{ijk\ell}}}}:{\underline{\underline{\sigma^{n}_{ij}}}}\,+\,{\underline{C^{n}_{ijk\ell}}}\cdot\left(\frac{T^{n}_{\left(i+1/2,\,j\right)}-T^{n}_{\left(i-1/2,\,j\right)}}{\Delta x},\,\frac{T^{n}_{\left(i,\,j-1/2\right)}-T^{n}_{\left(i,\,j-1/2\right)}}{\Delta y}\right)\Bigg)\,{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ijk\ell}\\
&+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{G}^{n}_{ijk\ell}-{\mathcal{M}}^{n}_{ijk\ell}\right),\qquad T^{n}_{\left(i+1/2,\,j\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{n}_{i+1\,j}+T^{n}_{ij}\right),\qquad T^{n}_{\left(i,\,j-1/2\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(T^{n}_{i\,j+1}+T^{n}_{ij}\right),\end{split}

(119)

where

{\underline{\underline{\sigma^{n}_{ij}}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}[2]\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}u^{n}_{1}}{\Delta x}-\frac{\Delta^{y}_{ij}u^{n}_{2}}{\Delta y}&\frac{\Delta^{y}_{ij}u^{n}_{1}}{\Delta y}+\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}u^{n}_{2}}{\Delta x}\\
\frac{\Delta^{y}_{ij}u^{n}_{1}}{\Delta y}+\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}u^{n}_{2}}{\Delta x}&-\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}u^{n}_{1}}{\Delta x}+\frac{\Delta^{y}_{ij}u^{n}_{2}}{\Delta y}\end{bmatrix}\quad\Delta^{x}_{ij}u^{n}:=\frac{u^{n}_{(i+1,\,j)}-u^{n}_{(i-1,\,j)}}{2},\quad\Delta^{y}_{ij}u^{n}:=\frac{u^{n}_{(i,\,j+1)}-u^{n}_{(i,\,j-1)}}{2},

(120)

and
{\underline{\underline{B}}}
and
{\underline{\underline{C}}}
are defined in (
100
).
Lastly, the numerical micro solution,
G^{\star\star}
, is updated to its new value,

,
by applying to micro equation (

) a backward Euler time discretization:

\displaystyle G^{n+1}_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{ij}}\right)G^{\star\star}_{ijk\ell}+\left(\frac{\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{ij}}{\varepsilon+\Delta t\,\tau^{n}_{ij}}\right)\widehat{G}^{n}_{ijk\ell}.

(121)

Note that this update is
asymptotic-preserving

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
of this expression for fixed mesh parameters,
\Delta t
,
\Delta x
, and
\Delta v
,
and arrive at the following result:

\displaystyle G^{n+1}_{ijk\ell}\rightarrow\widehat{G}^{n}_{ijk\ell}\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

(122)

In this section, we demonstrate how to update the current value of the macro variables at
t=t^{n}

to the new values at
t=t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t
:

\text{Input:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n}_{ij}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n}_{ij},\,\rho{\underline{u^{n}_{ij}}},\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{E}}^{n}_{ij}}}}\,\right)\quad\text{and}\quad{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{H}}^{n+1}_{ij}}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Output:}\quad{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{ij}}}=\left(\,\rho^{n+1}_{ij},\,\rho{\underline{u^{n+1}_{ij}}},\,{\underline{\underline{{\mathbb{E}}^{n+1}_{ij}}}}\,\right),

(123)

where the micro portion has already been updated to

\begin{pmatrix}[1.5]{\mathbb{H}}_{111}\\

\left(v_{1k}-u^{n}_{1i}\right)^{2}\left(v_{2\ell}-u^{n}_{2j}\right)\\
\left(v_{1k}-u^{n}_{1i}\right)\left(v_{2\ell}-u^{n}_{2j}\right)^{2}\\
\left(v_{2\ell}-u^{n}_{2j}\right)^{3}\end{pmatrix}\,G^{n+1}_{ijk\ell}.

{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}_{,t}=\frac{\tau\left(1-\nu\right)}{2\varepsilon}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}-{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
-2{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\

(125)

\displaystyle\Delta t:

\displaystyle\quad{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}={\underline{0}}

\displaystyle\left(\,{\underline{Q^{\star}}}\rightarrow{\underline{Q^{\star\star}}}\,\right),

(126)

\displaystyle\Delta t:

\displaystyle\quad{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{g}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,y}={\underline{0}}

\displaystyle\left(\,{\underline{Q^{\star\star}}}\rightarrow{\underline{Q^{\star\star\star}}}\,\right),

(127)

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{2}:

\displaystyle\quad\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}_{,t}=\frac{\tau\left(1-\nu\right)}{2\varepsilon}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}-{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\

\displaystyle\left(\,{\underline{Q^{\star\star\star}}}\rightarrow{\underline{Q^{n+1}}}\,\right).

The first collision step is discretized using the L-stable TR-BDF2 scheme

, which can be compactly written as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}^{\star}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+W^{n}_{ij}&0&1-W^{n}_{ij}\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\underline{Q^{\star\star}_{ij}}}&={\underline{Q^{\star}_{ij}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\,{\underline{\left[\mathcal{F}_{1}\right]^{\star}_{{\left(i+\frac{1}{2},\,j\right)}}}}-{\underline{\left[\mathcal{F}_{1}\right]^{\star}_{{\left(i-\frac{1}{2},\,j\right)}}}}\,\right)-{\Delta t}\begin{pmatrix}0,\,0,\,0,\,\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}_{111}\right)}{\Delta x},\,\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}_{112}\right)}{\Delta x},\,\frac{\Delta^{x}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}_{122}\right)}{\Delta x}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(131)

(132)

where the heat flux values are computed from (

\begin{gathered}\Delta^{x}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{H}\right)={\mathbb{H}_{\left(i+1/2,\,j\right)}-\mathbb{H}_{\left(i-1/2,\,j\right)}},\quad\Delta^{y}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{H}\right)={\mathbb{H}_{\left(i,\,j+1/2\right)}-\mathbb{H}_{\left(i,\,j-1/2\right)}},\\

To compute the fluxes via KFVS, we assume that at each cell interface, the distribution is Gaussian with moments
matching the first six moments in each cell:
{\underline{m}}\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right):=\left(1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{2}^{2},v_{1}v_{2},v_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\underline{\left[{\mathcal{F}_{1}}\right]^{\star}_{i-\frac{1}{2}j}}}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0}v_{1}\,{\underline{m}}\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\,{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}_{ij}\,dv_{1}\,dv_{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{1}\,{\underline{m}}\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\,{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}_{i-1j}\,dv_{1}\,dv_{2}\\

(134)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\underline{\left[{\mathcal{F}_{2}}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij-\frac{1}{2}}}}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0}v_{2}\,{\underline{m}}\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\,{\mathcal{G}}^{\star\star}_{ij}\,dv_{2}\,dv_{1}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}v_{2}\,{\underline{m}}\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\,{\mathcal{G}}^{\star\star}_{ij-1}\,dv_{2}\,dv_{1}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left[a_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij}\left[J_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij}+\left(1+\left[b_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij}\right)\left[K_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij}-\left[a_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij-1}\left[J_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij-1}+\left(1-\left[b_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij-1}\right)\left[K_{2}\right]^{\star\star}_{ij-1}\right),\end{split}

(135)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{matrix}a_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathbb{P}}_{11}}{\pi\rho}}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho u_{1}^{2}}{2{\mathbb{P}}_{11}}\right)\\

\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+2{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+2{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+\frac{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{12}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{11}}\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad K_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{1}^{3}+3u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+2u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}^{2}+u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+2u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\end{bmatrix},\\
\begin{matrix}a_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathbb{P}}_{22}}{\pi\rho}}\exp\left(-\frac{\rho u_{2}^{2}}{2{\mathbb{P}}_{22}}\right)\\
b_{2}=\mathrm{erf}\left(u_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{2{\mathbb{P}}_{22}}}\right)\end{matrix},\quad J_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+\frac{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{12}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{22}}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+2{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+2{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad K_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}+2u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}^{2}+u_{1}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}+2u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{12}\\
\rho u_{2}^{3}+3u_{2}{\mathbb{P}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(136)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbb{P}}_{11}\\

6.2
Lid Driven Cavity Problem

Now that we have established basic convergence of the micro-macro finite volume method, we apply it to a standard
test case in fluid dynamics: the lid-driven cavity problem. In this setup, a gas is confined in a box with four impermeable walls (modeled here as diffusely reflecting walls), and the top wall is moving to the right at a constant prescribed speed. The moving top wall induces a circular flow inside the box. Following the work of Rana, Torrilhon, and Struchtrup
Rana2013ACavity
,
we consider this problem with an intermediate Knudsen number,
\varepsilon=0.08
, for which dynamics beyond Navier-Stokes-Fourier occur. We demonstrate that the micro-macro approximation with a coarse mesh in velocity can produce the correct solution. The main challenge, just as in the R13 model considered by Rana et al.
Rana2013ACavity
, is the implementation of the boundary conditions.

The domain we consider is a unit square:
(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
.
At the kinetic level, we assume that the four edges of the unit square are diffusely reflecting walls,
meaning that the walls are in thermodynamic equilibrium with the following Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution functions:

\displaystyle={\mathcal{M}}^{\text{lft}}\left(t,y,v_{1},v_{2}\right)=\frac{\rho_{\text{lft}}(t,y)}{{2\pi T_{\text{wall}}}}\exp\left[-\frac{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}{2T_{\text{wall}}}\right]\quad

\displaystyle v_{1}>0

,

\displaystyle v_{1}<0

,

(150)

\displaystyle f\left(t,x,y=0,v_{1},v_{2}\right)

\displaystyle v_{2}>0

,

(151)

\displaystyle f\left(t,x,y=1,v_{1},v_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{M}}^{\text{top}}\left(t,x,v_{1},v_{2}\right)=\frac{\rho_{\text{top}}(t,x)}{{2\pi T_{\text{wall}}}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(v_{1}-U_{\text{lid}}\right)^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}{2T_{\text{wall}}}\right]\quad

7
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a finite volume method for solving the kinetic Boltzmann equation using the
micro-macro decomposition approach first developed for the one-dimensional Boltzmann-BGK equation by Bennoune, Lemou, and Mieussens
Bennoune2008UniformlyAsymptotics
. In particular, we extend the micro-macro approach to two spatial and two velocity dimensions, the ES-BGK collision operator, and examples with non-trivial boundary conditions.
The scheme developed in this work was based on coupling a microscopic kinetic equation with a macroscopic fluid equation. A projection operator was used to separate the macroscopic and microscopic quantities. The updated microscopic quantity was coupled to the macroscopic update equation via the heat flux variable, which is obtained by taking the moment of the microscopic distribution function. The updated macroscopic variables were then used to calculate the projection operator, which affects the microscopic update.
As it appears in both the micro and macro equations, the collision operator was handled via L-stable implicit time discretizations. At the same time, the remaining transport terms were computed via kinetic flux vector splitting (for the macro equations) and upwind differencing (for the micro equation). Operator splitting was used both in the update’s micro and macro portions to achieve optimal linear stability. The resulting scheme was applied to various test cases in 1D and 2D. In both 1D and 2D, we performed a convergence analysis using the method of manufactured solutions. We also tested the scheme on diffusely reflecting wall boundary conditions, showing how such boundary conditions can be incorporated into the micro-macro framework. The 2D version of the code was parallelized via MPI, and we presented weak and strong scaling studies with varying numbers of processors.
Ongoing and future work will focus on extending the scheme to higher orders and problems in complex geometries.
The complete companion code to this paper, including all the presented examples, can be found in reference
code:RossmanithSar2025
.
