Association of the remnant cholesterol inflammatory index and frailty with cardiometabolic multimorbidity among Chinese middle-aged and elderly populations: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Shiyuan Zhao, Zhenhan Wang, Liping Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is the co-occurrence of two or more cardiometabolic disorders including heart disease, diabetes and stroke. While the remnant cholesterol inflammatory index (RCII) and frailty have been independently associated with CMM, their joint association with CMM has not been fully elucidated. This study examined the joint-category associations of RCII or CumRCII with frailty in relation to incident CMM to characterize their combined epidemiological relationships with CMM risk.MethodsBased on the 2011–2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), 5,779 participants were included in the final analysis. CumRCII represented time-weighted cumulative RCII exposure during 2011–2015. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to calculate CMM incidence across exposure groups. Multivariable Cox models were used to evaluate the joint-category associations of RCII or CumRCII with frailty in relation to incident CMM. Additive and multiplicative interactions were assessed separately. Restricted cubic spline models investigated potential nonlinear associations of RCII and CumRCII with CMM risk. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used in an exploratory analysis of in-sample model discrimination. The stability of the findings was assessed through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ResultsA total of 510 participants were diagnosed with CMM. Higher RCII or CumRCII in combination with frailty was associated with increased CMM incidence. RCII and CumRCII exhibited positive nonlinear associations with CMM risk. Compared with individuals with low RCII and without frailty, those with concurrent high RCII and frailty showed a higher risk of incident CMM (HR = 2.622, 95% CI 1.972–3.488). Similar results were observed for CumRCII (above optimal threshold) combined with frailty (HR = 2.849, 95% CI 2.178–3.726). However, additive and multiplicative interaction analyses did not remain statistically significant after full adjustment. The model incorporating CumRCII and frailty had a modestly higher in-sample AUC than the CumRCII-only model (0.757 vs. 0.740; ΔAUC = 0.017; DeLong P < 0.001). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings.ConclusionIn joint-category Cox analyses, participants with concurrent elevated RCII or CumRCII and frailty showed the highest observed risk of incident CMM. These findings suggest that lipid-inflammatory burden and frailty may represent complementary dimensions of CMM risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06356-w.Keywords: Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Remnant cholesterol inflammatory index, Frailty, CHARLS

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) refers to having two or more cardiometabolic disorders, such as heart disease, diabetes and stroke [1]. With an aging population, the prevalence of these conditions has increased substantially [2]. CMM severely impairs patients’ physiological functions and increases psychological and economic burdens due to polypharmacy and adverse drug reactions, thereby reducing quality of life [3, 4].

As a novel biomarker combining lipid metabolic disorders and chronic inflammation, the remnant cholesterol inflammatory index (RCII) has been increasingly associated with the occurrence and progression of CMM. Remnant cholesterol promotes atherosclerosis through foam cell formation, vascular lipid deposition, platelet aggregation, and microvessel formation [5]. Persistent atherosclerosis triggers inflammatory mediator release, impairing pancreatic β-cells, inducing insulin resistance, worsening lipid disorders, and raising cardiovascular disease and stroke risks [6–8]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), a key inflammatory indicator, exerts a mediating role in vascular injury through the activation of pro-inflammatory pathways and impairment of vasodilation capacity [9]. Evidence confirms that high-sensitivity CRP is a strong predictor of cardiovascular diseases, stroke [10, 11], and diabetes [12]. Compared with RC or hs-CRP alone, RCII may better integrate lipid-related atherogenic burden and systemic inflammation, thereby capturing the lipid-inflammatory axis involved in cardiometabolic disease progression. Because CMM generally develops gradually over time, a single baseline RCII measurement may not fully capture sustained lipid-inflammatory exposure. In contrast, time-weighted cumulative RCII exposure (CumRCII) summarizes exposure over the 2011–2015 assessment period and may better characterize cumulative metabolic-inflammatory burden before CMM onset. Consistent with this concept, a Chinese cohort study found that cumulative average RCII significantly increased CMM risk [13]. Therefore, both baseline RCII and CumRCII were considered in the present study.

Frailty is a physiological syndrome characterized by multisystem deficit accumulation, reduced physiological reserve, and increased vulnerability to stressors [14]. The frailty index (FI) quantifies health deficits across physical, functional, psychological, and cognitive domains, and has been widely used to evaluate age-related health decline in clinical and epidemiological studies [15, 16]. Emerging evidence suggests that higher FI and accelerated frailty progression are associated with increased CMM risk [17] and frailty is also more prevalent among individuals with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and stroke [18–20]. Thus, frailty should not be merely regarded as a simple functional decline among the elderly; instead, it serves as an indicator reflecting the biological vulnerability of the individual. Recent evidence further indicates that frailty-related constructs may inform CMM risk assessment when considered together with metabolic burden [21]. Therefore, the joint-category assessment of RCII and frailty may characterize incident CMM risk from two complementary dimensions: metabolic-inflammatory burden and host physiological vulnerability.

Although previous studies have examined inflammatory-metabolic markers or frailty separately in relation to cardiometabolic outcomes, evidence regarding the joint-category associations of RCII or CumRCII with frailty in relation to incident CMM remains limited. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the joint-category associations of RCII or CumRCII with frailty in relation to incident CMM and to further assess additive and multiplicative interactions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used 2011–2020 cohort data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to explore how combinations of different RCII measurement methods and baseline frailty correlate with CMM incidence. Baseline RCII and FI were derived from the 2011 CHARLS wave. CumRCII was derived from RCII measurements obtained in 2011 and 2015 to represent time-weighted cumulative RCII exposure during the 2011–2015 exposure assessment period. Incident CMM was subsequently ascertained during follow-up from the 2015 to 2020 survey waves. Both analyses used the same study population and covariate adjustment strategy. Among the 17,705 participants enrolled in the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey, 7,399 individuals were excluded for the following reasons: missing age information or age younger than 45 years, prevalent CMM or incomplete CMM data before 2015, and missing CMM follow-up records from 2015 to 2020. Among the remaining 10,306 eligible participants, an additional 4,527 participants were excluded owing to missing 2011 or 2015 RCII data or absent baseline frailty assessment data. Ultimately, 5,779 participants were retained for the longitudinal analysis. Baseline characteristics of included versus excluded eligible participants are shown in Table S15. All research staff of the CHARLS received standardized training before data collection, and all respondents or their legal representatives signed informed consent. Definitions of RCII, time-weighted cumulative average RCII and FI

Baseline RCII was calculated using fasting blood biomarkers measured in the 2011 CHARLS wave. RCII was computed as: [RC(mg/dL) × hs-CRP(mg/L)] / 10 [22]. RC = TC − HDL-C − LDL-C. CumRCII was defined as the time-weighted cumulative exposure to RCII during 2011–2015 and was calculated using the trapezoidal rule : CumRCII = [(RCII2011 + RCII2015) / 2] × 4. The FI established in this study comprises 29 indicators, covering physical function, clinical symptoms, disease status (excluding heart disease, stroke, and diabetes), activity limitations, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function [23]. Twenty-eight indicators were binary (1 = impairment); cognitive function was scored 0–1. FI was the sum of impairments divided by 29 (range 0–1). Specific details regarding the construction of the frailty index are shown in Tables S1 and S2. Following the FI criteria used in prior studies, participants were divided into two groups: the non-frail group (FI < 0.25) and the frail group (FI ≥ 0.25) [24].

Definition of cardiometabolic multimorbidity

The outcome measure of this study was CMM, which refers to the concurrent presence of two or more conditions among heart disease, diabetes, and stroke [1]. The event time was assigned as the first follow-up wave in which participants reported a physician diagnosis. Diagnoses of heart disease and stroke were ascertained using standardized questionnaires: “Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” and “Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with stroke?” Any of the following criteria can be used to diagnose diabetes: HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L), random blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), and an affirmative answer was given to this question “Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with diabetes or high blood sugar?”

Covariates

Covariate selection for the multivariable models was guided by previous epidemiological evidence, clinical relevance, and a DAG-informed conceptual causal framework (Figure S3). Potential confounders of CMM risk were assessed through standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. The adjusted models included demographic and socioeconomic factors (age, gender, residence, marital status, and education), lifestyle and health-related factors (BMI, sleep duration, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and kidney disease), and biochemical indicators (triglycerides, uric acid, and creatinine). TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and hs-CRP were summarized in Table 1 as baseline laboratory characteristics but were not adjusted for in RCII or CumRCII models.

Statistical analysis

Research population baseline characteristics were analyzed according to CMM status. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t test. Skewed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared using the χ² test. Baseline RCII was dichotomized at the median in accordance with previous RCII studies [25]. Because no universally accepted cutoff has been established for CumRCII, the optimal threshold was determined using maximally selected rank statistics. Participants with different combined exposures were divided into four groups to assess each group’s CMM development risk. Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank test were used to evaluate group differences across joint categories.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the associations of RCII and CumRCII with incident CMM, both separately and across joint categories with frailty status. Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary Cox regression results. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests, including both variable-specific and global tests. Given that CMM onset was ascertained at discrete CHARLS follow-up waves, we performed a wave-based discrete-time survival sensitivity analysis using pooled logistic regression, with the follow-up interval included as a categorical variable.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) analyses were performed to investigate the potential dose–response relationships of continuous RCII and CumRCII with incident CMM. We compared alternative RCS models based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the four-knot model yielded the optimal model fit. Knots were placed at the 5th, 25th, 75th, and 95th percentiles of the respective exposure distributions. Hazard ratios were calculated using the median value of each exposure as the reference (HR = 1). We assessed the overall association and conducted a formal test for nonlinearity.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to compare the in-sample discriminative ability of the covariate-only model and expanded models including RCII, CumRCII, frailty, both RCII and frailty, or both CumRCII and frailty. The baseline model included age, gender, education, residence, marital status, body mass index, smoking status, drinking status, sleep duration, hypertension, kidney disease, triglycerides, uric acid, and creatinine. DeLong’s test was adopted to compare AUC values across different models.

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess whether the RCII–frailty and CumRCII–frailty joint-category associations varied across clinically relevant strata. In total, 18 multiplicative interaction tests were conducted across nine subgroup variables for the two joint-category variables. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure was used to correct interaction P-values for multiple testing. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations implemented in the mice package in R, with all variables having under 20% missing values (Table S3). Five imputed datasets were generated with 50 iterations. Participants with missing outcome, RCII, or frailty data were excluded before imputation. All statistical comparisons were two-sided, with a threshold of P < 0.05 adopted to denote statistical significance. R (version 4.5.2) was employed to carry out all statistical analyses in the current study.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 5,779 eligible participants completed exposure assessment during 2011–2015 and were subsequently followed for incident CMM from 2015 to 2020. Participants with CMM were significantly older and had a higher proportion of females, urban residents, and non-married. They also had higher BMI and higher prevalence of hypertension and kidney disease, as well as elevated levels of blood lipids, UA, and CRP (Table 1). Participants in the highest RCII quartile (Q4) were older, more likely to be rural residents and have hypertension, and exhibited higher levels of TC, TG, UA, and CRP (Table S4). Frail participants were older and more likely to be female, rural, married, and had lower educational attainment (Table S5).

Association between baseline RCII and risk of CMM

Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated a steady increase in CMM incidence with the progression of RCII quartiles (Figure S2A). The relationship between RCII and the risk of CMM was analyzed with Cox proportional hazards regression models (Table S6). In the fully adjusted model for confounders, participants in the highest RCII quartile had the highest risk of developing CMM (HR = 1.812, 95% CI 1.330–2.468).

Restricted cubic spline analysis

Unadjusted RCS analyses are presented in Fig. 2a, and covariate-adjusted RCS results are displayed in Fig. 2b and c. Using median RCII as the reference, multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between RCII and incident CMM. Similarly, RCS fitting identified a positive nonlinear relationship between CumRCII and incident CMM (Figure S4). To evaluate whether the RCS results were driven by extreme right-tail values, sensitivity RCS analyses were performed after excluding participants with exposure values above the 99th percentile. After excluding these extreme observations, the nonlinear patterns for both RCII and CumRCII remained consistent (Figure S5).

Joint-category associations of RCII or CumRCII with frailty in relation to incident CMM

Kaplan–Meier estimates of CMM incidence across the RCII–frailty and CumRCII–frailty joint categories are presented in Fig. 3. Highest CMM risk occurred in participants with either RCII (above median) or CumRCII (above the optimal threshold) who also had frailty. Cox models assessed the joint-category associations of RCII and frailty with CMM risk. After full adjustment, participants with both high RCII (above median) and frailty had the highest risk (HR = 2.622, 95% CI 1.972–3.488), followed by those with frailty but low RCII (HR = 1.985, 95% CI 1.435–2.746) and those with high RCII but without frailty (HR = 1.570, 95% CI 1.223–2.015) (Table 2). To confirm and further extend these findings, we examined the joint-category associations of CumRCII (optimal threshold) and frailty using identical Cox regression approaches. Consistent with the above results, individuals with both high CumRCII (above the optimal threshold) and frailty demonstrated the highest risk of incident CMM (HR = 2.849, 95% CI 2.178–3.726), whereas those with frailty but low CumRCII (HR = 1.659, 95% CI 1.255–2.192) and high CumRCII without frailty (HR = 1.541, 95% CI 1.222–1.943) showed progressively attenuated effect estimates (Table 3). These findings suggest that participants with concurrent elevated RCII or CumRCII and frailty had the highest observed risk of incident CMM in the joint-category Cox analyses.

Interaction analyses between RCII or CumRCII and frailty in relation to incident CMM risk

No significant additive or multiplicative interaction between RCII and frailty was observed in any model (Table S7). For CumRCII and frailty, both additive and multiplicative interactions were statistically significant only in the unadjusted model. The additive interaction estimates were RERI = 1.33 (95% CI 0.31–2.36), AP = 0.30 (95% CI 0.13–0.47), and SI = 1.43 (95% CI 1.05–1.80). After full adjustment, the additive interaction was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, with RERI = 0.04 (95% CI − 0.77–0.84), AP = 0.01 (95% CI − 0.29–0.32), and SI = 1.01 (95% CI 0.73–1.29); the multiplicative interaction also lost statistical significance (Table S8).

ROC analysis of model discrimination

An exploratory ROC analysis was conducted to compare the in-sample discriminative ability of the covariate-only model and the expanded models for incident CMM during follow-up (Fig. 4). The corresponding AUCs were 0.727 for the RCII model, 0.737 for the frailty model, 0.742 for the model including both RCII and frailty, 0.740 for the CumRCII model, and 0.757 for the model including both CumRCII and frailty. DeLong’s test showed that adding frailty to the CumRCII model significantly improved discrimination relative to the CumRCII-only model (P < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant differences were identified in the remaining pairwise model comparisons (Table S11).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to assess whether the RCII–frailty and CumRCII–frailty joint-category associations with incident CMM varied across nine predefined subgroups (Fig. 5). Among the 18 multiplicative interaction tests, only the interaction between the CumRCII–frailty joint-category variable and BMI remained statistically significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction (Table S14).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the findings, CumRCII was dichotomized using the optimal cutoff to examine its association with incident CMM and using the median cutoff to evaluate its joint-category association with frailty. Figure S2B demonstrated that participants with CumRCII above the optimal cutoff had a higher incidence of CMM. Further analysis in Figure S2C revealed that individuals with both high CumRCII (above the median) and frailty exhibited the highest CMM incidence. In Model 3 (Table S9), the high-CumRCII group had a higher risk of incident CMM (HR = 1.620, 95% CI 1.340–1.959). Similarly, when CumRCII was dichotomized using the median cutoff and jointly categorized with frailty, the effect estimates were comparable, supporting the consistency of the findings across different CumRCII categorization strategies (Table S10). The wave-based discrete-time survival sensitivity analysis yielded results consistent with those of the primary Cox proportional hazards models, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Table S12). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests. No evidence of violation was observed for the main exposure variables in either the RCII–frailty or CumRCII–frailty joint-category models. The global tests also did not indicate significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Table S13).

Discussion

Using prospective data from the CHARLS cohort, we examined the associations of baseline RCII and CumRCII exposure with incident CMM, both individually and jointly with baseline frailty. Higher RCII and CumRCII were nonlinearly associated with incident CMM, and participants with concurrent elevated RCII or CumRCII and frailty had the highest observed risk. However, neither additive nor multiplicative interaction remained statistically significant after multivariable adjustment. The model including both CumRCII and frailty had the highest observed AUC, although its discrimination was significantly better only than that of the CumRCII-only model. Subgroup analyses suggested possible heterogeneity, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously because most nominal interactions were no longer significant after FDR correction. The stronger association observed among participants with BMI < 24 should not be interpreted as evidence that higher BMI is protective, as lower BMI in middle-aged and older adults may reflect frailty, unintentional weight loss, underlying illness, or residual confounding.

RCII integrates remnant cholesterol and hs-CRP, thereby reflecting both remnant cholesterol-related atherogenic burden and systemic low-grade inflammation. These two biological domains are closely related to the development of cardiometabolic diseases. Remnant cholesterol has been associated with atherosclerotic processes, vascular lipid accumulation, inflammatory activation, and thrombotic tendency [5, 26–29]. Hs-CRP can disrupt insulin signaling, induce insulin resistance, and is strongly associated with increased coronary plaque burden and plaque vulnerability [30–33]. Population-based epidemiological and Mendelian randomization studies have shown that high RC concentrations are associated with higher risks of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, and stroke independent of LDL-C [34]. Cohort studies further show that higher RC predicts diabetes risk [35]. Recent studies have revealed that cumulative CRP is significantly and positively correlated with the risk of incident CMM [36]. A cohort study including 5,150 participants found cumulative average RCII independently raised CMM risk [13], which is consistent with our findings.

Frailty is closely related to the occurrence and progression of CMM. Longitudinal studies suggest that frailty accelerates the progression from a healthy state to first CMM and from single cardiometabolic disease to multimorbidity. Each 0.1-unit increase in FI has been associated with a 14%–48% higher risk of multimorbidity [17]. Mendelian randomization studies indicate that higher FI increases the risks of coronary artery disease, diabetes, and stroke [37]. Findings from a UK-based cohort study further suggest that frailty predicts both CMM incidence and mortality [38]. These studies indicate that frailty reflects multisystem deficit accumulation, biological aging, reduced physiological reserve, and impaired resilience to metabolic and inflammatory stress. Recent evidence also suggests that combining metabolic indicators with frailty-related constructs may improve the identification of individuals at high risk of incident CMM [21]. Chronic low-grade inflammation may link cardiometabolic dysfunction and frailty through vascular and metabolic impairment, as well as muscle loss and functional decline [39]. Frailty and cardiovascular aging may also share pathways involving metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular aging [40]. These findings suggest that metabolic-inflammatory burden and frailty may represent related but distinct dimensions of CMM risk.

Compared with a single baseline RCII measurement, CumRCII summarizes sustained lipid-inflammatory exposure during 2011–2015, whereas baseline frailty reflects multisystem vulnerability and reduced physiological reserve. The model including both CumRCII and frailty yielded the highest observed in-sample AUC during follow-up (0.757, 95% CI 0.739–0.775), although the improvement was modest and statistically significant only relative to the CumRCII-only model. Therefore, their modest incremental discrimination requires cautious interpretation and external validation.

The present study has several notable strengths. First, this research was based on a large nationwide prospective cohort with a clear temporal design, which allowed baseline frailty to be assessed at the beginning of the 2011–2015 CumRCII exposure assessment period, followed by CMM ascertainment. Second, RCII can be derived from routinely measured blood biomarkers, and frailty can be assessed using standardized instruments; therefore, their joint evaluation may be feasible in future epidemiological studies. Third, we incorporated CumRCII into the analysis, which reflects cumulative exposure to remnant cholesterol-related inflammatory burden rather than a single baseline measurement alone, providing a more comprehensive assessment of long-term metabolic-inflammatory exposure.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, physician-diagnosed heart disease and stroke were ascertained via self-reported information from biennial CHARLS follow-up interviews. Given the lack of precise onset dates, event time was approximated using the first survey wave recording incident CMM, which may introduce interval censoring. Additionally, diabetes was defined by self-reported physician diagnosis or laboratory evidence, whereas heart disease and stroke relied solely on self-reported clinical diagnoses. While this ascertainment strategy is commonly used in CHARLS longitudinal analyses, residual outcome misclassification remains possible. Second, although analyses were adjusted for multiple established confounders, medication history was not incorporated into the primary models. Detailed dietary data were not available within the CHARLS database, and physical activity records remained incomplete. Accordingly, these variables could not be entered into the main multivariable models, so residual confounding originating from unmeasured or inadequately assessed lifestyle factors cannot be fully ruled out. Third, substantial exclusions due to missing RCII or frailty data may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings, despite comparisons between included and excluded participants. Fourth, CumRCII was estimated from only two measurements using a trapezoidal approximation and therefore could not capture the detailed trajectory of RCII over time. Finally, although CHARLS was designed as a nationally representative survey, survey weights were not applied in the Cox models because the primary objective was to examine etiologic associations within the analytic cohort rather than to estimate nationally representative incidence. Thus, the population-level generalizability of our estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In joint-category Cox analyses, participants with concurrent elevated RCII or CumRCII and frailty showed the highest observed risk of incident CMM. However, formal additive and multiplicative interaction analyses were not statistically significant after adjustment. These findings suggest that lipid-inflammatory burden and frailty represent related but distinct dimensions of CMM risk. Because external validation, calibration analyses, and direct comparisons with established risk prediction tools were not performed, the present results should be interpreted as epidemiological evidence rather than definitive evidence for clinical risk prediction or routine implementation.
