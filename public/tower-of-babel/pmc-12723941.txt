Interactive and joint associations of prenatal ozone and PM2.5 exposure with fetal growth restriction: an Iranian nationwide birth cohort study
Authors: Ling Tong, Yixiang Wang, Yuqian Huang, Yalin Zhang, Fatemeh Mayvaneh, Yunquan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite extensive population-based evidence linking air pollution with adverse birth outcomes, the potential interactive effects of ambient ozone (O₃) and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) on low birth weight (LBW) and small for gestational age (SGA) remain largely unstudied.MethodsWe conducted a national retrospective cohort study using approximately 4.07 million birth records being registered in 749 hospitals across 31 provinces of Iran from 2013 to 2018. Gridded estimates of monthly O3 and PM2.5 concentrations were obtained from validated spatiotemporal models. Multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the associations between prenatal exposure to O3 and PM2.5 and adverse birth outcomes, including LBW, term LBW (TLBW), SGA, and term SGA (TSGA). Stratified analyses were performed to compare O₃-related effects in the lowest and highest tertiles of PM2.5 groups and PM2.5-related effects in lowest and highest tertiles of O₃ groups. The additive interaction effects were testified by introducing 2 × 2 dummy variables to model the joint exposure of O₃ and PM2.5. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by maternal age and educational attainment.ResultsA total of 3,976,387 singleton live births (6.0% LBW and 11.8% SGA) were included in this study. We observed consistent evidence for intensified effects of maternal O3 exposure on LBW and SGA under higher PM2.5 levels. For each 10-ppb increase in O₃ exposure, the estimated odds of LBW increased considerably from 0.99 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.96, 1.02) in the lowest tertile of PM2.5 level to 1.20 (95% CI: 1.16, 1.22) in the highest tertile of PM2.5 level (P <0.001 for trend), and the estimated odds of SGA parallelly increased from 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.07) in the lowest PM2.5 stratum to 1.26 (95% CI: 1.24, 1.28) in the highest PM2.5 stratum (P <0.001 for trend). Under the scenarios of co-exposure to high O₃ and PM2.5 levels, the excess odds increased by 8% (95% CI: 6%, 10%) for LBW and 4% (95% CI: 3%, 6%) for SGA, and 7% (95% CI: 5%, 9%) of the odds for LBW and 4% (95% CI: 2%, 5%) for SGA could be attributed to the additive interaction. Subgroup analyses showed more pronounced interactive effects among younger and less-educated mothers.ConclusionsThis nationwide birth cohort evidenced synergistic effects of maternal exposure to O₃ and PM2.5 during pregnancy in increasing LBW and SGA risks, underscoring the need for coordinated control strategies to effectively reduce the adverse effects of environmental pollution on maternal and infant health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25579-8.Keywords: Air pollution, Ozone, Fine particulate matter, Birth cohort, Low birth weight, Small for gestational age

Introduction

Low birth weight (LBW) and small for gestational age (SGA), two of the most prevalent adverse pregnancy outcomes, represented nearly 15% of global live births [4] and were widely linked to elevated risks of fetal/neonatal mortality [10] as well as lifelong chronic conditions including circulatory diseases [27], hypertension [14], and type 2 diabetes [9]. These outcomes are generally triggered by complex interactions among genetic predisposition and maternal behavioral and nutritional status [7, 31], with growing evidence implicating environmental exposures—particularly ambient air pollution—as modifiable contributors to their burden [1].

Pregnancy represents a period of heightened vulnerability to the adverse health effects of air pollution due to increased physiological demands and critical fetal developmental windows [10]. In the past decades, population-based studies have extensively linked prenatal exposure to ambient ozone (O3) and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) to adverse birth outcomes including LBW and SGA [5, 6]. While the individual effects of O3 and PM2.5 on adverse birth outcomes are well documented in both developed and less-developed locations [2, 33], the interactive or joint associations remain less understood compared to other endpoints like cardiovascular and respiratory diseases [40]. Given the co-exposure nature of ambient air pollution, assessments of combined effects are essential for more comprehensive understanding of exposure-related health risks. In a prior multi-country time-series analysis in 372 cities [23], data evidenced the interactive effects of ambient O3 and PM2.5 on daily nonaccidental and cardiorespiratory mortality, suggesting that joint exposure to air pollution may bidirectionally amplify exposure-related health risks. Nevertheless, efforts to translating these findings to the specific context of birth outcomes remain largely limited. To date, however, no nationwide investigations have reported the co-exposure effects of O3 and PM2.5 on pregnancy outcomes of LBW and SGA, particularly in low- and middle-income countries experiencing dual burdens of air pollution and neonatal health challenges [35].

In this study, we established a national birth cohort of over 4 million mother-infant pairs in Iran, and primarily aimed to investigate the interactive effects of prenatal exposure to O3 and PM2.5 on LBW and SGA using multiplicative and additive models. We further evaluate effect modification by maternal age and socioeconomic status (measured by educational attainment) to identify subpopulations at heightened risks.

Methods

Data sources

We collected data of 4,068,843 mother-baby pairs between 2013 and 2018 at 749 hospitals across 31 provinces in Iran (Fig. 1) from the nationwide healthcare delivery network managed by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education [24]. Maternal sociodemographic variables (e.g., age at delivery, educational attainment, urban/rural residence, parity, and health status) and infant characteristics (e.g., sex, birthweight, season at birth, year of birth, and gestational age [GA]) were documented and recorded by delivery hospitals.

After excluding births with undetermined sex (n = 1939), stillbirths (fetal death before or during delivery after 22 weeks of gestation, n = 30385), neonatal deaths (death within the first 28 days of life, n = 6136), cases with unmatched environmental exposures (n = 8642), and multiple births (n = 45354), a total of 3,976,387 singleton live births were finally included for analysis (Figure S1). Analyses were restricted to singleton births to avoid potential confounding from multiple births. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sabzevar University of Medical Sciences in Khorasan, Iran (No. IR.MEDSAB.REC.1396.99).

Outcome definitions

We included dichotomous outcomes of LBW and SGA in our analysis. LBW was defined as newborns with birthweight below 2500 grams, regardless of GA that was typically calculated by weeks from the first day of the last menstrual period to the date of birth. To account for fetal undergrowth in full-term pregnancies, term LBW (TLBW, birthweight below 2500 grams for pregnancies with at least 37 completed weeks of gestation) was also included as a secondary outcome. Similarly, we analyzed the outcomes of SGA and term SGA (TSGA), and SGA was defined as newborns with birthweight lower than the 10th percentile of neonatal birthweight records in Iran [43].

Exposure assessment

Monthly O3 concentrations at 0.125° × 0.125° resolution for the period from 2013 to 2018 were derived from well-established global surface ozone datasets [32]. Ozone levels for the period 2013–2018 were estimated using a two-stage space-time Bayesian neural network model. The model demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, showing high agreement with global surface ozone observations in both urban (R² = 0.97) and rural (R² = 0.94) locations. Parallelly, ground-level concentrations of monthly PM2.5 at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution across the entire Iran were obtained from the V5.GL.02 product, developed by the Washington University Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group [12]. This dataset was generated using a machine-learning approach that integrated spatiotemporal data from the GEOS-Chem chemical transport model, satellite observations, and ground-based monitoring sites [38]. The generated PM2.5 estimates were highly consistent with globally distributed ground monitors (R² = 0.81). Given the absence of detailed residential addresses for the mother-baby pairs, both O3 and PM2.5 exposure were estimated based on maternal delivery hospital locations and averaged across the entire pregnancy period [33, 43]. This exposure window for air pollution was prioritized in our primary analyses because the entire-pregnancy average has shown stronger and more robust associations with adverse birth outcomes than trimester-specific exposures [5, 35].

Covariates

Several sets of covariates were adjusted in association analyses in line with previous birth cohort studies [24, 41]. These covariates included: (1) maternal characteristics: age (≤25, 25–35, or >35 years), educational attainment (below high school, high school, college or above), residence (urban or rural), nationality (Iranian or non-Iranian), delivery mode (vaginal or cesarean), parity (primipara or multipara), and number of fetuses (singleton or multiple gestations); (2) health statuses before pregnancy: prior abortion, diabetes, hypertension, and delivery complications (present or absent); (3) infant variables: sex (male or female), season of birth (spring– winter), year of birth (2013–2018), and GA; (4) climate variables: mean temperature and relative humidity during the entire pregnancy. These climate variables were incorporated into the models using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) function with 3 knots placed at 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of their distributions [33, 43]. Daily estimates of temperature and humidity at a 0.1° × 0.1° resolution were obtained from the global climate reanalysis dataset developed by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecast.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression models adjusted for aforementioned covariates were used to testify the associations of air pollution during pregnancy with birthweight outcomes. We fitted single-pollutant models and two-pollutant models including both O₃ and PM2.5 as continuous variables. We assessed potential collinearity using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and did not identify the multicollinearity concern for two-pollutant models (VIF <2). We investigated the potential interactive effects of O₃ and PM2.5 exposure on LBW and SGA on both multiplicative and additive scales.

First, we performed stratified association analyses by grouping the co-exposure variable into tertiles, and compared the effects of O₃ across the lowest, middle, and highest PM2.5 levels, as well as the effects of PM2.5 across the lowest, middle, and highest O₃ levels. Stratum-specific associations were presented as the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each 10-ppb increase in O₃ exposure and each 10-µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 exposure. Between-stratum effect heterogeneity was testified via meta-regression approach using the lowest tertile group as the reference.

Second, we classified O₃ and PM2.5 exposure as binary variables based on median concentration in accordance with prior investigations [21, 39]. Dummy variables were created to represent the combinations of the 2×2 exposure categories, including (1) level 00: low-level O3 and low-level PM2.5 (reference group, OR00 = 1); (2) level 01: low-level O3 and high-level PM2.5 (OR01); (3) level 10: high-level O3 and low level PM2.5 (OR10); and (4) level 11: high-level O3 and high-level PM2.5 (OR11). The potential joint effects of O3 and PM2.5 were measured using relative excess odds due to interaction (REOI) and attributable proportion due to interaction (AP). These additive-scale metrics were calculated using the formulas and , respectively [17]. Here, REOI denotes the effect that is due to interaction; AP represents the proportion of the combined effect that is due to interaction; OR10, OR01, and OR11 represent the OR estimated for level 10, level 01, and level 11 compared with level 00, respectively. REOI >0 (AP >0) means that the combined effect is greater than individual effect of O3 and PM2.5 on LBW and SGA (synergistic interaction), otherwise REOI < 0 (AP < 0) signifies the antagonistic interaction. We performed subgroup analyses stratified by maternal age and educational attainment to examine how individual socioeconomic status may influence the associations between birthweight outcomes and exposure to O3 and PM2.5.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the observed additive interactions. First, trimester-specific exposure effects on birth outcomes were evaluated, including the first trimester (1–13 weeks), second trimester (14–26 weeks), and third trimester (27 weeks to delivery). Second, analyses were restricted to mother–infant pairs with gestational age between 37 and 42 weeks, and mothers without pre-pregnancy chronic conditions (such as diabetes and hypertension) or delivery complications. Third, alternative model specifications were also tested by varying the positions of RCS knots for temperature and relative humidity (e.g., 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles, or 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) and additionally adjusting for area-level socioeconomic status variables (e.g., gross domestic product per capita, rates of unemployment, and medical insurance participation) [24]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study participants. During 2013–2018, we totally included 3,976,387 singleton live births in the analysis, of which 237,562 (6.6%) were LBW and 468,480 (12.0%) births were SGA. Average birth weights were 2031.8 (standard deviation [SD]: 483.2) grams for LBW infants and 2502.1 (SD: 406.8) grams for SGA infants, respectively. Among mothers with LBW and SGA births, approximately three quarters (LBW: 75.5%, SGA: 73.7%) lived in urban areas, and more than a half (LBW: 52.8%, SGA: 52.5%) had an educational attainment below high school. For all pregnant women, the average (SD) exposure during the entire pregnancy was 50.2 (3.9) ppb for O3, 41.1 (12.6) µg/m3 for PM2.5, 16.7 (5.8) ℃ for mean temperature, and 43.5% (13.3%) for relative humidity, respectively.

We observed largely comparable risk estimates based on single-pollutant and two-pollutant models, and both analyses provided robust evidence for an increased odds of adverse birthweight outcomes in relation to elevated levels of O3 or PM2.5 exposure during pregnancy (Table S1). In co-exposure stratified analyses (Table 2), maternal O₃ exposure during pregnancy at the highest tertile of PM2.5 co-exposure level was consistently associated with the greatest risk of LBW and SGA in analyses of both overall and term births. Specifically, for each 10-ppb increase in O₃ exposure, the estimated odds of LBW increased considerably from 0.99 (95% CI: 0.96, 1.02) in the lowest tertile of PM2.5 level to 1.20 (95% CI: 1.16, 1.22) in the highest tertile of PM2.5 level (P <0.001 for trend), and the estimated odds of SGA parallelly increased from 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.07) in the lowest PM2.5 stratum to 1.26 (95% CI: 1.24, 1.28) in the highest PM2.5 stratum (P <0.001 for trend). Despite the mixed effects of O₃ level in PM2.5-associated risks, we observed robust evidence for the modifying effects of PM2.5 level on the associations of O₃ exposure with both LBW and SGA outcomes (P <0.001 for interaction).

Figure 2 displays subgroup analyses for O3 effects on the risks of LBW and SGA under tertiles of PM2.5 levels, stratified by maternal age and educational attainment. Overall, we observed highly consistent evidence for more pronounced associations of O3 exposure with LBW and SGA outcomes at higher levels of PM2.5 across subpopulations. This trend was in particular more evident among socioeconomically disadvantaged women (i.e., younger and less-educated). Among pregnant women under 25 years old, for each 10-ppb increase in O3 exposure, OR estimates increased markedly from 0.97 (95% CI: 0.92, 1.03) in the lowest PM2.5 stratum to 1.23 (95% CI: 1.18, 1.29) in the highest PM2.5 stratum for LBW, and from 1.04 (95% CI: 1.01, 1.08) to 1.28 (95% CI: 1.24, 1.31) for SGA. In less-educated pregnant women, the odds rose similarly from 1.03 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.07) to 1.20 (95% CI: 1.16, 1.25) for LBW and from 1.07 (95% CI: 1.04, 1.10) to 1.27 (95% CI: 1.24, 1.30) for SGA. These observed trends were highly consistent for term births (P <0.001 for trend).

Table 3 shows the additive interactive effects of O3 and PM2.5 exposure on LBW and SGA. For the overall population, we observed robust evidence for the synergistic effects of O3 and PM2.5 in collectively elevating the odds of LBW and SGA. Specifically, under co-exposure scenarios, the excess odds increased by 8% (95% CI: 6%, 10%) for LBW and 4% (95% CI: 3%, 6%) for SGA, and 7% (95% CI: 5%, 9%) of the odds for LBW and 4% (95% CI: 2%, 5%) for SGA could be attributed to the additive interaction. In population stratified analyses, we identified generally similar patterns across maternal age and educational subgroups, whereas only suggestive evidence of a potential synergistic effect was seen in women with advanced childbearing ages (>35 years) and education attainment (above high school). Overall, younger mothers (<25 years old) and those with lower educational attainment were more sensitive to the excessive risk of LBW and SGA due to co-occurrence of O3 and PM2.5 exposure. Sensitivity analyses considering trimester-specific exposures (Table S2), restricted birth cohorts (Table S3), and alternative modelling specifications (Table S4) yielded largely consistent findings with our primary analyses, further evidencing the robustness of the observed additive interactions between prenatal O3 and PM2.5 exposure.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nationwide birth cohort study in Middle East to investigate the interactive associations of maternal O3 and PM2.5 co-exposure with fetal growth restriction. Using nearly 4 million live birth records from 749 hospitals across 31 provinces, we provided compelling evidence for an increased risk of LBW and SGA associated with maternal exposure to O₃ and PM2.5. We observed intensified O₃-related effects under higher levels of PM2.5 exposure and synergistically additive interaction effects of co-exposure to O₃ and PM2.5. These findings highlight the importance of coordinated control strategies for both O3 and PM2.5, so as to reduce air pollution risks for pregnant women and their infants.

Our findings revealed an asymmetric interaction between O₃ and PM2.5 in relation to adverse birth outcomes, where high PM2.5 exposure significantly amplified O₃-associated risks of LBW and SGA while O₃ showed no modification of PM2.5 effects. This differential interaction may reflect their distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. PM2.5 likely exacerbates O₃-induced airway inflammation and oxidative stress through systemic circulation [34], whereas O₃ primarily affects the airways [19] with limited influence on PM2.5’s systemic effects. The heightened physiological vulnerability during pregnancy, characterized by increased oxygen demand and nutrient transport for fetal growth [16], may be disproportionately affected through oxidative stress pathways. As a potent oxidant, O₃ exposure can activate the generation of lipid peroxidation products and pro-inflammatory cytokines, potentially disrupting placental mitochondrial function, reducing uterine artery blood flow, and compromising maternal cardiovascular adaptation [13, 26]. PM2.5 appears to amplify these effects through synergistic enhancement of respiratory and systemic inflammation [36], with evidence suggesting that high PM2.5-O₃ co-exposure may produce supra-additive impacts on both pulmonary and cardiovascular systems [3]. Notably, trimester-specific analyses indicated that this synergistic effect was markedly stronger in the third trimester, suggesting that this period may represent the most sensitive window for fetal growth in response to air pollution. Sustained exposure during late gestation may further restrict placental nutrient transfer through oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby amplifying the combined toxicity of O₃ and PM2.5 on fetal growth [28, 29]. The particular susceptibility of pregnant women and fetuses to these combined pollutant effects [37] underscores the critical public health importance of these interactions in shaping perinatal outcomes. Therefore, while maintaining continuous protection throughout pregnancy remains essential, targeted interventions during late gestation may confer particularly substantial additional benefits in reducing the risk of fetal growth restriction.

Despite the discrepancies in study designs and populations, existing studies have reported the added effects of O3 and PM2.5 on a variety of health outcomes in multiple locations. Cross-sectional data during 2007–2010 from six low- and middle-income countries including China, Ghana, India, the Russian Federation, and South Africa revealed the synergistic effects of long-term exposure to O3 and PM2.5 on presbyopia and myopia in the elderly [22]. In two recent time-series analyses using death records across 372 cities in 19 countries [23] and 272 cities in China [39], colleagues observed consistent evidence for synergistic effects of short-term exposure to O3 and PM2.5 on daily deaths from non-accidental causes, but not from cardiovascular and respiratory causes [23]. Utilizing national birth data from Iran during 2013–2018, our study provided strong evidence for the synergistic effects of prenatal O3 and PM2.5 exposure in elevating the odds of LBW and SGA outcomes. Parallel results were also reported in a cohort study in Finland [30], demonstrating that the combined effect of O3 and PM2.5 on preterm birth was significantly greater than their individual effects (RERI=2.30, 95% CI: 0.95, 4.57). The observed synergistic effects of O3 and PM2.5 across diverse health endpoints highlight an urgent need for integrated air quality management strategies. Carbon mitigation policies represent a powerful embodiment of such strategies, and can achieve substantial health co-benefits through coordinated emission reductions [42]. Current single-pollutant control policies may insufficiently protect maternal and child health [8], particularly given the enhanced toxicity of co-exposure. Strengthening transboundary collaboration, expanding multipollutant monitoring networks, and incorporating synergistic risk assessments into prenatal care guidelines are critical steps toward safeguarding vulnerable populations.

The burden of air pollution on population subgroups is not borne equally. Younger and lowly-educated pregnant women are often at greater socioeconomic vulnerability, thus increasing their maternal risks of being exposed to higher air pollution during pregnancy [43]. These socioeconomically disadvantaged mothers may have insufficient healthcare knowledge and living conditions, reducing the likelihood of adopting effective pollution prevention measures (e.g., avoiding outdoor activities or using air purifiers) and worsening exposure-related health impacts [18]. Owing to the increased susceptibility of environmental effects on placental function development, younger pregnant women may have higher risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes related to ambient air pollution [15]. The disproportionate relationships between air pollution and neonatal health highlight the considerable importance of both environmental health and social equality [1]. Therefore, educational interventions and targeted health policies for high-risk populations should play a crucial role in mitigating the negative effects of air pollution on maternal and infant health, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities.

There are several limitations in our study. First, the use of delivery hospital location as a proxy for maternal residential exposure may introduce to-some-extent exposure misclassification, potentially biasing effect estimates. Such misclassification is likely non-differential and could bias the estimated associations towards the null directions, suggesting that the true association between co-exposure to O₃ and PM2.5 and adverse birth outcomes may be stronger than observed in this study. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as relatively conservative estimates. Also, the relatively coarse spatial resolution of PM2.5 and O₃ data (~0.1°) could not fully capture fine-scale variability of air pollution, particularly in the urban settings, which may introduce non-differential exposure misclassification and bias the estimated associations towards the null directions. Indeed, given the complexity of pregnant women’s activity patterns and exposure environments, using a very high spatial resolution may not necessarily provide a more accurate reflection of true exposure, whereas a relatively broader-scale assessment can help reduce measurement error arising from imprecise location assignment. Second, the primary analysis based on exposure assessment across the entire pregnancy period may introduce potential uncertainty or bias in comparisons between term and preterm infants due to variations in gestational age, despite the efforts of the inclusion of term LBW and term SGA as secondary outcomes. Third, the absence of individual-level data on some established risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as maternal body mass index [11] and smoking status [25], coupled with the failure to directly measure residential proximity to high-traffic roads—a recognized risk factor in which air pollution may act as a mediator [20]—has constrained our ability to fully account for potential confounding. Fourth, this study presented the interactive health effects of O₃ and PM2.5 using co-exposure stratified analyses in accordance with prior assessments. Although this approach improves interpretability and comparability with previous studies, it may fail to fully capture nonlinear dose–response patterns compared to continuous or spline-based models. In addition, this study did not consider the differential health effects by PM2.5 components or the interactions between temperature and air pollution. Notably, rising temperatures amid climate change may have complex impacts on the nexus between air pollution and population health, underscoring the need for an in-depth investigation into climate and multi-pollutant interactions in future environmental health assessments.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provided robust population-based evidence regarding the interactive and joint effects of maternal exposure to O₃ and PM2.5 on fetal growth restriction in the Middle East. Our results revealed heightened O₃-related risks of LBW and SGA at elevated PM2.5 concentrations, whereas the effect of PM2.5 on birth weight was not significantly modified by O₃ levels. Additionally, we identified a synergistic interaction between prenatal O₃ and PM2.5 exposure in triggering LBW and SGA events, particularly pronounced among younger mothers and those with lower educational attainment, suggesting potential socioeconomic disparities in vulnerability. These findings highlight the imperative for policymakers to implement integrated air quality management strategies that account for pollutant interactions. Concurrently, targeted public health interventions are urgently needed to mitigate both pollution levels and socioeconomic inequalities perpetuating adverse birth outcomes.
