My favourite flowering image: the Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem (Gagea lutea)
Authors: Mary Traynor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article reflects on a paired image of Gagea lutea (Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem): a herbarium specimen collected in Scroggs Wood, Kendal, in 1880 and a contemporary photograph taken in 2024 at the same location.

I was at first surprised, and then pleased, to be asked to contribute to My Favourite Flowering Image, the Journal of Experimental Botany series initiated by Nick Battey for the Flowering Newsletter (FNL) in 2008. I have greatly enjoyed reading many of the previous contributions, which have often provided fascinating and deeply personal insights into what it is about plants that excites, inspires, and delights their authors. The series also offers readers an opportunity to share in their passions and discoveries, and so, before choosing my own image, I revisited many of the earlier articles.

The first contributors selected images that astonished through revelation. Peter Endress (Endress, 2008) described his attempts to draw the flowers of Eupomatia bennettii, only to discover that all the floral organs were constantly, yet almost imperceptibly, in motion. Beverley Glover (Glover, 2013) chose an image of petal epidermal cells of Veronica caucasia that demonstrated the importance of looking more carefully and seeing more completely. The image revealed that colour differences resulted not simply from pigmentation, but from changes in cell morphology, and that these structural properties themselves might be significant to pollinators. Interestingly, 14 years later Vivian Irish (Irish, 2023) chose a very similar image from Arabidopsis and she expanded upon the possible significance of these sculptural epidermal cells.

John Bowman (Bowman, 2013) selected SEM images comparing mutant and wild-type flowers of Arabidopsis, representing for him a eureka moment in understanding floral developmental biology. These were followed by several more emblematic images connected with research into the timing of flowering. Georges Bernier (Bernier, 2013) chose an image of the shoot apical meristem of Sinapis alba demonstrating the up-regulation of a gene involved in one of the earliest steps in the transition to flowering. Maarten Koornneef (Koornneef, 2013) selected a drawing from 1951 by Friedrich Laibach illustrating early work on natural variation in flowering time in Arabidopsis, while Richard Amasino (Amasino, 2013) chose a photograph of a giant mutant tobacco plant that featured in some of the earliest studies of flowering mechanisms over a century ago.

Other contributors connected scientific imagery with personal experience. Gunter Theißen (Theißen, 2014) wrote about an image of a pod of cob corn that linked his research with memories from his own life, while the bright orange Californian poppies in Annette Becker’s article (Becker, 2025) performed a similar role. Many articles highlighted images that illustrated advances in understanding reproductive developmental biology and floral organ determination. Others celebrated extraordinary novelty and rarity, such as the inside-out flowers of Lacandonia brasiliana, discussed by Paula Rudall (Rudall, 2024), or the holoparasite Pilostyles boyacensis, introduced by Natalia Pabón-Mora (Pabón-Mora, 2021)—a plant discovered by the author and a colleague in 2014 that lives entirely within its host and produces no visible roots, stems, or leaves. Together, these articles reveal an extraordinary diversity of scientific experience, curiosity, and emotional connection expressed through flowering imagery.

The image I have chosen to share pairs a herbarium specimen of the Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem (Gagea lutea), collected in 1880 by John Hutchinson, with a photograph I took of the same species in 2024 at the very same location (Fig. 1).

Since retiring in 2020, after 25 years working in the Editorial Office of the Journal of Experimental Botany, I have been able to return to my original interests in plants and ecology. Initially, in an attempt to improve my sadly neglected botanical skills, I began searching for species known to occur in local habitats but which I could not recall ever having seen. The Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem was one such plant.

For several years, and without success, I searched local woodlands near the River Kent during March and April. Seeking clues, I turned to the Joseph Martindale herbarium collection held at Kendal Museum. Joseph Martindale, a schoolteacher and amateur botanist, together with his botanical colleagues, collected plant specimens throughout the local area during the 19th and early 20th centuries. Within the collection, I discovered a dried specimen of five Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem plants gathered by John Hutchinson from a small local woodland called Scroggs Wood in 1880.

Inspired by this discovery, I set out once more in March 2024 and, this time, I was successful. In a narrow strip of woodland along the lower reaches of a tributary flowing into the River Kent, I found a large, though not especially floriferous, colony of Gagea lutea. It is perhaps not surprising that the species proved difficult to find. Its leaves are easily overlooked, closely resembling those of several other woodland plants with which it commonly grows, and a review of the literature suggests that, in the north of the UK, G. lutea seldom flowers abundantly.

Its extended, variable, and often asynchronous flowering may reflect a reproductive strategy adapted to fluctuating spring temperatures and short windows of favourable conditions rather than synchronized mass flowering (Nishikawa, 1998; Yoshie, 2008; Kiss et al., 2024). By spreading flowering across changing weather conditions, the species may increase opportunities for successful sexual reproduction, although direct evidence linking flowering duration to pollinator limitation in G. lutea remains sparse (Nishikawa, 1998). When seeds are produced, they possess elaiosomes—nutrient-rich appendages that attract ants and facilitate dispersal through myrmecochory. However, it is possible that northern British populations of G. lutea rely heavily upon vegetative persistence and clonal spread because cool spring temperatures and environmentally constrained reproduction may limit regular seed production (Yoshie, 2008; Sunmonu and Kudo, 2015; Iwanami and Yoshie, 2017). This interpretation is broadly consistent with reproductive strategies described elsewhere in the genus (Sunmonu and Kudo, 2015). It may also help explain the long-term persistence of local populations within ancient woodland habitats, the apparent rarity of successful establishment of new populations, and why the species is frequently regarded as a useful Ancient Woodland Indicator Species.

This experience led to an extended and ongoing project tracing other plants recorded in the Martindale collection. By happenstance, I discovered that Martindale himself had spent considerable time searching for plants mentioned in even earlier historical records from Westmorland—notably a 1688 record of Bartsia alpina collected by John Ray (1627–1705), as well as numerous plants recorded by the Quaker botanist Thomas Lawson (1630–1691) (Hodkinson and Steward, 2012).

The Scroggs Wood population of G. lutea has, it seems, persisted for at least 144 years, and perhaps for millennia before that. It occupies a tiny fragment of ancient woodland, little more than 6 m wide on either side of the stream. The site is owned by Woodland Trust, a charity dedicated to the protection of ecologically important woodland, and the plant itself is rare in the UK. Yet the area immediately adjacent to the wood has been designated for development, while the local Environment Agency is considering strengthening the nearby road as part of flood management planning.

Perhaps this seems a parochial choice of image, yet it may point toward a more universal story. Whatever its wider significance, the Yellow Star-of-Bethlehem has given me both a reason for hope and a profound sense of belonging within a much larger continuum of history, landscape, and botanical memory.
