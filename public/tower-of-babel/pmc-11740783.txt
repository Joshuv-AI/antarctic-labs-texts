A new prescription
Authors: Andrew Duncombe
Published: 2024

Welcome to our latest issue of the Future Healthcare Journal (FHJ), where we are asking whether expansion of pharmacists’ roles and practice may be part of the solution to the current healthcare crisis. I am very grateful to our guest editor and editorial board member, Ravina Barrett, for leading on this issue. She has a wealth of experience as a pharmacist, having worked in primary and secondary care, in community pharmacy as well as in academia and regulation.

The new Pharmacy First service1 recently announced by the UK government, which increases the number of common conditions for which patients may get treatment directly from a pharmacy, is a clear indication of the direction of policy. This is further highlighted by the upcoming expansion of independent prescribing pharmacist numbers. All pharmacists in training become qualified as independent prescribing pharmacists from 2025 onwards, with a mandatory additional year of study. Undoubtedly, there will be some physicians concerned about mission creep and loss of medical autonomy. However, modern medicine is already a multidisciplinary enterprise and embedding these pharmaceutical specialists into existing multidisciplinary teams in both primary and secondary care could be an effective way of harnessing their expertise, as well as providing mutual learning opportunities. Those in independent pharmacy practices will need strong oversight and regulation, particularly because of the financial incentives in this sector.

Also in this issue, Warrens et al4 highlight that while refugee and migrant health needs are complex and multifaceted, UK medical schools do not currently equip students to care confidently for this population. They demonstrate that a purpose-built curriculum designed from a systematic literature review may fill this knowledge gap and equip students in unfamiliar areas such as legislation, access to care, effects of trauma, cultural differences and interpreter use. A similar programme would surely be of benefit in postgraduate training too.

Social prescribing is a non-medical intervention for patients, supporting their physical, psychological and social needs in a holistic way by utilising community-based resources, such as local charities, gyms, museums, gardening or arts-based groups. While well established in the UK within primary care, it is yet to be applied in an accident and emergency (A&E) setting, to which many patients with primary care needs now present. This phenomenon has been worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic, funding cuts, workforce strikes and lack of staff recruitment. Cooper and colleagues5 provide a conceptual overview of the potential implementation of social prescribing in A&E, discussing challenges and benefits as well as providing directions for future research.

The role and value of social media to medical discourse remain controversial. Guckian et al6 reflect on the changing social media environment, with educational activity becoming more prominent. Medical TikTok, the fastest-growing social media platform, and Instagram Reel content are dominated by students and doctors sharing lived experiences from their day-to-day lives, with some positive mentorships forming. However, the rise of anonymous accounts with massive followings on X and Reddit is especially troublesome. The authors ask whether there is a danger of validating faceless accounts, since many set the tone for medical social media, despite being criticised for bullying, toxicity and misinformation. Can an anonymous account itself be held to account? The authors conclude that to facilitate healthier, more constructive discourse, we must ensure the building of a culture that rewards openness, transparency and tolerance, that welcomes both energised learners and intimidated seniors and institutions.

Gjika7 asks whether it is ethical for immunisations against human papillomavirus (HPV) to be mandatory in young people aged 12–13 years old. While vaccination has been shown to reduce the incidence of HPV-related diseases by up to 90%, the author focuses on the ethical concerns of parental rights, gender equity and socioeconomic disparities, and states that the ethical justification for mandatory HPV vaccination lies in its potential to prevent significant harm and promote health equity across populations.

The theme of generalism versus specialism for physicians that we featured in our June 2024 issue is taken up again by two further papers. Estabrook and colleagues8 examine the role of the general internal medicine (GIM) consultant in inpatient medicine. They argue that the reintroduction of GIM could provide a sustainable and valuable contribution to high-quality, cost-effective inpatient care for medical as well as surgical patients with multimorbidity, drawing on parallels from the GIM hospitalist model in the USA.

Riley and Varughese9 highlight problematic perceptions of generalists, citing a widespread feeling that general medical wards are regarded as a ‘dumping ground’ with consequent difficulty to enthuse budding physicians among UK medical students to become generalists. Surveys conducted with the Royal College of Physicians (RCP) from foundation, core and medical registrar trainees have demonstrated disillusionment with the intensity of an unmanageable workload, poor work–life balance and lower job satisfaction in GIM compared to careers in other specialties. They argue that to elevate GIM to parity of esteem with specialist medicine, clinicians and management teams must begin to recognise its value once again and so attract both UK and international medical graduates.
