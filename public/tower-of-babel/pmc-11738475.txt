Current Status and Future Perspective of Seoul National University Hospital-Dementia Brain Bank with Concordance of Clinical and Neuropathological Diagnosis
Authors: Kwanghoon Lee, Seong-Ik Kim, Yu-Mi Shim, Eric Eunshik Kim, Sooyeon Yoo, Jae-Kyung Won, Sung-Hye Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper introduces the current status of Seoul National University Hospital Dementia Brain Bank (SNUH-DBB), focusing on the concordance rate between clinical diagnoses and postmortem neuropathological diagnoses. We detail SNUH-DBB operations, including protocols for specimen handling, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) and cerebral organoids establishment from postmortem dural fibroblasts, and adult neural progenitor cell cultures. We assessed clinical-neuropathological diagnostic concordance rate. Between 2015 and September 2024, 162 brain specimens were collected via brain donation and autopsy. The median donor age was 73 years (1-94) with a male-to -female ratio of 2:1. The median postmortem interval was 9.5 hours (range: 2.5-65). Common neuropathological diagnoses included pure Lewy body disease (10.6%), Lewy body disease (LBD) with other brain diseases (10.6%), pure Alzheimer's disease-neuropathological change (ADNC) (6.0%), ADNC with other brain diseases (10.7%), vascular brain injury (15.2%), and primary age-related tauopathy (7.3%). APOE genotype distribution was following: ε3/ε3: 62.3%, ε2/ε3: 9.6%, ε2/ε4: 3.4%, ε3/ε4: 24.0%, and ε4/ε4: 0.7%. Concordance rates between pathological and clinical diagnoses were: ADNC/AD at 42.4%; LBD at 59.0%; PSP at 100%; ALS at 85.7%; Huntington’s disease 100%. The varying concordance rates across different diseases emphasize the need for improved diagnostic criteria and biomarkers, particularly for AD and LBD. Tissues have been distributed to over 40 national studies. SNUH-DBB provides high-quality brain tissues and cell models for neuroscience research, operating under standardized procedures and international guidelines. It supports translational research in dementia and neurodegenerative diseases, potentially advancing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Brain bank, Brain donation, Autopsy, Act, Ethics, Research

INTRODUCTION

Brain banks (BBs) are important repositories of postmortem brain tissues and related data that have significantly advanced our understanding of neurological disorders and normal brain function [1]. The establishment of the Seoul National University Hospital Dementia Brain Bank (SNUH-DBB) represents a notable action to support neuroscience and medical research in Korea, aiming to promote research on various neurological diseases using human brain tissue.

Brain banking emerged in the late 19th century as researchers recognized the need for systematically studying brain tissues to understand neurological diseases. The West Riding Lunatic Asylum (later renamed Stanley Royd Hospital) in England pioneered the systemic collection and preservation of brains, focusing on neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease [2, 3].

As the scientific community recognized its potential, brain banking gained momentum. Organizations formalized the process, emphasizing standardized protocols for tissue collection, preservation, and distribution [3]. Technological advancements in molecular biology and imaging techniques further enhanced its importance, enabling analysis at cellular and molecular levels [4]. Collaboration between BBs, research institutions, and pharmaceutical companies have expanded, contributing to new discoveries in neuroscience and drug development.

In the 21st century, brain banking has evolved along with the precision medicine [5]. Brain tissues are now integrated with comprehensive medical and genetic data, facilitating the identification of genetic markers, biomarkers, and personalized treatments for various neurological disorders [4, 6]. Autopsy-confirmed studies from brain banks provide crucial validation for antemortem biomarkers in diagnosing and characterizing neurodegenerative diseases [7, 8].

The Korea National BB Project was initiated in 2016 by the Korea National Institute of Health (KNIH) and has evolved into the Korea National Dementia BB Network (KNDBBN). The network focuses on collecting postmortem brain tissue through brain donation programs, with the aim of promoting clinicopathological, radiological, and neuropathological research in neurodegenerative diseases. The KNDBBN has expanded through collaboration with four major medical institutions: Samsung Medical Center (http://www.kbrainbank.org), Seoul National University Hospital (http://brainbank.snuh.org), Pusan National University Hospital (http://pnuhbrainbank.org), and Myongji Hospital.

The SNUH-BB was founded on January 9, 2015, as one of three biorepositories under the Biomedical Research Institute at SNUH. It joined the KNDBB in 2017, receiving funding from the KNIH. The primary goal of the SNUH-DBB is to collect and properly preserve donated postmortem brain tissues, including control brains, from a wide range of brain diseases. These tissues are carefully stored to maintain structural and molecular integrity, with 24-hour deep freezer temperature monitoring and regular quality assurance audits. BB provides researchers with an invaluable resource for analyzing brain tissue samples to investigate a variety of neurological disorders, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), vascular dmentia (VaD), schizophrenia, and the neurotypical aging brains.

The establishment of BB offers several notable benefits, including accelerating scientific advancement, early diagnosis and treatment, translational research, drug development, public health impacts, and education and collaboration. Well-preserved brain tissues and associated data accelerate breakthroughs in our understanding of human brain function, disease mechanisms, and potential interventions. Insights from human brain tissue research can identify early diagnostic markers, enabling timely interventions and improved patient outcomes. Researchers can use the Bank's resources to test and develop novel drugs targeting specific neurological disorders, addressing unmet medical needs. Knowledge gained through BB research helps shape public health policies and strategies, contributing to the overall population’s well-being.

The SNUH-DBB can link genetic and molecular profiles to clinical information, paving the way for personalized, tailored treatment approaches. It also serves as a training hub for the next generation of neuroscientists and medical professionals using cutting-edge research methodologies, fostering collaboration between researchers, clinicians, and educators [9]. The SNUH-DBB drives groundbreaking discoveries, encourages collaboration, and improves human health worldwide by providing researchers with access to high-quality brain tissues and relevant data.

The ‘The Act on Dissection and Preservation of Corpses (ADPC) of Korea (https://elaw.klri.re.kr/eng_mobile/viewer.do?hseq=55437&type=part&key=36)’ governs the human cadavers for medical education, research, pathological examination, and forensic investigation. Key provisions include mandatory written informed consent from next of kin or ante-mortem consent for brain donation, emphasis on maintaining the dignity of the deceased, proper management of biological materials according to biosafety protocols, guidelines for institutions conducting research using cadaveric specimens, and requirements for protecting personal identifiers and designating data security officers.

To distribute cadaveric specimens, institutions must meet strict criteria and be accredited by the Ministry of Health and Welfare. The law enables ethical research using postmortem human brain tissue, contributing to the advancement of medical sciences and public health while respecting donor dignity.

The ADPC in Korea has been updated to reflect advances in medicine and growing concerns regarding patient privacy and the ethical handling of human remains. It now comprehensively covers various aspects of cadaveric processing, including research ethics and data security.

SNUH-DBB has implemented comprehensive standard operating procedures (SOPs) to ensure ethical compliance, transparency, and standardized operations. These SOPs cover various aspects of BB functions, including a registry of premortem brain donors, diagnostic protocols, and neuropathological autopsy procedures. The bank’s operations demonstrate its commitment to maintaining high standards in brain tissue collection, preservation, and research.

Here, we demonstrate SNUH-DBB’s key operational aspects, including the registry of premortem brain donors, SOPs, diagnostic approaches, the profile of neuropathological autopsy diagnosis, and comparison with premortem clinical diagnosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We outlined protocols for specimen collection and processing, cell cultures, neuropathological examination, diagnostic procedure, storage and quality control.

Autopsy cohort

From 2015 to September 2024, 162 brain specimens were collected by autopsy. Donors had a median age of 73 years (range: 1~94 years), with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1. Postmortem interval was 9.5 hours (range: 2.5~65 hours). Comprehensive gross and microscopic examinations following the latest consensus guidelines for neurodegenerative diseases were performed. Diagnostic processes including special stains, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and molecular genetic tests were utilized. Clinical and neuropathological diagnoses were compared, and the concordance rate was analyzed.

Specimen collection and processing

The brain was removed from the calvarium, weighed, and photographed. Olfactory bulbs, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and usually, the upper portion of the cervical spinal cord were retrieved when possible. Postmortem blood was sampled for serology and APOE genotype. The one hemisphere, usually the right hemisphere was dissected into coronal sections, approximately 1.0 cm thick, photographed, flash frozen at -160°C liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C deep freezer. The other hemisphere, usually the left hemisphere underwent fixation in 10% neutral formalin for 14~21 days. Following fixation, this hemisphere was dissected into slices approximately 1.0 cm thick. These sections were then carefully examined for any abnormlities, photographed for documentation, and further processed to create formalin fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks for comprehensive neuropathological assessment. Extra blocks were prepared and stored for future distribution to other researchers. The precise methodologies is elaborated upon the result section providing detailed procedures.

Cell cultures

Fibroblasts were isolated from postmortem dural tissue and used to generate induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) using standard reprogramming protocols. These iPSCs were then used to generate brain organoids following established protocols. Adult neural progenitor cells were isolated from the subventricular zone and the subgranular zone of the hippocampal dentate gyrus, and cultured using specialized media and growth factors. These three types of cell culture methods are summarized in Supplementary file 1.

Neuropathological examination

A gross examination of the brain was performed to identify any visible abnormalities, atrophy, or lesions. Brain weight and all observed abnormalities were recorded. The microscopic examination involved H&E (hematoxylin-eosin) stained sections prepared from key brain regions evaluated for cellular architecture, neuronal loss, and pathological features. Special stains (e.g., Luxol fast blue for myelin) were applied as needed, and IHC staining was performed for disease-specific markers (e.g., β-amyloid, phosphorylated Tau, phosphorylated α-synuclein, phosphorylate TDP43).

Diagnostic procedure

The clinical presentation and medical history of each donor were thoroughly reviewed. Comprehensive gross and microscopic examinations were performed following the latest consensus guidelines for neurodegenerative diseases [10-25]. Findings were correlated with clinical information. Relevant genetic tests were performed based on clinical suspicion and neuropathological findings.

Storage and quality control

Frozen tissues were stored at -80°C in labeled, self-sealing plastic bags within frozen storage boxes. FFPE blocks were stored at room temperature in appropriate cabinets.

Quality control measures included documentation of agonal state and postmortem interval (PMI), standardized sectioning, processing, and storage protocols, measurement of RNA integrity number (RIN) and pH, and regular monitoring of storage conditions and equipment performance.

Data analysis

Neuropathological diagnoses were categorized and summarized to create a disease profile. Clinical diagnoses were compared with neuropathological diagnoses, and concordance rates were calculated for each major diagnostic category.

RESULTS

Registry of premortem brain donors

Over the past 10 years, 1,001 brain donation applicants have registered with the SNUH-DBB. The median age of applicants was 64 years (range: 6~94), and the male-to-female ratio was 1:1.1. The geographic distribution of applicants was from Seoul (52.5%) and Gyeonggi-do/Incheon (34.9%), Chungcheong-do (3.7%), Gangwon-do (2.6%), Gyeongsang-do (2.1%), Jeolla-do (1.3%), and others (Jeju Island and other metropolitan cities) (2.9%) (Fig. 1).

Key component of autopsy and specimen acquisition protocol

In recent years, spatial technologies have gained prominence in neuroscience research, particularly for studies using FFPE tissue. Platforms such as 10x Genomics’ Visium and Xenium, and NanoString’s GeoMx and CosMx, are increasingly being adopted by researchers. These technologies allow for spatially resolved transcriptomics and proteomics, providing unprecedented insights into the molecular architecture of brain tissue. These spatial technologies have brought new considerations to the forefront of brain banking practice. Of these the most important is RNA quality for successful experiments. This emphasis on RNA integrity has made the optimization of fixation time even more crucial. Proper fixation is essential for preserving tissue morphology while maintaining RNA quality, a delicate balance essential for spatial transcriptome studies. This may involve improving fixation protocols to ensure optimal RNA preservation without compromising tissue structure (Supplementary file 2). The SNUH-DBB has significantly reduced formalin fixation time. Previously, the process took approximately 30 days, from autopsy to brain cutting and gross examination. As of mid-2022, critical regions like the midbrain and middle frontal gyrus are fixed in formalin for 2~4 days, while brain cutting and fixation of other regions are completed within 14 days. This protocol modification allows for more rapid tissue processing and preservation, potentially improving the quality of downstream analyses.

SNUH-DBB follows a rigorous protocol for autopsy and specimen acquisition optimized for various research modalities including special technologies. Autopsies were performed within 12 hours after death, when possible. After brain removal, the dura mater is sampled and rinsed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), with antibiotic-antimycotic, to prevent possible contamination, cut into small pieces using sterile scissors and the tissue pieces are placed in a culture dish with fibroblast growth medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum.

The right hemisphere is sliced into 1 cm thick section and photographed for easy identification of anatomical structures when sampling from the frozen brains for brain tissue distribution. Tissue sampling for molecular studies is then performed in the cortical regions including the frontal, temporal, and occipital cortices. DNA/RNA is extracted and stored for quality control. DNA/RNA is preserved using a DNA/RNA shield for future experiments such as NGS and spatial transcriptome. The frontal, temporal, and occipital cortices, midbrain, and hippocampus are collected to create OCT-embedded blocks. The slabs are frozen using a liquid nitrogen vapor system (-196°C) immediately after the autopsy. The slabs are sealed in plastic bags and stored in a -80°C freezer.

To prepare the formalin-fixed left hemisphere, the formalin solution is changed within 24 hours to prevent artifacts such as formalin pigmentation or blood staining. The left midbrain is harvested on days 2~3 after autopsy and prepared for FFPE blocks. The fixation time should be optimized for Visium studies. To enhance formalin invasion, on day 7 after an autopsy, the formalin-fixed left hemisphere is sectioned into 1-cm-thick slabs and returned to formalin for further fixation. Around day 14, a comprehensive gross examination is performed, photographs are taken, and FFPE blocks are prepared for neuropathological diagnosis using established guidelines and immunohistochemical (IHC) biomarkers (Fig. 2).

The overall process of SNUH-DBB. Applications for brain donation are received, and clinical information of the applicants is collected. This protocol ensures optimal tissue preservation for histopathological examination, molecular studies, and cell culture while complying with standard neuropathological diagnostic criteria. It also accommodates emerging research needs, such as spatial transcriptomics and advanced in vitro systems like organoid-on-a-chip models [26].

Quality assurance protocol for DNA and RNA

Depending on the experiment, RIN, RIN equivalent (RINe), and distribution value (DV) 200 (%) are used for RNA quality, with a target RIN of 6 or higher. Fresh or well-preserved frozen tissues often yield high-quality RNA, so RIN and RINe values are considered, with separate cut-off values for each [27]. Standard bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing require an RIN value of 7 or higher.

For FFPE tissues, where RNA degradation is more pronounced due to chemical treatment and high temperatures, DV200 (%) is the preferred quality control standard [28]. For spatial transcriptomics using FFPE samples, a DV200 of at least 50%, indicating that at least 50% of the RNA fragments are longer than 200 nucleotides, is recommended, while RNA in situ experiments like Xenium need a DV200 of at least 30%.

PMI can affect RNA quality in brain tissue, affecting RIN values, RNA quantity, and gene expression [29]. However, PMI alone does not determine RNA quality, and some studies suggested it might not significantly impact sequencing data quality [30, 31]. High-quality RNA had been obtained from tissue samples with PMIs up to 36 hours or more in some cases [30, 31].

DNA quality evaluation include gel electrophoresis and DNA integrity number (DIN) assessment [32]. To ensure optimal RNA and DNA preservation, tissue collection follows standardized protocols that prioritize rapid dissection and freezing post-autopsy. Proper storage at -80°C preserves RNA quality for decades.

We performed quarterly regular monitoring through quality control standards, proficiency evaluations, and audits. Detailed documentation of all procedures is maintained. We maintain regular quality inspections of stored samples and all procedures. Disease-specific considerations and qPCR are employed for comprehensive quality assurance. In addition, contamination control protocols are strictly followed, including the use of RNase-free reagents and consumables to prevent RNA degradation.

Optimization of FFPE brain tissue processing and histopathologic examinations

FFPE brain tissue processing and histopathologic examinations are important components of neurological studies. Sectioned tissues are air-dried for at least 60 minutes before the staining process because sufficient drying is essential to avoid tissue detachment from glass slides during staining procedures involving frequent washes (Fig. 3). Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) staining is routinely applied to key sections for neuroanatomical visualization of brain. This technique enhances the detection of white matter pathology and helps to identify neurological abnormalities.

The special staining and immunohistochemical protocol for neurodegenerative disease evaluation

SNUH-DBB uses a comprehensive IHC protocol for the evaluation of neurodegenerative diseases. This protocol ensures accurate neuropathological diagnosis and characterization of various disorders, including mixed pathologies.

The initial screening includes four antibodies, β-amyloid, phosphorylated Tau (AT8), TDP43, and a-synuclein, and three special stains: LFB, Bielschowsky, and Gallyas silver staining. They are applied to the anterior hippocampal section including entorhinal and transentorhinal cortices, basal fore brain, and brainstem section, containing substantia nigra [33, 34]. pTDP43 staining of the amygdala and phosphorylated α-synuclein staining of the olfactory bulb are also performed initially. If AD is suspected at initial screening, additional β-amyloid and AT8 immunostainings are performed on sections from the middle frontal, middle inferior, and parietal lobes, and calcarine gyrus cerebellum, midbrain, and olfactory section [10, 35]. Bielschowsky and thioflavin S staining are applied to the middle frontal and middle temporal lobes, inferior parietal lobule, and calcarine cortex to evaluate the CERAD score for neuritic plaques [36].

After screening H&E and IHC, if Lewy pathology is observed, α-synuclein IHC is added to the midbrain, medulla oblongata, amygdala and frontal cortex [37].

If frontotemporal lobar degeneration-Tau (FTLD-Tau) is suspected, AT8 IHC is added to the four neocortex, brainstem, amygdala, basal ganglia, and olfactory bulb. If FTLD-TDP43 (FTLD-TDP) or limbic predominant TDP43 encephalopathy-neuropathological change (LATE-NC) is suggested, pTDP43 IHC is applied to the amygdala, hippocampus, middle frontal lobe, basal ganglia, and brainstem [38-40]. If multiple system atrophy (MSA) is suggested, α-synuclein IHC is performed in several brain regions including midbrain, medulla oblongata, cerebellum, hippocampus, basal ganglia, thalamus, middle frontal, middle temporal, parietal lobes, and occipital lobes [41]. If amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is suggested, pTDP43, FUS, and SOD1 IHCs are applied to the motor cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, spinal cord, and olfactory bulbs[38]. If Huntington’s disease is suggested, 1C2 and p62 IHCs are added to the caudate nucleus, basal ganglia, and thalamus [42]. In cases where mixed diseases are suspected, all antibodies are studied in the above areas for precise diagnosis.

HuC/D vs. NeuN, Gallyas silver stain vs. AT8 IHC, and β-amyloid IHC vs. thioflavin S vs. Bielschowsky stain

In the neuropathological assessment of neurodegenerative diseases, neuronal loss is a critical finding. For accurate identification and quantification of neurons, HuC/D is a superior marker compared to NeuN (Fig. 3). HuC/D consistently labels neurons, regardless of their health status or PMI of the brain tissue. In contrast, NeuN primarily marks healthy neurons, potentially underestimating the total neuronal population in degenerative conditions or in tissue with longer PMIs. This distinction is crucial for accurate neuronal quantification and distribution analysis in neuropathological studies, particularly when evaluating the extent of neurodegeneration in various brain regions affected by neurodegenerative diseases.

The Gallyas silver stain is superior to AT8 IHC for detecting neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) in AD and primary agerelated tauopathy (PART) for several reasons: Gallyas silver staining is highly sensitive in detecting NFTs, particularly those in more advanced stages of maturation (Fig. 4). It can visualize the fibrillar structure of tangles with greater clarity than AT8 IHC [43, 44]. The Gallyas method specifically stains aggregated tau proteins in NFTs, providing a clear distinction between tangles and background tissue. This specificity allows for better quantification and analysis of NFT distribution. Gallyas silver staining is particularly effective in highlighting mature NFTs, which are composed of both 3R and 4R tau isoforms. This makes it especially useful for assessing the progression of tau pathology in AD and PART [45]. The Gallyas technique offers consistent results across different brain regions and cases, making it a reliable method for comparative studies and diagnostic purposes [44]. The Gallyas method exploits the argyrophilic nature of NFTs, which is linked to the molecular composition of tau aggregates. While AT8 IHC is valuable for detecting early-stage tau pathology, including pretangles and neuropil threads, the Gallyas silver stain provides a more comprehensive view of established NFTs, making it a preferred method for assessing overall tangle burden in AD and PART [45].

β-amyloid IHC is the most specific for amyloid plaques; Thioflavin S and Bielschowsky detect both plaques and tangles. Modified Bielschowsky stain and thioflavin S show similar sensitivity in NFT demonstration [46]. The thioflavin S labels amyloid plaques and NFTs clearly, but interpretation requires experience with fluorescence microscopy [46]. The fluorescence signal fades over time, which can limit long-term storage and re-examination of stained samples. Bielschowsky stain demonstrates multiple structures (nerve fibers, NFTs, plaques) in a single stain. Bielschowsky stain can be more technically challenging and temperature-sensitive [47]. β-amyloid IHC and thioflavin S are generally better suited for quantitative analysis compared to silver stains. In practice, these staining methods are often used complementarily to provide a comprehensive assessment of AD pathology, as each has its strengths in visualizing different aspects of the disease.

Final neuropathological diagnosis through autopsy at SNUH-DBB

Pathological examination of 162 donated brains at the SNUH-DBB revealed a diverse spectrum of pathologies. The most prevalent diagnosis was mixed pathology, accounting for 27.8% of cases. VBD was the second most common finding, present in 15.2% of cases. The remainder was FTLD (7.3%), and pure AD-neuropathological change (ADNC) (6.0%). Our FTLD cases comprised progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) (2.6%), corticobasal degeneration (CBD) (1.3%), FTLD-TDP with ALS (2.6%), MSA (2.6%), chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) (1.3%). Additionally, Huntington's disease (1.3%), dentatorubropallidoluysian disease (DRPLA) (0.7%), adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids (ALSP) (0.7%), adult-onset polyglucosan body disease (APBD) (0.7%, Schizophrenia (2.0%), alcohol-related brain disease (ARBD) (2.0%), Leigh disease (0.7%), primary age-related tauopathy (PART) (7.3%), and other disease including glioblastoma and metastatic carcinoma (12.6%) were present (Fig. 5).

The discrepancy between clinical diagnosis and autopsy-based neuropathological diagnosis

Our study revealed significant discrepancies between clinical diagnoses and neuropathological findings in various neurodegenerative diseases. In cases clinically diagnosed as AD or dementia, only 42.4% were pathologically confirmed to have ADNC (Fig. 6A). Among these ADNC-confirmed cases, 35.7% exhibited pure ADNC pathology, while the remainder showed comorbidities: 42.9% with LBD, 35.7% with LATE, and 57.1% with CVD. In cases without ADNC, alternative pathologies were identified, including CVD (47.4%), Alcohol-related brain damage (ARBD) (15.8%), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) (15.8%), LBD (10.5%), and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) (5.3%).

Among the cases clinically diagnosed as PD (n=39), those that were not pathologically diagnosed as LBD accounted for 41.0%. These cases exhibited a range of mixed diseases: MSA (37.5%), cerebrovascular disease (37.5%), PSP (25.0%), ADNC (18.8%), CTE (6.3%), FTLD-tau (6.3%), and ALSP (6.3%). Conversely, among the cases pathologically diagnosed as LBD (59.0%), the following co-existing diseases were identified: LBD only (60.9%), cerebrovascular disease (21.7%), ADNC (17.4%), LATE (13.0%), and PSP (4.3%) (Fig. 6B).

The concordance rate of clinical diagnosis varied considerably across different neurodegenerative diseases evaluated at our institution (SNUH-DBB). The confirmation rates (pathological/clinical diagnosis) were as follows: ADNC/AD or Dementia at 42.4%; PD/LBD at 59.0%; PSP at 100%; ALS at 85.7%; and Huntington’s disease at 100%. DISCUSSION

Strategies for operating brain donation programs and enhancing voluntary participation

BBs and brain donation programs are essential to advancing neuroscience research. The success and sustainability of these programs depend on long-term national support, and effective strategies to encourage voluntary participation.

National support is essential to provide financial and human resources, build state-of-the-art facilities, and standardized protocols, and create large databases that bring together extensive brain tissue collections and related information. These databases serve as important resources for advancing neuroscience research, enabling researchers to identify patterns and correlations that may not be apparent in smaller sample sizes.

National support is essential for setting ethical and legal guidelines for brain donation and protecting donor privacy and rights. Ethical considerations include informed consent, confidentiality, and compliance with institutional and national human tissue research guidelines. Nationally recognized standards foster public confidence and ensure consistent ethical practices. National campaigns can promote brain donation awareness, simplify procedures, and highlight its scientific significance through targeted education. Strategies to boost participation include donor family acknowledgment, network building, process streamlining, and emphasizing the scientific impact of brain donation.

Transparency in the use of donated tissue and research outcomes is essential. Researchers should receive comprehensive information on tissue quality, and post-mortem intervals.

APOE genotype of SNUH-DBB cohort

Worldwide APOE allele frequency is ε2: 8.4%, ε3: 77.9%, and ε4: 13.7% [48]. There are variations by population: European populations: ε3 allele frequency ranges from 69%~85%, African descent populations: Often have higher ε4 frequency, and Asian populations: Often have lower ε4 frequency. Age-related trends are interesting: Decreasing frequency of ε4 with increasing age, but Increasing frequency of ε3 with increasing age [49, 50]. The APOE genotype frequency is ε3/ε3 (53.3%~58.7%), ε3/ε4 (14.0%~26.8%), ε2/ε3 (8.6%~13.3%), ε2/ε4 (2.8%~3.1%), and ε4/ε4 (0.6%~2.7%) [51-53].

Analysis of APOE genotypes in our cohort of deceased brain donors revealed a distribution largely consistent with general population frequencies.[53] The ε3/ε3 genotype was most prevalent (56.2%), followed by ε3/ε4 (21.6%), e2/ε3 (8.6%), ε2/ε4 (3.1%), and ε4/ε4 (0.6%). The high frequency of ε3/ε3 aligns with its status as the most common genotype in the general population. The ε3/ε4 genotype, associated with increased Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk, represented over a fifth of our cohort (21.6%). The ε2/ε3 genotype, linked to slightly reduced AD risk, was present in 8.6% of cases. Less common were the ε2/ε4 genotype (3.1%), which presents mixed risk factors, and the ε4/ε4 genotype (0.6%), associated with the highest AD risk. The low frequency of ε4/ε4 is consistent with population estimates but may limit statistical power for analyses specific to this high-risk group. This distribution provides a valuable foundation for investigating the relationship between APOE status and neuropathological findings across various neurodegenerative diseases. However, the relatively small sample size, particularly for rarer genotypes, should be considered when interpreting results from this cohort. This data provides a foundation for investigating the relationship between APOE genotype and various neuropathological findings in a diverse group of brain donors.

The profiles of final neuropathological diagnoses at SNUH-DBB

The neuropathological examination of 162 donated brains at our SNUH-DBB revealed a diverse spectrum of pathologies. The findings underscore the complexity of neurodegenerative diseases and the importance of comprehensive postmortem evaluations. The most prevalent diagnosis was mixed pathology, accounting for 24.7% of cases. This high proportion of mixed pathologies highlights the frequent co-occurrence of multiple neurodegenerative processes in aged brains, a factor that can significantly complicate ante-mortem diagnosis and treatment strategies. CVD was the second most common finding, present in 14.2% of cases. This underscores the significant role of vascular factors in cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. FTLD was identified in 9.9% of cases, representing a substantial proportion of neurodegenerative pathologies. FTLD subtypes included PSP (2.5%), CBD (1.2%), and FTLD-TDP with ALS (2.5%). Interestingly, ADNC as a sole pathology was relatively uncommon, found in only 5.6% of cases. This finding emphasizes the importance of considering a broad differential diagnosis in cases of cognitive decline and dementia. Other neurodegenerative conditions identified included MSA (2.5%) and CTE (1.2%). Several rare neurological conditions were also identified, including Huntington's disease (1.2%), schizophrenia (1.9%), Leigh disease (0.6%), DRPLA (0.6%), and APBD (0.6%). The presence of these rare conditions in our cohort underscores the value of brain banks in facilitating research into less common neurological disorders. The high prevalence of movement disorders and mixed pathology, and relatively low frequency of pure ADNC in our cohort reflects our center's specialties and referral patterns, rather than indicating a general shift in neurodegenerative disease frequencies. These findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive neuropathological examination for validating clinical diagnoses in complex cases and highlight the need for further research into the interplay of multiple pathologies in age-related cognitive decline and dementia.

Diagnostic challenges in neurodegenerative diseases

The discrepancies between clinical diagnoses and autopsy findings highlights the inherent complexity in accurately diagnosing neurodegenerative diseases based on clinical presentation alone. This diagnostic challenge arises from overlapping symptoms, evolving clinical features, and limitations in current neuroimaging and biomarker technologies [54, 55].

Misdiagnosis have susbstantial implications for patient care, including inappropriate treatment strategies and inaccurate prognostic information. They also impact research validity, particularly in clinical trial relying on clinical criteria for participant selection.

Autopsy findings often reveal multiple copathologies, especially in older individuals, suggesting that pure neurodegenerative diseases may be less common than previously thought. This complexity underscore the need for therapeutic approaches targeting multiple pathological processes simultaneously.

Diagnostic accuracy varies across neurodegenerative diseases. While some disorders, such as Huntington's disease, may have higher diagnostic accuracy due to distinct clinical features or genetic testing, others like AD, CBD and PSP remain challenging to diagnose accurately during life. This variability suggests the need for improved diagnostic criteria and biomarkers for specific diseases.

Clinicians should consider broad differential diagnoses, conducting longitudinal evaluations, maintaining diagnostic flexibility, and communicate uncertainties, while researchers focus on neuropathological confirmation and biomarker development for neurodegenerative diseases.

Further research should focus on development and validation of in vivo biomarkers, conducting longitudinal clinicopathological studies, investigating mixed pathologies’ impact, refining diagnostic criteria, and exploring personalized treatment for multiple concurrent pathologies in neurodegenerative diseases.

Research using human brain tissue and organoids

Human brain tissue and organoids have become invaluable tools for neuroscience research, offering unique insights into human brain development, function, and disease. However, using these tools also raises important ethical and practical considerations. Key considerations include the sources of human brain tissue, such as surgical resections (e.g., from epilepsy or tumor surgeries) or post-mortem donations.

Brain organoids are derived from human pluripotent stem cells. Human brain tissue and organoids more accurately represent human brain development and function compared to animal models, enable the study of human-specific features and diseases, provide access to developing brain tissue that would otherwise be inaccessible, and enable personalized medicine approaches using patient-derived cells.

Research applications of organoids include studying brain development and cellular organization, modeling neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases, drug and screening for drugs and toxicity, investigating neural circuit function, and exploring human-specific brain features.

The ethical implications of advanced brain organoids, particularly those related to potential consciousness or sensory perception, must be carefully considered [56]. Challenges include developing more complex organoids improving methods for essential functions, and addressing the limited availability of human brain tissue. Future directions include developing more complex and mature brain organoids, combining organoids with other technologies (e.g., organ-on-a-chip), and establishing guidelines for the ethical use of advanced brain models, which are important issues for brain banking and donation programs.

In conclusion, human brain tissue and organoids offer tremendous potential to advance our understanding of the human brain and neurological diseases. However, their use requires careful consideration of ethical implications and continued improvement of research methods.

CONCLUSION

SNUH-DBB SOP and comprehensive approach ensure that pathological features are not overlooked, as neurodegenerative disorders often present with overlapping or co-existing pathologies. This study highlights the complexity of diagnosing neurodegenerative diseases and the valuable insights gained from neuropathological examination. The results presented in this study highlight significant discrepancies between antemortem clinical diagnoses and postmortem neuropathological diagnoses in neurodegenerative diseases. The varying concordance rates across different conditions underscore the need for improved diagnostic criteria and biomarkers, particularly for AD and LBD. It emphasizes the need for continued research to bridge the gap between clinical and pathological diagnoses, ultimately leading to improved patient care and more effective therapeutic interventions.
