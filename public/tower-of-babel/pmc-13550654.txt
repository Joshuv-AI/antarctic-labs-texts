Mechano-electrochemically stable fibrous zinc-ion batteries with unified aramid nanofibres skeleton
Authors: Yinan Yang, Zhaochang Chen, Xianda Ma, Bingyun Ma, Haochen Huang, Gang Xiao, Miaoyi Xu, Zewan Lin, Yanyan Shao, Ziyan Xiong, Weifeng Yang, Xiaoxu Zhao, Jin Zhang, Tao Cheng, Xuan Zhang, Yuanlong Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogel electrolyte-based fibrous zinc-ion batteries exhibit exceptional safety and flexibility, positioning them as promising candidates for wearable electronics. However, conventional hydrogel electrolyte-based fibrous batteries suffer from substantial interfacial mechanical mismatches between rigid electrodes and soft hydrogel electrolytes, leading to compromised mechano-electrochemical stability and inferior electrochemical performance under deformation. To address this critical issue, we simultaneously introduce aramid nanofibres into both electrodes and electrolyte via wet spinning, serving as a cognate skeleton to bridge the mechanical mismatch in hydrogel electrolyte-based fibrous zinc-ion batteries. This design enables highly concerted mechanical modulus across electrodes and electrolyte, effectively mitigating stress concentration-induced interfacial degradation during deformation. Furthermore, aramid nanofibres synergistically modulate anion migration kinetics and crystalline structure of hydrogel electrolytes, addressing intrinsic challenges in sluggish ion transport, parasitic byproduct formation, and uncontrolled zinc dendrite growth of deformed fibrous batteries. The resulting fibrous batteries exhibit robust mechano-electrochemical stability, sustaining stable operation over 100,000 deformation cycles (1% strain, 0.01% s−1) and maintaining high-capacity retention (90.3% ± 1.3%) in subsequent charge-discharge cycling. The potential application in textiles is further exemplified after weaving the fibrous batteries into an energy storage textile, offering a significant potential for durable, deformation-resistant flexible wearable electronics.

Introduction

Advancements in wearable electronics and fibrous materials have transformed textiles from passive thermoregulatory materials into multifunctional, intelligent platforms1–4. However, the development of truly wearable textile electronics remains fundamentally constrained by conventional energy storage systems, which suffer from inherent rigidity, safety limitations, and bulky constraints that render them unsuitable for seamless integration. Fibrous ion batteries (FIBs), leveraging their inherent one-dimensional architecture to deliver flexibility and weavability5, are compatible for wearable6–8 or implantable scenarios9. Among various battery systems, zinc-ion batteries featuring an aqueous electrolyte nature intrinsically suppress thermal runaway risks and toxic chemicals. Positioning fibrous zinc-ion batteries (FZBs) as a promising candidate for addressing the stringent safety concerns of wearable devices10,11. Hydrogel electrolytes (HEs), leveraging their favorable flexibility, mechanical adaptability, and functional group-rich structure, serve as a transformative alternative for liquid electrolytes (LEs)5,12,13, which are capable of enhancing the adaptability and implementation potential of FZBs14–18.

However, the realization of energy-storage textiles faces additional mechano-electrochemical challenges. The daily wearing of garments involves multiple dynamic deformations, including bending angles exceeding 120° at elbows and knees, circumferential fiber strain reaching at least 5%, and torsional deformation ranging from 10° to 30° along the limb5,19. These scenarios manifest a critical challenge, i.e., the interfacial mechanical incompatibility of conventional HEs-based FZBs (cHE-FZBs) between soft HEs and rigid electrodes15,16,20,21. The intrinsic sub-MPa modulus of HEs contrasts sharply with the GPa-level rigidity of inorganic positive electrodes or Zn metal negative electrodes, triggering severe stress concentration at the soft-rigid interface and eventual interfacial delamination during repetitive dynamic deformation22–24. Consequently, under daily wearing conditions involving complex motions, these physical defects, along with the polarized electric fields and severely hindered ion transport at high-curvature regions, collectively cause uncontrolled Zn deposition, exaggerated byproduct proliferation, surged interfacial resistance, and ultimately lead to premature failure of cHE-FZBs (Fig. 1a). The majority of the prevailing approaches for addressing mechano-electrochemical mismatch are focused on interfacial adhesion enhancement, either through flexible carbon-material incorporation28–30, hierarchical electrode microstructural engineering7,31, or HEs viscoelasticity manipulation14,15,20,32. Although the aforementioned strategies effectively strengthen electrode-electrolyte contact, the inherent soft-rigid mechanical mismatch at the HE-electrode interface remains unresolved23. Meanwhile, there is still a lack of studies focused on mechano-electrochemistry at the HE-electrode interface, especially ion transport kinetics, interfacial stress distributions, and dendrite-suppressive mechanisms under operational deformation. To this end, developing a robust unified matrix that harmonizes the intrinsic mechanical and electrochemical features among HEs and electrodes, with an emphasis on studying the interfacial mechano-electrochemistry of FZBs is urgently needed.

Herein, we rationally engineered a cognate aramid nanofibre (ANF) skeleton within the HE-FZBs through the synchronous incorporation of ANFs into both electrode and HE matrix (polyvinyl alcohol, PVA) via multicomponent wet spinning. Leveraging rapid proton-driven gelation33,34, ANFs establish a homogeneous and robust network throughout the FZBs, which fundamentally addresses interfacial instability under deformation. Simultaneously, the amide groups within ANFs provide abundant hydrogen-bonding sites, which not only anchor anions to regulate Zn deposition but also disrupt the crystallization of the HE to promote ion transport. This synergy of the unified ANF framework in optimizing both interfacial mechanics and electrochemistry effectively addresses key challenges of FZBs under dynamic deformation, including interfacial delamination, electric-field polarization, and uneven ion transport (Fig. 1b).

The resulting ANF-enabled full FZBs (ANF-FZBs, i.e., Zn-CNT-ANF|PVA-ANF|MnO2-CNT-ANF) exhibited robust mechano-electrochemical stability (Fig. 1b), maintaining undistorted galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) profiles even during continuous dynamic bending (0° to 120°, 5 mm radius, 10° s-1), effectively preventing the common short-circuit failures in cHE-FZBs (employ rigid Zn wire negative electrode with surface-coated PVA-ANF electrolyte) (Fig. 1c, d). Moreover, the ANF-FZBs demonstrated enhanced reliability under consecutive deformation, retaining 95.6% capacity and a slight increase in the charge transfer resistance (Rct, 104.4%) after 1000 bending cycles (Fig. 1e, f). The ANF-FZBs also demonstrate their feasibility in textiles by weaving them into a 30 × 40 cm2 fabric via industrial looms. The integrated textiles retained stable operation under multi-mode deformation (folding, twisting, compressing) while delivering an areal capacity of 342.5 mAh m-2. Notably, the textile prototype enabled direct integration with flexible display textiles, underscoring its viability as an energy-supply platform for smart textile systems (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Movie 1).

Results

Mechanically compatible interface design

HE-based FZBs exhibit intrinsic structural flexibility and weaving compatibility (Fig. 1b)5,7,35. However, under mechanical deformation, conventional HE-based FZBs still suffer from interfacial incompatibility stemming from the inherent modulus disparity between low-stiffness HEs and highly rigid electrode fibers (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

To address this challenge, we propose that mechanical homogeneity across the fiber battery could enable coherent interfaces. Inspired by the rapid protonation-induced gelation (Supplementary Fig. 3)33,34, ANFs could serve as a versatile nano-building block to construct a cognate nanofibrous network throughout HE-FZBs. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 2a, b, ANFs were synergistically integrated into electrodes (comprising MnO2/CNT positive electrodes and Zn/CNT negative electrodes) and PVA-based HE spinning dopes. These components are henceforth termed MnO2-CNT-ANF, Zn-CNT-ANF, and PVA-ANF, respectively. The dispersion stability of spinning dopes was well retained after the incorporation of ANFs, indicating suitable viscosity and shear-thinning rheology (Supplementary Figs. 4e and 5e). Moreover, protonation-induced gelation in an aqueous coagulation bath was directly verified from the digital photographs in Supplementary Figs. 4d and 5d. Rheological analysis further validated this synchronous phase transition for both electrode and HE, in which ANF hybridized wet-spinning dopes (ANF-PVA and ANF-CNT) exhibited a dramatic viscosity increase and storage modulus surge after water addition. Moreover, their viscoelastic-to-elastic transition shifted toward lower angular frequencies, which indicates the increase of polymeric network relaxation time and the formation of crosslinking sites36,37. Employing a customized triple-lumen spinneret (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Movie 2), we achieved simultaneous extrusion of electrode-core and electrolyte-sheath spinning dopes, with a full fiber battery assembled through synchronous solidification in an aqueous coagulant bath. Such electrode–electrolyte synchronous wet-spinning created an intimate interfacial contact between the individual components of FZBs without voids and defects, as validated by focused ion beam-scanning electron microscope (FIB-SEM) and Raman mapping (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) results also revealed a homogeneous distribution of active nanoparticles within the electrode alongside interwoven CNT and ANF networks (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). This morphology ensures intimate micro-interfacial interlinkage between active materials and CNT/ANF networks that facilitates electron and ion transport (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Furthermore, atomic force microscopy-infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR) revealed an interpenetrating interfacial morphology through the characteristic C-O stretching vibration of PVA at 1046 cm-1 (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicative of PVA chain extension and diffusion towards the electrode-core flow during wet-spinning. Such a configuration was conducive to interphase hydrogen bonding establishment and rapid ion transport across the interface. We further quantified this robust interfacial bonding via fiber pull-out tests (Supplementary Fig. 10). For ANF-FZBs, the interfacial shear strength reached 0.44 MPa with an adhesion energy of 3.05 mJ mm-2, outperforming that of cHE-FZBs employing Zn wire coated with PVA-ANF HEs, which exhibited only 0.18 MPa and 0.71 mJ mm-2. These results demonstrate the intrinsically strong interfacial adhesion of ANF-FZBs, providing a solid foundation for the mechano-electrochemical stability under dynamic deformation.

To verify the HE-electrode modulus harmonization across FZBs via shared ANF frameworks (Fig. 2b), we conducted comprehensive mechanical profiling of each component and its interfaces. As depicted in Fig. 2c, d, AFM mapping was conducted to reveal the uniform distribution of the Derjaguin–Müller–Toporov (DMT) modulus across the interface. After porosity correction, the positive electrode and negative electrode displayed average DMT modulus of 1.7 GPa and 1.4 GPa, respectively, which closely align with the values of HE (2.2 GPa on the negative electrode side and 2.4 GPa on the positive electrode side). Moreover, nanoindentation serves as a powerful characterization technique for probing mechanical responses under force loading/unloading38,39. Figure 2e depicts the similar strain responses for HE and electrodes under deformation, in which all components demonstrate nearly identical maximum displacements and creeps (21.5 nm for PVA-ANF, 27.5 nm for MnO2-CNT-ANF, and 22.0 nm for Zn-CNT-ANF) under consistent loading. Furthermore, analogous elastic deformation was also observed after unloading (37.4% for PVA-ANF, 29.9% for MnO2-CNT-ANF, 34.5% for Zn-CNT-ANF), which is essential for maintaining interfacial contact after the retreat of external stress. As summarized in Fig. 2f, this mechanical response coherence is attributed to the cognate ANF frameworks, which impart consistent modulus and hardness to both electrode and electrolyte (4.7 GPa and 0.20 GPa for PVA-ANF, 4.9 GPa and 0.16 GPa for MnO2-CNT-ANF, and 4.4 GPa and 0.18 GPa for Zn-CNT-ANF). Furthermore, we explored the dynamic mechanical properties of the individual components. This dynamic mechanical compatibility ensures that all components of the FZBs can respond in a coordinated manner and dissipate mechanical energy synchronously during wear-induced deformations. This constitutes the fundamental mechanical prerequisite for the battery to withstand repeated bending, while maintaining long-term electrochemical stability. This compatibility is quantitatively rooted in the matched viscoelasticity revealed by dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA), where all components exhibit highly matched storage modulus and loss modulus (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 13) under sustained oscillatory load. This indicates that the electrodes and electrolyte not only start with similar stiffness but, crucially, relax in unison over time, ensuring persistently uniform stress distribution. This dynamic synergy is further supported by stress relaxation tests (Supplementary Fig. 14a) and cyclic tensile results (Supplementary Fig. 14b), showing coordinated stress decay and matched energy dissipation. This orchestrated behavior ensures that under real-world dynamic deformation, mechanical energy is uniformly dissipated and interfacial stresses are actively relaxed, thereby preventing fatigue damage. Consequently, this coherent dynamic interface ensures the physical and electrochemical integrity of FZBs over long-term cycling.

In addition, the tensile test in Supplementary Fig. 15 demonstrated mechanical reinforcement in PVA-ANF HEs, achieving a tensile strength of 74.3 MPa, which is over six times higher than that of the pristine PVA counterpart of 9.9 MPa. We performed finite element method (FEM) simulations (Supplementary Fig. 16d) to study the fracture behavior of the PVA and PVA-ANF HEs. The crack propagated rapidly in the PVA HEs with a low stress at the crack tip of 12 MPa with a concentrated stress distribution. In comparison, according to the inherently robust ANFs interconnected network40,41, the PVA-ANF HEs presented an increased stress at the crack tip by 625% to 75 MPa, indicating a delay of crack propagation.

At the molecular level, the FTIR data in Supplementary Fig. 16b, c revealed strong hydrogen bonding between ANFs and PVA. In the solution state, the blueshift of C=O stretching frequency and redshift of N-H vibration frequency of ANFs amide group upon PVA incorporation, indicated the strong hydrogen bonding between N-H groups from ANF and O-H groups from PVA, which weakened the conjugation between nitrogen lone pairs and carbonyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 16c). These dynamic supramolecular interactions throughout the HEs enabled efficient stress transmission and fracture energy dissipation42. Furthermore, 2D wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) and azimuthal intensity distributions revealed enhanced molecular alignment in PVA-ANF (Supplementary Fig. 17). This hierarchical bonding, which combines transient interfacial interactions with permanent intra-fiber networks, coalesces the intrinsic mechanical strength of ANFs with PVA-ANF interfacial stress transfer capacity.

To clarify the advantages of the similarity of mechanical properties between HEs and electrodes, especially their Young’s modulus, FEM simulations (Fig. 2h) were performed to quantify stress distribution and interfacial dynamic contact evolution during bending deformation of FZBs. In cHE-FZBs, despite the PVA‑ANF HEs possessing a notably high modulus of ~4.7 GPa—it still fall far short of matching the mechanical stiffness of the Zn metal wires, whose Young’s modulus is ~108 GPa. This pronounced modulus mismatch induces a significant stress difference at the electrode–electrolyte interface in the bending regions. As summarized in Fig. 2i, upon exceeding a bending angle (derived from Supplementary Fig. 19) of 42°, interfacial failure is initiated in cHE-FZBs. It triggered abrupt interfacial decohesion with a rapid ascent of the cumulative scalar damage (CSDMG) to 1, which corresponds to complete interfacial failure. Conversely, ANF-FZBs with a unified ANF framework demonstrated well-preserved interface integrity under strain, given the comparable modulus across the electrode–electrolyte interface (Fig. 2h, i). This enhanced interfacial mechanics ensures unaltered CSDMG value and sustained contact integrity throughout deformation above a bending angle of 110°, effectively mitigating bending failure inherent to cHE-FZBs. Furthermore, our FEM simulations further validated the robustness of this mechanically matched interface strategy, consistently demonstrating its effectiveness in preserving structural integrity not only under individual tensile, bending, and torsional deformations but also, critically, under their coupled combinations (Supplementary Figs. 20–25).

Electrochemical interface modulation

As illustrated in Fig. 3a, beyond establishing mechanically compatible interfaces in FZBs, we also investigate the optimization effect of ANFs on the interfacial electrochemical behavior within full fibrous batteries, where the synergy between interfacial mechanics and ion regulation determines the mechano-electrochemical stability. The ionic conductivity of HEs and their interfacial ion transport are determinants of the interfacial electrochemical performance. In anhydrous or low-hydration systems, ion hopping dominates the ionic transport along with polymer segmental motion (Supplementary Fig. 26a). Conversely, in hydrated HE, the solvated-ion vehicle mechanism prevails because of the rapid ion migration through solvent networks (Supplementary Fig. 26b)43. Specifically, dense-packed crystalline domains will exclude solvent molecules, thereby obstructing the ion conduction pathways44–46. The enhanced ion transport kinetics in ANF-engineered HEs stemmed from structural modulation of crystalline domains, as systematically validated through multi-scale characterization and computational simulation. Whereas, PVA-ANF HEs exhibited no discernible peaks, indicating negligible chain orientation. Complementary WAXS profiles in Supplementary Fig. 28a confirmed the reduced crystallinity in PVA-ANF HEs, as evidenced by an increase in the full-width at half maximum (FWHM) from 2.69° to 5.44° for PVA-ANF HEs, reflecting reduced crystallite dimensions relative to those of PVA HEs. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) also demonstrated reduced melting enthalpy and advanced melting onset for PVA-ANF HEs (Supplementary Fig. 29). Given that, the introduction of ANFs disrupts the crystallinity of the PVA matrix, creating more free volume and a flexible polymer network for solvent accommodation. Consequently, the water content in the PVA-ANF HEs increases markedly from 30.5 wt% to 75.0 wt% (Supplementary Fig. 30).

Coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) simulations mechanistically linked the structural disorder to ion mobility enhancements. Segmental orientation analysis (Fig. 3d) corroborated disrupted chain alignment in PVA-ANF HEs, with the crystallinity decreasing from 21.32% (sharp alignment peak at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cos (\theta )=0.9$$\end{document}cos(θ)=0.9 in PVA) to 10.46% (random distribution in PVA-ANF). Equilibrated configuration visualizations confirm that PVA forms densely packed crystalline bundles, whereas PVA-ANF adopts a disordered morphology with a spatial chain arrangement (Insets in Fig. 3d, Supplementary Data 1 and 2). The ion conduct path in the polymeric network revealed that for the pristine PVA matrix, dense crystalline domains act as barriers that severely hinder and deflect ion migration paths (Supplementary Fig. 31a). In stark contrast, the disordered, amorphous structure of the PVA-ANF composite presents a homogeneous diffusion mode that ions can freely pass through the polymer network (Supplementary Fig. 31b), manifesting a 316% enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 32) in Zn2+ diffusivity within PVA-ANF HEs (4.61 × 10-7 cm2 s-1) compared with that of the PVA HEs (1.46 × 10-7 cm2 s-1). The crystalline structure modulation of the polymer matrix ensures rapid ion transport within the PVA-ANF HE, which effectively suppresses mechano-coupled inhomogeneous Zn-ion deposition.

In addition to the ionic conductivity of HEs, the effects of ANF on the Zn ion solvation structure and interfacial ion transport behavior are also critical for determining the electrochemical performance. FTIR spectra (Fig. 3e) and MD simulations (Supplementary Fig. 35, Supplementary Data 3 and 4) have been put forward to investigate the solvation structures of Zn ions. FTIR spectra in Fig. 3e revealed an apparent redshift of the SO42- vibration from 1085 cm-1 to 1062 cm-1 once ANFs were incorporated, which corresponds to weakened coordination between SO42- and Zn2+18. Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis according to the MD simulation in Supplementary Fig. 35 demonstrated lower SO42- average coordination numbers within PVA-ANF HEs (1.41) compared to PVA HEs (1.98). The variation in anion number within Zn solvation structures fundamentally arises from hydrogen bonding between ANFs and anions. According to the electrostatic potential (ESP) surface analysis of binding energy (Supplementary Fig. 36, Supplementary Data 5–12), a strong interaction between ANFs and anions ( − 50.31 kJ/mol) is suggested. We note that DFT calculations are performed on isolated molecular systems and may not fully capture the complex solvation environment in the actual electrolyte. This was further corroborated by Raman spectroscopy in Supplementary Fig. 37, where peak deconvolution of SO42- symmetric stretching vibrations revealed a distinct new peak at ~988 cm-1, which can be assigned to the specific SO42--ANF interaction48 (summarized in Supplementary Fig. 38). This unique chemical coordination enables the anchoring of anions, contributing to an increased Zn2+ transference number (Supplementary Fig. 39) in the PVA-ANF (0.74) compared with that in the PVA (0.33) and LE (0.44), which effectively suppresses parasitic anion migration and dendrite nucleation49.

As a result, the robust anion-anchoring capability of ANFs critically alleviates interfacial ion depletion, which is an important initiator for dendrite growth, by reconstructing the electric double layer on the Zn metal surface as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 40 (Supplementary Note 2). As shown in Fig. 3f, in-situ Raman spectra were conducted to directly monitor the ion transport at the electrolyte/electrode interface. For the case of PVA HEs, the characteristic SO42- signal intensity at ~980 cm-1 underwent a sharp decline of ~81% upon the initiation of Zn dendrite growth. In contrast, PVA-ANF HEs maintained a consistent signal intensity, indicating stable interfacial ion flux. Meanwhile, MD simulations under applied electric fields quantitatively depicted the anion distribution near the electrode interface. ANFs incorporation induced a fourfold decrease in anions within 10 Å of the interface, while enabling uniform distribution across the electrolyte matrix (Supplementary Fig. 43). Thus, the ion depletion during electrodeposition is alleviated to avoid the concentration-induced dendrite growth.

Synergizing their improved ion conductivity and anion immobilization capabilities, the PVA-ANF HEs exhibited enhanced Zn2+ desolvation kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 44). Moreover, Zn deposition uniformity was enhanced in PVA-ANF HEs. This enhancement originates from the homogeneous and rapid ionic flux enabled by PVA-ANF, which facilitates uniform 3D ion transport (Supplementary Fig. 45b). Consequently, grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) revealed preferential Zn deposition along the thermodynamically stable (002) crystallographic plane (74.4% for PVA-ANF HEs and 54.4% for PVA HEs, Supplementary Fig. 46e, f) and uniform distribution of Zn nucleation sites (Supplementary Fig. 46c, d)50. In addition, with immobilized anions, PVA-ANF HEs mitigated interfacial parasitic reactions (Supplementary Figs. 45 and 47), as evidenced by a reduced corrosion current density from 10.87 mA cm-2 (PVA HEs) to 1.19 mA cm-2 (PVA-ANF HEs) and a negative potential shift of 80 mV in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Correspondingly, Zn electrodeposition in PVA-ANF HE system exhibited uniform, planar morphologies (Supplementary Fig. 48b, d), which are different from the dendritic/mossy structures observed in PVA HEs (Supplementary Fig. 48a, c). This optimized deposition behavior further contributed to the enhanced Zn reversibility of plating/stripping even under harsh conditions such as high Zn utilization rates (ZUR, Supplementary Fig. 49 and Note 3).

Afterward, the positive electrode interface dynamics in ANF-FZBs were also systematically studied via a multimodal ex situ characterization. Cyclic voltammetry (CV, Supplementary Fig. 51 and Note 4) displayed two pairs of reduction/oxidation peaks, indicating a reversible Zn²⁺/H⁺ co-intercalation mechanism. Ex situ XRD pattern in Fig. 3g verified the reversible electrochemical reaction of the MnO2-CNT-ANF fibrous positive electrode. After initial discharge, a pronounced (001) diffraction signature emerges at ~8.1° for (Zn(OH)2)3(ZnSO4)(H2O)5 (zinc hydroxysulfate, ZSO), which is ascribed to the H⁺ intercalation into MnO2 tunnels. Further extinction of this signature after full charging indicated negligible formation of byproduct given the reversible H⁺ (de)intercalation. Throughout the whole cycling process, persistent MnO2 diffraction peaks were observed, indicating uncompromised tunnel integrity. In detail, periodic peak shifts in (101), (211), and (220) planes were demonstrated (Fig. 3g), manifesting reversible lattice inter-planar distance evolution. In addition, a one-electron reversible redox process in the Mn valence state was confirmed by ex-situ XPS (Supplementary Fig. 53). Collectively, these findings support a highly reversible dual-ion (Zn2+/H+) co-intercalation mechanism with minimal byproduct accumulation in the MnO2-CNT-ANF fibrous positive electrode.

To further verify the charge storage capability under a practical scenario, the ANF-FZB with limited negative electrode reservation (N/P = 2.66) was prepared to evaluate the effectiveness of the unified ANF skeleton on the overall electrochemical performance. As displayed in Fig. 3h, the ANF-FZB delivered a 77.8% capacity retention with an average CE over 99.8% at 1 A g-1 over 2200 charge–discharge cycles6,14,16,28,29,51–58 (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, a specific energy of 22.1 mWh g−1 (19.5 mWh g−1) at a specific power of 14.4 mW g−1 (73.1 mW g−1) was provided based on the total weight of ANF-FZBs (Supplementary Fig. 55). Such electrochemical performance of ANF-FZBs under harsh conditions demonstrates the positive impacts of ANFs in regulating Zn deposition behavior and ion transport kinetics.

Bending deformation can primarily impose severe interfacial perturbations on FZBs, inducing pronounced electric field polarization20,59. FEM simulations of the interfacial electric and ion field distributions (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Figs. 56 and 57) revealed elevated current density of ~350% at high-curvature regions in bent LE-FZBs. This heterogeneous field distribution drives disordered zinc deposition, which tends to induce premature failure of FZBs. For cHE-FZBs, mechanical property mismatch triggered severe interfacial delamination during deformation (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 2). As depicted in Fig. 4c, those physically separated regions blocked ionic and electronic transport, leading to a slump in the local current density to zero. However, a surge in the electric field intensity was also observed at the partially adhered electrode-electrolyte interface (Supplementary Fig. 58). In contrast, for ANF-FZBs, the well-designed unified ANF framework not only maintains seamless interfacial contact under bending through mechanically matched properties but also provides rapid ion transport to mitigate the electric field polarization. This is quantitatively confirmed by GITT measurements under identical bending conditions (Supplementary Fig. 64a, b), which reveal that the apparent Zn2+ diffusion coefficient in the bent ANF-FZBs (2.09 × 10-10 cm2 s-1) remains nearly identical to that measured in the straight state (2.26 × 10-10 cm2 s-1), indicating unimpeded ion transport through the mechanically coherent interface. Thus, as demonstrated in Fig. 4c, the homogeneous current density and ion concentration throughout bending are well-preserved for the ANF-FZBs.

In addition, in situ EIS (Fig. 4d–h) was employed to characterize the mechanical-coupling electrochemical reaction mechanism of the fibrous electrode/electrolyte interface. Moreover, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT, Fig. 4j–m) was also derived from the quantitative deconvolution of EIS spectra. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 59 and corresponding contour plots (Fig. 4j–m), six distinct peaks are observed, each corresponding to a specific relaxation time of the interfacial electrochemical reaction.

First, the LE-FZBs in straight configuration exhibited a low initial charge transfer resistance (Rct) of 39.8 Ω. However, a reduction to 3.4 Ω was observed upon the Zn plating (Fig. 4e, i), which was attributed to uncontrolled dendritic growth within the liquid electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 61). The ramified dendritic structure contributed to an increased electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), which in concert with the localized “tip effect” at dendrite extremities can enhance local charge-transfer kinetics temporarily (Supplementary Fig. 62). In contrast, the curved geometry of bent LE-FZBs (120°, 5 mm radius) impeded ionic transport pathways, which obstructed interfacial reaction kinetics initially. It demonstrated an elevated initial Rct of 227 Ω (Fig. 4f) with an enhanced intensity for all relaxation times compared with the straight state in the DRT profile (Fig. 4k). However, upon discharge to approximately 1.4 V, a pronounced decrease in Rct to 92.7 Ω emerged (Fig. 4f, i). This reduction in Rct is likely a consequence of preferential zinc ion deposition with dendritic morphology in high-curvature regions (Supplementary Fig. 61), where an intensified electric field polarization exists. Mechano-electrochemical performance and integrated textile design of ANF-FZBs

To verify the practical application of ANF-FZBs, we further evaluated their electrochemical performance under operando long-term dynamic deformation. The optical images in Supplementary Fig. 65 demonstrated that the encapsulated ANF-FZBs have flexibility and adaptability to various deformations. As illustrated in the inset schematic of Fig. 5a, the ANF-FZB was subjected to continuous bending from 0° to 120° at a rate of 10° s-1 while simultaneously undergoing galvanostatic charging/discharging cycling at 1 A g-1, satisfying the pragmatic application scenario of garments at elbows and knees. The charge–discharge profile in Fig. 5a exhibited that the ANF-FZB maintained stable operation for over 450 cycles with a 68% capacity retention even under such stringent conditions. The well-defined electrochemical plateaus remained unchanged throughout the bending process, indicating highly stable redox reactions during mechanical deformation. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 66, the ANF-FZBs could also operate stably across a 0–180° bending range. To further evaluate the practical lifespan, the ANF-FZBs demonstrated a capacity retention of 90.3% ± 1.3% with virtually unchanged median voltage even after a long cycle of 100,000 stretches (1% strain, 0.01% s-1) (Fig. 5b). To present the concept of wearable energy-storage textiles, ANF-FZBs were woven into a 30 × 40 cm2 fabric using a commercial loom machine (Supplementary Fig. 69). The resultant textile exhibited a capacity of 41.1 mAh (342.5 mAh m-2) with enhanced charge–discharge stability, achieving an energy density of 805.5 mWh m-2 at a power density of 796 mW m-2 (Fig. 5c, d). These electrochemical characteristics collectively ensure the practical viability of the energy storage textiles, enabling reliable power delivery to commercial mobile phones under simulated real-world perturbations without performance compromise (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Movie 3). Furthermore, inheriting the reliable mechano-electrochemical properties of individual ANF-FZBs, the textile maintained stable performance under folding, twisting, stretching and water immersion (Fig. 5f–h and Supplementary Movie 4). Such performance is rooted in the dual functions of the unified nanofibrous scaffold, such as redistributing localized stresses via its microarchitecture to prevent interfacial delamination and preserving ion-transport homogeneity across bending-induced strain gradients. This technology establishes a blueprint for stable energy textiles, resolving the incompatibility between textile-based deformability and FZB's performance stability.

Discussion

This work involves engineering a unified ANF skeletal framework within FZBs from a materials-design standpoint. This architecture effectively addresses mechanical interface mismatch under practical usage scenarios, leading to improved electrochemical performance during dynamic deformation. Our study focuses on the critical yet underexplored aspect of mechanically coupled electrochemical reactions at evolving electrode-electrolyte interfaces during deformation, a unique challenge in wearable electronics. Benefiting from the closely matched modulus enabled by the homologous framework, ANF-FZBs maintain uniform stress distribution and intact interfacial contact across various deformation modes. When woven into textiles, inheriting the mechano-electrochemical properties of individual ANF-FZBs, the textile retained reliable operation under multiple external stresses (compressing, twisting, stretching, and folding). Furthermore, its successful integration with functional display textiles confirms its potential for practical wearable applications.

Methods

Chemicals and resources

All chemicals, including PVA (M.W. 146000-186000, 99 + % hydrolyzed, Sigma-Aldrich), CNTs (F2020, Jiangsu Cnano Technology Co., Ltd., 99%), Aramid fibers (China Bluestar Chengrand Co., Ltd., commercial grade), MnO2 (Zhejiang Vastech Technology Co., Ltd., ≥99.5%), Zn powder (Beijing Gaoke New Material Technology Co., ≥99.5%), ZnSO4·7H2O (AR, Greagent), PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning), DMSO (HPLC, Adamas), KOH (AR, Adamas), and the other chemicals (Adamas) were purchased and used without further purification. Deionized water was generated by a Millipore water purification system.

Preparation of electrolyte and electrodes spinning dopes

Aramid fiber pulp (2.0 g) and KOH (2.0 g) were first mixed in 100 g DMSO, and then the mixture was magnetically stirred for 2 days at 25 ± 1 °C to obtain 2 wt% ANF dispersion. PVA (5.0–30.0 g) was dissolved in 100 g of DMSO and magnetically stirred at 60°C for 1 day to obtain PVA solution (5–30 wt%). Electrolyte spinning dopes were obtained after the mixing of ANFs dispersion in DMSO and PVA solution in DMSO with a 1:1 mass ratio. Electrode spinning dopes were composed of active material, ANFs, and CNTs in DMSO solution. CNTs (1.0 g) were dispersed in 100 ml of DMSO and magnetically stirred for two days. To match the solid content of ANFs within the HEs, 10.0 g ANFs dispersion (2 wt%), 40.0 g CNTs dispersion (1 wt%) and 0.8 g active material (zinc powder for negative electrode, MnO2 for positive electrode) were mixed and then stirred for 1 day before the final dopes were ready for wet spinning.

Preparation of FZBs

ANF-FZBs were fabricated via a wet-spinning process (YFWSS, Tianjin Yunfan Technology Co., Ltd.). Initially, the electrode and electrolyte spinning dopes were loaded into separate syringes. Subsequently, the individual components were extruded through a custom-designed spinneret into a deionized water coagulation bath using three independent syringe pumps. To ensure spinning stability, the extrusion rate of the inner electrode spinning dope was maintained at 30 μL min-1, while that of the outer electrolyte was set at 70 μL min-1. Following the extrusion, the resultant ANF-FZBs were collected on a winding spool and then immersed in a 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous solution for 24 h. The Zn‑CNT‑ANF fiber negative electrode had an average Zn loading of 0.81 mg cm-1, and the MnO2‑CNT‑ANF positive electrode had an average MnO2 loading of 0.78 mg cm-1. Subsequently, thermoplastic polyethylene (PE) heat-shrink tube was used as encapsulation. For comparison, LE-FZBs were assembled by helically wrapping NKKTF 4030 cellulose (Japan, NKK, TF 4030, 100 μm thickness) separator membranes around zinc metal wires (300 μm diameter, 5.0 mg cm−1), subsequently saturating the assembly with 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte. Positive electrode fibers were then placed in parallel to the separator-wrapped zinc wire prior to PE encapsulation. For the preparation of cHE-FZBs systems, a PVA-ANF hybrid solution was injected into heat-shrink tubing (1.5 mm inner diameter, to match the thickness of ANF-FZBs), followed by insertion of zinc metal wire and positive electrode fiber. The assembly was immersed in an aqueous coagulant for gelation, followed by an immersion in 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous solution. For the preparation of PVA-FZBs systems, due to mismatched coagulation thermodynamics, we could not employ the identical multi-channel wet-spinning process used for the ANF-FZBs. PVA solution was also injected into heat-shrink tubing, followed by insertion of CNT-ANF electrode fibers. The assembly was immersed in ethanol coagulant for gelation, followed by immersion in 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous solution. To eliminate positive electrode variability, MnO2-CNT-ANF fibrous positive electrodes were utilized across all systems.

Characterization and measurement

For the characterization of electrode samples after battery disassembly, the fibrous electrodes were carefully extracted, and the residual HEs were gently peeled off. The electrodes were then sequentially rinsed with deionized water and ethanol three times to remove any remaining salt residues, followed by drying at 60 ± 1 °C. No vacuum sealing or inert atmosphere protection was used during sample transfer or preparation. This drying process causes the porous fibrous electrodes to shrink, leading to a smaller diameter than that observed under wet conditions (e.g., by optical microscopy). Unless otherwise noted, all electrochemical tests and device assemblies were performed with the fibers in their fully hydrated state. All ex situ measurements were performed at 25 ± 1 °C under ambient atmosphere unless otherwise specified. The morphologies of ANF-FZBs and electrode fibers were characterized by field-emission SEM (SU8010, Hitachi) at an electric tension of 10 kV, and a current of 10 μA and TEM (JEOL JEM-F200). The crystal structure of fibrous Zn electrodes and MnO2 electrode was collected by XRD (Rigaku SmartLab 9KW, Cu Kα ray). To enable cross-section observation of ANF-FZBs’ interface, a focused ion beam was employed using an FEI Helios NanoLab 600i (the acceleration voltage was 30 kV and the current decreased from 230 pA to 40 pA).The sample was freeze-dried prior to imaging to preserve the porous structure of the fibrous electrode. Raman spectra were collected on a LabRAM HR800 (Horiba Jobin Yvon) using an incident laser (532 nm or 633 nm) beam. The XPS was performed on Thermo Scientific ESCA Lab 2500Xi to characterize the chemical valence states of the MnO2 electrode. WAXS and SAXS measurements were conducted on a Xenocs Xeuss SAXS/WAXS System using an incident Cu-Kα X-ray beam. AFM images and DMT modulus mapping were obtained using Dimension Icon (Bruker). AFM-IR was conducted using the Nano-IR3 system (Bruker). Mechanical properties of single filaments were assessed using a universal testing machine (CMT6103) equipped with a 5 N load sensor. Nanoindentation was conducted on Kla imicro. The rheological behavior of the spinnable dopes was obtained by a Rotational Rheometer (Kinexus pro). The interfacial adhesion strength between the Zn-CNT-ANF fiber electrode and the PVA-ANF HEs was evaluated using a customized pull-out test on a universal testing machine. The fiber was vertically embedded in the hydrogel matrix with an embedded length of 1 cm, and the exposed fiber end was pulled unidirectionally at a displacement rate of 1 mm/min while recording the force-displacement curve. The interfacial shear strength was calculated from the maximum debonding force divided by the embedded interfacial area.

Flexibility characterization of ANF-FZBs

The flexible properties and mechano-electrochemical performance of the assembled FZBs were obtained using a flexible electronic tester (MF-FT2000, Shanghai Mifang Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.), Neware battery test system and Biologic electrochemical workstation (Vsp-3e). In brief, the fiber battery was fixed onto a flexible testing platform. After the application of predetermined deformation protocols (stretching, twisting, bending), FZB's electrochemical performance was evaluated using the Neware battery test system or the Biologic electrochemical workstation. To better replicate real-world usage scenarios, cycling performance was further assessed while concurrently subjecting the FZBs to bending motions from 0° to 60° (5 mm radius, 10° s-1) on the platform, with the electrodes maintained in connection to the Neware system throughout the test. Unless otherwise specified, bending tests were performed with a radius of 5 mm. A bending angle of 60° was used for extended cycling tests to balance mechanical load and test duration, while a more severe 120° angle was applied to probe the limits of interfacial stability. To ensure consistent electrical contact between the electrodes and stainless-steel leads, UV-curable adhesive (acrylic adhesive) was employed to fix the electrode-to-current collector connections. Furthermore, conductive adhesive tape was utilized to establish connections between the battery testing system and the stainless-steel leads, thereby preventing disruption to the electrical interface during bending motions or mechanical stress.

Fabrication of the display textile

The electroluminescent (EL) fibers were directly purchased without further treatment. To fabricate the display textile and integrate with the energy storage textile woven by ANF-FZBs, the EL fibers (LITME®, Yantai Jingwei Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd.) were manually embroidered onto the surface of the fabric in a pre-designed pattern using a commercial sewing machine (Brother Innov-is M370, Brother (China) Ltd.). The EL fibers were then connected in parallel to a high-voltage AC inverter module. The inverter input terminals were connected to the output wires of the ANF-FZB textile. When the battery textile was switched on, it powered the inverter, which in turn drove the EL fibers to emit light, thereby demonstrating the integrated system’s functionality as a power supply for wearable displays. The study protocol was non-invasive; ethics oversight was waived.

DFT calculations

The binding energies and molecular electrostatic potentials (ESP) were calculated with density functional theory (DFT) at the level of B3LYP/6-311 + G(d,p) as implemented in the Gaussian 16 software package. vdW dispersion was corrected via Grimme’s DFT-D3, and solvation effects were modeled implicitly via the SMD (solvation model based on density) continuum approach.

Thus, a negative Ebind indicates energetically favorable association.

ESP maps were generated by mapping the electrostatic potential, which was calculated from Gaussian-generated cube files, onto the molecular electron density isosurface via Multiwfn (version 3.8.dev)63,64, and subsequently rendered with visual molecular dynamics (VMD, version 1.9.3)65.

CGMD simulations

All CGMD simulations were performed via the MARTINI 3.0 force field framework66. According to the standard MARTINI mapping strategy, each coarse-grained (CG) bead corresponds to approximately two to four heavy atoms, depending on the nature of the chemical fragment. Bead types and their associated interaction parameters were assigned following the MARTINI 3 guidelines, with bonded terms (bonds, angles, and, where applicable, dihedrals) parameterized using the default MARTINI values. The initial configurations were constructed with PACKMOL67, with the specific packing protocol tailored to the complexity of each system. The ANF-containing system consisted of 16 ANF chains (degree of polymerization, DP = 8), each comprising 64 coarse-grained beads, and 136 PVA chains (DP = 10), each comprising 10 coarse-grained beads. In addition, the system contained 800 SO42- anions, 800 Zn2+ cations, and 5400 water beads. For comparison, the ANF-free system comprised 600 PVA chains (DP = 10) of 10 coarse-grained beads each, together with the same number of SO42- anions (800), Zn2+ cations (800), and water beads (5400). All the systems were constructed to match the target density and concentrations determined experimentally. CGMD simulations were performed via GROMACS 202068, patched for full compatibility with the MARTINI 3 force field. A time step of 20 fs was employed for numerical integration, and periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three spatial dimensions. The system temperature was maintained at 300 K via a velocity-rescale thermostat69 with a coupling constant of 1.0 ps. The pressure was controlled at 1.0 bar, employing the Berendsen barostat during equilibration and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat70 during production runs, with a coupling constant of 12 ps and an isothermal compressibility of 3 × 10-4 bar-1. Nonbonded interactions were truncated at 1.1 nm, and potential shift functions were applied as recommended in the MARTINI 3 parameter set. The simulation protocol comprised a two-stage equilibration procedure: (i) a 5 ns NVT run to relax initial atomic overlaps, followed by (ii) a 10 ns NPT equilibration to stabilize the system density and pressure. Production simulations were subsequently performed for up to 5 ns to ensure adequate sampling of structural and dynamical properties. Visualization and trajectory post-processing were carried out using OVITO71. cMD (classical all-atom molecular dynamics) simulations

Radial distribution function (RDF) data were obtained from cMD simulations performed via the GROMACS 2020 simulation package. Prior to the cMD simulations, force field parameters compatible with the OPLS-AA framework were derived from geometries optimized via DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-311 + G(d,p) level, as implemented in Gaussian 16. These parameters were subsequently employed to construct solvation models designed to closely reproduce the actual composition of the experimental electrolyte systems. To reproduce the experimental electrolyte concentration of 2 M ZnSO4, two representative systems were constructed. In the ANF-containing system, the simulation box was defined as a cube with dimensions of 7.75 × 7.75 × 7.75 nm3 and comprised 400 Zn2⁺ cations, 400 SO42- anions, 10800 H2O molecules, 8 ANF chains, and 68 PVA chains. In the ANF-free system, the simulation box measured 8.87 × 8.87 × 8.87 nm3 and contained 400 Zn2⁺ cations, 400 SO42- anions, 10800 H2O molecules, and 300 PVA chains. Each system underwent geometry optimization, followed by energy minimization and equilibration under the NPT ensemble. Equilibration was performed for 2 ns at 300 K and 1 bar via a velocity-rescaling (V-rescale) thermostat (τT = 0.1 ps) and a Berendsen barostat (τP = 0.5 ps). Subsequently, production simulations of 5 ns were conducted under NVT conditions at 300 K, regulated by a V-rescale thermostat with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps. Post-simulation analysis of solvation structures was performed via in-house Python scripts. The coordination environments around the Zn2⁺ ions were characterized via radial distribution analysis, with a cut-off distance of 3.0 Å applied to define the first solvation shell.

FEM analyses were employed to investigate the fracture behaviors of hydrogels and the interfacial delamination of fibers. All simulations were conducted using the commercial software ABAQUS (Dassault Systems, France).

Simulation of the fracture behaviors of PVA hydrogel and PVA-ANF hydrogel under tensile loading was simulated using a third-order Ogden hyperelastic model with experimentally fitted coefficients and maximum principal stress criterion. The prescribed maximum principal stresses for PVA hydrogel and PVA-ANF hydrogel were set at 12 MPa and 76 MPa, respectively, based on the maximum stress values achieved in the tensile tests. The bottom of the model was fixed, and a linear tensile displacement was applied to the top. To better simulate crack propagation, the model was carefully cut and encrypted near the predetermined crack location.

The 2D finite element model was constructed to represent the fiber-shaped battery using a rectangular domain of 6 × 5 μm, where two curved lines were defined to represent the electrodes within the domain. In addition, the negative electrode and the positive electrode were represented by two boundaries consisting of a central arc segment with a radius of curvature of 0.25 μm-1 and a central angle of 35°, connected to straight tangential extensions on both sides. To simulate the zinc deposition process, a set of hump structures was introduced on the negative electrode surface to represent the deposited zinc. Besides, other parts in the domain represented electrolyte (ZnSO4).

The 3D model was constructed following a similar procedure to the 2D model. Both the fiber battery and the internal electrodes were composed of arc segments with a radius of curvature of 0.25 μm-1 and their corresponding tangential extensions. The thickness of the fiber battery was set to 0.5 μm. To mimic the practical bending condition, an arc (radius of curvature 0.1818 μm-1) was introduced above the positive electrode to account for the variation in the gap induced by the mechanical bending of the material.

Quantitative simulations of the stress distribution and interfacial dynamic contact evolution during various deformations of FZBs with different interfacial mechanical properties were conducted. The ANF-FZBs, composed of Zn-CNT-ANF, MnO2-CNT-ANF, and PVA-ANF hydrogel, were simulated to investigate their interfacial behavior under three-point bending, stretching and torsion. The Young’s modulus for Zn-CNT-ANF, MnO2-CNT-ANF, and PVA-ANF hydrogel was set to 4372 MPa, 4925 MPa, and 4744 MPa, respectively, based on nanoindentation tests. To investigate the effect of modulus matching on fiber mechanical performance, Zn-CNT-ANF was replaced with a conventional metallic electrode, Zn wire, with a Young’s modulus of 108 GPa, as a comparison case. The interfacial interaction was modeled using a zero-thickness cohesive contact approach, and the decohesion process was defined by a bilinear traction-separation law. Especially, CSDMG is a scalar parameter used to quantify the level of interfacial damage ranging from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates an intact interface with full contact, and 1 corresponds to complete interfacial failure. This parameter provides a straightforward way to monitor the initiation and progression of damage at the interface during loading, allowing a clear assessment of the interface's structural integrity.
