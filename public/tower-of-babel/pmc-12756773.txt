The YAG:Ce Phosphor Glass Prepared by a Low-Temperature Stirring Technique for WLED: Achieving High Luminous Efficiency of the Phosphor
Authors: Tiejun Zhang, Yayuan Liu, Xuanhang Wang, Xin Qiao, Juan Zhang, Jing Wang, Bo Liu, Jiangyan Wang, Bo Li, Zhongzhi Wang, Pengcheng Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fluorescent glass (PiG) has emerged as an important candidate material in the field of white light-emitting diode (WLED) illumination. However, its conventional preparation processes are often complex and time-consuming, resulting in a significant performance degradation during fabrication. In this study, we carefully designed a SiO2–P2O5–Al2O3–Na2O–K2O–BaO glass composition, which was combined with Y3Al5O12:Ce3+ (YAG:Ce) phosphor via a low-temperature stirring process at 750 °C. This approach aims to minimize the thermal and structural damage to the YAG:Ce phosphor, yielding high-quality fluorescent glass (PiG). For comparison, a control sample (S-PiG) was fabricated using the traditional sintering process, in which glass powder and the same amount of YAG:Ce phosphor were sintered in a muffle furnace. X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy analyses revealed that the mild synthesis method produced fluorescent glass with a uniform microstructure and an ideal crystalline phase, both contributing to the enhanced luminescent efficiency and optical performance. Subsequently, both PiG and S-PiG were coupled to blue LED chips to fabricate WLED devices, and their photoluminescent properties were systematically investigated. The results demonstrated that the newly developed PiG exhibits an excellent luminescent performance under blue-light excitation, achieving an internal quantum efficiency as high as 88.03%. Its emission spectrum covers the ideal white-light region, with a luminous efficacy (LE) of 157.43 lm/W, a correlated color temperature of 6097 K, and CIE chromaticity coordinates of (0.3340, 0.3524). These findings indicate that the low-temperature stirring process effectively preserves the luminescent properties of YAG:Ce phosphors within PiG. This not only highlights the importance of this technique in enhancing the performance of YAG:Ce-based fluorescent glass but also provides a novel and practical approach for future WLED lighting applications.

Introduction

Phosphor-converted white light-emitting diodes (WLEDs) have been widely utilized in various fields such as indoor illumination, display backlighting, and automotive lighting, owing to their long operational lifetime, low power consumption, and environmental friendliness. They have gradually replaced halogen and incandescent lamps to become the new generation of lighting technology. − With the continuous progress in lighting technology, the application of white LEDs has evolved beyond general lighting into high-power illumination fields, including projectors, automotive high-beam headlights, stage lighting, laser lighting, and outdoor illumination. These applications impose more stringent requirements on the luminous efficiency, thermal stability, and reliability of the devices, − to meet the demands of high-power plants, which typically suffer from poor thermal stability, yellowing, and degradation under high-temperature and high-power operating conditions. , Among various alternatives, phosphor-in-glass (PiG) composites, composed of thermally robust glass matrices and yellow-emitting YAG:Ce (Y3Al5O12:Ce3+) phosphors, have attracted increasing attention in recent years. This is due to their distinct advantages, including low fabrication cost, simple processing, superior thermal stability, and excellent optical performance. − However, conventional PiG composites are typically fabricated through high-temperature sintering processes. During this process, prolonged exposure of phosphor particles to elevated temperatures can partially destroy their crystalline structure, leading to a reduced luminescence efficiency. Moreover, interfacial reactions may occur between the phosphor and the glass matrix, forming reaction layers that further deteriorate the emission performance. ,− Therefore, developing low-temperature fabrication techniques capable of maintaining both high thermal and chemical stability in YAG:Ce-based phosphor-converted materials is of great significance for advancing high-power white LED technology.

Current research primarily focuses on optimizing the sintering temperature or the integration methods between phosphors and substrate matrices. − Recently, high luminous efficiency and thermal conductivity phosphor-converted materials have been developed using screen printing technology. In this method, a paste consisting of phosphor, organic solvents, and binders is prepared and coated onto a sapphire substrate to produce a phosphor-sapphire composite (Y3Al5O12:Ce3+-PSC). Although this approach enables the fabrication of phosphor-converted materials with high luminous efficiency and thermal conductivity, it also has notable drawbacks, including poor reducibility, lower thermal and chemical stabilities, and reduced optical transmittance. Meanwhile, a series of YAG:Ce3+ phosphor-in-glass (PiG) materials have been fabricated using the glass crystallization method. − Under blue light excitation, these phosphor glasses emit white light with emission spectra almost identical with those of commercial white LEDs. This method yields materials with high optical transmittance; however, it faces challenges in controlling the heat treatment temperature of the glass, which often results in low crystallinity and difficulty in precipitating certain crystalline phases. In contrast, the low-temperature cosintering method is advantageous due to its lower cost, ease of synthesis, and the ability to preserve the phosphor’s crystalline structure at lower temperatures, thus preventing thermal degradation. This enables the fabrication of highly transparent phosphor glasses via cosintering with a glass matrix. However, one of the challenges with this approach is the strong interfacial reaction between the phosphor and the glass matrix during cosintering, which can negatively affect the luminescence efficiency. To overcome this issue, researchers have explored modifying the glass matrix to enhance the luminescent properties of phosphor-in-glass materials. For instance, Zhang et al. developed a transparent Ce:YAG PiG using low-temperature cosintering for white LED lighting applications. The resulting material exhibited excellent thermal and moisture resistance as well as outstanding optical performance, achieving a luminous efficacy of 124 lm/W and a color rendering index (CRI) of 70. Similarly, Guo et al. synthesized a YAG:Ce PiG by cosintering YAG:Ce phosphor with an Al2O3–SiO2–B2O3–ZnO–Na2O–Li2O glass matrix at 650 °C. This method resulted in a high luminous efficacy (92 lm/W) and excellent color rendering, with a CRI reaching 90.3.

In this work, a simple low-temperature stirring technique was employed to disperse YAG:Ce phosphors into a SiO2–P2O5–Al2O3–Na2O–K2O–BaO glass matrix, successfully preparing a series of PiG samples. The results show that the addition of Na+ and K+ lowers the melting point of the glass, and the incorporation of Ba2+ enhances its mechanical strength, while the introduction of P5+ and Al3+ strengthens and tightens the glass network structure. − Furthermore, a detailed comparison of the microstructure, optical properties, and thermal performance of PiG samples with those of S-PiG samples prepared via traditional sintering methods was carried out. The findings indicate that the PiG samples, fabricated using the low-temperature stirring technique, exhibit significant advantages in maintaining the phosphor performance compared with the traditionally sintered samples. Finally, encapsulation tests revealed that the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of the PiG samples reached 88.03, close to that of the original phosphor (93.72), and the luminous efficiency under blue laser excitation at 4.26 W/mm2 reached 222.41 lm/W. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the designed glass matrix and the low-temperature stirring technique used to prepare YAG:Ce-PiG materials exhibit excellent performance, achieving high luminous efficiency for the phosphors.

Experimental Section

Materials and Synthesis

High-purity raw materials, including SiO2 (99.99%), P2O5 (99.99%), Al2O3 (99.99%), BaO (99.99%), Na2O (99.99%), and K2O (99.99%) from Aladdin Reagent Co., were weighed according to a molar ratio of 40:6:25:15:10:4. The components were thoroughly mixed in a ball mill and then dried. The mixture was subsequently subjected to melting in a muffle furnace at 1200 °C for 30 min. The molten glass was quickly quenched in deionized water to form a glass frit. The resulting glass was ground into a fine powder using a ball mill and sieved through a 1000-mesh screen to obtain a uniform glass powder. The glass powder was then sintered at 750 °C for 20 min in the muffle furnace. The molten glass was quickly poured into commercial Y3Al5O12: Ce3+ (YAG:Ce) phosphor powders (purchased from Beijing Nakamura Yuji Technology Co., Ltd.) had mass fractions of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%. The mixture was rapidly stirred and allowed to cool naturally to room temperature at 400 °C. The resulting phosphor-in-glass (PiG) materials were cut and polished into samples with a thickness of 0.5 mm. Optical characterization revealed that the sample containing 40 wt % phosphor exhibited the best optical performance. For comparison, S-PiG samples were prepared by thoroughly mixing the glass powder with 40 wt % phosphor, followed by sintering at 750 °C for 20 min. The mixture was then allowed to naturally cool in the furnace to room temperature. The resulting S-PiG samples were similarly cut and polished to a thickness of 0.5 mm for further characterization and experimental analysis. Measurements and Characterization

The crystal phase structure of all samples was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). The transmittance of the samples in the range of 300–800 nm was measured using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (UV–vis, Hitachi UH4150, Japan). Photoluminescence (PL) excitation (PLE) spectra, PL spectra, IQE, and temperature-dependent spectra were obtained by using an FLS1000 spectrometer equipped with a xenon lamp (Edinburgh Instruments). The size and morphologies of all samples were characterized by a scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU3500, Hitachi, Japan). The physical structure of the samples was examined by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, FEI Tecnai F20). The luminous efficiency (LE), color temperature, and color rendering index (CRI) of the packaged samples were measured using a photoelectric integrated testing system (Hangzhou Yuanfang Optoelectronic Information Co., Ltd., China).

Results and Discussion

Figure a presents photographs of samples prepared using the low-temperature stirring technique, incorporating YAG:Ce phosphor (10–50 wt %) and corresponding glass powder. As the phosphor concentration increases, the transparency of the samples gradually decreases and their appearance changes from light yellow to dark yellow. To explore the crystal structure of the samples, Figure b shows the XRD patterns of the matrix glass powder, Ce:YAG phosphor, S-PiG, and PiG. The XRD pattern of the matrix glass exhibits no distinct diffraction peaks but only a weak amorphous peak. The XRD patterns of PiG and S-PiG, however, reveal well-defined crystalline phases that match the Y3Al5O12 phase (PDF #33-0040), indicating that the phosphor in the glass matrix retains a well-maintained crystalline structure without impurity peaks Notably, the PL intensity of PiG is clearly higher than that of S-PiG, suggesting that the phosphor in the samples prepared using the low-temperature stirring technique maintains a more favorable state, resulting in higher crystallinity. Figure c shows the transmittance spectra of the samples. As the phosphor concentration increases, the transmittance decreases from 68.7% to 30.2%. The transmittance values for the S-PiG and PiG samples are 38.4% and 33.6%, respectively. Figure d displays the PL and PLE spectra for various phosphor concentrations. All samples retain the luminescent properties of the phosphor, and under 455 nm excitation, the fluorescence intensity of Ce:YAG-PiG increases with phosphor concentration, reaching a peak at 40%, before slightly decreasing. This is due to the distance between the two luminescent centers (Ce3+) being smaller than the critical distance, leading to energy migration between the centers and ultimately resulting in luminescence quenching. Figure e shows the PL and PLE spectra for PiG and S-PiG with a 40 wt % phosphor concentration. Clearly, the PiG sample, prepared using the low-temperature stirring technique, exhibits higher fluorescence intensity compared to S-PiG, further confirming that this preparation method better preserves the luminescent properties of the phosphor. Therefore, it can be confirmed that neither intermediate products are formed during the stirring process nor do interfacial reactions occur between the glass matrix and the phosphor.

To evaluate the feasibility of the prepared samples under long-term operational conditions, thermal stability tests were carried out. The temperature-dependent PL spectra of Ce:YAG, S-PiG, and PiG were recorded under 455 nm excitation in the temperature range 298–573 K, as shown in Figure a–c. The results clearly show that the relative PL intensities of all three samples decreased continuously with increasing temperature. At 423 K, the PL intensities of Ce:YAG, S-PiG, and PiG remained above 89.7%, 90.3%, and 92.6% of their initial values at room temperature, respectively. Evidently, PiG exhibits superior thermal stability compared to S-PiG at 423 K, indicating its enhanced ability to retain the luminescent performance of the phosphor. As illustrated in Figure d, the temperature-dependent PL intensity curves for Ce:YAG, S-PiG, and PiG are consistent with the corresponding variable-temperature PL spectra.

Figure a–c presents the temperature-dependent PL spectra of Ce:YAG phosphor, S-PiG, and PiG in the range of 303–573 K under 455 nm blue light excitation. Compared to the Ce:YAG phosphor, the emission peaks of S-PiG and PiG shift slightly toward shorter wavelengths with increasing temperature. This is likely due to the slight crystal field change caused by the thermal expansion of the YAG host lattice, leading to a minor splitting of the 5d energy levels. As shown in Figure d, the CIE color coordinates of Ce:YAG phosphor are (0.3425, 0.3697), while those of S-PiG and PiG are (0.3387, 0.3625) and (0.3340, 0.3524), respectively. To further explore the intrinsic properties of the samples, their quantum efficiencies were measured (Figure e). Under the excitation of 455 nm blue light (LED), the phosphor undergoes 4f → 5d transition. The IQE of the PiG sample reaches 88.03%, which is close to that of the pristine phosphor (93.72%), while the IQE of S-PiG is 84.97%. In addition, the external quantum efficiency (EQE) of PiG is evidently higher than that of S-PiG. These results indicate that the phosphor particles are more uniformly dispersed within the glass matrix in PiG, and the interfacial reactions between the phosphor and glass are much milder compared with those occurring in conventional sintering processes. Figure f shows the fluorescence decay curves of Ce:YAG phosphor, S-PiG, and PiG, monitored at 555 nm under 455 nm excitation, corresponding to the decay of Ce3+ ions. The decay curves of all the samples were well fitted to a single exponential decay function. , The fitted lifetime of Ce:YAG phosphor was 62.47 ns, while those of S-PiG and PiG were 61.66 and 61.12 ns, respectively. The decay times of PiG and S-PiG were within 1% of the lifetime of the Ce:YAG phosphor. The nanosecond-range decay helps in reducing luminescent quenching due to ground-state depletion or nonlinear transitions. This further confirms the excellent thermal stability of the PiG prepared in this study for white light LEDs (WLEDs).

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully designed a SiO2–P2O5–Al2O3–Na2O–K2O–BaO glass composition and employed it to fabricate two types of phosphor-in-glass (PiG) materials via a rapid-stirring process and a conventional sintering process, respectively. Comparative analysis confirms that the PiG prepared via the rapid-stirring route exhibits markedly higher luminous efficiency than the S-PiG obtained through conventional sintering. This improvement originates from the extremely limited corrosion and chemical interaction between the phosphor particles and the glass matrix during the rapid-stirring process, which allows the intrinsic luminescence properties of the phosphor to be better preserved. The optimized PiG, when applied in WLEDs, demonstrates an emission spectrum well covering the desirable white-light region, achieving a luminous efficiency (LE) of 157.43 lm/W, a CCT of 6097 K, and CIE chromaticity coordinates of (0.3340, 0.3524). More importantly, the raw materials for the proposed glass are inexpensive and readily accessible, and the fabrication procedure is straightforward and amenable to large-scale production. Therefore, this work provides a promising and practical pathway toward high-performance WLED illumination.
