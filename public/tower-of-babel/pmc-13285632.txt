Allelopathic effects of three dominant plant litters on seed germination and seedling growth of Elymus nutans
Authors: Zhiyi Chen, Xiuzhi He, Yu Zeng, Zunjian Ke, Zhouwen Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant litter, as a critical link between plants and soil, significantly influences seed germination and seedling growth by releasing chemicals during decomposition. While previous research on allelopathy has predominantly focused on living plants, the allelopathic effects of plant litter remain poorly understood. This study investigated the allelopathic effects of litter from dominant plant species on seed germination and seedling growth in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. We evaluated five concentrations of litter leachate (0%, 2.5%, 5.0%, 7.5%, and 10.0%) and four litter mass gradients (0, 100, 200, and 400 g m⁻2) from three dominant species (Elymus nutans, Kobresia setchwanensis, and Ligularia virgaurea) on the seed germination and seedling growth of E. nutans. Results showed that increasing litter leachate concentration significantly reduced the seed germination rate, germination potential, and germination index, and prolonged the mean germination time. Among the species, L. virgaurea litter exhibited the strongest inhibitory effects. Litter mass exceeding 100 g m⁻2 significantly decreased seedling dry weight, whereas litter mass above 200 g m⁻2 further significantly reduced seedling height and root length. In addition, allelopathic effects varied significantly among litter types and showed concentration-dependent patterns. Overall, these results identify litter-mediated allelopathy as a critical mechanism shaping E. nutans regeneration and inform effective strategies for alpine grassland restoration.

Introduction

Plant litter, as a crucial link between plants and soil, plays a vital role in decomposing soil organic matter, nutrient release, and enhancement of biological activity [6]. As the first physical environment encountered by seeds after dispersal, a protective barrier that offers insulation, shading, and acidification [25]. Furthermore, litter has been shown to reduce the evaporation of soil moisture, thereby influencing seed germination and seedling growth [12]. The organic matter within litter promotes soil microbial activity, alters physical and chemical properties, and enhances nutrient availability. In addition, the decomposition of litter represents a key pathway for material cycling and energy transformation in ecosystems and serves as a major route through which allelochemicals enter the soil [37].

Allelopathy is an important plant competitive strategy whereby releasing allelochemicals into the environment to inhibit or stimulate the growth of neighboring species, thereby regulating interspecific interactions [40]. It represents a key mechanism driving plant community succession and ecosystem dynamics, influencing vegetation regeneration and ecological stability [3]. Allelopathic effects are closely linked to plant growth strategies, as plants adapt to environmental conditions by modulating their morphological [11], physiological, and biochemical traits, or by releasing chemical compounds that alter the growth and development of neighboring plants [26]. Allelochemicals can inhibit radicle and hypocotyl elongation, disrupt subcellular structure, interfere with plant hormone synthesis and metabolism, and disturb the balance of reactive oxygen species, leading to cellular damage and suppressed seed germination [10]. In addition, allelochemicals may impede the mobilization of seed reserves, thereby restricting energy and nutrient conversion during germination and ultimately reducing germination success [28]. They can also affect plant hormone homeostasis, for example, some phenolic compounds stimulate indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) oxidation via indole-3-acetic acid oxidase (IAAO), increasing IAA decarboxylation and reducing its concentration, or inhibiting the interaction between peroxidase and IAA, thereby affecting plant growth and development [17]. Allelopathic effects often exhibit strong concentration dependence. In some cases, allelopathy follows a biphasic pattern characterized by low-concentration stimulation and high-concentration inhibition [30]. In other cases, it exhibits a purely inhibitory effect, with seed germination and seedling growth progressively suppressed as litter quantity increases [13]. For instance, fresh Ageratina adenophora contains allelochemicals that inhibit forage grass seed germination and seedling growth, whereas fermentation degrades these toxic compounds and can instead promote these processes [33]. Moreover, allelopathic intensity may vary among different organs of the same plant. For example, the aqueous root extract of Ligularia virgaurea exerts a stronger inhibitory effect on grasses than its leaf extract, indicating that the root may contain either a higher concentration or more potent and stable allelochemicals [22]. Similarly, Euphorbia jolkinii negatively affects local agriculture and animal husbandry, contributing to grassland degradation and ecological instability within its distribution range [15], and has been shown to inhibit the germination and seedling growth of Medicago sativa, Astragalus sinicus, and Poa annua [27].

Kobresia setchwanensis, Elymus nutans, and L. virgaurea are dominant species in alpine meadows on the eastern edge of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP). These species contribute to increased canopy cover, higher soil organic carbon content, and improved soil structure, thereby playing a crucial role in conserving water and maintaining regional ecological stability [20]. E. nutans, a perennial bunchgrass, is characterized by its high palatability, yield, salt tolerance, and grazing resistance, making it highly adaptable to diverse environmental conditions [21]. Consequently, it is widely used as a key species for the restoration and improvement of degraded grasslands on the QTP [41].

In this study, we investigated the allelopathic effects of litter from three dominant species on the seed germination and seedling growth of E. nutans. Our findings provide critical insights into the regeneration mechanisms of E. nutans and contribute to a broader understanding of alpine grassland. We hypothesized that the allelopathic effects of the three litter types on E. nutans seed germination and seedling establishment would exhibit a dose-dependent response, manifesting either as a monotonically inhibitory effect or as a typical biphasic pattern characterized by low-dose stimulation and high-dose inhibition (Fig. 1). We further expected a threshold within concentration or litter mass gradient, with L. virgaurea litter exerting the strongest inhibitory effect. Materials and methods

Sample collection and extract preparation

Plant litter from three dominant species–E. nutans, K. setchwanensis, and L. virgaurea representing different successional stages, were collected to study allelopathic effects. E. nutans which naturally grows alongside the litter collected species in the alpine meadow, was chosen as the plant to test how the litter affects seed germination and seedling growth. Both litter and seeds were obtained from the QTP research base of Southwest Minzu University in Hongyuan County, Sichuan Province, China. The entire aboveground biomass was harvested at ground level in late October, after the grasses had withered and turned yellow. Collected litter was air dried in the shade indoors, then pulverized. A 100 g of each species dry litter powder was steeped in 1 L of distilled water for 48 h in a constant-temperature oscillator at 25 ± 1 ℃. After thorough mixing and leaching, the mixture was filtered through double-layer sterile gauze to obtain a 10.0% (mass:volume) extract [24]. Lower concentrations of 2.5%, 5.0% and 7.5%, were prepared by serial dilution with distilled water. All leachates were stored in brown glass bottles at 4 ± 0.5 ℃ in the dark. Fresh soil samples were collected from 0 to 20 cm at the research base, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and mixed them evenly for the pot experiment. The soil type is classified as Mat Cry-gelic Cambisols (Chinese Soil Taxonomy 1995).

Seed germination of Elymus nutans

The seed germination experiment was conducted in an artificial light incubator [31]. Healthy seeds were surface-sterilized in 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 30 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water and soaking for 12 h to minimize any microbial influences on germination. Ninety-millimeter diameter petri dishes lined with double-layer filter paper received 5 mL of each litter leachate concentration (2.5%, 5.0%, 7.5%, 10.0%). Distilled water served as the control. Each dish contained 50 uniform, viable seeds with four replicates per treatment. Petri dishes were incubated at 25 °C/30 °C under a 16/8 h photoperiod with a light intensity of 3,500 lx following China national standard protocols (GB/T 3543.4–1995). The petri dishes were sealed to prevent evaporation and an additional 2 mL of distilled water or extract was added as needed. Seeds were monitored daily, and germination (radicle ≥ 2 mm) was recorded. The experiment ended when no new germination occurred for four consecutive days. Germination indices including germination rate, germination potential, germination index, mean germination time, and the allelopathy response index were calculated.

Seedling growth of Elymus nutans

To assess litter effects on seedling growth, a pot experiment was conducted under natural conditions (3485 m above sea level, mean temperature and precipitation are 11 ℃−15 ℃ and 110–150 mm) at the Southwest Minzu University research base. Field soil was uniformly packed into pots of equal dimensions (12 cm diameter × 15 cm depth) to a consistent weight. Four litter mass gradients (0, 100, 200 and 400 g m⁻2) for each species were thoroughly mixed with the topsoil to ensure even incorporation. When the germinated seeds reached a hypocotyl length of 1 cm, five uniform seedlings were transplanted into the pot. Each treatment was replicated four times. Natural precipitation in the study area is generally sufficient to support normal seedling growth, requiring only occasional supplemental water of equivalent volume. Seedlings were grown under natural conditions for 63 days. Seedling height was measured weekly, and root length and biomass were measured at the end of the experiment. Roots were carefully rinsed, shoots and roots separated and all plant parts oven dried at 65 ℃ for 48 h, to determine shoot, root and total dry weights using a precision balance.

Statistical analysis

The germination rate (GR) = (N₁₂d/N₀) × 100%, where N₁₂d is the number of seeds germinated over 12 days, and N₀ is the total number of seeds. Germination potential (GP) = (N₅d/N₀) × 100%, where N₅d is the number of seeds germinated within the first 5 days. Germination index (GI) = Σ (Gd/Dd), where Gd is the number of seeds germinated on day Dd. Mean germination time (MGT) = Σ (Gd × Dd)/Σ Gd, reflects the central tendency of the germination rate of the seed population [19, 32]. Allelopathy response index (RI) = ln (T/C), where T is the measured parameter for the treatment (seed germination or seedling growth parameter), and C is the corresponding control value. Positive RI indicates a promoting effect, while a negative RI indicates an inhibitory effect, with the absolute value reflecting effect strength.

The effects of litter leachate concentration (or mass), litter type, and their interaction on E. nutans seed germination and seedling growth were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance for the model were tested and met before proceeding with the parametric analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using the “lme4” package in R 4.3.0. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2025 and R 4.3.0.

Results

Effects of litter leachate on seed germination of Elymus nutans

Litter leachate concentration, litter type, and their interaction significantly (P < 0.001) affected the germination rate, germination potential, germination index, and mean germination time of E. nutans seeds (Fig. 2). With increasing leachate concentration, the germination rate, germination potential, and germination index decreased significantly, whereas mean germination time increased significantly regardless of the species (Fig. 2). The inhibitory effect of L. virgaurea litter leachate was significantly greater than that of E. nutans and K. setchwanensis (P < 0.001). The magnitude of inhibition varied among litter types: for E. nutans leachate, concentrations of ≥ 7.5% significantly reduced the germination rate and germination index, while germination potential was only significantly affected at 10.0% level. For K. setchwanensis, only the germination index was significantly reduced at concentrations above 7.5%. Discussion

Seed germination and seedling growth are among the most sensitive and vulnerable stages of the plant cycle during which, the allelopathic effects of litter are most pronounced [9]. In this study, germination rate, germination potential, and germination index of E. nutans decreased significantly with increasing leachate concentrations, whereas the mean germination time increased significantly (Fig. 2). These findings are consistent with previous reports showing litter leachate significantly inhibits germination rate, germination potential [10], and vitality index of K. setchwanensis, E. nutans, and L. virgaurea seeds [24]. Such inhibitory effects are likely attributable to allelochemicals present in litter leachates, particularly phenolic acids, which can penetrate the lipid bilayer of cell membranes. This penetration induces the overproduction of reactive oxygen species and triggers lipid peroxidation chain reactions, leading to the loss of membrane integrity and the disruption of ion homeostasis, and the reducing osmoregulatory capacity. Consequently, substance transport and energy supply required for seed germination are impaired [2]. Allelochemicals have also been shown to inhibit key metabolic enzymes, including α-amylase and dehydrogenase, while increasing the accumulation of malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide [29]. Moreover, phenolic acids suppress the activity of enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, thereby weakening seed stress resistance [14]. Among the litter types examined, L. virgaurea exhibited the strongest allelopathic inhibition of seed germination, significantly exceeding the effects of K. setchwanensis and E. nutans (Figs. 2 and 6a). This enhanced inhibition is likely related to the greater diversity and higher concentrations of allelochemicals in the volatile and water-soluble fractions of L. virgaurea litter [23]. In contrast, E. nutans litter leachate significantly inhibited seed germination only at concentrations of ≥ 7.5%, with germination potential further reduced at 10.0%. These effects may be associated with allelochemicals, such as phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic and vanillic acids), terpenes, or alkaloids, which can disrupt gibberellin/abscisic acid signaling balance and delay germination [1]. Similar concentration-dependent inhibitory patterns have been reported for the essential oil of Solanum rostratum on Poa annua germination [42], and for Pinus densiflora litter on Lepidium sativum and Digitaria sanguinalis [16]. Unlike the commonly reported “low-dose stimulation, high-dose inhibition” pattern (hormesis), our results revealed predominantly monotonic inhibitory responses across all tested leachate concentrations. No significant hormetic effects were observed for E. nutans germination (Figs. 2 and 6a), even at the lowest leachate concentration (2.5%), suggesting sensitivity of this species to litter-derived allelochemicals [42]. Similar inhibitory responses have been observed in Robinia pseudoacacia exposed to solutions extracted from the root zone of Ailanthus altissima, a phenomenon potentially linked to plant autotoxicity [4]. In our study, K. setchwanensis litter leachate exerted no significant inhibitory effect on the germination rate or potential of E. nutans seed, even at concentrations as high as 10.0% (Fig. 2a, b). This limited effect suggests that K. setchwanensis litter may be deficient in highly potent allelochemicals, instead containing secondary metabolites with relatively low activity such as common flavonoids or non-toxic terpenes, which may require much higher concentrations to induce an inhibitory response.

Allelochemicals released during litter decomposition also play a critical role in regulating seedling growth [8]. In this study, the allelopathic response indices for seedling height and dry weights (shoot, root, and total), and biomass of E. nutans consistently indicated inhibitory effects across all litter mass gradients (Fig. 6b, d, e, f). Once released into the soil, these compounds allelochemicals can inhibit root meristematic activity and hinder cell elongation, resulting in reduced root biomass and impaired seedling growth [7]. In addition to chemical effects, litter accumulation forms a physical barrier on the soil surface which further restricts seedling emergence and growth [35]. Consistent with previous findings root leachate of L. virgaurea exerts stronger inhibitory effects on Bromus inermis germination than stem and leaf leachates [36]. In our study, L. virgaurea litter caused the most pronounced suppression of E. nutans seedling growth compared to E. nutans and K. setchwanensis litter. This is likely because the litter of E. nutans and K. setchwanensis is characterized by high lignin and low nitrogen content, leading to slower decomposition rates. This, in turn, extends the release period of allelochemicals and limits the decomposition of other organic matter, thereby restricting nutrient exchange and transport in the soil. During the early-stage of litter decomposition, large amounts of phenolic compounds are released, which suppress microbial activity in the rhizosphere and indirectly limit nutrient mineralization [25]. Furthermore, allelochemicals can alter soil physicochemical properties, reduce enzymatic activity, and slow nutrient mineralization, leading to nutrient stress and inhibited seedling growth [34]. Although, low concentrations of decomposition products may promote root growth and enhance stress tolerance in E. nutans [5]. Increasing litter mass intensified allelopathic inhibition, progressively restricting root development [39].

Overall, our study demonstrates that alpine grassland plant litter exhibits broad allelopathic potential, suppressing seed germination and seedling growth in a species-specific and concentration-dependent manner. This litter-mediated allelopathy represents an important ecological mechanism within plant-soil feedbacks, influencing population regeneration and community dynamics. However, because this study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, caution is required when extrapolating the results to natural ecosystems. In the field, allelochemical release and persistence are regulated by variable rainfall, microbial activity and other environmental factors [18], while seed germination and seedling establishment are simultaneously influenced by multiple biotic and abiotic interactions [38]. Moreover, this study did not include chemical characterization of the plant litter derived compounds and the specific allelochemicals responsible for the observed inhibitory effects remain unidentified. Future research should combine chemical profiling with field experiments to identify key secondary metabolites and clarify their ecological roles under realistic environmental conditions.

Conclusions

This study investigated the allelopathic effects of litter from dominant alpine grassland species on E. nutans across the germination-to-establishment continuum, revealing pronounced species-specific responses and distinct concentration and mass-dependent thresholds. Among the species tested, L. virgaurea exerted the strongest inhibitory effects. Leachate concentrations of ≥ 7.5%, significantly altered seed germination rate, germination potential, germination index, and mean germination time. At the seedling stage low litter mass (100 ~ 200 g m⁻2) promoted root growth, high litter mass (400 g m⁻2) significantly suppressed seedling height as well as shoot and root dry weights. Because allelopathic effects are both species-specific and dose dependent, our findings highlight the need for targeted litter management in alpine grassland restoration. Such management should include the selective strategic removal or dilution of strongly inhibitory species (e.g., L. virgaurea) and the adaptive adjustment of reseeding density for target species (e.g., E. nutans) according to local litter composition and loading, thereby optimizing restoration efficiency and establishment success.
