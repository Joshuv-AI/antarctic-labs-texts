Risk factors analysis and predictive model of degree I degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis
Authors: RuiYang Wang, Neng Ru, Qing Liu, Fan Zhang, Yu Wu, ChangJin Guo, Jie Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy designRetrospective Case-Control Study.BackgroundThere have been some previous studies on the risk factors associated with lumbar spondylolisthesis, but there are few studies on the risk factors for disease progression in mild degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS). To analyze the risk factors associated with aggravation of spondylolisthesis in patients with grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis and construct a prediction model.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 220 patients diagnosed with DLS who were admitted to our hospital between January 2019 and January 2023. Data collected included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, hypertension, occupation, and imaging parameters.ResultsA total of 220 patients were included in this study, including 111 males and 109 females; 178 patients with no aggravation of lumbar spondylolisthesis (group A) and 42 patients with aggravation of lumbar spondylolisthesis (group B). Progression of grade I DLS was associated with single factors such as age, BMI, Occupation, vertebral CT value, facet joint angle (FJA), Modic change (MC), Pfirrmann grade of intervertebral disc (PG), Facet joint effusion (FJE), osteophyte formation, and Percentage of the Fat Infiltration (FIA%) of multifidus muscle (MM). BMI, FJA, PG, and FI% of MM had a significant impact on disease progression in lumbar spondylolisthesis.ConclusionBMI, FJA, PG, and FIA% of MM were independent risk factors for the progression of degenerative spondylolisthesis. The risk prediction model was established by including the above four variables and nomograms were drawn. The internal validation proved that the model had good discrimination, calibration, and clinical practicability.Keywords: Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS), Disc degeneration (DD), Facet joint angle (FJA), Multifidus muscle (MM), Percentage of the Fat Infiltration FI%

Introduction

Spondylolisthesis is defined as the anterior or sliding of one vertebral body relative to the next vertebral body, a concept first introduced by Junghanns in 1930 [1]. Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) was proposed by Newman and Stone [2] in 1955. DLS occurs predominantly at the L4/5 level in people over 40 years of age [3]. DLS is one of the most common indications for lumbar spine surgery in the elderly [4–6]. The development of DLS begins with DD, which alters spinal loading dynamics and increases facet joint load [7, 8]. This progression results in segmental lesions, including segmental hypermobility and facet arthropathy. Degeneration over time leads to the development of symptomatic lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) and progressive deformity. Current research on the historical course of degenerative spondylolisthesis is unclear, the natural history is not clearly defined, and despite the increasing frequency of diagnosis, the optimal treatment for low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis remains controversial [9]. Many authors have been proposed hypotheses for various risk factors associated with the pathogenesis of DLS, including female gender, advanced age, increased BMI, sagittal joint orientation, and reduced disc height [10–12]. This retrospective analysis of patients with DLS who were treated at our hospital from January 2019 to January 2023. To investigate the changes in the severity of spondylolisthesis and analyze the risk factors associated with the progression of DLS and construct a risk prediction model. It is hoped that it will be possible to identify whether the disease will worsen early in the development of DLS. To timely give intervention treatment, functional exercise, etc., to avoid the occurrence of surgery. It can guide the clinical prevention and treatment of degree I degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis. To provide more reference value for clinicians in diagnosing and treating DLS.

Subjects and methods

Study subjects

With institutional review board approval, 220 patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis who were diagnosed at the First College of Clinical Medical Science, China Three Gorges University from 2019 to 2023 were included. DLS was graded using the Meyerding classification (Grade 0, no spondylolisthesis; Grade I, spondylolisthesis<25%; Grade II, spondylolisthesis 25–50%; Grade III, spondylolisthesis 50–75%; Grade IV, spondylolisthesis 75–100%) [13]. Patients were divided into spondylolisthesis without recombination and spondylolisthesis with recombination according to previous and recent X-rays. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the People’s Hospital of China Three Gorges University, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study design.

Imaging indicators:

Imaging indicators:

①Cross-sectional area (CSA) of MM, degree of the fat infiltration of MM (spondylolisthesis level disc scans were taken on lumbar MRI using T2WI, patients’ MM cross-sectional area was measured by Image J software (Version 2.0.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to average, MM fat area was measured using threshold technique, degree of the fat infiltration = fat area/ CSA of MM).

Image J steps: (1) Open the Image J software, set the scale to centimeters. Convert measurement units from pixels to centimeters using the ruler in the image, and subsequently convert each image to an 8-bit grayscale image. (2) The region of the target MM was delineated to measure CSA. (3) Measure the degree of muscle fat infiltration using a threshold technique. Select Image, Adjust, Threshold, Dark Background, Default, and Auto in turn. Using the threshold tool in the procedure, the adipose tissue in the 8 images turned red, and the area measurement in the red area was called CSA. (4) FIA% of MM was calculated, and FIA% = fat area/ CSA of MM (Fig. 1).

Measurement of CSA and FIA% of multifidus muscle

②Modic changes (MC) [14]: Grade 0: normal, no abnormality in T1WI and T2WI; Grade A: amplitude of signal intensity change ≤ 25% of vertebral body height; Grade B: amplitude of signal intensity change 25–50% of vertebral body height; Grade C: amplitude of signal intensity change ≥ 50% of vertebral body height.

③Facet joint angle (FJA): CT transverse images at the level of the lumbar intervertebral disc at the slipped level were located to make tangents at the posterior margin of the vertebral body. The facet joint angle is defined as the angle formed between the line bisecting the bilateral facet joint spaces and the tangent line at the posterior edge of the vertebral body. The average value of bilateral facet joint angle is defined as FJA. (Fig. 2A)

FJA, Hu value, FJO and FJE measurement method

④Bone quality (Hu value of vertebral body CT): Locate the mid-vertebral level of L1-5 CT transverse image. Select the region of interest (ROI) in the mid-vertebral level plane, avoid the cortical bone and posterior venous plexus regions. Use the PACS system to automatically measure the mean Hu value within the ROI [15]. (Fig. 2B)

⑤Pfirrmann grading (PG) of lumbar disc degeneration.

⑥Facet joint effusion (FJE): MRI showed unilateral or bilateral high-intensity signal within unilateral or bilateral facet joints. (Fig. 2C)

⑦Facet joint opening (FJO): CT showed unilateral or bilateral facet joint width increase > 2.0 mm. (Fig. 2D)

⑧Osteophyte formation in the upper and lower vertebral bodies.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software. The normal distribution of measurement data was expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (𝒙̅±s), the skewed distribution was expressed as the median and interquartile range, and the enumeration data were expressed as frequency and percentage. Independent sample t-test or rank sum test was used for comparison between groups, and chi-square test was used for comparison of enumeration data. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis was performed for factors that were statistically significant in univariate analysis. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P<0.01 was considered highly significant. Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and significant multivariate factors in binary logistic regression equations were included to establish risk prediction models and construct nomograms. Bootstrap resampling was used to plot Calibration curves and DCA decision curves.

BMI was 16.90–33.32 kg/m2, with a mean of 23.90 ± 3.42 kg/m2. The CT value of vertebral body was 73–205 Hu, with an average of 133.42 ± 29.17 Hu. There were 74 diabetic patients and 94 hypertensive patients. (Table 1)

Risk factors associated with progression of DLS

Univariate analysis of risk factors for progression of DLS

Factors that were highly significant (P < 0.01) in the univariate analysis were included in the binary logistic regression equation. The results showed that higher BMI increased the risk of progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis with statistical significance (OR = 1.294, 95% CI 1.140–1.470, P < 0.001); higher FJA had a higher risk of progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis with statistical significance (OR = 1.232, 95% CI 1.109–1.369, P < 0.001); higher disc degeneration grade had a higher risk of progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis with statistical significance (OR = 1.642, 95% CI 1.169–2.306, P = 0.004); and higher multifidus FIA had a higher risk of progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis with statistical significance (OR = 1.156, 95% CI 1.044–1.280, P = 0.005) (Table 3). A forest plot of binary logistic visualization was drawn according to the above factors (Fig. 3).

Establishment of nomogram prediction model for progression of grade I DLS

The significant factors obtained from the above analysis included “BMI, FJA, PG, and FIA%” were integrated to establish a logistic regression equation: Y = (0.258 × BMI) + (0.209 × FJA) + (0.496 × PG) + (0.145 × FIA%) − 24.737. They were plotted as nomograms using R language and four predictors were assigned accordingly. A vertical line is drawn upwards at the specific score value of the patient predictor, and the score corresponding to the intersection of the line and the Point line segment is the specific score of this variable. By analogy, the scores corresponding to the intersection of the four predictors and the Point line segment are added to the total score of the patient model prediction, and a vertical line is drawn downwards at the point corresponding to the total score found at the Total Points, and the value corresponding to the intersection of this line and the Risk point is the risk of the patient experiencing the outcome event (Fig. 4).

Nomogram for predicting progression of grade I DLS

For example, a patient with grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis who had a BMI of 28 kg/m2, FJA of 56°, disc degeneration grade III, and multifidus muscle FIA% of 35%. Predicting the risk of progression of spondylolisthesis in this patient by nomograms constructed in this study for the risk of progression of spondylolisthesis. BMI 28 kg/m2 corresponds to a nomogram score of 62; FJA 56° corresponds to a nomogram score of 59; disc degeneration grade III corresponds to a nomogram score of 19; and multifidus muscle FIA% 35% corresponds to a nomogram score of 55. Total score 62 + 59 + 19 + 55 = 195 points. A total score of 195 points is predicted to correspond to 65% of the bleeding risk segments in the nomogram. The probability of progression of spondylolisthesis in this spondylolisthesis patient is therefore 65%.

Discrimination evaluation in disease progression of DLS using a risk prediction model

ROC curves were plotted to evaluate the discriminatory power of the model, and the results showed that AUROC = 0.841, 95% CI: 0.773–0.908. The area under the ROC curve is greater than 0.7, indicating that the model has good discrimination. The optimal cut-off point of this prediction model was 0.586, when the sensitivity was 0.738 and the specificity was 0.848 (Fig. 5).

Consistency assessment of predictive models in the progression of DLS

Calibration curves were used to visualize the results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test to evaluate measures of the accuracy of the model in predicting the probability of an outcome event, reflecting the degree of agreement between the model-predicted risk and the actual risk. Bootstrap resampling (1000 resamplings) was used to plot Calibration curves to assess the calibration of this prediction model. The prediction curve in this study is close to the diagonal line, and the H-L goodness of fit test in this study is P = 0.232 > 0.05. The consistency between the probability of predicting the occurrence of outcome events and the probability of the true occurrence of events is good, and the fitting of this model is good (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The incidence of progression of DLS

Spondylolisthesis is usually divided into isthmic and degenerative types. The prevalence of isthmic spondylolisthesis is approximately 11.5%. DLS is predominantly observed in adults and its risk escalates with age, showing a prevalence rate of around 13.6% [16, 17]. Most scholars have shown that female patients are more susceptible to lumbar spondylolisthesis, with the incidence in women being roughly double that in men [17–19]. This study showed that the probability of progression of DLS was 19.09%, with progression rates in male and female patients with spondylolisthesis being 17.12% and 21.10%, respectively. Female patients with spondylolisthesis had a higher probability of progression of spondylolisthesis than male patients. This result is similar to Denard [20] et al. who reported that 12% of men with prevalent spondylolisthesis progressed and that women were more prone to spondylolisthesis than men. Atalay [21] et al. found that as many as one-fifth of people without DLS may develop DLS within 15 years. Moreover, up to 34% of patients with previous DLS will experience DLS progression within 25 years. Women were more likely to have DLS and were more likely to have DS progression. In a prospective study, 12.7% of healthy women developed DLS over an 8-year period [22]. DLS is considered a female predilection for old age, presumably due to female pregnancy, increased ligament laxity, and other hormonal factors.

Risk factors for progression of DLS

Univariate analysis showed that age, BMI, occupation, FJA, PG, FJE, osteophyte formation, and multifidus muscle fat content had some correlation with the progression of DLS. Multivariate regression analysis showed that BMI, FJA, PG, and FI% of MM were independent risk factors for DLS progression. Many researchers consider age as a major risk factor for DLS, and DLS occurs predominantly in people over 50 years of age [23, 24]. In this study, it was found that lumbar degeneration also worsened with increasing age, and patients who had already developed DLS had a certain risk of progressing spondylolisthesis. Different occupations represent different workloads. Retired and unemployed patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis in this study had a higher probability of performing spondylolisthesis. We analyze the factors and consider that such patients have less exercise in daily life than workers and farmers. They do not engage in heavy physical work, and when such spondylolisthesis patients may be reluctant to exercise due to low back pain. This can lead to poor bone quality, severe cases may lead to lower back muscle atrophy, thereby aggravating spondylolisthesis.

As BMI increases, both biomechanical stress and axial load of lumbar facet joints increase [25]. This results in increased shear stress between the upper and lower vertebral bodies, which in the long term may lead to increased intersegmental mobility, trunk lordosis, and increased risk of progression of degenerative spondylolisthesis. Some scholars have found that people with higher BMI are more likely to develop spondylolisthesis, but interestingly, this only involves women, while no similar relationship has been observed in men [12]. However, it has also been shown that greater BMI is a risk factor for lumbar spondylolisthesis in both women and men [26]. Obesity poses a significant challenge for surgery in patients with spondylolisthesis [27]. Intraoperative screw placement and reduction of slippage are more difficult in such patients than in non-obese patients, and obese patients have a higher risk of re-slippage and screw extraction after surgery. Obese patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis should be informed to take preventive measures, lose weight, and control body weight, no matter whether the surgical criteria are met. This can not only avoid or delay the aggravation of spondylolisthesis but also help the rehabilitation of spondylolisthesis after surgical treatment.

The facet joints are key factors in maintaining spinal stability, and lumbar facet joints and intervertebral discs constitute the spinal triarticular complex [28]. When the lumbar spine is subjected to the shear force generated by gravity, there is a tendency for forward slippage, while the lumbar facet joint has the effect of resisting forward shear stress, which is a decisive structure to maintain the stability of the posterior column of the spine. Many studies have now shown some correlation between DLS and lumbar facet joint angle (FJA) [29, 30]. FJA plays an important role in the progression and prediction of DLS. Some scholars have studied that FJA is also of great value in differentiating isthmic spondylolisthesis from DLS [31, 32]. In this study, FJA was the angle between the straight line of the double facet joint space and the tangent line of the posterior vertebral body. We found that the FJA was significantly greater in the progressive lumbar spondylolisthesis group than in the control group. The larger the FJA (i.e., gradual sagittal and horizontal articulation), the more likely it is to lead to the progression of DLS at this time.

The pathogenesis of DLS may be due to excessive mechanical stress at each level between the lumbar spine, leading to the degeneration of the intervertebral disc and facet joints. This degeneration alters joint wear and deformity. Finally, the articular surface gradually sagittal and horizontal, and its ability to resist forward shear stress weakened, resulting in vertebral segmental instability and gradual DLS [33, 34]. It has previously been shown that patients with sagittal facet joint surfaces have an approximately threefold higher risk of progression of DLS than those without sagittal joints [35]. In patients with clinically increased FJA and sagittal lumbar facet joints, physicians should predict and assess the probability of aggravated spondylolisthesis and inform patients to take preventive measures. For example, low back muscle exercise, avoiding lumbar weight bearing, and long-term sedentary, to avoid a variety of factors to accelerate the process of DLS.

In this study, Pfirrmann grading was used to grade the lumbar intervertebral disc, which is an endogenous stabilization system for spinal stability, and disc degeneration is a risk factor for the development of lumbar spondylolisthesis [36]. In this study, logistic regression analysis showed that a higher grade of lumbar disc degeneration was a risk factor for the progression of DLS. When spondylolisthesis occurs in the lumbar spine, the intervertebral disc experiences high shear stress [37]. This leads to faster degeneration of the disc, causing loss of disc height and annulus relaxation [38]. Therefore, disc degeneration and degenerative spondylolisthesis promote each other and form positive feedback, resulting in a vicious cycle.

Fatty infiltration is caused by the aging process leading to decreased skeletal muscle mass as well as replacement of muscle by noncontractile connective tissue [39]. The degree of fatty infiltration is an important index to evaluate the quality of paravertebral muscles [40]. Some previous studies have found that the severity of fatty infiltration in the paraspinal muscles is closely related to low back pain, spinal dysfunction, and disc degeneration [41]. In this study, the MM was selected for the lower back muscles, which are most important for maintaining dynamic stability between the vertebral bodies and account for approximately two-thirds of the overall muscles [42]. It provides greater rigidity than other paravertebral muscles during spinal movement. Decreased multifidus function leads to decreased stability of the lumbar spine [42, 43]. In this study, the degree of MM lipidation was significantly increased in patients who developed progression of DLS relative to those who did not have aggravation of spondylolisthesis. This suggests that patients with approximately severe degrees of paraspinal muscle degeneration are approximately prone to aggravation of spondylolisthesis. Previous studies have suggested that BMI and subcutaneous fat levels may also influence fat infiltration and paravertebral muscle density has been shown to decrease with increasing BMI [44, 45]. Fortin [46] et al. found a relationship between increasing BMI and the presence of fat infiltration in paravertebral muscles. In this study, spondylolisthesis was more likely to worsen in patients with higher BMI and degree of muscle fatty infiltration in the paravertebral muscles. The dual effects of BMI and fatty infiltration of the paraspinal muscles promote each other, thereby further aggravating spondylolisthesis. For such patients, we recommend that appropriate measures should be taken in the absence of spondylolisthesis or mild spondylolisthesis without obvious symptoms. For example, weight loss, moderate low back muscle exercise, delay or avoid the aggravation of lipidation of paravertebral muscles.

Predictive model of degree I degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis

In recent years, many scholars have predicted the occurrence and development of degenerative spondylolisthesis through research, but at present, some scholars have constructed a model to predict the occurrence and progression of degenerative spondylolisthesis. In this study, we used R software to draw nomograms to predict the progression of grade I degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis. The AUROC of the predictive disease progression model for degenerative spondylolisthesis was 0.841, which was greater than 0.7. Therefore, the modeling in this study has good discrimination. The calibration curve for building the model is close to the diagonal dashed line of Ideal. It is proved that this prediction model has a high consistency between the probability of predicting the occurrence of outcome events and the probability of the occurrence of real events. The DCA decision curve is suitable for assessing the clinical value and clinical utility of the model. Based on the DCA decision curve drawn in this study, it can be concluded that the model constructed in this study has good clinical practical value.

Current research on predictive models for lumbar spondylolisthesis is scarce. It is particularly valuable if early detection of patients at risk of progression of mild spondylolisthesis can be achieved in clinical practice. In this study, we used univariate analysis and binary logistic regression equations to screen significant factors and construct a risk prediction model. This is helpful for clinicians to predict in advance whether grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis will progress. Clinicians can calculate BMI, FJA, PG, and multifidus muscle FIA% of patients by reading physical examinations, etc. Identify patients who may have progression of spondylolisthesis early and take appropriate precautions. Patients were instructed to perform appropriate functional exercises and treatments. Delay the progression and aggravation of lumbar spondylolisthesis disease, to avoid surgery as much as possible. The risk prediction model constructed in this study can accurately and efficiently predict the probability of lumbar spondylolisthesis progression and has a certain clinical promotion value.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, this study was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size. Secondly, this study was mainly for patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis and did not include patients with other types of spondylolisthesis, which may lead to some bias in the results. Thirdly, spondylolisthesis in this study was mainly L4/5 and L5/S1, and did not include patients with spondylolisthesis at all levels, which may have some limitations. A prospective study with a large sample size will be performed subsequently to further justify the conclusions of this study.

Conclusion

BMI, FJA, PG, and FIA% of MM were independent risk factors for the progression of degenerative spondylolisthesis. The risk prediction model was established by including the above four variables and nomograms were drawn. The internal validation proved that the model had good discrimination, calibration, and clinical practicability.
