Abelmoschus Manihot flowers inhibit dysbiotic Enterobacteriaceae expansion by promoting Clostridium bolteae-mediated hypoxia restoration
Authors: Chengxi Li, Yumeng Wang, Qinyu Zhang, Huashi Liang, Jianping Li, Shu Zhang, Jinao Duan, Jianming Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory disease that has become prevalent worldwide. Excessive expansion of Enterobacteriaceae is a key feature of dysbiosis in IBD patients, which further exacerbates intestinal inflammation. Therefore, inhibiting the dysbiotic expansion of Enterobacteriaceae is a promising strategy for the treatment of IBD. We investigated the effects of Abelmoschus manihot (A. manihot) flowers on the intestinal microbiota during colitis.ResultsWe found that A. manihot flowers are capable of ameliorating murine colitis and suppressing Enterobacteriaceae expansion, not by direct action but rather by promoting the growth of Lachnospiraceae, particularly Clostridium bolteae. Bacterial depletion and recolonization confirmed that Clostridium bolteae restored colonic hypoxia via PPAR-γ activation, creating an environment unfavorable for Enterobacteriaceae growth and reducing inflammation. Moreover, intestinal hypoxia is vital for Clostridium bolteae colonization and its effect on Enterobacteriaceae expansion, which involves crosstalk between gut microbe colonization and intestinal oxygen homeostasis.ConclusionOverall, our study provides evidence that modulating the gut microbiota to restore intestinal hypoxia is a promising therapeutic strategy for suppressing Enterobacteriaceae proliferation in the inflamed gut and for ameliorating intestinal inflammation, which could be applied to other Enterobacteriaceae-related diseases. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has become a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease worldwide and is characterized by recurring episodes of intestinal inflammation [1]. The progression of IBD is affected by the gut microbiota, which is a crucial factor closely associated with IBD [2, 3].

Several studies have indicated that the excessive expansion of Enterobacteriaceae is a key feature of dysbiosis in IBD patients, and Enterobacteriaceae expansion further exacerbates intestinal inflammation [3–9]. Bacteria belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family, such as Escherichia coli [10] and Klebsiella spp. [4], can induce intestinal inflammation in interleukin-10 (Il-10) gene knockout mice, an immunocompromised and IBD-prone mouse model. Conversely, inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion with tungstate could ameliorate intestinal inflammation in IBD mice [11]. Although tungstate cannot be administered to patients because of its toxicity, this finding identified Enterobacteriaceae as a potential drug target for IBD treatment.

The hypoxic lumen maintains anaerobic members of Firmicutes (such as Lachnospiraceae) as the dominant species, whereas the pathogenic facultative anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae is a minor species [12]. However, intestinal inflammation leads to an increase in oxygen leakage, which spreads to the intestinal cavity and disrupts microbiota homeostasis [13, 14], inducing the excessive expansion of Enterobacteriaceae [15]. A recent report indicated that enhancement of mucosal mitochondrial function by the compound AuPhos could induce luminal oxygen anaerobiosis and attenuate colitis [16]. Therefore, modulating intestinal oxygen homeostasis is an attractive strategy for inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion and counterbalancing microbiota dysbiosis.

Abelmoschus manihot (Linn.) Medicus (A. manihot), a widely distributed plant in Eastern Europe and Asia, has been demonstrated to have high nutritional value for human health [17]. For centuries, A. manihot flowers have been used as a traditional remedy in China for the treatment of inflammation and ulceration [18]. Previous studies revealed that A. manihot flowers exhibited antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [18] and ameliorated colitis by modulating the gut microbiota [19]. However, how A. manihot flowers modulate the gut microbiota to ameliorate colitis remains unknown.

Clostridium bolteae (C. bolteae), also known as Enterocloster bolteae, was first isolated from human fecal samples as a gram-positive obligate anaerobic strain in the Lachnospiraceae family [20]. Recent studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of C. bolteae in various diseases. In osteoarthritis patients, the abundance of C. bolteae decreased, and colonization of C. bolteae alleviated osteoarthritis in mice through an FXR-mediated gut–joint axis [21]. In Winnie mice, C. bolteae could ameliorate intestinal inflammatory responses and endoplasmic reticulum stress [22]. The combined administration of Blautia producta and C. bolteae could prevent the colonization of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), a major cause of hospital-acquired infections [23]. These findings collectively suggest that C. bolteae may serve as a potential anti-inflammatory therapeutic agent.

In this study, we found that the flowers of A. manihot inhibited the expansion of dysbiotic Enterobacteriaceae by promoting C. bolteae growth to restore intestinal oxygen homeostasis, thus alleviating intestinal inflammation. This study presents a potential therapeutic approach for IBD by targeting the gut microbiota and restoring intestinal oxygen homeostasis.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J (6‒8 weeks) mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and MUC2−/− and Il-10−/− mice (6‒8 weeks) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Both male and female mice were used for the experiments. All the mice were raised in a specific-pathogen-free facility. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. The mice were provided ad Libitum chow and drinking water and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at 24 ± 2 ℃ and 40–60% relative humidity. All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the regulations of the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. All mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel.

Animal models

For the dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute IBD model, colitis was induced in mice with 3% DSS (molecular mass 36,000–50,000 Da; MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, California, USA), which was added to their drinking water for 7–8 days. The mice were treated with AME (200 mg/kg and 400 mg/kg) or the vehicle control (water) for 10 days.

For the DSS-induced Il-10−/− acute IBD model, Il-10−/− mice were given drinking water containing vancomycin hydrochloride (VAN, 0.5 g/L; Macklin Biochemical, Shanghai, China) for 1 week before DSS exposure to eliminate Lachnospiraceae. Il-10−/− mice were given 2% DSS in their drinking water for 7 days to induce colitis. The mice received daily oral gavage of C. bolteae (1*108 CFU/200 μL) or the vehicle control (saline) for 10 days.

For the MUC2−/−-induced IBD model, MUC2−/− mice were raised to two months of age to develop chronic colitis. The mice were administered C. bolteae (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL) or the vehicle control (saline) for 28 days (Fig. 9).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining in the colon

A piece of fresh colon tissue wrapped with feces was fixed in Carnoy’s fluid (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for paraffin embedding and sectioning. The probes used were manufactured by Wuhan Seville Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (ENTER 1432-CY5: 5’-CTCTTTGGTCTTGCGACG-3’, Enterobacteriaceae-specific probe; EUB 338-CY3: 5’-GCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3’, universal bacterial probe; Clos bot-CY3: 5’-GTCCGCCACTCAGTCAATTAA-3’, Clostridium bolteae-specific probe, Wuhan, China). After dehydration and digestion of the paraffin-embedded sections, the samples were incubated at 1 μM in hybridization buffer for 16–20 h at 42 °C in a humidified chamber. After incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS and incubated with bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Subsequently, an anti-MUC2 antibody (1:500 dilution, Wuhan Seville Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) was applied to the sections, and the samples were placed flat in a humidified chamber at 4 °C for 16 h. The samples were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody at 25 ℃ for 50 min. Finally, DAPI staining was performed for 10 min, after which the slides were mounted for observation under a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 8 DIVE, LEICA, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany).

Clostridium bolteae culture

For the C. bolteae ATCC BAA-613 culture, C. bolteae was obtained from the Guangdong Microbial Culture Center (GDMCC, Guangdong, China). A single colony was picked from the blood agar plates and activated in brain heart infusion (BHI, Solarbio, Beijing, China) medium for 48 h. The activated C. bolteae was then diluted 1:20 in fresh BHI medium. A 480-μL aliquot of the diluted C. bolteae mixture was added to a sterile 1.5-mL centrifuge tube. For the control group, 20 μL of water was added. AME (20 μL) was added to achieve final concentrations of 0.4, 1, and 4 mg/mL in a 500-μL culture system. The compounds (quercetin, isoquercitrin, hyperoside, and myricetin; Chengdu Biopurify, Chengdu, China) were added at a volume of 20 μL to achieve final concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 200 μM. Each group was cultured at 37 °C anaerobically for the corresponding time.

Hypoxia staining

For the detection of intestinal hypoxia, the mice were treated with 60 mg/kg pimonidazole HCl (Hypoxyprobe, Inc., Burlington, MA, USA) intraperitoneally (i.p.) 1 h prior to euthanasia. Colon samples were fixed in 4% formalin, and paraffin-embedded tissue was probed with mouse anti-pimonidazole monoclonal IgG1 (MAb1 4.3.11.3, Hypoxyprobe, Inc., Burlington, USA), which was completed by Wuhan Seville Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were expressed by mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (SanDiego, CA, USA). P < 0.05 indicates that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Enterobacteriaceae are enriched in IBD mice and IBD patients and aggravate colitis in animal models

DSS-induced acute colitis is a well-established mouse model with similarities to IBD [25]. To characterize the gut microbiota profile after DSS exposure, we monitored fecal microbes using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Compared with control mice, DSS-treated mice presented fewer diverse gut microbiota (Fig. 1A) with a distinct community composition (Fig. 1B). Species composition analysis and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) revealed marked differences in the microbiota composition between the control and DSS-treated groups (Fig. 1C–D). We detected a significant increase in the abundance of γ-Proteobacteria in the DSS-treated group (Fig. 1E–F). Moreover, the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, a main family of γ-Proteobacteria, significantly increased in the DSS group (Fig. 1G), which was confirmed by qPCR analysis (Fig. 1H). The abundance of Escherichia, the main genus of Enterobacteriaceae, also increased in IBD mice (Fig. 1I). This finding is consistent with previous reports, as Enterobacteriaceae expansion is a characteristic of dysbiosis in the gut microbiota of IBD model mice [8].

To further confirm whether Enterobacteriaceae expansion was also present in other IBD models, we utilized Il-10−/− (Il-10 gene knockout mice, an immunocompromised and IBD-prone mouse model [26]) and MUC2−/− mice (MUC2 gene knockout mice, a model with mucus deficiency that spontaneously develops intestinal inflammation [27]) to assess Enterobacteriaceae abundance in these mice. Enterobacteriaceae culture on MacConkey agar plates revealed that Enterobacteriaceae abundance was significantly increased in both Il-10−/− and MUC2−/− mice (Fig. 1K), further confirming that Enterobacteriaceae expansion is characteristic of dysbiosis in intestinal inflammation. We then reanalyzed the composition of the gut microbiota from healthy people and IBD patients in the GMrepo database. There was also an increase in Escherichia abundance in IBD patients (Fig. 1J). Collectively, our analysis indicated that IBD mice and IBD patients manifested microbiota dysbiosis, accompanied by increased Enterobacteriaceae abundance.

To further elucidate the pathogenic role of Enterobacteriaceae, we used a previously isolated Escherichia coli (E. coli) strain EL-8 (which displayed 100% sequence identity with E. coli O157:H7 ATCC 43894) from IBD mouse feces in our laboratory and performed continuous colonization (1 × 109 CFU/mouse) for 10 days in DSS-induced colitis mice to evaluate its effect on colonic inflammation (Fig. S1A).

E. coli EL-8 colonization significantly exacerbated disease severity in IBD model mice compared with the DSS group. E. coli EL-8 colonization increased mortality rates (Fig. S1B) and decreased body weight (Fig. S1C), accompanied by significantly elevated DAI scores (Fig. S1D) during DSS administration.

FISH and IF analysis revealed that E. coli EL-8 promoted the colonic translocation of Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. S1E) and reduced the mucus layer thickness (Fig. S1F). Histopathological analysis revealed that compared with DSS-treated mice, E. coli EL-8-colonized mice presented elevated inflammatory scores; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. S1G). We attributed this discrepancy to survivor bias, as the most severely affected mice died prior to the endpoint, thus attenuating measurable histopathological differences in the remaining mice. Importantly, the observed trend toward increased pathology scores, coupled with the significantly reduced survival, suggested that E. coli EL-8 colonization exacerbated colonic inflammation in IBD mice. Taken together, our findings revealed that Enterobacteriaceae enrichment was a characteristic of IBD in both murine models and human patients and that its excessive proliferation aggravated colitis progression.

AME alleviates intestinal inflammation in acute colitis mice

To elucidate the effects of A. manihot flower ethanol extract (AME) on intestinal inflammation, C57BL/6 J mice were administered 3% DSS for 7 days to establish an acute colitis model [25]. The mice were orally administered water or AME for 10 days during DSS treatment. Compared with the control group, the DSS-treated group presented a shorter colon length, increased fecal water content, significant inflammatory cell infiltration, and a thinner mucus layer. AME considerably ameliorated colon shortening, decreased the fecal water content, alleviated intestinal inflammation, and increased the thickness of the mucus layer (Fig. 2A–G). AME also upregulated the mRNA transcription of the anti-inflammatory cytokine Il-10 and downregulated the transcription of the proinflammatory cytokines Il-1β and Tnf-α (Fig. 2D).

To evaluate whether AME could therapeutically alleviate intestinal inflammation after the onset of colitis, we performed another experiment in which mice were given AME for 10 days after the cessation of DSS treatment [28] (Additional files 2: Fig. S2A). Consistently, AME also significantly ameliorated colonic inflammation compared with that in the DSS group, as reflected by recovered body weight and restored colon length. AME also increased Il-10 mRNA levels, reduced Tnf-α mRNA levels, decreased histological scores, and increased mucus layer thickness (Fig. S2B–G). These results indicated that AME had a protective effect on mice with colitis.

AME inhibits the expansion and translocation of Enterobacteriaceae to ameliorate intestinal inflammation

To observe the effects of AME on the gut microbiota, we subsequently analyzed the bacterial taxonomic composition using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Compared with that in the control group, the gut microbiota composition in the DSS group markedly differed (Fig. S3A, B). AME reversed the dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, particularly by reducing the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae and E. coli (Fig. 3A–C), which was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 3D). Enterobacteriaceae culture on MacConkey agar plates also confirmed the effect of AME on Enterobacteriaceae expansion (Fig. 3E).

Next, we used the general bacterial probe EUB 338 [29] to observe the effect of AME on microbial translocation into colonic tissue. Compared with that in the control group, the number of bacteria that translocated to the colonic tissue increased in the DSS group, whereas AME decreased the number of EUB338-positive cells in the colonic tissue (Fig. 3F), indicating that AME inhibited bacterial translocation to the colon tissue in the mice with colitis.

To analyze whether specific bacteria, such as Enterobacteriaceae, were translocated into colonic tissue, we utilized the Enterobacteriaceae-specific probe ENTER 1432 to trace Enterobacteriaceae in colonic tissue. As shown in Fig. 3G, an increased abundance of translocated Enterobacteriaceae was observed in the colon of DSS-treated mice, whereas AME suppressed the colonic translocation of Enterobacteriaceae. Moreover, AME decreased Enterobacteriaceae translocation to extracolonic tissues such as mesenteric lymph nodes and the spleen (Fig. 3H, I). These results suggested that AME could inhibit the expansion of Enterobacteriaceae in the inflamed gut and probably could alleviate intestinal inflammation by inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion and translocation.

To elucidate whether AME ameliorates intestinal inflammation by inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion, we used vancomycin to induce excessive Enterobacteriaceae proliferation [30] (Fig. S4A). After vancomycin exposure, accompanied by the abnormal proliferation of Enterobacteriaceae, AME neither decreased Enterobacteriaceae abundance nor alleviated intestinal inflammation (Fig. S4B–H). AME failed to promote Il-10 mRNA expression, could not decrease Tnf-α mRNA levels, could not reduce mesenteric lymph node weight, and could not restore colon length or mucus layer thickness (Fig. S4C–H).

These findings suggested that when AME could not inhibit excessive expansion of Enterobacteriaceae, AME lost its effect on colonic inflammation, indicating that overproliferation of Enterobacteriaceae attenuated the ameliorative effects of AME on colitis. Collectively, these findings suggest that the therapeutic efficacy of AME in colitis is associated with the suppression of Enterobacteriaceae proliferation.

To investigate the safety of AME, we administered AME via oral gavage to healthy mice for 10 or 20 days to examine its effects on the systemic metabolic indicators and the gut microbiota. We did not observe any negative effects of AME on the mouse host and gut microbiota in healthy mice (Fig. S5A–K and Fig. S6 A–H).

AME inhibits Enterobacteriaceae expansion by restoring intestinal hypoxia

To investigate the mechanism by which AME inhibits Enterobacteriaceae expansion, we cocultured AME with the Enterobacteriaceae model bacterium E. coli in vitro. AME did not influence the growth of E. coli under aerobic or anaerobic cultivation (Fig. S7A).

During intestinal inflammation, the host restricts the expansion of pathogenic bacteria such as Enterobacteriaceae by limiting their uptake of iron [31–34]. Our results revealed that the fecal iron concentration of IBD model mice was significantly reduced, confirming that intestinal inflammation resulted in iron depletion in the colonic lumen (Fig. S7B). To mimic intestinal inflammation and the iron deficiency environment, we utilized the iron chelator 2,2'-dipyridyl (DL) [31] to deplete iron and investigated the effect of AME on E. coli growth. AME did not exhibit inhibitory effects on E. coli under iron-depleted conditions (Fig. S7C).

As AME could not directly inhibit Enterobacteriaceae growth, we investigated other mechanisms that influence Enterobacteriaceae growth. Oxygen leakage into the gut lumen led to disruption of the anaerobic microenvironment within the intestine, leading to excessive growth of facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Enterobacteriaceae [14, 15, 35]. These findings indicate that oxygen also serves as a crucial factor for regulating Enterobacteriaceae expansion. Therefore, we next investigated the impact of AME on intestinal hypoxia. Using the hypoxia probe pimonidazole [12], we found that, compared with control mice, DSS-treated mice presented increased oxygen leakage. Intriguingly, AME significantly restored the anaerobic environment in the lumen of mice with colitis (Fig. 4B).

Colonic cells obtain energy through the β-oxidation of microbiota-derived butyrate, which requires a considerable amount of oxygen, leading to intestinal hypoxia. However, during colitis, the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae is reduced, resulting in insufficient availability of butyrate for β-oxidation. Consequently, the energy metabolism of colonic cells is switched to anaerobic glycolysis. Anaerobic glycolysis does not consume oxygen, and as a result, oxygen diffuses into the intestinal lumen, exacerbating oxygen leakage [35]. Consistent with this metabolic reprogramming, compared with control mice, DSS-treated mice presented increased lactate levels and decreased ATP levels in the colon (Fig. 4C), along with significantly decreased expression of mitochondrial-related genes (Ndufs1, Ndufv1, Uqcr, and Atp5g1; Fig. S8A–D). AME decreased the lactate concentration, restored ATP levels, and increased the expression of these mitochondrial genes in the mice with colitis (Fig. 4C and Fig. S8A–D). This finding further supported the notion that AME could restore the anaerobic environment in the lumen of mice with colitis.

Mice treated with DSS exhibited increased oxygen leakage due to the lack of epithelial PPAR-γ [35], suggesting that inhibiting PPAR-γ expression might increase oxygen leakage in the lumen. To observe whether the inhibition of Enterobacteriaceae growth by AME was related to the restoration of intestinal hypoxia, colitis model mice were intraperitoneally injected with T0070907 [36] (a PPAR-γ antagonist). Inhibiting PPAR-γ signaling with T0070907 increased the oxygen concentration in the intestinal lumen (Fig. 4D–E). T0070907 diminished the effect of AME on luminal hypoxia, as well as the effect of AME on Enterobacteriaceae expansion (Fig. 4E–G). These findings suggested that AME inhibited Enterobacteriaceae growth by restoring intestinal hypoxia.

We further analyzed the effect of AME on intestinal inflammation after the loss of its effect on intestinal hypoxia. Compared with those in the AME group, the mice in the T0070907 group presented significant body weight loss and higher DAI scores (Fig. S9A–B). Compared with the AME group, the AME + T0070907 group presented increased fecal water content and shorter colon lengths, which were accompanied by a thinner mucus layer and more severe inflammatory infiltration (Fig. S9C–F). These results indicate that AME also relies on intestinal hypoxia to alleviate colonic inflammation.

AME promotes C. bolteae growth to restore intestinal hypoxia and limit Enterobacteriaceae expansion

To investigate how AME restored intestinal hypoxia, we reanalyzed the 16S rRNA gene sequencing data. Compared with that in the control group, the abundance of Lachnospiraceae was significantly lower in the DSS group (Fig. 5A). Both LEfSe and heatmap analyses revealed (Fig. 5B–D) that AME treatment could significantly restore Lachnospiraceae abundance in the feces of IBD model mice, which was further validated by qPCR analysis (Fig. 5E).

As Lachnospiraceae has been reported to play a crucial protective role in intestinal inflammation [37, 38], we hypothesized that AME restored intestinal hypoxia, probably by promoting Lachnospiraceae growth. To validate the above hypothesis, we used vancomycin to eliminate Lachnospiraceae and to observe the effects of AME on intestinal hypoxia (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, in the absence of Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 5G), AME failed to restore the anaerobic environment in the intestinal lumen (Fig. 5H). Moreover, AME was unable to restore the mRNA expression of PPAR-γ or regulate the levels of lactate and ATP (Fig. 5I–K).

Vancomycin targeted gram-positive bacteria and decreased the number of putative protective Clostridiaceae and Lachnospiraceae [39]. In addition to its effects on Lachnospiraceae, vancomycin depletes several bacterial taxa across the Actinobacteria and Firmicutes phyla, including Bifidobacteriaceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Staphylococcus [40, 41]. These data implied that vancomycin-sensitive bacteria were required for AME to restore intestinal hypoxia, with Lachnospiraceae being one possible contributor.

To further validate the effect of Lachnospiraceae on intestinal hypoxia, we initially attempted to isolate Lachnospiraceae species from fecal samples of mice. However, despite multiple attempts, we encountered persistent challenges in successfully isolating Lachnospiraceae species. Given these technical difficulties, we adopted an alternative approach by investigating commercially available Lachnospiraceae strains. Through qPCR detection of currently obtainable strains, we found that only four Lachnospiraceae strains were detectable in our animal model. Notably, AME increased the abundance of only C. bolteae but did not influence other bacteria of the Lachnospiraceae family (Fig. 6A). Based on this finding, we colonized C. bolteae after Lachnospiraceae depletion by vancomycin. C. bolteae colonization significantly increased Lachnospiraceae abundance, upregulated PPAR-γ transcription, and restored intestinal hypoxia (Fig. 6B–E). In addition, C. bolteae restored the effects of AME on Enterobacteriaceae expansion (Fig. 6F) and translocation to mesenteric lymph nodes, the spleen and the colon (Fig. 6F–H). Notably, the colonization of C. bolteae restored the ability of AME to ameliorate intestinal inflammation (Fig. 6I–N).

These data suggested that AME promoted the growth of C. bolteae to restore intestinal hypoxia, ultimately alleviating colitis and demonstrating that C. bolteae plays a crucial role in mitigating colitis. Furthermore, these results indicate that the protective effect of AME against colitis is likely mediated by the promotion of C. bolteae proliferation.

C. bolteae activates PPAR-γ to restore intestinal hypoxia, inhibit Enterobacteriaceae expansion, and alleviate intestinal inflammation

We further evaluated the effects of C. bolteae on intestinal hypoxia and intestinal inflammation in DSS-induced colitis mice (Fig. 7A). C. bolteae successively colonized the colon (Fig. 7F) and considerably restored intestinal hypoxia (Fig. 7B), promoted the mRNA expression of PPAR-γ (Fig. 7G), reduced lactic acid levels, and restored ATP levels (Fig. 7C). C. bolteae also inhibited the expansion and translocation of Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. 7D, E) and alleviated intestinal inflammation (Fig. 7H-K). These results indicated that C. bolteae could restore intestinal hypoxia, inhibit Enterobacteriaceae expansion, and alleviate colitis.

We subsequently evaluated the effects of C. bolteae alone and the AME + C. bolteae combination in DSS-induced IBD mice. Our data demonstrated that coadministration of AME + C. bolteae resulted in a 2.5-fold increase in C. bolteae abundance compared with C. bolteae treatment alone (Fig. 7F), suggesting that AME enhanced C. bolteae colonization in vivo. Further analysis revealed that while both treatments demonstrated comparable efficacy in ameliorating colonic inflammation (Fig. 7G–K), the AME + C. bolteae combination exhibited superior effectiveness in suppressing Enterobacteriaceae translocation (to mesenteric lymph nodes and the spleen, Fig. 7D) compared to C. bolteae monotherapy. These results confirm that AME improves colitis by promoting C. bolteae proliferation.

Intestinal hypoxia is vital for C. bolteae colonization and its effect on Enterobacteriaceae expansion

To gain insight into the relationship between the effects of C. bolteae on Enterobacteriaceae expansion and intestinal hypoxia, we used the PPAR-γ antagonist T0070907 to disturb lumen oxygen homeostasis and observed the effects of C. bolteae on Enterobacteriaceae proliferation (Fig. 8A). C. bolteae could not colonize and failed to restore the abundance of Lachnospiraceae when the oxygen concentration in the colonic lumen was increased by T0070907 (Fig. 8B–E). In particular, C. bolteae lost its effect on Enterobacteriaceae growth and failed to prevent the translocation of Enterobacteriaceae to the mesenteric lymph nodes, spleen, and colon (Fig. 8F, G). Moreover, C. bolteae did not alleviate symptoms of colitis (Fig. 8H–K). These results indicated that, as an obligate anaerobic bacterium, C. bolteae required lumen hypoxia for colonization to inhibit Enterobacteriaceae growth and alleviate intestinal inflammation.

C. bolteae requires successful intestinal colonization to exert its anticolitic effects

Il-10−/− mice that spontaneously developed intestinal inflammation were used to further observe the effects of C. bolteae on Enterobacteriaceae expansion and intestinal inflammation. However, C. bolteae was unable to suppress Enterobacteriaceae expansion in Il-10−/− mice (Fig. 9A). Moreover, FISH and qPCR experiments indicated that C. bolteae failed to colonize in Il-10−/− mice and that C. bolteae did not increase the abundance of Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 9B–D). Consequently, C. bolteae lost its ability to alleviate colonic inflammation in Il-10−/− mice (Fig. 9E–I). Thus, C. bolteae could not colonize in Il-10−/− mice and consequently lost its ability to suppress Enterobacteriaceae expansion and improve colitis.

The intestinal mucus layer serves as the primary defensive barrier. Mice with impaired mucus production, such as MUC2−/− mice, can develop spontaneous colitis [42–44]. To investigate the effects of C. bolteae on various IBD model mice, we utilized MUC2−/− mice to observe the effects of C. bolteae on Enterobacteriaceae expansion and intestinal inflammation. Consistent with the findings in Il-10−/− mice, C. bolteae could not colonize in MUC2−/− mice (Fig. 9J). Furthermore, C. bolteae could not inhibit Enterobacteriaceae expansion or translocation in MUC2−/− mice (Fig. 9K–M) and could not alleviate intestinal inflammation (Fig. 9N–Q). These results further confirmed that C. bolteae required colonization to exert its effects on intestinal inflammation.

Quercetin in AME promoted C. bolteae growth by increasing the expression of microbial genes involved in cell growth and energy metabolism

Finally, to investigate whether natural compounds promote C. bolteae growth, we cocultured AME with C. bolteae in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth in vitro. AME exhibited a dose-dependent growth-promoting effect on C. bolteae (Fig. 10A–C). Flavonoid compounds such as isoquercitrin, hyperoside, rutin, quercetin, and myricetin, and organic acids such as gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, and caffeic acid have been reported in AME [18]. Among these compounds, only quercetin promoted C. bolteae growth in BHI broth (Fig. 10D–F, Fig. S10A–G).

To evaluate the influence of different nutritional environments on AME-mediated growth promotion of C. bolteae, we assessed the effects of AME and QUE on C. bolteae growth in two other cultural media: chopped meat carbohydrate broth (CMC) and Gifu anaerobic medium (GAM). AME significantly increased C. bolteae growth in the two media (Fig. S11A), whereas QUE promoted C. bolteae growth in CMC but not in GAM (Fig. S11B). Notably, although C. bolteae presented the maximal growth rate in GAM, both AME and QUE presented the lowest growth enhancement in this nutrient-rich environment. These results suggest that when nutrients are highly abundant, C. bolteae can grow sufficiently well, making the promoting effects of AME and QUE less significant.

To further simulate the iron-limited intestinal nutrient environment, we supplemented the media with 100 μM iron chelator 2,2'-dipyridyl (DL). AME and QUE could still significantly increase C. bolteae growth (Fig. S11C, D) under iron-restricted conditions (in both BHI and CMC broths). These results demonstrated that both AME and QUE had growth-promoting effects on C. bolteae.

Next, we used RNA sequencing of quercetin-treated C. bolteae to investigate changes in microbial gene expression. The quality and quantity of the sequencing data demonstrated the accuracy and reproducibility of our RNA-seq results (Additional file 1: Table S4). Principal component analysis revealed significant differences between the control and quercetin groups, indicating global changes in C. bolteae function (Fig. 10G). We identified 143 upregulated genes and 89 downregulated genes in the quercetin-treated group (Fig. 10H).

GO and KEGG analyses were performed to predict the biological functions of the differentially expressed genes. GO enrichment analysis revealed that amino acid-related metabolic processes, particularly glycine metabolic processes and serine family amino acid metabolic processes, were significantly enriched following quercetin treatment. KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted pathways involved in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, propanoate metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, indicating that quercetin treatment altered the metabolism of C. bolteae (Fig. 10I).

Heatmap analysis combined with KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that genes associated with biotin metabolism, glycerophosphate metabolism, quorum sensing, and biofilm formation were significantly upregulated after quercetin treatment. In contrast, genes related to amino acid metabolism were significantly downregulated (Fig. 10J). These results indicated that quercetin altered the energy metabolism processes within C. bolteae, and this alteration might facilitate the growth of C. bolteae.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that microbiota dysbiosis is a significant factor in exacerbating IBD progression [45, 46], particularly an abnormal expansion in Enterobacteriaceae [7]. Currently, antibiotic therapy, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), phage therapy, and dietary interventions are well-established approaches for managing Enterobacteriaceae overgrowth.

Antibiotics can rapidly suppress Enterobacteriaceae proliferation, effectively control infection progression, and play a critical role in treating severe infections [47, 48]. Owing to its high specificity, phage therapy precisely targets particular pathogens (such as Escherichia coli O157:H7 and Klebsiella pneumoniae), eliminating pathogenic bacteria while preserving gut microbiota homeostasis [3, 49, 50]. FMT not only effectively inhibits Enterobacteriaceae overgrowth but also reduces antibiotic dependence and demonstrates unique efficacy against drug-resistant infections [51–54]. Dietary interventions modulate bacterial growth through nutritional regulation, simultaneously suppressing Enterobacteriaceae expansion and promoting beneficial microbiota colonization [55–58].

Our study demonstrates that C. bolteae significantly suppresses Enterobacteriaceae overgrowth by restoring intestinal hypoxia. Given the clinical value of existing treatments, our study could complement existing approaches and expand therapeutic strategies to control Enterobacteriaceae expansion. C. bolteae administration and quercetin supplementation may synergistically improve the management of Enterobacteriaceae expansion by maintaining colonic hypoxia.

As C. bolteae is present in the human intestine, it has considerable clinical translational value for in-depth functional research. Although C. bolteae has been known for more than 20 years, relatively few reports on its function exist. In this study, we found that C. bolteae not only inhibited Enterobacteriaceae expansion but also suppressed Enterobacteriaceae translocation into the tissues, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation. This highlights the importance of the gut microbiota in inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion and ameliorating IBD, which provides a potential strategy for inhibiting Enterobacteriaceae expansion to alleviate IBD.

The “oxygen hypothesis” was proposed in 2013 [59], which suggested that gut oxygen leakage is an important reason for the transition of gut microbes from absolute anaerobic to facultative anaerobic during IBD, thus exacerbating intestinal inflammation. Under normal physiological circumstances, colonocytes obtain energy through β-oxidation of microbiota-derived butyrate, which consumes a considerable amount of oxygen, rendering the lumen hypoxic [35]. Our research revealed a novel mechanism by which C. bolteae treats colitis. C. bolteae promoted PPAR-γ mRNA expression to restore intestinal hypoxia, thus inhibiting the growth of Enterobacteriaceae and ultimately alleviating colonic inflammation. Our study provides experimental evidence for the role of C. bolteae in regulating intestinal immune homeostasis.

Our results demonstrated that A. manihot flowers could directly facilitate the colonization of C. bolteae in vivo. Under a disturbed intestinal microenvironment, ensuring stable colonization of bacteria in the intestine is challenging. For example, the colonization of Faecalibaculum rodentium was influenced by the composition of the colonic mucus. During colonic tumorigenesis, epithelial cell transformation can influence mucin production and modification, thus hindering Fecalibaculum rodentium colonization [60]. This finding suggested that successful colonization of gut bacteria through direct supplementation might not always be achievable and that the colonic environment should be considered an important factor that influences this process. Therefore, compared with direct supplementation with probiotics, strategies to maintain the colonic anaerobic microenvironment and therefore promote the colonization of beneficial bacteria (especially obligate anaerobic bacteria) may offer a more distinctive and practical approach.

This study has several limitations. Owing to the current lack of specific pharmacological agents or technical approaches that target Lachnospiraceae elimination, we could infer only the potential involvement of Lachnospiraceae in A. manihot flower-mediated suppression of Enterobacteriaceae expansion through vancomycin depletion experiments. We cannot rule out that other microbes may also play a role in the effect of A. manihot flowers, which needs to be investigated in the future. Second, in two spontaneous IBD mouse models (MUC2−/− and Il-10−/− mice), we found that C. bolteae was unable to colonize and consequently lost its ability to suppress Enterobacteriaceae expansion. Therefore, successful colonization is vital for C. bolteae to exert its biological effects, which may limit its therapeutic applicability. Third, although recent studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of C. bolteae in various diseases, it is potentially pathogenic depending on the context. For example, it was reported that C. bolteae abundance was positively correlated with autism spectrum disorder [61]. Therefore, C. bolteae may serve as a potential anti-inflammatory therapeutic agent but should be used cautiously, especially for diseases beyond intestinal inflammation. Furthermore, although in vitro experiments revealed that quercetin significantly promoted C. bolteae growth, this effect remains to be validated in vivo. Notably, although previous studies have reported the colitis-alleviating effects of quercetin [62, 63], it remains unclear whether this flavonoid exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through hypoxia modulation via C. bolteae proliferation. This mechanism requires further study.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that A. manihot flowers do not directly inhibit Enterobacteriaceae but instead promote the growth of Lachnospiraceae, particularly C. bolteae, which restores intestinal hypoxia through the upregulation of colonic PPAR-γ expression, creating an environment unfavorable for Enterobacteriaceae and reducing inflammation. This study highlights a potential therapeutic strategy for IBD by modulating the gut microbiota to restore intestinal oxygen homeostasis and control inflammation, which could be applied in other Enterobacteriaceae-related diseases, such as irritable bowel syndrome [64] and antibiotic-associated diarrhea [65].
