SALAD: Self-Adaptive Link Adaptation
Reinhard Wiesmayr*, Lorenzo Maggi*, Sebastian Cammerer, Jakob Hoydis, Fayçal Aït Aoudia, Alexander Keller

Abstract
Adapting the modulation and coding scheme (MCS) to the wireless link quality is critical for maximizing spectral efficiency while ensuring reliability. We propose SALAD (self-adaptive link adaptation), an algorithm that exclusively leverages ACK/NACK feedback to reliably track the evolution of the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), achieving high spectral efficiency while keeping the long-term block error rate (BLER) near a desired target. SALAD infers the SINR by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between received ACK/NACKs and predicted BLER values, with a learning rate that self-adapts online through knowledge distillation. Based on this inference, SALAD selects the MCS via hypothesis testing: if the SINR is likely underestimated, a higher MCS is selected to accelerate link adaptation under improving channel conditions. To prevent BLER drift from its long-term target, SALAD incorporates a feedback control loop that adjusts the instantaneous BLER target. Over-the-air experiments on a 5G testbed demonstrate that SALAD consistently outperforms the industry-standard outer-loop link adaptation (OLLA). With a single set of parameters, SALAD achieves up to 15% higher throughput and spectral efficiency than multiple OLLA variants across different traffic regimes, while meeting the BLER target.



I
Introduction

Adapting the modulation and coding scheme (MCS) to varying wireless channel conditions, known as link adaptation (LA), is essential for achieving high spectral efficiency (SE). Higher SE translates into increased user throughput, lower latency, and reduced transmission energy thanks to shorter transmission durations and fewer retransmissions.
An effective LA strategy must also guarantee a sufficiently low block error rate (BLER), as excessive re-transmissions waste resources, increase latency, and degrade performance. In fact, transport protocols such as TCP misinterpret packet losses caused by link-level errors as signs of network congestion, resulting in an unwarranted reduction in transmission rate
[
1
]
.
For these reasons, a target BLER value is typically defined to strike a balance between throughput and reliability. Empirical evidence and theoretical analyses
[
2
]
support a fixed target BLER value of approximately
10\%
, although adapting the target dynamically based on the current signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) can improve throughput
[
3
]
.

LA techniques can be broadly categorized into sampling-based and model-based approaches.
Sampling-based methods, such as auto rate fallback (ARF)
[
4
]
and sample rate
[
5
]
, adapt the MCS by trial-and-error, solely relying on statistics on transmission successes (ACK) and failures (NACK) for each available configuration, received via HARQ (hybrid automatic repeat request) feedback.
Due to their slow convergence, these methods are mainly suitable for scenarios with few MCS configurations and relatively static channels, as in Wi-Fi.

In contrast, model-based methods leverage statistical models of the wireless channel and side-information, such as SINR estimates, to make more informed decisions.
A prominent example is outer-loop link adaptation (OLLA)
[
6
]
, the current industry-standard for cellular networks.
OLLA selects the MCS that meets a target BLER according to pre-computed BLER tables and the latest SINR estimation. The latter is adjusted by an offset which is updated according to a fixed stepsize rule based on the received ACK/NACKs.

Despite its simplicity, OLLA is grounded in stochastic approximation theory (see Section
II
) and can provably drive the long-term BLER toward the desired target.
Although OLLA’s fixed stepsize works reasonably well across different mobility scenarios, it excels in none. A small stepsize results in slow adaptation to rapidly changing channel conditions, which is particularly detrimental for short-lived connections
[
7
]
.
Conversely, a large stepsize can cause instability in static regimes, leading to SE loss and/or short-term BLER degradation.
Our contribution aims at combining the best of both worlds: the stepsize for SINR updates increases as the channel changes more rapidly.

SINR estimation is a notoriously difficult challenge, especially for the base station (BS) in the downlink (DL), which is our main focus.
Considering 5G NR with time-division multiplexing (TDD), channel reciprocity allows the BS to estimate the useful signal strength via sounding reference signals (SRS), although interference remains unobservable. An alternative common approach is to let the user equipment (UE) estimate the SINR based on channel state information reference signals (CSI-RS) and report it to the BS using the channel quality indicator (CQI). Conversely, in frequency-division multiplexing (FDD), the only option is to rely on CQI reports
[
8
]
.
Yet, CQI reports present several well-known limitations. First, their granularity is coarse, as the CQI is an integer ranging from
0
to
15
. Second, the reports can be infrequent, with periodicity configurable from a few to several hundreds of slots. Moreover, even when frequent, CQI reports are still delayed in TDD systems, as they can only be transmitted during uplink slots. Finally, reports are wide-band, while transmission often occurs over a narrow band.
Motivated by this, we reduce the reliance on CQI reports by introducing a principled method to infer the SINR using only ACK/NACK feedback.

I-B

Our contribution

We propose self-adaptive link adaptation (SALAD), a novel algorithm that estimates the SINR by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the observed ACK/NACK feedback and the BLER via gradient descent, as described in Section
III-A
.
Its learning rate can be adjusted in an online fashion by a mechanism inspired by knowledge distillation, which compares the gradient-based estimate to a more powerful batch estimator.
When the SINR is deemed to be underestimated—based on a hypothesis testing—SALAD increases the
instantaneous
BLER target to probe a higher MCS value and corrects the SINR estimation accordingly (Section
III-B
).
To meet a desired
long-term
BLER, SALAD adjusts the instantaneous BLER target via a feedback loop (Section
III-C
).

We demonstrate that SALAD outperforms OLLA in system-level simulations and over-the-air experiments conducted within a controlled setup comprising a rotational absorber table between a commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) open-RAN radio unit (O-RU) and a COTS user equipment (UE) in an anechoic chamber.
In Section

15

Finally, parameter tuning allows SALAD to adapt to extreme throughput scenarios, yielding up to an additional

Related work

In the context of cellular networks, OLLA was first proposed for link adaptation in the early 2000s
[
10
,
6
]
, although its mechanism and theoretical justification via Markov chain arguments dates back to the 1990s, initially applied to power control

Several heuristic extensions of OLLA have since been proposed. These include stepsize adaptation
[
12
,
13
]
, offset dynamics optimization via reinforcement learning (RL)
[
14
]
, initialization strategies of the offset based on prior connection statistics
[
15

, parameter tuning through deep learning
[
16
]
, enhanced feedback mechanisms for ultra-reliable connections
[
17

, and customization for emerging applications such as extended reality
[
18

.
Alternative link adaptation strategies with firm theoretical foundations formulate the problem as a multi-armed bandit (MAB), where each MCS corresponds to an “arm”
[
19
,
20
]
. However, these methods often exhibit slow convergence, similar to other sampling-based methods originally designed for Wi-Fi, such as ARF
[
4
]
and sample rate
[
5
]
.
To address this limitation,
[
21
]
formulates a contextual MAB framework that jointly learns the BLER for each of the MCS configuration given the current network context.
In
[
22
]
, a Bayesian approach is used to learn the BLER for each MCS, with Thompson sampling guiding the MCS selection process.
Subsequently,
[
23
]
proposes latent Thompson sampling to infer the underlying SINR. However, such approaches are not designed to accommodate rapid SINR variations and do not explicitly target a predefined BLER.
A supervised learning framework is introduced in
[
24
]
to directly predict the BLER.
A practical approach that combines deep learning for BLER prediction and a calibration method to compensate for prediction errors is studied in
[
25
]
. The work in
[
26
]
employs offline RL approach via decision transformers under the assumption of persistent CQI reporting.

II-A

OLLA through the lens of stochastic approximation

To build a solid foundation for our algorithm, it is instructive to reinterpret OLLA within the context of stochastic approximation (SA).
Consider an unknown non-decreasing function
f
and a constant target
\tau
, and assume that the equation
f(y)=\tau
has a root at
y=y^{*}
that we want to find.
The function
f
can only be observed via noisy samples
\widetilde{f}
, providing an unbiased estimate of the true value, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{f}(y)]=f(y)
for all
y
.

SA starts with an initial guess
y_{0}
of the root
y^{*}
; at time
t>0
, upon observing
\widetilde{f}(y_{t-1})
, it updates its guess as
[
27
]
:

y_{t}=y_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}\left(\tau-\widetilde{f}(y_{t-1})\right),

(5)

where
\alpha_{t}>0
is SA’s stepsize. If
\widetilde{f}(y_{t-1})
undershoots the target
\tau
, i.e.,
\widetilde{f}(y_{t-1})<\tau
, then
y_{t-1}
is likely an underestimation of the root
y^{*}
, since
f
is non-decreasing. Thus,
y_{t}>y_{t-1}
, and their difference
y_{t}-y_{t-1}
is proportional to the undershoot. The reverse argument holds in case of an overshoot.

OLLA is indeed an SA algorithm under two assumptions:

i)

the user’s SINR report is fixed, e.g.,
\gamma^{\mathrm{rep}}:=0

ii)

the ACK/NACK feedback
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t}
is received immediately upon MCS selection
u_{t}
, i.e.,
\delta_{t}=0
for all
t
.

In this case, the SINR estimate
\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t}
plays the role of
y_{t}
, and the unknown function
f
at time
t
is

f_{t}(\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t}):=\text{\small{BLER}}\left(u^{\mathrm{ILLA}}(\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t},\tau,b_{t}),\gamma_{t},b_{t}\right),

(6)

which is time-varying due to the true SINR
\gamma_{t}
and the TBS
b_{t}
.
The function
f_{t}
is observed via the feedback
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t}
, which plays the role of the noisy observation
\widetilde{f}_{t}(\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t})
. The latter is indeed unbiased since
\mathbb{E}[1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t}]=f_{t}(\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t})

II-B

Fixed stepsize: Strengths and weaknesses

If the unknown function
f
is time-invariant and the stepsize
\alpha_{t}
decreases to 0 sufficiently slowly over time (specifically,
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}^{2}=0
), then the SA iterates
y_{t}
will converge to the root
y^{*}
almost surely
[
28
]
.

Conversely, if the step size is constant, i.e.,
\alpha_{t}=\alpha
for all
t
, and
f
remains time-invariant, the sequence of iterates
y_{t}
will form a Markov chain which, under suitable conditions
[
29
]
, will converge exponentially fast to its stationary distribution, with a rate of convergence proportional to the step size
\alpha
.
Hence, in this
transient
phase, higher values of
\alpha
result in faster convergence.
Once the stationary distribution is reached, the Markov chain fluctuates around the root
y^{*}
within a region of size
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
. Thus, lower values of
\alpha
help reducing the iterates’ variance in the
stationary
phase.

When the unknown function
f
is time-dependent, using a constant stepsize is a common design choice to effectively track the time-varying root, as in the case of OLLA.
Yet, the considerations above also suggest that there is no universal stepsize value that performs well for OLLA in all scenarios:

•

When the SINR changes abruptly, a large stepsize is needed to follow the rapid root fluctuations;

•

When the SINR stabilizes, a smaller stepsize is preferable to avoid significant MCS fluctuations, which would degrade the achieved spectral efficiency. Indeed, when OLLA overshoots by selecting a high MCS, the offset is harshly penalized, causing the MCS to drop well below the optimal value. This behavior is depicted in Figure
1
.

Our proposed SALAD algorithm is designed to combine the advantages of both strategies: it increases the speed of link adaptation during abrupt SINR changes, and reduces it when the SINR stabilizes, thereby ensuring accurate channel inference while mitigating inefficient MCS fluctuations.

II-C

The impact of delayed HARQ feedback

The analogy between OLLA and SA holds in the absence of HARQ feedback delay, i.e.,
\delta=0
. Yet, in real systems, feedback delay is unavoidable and can lead to performance degradation.
For instance, suppose that the channel quality deteriorates at time
t
, but the corresponding ACK/NACKs are reported at
t+\delta_{t}
.
During the interval
[t,t+\delta_{t}]
, OLLA continues to select a high MCS, resulting in a series of NACKs received after
t+\delta_{t}
. This causes the SINR estimate to drop sharply, potentially well below the actual SINR value, ultimately causing the selection of over-conservative MCS.

SALAD is more robust to HARQ feedback delay, because it updates the SINR estimate using the likelihood of receiving an ACK/NACK, instead of applying a fixed additive step.

III
SALAD: Self-Adaptive Link Adaptation

Our link adaptation algorithm, called SALAD (self-adaptive link adaptation), consists of three modules:

•

SINR inference
(Section
III-A
): The SINR is estimated by binary cross-entropy loss minimization. Unlike OLLA, the SINR update is adaptive and depends on the discrepancy between the ACK/NACK observations and their expected likelihood.

•

MCS selection
(Section
III-B
): When the SINR is deemed to be underestimated—via hypothesis testing—a high MCS value is probed. This creates a virtuous cycle, enabling faster tracking of sudden SINR surges.

•

Long-term BLER target enforcement
(Section
III-C
): A feedback-loop mechanism enforces the BLER target
\tau
, compensating for excessive probing when necessary.

III-A

SINR inference

We first illustrate how SALAD produces an estimate
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
of the SINR at slot
t
from past MCS values and the corresponding ACK/NACK feedback. Our method works across two different time-scales. At every slot, a simple “student” iterate infers the next SINR from the last estimate. Such iterate depends on a learning rate called
\varepsilon
. Optionally, every
N_{\varepsilon}
slots, a powerful “teacher” model infers the whole time-series of past SINR. The student’s learning rate is finally optimized to best mimic the teacher’s behavior over the past slots. This allows SALAD to capture long-term SINR volatility patterns. We start by presenting our teacher model.

Teacher model
. We introduce a parametric model
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(\theta)
of the estimated SINR at each past slot
i\leq t
, parametrized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
To infer the SINR evolution, we seek the parameters
\theta
that minimize the discrepancy between the observed ACK/NACKs and the corresponding likelihood, quantified by the empirical binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss.
Our “teacher” SINR inference formulation is as follows:

\theta^{*}_{t}:=\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta}\ \sum_{i<t}\text{\small{BCE}}_{i}(\theta)+\Omega(\theta),

(8)

where
\Omega(.)
is a regularizer, promoting temporal smoothness of the estimated SINR, and

\displaystyle\text{\small{BCE}}_{i}(\theta):=

\displaystyle\,-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}\log\Pr\left(1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}=1\,|\,u_{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(\theta),b_{i}\right)

\displaystyle-\big(1-

\displaystyle 1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}\big)\log\Pr\left(1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}=0\,|\,u_{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(\theta),b_{i}\right).

(9)

The probability of receiving a NACK in an equivalent AWGN channel can be pre-computed and stored in BLER tables
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}

\displaystyle\text{\small{BCE}}_{i}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\,-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}\log\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{i}(\theta)

\displaystyle-\big(1-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}\big)\log\left(1-\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{i}(\theta)\right),

(10)

\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{i}(\theta):=\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}\left(u_{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(\theta),b_{i}\right).

(11)

Gradient computation:
To solve (
8

\theta

To obtain a convenient closed form, we approximate

as a suitably translated and scaled sigmoid

[

\displaystyle 1\!-\!\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}(u,\gamma,b)\!\approx\!\left(1+e^{-\frac{\gamma-c(u,b)}{s(u,b)}}\right)^{-1}\!\!:=\!\sigma_{u,b}(\gamma),

where

Piece-wise linear model:
A simple yet effective model is the piece-wise linear one, also called first-order spline:

\widetilde{\gamma}^{k}(i,\theta_{k}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\theta_{k}\tfrac{t_{k+1}-i}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\quad\text{if }i\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]\\

0\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(15)

In this case, a reasonable regularizer is the total variation one:

\Omega(\theta):=\beta\sum_{k=2}^{K}\left(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k-1}\right)^{2},\quad\beta\geq 0.

(16)

\nabla_{\theta}\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(\theta)
,
\nabla_{\theta}\Omega(\theta)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}\gamma_{i}(\theta)=

\frac{i-t_{k-1}}{t_{k}-t_{k-1}}\quad\text{if }i\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]\\
0\qquad\quad\ \ \text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,\ \forall k

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}\Omega(\theta)=

Student model
. It may be computationally prohibitive to re-optimize the teacher model at every slot to produce the estimate
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}(\theta^{*}_{t})
.
We then propose a computationally cheaper but effective heuristic alternative, called student inference, which infers the SINR at slot
t
as a gradient update of the previous estimate:

\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}-\varepsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial\,\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}}\text{\small{BCE}}_{t-1}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1})\quad\forall\,t>0

(23)

where
\varepsilon>0
is the learning rate and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}
is initialized arbitrarily.
By using the sigmoid approximation (
12
) and exploiting the property
\sigma^{\prime}=\frac{1}{s}\sigma(1-\sigma)
, we can rewrite (
23
) in closed-form:

\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}+\varepsilon s_{t-1}^{-1}\left(\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}\right)\quad\forall\,t>0,

(24)

where we define:

\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t}:=\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}\left(u_{t},\widetilde{\gamma}_{t},b_{t}\right)\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

(25)

and
s_{t}:=s(u_{t},b_{t})
is the sigmoid scale corresponding to MCS
u_{t}
and TBS
b_{t}
.

Surprise effect:
We observe that the student’s SINR increment in (
24
) is proportional to the term
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}
which quantifies the “surprise” of observing
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}
relative to its expected likelihood
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}
. Indeed,

•

Suppose
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}\approx 1
:

–

If an ACK is unexpectedly received (
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}=0
), then
\widetilde{\gamma}
is increased significantly, confirming that the SINR is currently underestimated.

–

Receiving a NACK aligns with expectations and has virtually no impact on the SINR update;

•

Conversely, if
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}\approx 0
:

–

A NACK indicates SINR overestimation, resulting in a significant SINR decrease;

–

an ACK only slightly affects the SINR estimation.

Knowledge distillation
. For the student SINR estimate (
24
) to accurately track actual SINR variations, the learning rate
\varepsilon
should ideally increase with the volatility of the SINR.
Yet, the ground truth is inherently unobservable.
To overcome this, we adopt a procedure inspired by knowledge distillation in neural networks
[
32
]
, where the student estimator learns to imitate a more powerful teacher estimator.
Specifically, the teacher SINR estimator is first optimized by solving (
8
) according to recent observations every
N_{\varepsilon}
slots.
Then, the student estimator (
24
) is replayed on the same data using different values of
\varepsilon
, and the value that yields the closest match to the reference estimator is selected for use in the subsequent slots. This procedure is described in Algorithm
2
.

III-B

MCS selection

SALAD selects the MCS
u_{t}
at slot
t
via ILLA under an optimized
instantaneous
BLER target
\tau_{t}
, i.e.,

u_{t}:=u^{\mathrm{ILLA}}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-\delta_{t}},\tau_{t},b_{t})\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

(26)

Our dynamic target selection philosophy is the following:

•

If the SINR inference is considered accurate at time
t
, we select the instantaneous target optimally as
\tau_{t}=\tau
.

•

If the SINR is detected to be underestimated, a high instantaneous BLER target
\tau_{t}:=\tau^{\mathrm{probe}}\gg\tau
is probed. This corresponds to a high MCS index and a high BLER (
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t}\approx 1
). Receiving an ACK (
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t}=0
) in this setting provides two advantages:

i)

The spectral efficiency immediately increases;

ii)

Thanks to the “surprise effect”, the inferred SINR quickly catches up with the ground truth. This creates a virtuous circle that improves the accuracy of future MCS selections.

We now turn to the design of a method for detecting SINR underestimation.

Detection of SINR underestimation and MCS probing
. The quality of SINR estimation can be assessed by comparing the BLER of recent transmissions (estimated from pre-computed tables under the assumption of accurate SINR) and the corresponding ACK/NACKs, providing an empirical BLER estimate.
Intuitively, if the BLER significantly exceeds the observed portion of NACKs, the former is being overestimated, while the SINR is underestimated.
To formalize this, we use a simple statistical hypothesis test.

We first introduce the
calibration bias score
, summing up all signed differences between the BLER values and the ACK/NACK observations
1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}
over the last
T
samples:

\mathcal{S}_{t}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-\delta_{t}-i}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-\delta_{t}-i}.

(27)

The score has zero mean under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0,t}
that all BLER and SINR estimates are correct, where

\mathcal{H}_{0,t}:=\!\left\{\mathbb{E}[1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i-\delta_{t}}]\!=\!\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{i-\delta_{t}},\ t-T\leq i\leq t-1\right\}.

(28)

We reject the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and conclude that the SINR estimation is poor, when the value of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is unlikely under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. More specifically, we deem the SINR underestimated when
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, normalized by its variance under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, exceeds a design parameter
\rho>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{S}_{t}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\mathcal{S}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{0,t}]}}>\rho\ \

\displaystyle\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \text{SINR is underestimated,}

(29)

where

\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathcal{S}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{0,t}\right]=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-\delta_{t}-i}(1-\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-\delta_{t}-i}).

(30)

In this case, as motivated above, a high BLER target
\tau_{t}:=\tau^{\mathrm{probe}}\gg\tau
is selected, resulting in the probing of an aggressive MCS value.

Remark
. A non-negligible HARQ feedback delay may render the score
\mathcal{S}_{t}
temporarily inaccurate and trigger a sequence of poor probing decisions and a transient BLER increase.
To mitigate this behavior, we recommend probing high MCS values only with some probability
p^{\mathrm{probe}}
if (
29
) holds.

III-C

Long-term BLER target enforcement

An overly aggressive MCS probing strategy as defined in (
29
) can lead to a transient increase in BLER.
Moreover, according to (
24
), the inferred SINR is only mildly decreased following a NACK, which may promote further probing in future transmissions.
To address this, we introduce a feedback mechanism that adapts the instantaneous BLER target
\tau_{t}
based on the achieved BLER, inspired by the Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control approach in
[
33
]
.

Specifically, we define the integral error

E_{t}:=\sum_{i<t-\delta_{t}}\tau-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{i}

(31)

and adapt the instantaneous BLER target
\tau_{t}
as follows:

\tau_{t}\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}\left(\tau_{t}+k_{E}E_{t},0,1\right),\quad k_{E}>0.

(32)

Observe that if the portion of NACKs exceeds the BLER target
\tau
,
E_{t}
becomes negative, resulting in a lower BLER target and a more conservative MCS selection. Conversely, a positive
E_{t}
leads to a more aggressive MCS selection.
This mechanism is instrumental for maintaining the BLER, computed over the whole connection duration, close to the desired target
\tau
.

III-D

A practical consideration

For large CBS values, the BLER exhibits a steep transition between its extreme values, 1 and 0, near the Shannon SINR limit. This is illustrated in Table
II
, showing smaller scale parameter
s
for larger CBS, indicating a steeper BLER cutoff.

As a result, the term
s_{t-1}^{-1}\left(\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}\right)
, which appears in SALAD’s SINR update (
24
), can take on extreme values when the TBS is large. Specifically, upon probing, a received ACK leads to a substantial SINR increase
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\gg\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}
.
Conversely, receiving a NACK results in only a negligible update
\widetilde{\gamma}_{t+1}\approx\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}
.
In the absence of probing, the situation is reversed: an ACK leads to a negligible SINR update, while a NACK causes a significant SINR decrease.

A simple and effective remedy is to clip the values of
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t}
and
s_{t}
to appropriate intervals, e.g.,
(0.01,0.99)
and
(0.5,10)
, respectively.

III-E

The impact of delayed HARQ feedback

We conclude the presentation of SALAD by analyzing the impact of delayed HARQ on SINR inference and MCS selection, while emphasizing its connection to OLLA.

Absence of HARQ feedback delay (
\delta=0
).
In this case, SALAD’s SINR inference update can be interpreted as a variant of OLLA with adaptive instantaneous BLER target and offset parameter
\Delta^{\textsc{NACK}}
.
In fact, since the MCS is selected via the up-to-date SINR estimation, i.e.,
u_{t-1}=u^{\mathrm{ILLA}}_{t-1}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1},\tau_{t})
, we can approximate the target
\tau_{t-1}
as the
effective
BLER:

\tau_{t-1}\approx\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}:=\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}(u_{t-1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}),\ \text{if }\delta=0.

(33)

Then, SALAD’s SINR update (
24
) can be rewritten as

\widetilde{\gamma}_{t}\approx\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1}+\varepsilon s_{t-1}^{-1}\left(\tau_{t-1}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}\right)\quad\forall\,t>0

(34)

which corresponds to OLLA’s update—cfr. with (
7
):

\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t}=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\mathrm{OLLA}}_{t-1}+\frac{\Delta^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}}{1-\tau_{t-1}}\left(\tau_{t-1}-1{\hskip-2.5pt}\mathrm{I}^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}\right)

(35)

with a time-adaptive offset parameter
\Delta^{\textsc{NACK}}_{t-1}:=\varepsilon s^{-1}_{t-1}(1-\tau_{t-1})
and a dynamic target
\tau_{t-1}
.

Presence of HARQ feedback delay (
\delta>0
).
In this scenario, SALAD’s SINR update differs significantly from that of time-adaptive OLLA. In both algorithms, the MCS
u_{t-1}
is selected based on an
outdated
SINR estimation, specifically,
u_{t-1}=u^{\mathrm{ILLA}}_{t-1}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{t-1-\delta_{t-1}},\tau_{t-1})
.
Yet, the SINR staleness renders (
33
) invalid: the target
\tau_{t-1}
, used by OLLA in its SINR update, is different from the
effective
BLER,
\text{\small{BLER}}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{t-1}
, used by SALAD. In other words, SALAD updates the SINR using fresher information than OLLA.
As a result, SALAD’s SINR estimation is more robust to feedback delay, as confirmed by the experiments in Section
IV-A
.

SALAD’s parameter tuning
.
Finally, we investigate the extent to which SALAD’s parameters can be customized in a specific scenario, and how this may negatively impact SALAD’s performance in different traffic regimes.
To this end, SALAD’s parameters are optimized in the low throughput scenario (denoted as “opt. low TP” in Figure
8
) and the high throughput scenario (“opt. high TP”) separately.

For parameter optimization we use the Nelder-Mead algorithm
[
38
]
, which (i) does not require any gradient information and (ii) is well-suited for objective functions that are expensive to evaluate
[
39
]
.
Such features suit our needs: (i) the objective, defined as a linear combination of normalized throughput and the negative mean squared deviation of the BLER from the target
\tau
, is not available in closed-form.
Furthermore, (ii) evaluating the objective for one parameter configuration requires multiple full table rotations, lasting several tens of seconds overall, with an additional settling time of a few seconds between consecutive trials to allow transient effects to vanish.
In each traffic regime, the initial configuration is set to the “default” one, and Nelder-Mead iterates over 25 different steps. The same process is repeated three times in the low throughput regime and five times in high-throughput.
The resulting optimal parameters are reported in Table
III
.

Finally, we evaluate the performance of both fine-tuned configuration across all traffic regimes (low, mid and high throughput), as shown in Figure
8
.
As expected, the fine-tuned SALAD configuration outperforms the other SALAD’s variants in its preferred regime, although the gains remains limited. Moreover, the default parameters, manually selected prior to the optimization campaign, turns out to be a reasonable choice.
This demonstrates that SALAD’s performance is quite stable across a range of parameter settings.

IV-C

Knowledge distillation for learning rate self-adaptation

To conclude, we evaluate learning rate
\varepsilon
online optimization via knowledge distillation (see Section
III-A
) under controlled SINR evolution in pure simulation.
Every
N_{\varepsilon}
slots, the history of ACK/NACK feedback, TBS, and selected MCS is collected.
The “teacher” inference model—the piece-wise linear model (
14
)—is optimized using batch gradient descent, using the gradient expressions (
13
),(
III-A
). The number of knots
K
is selected via cross-validation, by splitting the history into training and test sets, and minimizing the loss on the latter.
Next, the “student” gradient-based inference algorithm is replayed on the same data using different candidate values of
\varepsilon
. The learning rate that minimizes the squared difference between the student’s and the teacher’s inferences is selected for use in the next slots.
Figure
9
illustrates the qualitative effect of knowledge distillation when the SINR oscillates according to a sinusoid with time-varying frequency.
The learning rate
\varepsilon
adapts to the frequency of the unknown SINR, allowing SALAD to closely track the ground truth. As the frequency increases,
\varepsilon
increases accordingly to follow faster SINR variations. Conversely, as the frequency decreases,
\varepsilon
is reduced to prevent excessive fluctuations.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we have introduced SALAD, a novel algorithm for adaptive MCS selection for wireless communications.
SALAD infers the user’s effective SINR from ACK/NACK feedback alone by minimizing a regularized cross-entropy loss with respect to predicted BLER values using gradient descent.
Its learning rate can be optimized at run-time via knowledge distillation, by occasionally comparing its performance against a more powerful “teacher” inference performed in hindsight.
To accelerate MCS adaptation under improving channel conditions, SALAD leverages hypothesis testing to detect SINR underestimation and safely probes high MCS values.
This improves both short-term spectral efficiency and long-term SINR inference accuracy.
A feedback control loop ensures that the long-term BLER remains close to the desired target.

We have evaluated SALAD in OTA experiments and benchmarked it against the industry-standard OLLA.
Results demonstrated that SALAD delivers robust and consistent performance across a wide range of traffic regimes, achieving up to 15% gains in throughput and spectral efficiency over default OLLA configurations while reliably meeting the BLER target.
Additionally, SALAD can be fine-tuned to specific traffic regimes using derivative-free optimization techniques.

A natural extension of this work involves learning an optimal MCS selection rule conditioned on the latest SINR estimate, bias score, and integral error, thereby replacing the heuristic in lines
15
–
24
of Algorithm
3
. This approach may be extended to handle CQI reports more effectively.
