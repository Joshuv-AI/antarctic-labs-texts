Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Modifies the Association Between Exercise Therapy and Injury Risk and Long-Term Outcomes in Patients with Knee Osteoarthritis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Junhong Yan, Jianing Lu, Yundong Chen, Yijun Lian, Guangming Niu, Shiwen Zhao, Tan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionStructured exercise is a core treatment for knee osteoarthritis (KOA), but whether its associations with musculoskeletal injury, knee arthroplasty utilization, and opioid use differ by type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) status remains uncertain.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used electronic health records from 2014–2024. A 3-month landmark design classified patients as receiving structured exercise therapy or usual care. Propensity score matching yielded 1,329 pairs in the T2DM+KOA cohort and 3,441 pairs in the KOA-only cohort. Injury was analyzed using clustered Cox regression, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) utilization using Fine–Gray models, and opioid-use trajectories using group-based trajectory modeling.ResultsOver 3.4 years of follow-up, exercise therapy was not significantly associated with injury in the KOA-only cohort (adjusted HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.76–1.13; p = 0.462), but was associated with increased injury risk in the T2DM+KOA cohort (adjusted HR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.11–1.74; p = 0.004). The Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction was significant (p = 0.006). Exercise therapy was associated with lower TKA utilization in the KOA-only cohort (adjusted subdistribution HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.68–0.92; p = 0.003), but not in the T2DM+KOA cohort (adjusted subdistribution HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.76–1.12; p = 0.408). The interaction was significant for the Progressive/Worsening opioid-use trajectory (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.12–2.61; p = 0.014). Among patients with T2DM receiving exercise therapy, exploratory spline analysis showed a nonlinear association between glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and injury risk (p for nonlinearity = 0.006).ConclusionExercise therapy showed different associations with injury according to T2DM status, but causal treatment-effect modification cannot be inferred. It was associated with lower TKA utilization in the KOA-only cohort, whereas no significant association was observed in the T2DM+KOA cohort. The HbA1c finding was exploratory and requires external validation. Individualized assessment and monitoring warrant evaluation, particularly among patients with poorer glycemic control.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a leading cause of chronic pain, functional limitation, and disability worldwide and represents a substantial and growing public health burden. Data from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study estimated approximately 374.7 million prevalent cases globally, with further increases projected as populations age and the prevalence of obesity rises.1,2 KOA is now understood as a heterogeneous whole-joint disease rather than an isolated disorder of articular cartilage. Pathological and biomechanical changes involve articular cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium, menisci, ligaments, joint capsule, periarticular muscles, and intra-articular fat pads.3 In particular, the infrapatellar fat pad is an intracapsular, extrasynovial structure with mechanical, metabolic, and immunoinflammatory functions and may actively contribute to joint inflammation and tissue crosstalk in KOA.4 Recent tissue-level evidence further showed that elevated glycated hemoglobin was associated with increased mast-cell marker expression in the infrapatellar fat pad of patients with KOA, providing a potential link between poor glycemic control and local inflammatory changes within the joint.5

Structured exercise therapy is a core component of conservative KOA management. Clinical practice guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology, and the Osteoarthritis Research Society International consistently recommend exercise as a first-line non-pharmacological intervention.6–8 Across randomized trials, exercise therapy has been associated with improvements in pain, physical function, performance, mobility, and health-related quality of life, although the magnitude and persistence of benefit vary according to exercise type, dose, adherence, and patient characteristics.9 Exercise should therefore be regarded primarily as a symptom- and function-directed intervention rather than an established disease-modifying treatment.

The management of KOA is frequently complicated by coexisting type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The two conditions share several clinical and metabolic features, including obesity, insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, impaired skeletal-muscle function, oxidative stress, and accumulation of advanced glycation end products.10,11 These factors may influence pain, physical capacity, joint loading, and musculoskeletal tissue repair. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) may additionally impair proprioception, balance, gait, and protective sensation, potentially affecting the ability to perform exercise safely and consistently.12 Exercise-related outcomes in patients with T2DM may also vary according to glycemic control, diabetes duration, diabetic complications, and glucose-lowering treatment. In particular, insulin and some insulin-secretagogue therapies may increase the risk of exercise-associated hypoglycemia, whereas neuropathy, vascular disease, and other complications may require modification of exercise selection and progression.13

Clinical evidence specifically addressing exercise therapy in patients with both KOA and T2DM remains limited. Recent randomized studies have shown that aerobic or home-based circuit training can improve glycemic control, musculoskeletal fitness, and health-related quality of life in selected patients with both conditions, with few reported serious adverse events.14,15 However, these trials were relatively short and were not designed to evaluate incident lower-limb musculoskeletal injuries, subsequent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) utilization, or long-term medication-use patterns. A secondary analysis of three KOA exercise trials did not identify statistically significant moderation of pain or functional outcomes by diabetes, but the number of participants with diabetes and the statistical power for interaction testing were limited.16 Consequently, uncertainty remains regarding whether the observed associations between documented exercise therapy and longer-term clinical outcomes differ according to T2DM status, glycemic control, DPN, diabetes duration, or glucose-lowering treatment.

Longitudinal opioid use was included as an additional outcome because persistent opioid exposure may reflect ongoing symptom burden, treatment complexity, painful comorbidities, and patterns of healthcare utilization, although it cannot be considered a direct measure of pain intensity or physical function. Previous research following hip or knee arthroplasty has identified distinct patterns of persistent opioid use associated with baseline opioid exposure, comorbidity burden, painful conditions, and healthcare-use characteristics.17 Characterizing opioid-use trajectories may therefore provide complementary information beyond individual prescription records.

Accordingly, this retrospective cohort study evaluated the associations of documented structured exercise therapy with incident lower-limb musculoskeletal injury, subsequent TKA utilization, and longitudinal opioid-use patterns in patients with KOA. We tested the Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction for the primary injury outcome and conducted exploratory analyses according to DPN, glycemic control, diabetes duration, and glucose-lowering treatment.

Methods

Study Design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University using de-identified electronic health record data collected between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2024.

A 3-month landmark design was used to classify exercise exposure and establish a common start of follow-up. The baseline date was defined as the first eligible KOA encounter, and the landmark date was defined as 3 months after the baseline date. Patients who experienced a lower-limb musculoskeletal injury, underwent knee arthroplasty, died, or were lost to follow-up before the landmark date were excluded from the landmark cohort. Follow-up for both exposure groups began at the landmark date.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University (Approval No. EC-LW026-005), which waived the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective design and use of de-identified data.

Study Population

Patients with KOA were initially identified from the institutional electronic health record system using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes for KOA (M17.x). Cohort identification and key-variable abstraction were validated using a two-stage procedure. First, a prespecified electronic algorithm was applied to structured clinical data. Second, a random sample comprising 10% of potentially eligible records, with a minimum of 200 records, was independently reviewed by two trained clinical research coordinators. The review covered confirmation of KOA diagnosis, exercise-exposure classification, T2DM status, and outcome ascertainment. The observed Cohen’s κ values were 0.94 for KOA diagnosis, 0.91 for exercise-exposure classification, 0.96 for T2DM status, and 0.89 for outcome ascertainment. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with final adjudication by a senior orthopedic clinician when required.

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥50 years on the baseline date, given the marked increase in symptomatic KOA prevalence from middle age onward;18,19 (2) a clinician-recorded diagnosis of KOA supported by an M17.x diagnosis code and either clinical documentation of activity-related knee pain without features suggesting an alternative diagnosis or a radiology report documenting degenerative knee changes, such as osteophyte formation or joint-space narrowing;20 (3) at least 12 months of continuous clinical records within the hospital information system before the baseline date to permit ascertainment of comorbidities, medication use, healthcare utilization, and previous treatment; and (4) continued follow-up within the institutional electronic health record system through the end of the 3-month landmark period.

Both unilateral and bilateral KOA cases were eligible. Because exercise exposure, medication use, and the study outcomes were ascertained at the patient level, unilateral and bilateral cases were combined in the primary analyses and were not analyzed as separate laterality strata.

Patients were excluded if they had: (1) previous major surgery on the index knee, including TKA, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, corrective osteotomy, or major ligament reconstruction; (2) rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, or another systemic inflammatory arthritis with a pathophysiology and treatment course distinct from KOA;21,22 (3) active malignancy, other than non-melanoma skin cancer, or receipt of anticancer treatment during the preceding 5 years; (4) end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis or previous renal transplantation; (5) dementia, Alzheimer disease, or another major neurocognitive disorder; (6) insufficient baseline or follow-up information to determine eligibility or outcome status; or (7) indeterminate exercise exposure during the landmark period. Missing values for potentially imputable baseline covariates were not used as exclusion criteria and were addressed using multiple imputation.

Patients were further stratified according to T2DM status assessed before the landmark date. The T2DM+KOA cohort included patients with either one inpatient diagnosis or at least two outpatient diagnoses of T2DM recorded on separate dates using E11.x codes, together with either an active prescription for glucose-lowering treatment or a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) value ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol) recorded before the landmark date.23 A qualifying HbA1c value was used to corroborate the coded clinical diagnosis and was not used as the sole criterion for T2DM ascertainment. Metformin prescribed solely for prediabetes or polycystic ovary syndrome was not considered evidence of T2DM. Patients who did not meet the prespecified T2DM definition constituted the KOA-only cohort.

Exposure Definition and Grouping

The primary exposure was participation in a structured, active exercise therapy program during the 3-month landmark period. Patients were referred for supervised exercise therapy by a treating orthopedic or rehabilitation physician following clinical assessment. The institutional care pathway generally supported referral when activity-related knee symptoms or functional limitations persisted despite routine management, or when supervised instruction was considered necessary because of impaired strength, mobility, balance, or exercise technique. However, no mandatory referral score or uniform eligibility threshold was used, and the final decision reflected physician judgment, patient willingness, treatment feasibility, and appointment availability. This referral process was therefore considered a potential source of confounding by indication.

Exposure classification was based on rehabilitation orders, treatment records, local procedure codes, and standardized treatment descriptions recorded in the institutional electronic health record system. Rehabilitation exercise information can be systematically identified and categorized when structured procedure data are combined with clinical treatment descriptions.24 The local clinical order and billing codes were routinely used for rehabilitation delivery and administrative documentation within the institution but had not previously undergone external validation for research purposes. Codes were therefore not used in isolation. Their associated treatment descriptions were reviewed to distinguish active therapeutic exercise and functional training from assessment-only encounters and passive physical modalities.

Patients were assigned to the exercise therapy group if they completed at least six documented rehabilitation sessions during the 3-month landmark period, with at least three sessions, or ≥50% of all sessions, involving active therapeutic exercise or functional task training. Exercise programs followed a standardized institutional framework but were individualized according to symptoms, physical capacity, comorbidities, and treatment tolerance. Active interventions included lower-limb muscle strengthening, active range-of-motion exercise, balance or neuromuscular training, endurance exercise, and gait-related activities. Functional task training included supervised sit-to-stand practice, step-up and step-down exercises, gait retraining, stair negotiation, transfers, and other dynamic activities intended to improve performance of daily lower-limb tasks.

Supervised sessions were generally scheduled two to three times per week and lasted approximately 30–45 minutes. Strengthening exercises were typically performed at a moderate intensity for one to three sets of 8–12 repetitions, whereas endurance activities were generally prescribed at a rating of 11–13 on the Borg 6–20 Rating of Perceived Exertion scale. Exercise duration, repetitions, resistance, or task complexity were increased according to individual tolerance, provided that treatment did not produce clinically important or persistent symptom exacerbation. These principles were consistent with recommendations that exercise for KOA should be individualized, progressively adjusted, and include strengthening, aerobic, neuromuscular, or functional components.6,8,25

Patients were generally instructed to perform an individualized home exercise program on three to five days per week, including selected strengthening, mobility, or balance exercises. However, completion of home exercise, exercise performed outside the institution, and adherence after the landmark date were not systematically documented. Consequently, exposure classification was based on documented institutional treatment sessions and did not require confirmation of long-term home-based adherence.

The minimum treatment-dose criterion was prespecified to improve the specificity of identifying a substantive exercise program rather than isolated, assessment-only, or predominantly passive rehabilitation encounters. The usual-care group comprised eligible patients who did not meet the structured exercise therapy definition during the landmark period. These patients could receive pharmacotherapy, general lifestyle or exercise advice, intra-articular treatment, passive physical modalities, or isolated rehabilitation visits that did not satisfy the prespecified frequency and active-treatment criteria.

Exposure status was determined exclusively from information recorded during the 3-month landmark period and was fixed at the landmark date. Rehabilitation treatment received after the landmark date did not alter the original exposure assignment. Exercise therapy was therefore analyzed as a fixed exposure rather than as a time-dependent covariate. Patients whose rehabilitation records were insufficient to determine whether the structured exercise criteria had been met were classified as having indeterminate exposure and were excluded before propensity score matching.

The reliability of exposure classification was evaluated in the manual validation sample. The procedure-code algorithm and associated treatment descriptions were compared with independent review of the complete rehabilitation records by two trained clinical research coordinators. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with adjudication by a senior rehabilitation or orthopedic clinician when necessary. Agreement between algorithm-based and manually adjudicated exposure classification was excellent, with an observed Cohen’s κ of 0.91.

Data Collection

The primary outcome was the first incident lower-limb musculoskeletal injury occurring after the landmark date. Injuries were identified from diagnosis records using a prespecified code list covering acute ligament, meniscal, patellar, tendon, muscle, soft-tissue, and stress-fracture injuries of the knee or lower limb. Chronic degenerative conditions, including progression of osteoarthritis, chronic tendinopathy, and degenerative meniscal disease without a documented acute injury encounter, were not counted as outcome events. Diagnosis codes can support identification of musculoskeletal outcomes in electronic health records, although their validity varies according to the condition and clinical setting.26,27 Because the exact temporal relationship between an injury and an individual exercise session was unavailable, the endpoint represented incident musculoskeletal injuries occurring during follow-up rather than confirmed exercise-related injuries. Detailed coding rules are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

A fall-related injury was defined as a qualifying lower-limb injury accompanied by a fall-related external-cause code during the same encounter or within 1 day of the injury encounter. A fall code without a corresponding injury diagnosis was not counted as an outcome. To distinguish incident injuries from pre-existing conditions, patients were required to have no record of the same injury category during the 6 months preceding the baseline date.28 Only the first qualifying injury after the landmark date was included in the primary time-to-event analysis.

The principal long-term clinical outcome was progression to total knee arthroplasty (TKA). TKA was identified using standardized surgical procedure records, local procedure codes, and operative documentation corresponding to primary total knee replacement. For patients with bilateral KOA, the first primary TKA performed on either affected knee was counted as the outcome event. TKA represents a clinically relevant measure of conversion to surgical treatment in patients with advanced KOA.29 The procedure-identification algorithm was verified against operative notes in the manual validation sample. TKA was treated as a surgical conversion outcome rather than as a direct measure of pain or physical function.

Longitudinal opioid use was evaluated as an additional secondary outcome. Opioid prescriptions were standardized as morphine milligram equivalents (MME) using a prespecified conversion table based on established opioid conversion factors.30 For each prescription, the total opioid dose was converted to MME and divided by the intended treatment duration to estimate mean daily MME. Monthly mean daily MME values were then constructed for the trajectory analysis. Non-opioid analgesic use was retained as a baseline treatment characteristic where available but was not included in the opioid-use trajectory outcome.

Baseline covariates were selected a priori on the basis of previous literature, clinical relevance, and established knowledge of factors potentially associated with receipt of exercise therapy, musculoskeletal injury, progression to TKA, opioid use, or healthcare contact. Covariates were not selected using univariable statistical significance or other data-driven screening procedures. Demographic and anthropometric variables included age, sex, and body mass index.

Overall comorbidity burden was summarized using the Charlson Comorbidity Index calculated from diagnoses recorded during the 12 months preceding the baseline date.31 KOA severity was assessed using the Kellgren–Lawrence (K–L) grading system.32 K–L grades were obtained from radiology reports that explicitly documented the grade or from grades extracted using a prespecified rules-based natural language processing algorithm. The use of natural language processing to extract structured variables from radiology reports is established, although local validation remains necessary before research implementation.33

The algorithm recognized explicit K–L terminology, grade expressions, laterality, and equivalent Chinese-language descriptions, while excluding negated, uncertain, historical, or non-index-knee statements. In a random sample of 300 radiology reports, two trained reviewers independently assigned the reference K–L grade, with disagreements adjudicated by an orthopedic clinician. Compared with the adjudicated reference, the algorithm achieved 92.0% exact agreement and a weighted Cohen’s κ of 0.90. For identifying K–L grade ≥3, sensitivity was 94.1% and specificity was 96.3%. Reports without an explicit or reliably extractable grade were treated as having missing K–L data and addressed using multiple imputation. ICD-10 M17.x subclassifications were not used as proxies for radiographic severity.

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) was identified before the landmark date using documented clinical diagnoses and diabetes-with-neuropathy codes. DPN was included because sensory loss, impaired proprioception, balance impairment, and related lower-limb dysfunction may increase the risks of falls and injury.34,35 Diabetes duration was calculated from the earliest documented T2DM diagnosis in the available longitudinal record. For baseline description and propensity score estimation, duration was categorized as <10 or ≥10 years to provide a clinically interpretable measure of longer-standing diabetes and because a duration of at least 10 years has been associated with a higher prevalence of DPN and diabetic foot risk factors.36

Additional covariates included prior intra-articular corticosteroid injection and systemic corticosteroid use. Prior intra-articular corticosteroid treatment was identified from procedure and medication-administration records before the baseline date because such treatment may be associated with subsequent progression to arthroplasty.37 Systemic corticosteroid use was defined as an average prednisone-equivalent dose of at least 5 mg/day for more than 30 cumulative days during the preceding 6 months, given its association with fracture risk.38 Depression or anxiety was identified from documented depressive or anxiety disorders before the baseline date because these conditions may influence pain burden, healthcare utilization, rehabilitation participation, and analgesic treatment.39

Baseline opioid exposure was assessed during the 3 months preceding the baseline date. A binary indicator identified patients with at least one active opioid prescription, and mean daily MME was calculated among baseline opioid users. This distinction was retained because the opioid-trajectory analysis was restricted to patients with active opioid treatment at baseline and sufficient longitudinal prescription data. Healthcare utilization was measured as the total number of outpatient encounters during the 12 months preceding the baseline date to account for differences in surveillance intensity and contact with the healthcare system.40,41

All baseline covariates were ascertained using information recorded before the baseline date. Data generated during the 3-month landmark period or after the landmark date were not used to define baseline confounders.

Statistical Analysis

The distributions of continuous variables were assessed by visual inspection of histograms and normal quantile–quantile plots, with skewness and kurtosis used as supplementary indicators. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation when approximately normally distributed and as median with interquartile range otherwise. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons used the independent-samples t test, Mann–Whitney U-test, chi-square test, or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with an absolute value <0.10 indicating adequate balance.

Propensity score matching was performed separately in the T2DM+KOA and KOA-only cohorts. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression including age, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index, depression or anxiety, Kellgren–Lawrence grade, prior intra-articular corticosteroid injection, baseline opioid exposure and mean daily morphine milligram equivalents, systemic corticosteroid use, and outpatient visit frequency. Diabetes duration and diabetic peripheral neuropathy were additionally included in the T2DM+KOA cohort. Patients were matched 1:1 without replacement using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.20 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption, generating 20 imputed datasets. The extent and pattern of missing baseline data were examined before imputation and are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Variables used to determine eligibility, exposure, or outcomes were not imputed. Baseline C-reactive protein was not imputed because it was used only in the complete-case sensitivity analysis. Propensity score matching and outcome modeling were conducted separately within each imputed dataset, and estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules.

Follow-up began on the landmark date and continued until the first qualifying lower-limb musculoskeletal injury, death, loss to follow-up, or December 31, 2024, whichever occurred first. For the analysis of progression to TKA, follow-up continued until TKA, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period. Time to first lower-limb musculoskeletal injury was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, Log rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models. Exercise therapy was treated as a fixed exposure. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using cluster-robust sandwich standard errors with matched pair as the clustering unit. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

The primary injury analysis, the Exercise Therapy × T2DM multiplicative interaction, the TKA analysis, and the opioid-use trajectory analysis were prespecified before fitting the outcome models. Effect modification by T2DM was evaluated in the pooled matched cohorts using an Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction term. Additive interaction was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and synergy index (SI), with confidence intervals obtained by bootstrap resampling of matched pairs. The null values were 0 for RERI and AP and 1 for SI; RERI >0, AP >0, and SI >1 indicated a positive departure from additivity.

Prespecified exploratory analyses included fall-related injury analyses according to diabetic peripheral neuropathy status, analyses according to HbA1c and glucose-lowering treatment, and subgroup analyses according to insulin use and prior intra-articular corticosteroid injection. The analysis according to metformin use was exploratory. Additional adjustment for baseline C-reactive protein among matched pairs with available measurements in both patients was conducted as a sensitivity analysis.

For progression to TKA, death was treated as a competing event. Associations between exercise therapy and TKA were evaluated using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models with robust standard errors clustered by matched pair. Results were reported as subdistribution hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and crude TKA event proportions were reported descriptively.

Opioid-use trajectory analysis was restricted to patients with an active opioid prescription at baseline and adequate longitudinal prescription data. Mean daily morphine milligram equivalents were calculated for successive 30-day intervals over 36 months. Months without an active prescription while the patient remained under observation were assigned a value of zero, whereas months after loss to follow-up or death were treated as missing. Group-based trajectory models with a censored normal distribution were fitted using two to six groups and linear, quadratic, or cubic polynomial terms. Model selection was based on the Bayesian information criterion, clinical interpretability, a minimum group proportion of 5%, an average posterior probability ≥0.70, and odds of correct classification ≥5. Associations with trajectory-group membership were examined using multinomial logistic regression with cluster-robust standard errors. The latent trajectory model itself was fitted without covariates. Calendar year of the baseline encounter, centered at 2019, was included as a continuous covariate in the subsequent multinomial regression model to account for secular changes in opioid prescribing practices.

Among patients with T2DM receiving exercise therapy, the association between baseline HbA1c and injury risk was examined using restricted cubic splines with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. HbA1c 7.0% was used as the reference value, and nonlinearity was evaluated using a joint test of the nonlinear spline terms. The value of approximately 7.8% was interpreted as a visual change in slope rather than as a formally estimated threshold. Additional Cox models evaluated the prespecified HbA1c categories and glucose-lowering treatment groups.

All tests were two-sided. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for the prespecified principal analyses. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to the subgroup and exploratory analyses; therefore, their p values were considered nominal, and the possibility of inflated type I error was acknowledged when interpreting these findings. Analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.0. The principal packages were MatchIt for propensity score matching, mice for multiple imputation, survival for Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses, crrSC for clustered competing-risk regression, trajeR for group-based trajectory modeling, rms for restricted cubic spline analyses, and nnet and sandwich for multinomial regression and cluster-robust variance estimation.

Results

Patient Flow and Baseline Characteristics

The initial electronic health record query identified 24,386 patients with knee osteoarthritis between January 2014 and December 2024. After application of the eligibility criteria, 18,474 patients remained. Of these, 6,742 had coexisting type 2 diabetes mellitus, including 1,347 who received structured exercise therapy and 5,395 who received usual care. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 1,329 matched pairs were retained for the primary analysis. The remaining 11,732 patients constituted the KOA-only cohort (Figure 1). Trajectory Analysis of Analgesic Use

Of the 9,540 patients in the two matched cohorts, 7,356 were excluded from the opioid-use trajectory analysis because they did not have an active opioid prescription at baseline or lacked sufficient longitudinal prescription data. The remaining 2,184 patients (22.9%) were included in the analysis.

Among candidate models containing two to six trajectory groups, the four-group solution was selected because it provided the most favorable balance of Bayesian information criterion, clinical interpretability, and classification adequacy, while satisfying the prespecified requirements for minimum group size, average posterior probability, and odds of correct classification. The identified groups represented distinct longitudinal patterns of opioid exposure rather than direct measures of pain severity or physical function.

The Rapid Responder group included 786 patients (36.0%) and showed a rapid decline to near-zero mean daily morphine milligram equivalents within approximately 6 months, followed by sustained minimal use. The Slow Responder group comprised 582 patients (26.6%) and demonstrated a gradual reduction in opioid dosage over time. The Chronic High User group included 414 patients (19.0%) and maintained persistently high opioid use throughout follow-up. The Progressive/Worsening group comprised 402 patients (18.4%) and was characterized by initially lower opioid use followed by a sustained increase after the first year (Figure 3). Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

In the prespecified subgroup analyses according to insulin use and prior intra-articular corticosteroid injection, neither interaction term was statistically significant (Supplementary Table S4).

In the prespecified sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for baseline C-reactive protein in the complete-case matched sample, the adjusted HR was 1.37 (95% CI, 1.07–1.76; p = 0.013).

In an exploratory analysis according to metformin use, the Exercise Therapy × metformin interaction term was statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.041). Exercise therapy was not significantly associated with injury risk among metformin users (adjusted HR, 1.08; 95% CI, 0.80–1.46; p = 0.618), whereas a higher injury risk was observed among non-users (adjusted HR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.25–2.43; p = 0.001).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, documented structured exercise therapy was not significantly associated with incident lower-limb musculoskeletal injury in the KOA-only cohort but was associated with a higher injury risk in the T2DM+KOA cohort. The Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction was statistically significant, and the additive interaction measures indicated a positive departure from additivity. These findings indicate heterogeneity in the observed associations according to diabetes status but should not be interpreted as evidence that T2DM modifies a causal treatment effect. Exercise therapy was also associated with lower subsequent TKA utilization in the KOA-only cohort, whereas no statistically significant association was observed in the T2DM+KOA cohort. Because a formal Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction was not evaluated for the TKA outcome, the difference between these stratum-specific estimates does not establish that T2DM modified the association with TKA utilization. A statistically significant Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction was also observed for membership in the Progressive/Worsening opioid-use trajectory group.

The present findings should be considered alongside previous clinical studies of exercise in patients with KOA and T2DM. In a randomized trial of 228 patients with both conditions, high-intensity stationary cycling improved glycemic control but was not superior to moderate-intensity training or regular rehabilitation for KOOS pain or quality-of-life outcomes.14 A separate randomized trial found that a 12-week home-based circuit-training program improved musculoskeletal fitness and health-related quality of life in overweight or obese older adults with KOA and T2DM.15 In secondary analyses of three exercise trials in KOA, diabetes was not a statistically significant moderator of pain or functional response, although the number of participants with diabetes and the ability to detect interaction effects were limited.16 These controlled studies primarily evaluated short-term symptoms, function, fitness, and metabolic outcomes in selected participants. The present study instead examined incident injuries, subsequent TKA utilization, and prescription trajectories over longer follow-up in routine clinical practice. Differences in populations, exposure ascertainment, outcomes, and follow-up therefore limit direct comparison, and the current results should not be interpreted as contradicting the established symptomatic and functional benefits of appropriately prescribed exercise.

The TKA findings require particular caution. Long-term exercise therapy has not been shown to consistently alter radiographic disease severity, cartilage morphology, or other structural features of KOA.42 TKA is also influenced by symptom burden, patient preferences, medical fitness for surgery, access to care, surgeon recommendations, and local practice patterns. Accordingly, the lower subdistribution hazard observed in the KOA-only cohort should be described as lower subsequent TKA utilization rather than prevention of structural progression or disease modification. The absence of a statistically significant association in the T2DM+KOA cohort may reflect differences in measured or unmeasured clinical characteristics, competing health priorities, surgical decision-making, or statistical precision, rather than a diabetes-related attenuation of a causal exercise effect.

Several biological mechanisms could plausibly contribute to differences in musculoskeletal vulnerability among patients with T2DM, but these mechanisms were not measured in the present study. Experimental evidence indicates that advanced glycation end products can increase extracellular matrix stress, reduce tendon biomechanical plasticity, and impair tendon repair.43–46 Diabetes-related sensory and motor impairment may also affect proprioception, balance, and postural responses.47 These observations provide biological context but do not demonstrate that glycation, neuropathy, or impaired tissue repair mediated the associations observed in this cohort. Although the point estimate for fall-related injury was higher among patients with DPN, the formal Exercise Therapy × DPN interaction was not statistically significant. The DPN-stratified results should therefore be regarded as exploratory and do not establish differential associations according to neuropathy status.

The nonlinear association between baseline HbA1c and injury risk was also exploratory. The spline curve suggested a steeper increase above approximately 7.8%, but this value was identified visually and was not estimated or validated as a clinical threshold. Higher HbA1c levels have been associated with vascular complications and death in other diabetes populations, although the shape and location of these associations varies across outcomes and study populations.48 The current analysis cannot determine whether glycation, microvascular disease, neuropathy, diabetes duration, or other correlated factors account for the observed pattern. External validation in independent cohorts and prospective studies with standardized exercise exposure are required before HbA1c is used to modify exercise eligibility, intensity, or surveillance. The findings do not support withholding exercise until a particular HbA1c target has been achieved.

The opioid-use trajectory analysis characterized longitudinal prescription exposure rather than pain severity, treatment efficacy, or physical function. Previous studies in patients undergoing hip or knee arthroplasty have shown that persistent or high-dose opioid use is associated with baseline opioid exposure, painful comorbidities, psychosocial characteristics, overall comorbidity burden, and healthcare-use patterns.17,49,50 Although those studies involved perioperative populations, they illustrate that opioid trajectories reflect multiple patient-level and prescribing factors. In the present study, exercise therapy was associated with lower odds of membership in the Chronic High User and Progressive/Worsening groups, and the Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction for the Progressive/Worsening group was statistically significant. These associations may reflect differences in pain burden, treatment response, prescribing practice, comorbid conditions, or unmeasured healthcare factors. They should not be interpreted as evidence that exercise directly reduced opioid requirements or that diabetes causally altered this response.

The metformin findings were exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Experimental and translational studies suggest that metformin may influence osteoarthritis-related inflammation, autophagy, and cartilage metabolism.51–53 However, these data do not establish a clinically protective musculoskeletal effect. In the present observational analysis, metformin use was influenced by renal function, diabetes severity, contraindications, treatment history, and clinician selection, creating substantial potential for confounding by indication. The nominal interaction p value was also obtained within multiple exploratory analyses without formal multiplicity adjustment. Independent confirmation is therefore required before any medication-specific interpretation can be made.

Within the exercise therapy group, insulin use was associated with a higher risk of fall-related injury than oral glucose-lowering treatment alone. Severe hypoglycemia has been associated with falls, and insulin treatment increases susceptibility to hypoglycemic episodes.54,55 However, hypoglycemic events were not directly measured in this study. Insulin use may also serve as a marker of longer-standing or more clinically complex diabetes, because real-world treatment patterns change as diabetes and complication burden progress.56 The formal insulin subgroup interaction for the primary musculoskeletal injury outcome was not statistically significant. Consequently, the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence that insulin directly caused fall-related injury.

The findings do not justify restricting established exercise therapy solely on the basis of T2DM, HbA1c, DPN, metformin use, or insulin treatment. Individualized assessment of symptoms, functional capacity, balance, previous falls, glycemic management, medication-related hypoglycemia risk, and other comorbidities remains clinically appropriate when prescribing rehabilitation. However, the non-significant DPN interaction does not support DPN-specific exercise recommendations from the present data, and the exploratory HbA1c findings should not be used to define treatment thresholds. Prospective studies should determine whether standardized progression, balance training, closer symptom monitoring, or other adaptations improve outcomes in clinically defined higher-risk groups while preserving the established benefits of exercise.

This study has several strengths. It included a large real-world cohort, used a 3-month landmark design to align exposure classification and follow-up, and performed propensity score matching separately according to diabetes status. Matching-pair clustering was incorporated into the outcome models, death was treated as a competing event in the TKA analysis, and longitudinal opioid exposure was characterized using group-based trajectory modeling. The use of diagnosis and procedure information from electronic health records is feasible for large-scale musculoskeletal research, although validity depends on the coding system, local documentation, and outcome definition.26,57

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design precludes causal inference. Referral to rehabilitation was not randomized and depended partly on physician judgment, patient willingness, treatment feasibility, and appointment availability. Propensity score matching balanced measured baseline covariates but could not eliminate confounding by indication or unmeasured confounding. Important unavailable or incompletely recorded KOA-related factors included symptom duration, knee-specific pain and function scores, range of motion, lower-limb alignment, muscle strength, balance, physical activity, occupational loading, and unilateral versus bilateral disease burden. Socioeconomic factors and clinician selection of patients for rehabilitation may also have contributed to residual confounding.

Second, exercise exposure was identified from rehabilitation orders, procedure codes, and clinical documentation. Although typical session duration, intensity, progression principles, and home-exercise recommendations could be described, individual-level dose, fidelity, long-term adherence, exercise performed outside the institution, and therapist-level variation were not consistently available. Patients assigned to usual care may also have undertaken undocumented exercise. These limitations may have resulted in exposure misclassification and prevent attribution of the observed outcomes to specific exercise components or doses.

Third, the injury outcome represented incident acute lower-limb musculoskeletal injuries recorded during follow-up, not injuries confirmed to have occurred during or because of an exercise session. Chronic degenerative conditions without a documented acute injury encounter were excluded, but diagnostic-code misclassification remains possible. Events occurring outside the institutional system may also have been missed. Fourth, TKA utilization and opioid-prescription trajectories are clinically relevant healthcare outcomes but are not direct measures of structural KOA progression, pain severity, or physical function. Direct patient-reported measures such as WOMAC and KOOS were unavailable.

Fifth, the DPN, HbA1c, insulin, and metformin analyses were exploratory, involved multiple comparisons, and were susceptible to residual confounding and inflated type I error. The approximate change in the HbA1c spline near 7.8% was not a formally estimated threshold. Finally, this was a single-center study, and differences in rehabilitation referral, exercise delivery, diabetes management, opioid prescribing, coding systems, and population characteristics may limit generalizability. Multicenter prospective studies with standardized exercise protocols, adherence assessment, clinically adjudicated injuries, and direct pain and functional outcomes are needed to confirm these associations.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, documented structured exercise therapy was not significantly associated with incident lower-limb musculoskeletal injury in the KOA-only cohort but was associated with a higher injury risk in the T2DM+KOA cohort. The significant Exercise Therapy × T2DM interaction indicated heterogeneity in the observed association but did not establish causal treatment-effect modification. Exercise therapy was associated with lower subsequent TKA utilization in the KOA-only cohort, whereas no statistically significant association was observed in the T2DM+KOA cohort; because no formal interaction analysis was performed for TKA, these findings do not establish that T2DM altered this association. A significant interaction was also observed for the Progressive/Worsening opioid-use trajectory. The HbA1c, DPN, insulin, and metformin findings were exploratory and should not be used to define exercise restrictions, glycemic thresholds, or medication-specific recommendations. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized exercise exposure and direct clinical outcomes are required to confirm these associations.
