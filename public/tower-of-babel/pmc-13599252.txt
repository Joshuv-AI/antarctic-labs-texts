Baloxavir marboxil versus oseltamivir for influenza A in children aged 5 years and older: a community-based prospective cohort study
Authors: Wei Li, Min Zhou, Haiyan Ye, Linyan Liu, Jun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfluenza A places a substantial burden on children. Real-world comparisons of antiviral regimens in community pediatric settings remain limited. This prospective cohort study compared the effectiveness and safety of a single-dose baloxavir marboxil regimen with a standard five-day oseltamivir course in children aged 5 years and older with influenza A, using fever duration as the primary outcome.Main bodyIn this multicenter, prospective, non-randomized observational cohort study, 750 children aged 5–14 years with reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)-confirmed influenza A were enrolled from eight community health centers in Chongqing, China, between October 2024 and December 2025. Treatment assignment was based on guardian preference rather than randomization. The median duration of fever was shorter in the baloxavir group (22.0 h versus 40.5 h; P < 0.001). In exploratory analyses adjusted for multiple comparisons, baloxavir was associated with a shorter median time to viral clearance (24.0 h vs. 72.0 h; false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted P < 0.001), a lower total complication rate (3.7% vs. 11.2%; FDR-adjusted P = 0.042), more rapid normalization of inflammatory markers and the CD4+/CD8 + ratio, lower revisit rate (2.1% vs. 6.9%; FDR-adjusted P = 0.003), fewer parental work-absence days (2.0 vs. 3.5 days; FDR-adjusted P < 0.001), and fewer adverse drug reactions (6.7% vs. 15.2%; FDR-adjusted P = 0.049).Short conclusionIn community-dwelling children with influenza A, baloxavir marboxil was associated with more rapid fever resolution than oseltamivir. Exploratory findings suggested potential benefits regarding viral clearance, complications, healthcare utilization, and tolerability. Because treatment selection was preference-based and open-label, and because influenza A subtype and reduced-susceptibility substitutions were not evaluated, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal.Trial registrationThis study was not prospectively registered because it was an observational study. However, the protocol was approved by the relevant ethics committee, and the study was reported following STROBE guidelines for observational research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14345-9.Keywords: Baloxavir marboxil, Oseltamivir, Pediatric influenza A, Prospective cohort study, Efficacy and safety comparison

Introduction

Influenza A remains a major global health burden, particularly among children [1, 2]. Each season, the high incidence among children aged 5–14 years increases outpatient visits and hospitalizations, strains healthcare systems, and causes socioeconomic losses [3, 4]. Beyond disrupting daily activities and school attendance, influenza A can precipitate pneumonia, febrile seizures, and neurological complications [4, 5]. Although vaccination and public health measures are critical, antigenic drift and vaccine mismatch may reduce vaccine effectiveness in some seasons [6, 7].

Antiviral therapy is an important strategy for reducing symptom duration and the burden of influenza-related complications when started early [8, 9]. Oseltamivir, a neuraminidase inhibitor, is an established oral treatment for pediatric influenza and has broad clinical consensus supporting its ability to shorten illness duration, although its clinical benefit is greatest when therapy is initiated soon after symptom onset [9–11]. The five-day regimen may be challenging for some children and caregivers, and gastrointestinal adverse events, particularly vomiting, are well recognized in pediatric practice [11]. Antiviral resistance is uncommon but clinically relevant when it occurs, and ongoing surveillance remains necessary [9, 12].

Baloxavir marboxil is a cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor that blocks an earlier step in influenza viral replication than neuraminidase inhibitors [13, 14]. In randomized trials, baloxavir shortened time to symptom improvement compared with placebo and produced more rapid reductions in viral load than oseltamivir in adults and adolescents, while pediatric trials such as miniSTONE-2 demonstrated generally comparable clinical symptom outcomes and favorable tolerability relative to oseltamivir [15–17].

Nevertheless, real-world comparative data in community-dwelling Chinese children remain limited. Important gaps include the performance of baloxavir and oseltamivir in routine outpatient practice, the relationship between virological response and clinical recovery, caregiver-reported outcomes, family burden, and safety under community follow-up conditions.

The objective of this multicenter prospective observational cohort study was to compare baloxavir marboxil with oseltamivir in children aged 5–14 years with RT-PCR-confirmed influenza A treated in urban community health centers in Chongqing, China. The primary endpoint was fever duration. Secondary and exploratory endpoints included time to relief of cough, sore throat, and fatigue, viral RNA dynamics, FLU-PRO disease-severity scores, complications, inflammatory and immune markers, healthcare resource utilization, family burden, adherence, and adverse drug reactions.

Because treatment choice was made by guardians rather than randomized, this study was designed to generate real-world evidence rather than definitive causal evidence. We therefore emphasize transparent reporting of treatment allocation, potential confounding, outcome ascertainment, and sensitivity analyses in accordance with principles for observational research.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a multicenter, prospective, non-randomized observational cohort study conducted from October 1, 2024, to December 31, 2025, at eight community health service centers authorized to provide pediatric care in Jiulongpo District, Chongqing. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Jiulongpo District Central Hospital (Approval No: 20240901-2), and written informed consent was obtained from all children’s guardians before enrollment.

Inclusion criteria

Age 5–14 years; Acute onset, illness duration ≤ 48 h, with fever (axillary temperature ≥ 37.8 °C) accompanied by influenza-like symptoms such as cough or sore throat; Confirmed positive for influenza A virus nucleic acid via RT-PCR testing of throat swabs; Guardian voluntarily chooses one of the treatment regimens and agrees to participate in follow-up.

Exclusion criteria

Suspected or confirmed severe influenza requiring immediate hospitalization; Known allergy to baloxavir marboxil, oseltamivir, or their excipients; Comorbid severe chronic underlying diseases (e.g., congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, severe asthma, liver or kidney failure); Use of other anti-influenza antiviral drugs within 48 h before enrollment; Inability to comply with the follow-up protocol.

Treatment allocation was based on guardian preference after a standardized explanation that both baloxavir and oseltamivir were clinically accepted antiviral options for uncomplicated influenza when used according to approved or institutional dosing recommendations. Guardians were told that the two regimens differed mainly in dosing schedule (single-dose baloxavir versus 5-day oseltamivir), common adverse reactions, and the importance of initiating treatment within 48 h; they were not told that either regimen was expected to be superior. Investigators did not assign treatment, and neither guardians nor outcome assessors were blinded. To avoid creating an artificial post hoc matched cohort, enrollment in each treatment arm was closed once the prespecified target of 375 evaluable children was reached; the final equal group size therefore reflected a prospective parallel enrollment cap rather than propensity score matching. Medication use and payment followed routine outpatient practice and local insurance/self-payment policies at participating centers; no medication was provided free of charge and no study-specific reimbursement or financial incentive was offered. Exact out-of-pocket costs and household income were not recorded. Guardian health literacy, household economic status, prior experience with antiviral drugs, perceived disease severity, and expectations regarding single-dose versus five-day therapy were therefore treated as important sources of potential residual confounding.

Interventions

Baloxavir marboxil group: A single oral dose of baloxavir marboxil (Xofluza®) was administered as soon as possible after diagnosis and within 48 h of symptom onset according to the Chinese prescribing information approved by the National Medical Products Administration and the participating centers’ institutional protocol: 2 mg/kg for body weight < 20 kg when oral suspension was used, 40 mg for body weight ≥ 20 to < 80 kg, and 80 mg for body weight ≥ 80 kg [18]. Children weighing ≥ 20 kg received the approved tablet, granule, or suspension formulation available at the center; children weighing < 20 kg received the measured oral suspension dose.

Site investigators verified the administered baloxavir dose against source records before the safety analysis.

Oseltamivir group: Oral oseltamivir granules (Tamiflu®) were administered twice daily for 5 consecutive days. The dose was adjusted based on body weight: 30 mg twice daily for weights ≤ 15 kg, 45 mg twice daily for > 15 to 23 kg, 60 mg twice daily for > 23 to 40 kg, and 75 mg twice daily for > 40 kg, respectively.

Both groups adhered to identical protocols for symptomatic supportive treatment (e.g., ibuprofen or acetaminophen for fever reduction, hydration, etc.), but the use of other antiviral drugs was prohibited. Decisions regarding hospitalization or the initiation of antibiotics due to disease progression were made by the attending physician based on clinical judgment and documented in detail.

Observation indicators and assessment methods

Primary Efficacy Indicator: Fever duration. Fever duration was defined as the time interval (hours) from the first administration of the study drug until the axillary temperature first dropped to < 37.3 °C and remained below this temperature for a continuous 24-hour period thereafter. Periods immediately following antipyretic administration were not counted as fever resolution. Guardians used standardized electronic thermometers and recorded temperature at least four times daily in diary cards. Use of ibuprofen or acetaminophen was permitted according to the shared supportive-care protocol and was recorded to minimize differential ascertainment.

Secondary efficacy indicators

Time to clinical symptom resolution was derived from daily symptom diary cards completed by guardians and reviewed by investigators. These diary-based outcomes included time to relief of cough, sore throat, and fatigue. Symptom resolution was defined as follows: cough, daytime cough frequency reduced by ≥ 75% compared with peak and not affecting nighttime sleep; sore throat, ability to swallow liquids normally without painful expression; fatigue, return to pre-illness daily activity level with no complaints of fatigue. These diary-derived resolution times were analyzed separately from FLU-PRO scores, which were used only as a multidomain disease-severity measure at scheduled time points.

Virological indicators

Viral load dynamics: Throat swab samples were collected before medication (baseline, 0 h) and at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, day 5, and day 7 after medication. A standardized RT-PCR kit (Shanghai ZJ Bio-Tech, Influenza A Virus Nucleic Acid Detection Kit) was used, and detection was performed on an ABI 7500 system to record cycle threshold (Ct) values for the viral matrix protein (MP) gene. A higher Ct value indicates a lower viral RNA load. Influenza A subtype testing [A(H1N1)pdm09 vs. A(H3N2)] was not routinely performed in this community protocol; therefore, subtype-specific analyses could not be conducted. The protocol also did not include sequencing for baloxavir reduced-susceptibility substitutions, including PA/I38 substitutions. Viral rebound (a renewed increase in viral RNA after an initial decline) and prolonged viral shedding were not prospectively defined or adjudicated as resistance endpoints; failure to achieve Ct-defined clearance by day 7 was captured only as a virological non-clearance outcome.

Time to viral clearance was defined as the sampling time point when the Ct value first reached ≥ 35 (negative according to the kit instructions and laboratory standards) and remained negative (Ct ≥ 35) in at least one subsequent follow-up sample. Baseline Ct value was controlled for in adjusted sensitivity analyses of virological endpoints because baseline viral RNA load may influence subsequent clearance.

Area under the viral shedding curve (AUC): Ct values were converted to approximate viral copy numbers (log10 copies/mL) using a standard curve, and the area under the viral load-time curve from baseline to day 7 was calculated to assess overall viral RNA burden. Ct values were interpreted as a measure of viral RNA rather than infectious virus; virus culture was not performed.

Disease severity score: Disease severity was assessed using FLU-PRO, a 32-item influenza patient-reported outcome diary covering six symptom domains (nose, throat, eyes, chest/respiratory, gastrointestinal, and body/systemic symptoms) [19, 20]. Guardians assisted children in completing the instrument before treatment and at 24 h, 72 h, and day 7 post-dose. For this study, an item-summed score (0–96) was used, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden. Because a validated Chinese pediatric cutoff defining “normal” FLU-PRO status has not been established, a total score < 10 at day 7 was prespecified as a descriptive near-resolution threshold rather than a validated normal-range threshold.

Complication monitoring and 14-day follow-up: All children were followed until day 14 of illness through scheduled clinic visits on day 3 and day 7, review of diary cards, telephone follow-up on day 14, and unscheduled visits whenever symptoms worsened. At scheduled clinic visits, trained community physicians performed routine clinical examinations, including otoscopy and lung auscultation when indicated. Diagnostic criteria for complications were standardized. Acute otitis media required otoscopic tympanic membrane congestion, bulging, or perforation with purulent discharge. Pneumonia required new clinical signs such as tachypnea, lung crackles, or hypoxemia plus chest X-ray evidence of new or progressive infiltrates when clinically indicated. The protocol recorded pneumonia as an influenza-related complication but did not routinely distinguish primary viral influenza pneumonia from secondary bacterial pneumonia; suspected bacterial infection and antibiotic initiation were recorded separately. Bronchitis, sinusitis, febrile convulsions, and acute necrotizing encephalopathy were defined according to prespecified clinical criteria, with imaging or specialist evaluation when clinically indicated.

Inflammatory and immune indicators

Fasting venous blood was collected from children before medication, on day 3, and day 7.

Inflammatory markers

Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured by immunoturbidimetry, and interleukin-6 (IL-6) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were quantified by chemiluminescence.

Lymphocyte subset analysis

Flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II) was used to detect the absolute count and percentage of CD3+, CD4+, CD8 + T lymphocytes in peripheral blood, and the CD4+/CD8 + cell ratio was calculated.

Healthcare resource utilization and family burden

Re-visit rate

The proportion of children who revisited any medical institution’s outpatient or emergency department within 14 days after medication due to unresolved or worsening influenza symptoms was recorded.

Unplanned medical interventions

The proportion of children who required the addition of systemic antibiotics or systemic corticosteroids beyond the study-defined supportive therapy due to clinical need was recorded.

Family burden

The total number of days at least one primary caregiver had to be absent from work due to caring for the sick child (work absence days) was recorded.

Safety indicators

Adverse drug reactions (ADR): All adverse events occurring from the start of medication until day 10 were collected using investigator-initiated inquiry and family-completed adverse reaction diary cards. Investigators assessed severity according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 and assessed causality to the study drug as definitely related, probably related, possibly related, unrelated, or indeterminate. The type, time of occurrence, duration, outcome, and relationship to treatment were recorded. Safety interpretation was based on the approved weight-based baloxavir regimen described above.

Laboratory safety: Complete blood count, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum creatinine (Cr) were measured prior to treatment initiation and the day after completing antiviral treatment (day 6 for the oseltamivir group, day 2 for the baloxavir marboxil group). Clinically significant laboratory abnormalities were defined as: ALT or AST > 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN); Cr > 1.2 times ULN.

Adherence Assessment: For the oseltamivir group, treatment adherence was assessed by counting returned leftover drug packaging combined with daily medication records. Completion of the full course of treatment was defined as taking at least 80% of the prescribed dose during the 5-day treatment period. The baloxavir group received a single observed or guardian-confirmed dose, and adherence was therefore considered complete when the dose was documented.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t tests. Non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as counts and percentages, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Sample size calculation: The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of fever duration and was prespecified in the study protocol. Based on a previous pilot study in our region (unpublished data) and published literature, the standard deviation of fever duration was estimated to be approximately 18 h. A between-group difference of 8.5 h was considered the minimum clinically meaningful difference for planning purposes. Assuming a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and power (1 − β) of 0.90, a two-sample comparison required approximately 98 evaluable participants per group. To allow for attrition, measured-confounder adjustment, inverse probability of treatment weighting, and exploratory analyses of virological, clinical, safety, and healthcare-utilization outcomes, the enrollment target was increased to 375 participants per group, yielding a total target of 750 participants.

Primary outcome analysis

Statistical significance for the primary outcome (fever duration) was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. No adjustment for multiple testing was necessary for this single primary comparison.

Exploratory outcome analysis and multiplicity adjustment

Due to the large number of exploratory endpoints assessed, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05. All reported P-values for exploratory endpoints represent FDR-adjusted values (q-values) unless otherwise noted. For reference, unadjusted P-values are also presented in the tables. Statistical significance for exploratory outcomes was defined as an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Adjustment and sensitivity analyses: The primary analysis was performed in the complete-case cohort and was not based on propensity score matching. Because treatment was selected by guardians, multivariable regression and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using the propensity score were prespecified as sensitivity analyses to reduce measured confounding while retaining the enrolled cohort. Propensity-score models included age, sex, body weight, time from symptom onset to treatment, baseline body temperature, baseline FLU-PRO score, current-season influenza vaccination, prior-season influenza history, and household exposure to influenza-like illness. For virological endpoints, baseline Ct value was additionally included in outcome models: Cox proportional-hazards models were used for Ct-defined viral clearance, and linear regression was used for viral-load AUC. PSM was not used to create the analytic cohort because matching can discard eligible participants and may not improve covariate balance in all settings; therefore, PSM was retained only as a post-hoc supplementary robustness check rather than as the basis for inference [21].

Accordingly, the complete enrolled cohort, rather than a matched subset, was used for primary inference and for the main tables. Regression adjustment and IPTW were selected as sensitivity approaches because they retained participants and addressed measured confounding without creating a new matched cohort. No enrolled child was excluded from the main analysis because of lack of a propensity-score match. After IPTW, standardized mean differences for all measured baseline covariates were < 0.10, indicating acceptable balance for measured covariates in the weighted sensitivity analysis.

Time-to-event analyses and subgroup analyses: Fever resolution and Ct-defined viral clearance were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method based on participant-level event-time data from the study database. Curves were not reconstructed from medians, interquartile ranges, or other summary statistics. For fever resolution, the event was the first time after treatment at which axillary temperature decreased to < 37.3 °C and remained below this threshold for 24 h. Participants without documented fever resolution during follow-up were censored at the last available temperature assessment. For viral clearance, the event was the first post-treatment sampling time at which the Ct value reached ≥ 35 and remained negative at a subsequent sample; participants without confirmed clearance by day 7 were censored at their last virological assessment. Between-group curves were compared using the log-rank test, and number-at-risk tables were displayed at prespecified time points. Prespecified subgroup analyses of fever duration evaluated age (< 8 vs. ≥8 years, consistent with Fig. 3), sex, time from symptom onset to treatment (≤ 24 h vs. >24 h), baseline temperature (< 39.0 °C vs. ≥39.0 °C), and current-season vaccination status. Interaction was evaluated separately for each subgroup variable by adding a treatment-by-subgroup product term to the fever-duration model; the P value for that product term was reported as the treatment-by-subgroup interaction P value.

Missing data

In this prospective cohort study, data attrition was limited despite standardized follow-up procedures including diary cards, scheduled visits, and telephone reminders. The overall proportion of missing data for the primary outcome (fever duration) was negligible (< 1%). For secondary outcomes, missing values were observed mainly in laboratory parameters (e.g., viral load at day 7 and inflammatory markers) because of insufficient specimen volume or withdrawal from blood sampling, with missing rates of approximately 4.5% in the baloxavir group and 5.2% in the oseltamivir group. No missing data were recorded for the 14-day revisit rate or parental work-absence days. The primary analysis was conducted in the final primary-outcome analytic cohort of 375 participants per group; secondary analyses used available measurements for each endpoint, with multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets used as a sensitivity analysis for outcomes with missing values.

The imputed results were consistent in direction with the available-case analyses, supporting the robustness of the main findings despite limited missing secondary laboratory data.

Results

Participant flow

A total of 1,250 children with suspected influenza A infection were screened for eligibility. Five hundred children were not included because screening identified at least one non-inclusion reason, including RT-PCR negativity for influenza A, presentation more than 48 h after symptom onset, suspected severe influenza requiring immediate hospitalization, exclusionary comorbidity or drug allergy, prior use of anti-influenza antivirals, guardian refusal, or inability to comply with follow-up. These 500 children were excluded before treatment selection and were not unmatched participants removed after enrollment. The operational screening log did not retain mutually exclusive category-specific exclusion counts or baseline covariates for non-enrolled children; therefore, baseline characteristics can be reported only for the enrolled analytic cohort, and this deficiency is acknowledged as a reporting limitation. Because no PSM-defined analytic cohort was used, there was no separate original unmatched cohort to compare with a matched cohort. No eligible enrolled participant was removed by propensity score matching. The final primary-outcome analytic cohort comprised 750 children, with 375 in the baloxavir marboxil group and 375 in the oseltamivir group according to the prespecified equal-size analytic target. This 1:1 distribution reflected the prospective parallel enrollment cap and was not generated by post hoc matching. The participant flow diagram is shown in Figure S1.

Primary and secondary efficacy outcomes

Clinical Symptom Relief: The median duration of fever was 22.0 h in the baloxavir marboxil group and 40.5 h in the oseltamivir group (P < 0.001). Times to diary-defined relief of cough, sore throat, and fatigue were also shorter in the baloxavir marboxil group (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.001) (Table 2). Exploratory outcome results include P-values adjusted for the false discovery rate (FDR) to account for multiple testing. Sensitivity analyses of the primary and key secondary outcomes using multivariable adjustment and inverse probability of treatment weighting were directionally consistent with the complete-case comparisons (Supplementary Table S1).

Virological Response: No significant difference was observed in baseline Ct values between groups (P = 0.721). The median Ct value in the baloxavir marboxil group increased from 21.5 at baseline to 34.8 at 24 h post-treatment, whereas the median Ct value in the oseltamivir group increased from 21.3 to 26.4, indicating a larger early decline in viral RNA load in the baloxavir group (FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). The median time to viral clearance was 24.0 h in the baloxavir group and 72.0 h in the oseltamivir group (FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). Viral load AUC from day 0 to day 7 was also lower in the baloxavir group (4.2 vs. 5.8 log10 copies × day; FDR-adjusted P < 0.001) (Table 3). Results remained in the same direction in Cox and linear-model sensitivity analyses that adjusted for baseline Ct value. Influenza A subtype distribution and PA/I38 reduced-susceptibility substitutions were not measured. Therefore, subtype-specific viral clearance, sequence-confirmed resistance, and resistance-related viral rebound or prolonged shedding could not be evaluated; participants without confirmed Ct-defined clearance by day 7 were handled as censored or not-cleared observations according to the time-to-event and endpoint definitions, not as confirmed resistance cases. The dynamic changes in median Ct values over the 7-day follow-up period are shown in Fig. 2.

Subgroup Analysis of Fever Duration: Prespecified subgroup analysis showed that the observed reduction in fever duration in favor of baloxavir was consistent across subgroups defined by age (< 8 years vs. ≥8 years, consistent with Fig. 3), sex, time from symptom onset to treatment (≤ 24 h vs. >24 h), baseline temperature (< 39.0 °C vs. ≥39.0 °C), and current-season influenza vaccination status. Incidence of Complications: At 14-day follow-up, 14 children (3.7%) in the baloxavir group and 42 children (11.2%) in the oseltamivir group developed prespecified complications (FDR-adjusted P = 0.042). Pneumonia (1.1% vs. 3.2%) and febrile seizures (0.5% vs. 2.9%) were less frequent in the baloxavir group in exploratory comparisons (Table 5). No cases of acute necrotizing encephalopathy requiring intensive care were observed in either group. The study did not routinely distinguish primary viral influenza pneumonia from secondary bacterial pneumonia, and complication findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than proof of causal prevention.

Drug safety analysis

A total of 25 children (6.7%) in the baloxavir group experienced adverse drug reactions, all Grade 1 or 2. In the oseltamivir group, 57 children (15.2%) experienced adverse drug reactions, including three cases that led to early treatment discontinuation because of Grade 3 vomiting. Gastrointestinal events were more frequent in the oseltamivir group: vomiting (1.9% vs. 9.6%, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001), diarrhea (0.8% vs. 4.8%, FDR-adjusted P = 0.001), and nausea (1.3% vs. 4.0%, FDR-adjusted P = 0.028). Transient dysgeusia occurred in five children (1.3%) in the baloxavir group and resolved without intervention. Fewer than 2% of children in each group had transient mild ALT elevation not exceeding 3 times the upper limit of normal, and no serious liver or kidney injury attributable to study drugs was observed (Table 8).

Discussion

In this multicenter community-based prospective cohort, baloxavir was associated with faster recovery than oseltamivir across several clinical, virological, and patient-centered measures in children aged 5–14 years with RT-PCR-confirmed influenza A. The clearest finding was shorter fever duration, while concordant differences in diary-defined symptom relief and viral RNA kinetics support a potentially more rapid early treatment response. Lower complication, healthcare-utilization, and adverse-reaction rates may also be clinically relevant, although these secondary findings were exploratory and susceptible to residual confounding and open-label outcome assessment. Overall, the results extend the evidence base for baloxavir in routine pediatric outpatient care while requiring confirmation in randomized or rigorously adjusted comparative studies.

Duration of fever was the primary endpoint. The observed median fever duration was 22.0 h with baloxavir and 40.5 h with oseltamivir, an approximately 18.5-hour difference. This direction is consistent with several Chinese real-world studies. In a retrospective pediatric cohort from Shanghai, Ge et al. reported a shorter mean fever-resolution time with baloxavir than with oseltamivir after matching (1.44 vs. 2.29 days) [22]. In a multicenter East China outpatient study, Cai et al. reported median times to fever alleviation of 18 versus 30 h [23], and a Beijing pediatric retrospective study reported a median fever-clearance time of 1 day with baloxavir versus 2 days with oseltamivir capsules and 1.5 days with oseltamivir granules [24]. However, the magnitude of benefit has not been uniform. The difference observed here also exceeds the generally smaller clinical differences reported in miniSTONE-2 and the adult/adolescent CAPSTONE trials [15–17]. The Japanese pediatric active-controlled trial by Ishiguro et al. found similar fever resolution (22.0 vs. 23.1 h) despite faster illness alleviation with baloxavir [25], and a Japanese subtype-specific observational study found no significant multivariable difference for A(H1N1)pdm09 and similar fever durations across antiviral groups for A(H3N2) [26]. A pediatric meta-analysis combining randomized and cohort studies likewise found no significant overall difference in fever duration [27]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in age, influenza subtype, treatment timing, fever definitions, antipyretic use, and outcome ascertainment; therefore, the relatively large fever-duration difference in the present cohort requires confirmation.

Diary-defined relief of cough, sore throat, and fatigue was also faster in the baloxavir group. This finding is directionally consistent with the multicenter observational study by Cai et al., which reported shorter times to alleviation of influenza symptoms and shorter durations of several individual symptoms with baloxavir [23], and with the Japanese pediatric trial by Ishiguro et al., which reported shorter times to illness alleviation and sustained symptom resolution [25]. In contrast, Ge et al. found no significant between-group differences in the resolution of nasal congestion or rhinorrhea, sore throat, or cough despite a shorter fever-resolution time [22]. Differences in symptom definitions, age distribution, follow-up schedules, and caregiver reporting may account for these variable results. In the present open-label study, guardians knew the assigned therapy, so both diary-derived symptom outcomes and FLU-PRO score changes remain susceptible to ascertainment bias.

The virological findings showed an early increase in Ct value at 24 h, shorter time to Ct-defined viral clearance, and lower viral RNA AUC in the baloxavir group. This pattern is biologically plausible because baloxavir inhibits the cap-dependent endonuclease required early in viral replication [14] and is consistent with the more rapid viral-load reduction observed in CAPSTONE-1 and CAPSTONE-2 [15, 17]. More recent pediatric evidence is also supportive: Ishiguro et al. reported a shorter time to first cessation of infectious virus shedding (48.0 vs. 192.0 h) and a greater day-2 reduction in infectious viral titer with baloxavir [25], while a pediatric meta-analysis found greater reductions in viral titer and RNA load with baloxavir than with oseltamivir [27]. Hospitalized adult data reported by Shah et al. [28] are less directly comparable to this community pediatric cohort. Importantly, Ct values measure viral RNA rather than infectious virus, and specimen quality, assay platform, baseline Ct value, viral subtype, and reduced-susceptibility substitutions may influence apparent clearance. PA/I38 substitutions have been reported after baloxavir treatment in pediatric studies [29]. Because sequencing was not performed and viral rebound was not prospectively adjudicated, resistance-mediated rebound or prolonged shedding cannot be excluded.

FLU-PRO was used to evaluate multidomain disease severity at baseline, 24 h, 72 h, and day 7. The larger 24-hour percentage reduction and higher proportion of children reaching an item-summed score < 10 by day 7 suggest faster overall recovery with baloxavir. Nevertheless, the standard FLU-PRO scoring approach is commonly reported as mean domain or total scores, and the < 10 summed-score threshold used in this study was prespecified for descriptive near-resolution rather than validated as a Chinese pediatric normal cutoff [19, 20]. Future studies should validate pediatric Chinese scoring and thresholds before using them for clinical decision-making.

Subgroup analyses used separate treatment-by-subgroup interaction tests for age, sex, treatment-initiation timing, baseline temperature, and current-season vaccination status. For each variable, the reported interaction P value corresponded to the product term between treatment group and that subgroup variable; all product-term P values were > 0.05. Thus, the data did not provide statistical evidence that the baloxavir-oseltamivir difference in fever duration varied across the prespecified subgroup categories. However, nonsignificant interaction tests do not establish identical effects, particularly because exploratory subgroup analyses may have limited power. No subtype-specific interaction analysis could be performed because A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2) were not distinguished.

Influenza-related complications were recorded less frequently in the baloxavir group, including pneumonia and febrile seizures. Real-world studies provide some supportive but not definitive context. In a Japanese health-insurance claims database, hospitalization was more frequent among oseltamivir-treated than baloxavir-treated outpatients (risk ratio, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.00–2.00), and additional anti-influenza drug use was also higher with oseltamivir [30]. Conversely, a multicenter Chinese cohort of children aged 1 to < 5 years found hospitalization to be uncommon in both treatment groups [31]. These studies differed in age, data source, outcome definitions, and confounding control and did not establish that baloxavir prevents complications. In the present study, open-label ascertainment, differences in revisit behavior and imaging thresholds, and the inability to routinely distinguish primary viral from secondary bacterial pneumonia further limit causal interpretation. Randomized trials and carefully adjusted real-world studies are needed to determine whether faster viral suppression translates into fewer pediatric complications.

Inflammatory and immune markers, including CRP, IL-6, and the CD4+/CD8 + ratio, appeared to normalize more rapidly in the baloxavir group. These exploratory biomarker findings are compatible with the hypothesis that faster viral suppression reduces ongoing antigenic stimulation. However, the clinical significance of short-term differences in these markers in uncomplicated pediatric influenza is uncertain, and measurements may reflect sampling timing, baseline disease intensity, concomitant antipyretic or antibiotic use, and other unmeasured host factors. These results should therefore be considered supportive rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Healthcare resource utilization and family burden were lower in the baloxavir group, as shown by fewer revisits, less additional antibiotic use, and fewer parental work-absence days. This direction is consistent with a United States prospective outpatient survey reporting lower household transmission and healthcare resource utilization with baloxavir than with oseltamivir [32] and with the Japanese claims-database findings of less hospitalization and additional anti-influenza drug use among baloxavir-treated patients [30]. Nevertheless, treatment selection and affordability may confound these outcomes. Families choosing a newer single-dose drug may differ in socioeconomic status, health literacy, ability to miss work, access to follow-up, and care-seeking thresholds. These factors should be measured directly in future health-economic evaluations.

Safety findings favored baloxavir, with fewer reported adverse drug reactions and fewer gastrointestinal events than oseltamivir. This direction is consistent with miniSTONE-2 [16], the Shanghai pediatric real-world study by Ge et al., which reported lower nausea and vomiting rates with baloxavir [22], and a pediatric meta-analysis that found a lower overall incidence of adverse events with baloxavir [27]. Current prescribing information lists vomiting and diarrhea among adverse events reported in pediatric subjects treated with baloxavir [18], and recent pediatric trial data in infants support continued safety surveillance [33]. Transient dysgeusia occurred only in the baloxavir group and resolved spontaneously. Safety interpretation in this study is based on the approved weight-based baloxavir regimen administered and verified against source records.

The strengths of this study include its multicenter community setting, prospective follow-up, large sample size, standardized symptom diaries, serial RT-PCR testing, participant-level time-to-event analysis, and collection of family-burden outcomes. Major limitations include non-randomized guardian-selected treatment, absence of blinding, potential selection and ascertainment bias, incomplete measurement of socioeconomic status, health literacy, and exact out-of-pocket costs, inability to provide category-specific exclusion counts or baseline characteristics for non-enrolled screened children, lack of influenza A subtype data, absence of PA/I38 sequencing or virus culture, lack of a prospectively defined viral-rebound or prolonged-shedding endpoint, limited ability to distinguish viral from bacterial pneumonia, use of a nonvalidated FLU-PRO summed-score threshold, and 14-day follow-up that may miss late complications. Because the 1:1 cohort resulted from a prospective enrollment cap and not from randomization or matching, the equal group size should not be interpreted as evidence of exchangeability between groups. Consequently, all findings beyond the primary comparison should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future double-blind randomized controlled trials with subtype testing, resistance surveillance, detailed screening logs, cost data, and at least 21–28 days of follow-up are needed.

For these reasons, the magnitude of several observed associations—including fever duration, viral clearance, complication rates, and adverse-event rates—may partly reflect residual confounding inherent to treatment preference, open-label outcome assessment, socioeconomic factors, treatment affordability, and healthcare-seeking behavior. These effect estimates should not be interpreted as direct treatment effects unless confirmed in randomized, blinded trials.

Conclusions

In this community-based prospective cohort of children aged 5–14 years with uncomplicated influenza A, baloxavir marboxil was associated with faster fever resolution than a standard five-day oseltamivir course. Exploratory analyses suggested faster diary-defined symptom relief, more rapid decline in viral RNA, lower FLU-PRO disease-severity scores, fewer recorded complications, lower healthcare utilization, and fewer adverse drug reactions, particularly gastrointestinal events.

These observations require cautious interpretation because the study was non-randomized and open-label, treatment was selected by guardians, and important virological variables such as influenza A subtype and PA/I38 reduced-susceptibility substitutions were not assessed. Causality cannot be inferred. Shared decision-making should weigh the practical advantages of single-dose baloxavir against the established efficacy and safety record of oseltamivir and individual patient characteristics. Future randomized, blinded pediatric trials with extended follow-up, subtype-specific analyses, resistance monitoring, predefined viral-rebound and prolonged-shedding endpoints, and participant-level time-to-event analyses are warranted.
