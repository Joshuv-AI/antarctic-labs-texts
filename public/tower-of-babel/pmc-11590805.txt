Evaluating the effectiveness of the Smart About Meds (SAM) mobile application among patients discharged from hospital: protocol of a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Robyn Tamblyn, Bettina Habib, David L Buckeridge, Daniala L Weir, Elizaveta Frolova, Rolan Alattar, Jessica Rogozinsky, Caroline Beauchamp, Rosalba Pupo, Susan J Bartlett, Emily McDonald
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionAlmost half of patients discharged from hospital are readmitted or return to the emergency department (ED) within 90 days. Non-adherence to medication changes made during hospitalisation and the use of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) both contribute to postdischarge adverse events. We developed Smart About Meds (SAM), a patient-centred mobile application that targets medication non-adherence and PIMs use. This protocol describes a randomised controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate SAM.Methods and analysisA pragmatic, stratified RCT will evaluate SAM among 3250 adult patients discharged from hospital. At discharge, consenting participants will be randomised 1:1 to usual care or SAM. SAM integrates novel patient-centred features with pharmacist monitoring to manage non-adherence to new medication regimens. SAM also notifies patients of PIMs in their regimen, with advice to discuss with their physician.Following discharge, patients will be followed for 90 days to measure the primary composite outcome of ED visits, hospital readmissions and death. Secondary outcomes will include primary adherence to medication changes, secondary adherence to disease-modifying medications, patient empowerment and health-related quality of life.The primary outcome will be analysed according to intention-to-treat. Multivariable logistic regression will estimate differences between treatment groups in the proportion of patients experiencing the primary outcome and will assess modification of intervention effects by hospital, unit, age, sex and comorbidity burden. With a sample size of 3250, the study will have 80% power to detect a 5% absolute reduction in the primary outcome. Binary and continuous secondary outcomes will be assessed using multivariable logistic and linear regression, respectively.Ethics and disseminationThe Research Ethics Board of the McGill University Health Centre in Montréal, Canada has approved this study. Results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at scientific conferences. Introduction

Over 3 million hospitalisations and 17 million emergency department (ED) visits occur in Canada each year.1 2 Moreover, up to 35% of patients discharged from hospital are readmitted or return to the ED within 30 days.3,6 In a recent study, we found that this proportion increased to over 45% when follow-up was extended to 90 days postdischarge.6 Patient non-adherence to prescribed medications and the use of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) are modifiable risk factors that contribute to the risk of adverse events following hospital discharge.7,9 Targeting these risk factors is essential to any approach that aims to minimise preventable adverse events postdischarge.9 10

The prevalence of medication non-adherence varies by condition and study,11 12 with up to 30% of patients failing to fill a new prescription in some cases713,16 and 45% failing to take their filled medication as prescribed.17,21 Non-adherence is also a major concern following hospital discharge, when most patients have changes made to their drug regimen,22 with a mean 4.4 changes per patient.8 23 In a recent study of 2655 patients discharged from hospital, we found that 61% of changes were the addition of new medications, 24% were discontinuations, and 15% were dose changes.8 After discharge, 44% of patients were non-adherent to at least one change and 6% were non-adherent to all changes.8 10 Although there is limited evidence on the impact of patients not adhering to medication changes, one study showed it increased mortality risk after myocardial infarction by 44%.7 We also found that non-adherence to all changes was associated with a 35% increased risk of subsequent ED visits, unplanned readmissions or death.10 These data suggest that hospitalised patients are a vulnerable and important target group for interventions that improve adherence to appropriate changes made to their drug regimen.

There is a wide variety of adherence interventions, which vary in the medications, health conditions and patient populations they target.24,28 What most have in common, however, is that they are typically tailored to a specific drug class, have small to modest effect sizes and rarely improve clinical outcomes. One reason for this is may be that the majority employ only basic behavioural strategies that may be limited in their impact, such as pill organisers or medication reminders.24,28 It may be more effective to design interventions that directly target autonomy, motivation and confidence by integrating methods from motivational interviewing and self-determination theory.29,33 There is evidence to suggest that interventions that incorporate such methods have improved effectiveness, as has been observed in interventions that target weight loss, smoking cessation, and, in one instance, even medication adherence.30 34 35 However, due to the absence of reimbursement models, inadequate training of healthcare personnel, and resource intensity, interventions such as these have not been widely implemented.24 26

One medium which has emerged in recent years with the potential to change health behaviours by way of individualised support is that of mobile technologies.36 Over half of older adults owned a smartphone in the USA in 2019.37 However, there is insufficient scientific evaluation of the over 700 mobile apps that target medication management and are available to the public,38 39 the majority of which have not made full use of mobile platforms and offer only basic features such as refill requests, pill reminders and a display of manually entered medications.3840,43 This has created an opportunity to design and evaluate mobile apps that integrate multiple, advanced features that target medication adherence.

The benefits of adherence interventions may be undermined if they enhance adherence to PIMs. Defined as medications with greater harm than benefit and for which there are safer alternatives, PIMs are a preventable cause of adverse events that lead to ED visits, hospitalisations and death. PIM prevalence varies from 18% to 100% in different studies44,48 and is more common in older adults.45 In observational studies, patients with PIMs have up to a 78% increased hospitalisation risk.49,55 Systematic reviews of interventions to reduce PIMs suggest that pharmacist review and recommendations, computerised decision-support, academic detailing and other resource-intensive combined interventions can reduce PIMs.4856,58 However, for the vulnerable subgroup of hospitalised patients, there are particular challenges. Studies have shown that PIMs prevalence in hospitalised patients ranges from 25% to 82%, and that PIMs increase the risk of adverse events postdischarge.959,62 Importantly, over two-thirds of these PIMs are prescribed by community physicians.959,62 Although hospitalisation provides an opportunity for medication review, hospital physicians can be reluctant to discontinue medications started by other prescribers. Even when hospital physicians discontinue PIMs, up to 12% are restarted after discharge.63 64 Physicians also often cite patient resistance as a reason they do not deprescribe PIMs.65 However, deprescribing interventions have largely excluded patients as a target.66 In the few instances when they have targeted patients, interventions have had significant success.67

For these reasons and to maximise the benefit of adherence interventions, it is essential to simultaneously educate patients on the importance of discontinuing PIMs. This protocol, funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (grant number 470182), describes our planned randomised controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate Smart About Meds (SAM), an innovative, patient-centred medication management mobile application. By integrating novel user-centred features with pharmacist monitoring, SAM aims to tackle postdischarge non-adherence to new medication regimens to improve long-term outcomes in hospitalised patients. The app also notifies patients of PIMs in their regimen, with advice to discuss with their physician. Targeting non-adherence while raising patient awareness to PIMs may have a synergistic effect because elimination of PIMs will reduce the number of drugs in a patient’s regimen and associated costs—established barriers to medication adherence.2968,72

Trial design

A pragmatic, stratified RCT will be conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of SAM, a pilot-tested, patient-centred medication management mobile application. In this study, 3250 eligible and consenting adult patients discharged from internal medicine, cardiac care, cardiac surgery, vascular surgery and respiratory units of two academic medical hospitals in Montreal, Quebec, Canada will be randomised to the SAM intervention or to usual care. Following discharge from hospital, patients will be followed for 90 days, during which the occurrence of ED visits, hospital readmissions and death will be measured as the primary composite outcome. Primary adherence to medication changes, secondary adherence to disease-modifying medications, individual components of the primary composite outcome, patient empowerment and health-related quality of life will be measured as secondary outcomes.

Data sources

RAMQ Bridge

The RAMQ Bridge is an established secure, real-time link to the provincial health insurer (Régie de l’assurance maladie du Québec—RAMQ) through which individual patient data will be retrieved on all insured services for consenting patients, including physician claims data, pharmacy claims data and patient demographic information. Physician claims data include information on all medical services provided by fee-for-service physicians in Québec (98% of all physicians) and include the date the service was provided, the location in which the service was provided (inpatient, ED, outpatient), the billed procedure (eg, consultation) and an ICD-9 code diagnosis for the service. Pharmacy claims data include information on all RAMQ-covered medications dispensed to those with provincial drug insurance coverage, which includes those 65 or older, those on welfare and those who lack insurance through their employer (ie, approximately 50% of the population). Pharmacy claims data include the drug identification number of the dispensed medication (which provides information on the drug molecule and strength), the date on which the medication was dispensed, the duration of the filled prescription, the quantity dispensed and the pharmacy that dispensed the medication.

Discharge prescription

The hospital discharge prescription will be retrieved for all consenting patients from their hospital medical record. The prescription lists all medications prescribed to the patient at discharge, with each medication classified by the prescribing attending physician as a new medication, modified community medication, discontinued community medication or continued community medication. Study pharmacists will enter discharge prescription information into an electronic tool developed by our team, which consists of structured data entry fields for the medication brand name and drug molecule, as well as the prescribed dosage, route, frequency, duration, number of refills and meal directives.

Participant characteristics

During enrolment, trained research assistants will obtain information on consenting patients’ age, sex, gender, mother tongue, presence of a caregiver, whether or not the patient lives alone, availability of a family physician and highest attained level of education. These data, along with other information such as hospital, unit, discharge date and treatment group will be recorded using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure web application for data collection.

Vigilance Santé

Vigilance Santé is a bilingual commercial drug knowledge database that provides information on all drug products approved for use in Canada and Quebec, including their ingredient, strength, route of administration, format, RAMQ-coverage status, over-the-counter status, cost, side effects, interactions, pill images and patient-friendly drug monographs.

Intervention

Control group

Patients randomised to the control group will receive usual care at discharge. On study units, usual care consists of medication reconciliation, which is conducted for all patients by the unit pharmacist. Medication reconciliation consists of obtaining the community medication list from the patient’s community pharmacy, validating and reconciling (comparing) the list with admission orders, and recommending changes as needed to the care team based on medication interactions, acute medical diagnoses and any changes to kidney function. At discharge, the community drug list is again reconciled with medications administered in hospital and the discharge prescription is generated by the care team, often classifying each medication as a new medication, dose modification, discontinued therapy or continued community medication. The discharge prescription is provided to the patient or their family, and can be faxed to the community pharmacy on request. Patients fill their discharge prescription at their community pharmacy of choice. If there are questions about changes to the community drug list, the community pharmacist may ask the patient, and if not clear, may contact the discharging care team.

Intervention group

Patients randomised to the intervention group will receive, in addition to usual care, training in and access to the SAM mobile app just prior to discharge. SAM, which is available in both English and French, was developed through a user-centred design development process and with input from patients and caregivers.73 The app leverages the Information–Motivation–Behavioural Skills (IMB) Model, a three-factor model of behaviour change that targets patient adherence.29 The model integrates key concepts from classic health behaviour models (Theory of Planned Behaviour, Self-Determination Theory and Health Belief Model) to advance interventions targeting behaviour change. In brief, IMB posits that successful behaviour change requires individualised interventions to address patients’ IMB skills needs. For medication adherence, patients must first understand their condition and how prescribed medication(s) help manage symptoms (information), must be committed to adhering to recommended therapy (motivation) and have the skills and support that facilitate long-term adherence (behavioural skills). SAM retrieves medications prescribed to a patient from the discharge prescription, and RAMQ-covered medications dispensed before and after admission via the RAMQ Bridge. SAM uses these data to display a continuously updated list of prescribed and dispensed medications and provides patients and caregivers with tools to address knowledge gaps, reduce uncertainty and identify and support behaviours needed to use appropriate medications as prescribed (figure 1).

Information

Medication list and pill images

SAM displays a continuously updated list of medications prescribed at discharge and RAMQ-covered medications that are dispensed postdischarge. Medications are grouped by patient-friendly labelled therapeutic class (eg, blood pressure treatments and blood clot treatments) based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification. Pill images of dispensed medications are obtained from Vigilance Santé and displayed alongside medication names to help patients and caregivers identify the drugs they are taking.

Drug information

Uncertainty about medications is pervasive, as most older patients with multiple medications do not know which of their medications are used for which problems.74 Improved awareness about the role medications play in managing long-term health conditions is essential to reducing ambivalence and resistance to continued use. SAM provides patient-friendly monographs, obtained from Vigilance Santé, to help patients and caregivers understand the indications for treatment, as well as the harms and benefits of their medications.

Interaction checker

Drug–drug interactions are among patients’ top medication-related informational needs.75 This feature uses Vigilance Santé data to generate a list of drug–drug interactions between medications in the patient’s list and any over-the-counter medications they consider purchasing. The severity of each interaction is also displayed, with interactions ranked from most to least severe.

Motivation

Adherence monitoring and feedback

Many patients receive only a cursory review of changes to their drug regimen, which contributes to non-adherence.75,81 This feature alerts patients, caregivers and study pharmacists to adherence problems with the patient’s new regimen. The discharge prescription, retrieved from the patient’s hospital medical record, is linked to dispensed medication data, retrieved through the RAMQ Bridge. Decision algorithms generate alerts on: (1) prescribed medications that are not filled, (2) prescribed medications that are filled at the wrong dose and (3) discontinued medications that are refilled. Alerts are not generated for medications that are not covered by the RAMQ. Once an alert is generated, patients can resolve it by selecting, from a drop-down menu, an explanation for non-adherence (eg, ‘I am worried about the side effects of this medication’). This prompts communication with the study pharmacist, who can follow-up with the patient or caregiver to resolve the issue.

Side effect checker

Patients often discontinue medications due to fear of or experience with side effects.2968,71 However, when taking multiple medications, it is not obvious which drug, if any, may be contributing to new symptoms. To assist patients in understanding which drug(s) may be implicated in a new symptom, SAM uses the Vigilance Santé database to identify and display possible side effects for each prescribed and dispensed medication and their frequency of occurrence, ranking them in order of likelihood.

PIM alerts

To reduce patient resistance to medication deprescribing, SAM alerts patients to PIMs in their drug list. This feature identifies PIMs in the patient’s medication list using the Beers, STOPP and Choosing Wisely criteria.82,86 For each identified PIM, the feature provides patients with patient-centred deprescribing brochures that are freely available through the Canadian Deprescribing Network,87 and suggests that they review the need for these medications with their community-based prescriber.

Behavioural skills and support

Pharmacist connect

To address medication ambivalence and misinformation, SAM empowers patients and caregivers to seek relevant information ‘as needed’ and for shared decision-making. This feature connects patients and caregivers with study pharmacists through a secured messaging service in the app. Patients can ask for information, describe concerns and side effects, ask about interactions and therapy changes and receive support for problem-solving.

Pharmacist dashboard

The dashboard allows study pharmacists to manage a group of patients, view patients’ prescribed and dispensed medications, view medication-related information such as RAMQ coverage status, receive and respond to questions from patients and caregivers, manage alerts or potential medication-related problems, transmit and manage requests for consultation from treating physicians and pharmacists, and document services.

Social connect

Patients value the opportunity to connect with patients with similar problems and treatments.88,90 Having access to the experiences of patients prescribed the same medication reduces social isolation and increases medication self-efficacy and autonomy in platforms such as PatientsLikeMe.91 This feature increases social support by providing opportunities for patients to share medication experiences and strategies to increase adherence. For each medication prescribed or dispensed to a patient, the patient can rate the medication on a scale of 1–5 stars and can add a ‘review’ of the medication, describing their experience. All other patients who were prescribed or dispensed the same medication will be able to read that review, and any other reviews other patients have made.

Caregiver connect

Caregivers play an important role in medication management.92,94 This platform allows patients to identify and enrol one or more caregivers, colocated or at a distance, who can access the patient’s medication profile in SAM and all the app’s features, provide emotional support and share ideas to facilitate medication management.

Weekly medication schedule

The discharge prescription is used to create a weekly schedule, listing which drugs to take each day and at what time. The schedule allows patients to select the times of day that work best for their lifestyle.

Pill reminders

Patients or caregivers can set-up reminders for taking their medications. This enables patients to control alerts for aspects of their medication regimen where it will be most valuable (eg, once-a-week medication, alternate day dosing).

Patient and public Involvement

Patients, caregivers and pharmacists have been involved in the development of the SAM app and the pharmacist dashboard. They have identified the features they believe would be most useful in medication management, have been engaged in the design of the application interfaces and have assessed its usability and suggested modifications that would make it easier to use and fit into their respective workflows.

Pilot data

A pilot RCT of SAM was conducted among patients discharged from two internal medicine units of the McGill University Health Centre in Montreal, Quebec, Canada.95 Between June 2019 and March 2020, we enrolled 51 patients, 24 of whom were randomised to the intervention group and 27 to the control group. Following randomisation, two withdrew consent, leaving 49 patients available for analysis.

Usability assessments and a questionnaire designed using the Technology Assessment Model (TAM) found SAM to be both usable and accepted by patient and caregiver users.73 During usability assessments, 23 patients and eight caregivers conducted a standardised set of tasks in SAM. User performance was observed to determine the ease with which tasks were completed, rated on a scale of 1 to 4 where 1 was ‘user can easily complete the task with no help’ and 4 was ‘user cannot complete the task, even with help’. Users completed tasks successfully 98% of the time, generally with little difficulty (mean difficulty score 1.4). This suggests that SAM is usable. Results of the TAM questionnaire, administered 2 weeks postdischarge and completed by 12 users, also suggest that SAM was accepted and valued by users: on a Likert scale of 1–7 where 1 was ‘completely disagree’ and 7 was ‘completely agree’, users reported a mean score of 5.65 on SAM’s perceived ease of use and 5.29 on SAM’s perceived usefulness.

A preliminary analysis of SAM’s effectiveness in improving adherence and reducing adverse event risk was also conducted.95 Analysis of pharmacy claims data of the 30 days postdischarge suggested that intervention patients adhered to a higher proportion of medication changes (84%) compared with control patients (78%). This included new medications (73% adhered to in the intervention group vs 62% in the control group) and dose changes (91% adhered to in the intervention group vs 82% in the control group). Furthermore, analyses of medical services claims data suggested that a smaller proportion of patients in the intervention group (9%) were readmitted to hospital in the 30 days postdischarge compared with the control group (15%).

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Permuted block randomisation of block sizes of 6 and 8 will be used to randomise 3250 patients 1:1 to the control group or the SAM intervention group. Randomisation will be stratified by hospital and unit to ensure balance between treatment groups in the type and quality of care patients receive. The allocation sequence will be generated by a data analyst using Statistical Analysis Software (SAS) and will be implemented using the randomisation module in REDCap, a secure web application for data collection.

Given the nature of the intervention, study participants and personnel cannot be blinded to group allocation but will be blinded to study outcomes to minimise any impact on medication-taking and prescribing behaviours, beyond what might be attributed to the intervention itself. Data analysts will also be blinded to study group allocation to minimise bias in the analysis of trial data.

The study population will include 3250 patients discharged from internal medicine, cardiac care, cardiac surgery, vascular surgery and respiratory units of the Montreal General Hospital and the Royal Victoria Hospital in Montreal, Quebec, Canada. To be eligible, patients must be covered by the provincial health insurance and drug insurance plans. Patients must also be 18 years or older at admission, own a tablet or smartphone with an internet connection, have at least one medication prescribed at discharge and speak and read either English or French. Patients transferred to a non-study unit or discharged to a rehabilitation or long-term care centre will be excluded.

Patients who are cognitively impaired or unable to provide consent will be included if surrogate consent is granted by a legally authorised representative, and if a caregiver consents to participate and use SAM on the patient’s behalf. Similarly, patients dependent on a caregiver but capable of providing consent will be enrolled only if the caregiver consents to participate and use SAM.

Participant enrolment

Research assistants trained in Good Clinical Practice will enrol all eligible and consenting patients from participating hospital units. Eligibility will be assessed in coordination with hospital nurses and the patients themselves. Newly admitted patients will be approached on a daily basis to ensure that all potentially eligible patients are assessed. Research assistants will document patient eligibility, consent and baseline characteristics in REDCap, will obtain a signed informed consent form from consenting participants (online supplemental material), will randomise consenting patients using REDCap and will train those assigned to the intervention group in use of the SAM app.

Trial outcomes

Primary outcome

Composite of ED visits, hospital readmissions and deaths occurring in the 90 days postdischarge will be identified using RAMQ medical services claims retrieved through the RAMQ Bridge. In Quebec, physicians must record the location of the services they provide (eg, ED, inpatient unit) to be remunerated on a fee-for-service basis (more than 95% of physicians). These data are timely and accurate in measuring hospitalisation occurrence compared with the provincial hospitalisation database.96,98 Patients will be classified as having an ED visit or hospital readmission if they received a service whose location is recorded as an ED or inpatient hospital unit, respectively. Postdischarge deaths will be retrieved from the RAMQ beneficiary database. Patients who experience any one of an ED visit, hospital readmission or death in the 90 days postdischarge will be classified as having experienced the binary composite outcome.

Secondary outcomes

Individual components of the primary composite outcome include ED visits, hospital readmissions and deaths occurring in the 90 days postdischarge, each of which will be measured separately as a binary secondary outcome.

Non-adherence to medication changes is defined as a failure to fill a new prescription within 30 days of discharge, filling a modified prescription at the incorrect dose or filling any discontinued medication in 30 days. To measure primary non-adherence, the patient’s discharge prescription will be linked to medications dispensed postdischarge, based on daily updates of prescription claims from the RAMQ Bridge. Each medication change on the patient’s prescription will then be given a binary classification of whether or not it was adhered to postdischarge.

Secondary adherence: disease-modifying medications are defined as those that prevent health deterioration and typically need to be taken on a daily basis to be effective. A list of these medications will be compiled based on the WHO’s list of essential medicines.99 Secondary adherence will be measured for each disease-modifying medication that is filled using the medication possession ratio,100 which is calculated as the days of supply (based on the duration of dispensed prescription during follow-up) divided by the number of days of follow-up. Left-over supply prior to admission, when relevant, will be included in the calculation and only supply within the 90-day period for postdischarge dispensations will be included. In Quebec, over 94% of prescriptions are dispensed for 30 days or less,101 which should provide an accurate measure of persistence.

Patient and caregiver empowerment will be measured using the PROMIS Self-Efficacy Measure for Managing Medication and Treatments (PROMIS-SE Meds). This 8-item measure uses 5-point rating scales to assess patient confidence in managing medication schedules of varying complexity and in challenging situations, such as when running out of supply or when adverse effects occur.102 Scores are calibrated as T-scores (mean 50, SD 10), where higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy. PROMIS-SE Meds is reliable and valid across a range of chronic conditions,103 and has been translated and culturally validated in French Canadians. The online questionnaire will be administered 90 days postdischarge.

Patient health-related quality of life (HRQoL) will be measured using PROMIS-29, which measures health status across seven domains (physical function, fatigue, pain, depressive symptoms, anxiety, ability to participate in social roles and activities and sleep disturbance).104 Responses will be converted into a single summary score that reflects HRQoL. The online questionnaire will be administered 90 days postdischarge.

Sample size

In our recent RCT on medication reconciliation of 3491 patients, 48% of patients in the control group experienced an ED visit, hospital readmission or death in 90 days postdischarge.6 Assuming a similar proportion will experience this outcome in the control group of the SAM RCT, to detect the smallest clinically relevant reduction of 5% (from 48% to 43%), with a type 1 error of 5% and a type II error of 20%, using a two-sided test, 1556 patients will be required per treatment group, yielding a sample size of 3112.

Since over 99% of participants are expected to remain in the province during follow-up, we expect less than 1% loss to follow-up resulting from loss of access to medical services claims data used to measure this outcome. In addition, based on our SAM pilot RCT, we expect 3% of patients to withdraw consent following randomisation.95 For the purposes of sample size calculation, we have assumed an overall 4% loss to follow-up rate. To account for this, we need to enrol 3250 patients. This sample size would provide >80% power to detect an absolute reduction of 5% in the secondary outcome of non-adherence to medication changes, which was experienced by 44% of patients in our recent study of 2655 patients.8,10

Analyses

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population and primary and secondary outcomes, and evaluate whether randomisation produced a balance in patient characteristics between study arms. In the intervention group, we will evaluate the frequency of use of SAM by patients and/or caregivers, responses to alerts and use of the pharmacy connect feature.

An intention-to-treat analysis will evaluate the effectiveness of SAM. Multivariable logistic regression will estimate differences between treatment groups in the proportion of patients with an ED visit, hospital readmission or death in 90 days postdischarge. Strata used in the randomisation process will be included as binary variables to improve precision. Two-way interaction terms will test hypothesised modifiers of the effectiveness of SAM, using the Wald statistic and likelihood ratio test and Akaike information criterion to assess model fit. Hypothesised effect modifiers include hospital site, unit type, age, sex, gender, comorbidity burden, caregiver participation and frequency of SAM use.

Multivariable logistic regression will also be used to assess binary secondary outcomes, including the proportion of patients non-adherent to at least one medication change. We will also examine adherence using medication as the unit of analysis, where the outcome will be adherence to medication changes, and clustering of medications within patient will be accounted for using generalised estimating equations logistic regression with an exchangeable correlation structure. Multivariable linear regression will be used to determine if there is a difference between treatment arms in continuous outcomes, including secondary adherence to disease modifying medications, patient empowerment (PROMIS-SE Meds scores) and HRQoL (PROMIS29 scores). For secondary adherence, an overall value for each patient will be calculated by taking the arithmetic mean of adherence scores for each disease-modifying medication.

Data storage, privacy and confidentiality

An account in the SAM app will be created for each newly consented and enrolled patient using their first and last names. Data collected via the SAM mobile application and acquired from the RAMQ and patients’ hospital records will be stored on secure servers at the McGill Clinical and Health Informatics Research Group with daily backup. Patient identifying information will be replaced with unique encrypted numbers and the file linking these unique numbers to patients’ names will be accessible only by the Principal Investigator (PI) and the system administrator. All research staff involved in this project and who have access to study data will sign confidentiality agreements.

No data or information from the app will be accessible by third parties; only the patient, their chosen caregiver, the study pharmacist, the PI and the PI’s research team will have access to data in the app. Furthermore, the app will not be able to access any other apps on the patient’s (or caregiver’s) device, nor will it access any personal information besides that which has been entered into the app or has been obtained from the RAMQ or the patient’s discharge prescription.

The backend/server of the app will be hosted on the McGill University network. The network is a highly restricted environment with no access to the internet. The user accesses the app via a remote proxy server which acts as a mediator from the internet to the server. A robust firewall system restricts access to and from the server to the internet. There is also a campus-wide antivirus system that monitors network traffic and keeps our systems safe. User access directly to the server is highly restricted to the support staff and is logged.

Any research publications related to this study will present data in aggregate form only. Study data will be used for the exclusive purposes described in this protocol and will be retained for a period of 7 years following study completion, after which they will be destroyed.

Trial steering committee and data safety and monitoring board

The trial steering committee will consist of the PI, coinvestigators and key stakeholders. A stakeholder advisory group with representation from patients, caregivers, discharge nurses, community physicians and pharmacists will review and provide feedback to the trial steering committee on SAM, its implementation and communication plan.

The data safety and monitoring board (DSMB) will monitor trial safety and potential harms of SAM. We will conduct analyses every 12 months and provide the DSMB with information on participant safety. The trial will be terminated early if interim analyses reveal evidence of harm of SAM, that is, a 25% or more increase in ED visits, hospital readmissions or death in the intervention group compared with the control group.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol and trial documents have been approved by the Research Ethics Board (REB) of the McGill University Health Centre (MUHC), study number 2011-930.

Results of the RCT will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and will be presented at national and international conferences. If effective, SAM will also be made available to the general public in Apple and Android (Google Play) app stores. The study team will promote its implementation as an open-source service option provided by provincial PharmaNets and community-based pharmacies. To increase awareness of this service, we will present SAM to interest groups for older adults, such as the Canadian Association of Retired Persons, and at professional meetings such as the Canadian Association of Gerontology.
