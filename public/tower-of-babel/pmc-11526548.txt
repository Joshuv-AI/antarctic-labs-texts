Causal linkage of Graves’ disease with aging: Mendelian randomization analysis of telomere length and age-related phenotypes
Authors: Jingwen Hu, Jin Zhang, Yingshu Liu, Jiahui Qin, Haixia Bai, Xiaosong Qin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging is an irreversible progressive decline in physical function. Graves’ disease (GD) is a common cause of hyperthyroidism and is characterized by elevated levels of the thyroid hormone (TH). High TH levels are associated with aging and a shortened lifespan. The causal relationship between GD and aging has yet to be investigated.MethodsWe used genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore the causal link between GD and aging. To assess the statistical power of instrumental variables (IVs), F-statistics and R2 were used. MR analysis was conducted using inverse-variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were calculated to estimate the relative risk of GD to the outcomes. The Cochran Q test, I2, MR-PRESSO test, and MR-Egger regression intercept were calculated using statistical and leave-one-out analyses to test the heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and stability of the IVs on the outcomes.ResultsF-statistics of the five IVs were greater than 10, and the R2 values ranged from 0.033 to 0.156 (R2 > 0.01). According to the results of the IVW analysis, GD had no causal effect on facial aging (p = 0.189), age-related macular degeneration (p = 0.346), and Alzheimer’s disease (p = 0.479). There was a causal effect of GD on the remaining outcomes: telomere length (TL) (OR = 0.982; 95%CI:0.969–0.994; p = 0.004), senile cataract (OR = 1.031; 95%CI:1.002–1.060; p = 0.033), age-related hearing impairment (OR = 1.009; 95%CI:1.004–1.014; p = 0.001), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (OR = 1.055; 95%CI:1.008–1.103; p = 0.020), and sarcopenia (OR = 1.027; 95%CI:1.009–1.046; p = 0.004).ConclusionsGD accelerates the occurrence of age-related phenotypes including TL, senile cataracts, age-related hearing impairment, COPD, and sarcopenia. In contrast, there are no causal linkages between GD and facial aging, age-related macular degeneration, or Alzheimer’s disease. Further experimental studies could be conducted to elucidate the mechanisms by which GD facilitates aging, which could help slow down the progress of aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05379-2.

Introduction

With the global increase in life expectancy, research on aging is attracting interest. Aging is the gradual loss of tissue and organ function over time [1]. The substantial increase in the proportion of older persons poses unprecedented challenges to societies worldwide. The increased prevalence, poor prognosis, and longer hospital stays of age-related diseases result in reduced quality of life and enormous medical expenditures and have profound consequences for individuals, families, and the society [2]. The thyroid hormone (TH) plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism, growth, and development of many tissues in the body. In most tissues, TH can increase energy expenditure in a dose-dependent manner, a characteristic that is associated with accelerated kinetics and life processes [3, 4]. High TH levels are linked to aging and a shortened lifespan, and the lifespan of vertebrate species is generally associated with low metabolic rates and low TH levels [5, 6]. Qin et al. discovered that thyrotoxicosis resulted in aging-related characteristics such as malnutrition, immune senescence, and lipid toxicity in a mouse model [2].

Graves’ disease (GD) is a thyroid-specific autoimmune disease that is the primary cause of hyperthyroidism [7]. Autoimmune hyperthyroidism results from the presence of pathognomonic thyrotropin receptor autoantibodies (TRAb) that activate the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor in thyroid follicular cells, leading to the overproduction of TH [7, 8]. Approximately 0.5% of men and 3.0% of women develop GD during their lifetime [9]. The incidence peaks between the ages of 30 and 50 years; however, all ages are affected [7]. The pathogenesis of GD is complex and involves a wide range of genetic, endogenous, and predisposing environmental factors [10]. Symptoms and signs result from goiter and hyperthyroidism. Skin and orbital tissue manifestations outside the thyroid gland should not be ignored [11]. The diagnosis of GD is based on characteristic clinical symptoms and biochemical abnormalities, including elevated TH levels and decreased TSH levels. Other diagnostic tests for GD include the measurement of TRAb, radioactive iodine uptake, and Doppler thyroid ultrasound [12, 13].

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a powerful statistical method that uses genetic variation as a proxy (or instrumental variable) for exposure factors of interest to draw causal inferences between potentially modifiable exposures and outcomes [14]. MR analysis is not as biased as traditional observational epidemiology owing to confounding effects or reverse causality [14, 15].

Although GD may cause aging, the exact causal relationship between GD and aging has not yet been investigated. Therefore, in this study, we attempted to explore the causal link between GD and aging by using MR analysis. We selected telomere length (TL) and age-related phenotypes as outcome variables of aging. TL is at a maximum at birth and gradually decreases with age; therefore, it has been extensively studied as a biomarker of cellular aging and risk factor for age-related diseases [16, 17]. We included seven age-related phenotypes based on the site of GD involvement or a high incidence of age-related diseases: facial aging, age-related macular degeneration, senile cataract, age-related hearing impairment, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Alzheimer’s disease, and sarcopenia.

Materials and methods

Study design and the assumption of Mendelian randomization

First, we selected GD as an exposure factor, genetic instrumental variables (IVs) as a proxy for the exposure, and aging as an outcome to clarify whether there was a causal relationship between GD and aging. We then used the available genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to explore this relationship using two-sample MR methods. Finally, we evaluated the reliability of the MR results using a sensitivity analysis. The selection of genetic IVs is crucial for a successful MR analysis. To allow an unbiased estimation of the causal effect of the exposure factors on the outcome, the IVs must fulfill three core assumptions in our study: (i) IVs must be strongly associated with the exposure factor-GD; (ii) IVs must be independent of confounders, i.e., other factors that may influence the exposure-outcome relationship; and (iii) IVs must be associated with the outcome only through exposure, i.e., there must be no direct association due to horizontal pleiotropy. For outcome variables, we selected TL and seven age-related phenotypes, including facial aging, age-related macular degeneration, senile cataract, age-related hearing impairment, COPD, Alzheimer’s disease, and sarcopenia. In our study, TL refers to leukocyte TL. To investigate the markers of sarcopenia, we gathered an objective parameter for muscle mass loss: the appendicular lean mass [18]. A workflow chart illustrating our research process was shown in Fig. 1. Our study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [19].

Genetic instrumental variables selection

First, appropriate IVs associated with GD at the genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10–8) were identified. To ensure independence among single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), linkage disequilibrium clamping was performed with the r2 cutoff of 0.001 and a clumping window of 10,000 KB, palindromic SNPs were eliminated, and SNPs associated with the outcome with p < 5 × 10–8 were excluded. We then harmonized the exposure and outcome SNPs. In addition, we examined each IVs individually in PhenoScanner (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to ensure that the SNPs included did not affect the outcomes otherwise [26]. To assess the statistical power of the IVs, we used variance R2 and F-statistics [27]. The exposure variance ratio (R2) in MR studies refers to the proportion of the total variation in exposure explained by the IV. The formula used to calculate R2 for significant IVs is R2 = 2 × β2 × EAF×(1-EAF) [28, 29]. The F-statistic is calculated to estimate the strength of a single IV using the following formula: F = β2/SE2 [28]. EAF is the effect of allele frequency of exposure, β is the estimated genetic effect on GD, and SE is the standard error of the genetic effect. To minimize potential weak IVs bias, we considered an F statistic of at least 10 and R2 > 0.01 to be sufficient for MR analysis [28, 30]. Finally, five IVs were selected, the details of which were summarized in Table 2.

Mendelian randomization analysis

The inverse-variance weighting (IVW) method was used as the principal MR analytical approach in this study as it provided accurate causal estimates assuming that all IVs were valid [31]. Random effects rather than fixed effects were exploited because they accounted for the heterogeneity of the individual causal estimates [32]. We also performed several additional analyses to check the robustness of the results, these included MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode, which consider different types of genetic pleiotropy and were based on potentially different assumptions [33–35]. The estimated accuracy generated by the MR-Egger method is very low; however, the MR-Egger regression intercept can detect horizontal pleiotropy [33, 34]. The weighted median method allows for the correct estimation of causality when up to 50% of the IVs are invalid [33]. The weighted-mode method is used to obtain a single causal effect estimate from multiple genetic IVs. The weighted-mode approach assums the most frequently occurring association estimate was unaffected by pleiotropy, indicating that it must correspond to the true causal effect [36]. However, compared with IVW methods, the MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode methods have a reduced efficacy, as shown by the wide confidence interval (CI), and should only be used as supplementary methods for MR analysis in this study [37]. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were used to estimate the relative risk of caused by GD to the outcomes. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. When the effect estimates were in the same direction as IVW, other methods were considered to support the causal conclusion of the IVW method [19, 38]. For example, the results of IVW method were significant; even if the results from the other methods were not significant and no pleiotropy or heterogeneity was found, the results could be considered significant if the β values of the other methods were in the same direction [34, 39, 40].

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is critical in MR studies to evaluate potential heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. We used the heterogeneity marker of the IVW method (Cochran’s Q-derived p < 0.05) to indicate potential heterogeneity. The I2 statistic evaluates the degree of heterogeneity, with a value of I2 between 25% and 50% considered mild heterogeneity, between 50% and 75% considered moderate heterogeneity, and greater than 75% considered severe heterogeneity [41]. The formula for I2 is as follows: I2=(Q-df)/Q × 100%, Where Q is the Cochran heterogeneity statistic and df is the degree of freedom. A negative I2 value is equal to zero; thus, I2 ranges between 0% and 100% [41]. When the IVs strength is independent of the direct effects (InSIDE) hypothesis, the intercept obtained from the MR-Egger regression provides valid estimates even if all SNPS are invalid [42]. A zero intercept in the MR-Egger regression analysis (p > 0.05) indicates no horizontal pleiotropy. The MR-PRESSO method evaluates horizontal pleiotropy and corrects outliers for horizontal pleiotropy in MR analysis and consists of three components: (a) MR-PRESSO global test: detecting horizontal pleiotropy, p > 0.05 means no horizontal pleiotropy; (b) MR-PRESSO outlier test: correcting for horizontal pleiotropy via outlier removal; and (c) MR-PRESSO distortion test: testing of significant differences in the causal estimates before and after correction for outliers [43]. Based on the MR-PRESSO outlier test p-values, the IVs were sorted in ascending order and then removed individually. Each time an IV was removed from the list, the MR-PRESSO global test was performed on the remaining IVs. This was repeated until the p-value of the global test was not significant (p > 0.05). After removal of the pleiotropic IV, the remaining IVs were utilized for subsequent MR analysis [44]. Our initial approach utilized the MR-Egger regression intercept test to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. Then, we used the MR-PRESSO method to test horizontal pleiotropy and to rectify it by excluding outlier IV [43]. A leave-one-out analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each IV, and an IVW method was performed on the remaining IVs to assess the potential impact of specific IVs on the estimates (Fig. 3). Post hoc power calculations for MR analysis are available online (https://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/) [18, 28]. The effect sizes of the causal estimate outcomes were chosen as the effect sizes of the causal effect using the IVW method to compute the statistical power of the MR analysis.

All of the above analyses were performed using the Two Sample MR package (version 0.5.7) and MR-PRESSO (version 1.0) in R Studio (version 4.1.3). The details of the sensitivity analysis are shown in Fig. 3.

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample MR Analysis showed that the genetic prediction of the causal effect of GD on aging was possible using five SNPs as tools. The F-statistic of all IVs was greater than 10; R2 ranged from 0.033 to 0.156, indicating that IVs explained phenotypic variability ranging from 3.3 to 15.6% (Table 2). In the MR results obtained using the IVW method as the primary approach, GD had no causal effect on facial aging (p = 0.189), age-related macular degeneration (p = 0.346), and Alzheimer’s disease (p = 0.479). There was a causal effect of GD on the remaining outcomes: TL (OR = 0.982; 95%CI: 0.969–0.994; p = 0.004), senile cataract (OR = 1.031; 95%CI:1.002–1.060; p = 0.033), age-related hearing impairment (OR = 1.009; 95%CI:1.004–1.014; p = 0.001), COPD (OR = 1.055; 95%CI:1.008–1.103; p = 0.020), and sarcopenia (OR = 1.027; 95%CI:1.009–1.046; p = 0.004). All the β values of the MR methods were in the same direction. GD showed a positive causal association with all the significant outcomes, except for TL (see more details in Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis

The reliability of the IVW results was verified using a sensitivity analysis. Among the outcomes of the IVW method proving causality, the IVW method heterogeneity tests showed no heterogeneity in the MR analysis of senile cataracts (Cochran Q-derived p = 0.719, I2 = 0%), age-related hearing impairment (Cochran Q-derived p = 0.404, I2 = 0%), or COPD (Cochran Q-derived p = 0.960, I2 = 0%). Heterogeneity was observed in the MR analysis results for TL (Cochran’s Q-derived p < 0.001, I2 = 87%) and sarcopenia (Cochran’s Q-derived p < 0.001, I2 = 94%). The intercept of the MR-Egger regression for horizontal pleiotropy showed no horizontal pleiotropy in the MR analysis results for senile cataracts (intercept=-0.025, p = 0.338), age-related hearing impairment (intercept=-0.004, p = 0.395), and COPD (intercept=-0.007, p = 0.853). Horizontal pleiotropy was observed in the MR analysis results for TL (intercept = 0.018, p = 0.020) and sarcopenia (intercept=-0.026, p = 0.022). We performed MR-PRESSO global tests on outcomes with horizontal pleiotropy using the intercept of MR-Egger regression. The p-value of MR-PRESSO global test for TL was 0.078 (p > 0.05), while the p-value of MR-PRESSO global test for sarcopenia was 0.031 (p < 0.05). We further analyzed the horizontal pleiotropic effect of IVs on sarcopenia using the MR-PRESSO method. The p-value of the MR-PRESSO distortion test for sarcopenia was 1.0 > 0.05. We sorted in ascending order according to the p-value of the MR-PRESSO outlier test and then removed them individually (prs179247<0.005; prs9275576<0.005; prs72891915=0.04; prs6679677=0.14; prs942495=1.0). When we removed rs179247, the p-value of the MR-PRESSO global test for sarcopenia was 0.207 > 0.05. Then we repeated MR analysis with the remaining four IVs and found the following: IVW method for sarcopenia (β = 0.033,OR = 1.034,95%CI:1.018–1.051, p < 0.001); MR-Egger method for sarcopenia (β = 0.070,OR = 1.072,95%CI:1.037–1.109, p = 0.054); weighted mode method for sarcopenia (β = 0.041, OR = 1.042, 95%CI:1.031–1.053, p = 0.005); weighted median method for sarcopenia (β = 0.024, OR = 1.024, 95%CI:1.013–1.036, p < 0.001). The IVW method showed heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q-derived p < 0.001, I2 = 91.1%). According to the results of the leave-one-out analysis, rs9275576 had a more remarkable effect on the IVW results of TL and sarcopenia (Fig. 3). The post hoc power calculations for the MR analysis were as follows: PTL=95.6%, Pfacial ageing=8.3%, Page−related macular degeneration=18.0%, Psenile cataract=78.4%, Page−related hearing impairment=32.8%, PCOPD=72.8%, PAlzheimer’s disease=2.5%, and Psarcopenia=99.9%.

Discussion

Aging is a progressive physical degeneration that is the most important risk factor for the development of various human diseases [45]. GD is the main cause of hyperthyroidism, with an incidence of 20–40 cases per 100,000 people per year [46]. It affects the quality of life and is associated with an increased risk of death [7]. Excessive TH is a characteristic of GD, and TH is an essential hormone that coordinates metabolism throughout the body and acts on every cell in the body. Animal models and human studies have determined that TH regulates cellular processes associated with aging [5, 47, 48]. However, whether GD is a risk factor of aging remains debatable. In our MR analysis, we examined whether there was a causal relationship between GD and aging. Our MR results supported that GD had a causal relationship with TL, senile cataracts, age-related hearing impairment, COPD, and sarcopenia. A negative causal relationship was observed between GD and TL. This meant that having GD resulted in the shortening of the TL. There was a positive causal relationship between GD and senile cataracts, age-related hearing impairment, COPD, and sarcopenia. There was no strong evidence supporting a causal link between GD and facial aging, age-related macular degeneration, and Alzheimer’s disease. This is the first demonstration of a causal relationship between GD and aging.

A prominent molecular phenomenon underlying the aging process is gradual shortening of telomeres [17]. Existing research suggests that TL is a biomarker of cellular aging and a risk factor for age-related diseases [17, 49]. Studies have shown that TH can promote aging by binding to subtypes to induce DNA damage and premature aging, leading to a causal relationship with the molecular processes that accelerate aging [50]. This finding is consistent with our conclusion that GD shortens the TL. Senile cataracts are primarily age-related diseases and the most common cause of reversible loss of useful vision worldwide [51]. To date, no studies have directly investigated the relationship between GD and cataracts, and it is known that GD is often accompanied by thyroid orbitopathy, dry eye syndrome, intraocular pressure elevation, and compressive neuropathy, which does not exclude the possibility of developing cataracts [52]. We present, for the first time, strong evidence that GD can cause senile cataracts. Age-related hearing impairment is the most common sensory deficit in older adults and is caused by the cumulative effects of aging on the auditory system [53]. Studies have shown that TH can affect the distribution of subcellular prestin (a motor protein of outer cochlear hair cells) and enhance the expression of prestin mRNA through TH-receptor beta (TRβ) [54, 55]. TRβ mutations have been shown to be associated with TH resistance, and TRβ has been found to be essential in auditory development [56]. Our data show that GD can cause age-related hearing impairment. Therefore, clinicians should pay attention to changes in patients’ hearing ability. COPD can accelerate lung aging, and its complications lead to a deteriorating quality of life and premature death [57, 58]. Metabolic rate changes may be an important link between TH and respiratory muscle drive [59]. In thyrotoxicosis, respiratory muscle weakness affects both the inspiratory and expiratory muscles. As the severity of hyperthyroidism increases, both the maximum inspiratory pressure and expiratory pressure decrease [60]. In our study, a causal relationship between GD and COPD was confirmed. Skeletal muscles are particularly susceptible to metabolic and nutritional effects with high levels of TH. Poliana C Marinello et al. have found that free radicals participate in the hydrolysis of muscle proteins in a thyrotoxicosis rat model leading to significantly reduced muscle weight [61]. A previous study has found that TSH is U-shaped in relation with sarcopenia and low muscle strength. We use the indicator appendicular lean mass to represent sarcopenia. We observed a causal relationship between GD and sarcopenia. Despite the presence of horizontal pleiotropy during the analysis, GD could still cause sarcopenia after correction.

This study highlights the power of MR analysis in constructing phenomic range maps of traits of interest, as opposed to the traditional “exposure to one outcome at a time” MR analysis. The advantages of this study included the use of Two Sample MR analysis, which largely avoids confounding and reverse causation bias; the substantial number of participants to be disclosed in the GWAS data; and we ensured human racial uniformity and avoided racial bias. The causal relationship between exposure and outcome was detected using the IVW method as well as the MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode methods to further validate the results. The direction of the estimated effects was consistent across the four MR methods, supporting the verified causal relationship. Although heterogeneity was suggested during the sensitivity analysis, the random effect of the selected IVW method could ignore the influence of heterogeneity [28]. The horizontal pleiotropy test was evaluated and corrected using the MR-Egger regression intercept and MR-PRESSO methods. In addition to sarcopenia, the positive outcomes did not show horizontal pleiotropy in our study. Using this powerful analysis method, we proved that GD can promote cellular aging and lead to the occurrence of some age-related phenotypes, increasing the burden of aging problem. According to our analyses, patients with GD should be warned of the possibility of developing or aggravating age-related diseases.

Our study’s limitations need to be acknowledged despite the advantages of MR analysis. First, regarding the selection of age-related phenotypes, only seven age-related phenotypes were selected, which could not truly and comprehensively reflect the causal relationship between GD and aging. Second, although we used PhenoScanner to minimize bias due to confounding factors, these confounding factors could not be completely ruled out. Third, we limited the analysis to individuals of European descent to minimize demographic bias, but this would affect the generalizability of the results to other populations; finally, there is a null association between GD and facial aging, age-related macular degeneration, and Alzheimer’s disease, which may be due to a lack of statistical power and needs to be validated in greater sample size studies. Among the positive results, in addition to senile cataract, age-related hearing impairment, and COPD, our MR study’s statistical efficacy was more than 95%.

Conclusions

In summary, our data provided powerful novel evidence to support the hypothesis that GD was instrumental in the occurrence of certain age-related phenotypes. Further experimental studies should be conducted to elucidate the mechanisms by which GD facilitates aging, the results of such studies will greatly help the formation of strategies to slow down the progression of aging.
