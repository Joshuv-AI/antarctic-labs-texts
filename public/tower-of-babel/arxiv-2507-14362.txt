Stable matchings with switching costs
Boris Pittel and Kirill Rudov

Abstract
. In a stable matching problem there are two groups of agents, with agents on one side having their individual preferences for agents on another side as a potential match. It is assumed silently that agents can freely and costlessly “switch” partners. A matching is called stable if no two unmatched agents prefer each other to their matches. Half a century ago, for equinumerous sides, Knuth demonstrated existence of preferences for which there are exponentially many stable matchings, and he posed a problem of evaluating an expected number of stable matchings when the preferences are uniformly random and independent. It was shown later by Pittel that this expectation is quite moderate, asymptotic to e − 1 ​ n ​ log ⁡ n e^{-1}n\log n , n n being the number of agents on each side. The proof used Knuth’s integral formula for the expectation based on a classic inclusion-exclusion counting. In later papers by Pittel this and other integral formulas were obtained by generating preference lists via a pair of random matrices, with independently random, [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] -uniform entries, rather than from a progressively problematic counting approach. The novelty of this paper is that we view the matrix entries as a basis for cardinal utilities that each agent ascribes to the agents on the other side. Relaxing the notion of stability, we declare matching stable if no unmatched pair of agents strongly prefer each other to their partners, with “strength” measured by a parameter ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , ε = 0 \varepsilon=0 corresponding to classic stability. We show that for ε \varepsilon of order n − 1 ​ log ⁡ n n^{-1}\log n the expected number of ε \varepsilon -stable matchings is polynomially large, but for ε ≫ n − 1 ​ log ⁡ n \varepsilon\gg n^{-1}\log n however slightly the expectation is suddenly super-polynomially large. This “explosion” phenomenon for a very small strength parameter ε \varepsilon continues to hold for imbalanced matchings, regardless of the imbalance size.



1.
Introduction and main results

In a standard stable matching problem, we have two finite sets:

n
“firms”
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
n+k
“workers”
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in[n+k]}
,
k\geq 0
measuring the imbalance of the two-sided
“market”. Each agent ranks the agents on the other side from most preferred to least preferred, with no ties. A matching is a one-to-one assignment (matching)
M
of
n
workers to
n
firms. The classic definition of stability is: (a)
M
is stable
if no two agents, both matched in
M
but with somebody else, prefer each other to their partners in
M
; (b) each agent matched in
M
prefers its partner in
M
to any other unmatched agent on the other side.

Following the tradition, we consider the case when each worker’s (firm’s) preference list is chosen uniformly at random, and independently from all
n!
orderings of firms, (from all
(n+k)!
orderings of workers respectively). Admittedly, it is a highly idealized model of the random preferences, its huge advantage being a possibility to learn intricate details of such a random market, some of which hopefully will survive in a broader class of random preferences, when a rigorous analysis is
out of reach entirely.

The reason for this model’s surprising tractability, and its usefulness for our goal to develop a relaxed stability notion, is existence of a conceptually simple way to generate the uniform/independent random preferences. Here it is. Introduce two

n\times(n+k)
matrices

\mathbb{X}=\{X_{i,j}\}_{i\in[n],j\in[n+k]},\quad\mathbb{Y}=\{Y_{i,j}\}_{i\in[n],j\in[n+k]},

whose

entries are independent random variables, each distributed uniformly on
[0,1]
. We postulate that each
firm
f_{i}
(worker
w_{j}
resp.) ranks the
(n+k)
workers (the
n
firms, resp.) in the increasing order of row entries
X_{i,j}
(column entries
Y_{i,j}
resp.). Continuity of the uniform distribution implies that with probability
1
there are no ties. Clearly, the resulting preference lists are all uniformly random and mutually independent. The appearance of
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}
is unavoidable.
The
(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
–induced preference model is
the limit of a discrete model
, when each agent’s ranking of potential partners is obtained by a uniformly random allocation of their names among the ordered set of
n
(
n+k

As a warm-up, let us see how this “embedding” works for the standard stability. Consider a “diagonal” matching

with worker

,

. Introduce the events

&(X_{i,j}<X_{i,i}),&&i\in[n],j\in[n+1,n+k].\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly
\{M\text{ is stable}\}=\bigcap_{i,j}A^{c}_{i,j}
.
The interdependent events

X_{i,i}=x_{i}
,

,

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(A_{i,j}|\circ)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(X_{i,j}\leq x_{i},\,Y_{i,j}<y_{j}\bigr)=x_{i}y_{j},&&i,j\in[n],\\
&\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(X_{i,j}\leq x_{i}\bigr)=x_{i},&&i\in[n],j\in[n+1,n+k].\end{aligned}\right.

Integrating over
x_{i},y_{j}\in[0,1]

(1.1)

P(n,n+k):=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(M\text{ is stable})\\
=\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-x_{i}y_{j})\cdot\prod_{\ell\in[n]}(1-x_{\ell})^{k}\,d\mathbb{x}d\mathbb{y}.

(It is by no means clear at the moment how to handle this, and similar integrals below, but obviously the enumerative issue is not a problem any more.) For

The above argument for

6

By analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the integral representing

This certainly signaled that the classic definition of stability was unrealistically rigid, giving rise to a problem: how can we relax definition of stability which would reflect better the reality of decision–making in the two-side markets when changing partners is far from being costless, whatever the nature of these costs is?

While the entries of those matrices

In this work we decided to actually use

Our strategy was to use an auxiliary parameter

Clearly, the case

Indeed, these ratios can be viewed as the

What is a counterpart of the formula (

, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{1}=\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{ps(n)}\!\!\!e^{\Phi_{n}(\eta)}\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}e^{\Phi_{n}(ps(n))}\int_{0}^{ps(n)}\!\!\!\!\exp\bigl(\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(ps(n))(\eta-ps(n))\bigr)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{e^{\Phi_{n}(ps(n))}}{\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(ps(n))}.

To sum up, the contribution of
n!\int_{1}
to
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n)]
is

(2.6)

\displaystyle n!\int_{1}

\displaystyle\leq n!\,\tfrac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{(1-e^{-ps(n)})^{n}}{ps(n)}\cdot\frac{e^{ps(n)}-1}{n}\left(1-\frac{e^{ps(n)}-1}{nps(n)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=O(p^{-n}n^{1/2})=\exp(O(\log n)),

since
p=e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}
and
\varepsilon\lambda=O(n^{-1}\log n)
.

Integral 2.
We now turn attention to the second integral

\int_{2}=\int_{s(n)}^{n}\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{(1-e^{-sp})^{n}}{s}\,ds\leq\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\cdot\int_{s(n)}^{n}\tfrac{1}{s}\,ds\leq\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\log n.

Consequently, the contribution of
n!\int_{2}
to
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n)]
is

\displaystyle n!\int_{2}=O\left(np^{-n}\log n\right)=\exp(O(\log n)),

because
p=e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}
and
\varepsilon\lambda=O(n^{-1}\log n)
. Combining this bound with (
2.6
), we complete the proof of Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\varepsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)
, i.e.
\varepsilon\lambda\gg n^{-1}\log n
, then
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n)]
is super-polynomially large.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n)]
is increasing in
\epsilon\lambda
, and
\epsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)
, it is sufficient to focus only on
p=e^{-2\epsilon\lambda}=1-o(1)
in our calculations below.

Define the following variables:

s\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i},\quad s_{j}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}=s-x_{j},\quad t\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad t_{j}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}^{2}=t-x_{j}^{2}.

Restrict attention to the set
D
of all non-negative
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
such that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{1}(n))}\leq s\leq\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{2}(n))},\\
&s^{-1}x_{i}\leq\frac{\log(ns_{2}(n))}{n},\quad i\in[n],\\
&s^{-2}t\leq\frac{3}{n},\end{split}

where
s_{1}(n)>s_{2}(n)
, with
s_{2}(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, are to be defined later. (The two bottom inequalities are essentially dictated by the connection between the fractions
s^{-1}x_{i}
and the lengths
L_{i}
of random subintervals
of
[0,1]
, and Lemma
2.1
.) According to the first two conditions, we have

x_{i}\leq s\frac{\log(ns_{2}(n))}{n}=(\log n)^{-1}<1

for sufficiently large
n
, so that
D\subset[0,1]^{n}
. Consequently, for such large
n
,

P^{\epsilon}(n)\geq\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{x}\in D}\!\left(\prod_{j\in[n]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\,dy_{j}\right)\right)\,d\mathbb{x}.

Since
1-\alpha=\exp(-\alpha-\alpha^{2}(1+O(\alpha))/2)
as
\alpha\to 0
, for each fixed
j
, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in[n],i\neq j}\left(1-px_{i}y_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-py_{j}s_{j}-p^{2}y^{2}_{j}t_{j}\frac{1+O\left((\log n)^{-1}\right)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-py_{j}s_{j}-\frac{2p^{2}y^{2}_{j}t_{j}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-py_{j}s-\frac{2p^{2}y^{2}_{j}t}{3}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\prod_{j\in[n]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\,dy_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(-pys-\frac{2p^{2}y^{2}t}{3}\right)\,dy\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=(ps)^{-n}\left(\int_{0}^{ps}\exp\left(-\eta-\frac{2\eta^{2}t}{3s^{2}}\right)d\eta\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\geq(ps)^{-n}\left(\int_{0}^{ps}\exp\left(-\eta-\frac{2\eta^{2}}{n}\right)d\eta\right)^{n}

since
t/s^{2}\leq\frac{3}{n}
for
\mathbb{x}\in D
.

By noting that
\int_{0}^{ps}e^{-\eta}d\eta=1-e^{-ps}
and employing Jensen’s inequality for the exponential function, we can bound

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{ps}\exp\left(-\eta-\frac{2\eta^{2}}{n}\right)d\eta

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-ps})\int_{0}^{ps}\exp\left(-\frac{2\eta^{2}}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{-\eta}}{1-e^{-ps}}d\eta

\displaystyle\geq(1-e^{-ps})\exp\left(-\frac{2/n}{1-e^{-ps}}\int_{0}^{ps}e^{-\eta}\eta^{2}d\eta\right)

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-ps})\exp\left(-\frac{4}{n}\cdot\frac{e^{ps}-\left(1+ps+(ps)^{2}/2\right)}{e^{ps}-1}\right).

Since

\frac{e^{ps}-\left(1+ps+(ps)^{2}/2\right)}{e^{ps}-1}\leq 1,

we obtain

\int_{0}^{ps}\exp\left(-\eta-\frac{2\eta^{2}}{n}\right)d\eta\geq(1-e^{-ps})e^{-4/n}.

Consequently,

\prod_{j\in[n]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})dy_{j}\right)\\
\geq e^{-4}\left(\frac{1-e^{-ps}}{ps}\right)^{n},

implying that

\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)\geq e^{-4}p^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{x}\in D}\!\left(\frac{1-e^{-ps}}{s}\right)^{n}\,d\mathbb{x}.

We then switch to new variables
(u,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n-1})
defined as follows:

u\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=s\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{i}\equiv s^{-1}x_{i},\quad i\in[n-1].

We also define
v_{n}\equiv s^{-1}x_{n}=1-\sum_{i\in[n-1]}v_{i}
. The conditions (
2.7
) then become

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{1}(n))}\leq u\leq\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{2}(n))},\\
&v_{i}\leq\frac{\log(ns_{2}(n))}{n},\quad i\in[n],\\
&\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{n},\end{split}

Importantly, for these new variables, the resulting region is the direct product of the range of
u
and the range of
\mathbb{v}
. By using the key equality (
2.1
), we get then

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-4}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\left(\int\limits_{\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{1}(n))}}^{\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{2}(n))}}\frac{(1-e^{-pu})^{n}}{u}du\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(L^{+}_{n}\leq\frac{\log(ns_{2}(n))}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{n}\leq\frac{3}{n}\right),

where
L^{+}_{n}\equiv\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}
and
U_{n}\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}L_{i}^{2}
. By Lemma
2.1
, the probability of the event on the right-hand side tends to one as
n\to\infty
. Choose
s_{1}(n)=o(\log n)
and
s_{2}(n)=\frac{s_{1}(n)}{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{1}(n))}}^{\frac{n}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{2}(n))}}\frac{1}{u}du=\log\left(\frac{\log(ns_{1}(n))}{\log(ns_{2}(n))}\right)

is asymptotic to
\frac{\log 2}{\log n}
. Furthermore, since
p=1-o(1)
, for the considered range of
u
, we have

\displaystyle pu\geq\frac{pn}{\log n\cdot\log(ns_{1}(n))}\geq\frac{n}{2(\log n)^{2}},

so that

\displaystyle(1-e^{-pu})^{n}=\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2(\log n)^{2}}\right)\right)^{n}=1-o(1).

To conclude,

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)\geq\frac{e^{-4}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\frac{\log 2}{\log n}(1-o(1)),

and thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\epsilon}(n,n)]\geq n!\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)\geq\frac{ne^{-4}p^{-n}\log 2}{\log n}(1-o(1))=\exp(\omega(\log n))

since
p=e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}
and
\varepsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)
.
∎

Combining Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
k=0
.

2.2.
The case
k\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\varepsilon\lambda=O(n^{-1}\log n)
, then
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)]
is polynomially large.

Proof.

As shown before,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)]=(n+k)_{n}P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)
, where

P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in[0,1]^{n}}\!\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\cdot\prod_{\ell\in[n]}(1-\sqrt{p}x_{\ell})^{k}\,d\mathbb{x}d\mathbb{y}\quad\text{and},

p=\exp(-2\varepsilon\lambda)
. Let us upper bound
P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)
.
Since
1-\xi\leq\exp(-\xi)
, we get

(2.9)

P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}}\left(\prod_{j\in[n]}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(-pys_{j}\right)\,dy\right)\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s\right)d\mathbb{x},

where

s\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{j}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}=s-x_{j}.

Integrating with respect to
y\in[0,1]
, we obtain

(2.10)

P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}}\left(\prod_{j\in[n]}F(s_{j}p)\right)\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s\right)\,d\mathbb{x},\quad F(z)=\frac{1-e^{-z}}{z}.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, for some
A>0
, we have

\prod_{j\in[n]}F(s_{j}p)\leq e^{A}F^{n}(sp).

Then, by changing variables and using (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in[0,1]^{n}}e^{A}F^{n}(sp)e^{-k\sqrt{p}s}\,d\mathbb{x}\leq e^{A}\int_{0}^{n}F^{n}(sp)e^{-k\sqrt{p}s}\,\frac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,ds

\displaystyle=e^{A}\int_{0}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{1-e^{-sp}}{sp}\biggr)^{n}e^{-k\sqrt{p}s}\frac{s^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,ds=\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s}(1-e^{-sp})^{n}e^{-k\sqrt{p}s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\eta}(1-e^{-\eta})^{n}\exp\left({-\frac{k\eta}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\eta})^{n-1}\exp\left({-\frac{k\eta}{\sqrt{p}}}\right)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)^{n-1}z^{\frac{k}{\sqrt{p}}-1}\,dz=\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}B(n,k/\sqrt{p}),

where

B(a_{1},a_{2})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}z^{a_{1}-1}(1-z)^{a_{2}-1}\,dz
is the beta function, also known as the Euler integral of the first kind.
The beta function is closely connected to the gamma function

B(a_{1},a_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(a_{1})\Gamma(a_{2})}{\Gamma(a_{1}+a_{2})},\quad\text{with}\quad\Gamma(a)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{a-1}e^{-z}\,dz,

known as the Euler integral of the second kind, and the binomial coefficients

\binom{n+k}{k}=\frac{(n+k)!}{n!k!}=\frac{n+k}{nk}\frac{\Gamma(n+k)}{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(k)}=\frac{n+k}{nk}\frac{1}{B(n,k)}.

By using these connections, we obtain the following bound:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)]

\displaystyle=(n+k)_{n}\cdot P^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)

\displaystyle\leq n!\cdot\frac{n+k}{nk}\frac{1}{B(n,k)}\cdot\frac{e^{A}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}B(n,k/\sqrt{p})

\displaystyle=e^{A}\frac{(n+k)}{k}p^{-n}\frac{B(n,k/\sqrt{p})}{B(n,k)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{A}\frac{(n+k)}{k}p^{-n}=\exp(O(\log n))

because
p=e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}\leq 1
and
\varepsilon\lambda=O(n^{-1}\log n)
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\varepsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)
, i.e.
\varepsilon\lambda\gg n^{-1}\log n
, then
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)]
is super-polynomially large, uniformly for all
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\varepsilon}(n,n+k)]
is increasing in
\epsilon\lambda
, and
\epsilon\lambda=\omega(n^{-1}\log n)
, it is sufficient to focus only on
p=e^{-2\epsilon\lambda}=1-o(1)
in our calculations below.

As before, we define the following variables:

s\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i},\quad s_{j}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}=s-x_{j},\quad t\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad t_{j}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}^{2}=t-x_{j}^{2}.

Restrict attention to the set
D
of all non-negative
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
such that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&s_{1}(n)\leq s\leq s_{2}(n),\\
&s^{-1}x_{i}\leq\frac{2\log n}{n},\quad i\in[n],\\
&s^{-2}t\leq\frac{3}{n},\end{split}

where
s_{2}(n)>s_{1}(n)
are to be defined later. (As before, the two bottom inequalities are essentially dictated by the connection between the fractions
s^{-1}x_{i}
and the lengths
L_{i}
of random subintervals
of
[0,1]
, as well as Lemma
2.1
.) According to the first two conditions, we have

x_{i}\leq s\frac{2\log n}{n}\leq\frac{2s_{2}(n)\log n}{n}\equiv\sigma_{n}.

In what follows, we choose
s_{2}(n)
such that
\sigma_{n}\to 0
(to be verified later). Thus, for sufficiently large
n
, we have
x_{i}\leq\sigma_{n}<1
and
D\subset[0,1]^{n}
. Consequently, for such large
n
,

P^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)\geq\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{x}\in D}\!\left(\prod_{j\in[n]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})\,dy_{j}\right)\right)\\
\times\prod_{\ell\in[n]}(1-\sqrt{p}x_{\ell})^{k}\,d\mathbb{x}.

Since
1-\alpha=\exp(-\alpha-\alpha^{2}(1+O(\alpha))/2)
as
\alpha\to 0
, we have: for each
j
,

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in[n],i\neq j}\left(1-px_{i}y_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-py_{j}s_{j}-p^{2}y^{2}_{j}t_{j}\frac{1+O\left(\sigma_{n}\right)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-py_{j}s-\frac{2p^{2}y^{2}_{j}t}{3}\right).

By proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, and using
t/s^{2}\leq 3/n
, we bound

\prod_{j\in[n]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i\neq j}(1-px_{i}y_{j})dy_{j}\right)\\
\geq e^{-4}\left(\frac{1-e^{-ps}}{ps}\right)^{n}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell\in[n]}(1-\sqrt{p}x_{\ell})^{k}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s-kpt\frac{1+O\left(\sigma_{n}\right)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s-\frac{2kps^{2}}{3}\frac{t}{s^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s-\frac{2kps^{2}}{n}\right)

Based on the previous calculations, we have

\displaystyle P^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)\geq\int_{\mathbb{x}\in D}\!e^{-4}\left(\frac{1-e^{-ps}}{ps}\right)^{n}\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}s-\frac{2kps^{2}}{n}\right)\,d\mathbb{x}.

We then switch to new variables
(u,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n-1})
defined as follows:

u\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=s\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{i}\equiv s^{-1}x_{i},\quad i\in[n-1].

We also define
v_{n}\equiv s^{-1}x_{n}=1-\sum_{i\in[n-1]}v_{i}
. The conditions (
2.11
) then become

(2.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&s_{1}(n)\leq u\leq s_{2}(n),\\
&v_{i}\leq\frac{2\log(n)}{n},\quad i\in[n],\\
&\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{n},\end{split}

Importantly, for these new variables, the resulting region is the direct product of the range of
u
and the range of
\mathbb{v}
. By using the key equality (
2.1
), we get then

\displaystyle\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-4}p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\left(\int_{s_{1}(n)}^{s_{2}(n)}(1-e^{-pu})^{n}\exp\left(-k\sqrt{p}u\right)\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{2kpu^{2}}{n}\right)}{u}du\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(L^{+}_{n}\leq\frac{2\log(n)}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{n}\leq\frac{3}{n}\right),

where
L^{+}_{n}\equiv\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}
and
U_{n}\equiv\sum_{i\in[n]}L_{i}^{2}
. By Lemma
2.1
, the probability of the event on the right-hand side tends to one as
n\to\infty
. Thus, it remains to examine

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int^{\star}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{s_{1}(n)}^{s_{2}(n)}\frac{\exp\left(H(u)-\frac{2kpu^{2}}{n}\right)}{u}du,

\displaystyle H(u)

\displaystyle\equiv n\log(1-e^{-pu})-k\sqrt{p}u.

for appropriately chosen
s_{1,2}(n)
. Note that

H^{\prime}(u)=\frac{np}{e^{pu}-1}-k\sqrt{p},\,\,H^{\prime\prime}(u)=-\frac{np^{2}e^{pu}}{(e^{pu}-1)^{2}}=-\frac{np^{2}}{(e^{pu/2}-e^{-pu/2})^{2}}\geq-\frac{n}{u^{2}},

i.e., the function
H(u)
attains its maximum at

s(n)\equiv\frac{\log\left(\frac{n+k/\sqrt{p}}{k/\sqrt{p}}\right)}{p}
.
We anticipate that the dominant contribution to the integral
\int^{\star}
is obtained when we set

s_{1,2}(n)=s(n)(1\mp\delta(n))

for some
\delta(n)\to 0
. And for
u\in[s_{1}(n),s_{2}(n)]
, we have
H^{\prime\prime}(u)=-\Theta(n/s^{2}(n))
. (Note that
\sigma_{n}=\frac{2s_{2}(n)\log n}{n}\to 0
for
n\to\infty
, as required.) Further,

H(s(n))=J(k/\sqrt{p}),\quad J(z):=n\log\frac{n}{n+z}+z\log\frac{z}{n+z},

where
J^{\prime}(z)=\log\tfrac{z}{n+z}
,
J^{\prime\prime}(z)=\tfrac{n}{z(n+z)}>0
. It follows that

H(s(n))\geq n\log\frac{n}{n+k}+k\log\frac{k}{n+k}-\tfrac{1-p}{1+\sqrt{p}}k\log\frac{n+k}{k}.

So, expanding
H(u)
in powers of
z:=u-s(n)
, we see that the integral
\int^{*}
(defined in (
2.13
)) is at least of order

\frac{\exp(H(s(n))-3.1kps^{2}(n)/n)}{s(n)}\int_{-\delta(n)s(n)}^{\delta(n)s(n)}\exp\bigl(-z^{2}\Theta(n/s^{2}(n)\bigr)\,dz.\\

Here the integral equals

\tfrac{s(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{-\delta(n)\sqrt{n}}^{\delta(n)\sqrt{n}}e^{-\Theta(\eta^{2})}\,d\eta=\Theta(\tfrac{s(n)}{\sqrt{n}}),

provided that
\delta(n)\sqrt{n}\to\infty
, which we assume. Therefore the integral
\int^{*}
is at least of order

\frac{\exp(H(s(n))-3.1kps^{2}(n)/n)}{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}\\
=\frac{n^{n}k^{k}}{(n+k)^{n+k}\Theta(\sqrt{n})}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{1-p}{1+\sqrt{p}}k\log\frac{n+k}{k}-3.1kps^{2}(n)/n\Bigr).

So,
\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n)
is at least of order

\frac{p^{-n}}{(n-1)!}\frac{n^{n}k^{k}}{(n+k)^{n+k}\sqrt{n}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1-p}{1+\sqrt{p}}k\log\frac{n+k}{k}-3.1kps^{2}(n)/n\Bigr).

Combining this estimate with

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)]\geq(n+k)_{n}\cdot\tilde{P}^{\epsilon}(n),\quad(n+k)_{n}\geq ce^{-k}(n+k)^{n+k}e^{-n},

c
being an absolute constant, we obtain that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)]
is at least of order

\exp\Bigl(n\log(1/p)-\tfrac{1-p}{1+\sqrt{p}}k\log\tfrac{n+k}{k}-3.1kps^{2}(n)/n\Bigr).

Here, by the definition of
s(n)
,

kps^{2}(n)/n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\cdot\tfrac{k}{n\sqrt{p}}\log^{2}\bigl(\frac{1+k/n\sqrt{p}}{k/n\sqrt{p}}\bigr)=O(1/\sqrt{p}),

since
\sup_{z>0}z\log^{2}\bigl(\frac{1+z}{z}\bigr)<\infty
. In addition,
\frac{k}{n}\log\tfrac{n+k}{k}\leq\frac{k}{n}\cdot\tfrac{n}{k}=1
, and
\log(1/p)>1-p
. Therefore
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[S^{\epsilon}(n,n+k)]
is at least of order

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\Bigl(n\bigl(\log(1/p)-\frac{1-p}{1+\sqrt{p}}\bigr)+O(1/\sqrt{p})\Bigr)\\
\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\bigl(n(1-p)\frac{\sqrt{p}}{1+\sqrt{p}}+O(1/\sqrt{p})\bigr),

which is super-polynomially large for
p=e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}
,
\varepsilon\lambda\to 0
,
\varepsilon\lambda=\omega\tfrac{\log n}{n}
,
\omega=\omega(n)\to\infty
. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.5
.
∎

Hence, the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
k\geq 1
is complete as well.
