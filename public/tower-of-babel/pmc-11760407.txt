Introduction

Lesch–Nyhan syndrome (LNS) is a rare genetic condition caused by a defect in the purine metabolism enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT). The estimated prevalence of LNS ranges from 1:2,35,000 to 1:3,80,000, with males being the most affected and females serving as carriers.1 LNS arises from mutations within the HPRT1 gene, which alters purine metabolism, slowing nucleotide production and accumulating purine metabolites, most notably guanine and hypoxanthine. This disrupted purine equilibrium contributes to elevated uric acid levels, manifesting as neurological and behavioral abnormalities.1,2 Symptoms of the disorder emerge between 3 and 6 months of age, including muscular hypotonia, difficulties retaining a sitting position, and renal problems like hematuria and crystalluria.3

Among the many manifestations of LNS, a striking, prominent, and bizarre characteristic is oral self-injury—a behavior marked by repetitive and severe self-inflicted lesions to the oral tissues, including lip, cheek, and finger biting.4,7 While the biological principles underlying LNS are well understood, the emergence of oral self-injury as a distinct and persistent behavior continues to elicit concern among clinicians. As high as 85% of affected patients allegedly commit self-inflicted biting, resulting in severe tissue injury.2 These injuries frequently result in infections, bleeding, and scarring, posing a significant risk to the individual's health and well-being. A recent cross-sectional study conducted in Italy by Isola et al. illustrated the severity of the problem, with 16 of the 19 LNS patients included in the study demonstrating permanent scars attributed to oral self-mutilation. Various symptomatic treatments, from pharmaceutical to dental management, have been employed to limit self-mutilation in these syndromic patients.4

However, due to the singularity of LNS, valuable information and definitive treatments for the disorder have been difficult to establish. There are a few isolated published case reports.5,9 This systematic analysis of case reports aimed to compile available knowledge and identify trends in disorder onset, symptoms, treatments, and possible interventions across individual cases associated with LNS oral self-injury. The findings will contribute to a better understanding of this distressing behavior and potentially guide the development of specific interventions for patients afflicted by this rare syndrome with a higher level of evidence.

Materials and Methods

The systematic review conformed to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines10 and was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [Registration Number CRD42023455110].

Search Strategy

Lesch–Nyhan syndrome and oral self-mutilation were entered as keywords or phrases in the PubMed-MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar search engines. To find pertinent case reports, references in case reports and other relevant peer-reviewed literature were also carefully examined. We considered all case reports published in the English language from inception until July 30, 2023.

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

LNS diagnoses involved oral self-mutilation in the case reports without timeframe restriction. The case reports must, at the very least, (1) address the patient's demographics and any documented episodes of self-mutilation at the time of presentation; (2) describe the steps taken to treat and manage the patients.

Case reports of LNS involving nonoral self-mutilation and unclear case descriptions were discarded.

Study Selection, Data Extraction, and Quality Appraisal

Two independent reviewers (SN and ST) used a two-step method to assess eligibility. The Rayyan online software was used for the initial screening, which included evaluating the titles and abstracts of all studies found using search engines. Following that, titles demonstrating prospective eligibility were subjected to a detailed evaluation involving full-text reading. Articles that were unavailable through open-access sources were retrieved using two basic strategies. Primarily, Sci-Hub was used to retrieve the desired content. Additionally, at least three separate emails were sent to the study's authors to obtain the publications. Following that, papers for which no response was received from the authors or that did not fit the predefined eligibility criteria were rejected. A third reviewer (VM) was engaged in cases where the eligibility criteria created ambiguity or confusion to ensure a comprehensive and objective assessment.

The data from each article were filled in a sheet, including the following columns by two reviewers independently: author name, year of publication, country, total number of cases, age of patient, gender, age of developing self-mutilation symptoms, disorder of development, presenting symptoms, physical examination findings, laboratory findings, treatment, follow-up, and treatment outcome.

Quality assessment

We utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal tool for case reports to assess the article's quality.11 Two researchers independently analyzed all case reports based on eight questions prespecified in the tool. Based on the evaluation, each question was assigned a “yes,” “no,” “maybe,” or “unclear” status. A quality rating was provided to the included studies, with scores above 70% considered good quality. Articles with scores between 40 and 70% were labeled as moderate quality, while scores <40% were designated as low quality. These categories were chosen by agreement among the reviewers to facilitate a comprehensive and unbiased appraisal of study quality.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel® version 2010. Patient demographic information was summarized using descriptive statistics such as mean, standard deviation, frequencies, and percentages. A narrative approach was used to identify trends and commonalities in presenting symptoms, physical assessment findings, laboratory results, and treatment modalities.

Results

Search Results

Our comprehensive search found 3,949 articles, including 162 duplications. Based on the title and abstract evaluation, 3,744 of 3,787 nonduplicates were eliminated. Thus, 43 articles were sought for retrieval, of which only 35 were accessible. Based on full-text reading, 16 studies failed to satisfy the eligibility criteria due to wrong research design (n = 9) and not related to self-injury in LNS (n = 7). As a result, 19 publications have been chosen for analysis in this paper. Figure 1 depicts the systematic review's flowchart. An overview of the studies included is provided in Tables 2 and 3.

Geographical Distribution

The included case reports were published between the years 1972 and 2022. The case reports were from Chile, Australia, India, Belgium, Egypt, Ethiopia, Greece, the US, Portugal, Scotland, Spain, the Philippines, and Japan (Fig. 2). Most case reports included just one case. However, two studies, Wood et al.12 and Goodman et al.,8 recorded 3 and 5 cases, respectively.

Geographical distribution of case reports

Patient Characteristics

The median age of the reported 25 patients was 7 years, ranging from 8 months to 22 years old, with all cases being males. The patients were reported to develop the symptoms of oral self-mutilation at the average age of 4 ± 3.42 years (Table 2).

Medical History of Patients

The medical history of the majority, that is, 18 out of 25 (72%) patients, suggested developmental delays, particularly the inability to hold the head and sit, feeding difficulty, growth retardation, delayed psychomotor development, and difficulty in speech.5,9,12,19 Moreover, choreoathetoid movements or epilepsy5,7,9,18,20,21 and diagnosed cerebral palsy8,13,16,17,20,22 were also reported in 7 (28%) and 8 (32%) patients, respectively. One study (4%) reported patients having congenital anomalies such as cleft palate, ventricular septal defect, and cryptorchidism.15 The reports of Maroto et al.23 and Sugahara et al.24 did not mention the medical history of the patient.

Presenting Symptoms

In the health facility setting, LNS patients were commonly presented with various symptoms related to their condition. Notably, perioral or intraoral lesions were a prominent feature,5,13 often characterized by bleeding. In a significant portion of cases (56% of patients, 14 out of 25), individuals exhibited ulceration or lacerations across various anatomical sites, including the tongue, lower lip, thumbs, cheeks, index finger, dorsum of hands, and extensor surface of forearms.6,8,14,18,20,22,25

In one patient (4%), additional self-mutilating behaviors were observed in the form of hand and head banging.22 Weight loss and failure to thrive due to decreased food intake were identified as presenting symptoms in five (20%) cases.6,7,16,19,21

Distinctly, two (8%) of the reported patients sought a consultation for the repair of facial disfigurement resulting from serious self-mutilation injuries.8,17 In addition, 4 (16%) patients also exhibited renal issues such as crystalluria, hematuria, urinary tract infections, and difficulty passing urine.9

Physical Examination Findings

Intra- and Extraoral Findings

Intraoral examinations of the patient cohort revealed several noteworthy findings. Two patients, accounting for 8% of the total cases, displayed poor oral hygiene.5,9 Dental caries were identified in four individuals (16% of cases).5,7,20,22 Additionally, gingival inflammation was reported in one patient (4%).7 Notably, a significant proportion of patients, numbering five (20% of cases), exhibited inflammation or ulceration of the tongue and surrounding tissues.5,14,16,20,21 A single case (4%) exhibited active bleeding of the tongue,13 highlighting diverse intraoral issues.

A broad spectrum of observations regarding extraoral findings was also made. Lacerated wounds in the lower lip were documented in three patients (12%).7,25 A more substantial proportion, eight patients (32%), were found to have ulcerative lesions in the lower lip.5,6,9,12,14,16,21,24 Erythema of the perioral skin was evident in two cases (8%).5,15 On the contrary, a solitary instance (4%) exhibited suppuration.23 Most notably, 10 (40%) patients displayed scarring and deformation of the lower lip due to chronic and persistent lip chewing.7,9,13,17,18,20,22

Hand, Nails, and Fingers

Within the patient cohort, examining fingers and hands revealed a range of distinctive observations. In four cases (16%), ulcers or lacerations on fingers and hands were reported.6,16,21,23 A single instance (4%) displayed bleeding and aberrated wounds on fingers and hands.7 Notably, scarring was observed on fingers and hands in a separate case (4%).20 A particularly severe manifestation was observed in one patient (4%), characterized by amputated and necrotized areas in the fingers and thumb.13 Arms were restrained with wraps to manage self-injurious behavior in three cases (12%).5,9,19 Conversely, for the remaining 16 cases (64%), hands, fingers, and nail injuries were not explicitly mentioned in the provided case reports.8,12,14,15,17,18,24,25

Laboratory Findings

Out of 19, only 14 studies mentioned the laboratory findings of the LNS patients. Laboratory assessments among 8 (32%) patients consistently revealed elevated uric acid levels and a high uric acid-to-creatinine ratio across several studies, reflecting excessive purine production.6,8,12,18,19 Furthermore, a genetic mutation in the HPRT1 gene was identified in five patients (20%).8,12,19

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, coagulation profile, and liver function tests, yielded normal results in two (8%) cases.6,15 The remaining 14 (56%) cases mentioned no specific laboratory findings in the included studies, underscoring potential gaps in the available data.5,7,8,13,14,16,17,20,25

Treatment

Pharmacological

Antipsychotic medication like risperidone (no dosage information given) was administered in 12% of cases (3 out of 26) to manage neurological symptoms and self-mutilation.7,15,21

In four (16%) patients, anticonvulsant medication was prescribed. Gabapentin (100 mg, three times daily) was prescribed in one case.5 Carbamazepine (200 mg/24 hours) was used in two (8%) of cases.15,23 Trihexyphenidyl (dose not mentioned) was given to one (4%) case to control dystonia and compulsive self-aggressive behavior.7

Antianxiety medication was used in 3 cases. Lorazepam (1 mg/24 hours) was administered in one (4%) case.5 Diazepam (2 mg/mL: 0.4 mL × 4 doses), clonazepam, and L-5-hydroxytryptophan (5 mL/5 doses/24 hours) were used in two (8%) of cases.7,23

Botulinum toxin A injections into bilateral masseters were performed in three (12%) of cases to reduce self-abusive behavior.5,8,16 Allopurinol at a dose of 15 mg/kg/day was given in five (20%) cases to control serum creatinine and serum uric acid levels.7,9,12,16,23

Ural and urate (4–5 gm/24 hours) were used in one case (4%) for uric acid control.23 Glutamine therapy was used in one case (4%).12 Pharmacological treatment of 36% (9 out of 25) patients was not disclosed.6,8,13,14,18,20,22,24,25

Surgical

Multiple teeth were extracted in 11 patients (44%) because lip guards or splints were unsuccessful or not appropriate due to lip ulcers or scarring.5,7,8,13,14,17,22 Among the cases described, a particular patient underwent a partial glossectomy, debridement, and control of bleeders and tongue repair under general anesthesia.13 In another extreme case, reconstructive surgery addressed a lower lip defect, primarily improving cosmesis and preventing drooling.17

Management/Conservative Treatment

The tooth cavity was restored with amalgam under local anesthesia in one patient.20 Intraoral devices in the form of mouthguards were used in 14 (56%) of the cases across various studies.5,6,8,9,14,16,18,19,23,25 Lip bumpers were observed in three (12%) of cases.8,23,25 Behavioral management techniques were employed in two (8%) of cases.13,16

Follow-up and Treatment Outcome

Eleven patients underwent follow-up assessments from 2 weeks to 1 year, accounting for 44%. In contrast, for a substantial 60% of cases (15 out of 25), specific timeframes for patient follow-up were not explicitly mentioned in the respective studies.

In terms of treatment outcome, intraoral devices such as splints and guards were used among 14 cases (56%).5,6,8,9,14,16,18,19,23,25 Whereas lip bumpers were reported among three cases (12%).8,23,25 These interventions were deemed effective in eight (32%) of cases.8,9,18,23,25 Similarly, pharmacological interventions yielded varying levels of efficacy. For instance, botulinum-A injections were well tolerated and exhibited benefits lasting for 10 weeks, after which the behavior resurfaced, necessitating restraint and subsequent injections every 12 weeks.7,16 Conversely, glutamine therapy was found ineffective in a study by Wood et al.12 Notably, allopurinol demonstrated effectiveness as a pharmacological intervention.12,23

Dental extraction emerged as the most effective treatment modality for addressing oral self-mutilation in 11 (44%) LNS patients across studies.5,7,8,13,17,19,22 Behavioral management techniques were noted as effective in a single study (4% of cases).13 Three studies did not report the treatment outcome of patients.6,20,21

Quality Assessment

Five of the 19 studies under consideration received scores below 70%, classifying them as moderate in quality. The remaining 14 studies, on the contrary, were classified as high quality since their overall score exceeded 70%. The most poorly documented criteria in all of these studies were the diagnostic criteria and any side effects following treatment. Notably, three of these high-quality studies received a perfect score of 100%, indicating that they fulfilled all the criteria given in the JBI instrument to appraise case reports.

Discussion

Lesch–Nyhan syndrome is a rare genetic disorder due to the deficiency of an enzyme that plays a pivotal role in the recycling of purine nucleotides essential for DNA and RNA synthesis. The pathophysiology of self-inflicting injurious behavior is not clearly defined but is believed to be due to a complex interplay of biological and neurological factors. Accumulation of uric acid in serum due to deranged purine metabolism may be responsible for neurological manifestations.

Our systematic review and analysis of 26 cases from 19 case reports provide valuable insights into the diverse clinical spectrum and management strategies associated with this complex syndrome. Individuals with this syndrome are born healthy and asymptomatic, with no clinical signs throughout the prenatal period. Although hyperuricemia is common at birth, it is usually mild, with the predominant clinical manifestation being orange-colored crystals in the diapers. Neurological problems, developmental delay, and extrapyramidal symptoms appear only as the infant grows.1 However, clinicians may struggle to diagnose the illness based solely on these symptoms. Oral self-mutilation behavior is a distinguishing trait of LNS, manifested by biting of lips, fingers, and cheeks, resulting in severe deformity. In infants, this activity frequently begins with the eruption of teeth. As a result, a definitive diagnosis is frequently made later.26 In our review, the average age of onset of symptoms of oral self-mutilation was 4 ± 3.42 years. This concurs with Isola's observation4 that the syndrome is identified at a mean age of 4.7 years. Li et al. noted the median age of diagnosis earlier at 2.7 years.26

The medical history of 72% of LNS patients in our review revealed developmental delays, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, and extrapyramidal features such as choreoathetosis movements. Hypotheses put forth to explain these phenomena include decreased basal ganglia volume,27 disturbed adenosine and dopamine neurotransmission,28 and significant reductions and abnormalities in white matter.29,30 Literature is equivocal about the etiology of the unique neurological symptoms due to this metabolic disease. To fully understand the complex mechanisms behind the neurological symptoms of LNS, more research integrating advanced imaging techniques, neurochemical analyses, and genetic investigations is required. The significance of long-term video-EEG surveillance in LNS is emphasized by Roza et al.31 to detect any potential clinical or latent epileptic episodes that may further harm an LNS child's neuropsychomotor development.

Laboratory tests in included studies revealed consistently higher uric acid levels and a high uric acid-to-creatinine ratio, indicating an excess of purine synthesis. Detecting genetic abnormalities in the HPRT1 gene is regarded as a gold standard for making a final diagnosis of the condition. LNS screening should be performed in patients with intellectual disability, dystonic movement disorder, and hyperuricemia. The commercial hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) enzyme activity detection reagent may significantly enhance detection efficiency while lowering screening costs.26 Similarly, Ceballos-Picot et al.'s32 study of metabolites isolated from red blood cells of 56 LN cases indicates unique variations in six metabolites—AICAR mono- and triphosphate, nicotinamide, nicotinic acid, ATP, and succinyl-AMP—that can be utilized to differentiate HGPRT deficiency. These metabolites offer an effective method of diagnosing disease timely and precisely. Prenatal testing is an alternative, particularly for male infants with a familial diagnosis of LNS. Methods such as chorionic villus collection and amniocentesis can detect genetic abnormalities in pregnancy.1

Oral self-mutilation in LNS is distinct from spasticity to choreoathetosis, warranting particular attention. Both the modification of self-harming behaviors and the control of CNS dynamics should be part of therapeutic approaches. Antipsychotic, anticonvulsant, and antianxiety drugs are among the pharmacological therapies for neurobehavioral symptoms, with Allopurinol7,9,12,16,23 and recurrent botulinum toxin (BTX) injections into facial muscles emerging as promising therapeutic choices in our review,7,16 which is in line with other studies as well.1,33 Notably, surgical procedures like tooth extraction5,7,8,13,14,17,22 and reconstructive surgery show promise in treating oral self-mutilation, as shown in our findings, underlining the need for dental care. Conservative techniques such as intraoral devices,5,14,16,22 lip bumpers,25 and behavioral techniques help reduce symptoms. A combination of physical restriction, behavioral management approaches, and medication is required to control episodes of oral self-mutilation. Avoiding stressful situations and avoiding harsh approaches are critical. Under controlled conditions, behavioral suppression strategies show some efficacy.33 Some case reports have shown deep brain stimulation to be effective in reducing self-injurious behavior,34 while enzyme transfer and gene therapy applications hold promise for a complete treatment of this metabolic anomaly. A varied response to different therapies in different individuals necessitates individualized, multidisciplinary strategies.

Most patients in the included studies presented with perioral symptoms, dental caries, and poor oral hygiene. Loss of substance from the lip was a prominent extraoral sign. It is pertinent to note that most of the literature we reviewed did not mention laboratory investigations and serum uric acid levels. Given the challenges faced by researchers and clinicians in syndromic patients, the paper tabulates pharmacological and nonpharmacological management with their reported success. These suggestions can potentially guide pediatricians and dentists to formulate a treatment plan. Acrylic appliances with minimal appendages and clasps have shown promise in case reports to prevent oral lesions and facilitate their healing.9 However, empathy and alleviating the underlying biophysiological cause will remain the mainstay of definitive management in the future.

Strength and Limitation

With clear inclusion and quality assessment criteria, we conducted a thorough literature search across five databases for our systematic review without time constraints. However, it is restricted by articles published in English and the quality of data provided in case reports. Without randomized trials and clinical papers, we included case reports, which may be prone to inadequate information, reporting errors, and restricted statistical analysis. Since there are no standardized diagnostic criteria or treatment methods for LNS, it may inadvertently result in inconsistencies. In many cases, the absence of follow-up data limits our capacity to analyze treatment outcomes comprehensively. To strengthen the rigor of our knowledge of LNS and its management, future research should aim for more extensive, broader samples, standardized diagnostic criteria, and robust methodology.

Conclusion

The rare nature of this syndrome and the delay in the onset of definitive symptoms pose a challenge to its diagnosis. The compilation of physical findings, laboratory investigations, and treatment summarized in our systematic review provides a comprehensive update on the status of the literature on LNS. Allopurinol, BTX-A, and tooth extraction are prominent in the therapeutic landscape. Collaborative research, documentation, and follow-up of these unique cases with an assessment of which therapy works optimally would help clinicians formulate standard robust guidelines.
