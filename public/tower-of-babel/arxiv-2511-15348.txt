Introduction

The direct and inverse scattering theory of the AKNS system

\boldsymbol{v}_{x}=(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q})\boldsymbol{v},

(1.1)

where
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix of order
n
with nonzero real diagonal
entries,
\mathcal{Q}(x)
is an
n\times n
matrix potential anticommuting with
\Sigma

with its entries belonging to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
is position, has been
studied at great length
[
31
,
2
,
1
,
4
,
8
,
13
,
3
]
. Its direct scattering
theory relies primarily on the linear relation between
n
linearly independent
Jost solutions continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
and analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
n

linearly independent Jost solutions continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
and analytic
in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
, where
\Sigma
is a real diagonal matrix of order
n
. The
scattering data resulting from the direct scattering theory consist of two sets
of reflection coefficients and as many norming constants as there are discrete
eigenvalues in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
or in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
. Its inverse scattering theory consists of
converting the scattering data in a Marchenko integral equation whose solution
leads to the potential
\mathcal{Q}(x)
. An alternative way of implementing inverse
scattering consists of converting the scattering data in a Riemann-Hilbert
problem whose solution leads to the potential
\mathcal{Q}(x)
. The direct and inverse
scattering theory of the AKNS system (
1.1
) with nonsingular real diagonal
matrix
\Sigma
has been worked out in various textbooks
[
1
,
8
,
13
,
3
]
.
A general theory of developing the direct and inverse scattering for arbitrary
nonsingular complex diagonal matrix
\Sigma
can be found in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Few publications are devoted to AKNS systems (
1.1
), where
\Sigma
is a real
diagonal matrix with at least one of its diagonal entries vanishing. Among the
very few papers devoted to the subject matter is that by Newell
[
25
]
, where

\Sigma=\text{diag}(1,0,-1)
. In this article the author defines two Jost solutions
analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and two Jost solutions analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
.
Unfortunately, one would expect three Jost solutions analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
three Jost solutions analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
, thus rendering incomplete the
direct scattering theory expounded in
[
25
]
. In a recent paper
[
12
]

we have solved this problem for
n=3
and
\Sigma=\text{diag}(1,0,-1)
. In the
present article we shall extend this result to arbitrary
n\geq 3
and

\Sigma=\text{diag}(I_{m_{+}},0,-I_{m_{-}})
with
\min(m_{+},m_{-})\geq 1
.

In this article we develop a direct and inverse scattering theory of the AKNS
system (
1.1
), where

\Sigma=\text{diag}(1,\ldots,1,0,-1,\ldots,-1)

with
m_{+}\geq 1
diagonal entries
+1
, one diagonal entry zero,
m_{-}\geq 1

diagonal entries
-1
, and
m_{+}+m_{-}=n-1
. To define the
n
-th linearly
independent Jost solution analytic in either
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
or in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
,
we take the wedge products of the complex conjugates of the
n-1
dual Jost
solutions satisfying
\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}_{x}=-\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q})
, where we adopt
an idea applied before to the Manakov system with
\Sigma=\text{diag}(1,-1,-1)

with nonvanishing boundary conditions
[
26
]
. Similar constructions of
an
n
-th Jost-like solution have been given before by Deift et al.
[
11
]
,
Kaup
[
18
]
, and McKean
[
24
]
. Once we have defined
n
linearly
independent Jost solutions analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
written as the columns of
an
n\times n
matrix and
n
linearly independent Jost solutions analytic in

k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
written as the columns of another
n\times n
matrix, we write the
first
n\times n
matrix as the other multiplied from the right by
an
n\times n
matrix depending only on
k\in\mathbb{R}
, where the diagonal entries are
called the transmission coefficients and, apart from minus signs, the
off-diagonal entries are called reflection coefficients. We thus find the
reflection coefficients and, corresponding to each pole of the transmission
coefficients in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
or in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
, the so-called norming constants. The
inverse scattering problem can then be solved alternatively by either the
Marchenko method or the Riemann-Hilbert method. The direct and inverse
scattering theory of the AKNS system (
1.1
), where
\Sigma
contains at least
two zero diagonal entries and the other diagonal entries are
\pm 1
, remains
open.

The study of one-dimensional wave propagation poses several challenging
mathematical problems
[
28
,
20
]

studying the interaction between long waves and short waves is the AKNS system
studied by Newell
[
25
]
. Similar integrable equations were formulated by
Yan-Chow Ma
[
22
]
, Yajima and Oikawa
[
30
]
, Ruomeng Li and Xianguo Geng

[

]
, and Wang et al.
[
27
]
. More recently, Caso-Huerta et al.

[
10
,
9
]
have studied more general long-wave-short-wave interaction
models. These so-called YON equations can be derived from the zero curvature
condition

\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{T}_{x}+\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{X}=0_{3\times 3},

(1.2)

where the Lax pair
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{T})
is given by
[
10
, Eqs. (2.2), (3.1), and
(3.2)]
as well as by
[
9
, Eqs. (7)]
. The nonlinear wave equation
systems studied in
[
10
,
9
]
have been generalized by Wright

[
29
, Eqs. (52)-(53)]
.

In the present
n\times n
situation the Lax pair
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{T})
, where

\boldsymbol{X}=ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q}
with potential
\mathcal{Q}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&S&iL\\
T^{\dagger}&0&S^{\dagger}\\
iK&T&\boldsymbol{0}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and

\boldsymbol{T}=(ik)^{2}A+ikB+C
with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{n}\text{diag}(I_{m_{+}},1-n,I_{m_{-}}),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&iS&\boldsymbol{0}\\
iT^{\dagger}&0&-iS^{\dagger}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&-iT&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}-iST^{\dagger}&iS_{x}-LT&iSS^{\dagger}\\
-T_{x}^{\dagger}-S^{\dagger}K&i[T^{\dagger}S+S^{\dagger}T]&-iS_{x}^{\dagger}-T^{\dagger}L\\
iTT^{\dagger}&iT_{x}-KS&-iTS^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix},

leads to the six coupled long-wave-short-wave equations

\displaystyle iL_{t}-2i(SS^{\dagger})_{x}-2ST^{\dagger}L+2LTS^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=0_{m_{+}\times m_{-}},

(1.3a)

\displaystyle iK_{t}-2i(TT^{\dagger})_{x}-2TS^{\dagger}K+2KST^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=0_{m_{-}\times m_{+}},

(1.3b)

\displaystyle T_{t}-iT_{xx}+K_{x}S-iKLT+2iTS^{\dagger}T

\displaystyle=0_{m_{-}\times 1},

(1.3c)

\displaystyle S_{t}-iS_{xx}+L_{x}T-iLKS+2iST^{\dagger}S

\displaystyle=0_{m_{+}\times 1},

(1.3d)

\displaystyle T_{t}^{\dagger}+iT_{xx}^{\dagger}+S^{\dagger}K_{x}+iT^{\dagger}LK-2iT^{\dagger}ST^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=0_{1\times m_{+}},

(1.3e)

\displaystyle S_{t}^{\dagger}+iS_{xx}^{\dagger}+T^{\dagger}L_{x}+iS^{\dagger}KL-2iS^{\dagger}TS^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=0_{1\times m_{-}},

(1.3f)

where

and

are real symmetric long-wave square matrix functions and

and

are complex short-wave column vector functions. Note that (

)
and (

) and

[

9
]
the special case where
n=3
,

, and

\alpha>0

Jost solutions

Let us define the Jost matrices
\Psi(x,k)
and
\Phi(x,k)
as those
n\times n

matrix solutions of the AKNS system (
1.1
) satisfying the asymptotic
conditions

\displaystyle\Psi(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to+\infty,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+o(1)],

\displaystyle x\to-\infty,

(2.1b)

where
\det\Psi(x,k)=\det\Phi(x,k)=e^{ikx(m_{+}-m_{-})}
for
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Using
that the entries of the off-diagonal matrix
\mathcal{Q}(x)
belong to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we
easily arrive at the Volterra integral equations

\displaystyle\Psi(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{ikx\Sigma}-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)\Psi(y,k),

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{ikx\Sigma}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)\Phi(y,k).

(2.2b)

In terms of the Faddeev functions
M(x,k)=\Psi(x,k)e^{-ikx\Sigma}
and

N(x,k)=\Phi(x,k)e^{-ikx\Sigma}
, we obtain the Volterra integral equations

\displaystyle M(x,k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)M(y,k)e^{ik(y-x)\Sigma},

(2.3a)

\displaystyle N(x,k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)e^{-ik(x-y)\Sigma}.

(2.3b)

Letting
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
stand for a hermitian and unitary
n\times n
matrix, we call the
potential
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-
focusing
if

\mathcal{Q}(x)^{\dagger}=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathcal{Q}(x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(2.4)

where the dagger stands for the matrix conjugate transpose. If
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=I_{n}
, the
potential is called
focusing
. It is easily verified that for

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing potentials and
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\Psi(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Psi(x,k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad\Phi(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Phi(x,k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(2.5a)

\displaystyle M(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}M(x,k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad N(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}N(x,k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(2.5b)

whenever
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
\Sigma
commute. On the contrary,

\displaystyle\Psi(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Psi(x,-k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad\Phi(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Phi(x,-k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle M(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}M(x,-k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad N(x,k)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}N(x,-k)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(2.6b)

whenever
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
\Sigma
anticommute. We observe that the
n\times n
matrices

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
having diagonal blocks of the respective orders
m_{+}
,
1
, and
m_{-}
,
have the form

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\sigma_{00}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{if}\ \boldsymbol{\sigma}\Sigma=\Sigma\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\sigma_{00}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{if}\ \boldsymbol{\sigma}\Sigma=-\Sigma\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ \text{and}\ m_{+}=m_{-}}.

(2.7)

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is hermitian and unitary and commutes with
\Sigma
, then

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}
are hermitian and unitary and
\sigma_{00}=\pm 1
. On
the other hand, if
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is hermitian and unitary and anticommutes with
\Sigma
,
then
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}
are unitary and each other’s adjoints (and
hence
m_{+}=m_{-}=\tfrac{1}{2}(n-1)
) and
\sigma_{00}=\pm 1
.

Define the following submatrices of the
n\times n
identity matrix
I_{n}
:

\boldsymbol{e}_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m_{+}}\\
0_{(1+m_{-})\times m_{+}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{m_{+}}\\
1\\
0_{m_{-}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{-}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{(m_{+}+1)\times m_{-}}\\
I_{m_{-}}\end{bmatrix}.

Theorem 2.1

There exist
n\times n
matrices
K(x,y)
and
J(x,y)
such that the triangular
representations

\displaystyle M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{ik(y-x)}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{-ik(x-y)}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

(2.8c)

\displaystyle N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

(2.8d)

are true, where the integrals

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\|K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}\|+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\|J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}\|

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\|K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\|+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\|J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\|,

(2.9b)

converge uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Define the
n\times n
diagonal matrices

\boldsymbol{N}_{+}=2I_{m_{+}}\oplus 1\oplus 0_{m_{-}},\qquad\boldsymbol{N}_{-}=0_{m_{+}}\oplus 1\oplus 2I_{m_{-}}.

Postmultiplying (
2.3
) by
\boldsymbol{e}_{+}
or
\boldsymbol{e}_{-}
, we obtain

\displaystyle M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{ik(y-x)\boldsymbol{N}_{-}}\mathcal{Q}(y)M(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)\boldsymbol{N}_{+}}\mathcal{Q}(y)M(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{-ik(x-y)\boldsymbol{N}_{-}}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)\boldsymbol{N}_{+}}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}.

Substituting (
2.8
) in (
2.3
) and stripping off the Fourier transform
we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=-\int_{x}^{\infty}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,z+y-x)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}-\int_{x}^{y}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y])\boldsymbol{e}_{+}-\int_{x}^{\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y]}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,x+y-z)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y])\boldsymbol{e}_{-}-\int_{x}^{\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y]}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,x+y-z)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}-\int_{x}^{y}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=-\int_{x}^{\infty}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)K(z,z+y-x)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,z+y-x)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\int_{y}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y])\boldsymbol{e}_{+}+\int_{\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y]}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,x+y-z)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y])\boldsymbol{e}_{-}+\int_{\tfrac{1}{2}[x+y]}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,x+y-z)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}+\int_{y}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(z)J(z,z+y-x)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}.

Since each entry of
\mathcal{Q}(x)
belongs to

, we obtain (
2.9
) with
the help of Gronwall’s inequality.

(2.11b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}J(x,x)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{+0}(z)\boldsymbol{q}_{0+}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{+-}(z)\boldsymbol{q}_{-+}(z)\right),

(2.11c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}J(x,x)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}dz\left(\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{-+}(z)\boldsymbol{q}_{+-}(z)+\boldsymbol{q}_{-0}(z)\boldsymbol{q}_{0-}(z)\right).

(2.11d)

The last four expressions allow us to compute the remaining blocks
\boldsymbol{q}_{+0}(x)

and
\boldsymbol{q}_{-0}(x)
of
\mathcal{Q}(x)
from either
K(x,x)
or
J(x,x)
, provided

\boldsymbol{q}_{0+}(x)
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{0-}(x)
are nonsingular matrices. The remaining blocks
can also be computed from either
K(x,x)
or
J(x,x)
if the potential is

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing.

3
Dual scattering solutions

In the preceding section we have defined
2(n-1)
out of the
2n
Jost
solutions, derived their Volterra integral equations, and proved their
triangular representations. To define the remaining two Jost solutions, we first
need to introduce the so-called dual Jost solutions.

3.1
Dual Jost solutions

The dual Jost solutions
\breve{\Psi}(x,k)
and
\breve{\Phi}(x,k)
are defined
as the unique solutions of the Volterra integral equations

\displaystyle\breve{\Psi}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\Sigma}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\breve{\Psi}(y,k)\mathcal{Q}(y)e^{ik(y-x)\Sigma},

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\breve{\Phi}(x,k)

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\Sigma}-\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\breve{\Phi}(y,k)\mathcal{Q}(y)e^{-ik(x-y)\Sigma},

(3.1b)

where
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then
\breve{\Psi}(x,k)
and
\breve{\Phi}(x,k)
satisfy
the first order system

\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}_{x}=-\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q})

(3.2)

with asymptotic conditions

\displaystyle\breve{\Psi}(x,k)

\displaystyle=[I_{n}+o(1)]e^{-ikx\Sigma},

\displaystyle x\to+\infty,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\breve{\Phi}(x,k)

\displaystyle=[I_{n}+o(1)]e^{-ikx\Sigma},

\displaystyle x\to-\infty.

(3.3b)

It is easily verified that

\breve{\Psi}(x,k)=\Psi(x,k)^{-1},\qquad\breve{\Phi}(x,k)=\Phi(x,k)^{-1},

(3.4)

where
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We now introduce the dual Faddeev functions

\breve{M}(x,k)=e^{ikx\Sigma}\breve{\Psi}(x,k),\qquad\breve{N}(x,k)=e^{ikx\Sigma}\breve{\Phi}(x,k).

Then
\breve{M}(x,k)=M(x,k)^{-1}
and
\breve{N}(x,k)=N(x,k)^{-1}
satisfy the
Volterra integral equations

\displaystyle\breve{M}(x,k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)\Sigma}\breve{M}(y,k)\mathcal{Q}(y)e^{ik(y-x)\Sigma},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\breve{N}(x,k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)\Sigma}\breve{N}(y,k)\mathcal{Q}(y)e^{-ik(x-y)\Sigma}.

(3.5b)

Moreover, we have the triangular representations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{M}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-ik(y-x)}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x),

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{M}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}+\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,e^{ik(y-x)}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x),

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{N}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{ik(x-y)}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x),

(3.6c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{N}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,e^{-ik(x-y)}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x),

(3.6d)

where

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\|\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\|

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\|\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\|<+\infty,

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\|\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\|

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{x}dy\,\|\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\|<+\infty.

The proof of these triangular representations is based on Volterra integral
equations for
\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}^{T}\breve{J}(x,y)
which are
very similar to those for
K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}
and
J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{\pm}
.

Let us consider the adjoint symmetries of the first and third columns of the
Faddeev functions. If the potential is
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
commutes with

\Sigma
and hence satisfies
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}\oplus\sigma_{00}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}
, we get

\displaystyle[M(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{M}(x,k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.7a)

\displaystyle[N(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{N}(x,k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.7b)

\displaystyle[M(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{M}(x,k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.7c)

\displaystyle[N(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{N}(x,k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(3.7d)

Using (
2.8
) and (
3.6
) we obtain from (
3.7
)

\displaystyle[K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad[J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{++}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.8a)

\displaystyle[K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

\displaystyle\quad[J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{--}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(3.8b)

On the other hand, if the potential is
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
anticommutes
with
\Sigma
and hence has the form (
2.7
) for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Sigma=-\Sigma\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we get

\displaystyle[M(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{M}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.9a)

\displaystyle[N(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{N}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.9b)

\displaystyle[M(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{M}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.9c)

\displaystyle[N(x,k^{*})\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{N}(x,-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(3.9d)

Using (
2.8
) and (
3.6
) we obtain from (
3.9
)

\displaystyle[K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},\quad[J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+-}\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(3.10a)

\displaystyle[K(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{K}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma},\quad[J(x,y)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-+}\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\breve{J}(y,x)\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(3.10b)

3.2
The missing two Jost solutions

The
n-1
columns of the Faddeev matrices
M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}
and
N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}
yield

n-1
linearly independent solutions of the differential system

W_{x}=(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q})W-ikW\Sigma

(3.11)

that are continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
, are analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and tend to
one of the columns of
I_{n}
(with the exception of the
(m_{+}+1)
-st column) as

k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
. Analogously, the
n-1
columns of the
Faddeev matrices
N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}
and
M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}
yield
n-1
linearly
independent solutions of the differential system (
3.11
) that are
continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
, are analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
, and tend to one of
the columns of
I_{n}
(with the exception of the
(m_{+}+1)
-st column) as

k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
. Our objective is to construct the missing
two Faddeev functions with the correct analyticity properties.

Let
\breve{\Psi}(x,k)
and
\breve{\Phi}(x,k)
denote the dual Jost solutions.
Then their rows
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{T}\breve{\Psi}(x,k)
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}^{T}\breve{\Phi}(x,k)

satisfy (
3.2
). Thus their transposes
\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
and

\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
satisfy the differential system

\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}_{x}=-(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q}^{T})\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}.

Hence for each constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
the function

\breve{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)=e^{\alpha x}\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}(x)^{T}
satisfies the differential system

\breve{\boldsymbol{w}}_{x}=(\alpha I_{n}-ik\Sigma-\mathcal{Q}^{T})\breve{\boldsymbol{w}}=B\breve{\boldsymbol{w}},

where
B=\alpha I_{n}-ik\Sigma-\mathcal{Q}^{T}
. To get
\breve{\boldsymbol{w}}
to satisy the AKNS system
(
1.1
) we need to choose
\alpha
in such a way that the identity

(\text{Tr}\,B)I_{n}-B^{T}=ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q}

is satisfied. To do so we choose
\alpha=\text{Tr}\,B=\tfrac{ik(m_{+}-m_{-})}{n-1}
.
Thus if
\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}^{1},\ldots,\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}^{n-1}
are
n-1
linearly independent
row vector solutions of the AKNS system
\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}_{x}=-\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}(ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q})

and
\alpha=\tfrac{ik(m_{+}-m_{-})}{n-1}
, then the wedge product

(e^{\alpha x}{\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{1})^{T}\wedge\ldots\wedge(e^{\alpha x}{\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}}^{n-1})^{T},

is a vector solution of the AKNS system (
1.1
), provided we write this
wedge product with respect to the basis
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\}
of

\Lambda^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
[See Appendix
A
]. Hence, with respect to the basis

\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\}
of
\Lambda^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the vector functions

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}(x,k)

\displaystyle\simeq(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\left(e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1-m_{-}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\right),

(3.12a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{-}(x,k)

\displaystyle\simeq(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\left(e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1-m_{-}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{\alpha x}\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\right),

(3.12b)

are solutions of the AKNS system (
1.1
). Using that

\displaystyle\breve{\Psi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e^{-ikx}\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j},&j=1,\ldots,m_{+},\\
e^{ikx}\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j},&j=n+1-m_{-},\ldots,n,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\breve{\Phi}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e^{-ikx}\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j},&j=1,\ldots,m_{+},\\
e^{ikx}\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j},&j=n+1-m_{-},\ldots,n,\end{cases}

while

\left(e^{\alpha x}e^{-ikx}\right)^{m_{+}}\left(e^{\alpha x}e^{ikx}\right)^{m_{-}}=\left[e^{-\tfrac{2ikm_{-}x}{n-1}}\right]^{m_{+}}\left[e^{\tfrac{2ikm_{+}x}{n-1}}\right]^{m_{-}}=1,

we obtain

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}(x,k)

\displaystyle\simeq(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\left(\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\wedge\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1-m_{-}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\right),

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{-}(x,k)

\displaystyle\simeq(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\left(\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\breve{M}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\wedge\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1-m_{-}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\breve{N}(x,k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\right).

(3.13b)

Hence,
\Omega^{\pm}(x,k)
is continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}
, is analytic in

k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}
, and tends to
(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\boldsymbol{g}_{m_{+}+1}=\boldsymbol{d}_{m_{+}+1}
(to be
identified with
\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}+1}
) as
k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

Let us now write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{+}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}&\Omega^{+}(x,k)&\Phi(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}&\Omega^{-}(x,k)&\Psi(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\end{bmatrix}.

Then
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\pm}(x,k)
is an
n\times n
matrix solution of the AKNS system
(
1.1
) such that
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\pm}(x,k)e^{-ikx\Sigma}
is continuous in

k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}
, is analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}
, and tends to
I_{n}
as

k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}
. We observe that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{+}(x,k)e^{-ikx\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}&\Omega^{+}(x,k)&M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-}(x,k)e^{-ikx\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}M(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}&\Omega^{-}(x,k)&N(x,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}\end{bmatrix}.

4
Scattering matrices

In this section we define the transition coefficients
A_{l}(k)
and
A_{r}(k)

which act as matrix coupling constants between the Jost matrices
\Psi(x,k)
and

\Phi(x,k)
and between the dual Jost matrices
\breve{\Psi}(x,k)
and

\breve{\Phi}(x,k)
. We then go to define the transition coefficients coupling

\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{+}(x,k)
and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-}(x,k)
.

4.1
Transition matrices

Since the Jost solutions
\Psi(x,k)
and
\Phi(x,k)
satisfy the same
homogeneous first order system (
1.1
), there exist so-called transition
matrices
A_{l}(k)
and
A_{r}(k)
, one the inverse of the other, such that

\Psi(x,k)=\Phi(x,k)A_{l}(k),\qquad\Phi(x,k)=\Psi(x,k)A_{r}(k),

(4.1)

where
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Similarly, since the dual Jost solutions satisfy the same
homogeneous linear first order system (
3.2
), there exist so-called dual
transition matrices
\breve{A}_{l}(k)
and
\breve{A}_{r}(k)
, one the inverse of
the other, such that

\breve{\Psi}(x,k)=\breve{A}_{l}(k)\breve{\Phi}(x,k),\qquad\breve{\Phi}(x,k)=\breve{A}_{r}(k)\breve{\Psi}(x,k),

(4.2)

where
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then (
3.4
) imply that

\breve{A}_{l}(k)=A_{l}(k)^{-1}=A_{r}(k),\qquad\breve{A}_{r}(k)=A_{r}(k)^{-1}=A_{l}(k),

(4.3)

where
\det A_{l}(k)=\det A_{r}(k)=1
for every
k\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing
potentials commuting with
\Sigma
we obtain for
k\in\mathbb{R}
the symmetry relations

A_{l}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}A_{r}(k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad A_{r}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}A_{l}(k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(4.4)

thus making
A_{l}(k)
and
A_{r}(k)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-unitary. Thus for focusing potentials
the transition matrices
A_{l}(k)
and
A_{r}(k)
are unitary. For
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-focusing
potentials anticommuting with
\Sigma
we obtain for
k\in\mathbb{R}
the symmetry
relations

A_{l}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}A_{r}(-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad A_{r}(k)^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}A_{l}(-k)\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

(4.5)

Premultiplying (
2.2
) by
e^{-ikx\Sigma}
and using (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{l}(k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-iky\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)M(y,k)e^{iky\Sigma},

(4.6a)

\displaystyle A_{r}(k)

\displaystyle=I_{n}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-iky\Sigma}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)e^{iky\Sigma}.

(4.6b)

As a result,

\displaystyle A_{l}(k)_{++}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}A_{l}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}=I_{m_{+}}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)M(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle A_{r}(k)_{--}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}A_{r}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}=I_{m_{-}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

(4.7b)

are continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
, are analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and
tend to the identity as
k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
, whereas

\displaystyle A_{r}(k)_{++}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}A_{r}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+}=I_{m_{+}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{e}_{+}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{+},

(4.7c)

\displaystyle A_{l}(k)_{--}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}A_{l}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-}=I_{m_{-}}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\boldsymbol{e}_{-}^{T}\mathcal{Q}(y)N(y,k)\boldsymbol{e}_{-},

(4.7d)

are continuous in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
, are analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
, and tend to the
identity as
k\to\infty
from within
\mathbb{C}^{-}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

4.2
Asymptotic properties

Using (
2.2
) and (
3.1
) we easily prove that

\displaystyle e^{-ikx\Sigma}\Psi(x,k)

\displaystyle\to\begin{cases}I_{n},&x\to+\infty,\\

\displaystyle\ \breve{\Phi}(x,k)e^{ikx\Sigma}

\displaystyle\to\begin{cases}A_{l}(k),&x\to+\infty,\\
I_{n},&x\to-\infty.\end{cases}

Using the above, we easily verify the asymptotic identities

\displaystyle e^{-ikx\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{+}(x,k)

\displaystyle\to\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m_{+}}&\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)_{+0}&A_{r}(k)_{+-}\\

A_{l}(k)_{-+}&\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)_{-0}&I_{m_{-}}\end{bmatrix},&x\to-\infty,\end{cases}

(4.8a)

\displaystyle e^{-ikx\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-}(x,k)

\displaystyle\to\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}A_{r}(k)_{++}&\xcancel{\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)_{+0}}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
A_{r}(k)_{0+}&\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)_{00}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
A_{r}(k)_{-+}&\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)_{-0}&I_{m_{-}}\end{bmatrix},&x\to+\infty,\\
\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m_{+}}&\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)_{+0}&A_{l}(k)_{+-}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)_{00}&A_{l}(k)_{0-}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\xcancel{\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)_{-0}}&A_{l}(k)_{--}\end{bmatrix},&x\to-\infty,\end{cases}

(4.8b)

where the cancelled entries will turn out to be zero matrices. Then the second
columns coincide with the expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\text{\tiny up}}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{0}=(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\times

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\times\left(e^{ikx\boldsymbol{N}_{-}}A_{l}(k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{ikx\boldsymbol{N}_{-}}A_{l}(k)^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{+}}\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1-m_{+}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{+}^{\text{\tiny dn}}(k)\boldsymbol{e}_{0}=(-1)^{m_{+}m_{-}}\times

(4.9b)

\hat{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}(\gamma+2x)_{31}&\hat{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}(\gamma+x)_{32}&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\gamma\,e^{ik\gamma}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\gamma+(\Sigma_{j}-\Sigma_{i})x)_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{3},

(5.2b)

where

with
\Sigma_{1}=1
,

\Sigma_{2}=0

\Sigma_{3}=-1
, and the entries of

. Consequently, we have arrived at the

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{-}(x,x-\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\left(e^{ik_{j}\gamma}\boldsymbol{N}_{j}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\overline{\gamma}\,e^{ik_{j}(\gamma+\overline{\gamma})}\mathbb{K}^{+}(x,x+\overline{\gamma})\boldsymbol{N}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle-\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\gamma+(\Sigma_{i}-\Sigma_{j})x)_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{3}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\overline{\gamma}\,\mathbb{K}^{+}(x,x+\overline{\gamma})\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{\gamma}+\gamma+(\Sigma_{i}-\Sigma_{j})x)_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{3},

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{+}(x,x+\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\left(e^{-i\tilde{k}_{j}\gamma}\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}_{j}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\overline{\gamma}\,e^{-i\tilde{k}_{j}(\gamma+\overline{\gamma})}\mathbb{K}^{-}(x,x-\overline{\gamma})\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle-\left[\hat{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}(\gamma+(\Sigma_{i}-\Sigma_{j})x)_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{3}

(5.4b)

Time evolution of the scattering data

In this section we study the time evolution of the scattering data of the AKNS
system (
1.1
) with
\Sigma=\text{diag}(I_{m_{+}},0,-I_{m_{-}})
associated with
the solution of the generalized long-wave-short-wave equations (
1.3
) by
means of the inverse scattering transform.

Let
\boldsymbol{V}(x,t)
be a nonsingular
n\times n
matrix solution of the first order
system

\boldsymbol{V}_{t}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V},\qquad\boldsymbol{V}_{t}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{V},

(6.1)

where
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{T})
is the Lax pair given by
\boldsymbol{X}=ik\Sigma+\mathcal{Q}
and

\boldsymbol{T}=(ik)^{2}A-ikB+C
with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{n}\text{diag}(I_{m_{+}},1-n,I_{m_{-}}),

(6.2a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{m_{+}\times m_{+}}&S&iL\\
T^{\dagger}&0&S^{\dagger}\\
iK&T&0_{m_{-}\times m_{-}}\end{bmatrix},

(6.2b)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{m_{+}\times m_{+}}&iS&0_{m_{+}\times m_{-}}\\
iT^{\dagger}&0&-iS^{\dagger}\\
0_{m_{-}\times m_{+}}&-iT&0_{m_{-}\times m_{-}}\end{bmatrix},

(6.2c)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-iST^{\dagger}&iS_{x}-LT&iSS^{\dagger}\\
-iT_{x}^{\dagger}-S^{\dagger}K&i[T^{\dagger}S+S^{\dagger}T]&-iS_{x}^{\dagger}-T^{\dagger}L\\
iII^{\dagger}&iT_{x}-KS&-iTS^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}.

(6.2d)

Then
\boldsymbol{V}(x,t)
can be postmultiplied in (
6.1
) by any nonsingular

n\times n
matrix independent of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For any invertible matrix
solution
W(x,t)
of (
1.1
) there exists an invertible
n\times n
matrix

C_{W}
, not depending on
x
, such that

W=\boldsymbol{V}C_{W}^{-1}.

Then

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}_{t}C_{W}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{V}C_{W}^{-1}[C_{W}]_{t}C_{W}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{V}C_{W}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{V}C_{W}^{-1}[C_{W}]_{t}C_{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{T}W-W[C_{W}]_{t}C_{W}^{-1}

implies the important identity

[C_{W}]_{t}C_{W}^{-1}=W^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}W-W^{-1}W_{t},

(6.3)

where the left-hand side (and hence also the right-hand side) does not depend on

x
. We may therefore exploit the asymptotic behavior of
W
and
\boldsymbol{T}
as

x\to\pm\infty
to write the right-hand side of (
6.2
) in a simplified way.
Using that
\Sigma
and
A
commute, we thus get as

\displaystyle[C_{\Psi}]_{t}C_{\Psi}^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+0(1)][(ik)^{2}A+o(1)]e^{ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+o(1)]=-k^{2}A,

(6.4a)

\displaystyle[C_{\Phi}]_{t}C_{\Phi}^{-1}

\displaystyle=e^{-ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+0(1)][(ik)^{2}A+o(1)]e^{ikx\Sigma}[I_{n}+o(1)]=-k^{2}A.

(6.4b)

Hence,
C_{\Psi}(k;t)=e^{-k^{2}tA}C_{\Psi}(k;0)
and

C_{\Phi}(k;t)=e^{-k^{2}tA}C_{\Phi}(k;0)
.

Recalling the definitions (
4.1
) of the transition matrices

A_{l}=\Phi^{-1}\Psi
and
A_{r}=\Psi^{-1}\Phi

\displaystyle[A_{l}]_{t}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}\Psi_{t}-\Phi^{-1}\Phi_{t}\Phi^{-1}\Psi

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}\Psi_{t}-\Phi^{-1}\Phi_{t}A_{l}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{T}\Psi-\Psi[C_{\Psi}]_{t}C_{\Psi}^{-1}\right)-\Phi^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{T}\Phi-\Phi[C_{\Phi}]_{t}C_{\Phi}^{-1}\right)A_{l}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}\Psi-A_{l}[C_{\Psi}]_{t}C_{\Psi}^{-1}-\Phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}A_{l}+[C_{\Phi}]_{t}C_{\Phi}^{-1}A_{l}

\displaystyle=[C_{\Phi}]_{t}C_{\Phi}^{-1}A_{l}-A_{l}[C_{\Psi}]_{t}C_{\Psi}^{-1}=k^{2}(A_{l}(k)A-AA_{l}(k)).

Appendix A
Linear equations for wedge products

The exterior algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as the graded associate algebra

\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\,\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathbb{R}
,
\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consists of the
finite linear combinations of all elements
\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{v}^{k}
for

\boldsymbol{v}^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,k
) (See
[
23
]
). Here the wedge product

\wedge
is multilinear, associative, and antisymmetric. The canonical basis of

\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of
\binom{n}{k}
entries

\left\{\boldsymbol{e}_{r_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{r_{k}}\right\}_{r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}},

where
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\Lambda^{n-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an
n
-dimensional real vector space with bases

\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\}
related by the
equations
[
23
]

\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\qquad\boldsymbol{d}_{j}=(-1)^{(j-1)(n-j)}\boldsymbol{g}_{j},\quad j=2,\ldots,n-1,\qquad\boldsymbol{d}_{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{n},

(A.1)

where, for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{j-1}
and

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=\boldsymbol{e}_{j+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\boldsymbol{e}_{j+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{j-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\xcancel{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{e}_{n}.

For
n=3
we can identify the wedge product with the vector product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

and obtain
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\simeq\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
(
j=1,2,3
).

Writing
A=e^{xB}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

another linear transformation, then the mapping
B^{(n)}_{k}
defined by

\displaystyle B^{(n)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\boldsymbol{v}^{k})=\left.\frac{d}{dx}e^{xB}\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{xB}\boldsymbol{v}^{k}\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dx}(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}^{k}\right|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\,(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}^{j-1}\wedge B\boldsymbol{v}^{j}

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}}\left.\wedge(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}^{j+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge(I_{n}+xB)\boldsymbol{v}^{k}\right|_{x=0}

(A.2)

Appendix B
Wiener algebras

By the (continuous) Wiener algebra
\mathcal{W}
we mean the complex vector space of
constants plus Fourier transforms of
L^{1}
-functions

\mathcal{W}=\{c+\hat{h}:c\in\mathbb{C},\ h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\}

endowed with the norm
|c|+\|h\|_{1}
. Here we define the Fourier transform as
follows:
(\mathcal{F}h)(k)=\hat{h}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{iky}h(y)
.
The invertible elements of the commutative Banach algebra
\mathcal{W}
with unit
element are exactly those
c+\hat{h}\in\mathcal{W}
for which
c\neq 0
and

c+\hat{h}(k)\neq 0
for each
k\in\mathbb{R}

[
14
,
15
,
17
]
.

The algebra
\mathcal{W}
has the two closed subalgebras
\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{W}^{-}
consisting
of those
c+\hat{h}
such that
h
is supported on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{-}
,
respectively. The invertible elements of
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
are exactly those

c+\hat{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
for which
c\neq 0
and
c+\hat{h}(k)\neq 0
for each

k\in\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\cup\mathbb{R}

[
14
,
15
,
17
]
. Letting
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}
stand for
the (nonunital) closed subalgebras of
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{W}
consisting of those

c+\hat{h}
for which
c=0
, we obtain the direct sum decompositions

\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{W}_{0}=\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0}.

By
\Pi_{\pm}
we now denote the (bounded) projections of
\mathcal{W}
onto
\mathcal{W}^{\pm}_{0}

along
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathcal{W}^{\mp}_{0}
. Then
\Pi_{+}
and
\Pi_{-}
are complementary
projections. In fact,

(\Pi_{\pm}f)(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-(k\pm i0^{+})},

(B.1)

where
f\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some
p\in(1,+\infty)
. These direct sum
decompositions coupled with the Fourier transform can be schematically represented
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}L^{1}(\mathbb{R})&=&L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{-})&\oplus&L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\\
\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}&&\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}&&\Big\downarrow\vbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\mathcal{F}$\hss}}\\
\mathcal{W}_{0}&=&\mathcal{W}^{-}_{0}&\oplus&\mathcal{W}^{+}_{0}\end{array}

Now observe that
\mathcal{F}
acts as an isometric linear one-to-one correspondence
from
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
onto
\mathcal{W}_{0}
. If we define the norm of
\mathbb{C}\oplus L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as

\|c+h\|=|c|+\|h\|_{1}
, we obtain the direct sum decomposition

L^{1}(\mathbb{R})=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\oplus L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{-}),

where the projection
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Pi_{\pm}\mathcal{F}
is the restriction of an arbitrary

h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
to the half-line
\mathbb{R}^{\pm}
.

The following result is most easily proved using the Gelfand theory of
commutative Banach algebras
[
14
,
15
,
17
]
.

Theorem B.1

If for some complex number
h_{\infty}
and some
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
the Fourier
transform
h_{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}h(z)\neq 0
for every

k\in\mathbb{R}
and if
h_{\infty}\neq 0
, then there exists
\kappa\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\frac{1}{h_{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}h(z)}=\frac{1}{h_{\infty}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}\kappa(z)

for every
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
