M2 type macrophages promote OSCC progress via conferring resistance to Erastin-induced ferroptosis
Authors: Chuanchao Su, Zhen Gu, Jingfei Wang, Yiwen Xue, Xiao Song, Meng Yuan, Huijie Ke, Runzhi Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionM2 type macrophages could promote oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) progress. Ferroptosis is a newly discovered type of programmed cell death and inhibition of ferroptosis in many tumor models could promote tumor progression. This study aims to investigate whether M2 type macrophages could influence the progression of OSCC by regulating the ferroptosis of tumor cells.MethodsImmunohistochemical staining was performed to verify the correlation between the expression of CD206 representing M2 type macrophage infiltration in tumor tissue, and GPX4 representing the ability to resist ferroptosis in vivo. In vitro, treat OSCC cells with ferroptosis inducers at different concentrations, detect their effects on ferroptosis, and meanwhile observe their impacts on the proliferation, migration and invasion abilities of OSCC cells; stimulate macrophages to generate different polarization states and identify them. Then, OSCC cells with different levels of ferroptosis were treated with products from macrophages of different polarized states, and the effects on ferroptosis and proliferation, migration and invasion ability of OSCC cells were observed and detected.ResultsCD206 and GPX4 expression in OSCC clinical samples were positively correlated. With the downregulation of ferroptosis, the proliferation, migration and invasion of OSCC cells were inhibited, and M2 type macrophages can upregulate ferroptosis of OSCC cells, meanwhile enhancing their proliferation, migration and invasion capacity.ConclusionsOur studies demonstrated M2 type macrophages could accelerate the progression of OSCC by augmenting the capacity of OSCC cells to resist ferroptosis.Clinical significanceOur studies might offer insights for the application of ferroptosis or macrophage polarization in the therapy of OSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48627-8.Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) commonly gives rise to impairments in chewing, swallowing, and speech functions. Facial deformities associated with this disease also inflict both physical and psychological distress upon patients1. OSCC originates from epithelial cells and makes up roughly 90% of all oral cancers. The principal factors contributing to the high incidence and mortality rates of OSCC are lymphatic and hematogenous metastases2. Notwithstanding the substantial progress made in various diagnostic and therapeutic modalities for OSCC, its prognosis has not been significantly improved. It continues to display a high recurrence rate along with a relatively low 5-year survival rate3.

Cancer is composed of not only cancer cells with the potential for unlimited proliferation but also a wide array of diverse cell types, namely T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, fibroblasts, among others. Emerging evidence conceptualizes cancer as a “pathological ecosystem of ecology and evolution unity”4, emphasizing that tumor progression is a dynamic process shaped by the co-evolution and interactions between cancer cells and their surrounding TME components. This ecological perspective underscores the complexity of cancer beyond mere malignant cell proliferation, providing a more comprehensive framework for understanding tumor development and therapeutic resistance. Macrophages constitute one of the most plentiful immune cells in the TME and are pivotal in regulating tumor progression5. Depending on their phenotypic expressions as well as the amounts and proportions of secreted substances, they can be classified into multiple polarization patterns, such as M1, M0 (non-polarized), M2a, M2b, M2c, and M2d6. Macrophages in different polarization states can have distinct impacts on tumor development7. Ample prior research has suggested that M2 type macrophages can promote tumor progression through various ways, including angiogenesis and immunosuppression8. Given the tumor-promoting mechanisms of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), immunotherapy targeting TAMs has surfaced as a prospective anti-tumor therapeutic strategy. A considerable number of treatments aimed at TAMs have shown encouraging results in preclinical studies9.

There have been clinical drugs such as cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil used to treat OSCC by inducing apoptosis. However, the drug resistance of tumor cells to these clinical drugs poses a significant challenge in cancer treatment. Research indicates that many tumor cell lines resistant to apoptosis are sensitive to ferroptosis. Ferroptosis holds promise for opening new avenues to overcome the challenge of tumor drug resistance10. Ferroptosis represents a form of programmed cell death that has been recently uncovered, featured by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation11. It is associated with a diverse range of clinical circumstances like cancer, acute renal failure, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and neurodegeneration12. CD8+ T cells have been uncovered to enhance the ferroptosis of cancer cells via secreting IFNγ13. Tumor-associated fibroblasts are capable of suppressing tumor ferroptosis through the secretion of exosomes14. The effects of IL6 on ferroptosis vary in different cell types. It could enhance the advancement of HNSCC via suppressing ferroptosis on tumor cells15, yet it can also facilitate the ferroptosis of bronchial epithelial cells16. These findings imply that numerous cells and their secreted substances within the TME can exert an influence on the ferroptosis of tumor cells,. Bioinformatics analysis has indicated a correlation between macrophage polarization and tumor ferroptosis17,18. For instance, it has been revealed that ferroptosis-promoting and ferroptosis-suppressing genes are respectively correlated with M1 and M2 type macrophages. However, there exists a paucity of experimental studies exploring the relationship between M2 type macrophage polarization and the ferroptosis of tumor cells, as well as whether this relationship can impact tumor growth. Consequently, in this study, we will investigate whether M2 type macrophage polarization can influence the growth of OSCC by modulating the ferroptosis of tumor cells, with the anticipation of providing valuable insights with the aim of treating and managing OSCC.

Materials & methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human mononuclear THP1(purchased from Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences). macrophages were cultured using RPMI1640 medium(KeyGen, China). Human tongue squamous cell CAL27 and HN6(purchased from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were both cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium(DMEM)(Gibco, USA) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin(Gibco, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum(Gibco, USA). To confirm these cell lines, we used Short Tandem Repeat(STR) analysis. Cells were bred in An incubator that is maintained at a temperature of 37 °C and contains 5% carbon dioxide.

THP1 that had been centrifugally deposited were resuspended by 100 ng/ml PMA(Sigma, USA). And they were inoculated into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well cultured with 100 ng/ml PMA for 24 h. After THP1 got adherent, the cells underwent washing three times with PBS, and then they were then treated with 100ng/ml PMA as the control group and various concentrations of human IL10(GenScript, China) as the experimental groups for 48 h. Cells in each group were then washed three times with PBS and all treated with DMEM for 12 h to rule out the effects on target cells of IL10. After 12 h, DMEM supernatants from cells cultured in each group were collected. The expression of CD206 protein in each group was determined by WB, and we choosed the IL10 concentration with the highest CD206 protein expression as the treatment condition for next experimental group and 100 ng/ml PMA as the control group.

At 4 ℃, the collected cell supernatants were centrifuged at 500g for 10 min to remove dead cells, centrifuged at 1500g for 15 min to remove cell debris, centrifuged at 3000g for 25 min to remove the precipitated protein, filtered through a 0.22 μm diameter sterilizing filter and finally centrifuged at 1000g for 1 h to get our agents for treatments on CAL27 and HN6.

To evaluate the effects of M2 type macrophage on CAL27 and HN6, cells underwent incubation in the presence of corresponding supernatants and varying concentrations of Erastin.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

In our study, OSCC tissue samples were obtained from the Pathology Department, Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University gave the approval to the protocol(approval number: NJSH-2024NL-014). Before surgery, every patient consented to the sample collection process and affixed their signatures to the informed consent forms. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The The primary antibodies employed in this study are listed below: GPX4 (Abcam, ab125066, 1:100), CD206 (Proteintech, 60143-1-Ig, 1:500).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Cell Total RNA was isolated from cells by making use of the Cell Total RNA Extraction Kit (Novizan, Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the isolated RNA was further reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit(Novizan, Nanjing, China). Each reaction was carried out in a total volume of 10 µl. The reaction mixture consisted of 4 µl of cDNA, 0.5 µl of each primer, and 5 µl of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Novizan, Nanjing, China). The amplification process was executed in strict accordance with the instructions provided. Subsequently, the relative expression levels of the target genes, namely CD163, CD206, IL10, and TGFβ1, were computed via the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method using. Every sample was assayed in triplicate and normalized against GAPDH. The primer sequences are presented in Supplementary File 2.

Western blotting

Proteins were subjected to lysis and extraction using a whole-protein extraction kit (KeyGEN, Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the extracted proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto NC membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1.5 h and subsequently incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-GPX4 (Beyotime, AF7020, 1:1000), anti-CD206 (Beyotime, AF7500, 1:1000) and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:100000). Subsequently, the blot was subjected to staining with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Abmart, M21002, 1:5000) or horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abmart, M21001, 1:5000). Ultimately, the protein expression level was determined through the utilization of a chemiluminescence detection system. Every experiment was replicated three times.

MDA quantitation

Cellular MDA concentrations were measured using Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0131S, MA, China).

CCK8 assay

CAL27 and HN6 cells were inoculated onto plates at a seeding density of 1–4 × 104/ml. Cell proliferative capacity was detected by CCK8 assay(KeyGen, China). After 24 h, 48 h and 72 h, their OD450nm were measured.

Cell wound scratch assay

CAL27 and HN6 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates. When the confluence is up to 90%, we used a 100 µL gun head with a straught ruler to vertically mark on the cells. Wash twice with PBS to make the scratches clear, cells were observed under the microscope and photographed for 0 h and added DMEM for the control group, other corresponding agents for the experimental groups. After 24 h, the healing degree of cell scratches were observed under a microscope and pictures were taken.

Cell invasion assay

A mixture of matrigel and DMEM(ratio 1:8) were incubated and solidified at 37 ℃ for 3 h. Then hydrated with DMEM for half an hour. CAL27 and HN6 diluted in DMEM for 80 µL without serum was added to each chamber. The lower rooms were filled with corresponding treatment. After incubation at 37℃ for 24 h. They were washed in PBS and then filled with 4% paraformaldehyde in upper chamber and lower room for 20 min, crystal violet staining for half an hour. Finally, they were washed using PBS for 3 times and inverted pictures were taken under a microscope.

Data analysis

All numerical data are presented in the form of the mean and standard deviation. For comparisons between two groups, independent samples t-tests were employed. When it came to analyzing differences among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was utilized. In cases where the data were normally distributed and the group variances were equal, Tukey’s post-hoc test was conducted after the one - way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 (full name: GraphPad Prism 9, version 9.0.0; URL: https://www.graphpad.com/) (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Positive correlations among infiltration quantity of M2 macrophages, GPX4 expression and tumor progression

In this study, pathologically confirmed oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) or dysplasia patients were enrolled. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using antibodies against GPX4 or CD206. GPX4, a core regulator of ferroptosis resistance, inhibits ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxides. CD206 is a marker of M2-type macrophages. Microscopically, GPX4 positive signals (yellow/brown particles) were mainly localized in the cytoplasm, with tumor cells as the target (Fig. 1A). CD206 positive signals, also yellow/brownish-yellow particles, were expressed on the cell membrane and distributed in tumor stroma, peritumoral connective tissues and periepithelial connective tissues (Fig. 1A).

High CD206 and GPX4 expression are correlated with OSCC progression. A The expression regions and patterns of GPX4 and CD206. B GPX4 is highly expressed in immune cells, basal cells and basaloid cells. C The expression of GPX4 in tumor cells is higher than that in the epithelial cells adjacent to the cancer. D Overall, the expression of GPX4 and CD206 increases as the degree of differentiation of tumor cells deteriorates. E The infiltration of M2 macrophages in OSCC was positively linked to GPX4 expression.

Ferroptosis resistance quantification: Tumor cells graded 0–4 based on brown staining depth; score = Σ (cell percentage per grade × grade value). Average of three random regions was used (score range: 0–400). M2-type macrophage counting: 3 dense positive regions selected under low-power microscope; positive cells counted under high-power microscope. Mean count of the 3 fields was the final result.

Microscopically, GPX4 was highly expressed in peritumoral basal cells, basaloid cells surrounding cancer nests, and some stromal cells (most likely immune cells) (Fig. 1B). GPX4 expression was higher in tumor cells than in peritumoral epithelial cells (Fig. 1C). This difference was particularly pronounced in poorly differentiated OSCC sections, and all such sections exhibited this characteristic (Fig. 1C and Supplementary File 1). We statistically analyzed GPX4 and CD206 expression in three regions: peritumoral epithelium or epithelial dysplasia (PT), tumor center (TC), and tumor invasive front (ITF). Results showed statistically significant GPX4 expression difference between TC and PT (Fig. 1C).

Film reading and statistical analysis revealed that GPX4 expression and M2-type macrophage infiltration were highly correlated with tumor cell differentiation degree, with both GPX4 and CD206 upregulated as differentiation deteriorated (Fig. 1D).

Film reading revealed that areas surrounding tumor cells with high GPX4 expression showed relatively dense M2-type macrophage infiltration.(Fig. 1E). We analyzed the Pearson correlation between CD206 expression intensity and GPX4 expression in the OSCC TC region (p < 0.05 for statistical significance). As shown in Fig. 1E, M2-type macrophage density was positively correlated with ferroptosis resistance in OSCC cells.

This study confirmed a positive correlation between M2-type macrophage infiltration and GPX4 expression in OSCC tissues. We speculate that M2 macrophages may promote GPX4 expression in OSCC cells by secreting certain cytokines and modulating the local tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing tumor cells’ antioxidant defense capacity against oxidative stress damage and supporting tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Or conversely, high GPX4 expression may also shape a tumor microenvironment more conducive to M2-type macrophage infiltration and aggregation. In conclusion, this study reveals a close correlation between M2-type macrophages and GPX4 in OSCC, providing a novel theoretical basis for understanding OSCC pathogenesis and exploring potential therapeutic targets.

Enhancing ferroptosis in OSCC tumor cells inhibited their proliferation, migration and invasion ability

Erastin, a ferroptosis inducer, was used to treat OSCC cell lines CAL27 and HN6. Detection of ferroptosis-related indicators revealed dose-dependent downregulation of GPX4, a key ferroptosis regulatory protein, as evidenced by Western blot results (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, intracellular lipid peroxidation levels represented by malondialdehyde, a ferroptosis end product rose significantly with increasing erastin concentration (Fig. 2B), indicating elevated ferroptosis levels in OSCC cells.

Enhancement of ferroptosis resulted in decreased proliferative, migratory, and invasive capabilities of OSCC cells. A Elevating the concentration of Erastin led to a reduction in GPX4 expression in OSCC cells. (from different parts of the same gel, the same exposure). B Elevating the concentration of Erastin led to an increase in MDA content. C An increase in Erastin concentration resulted in decreased proliferative capacity. D An increase in Erastin concentration resulted in decreased migratory capacity. E An increase in Erastin concentration resulted in decreased invasive capacity.

CCK-8 assays showed that Erastin reduced OSCC cell OD values in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, indicating inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 2C). Scratch assays revealed that Erastin treatment resulted in significantly wider scratch widths relative to the untreated group, with width increasing alongside drug concentration, reflecting suppressed cell migration (Fig. 2D). Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that Erastin markedly decreased the number of OSCC cells invading through Matrigel-coated membranes, with a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect, suggesting that elevated ferroptosis levels can effectively impair OSCC cell invasion (Fig. 2E).

Our findings suggest that enhancing ferroptosis in OSCC cells can significantly inhibit their proliferation, migration, and invasion. During ferroptosis, excessive intracellular lipid peroxide accumulation and downregulation of key antioxidant proteins (e.g., GPX4) may disrupt tumor cell redox balance, impair cell metabolism and signal transduction, and thus prevent them from performing normal biological behaviors like proliferation, migration and invasion. This provides a novel perspective for OSCC treatment, indicating that inducing ferroptosis may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy to suppress tumor progression.

PMA and IL10 mediated the M2 type macrophage polarization

After stimulation with 100 ng/ml PMA, microscopic observation showed that suspension-grown THP1 cells gradually adhered and changed morphology from round to irregular with pseudopodia, exhibiting typical macrophage characteristics. Further morphological changes occurred following additional polarization induction with IL10 or PMA treatment, with cells becoming flatter, more stretched, and having more and longer pseudopodia (Fig. 3A). THP1 cells were divided into groups treated with different IL10 concentrations for 48 h; after supernatant removal, equal volumes of complete medium were added for another 12 h. Macrophage supernatants were then collected, and we detected the relevant protein and gene expressions of macrophages in each group.

M0 type and M2 type macrophages are successfully acquired. A After treatment of 100ng/ml PMA for 24 h, THP1 was transformed into M0 type macrophage; After treatment of 200ng/ml IL10 for 48 h, M2 type macrophage was acquired. B Treatment of M0 macrophages with IL10 caused elevated CD206 expression compared to treatment with PMA. And along with the progressive rise of IL10 concentration, the expression of CD206 gradually elevated (from different parts of the same gel, different exposures). C Various M2 type macrophage-associated genes underwent upregulation within the IL10 group.

Western blot results showed that CD206 expression was significantly upregulated in polarized macrophages compared with those stimulated only with PMA. Using GAPDH as the internal reference and analyzing protein band gray values, we observed a concentration-dependent increase in CD206 expression, confirming successful polarization of cells into M2-type macrophages at the protein level (Fig. 3B). The qRT-PCR results showed that relative expression levels of M2 macrophage marker genes (e.g., CD163 and CD206) were significantly elevated in IL10-induced M2-polarized THP1 cells compared with untreated cells, confirming M2 macrophage characteristics at the gene level (Fig. 3C).

Macrophage polarization towards M2 type promotes tumor cells progress via conferring resistance to Erastin-induced ferroptosis

Prior to the conditional treatment of OSCC cells, we detected GPX4 expression in normal oral mucosal cell lines and OSCC cell lines. Results showed that normal oral mucosal cells could exhibit higher GPX4 expression than OSCC cells (Fig. 4A), indicating that without the tumor microenvironment (TME), GPX4 expression in normal oral mucosal cells is not necessarily lower than that in OSCC cells.

M2 type macrophage reversed Erastin’s inhibitory effects on OSCC cells’ proliferation, migration and invasion via downregulating ferroptosis. A The GPX4 expression of oral mucosal normal cell lines versus OSCC cell lines.(from different parts of the same gel, the same exposure). B M2 partly counteracted the down-regulatory effect of Erastin on GPX4 expression in OSCC cells.(from different parts of the same gel, CAL27 from the same exposure, HN6 from different exposures). C M2 partly counteracted the down-regulatory effect of Erastin on MDA content in OSCC cells. D M2 partly counteracted the down-regulatory effect of Erastin on OSCC cells’ proliferation ability. E M2 partly counteracted the down-regulatory effect of Erastin on OSCC cells’ migration ability. F M2 partly counteracted the down-regulatory effect of Erastin on OSCC cells’ invasion ability.

Four treatment groups were established for OSCC cell lines: M0 macrophage supernatant + equal-volume medium; M2 macrophage supernatant + equal-volume medium; M0 supernatant + equal-volume 100 μm Erastin; M2 supernatant + equal-volume 100 μm Erastin. After treatment, multiple indicators were detected to assess the impacts of different groups on OSCC cell ferroptosis, proliferation, migration, and invasion. A 24-hour treatment of CAL27 cells revealed that M2 macrophages enhanced tumor cell anti-ferroptosis capacity, as reflected by GPX4 expression and MDA content in each group (Fig. 4B, C). Compared with M0 macrophage supernatant (M0 CM) treatment, M2 macrophage supernatant (M2 CM) upregulated GPX4 expression and reduced MDA content in CAL27 and HN6 cells after 24 h. Moreover, M2 CM partially reversed the Erastin-induced decrease in GPX4 activity and increase in MDA levels (Fig. 4B, C). Compared with M0 CM, M2 CM not only enhanced OSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, but also partially reversed the inhibitory effects of Erastin on these malignant phenotypes (Fig. 4D–F).

We speculate that M2 macrophage supernatant may contain specific cytokines, growth factors, or other bioactive substances that bind to OSCC cell surface receptors and activate related intracellular signaling pathways. These pathways may counteract Erastin-induced ferroptosis by regulating ferroptosis-associated genes and proteins, while simultaneously activating signaling networks linked to cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, thereby fostering a favorable microenvironment for OSCC malignant progression. However, the underlying specific molecular mechanisms and key regulatory factors require further investigation, which will facilitate a deeper understanding of the complex crosstalk between macrophages, OSCC cells, and ferroptosis inducers within the TME, and lay a more precise theoretical foundation for the development of OSCC therapeutic strategies.

Discussion

To sum up, macrophages represent a crucial type of immune cells within TME and are capable of modulating tumor growth, progression, and metastatic potential through a diverse array of context-dependent mechanisms. As highly plastic cells, they can polarize into distinct functional phenotypes in response to cues from the TME: classically activated M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effects by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ) and presenting tumor antigens to initiate adaptive immune responses, while alternatively activated M2 macrophages promote tumorigenesis through multiple pro-tumor actions. These include enhancing cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via the secretion of growth factors and matrix-degrading enzymes, suppressing anti-tumor immune surveillance by inhibiting the activity of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells19–21, and facilitating angiogenesis by releasing pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF22 and angiogenesis23. In this way, the functional status of macrophages serves as a critical determinant of tumor fate, making them a promising target for developing novel anti-tumor immunotherapies. Our study was the first to experimentally demonstrate that macrophage polarization towards the M2 type could play a role in facilitating the advancement of OSCC via suppressing ferroptosis of OSCC cells. The proportion of M2-type macrophages exhibits a significant upward trend during the malignant transformation process originating from normal oral mucosal tissues to OSCC24. This phenotypic shift is not unique to OSCC; prior investigations have also indicated that TAMs predominantly display the pro-tumor M2 phenotype in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), a tumor category that encompasses OSCC25–27. Moreover, accumulating clinical evidence has verified that the number of intratumoral TAMs is notably and negatively associated with key patient prognosis indicators, including overall survival rate, disease-free survival period, and tumor recurrence rate28. This close link between M2-polarized TAMs and poor clinical outcomes has underscored the critical value of targeting TAMs in anti-tumor therapy. Consequently, current tumor-targeting therapeutic strategies that focus on TAMs center around two core dimensions: the first is to directly eliminate existing TAMs within the TME, either by blocking macrophage recruitment chemokines (such as CCL2 and CSF-1) or depleting infiltrated TAMs via specific antibody-mediated clearance; the second is to induce the functional re-polarization of pro-tumor M2-type macrophages towards the anti-tumor M1 phenotype, which can be achieved through small-molecule drugs, cytokine stimulation, or immune checkpoint modulators that reprogram macrophage metabolic and signaling pathways29–31.

OSCC is a highly invasive subtype of squamous cell carcinoma, characterized by high rates of local lymph node and distant metastases32. A key contributor to this aggressive phenotype is the recruitment of macrophages to the tumor invasive front via tumor-derived chemotactic agents33. Once localized at this site, these cells differentiate into TAMs, which enhance cancer cell motility and remodel the extracellular matrix through the secretion of pro-migration factors such as epidermal growth factor34,35. Another critical driver of OSCC metastasis is epithelial-mesenchymal transformation (EMT), a biological process that endows epithelial cancer cells with potent invasive and metastatic capabilities36; notably, multiple EMT-related genes are closely correlated with ferroptosis-associated genes37, hinting at an intrinsic link between these two pro-tumor processes.

From a tumor ecosystem perspective, OSCC development is underpinned by multi-level interactions among cell populations, stromal networks, immune-metabolic microenvironments, microorganisms, and molecular signals—all hallmarked by high self-organization and hierarchical heterogeneity. Within this intricate system, constituent components engage in dynamic crosstalk and execute adaptive responses based on ecological evolutionary dynamics principles. Cancer cell populations evolve survival strategies (e.g., coexistence, necrosis, mimicry) through communication, cooperation, and competition; this, in turn, drives synchronous adaptive adjustments in other TME cell populations such as immune cells and fibroblasts, including the shift from competitive or predatory relationships to mutualistic symbiosis. Such reciprocal interactions fuel co-evolution and adaptation between tumor cells and the microenvironment, ultimately leading to tumor ecological succession and landscape heterogeneity38,39. Within this complex interplay, M2-type macrophages— the predominant TAM subset in OSCC—promote malignant progression by enhancing OSCC cell resistance to ferroptosis. Collectively, these observations suggest that M2 macrophage polarization may facilitate tumor invasion by regulating ferroptosis, which in turn modulates EMT processes in OSCC cells, though the deeper underlying mechanisms remain elusive.

To further dissect these mechanisms, it is hypothesized that M2 macrophage-derived signals counteract Erastin-induced ferroptosis via the regulation of ferroptosis-associated genes and proteins. For instance, M2 macrophages may upregulate the expression of GPX4, a key negative regulator of ferroptosis that catalyzes glutathione (GSH)-mediated reduction of lipid peroxides, thereby lowering intracellular lipid peroxidation levels and inhibiting ferroptosis initiation40. The specific molecular cascade likely involves the activation of canonical signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, which trigger phosphorylation cascades to modulate transcription factors, thereby promoting GPX4 gene transcription and subsequent protein expression41. Concurrently, these M2 macrophage-derived signals may activate signal transduction networks governing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. For example, activation of the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway can upregulate cell cycle-related proteins and accelerate cell cycle progression, creating a permissive microenvironment that fosters OSCC cell survival and malignant behavior42.

Despite these insights, the precise molecular mechanisms and key regulatory factors involved in M2 macrophage-mediated modulation of ferroptosis and EMT remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies can employ RNA interference technology to silence candidate key genes, followed by assessment of alterations in cell phenotypes and associated signaling pathways. Alternatively, proteomic techniques can be utilized to comprehensively characterize differences in the protein expression profiles of OSCC cells before and after treatment with M2 macrophage supernatant, enabling the identification of critical regulatory factors. These investigations will deepen our understanding of the complex crosstalk among macrophages, OSCC cells, and ferroptosis inducers within the TME, and lay a solid theoretical foundation for the development of novel OSCC therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings indicated that human IL10-induced shift of macrophage polarization towards the M2-like state could facilitate the progression of OSCC by suppressing ferroptosis of tumor cells. Nevertheless, the more intricate underlying mechanisms warrant further in-depth exploration. Our study holds the promise of offering a prospective therapeutic approach to enhance the management of OSCC. This could be achieved either by regulating the polarization direction of macrophages within the neoplastic microenvironment or by modulating the ferroptosis of OSCC cells.
