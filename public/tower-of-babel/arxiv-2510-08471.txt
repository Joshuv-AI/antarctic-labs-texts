Learning Coulomb Potentials and Beyond with Fermions in Continuous Space
Andreas Bluhm, Marius Lemm, Tim Möbus, Oliver Siebert

Abstract
We present a modular algorithm for learning external potentials in continuous-space free-fermion models including Coulomb potentials in any dimension. Compared to the lattice-based approaches, the continuum presents new mathematical challenges: the state space is infinite-dimensional and the Hamiltonian contains the Laplacian, which is unbounded in the continuum and thus produces an unbounded speed of information propagation. Our framework addresses these difficulties through novel optimization methods or information-propagation bounds in combination with a priori regularity assumptions on the external potential. The resulting algorithm provides a unified and robust approach that covers both Coulomb interactions and other classes of physically relevant potentials. One possible application is the characterization of charge and position of nuclei and ions in quantum chemistry. Our results thus lay the foundation for a scalable and generalizable toolkit to explore fermionic systems governed by continuous-space interactions.



I
Introduction

Characterizing Hamiltonian evolution is essential for both the calibration and the deeper understanding of quantum devices and quantum mechanical systems. This task, often framed in the language of quantum information, is closely connected to classical inverse problems such as learning differential operators or reconstructing physical fields from observations, since it involves inferring the defining parameters of a Hamiltonian in a chosen representation from experimental data. Although Hamiltonian learning has been extensively studied in spin systems
Bakshi et al. 2024
;
Stilck França et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Zubida et al. 2021
;
Bairey et al. 2019

Caro 2024
and many more, where the local Hilbert space dimension is finite and the Hamiltonian is a bounded operator, much less is known about fermionic systems in continuous space. Unlike lattice models, these systems are naturally described by infinite-dimensional state spaces and unbounded operators such as the Laplacian, which plays a central role in quantum theory. Physically, this setting is indispensable for modeling ions or molecules in free space, i.e., in the absence of a spatial lattice. To the best of our knowledge, no comparable continuous space fermionic learning model has yet been established, which is one motivation for the present work. Due to the enormous literature on Hamiltonian learning in the last years, we restrict ourselves to review a few works close to ours. Recent work on fermionic Hamiltonian learning treated lattice approximations
Mirani and Hayden 2024
, leaving open the challenge of handling the continuous space directly. Beyond fermionic models, we note works such as
Huang et al. 2023
;
Bakshi et al. 2024
, which achieve the information-theoretic optimal Heisenberg scaling for lattice qubit systems, as well as
Stilck França et al. 2024
, which achieves the standard quantum limit for lattice qubit systems under weak assumptions on the experimental setup, and extensions of these methods to bosonic lattice models
Möbus et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Möbus et al. 2025
. Another interesting work is
Metz et al. 2024
, which presents the simulation of non-relativistic quantum many-body systems. Simulation can be seen as the dual problem to learning.

In this work, the Hamiltonian Learning algorithm is implemented entirely in continuous space, which is the natural configuration space of particle positions ubiquitous, e.g., in quantum chemistry and the description of trapped quantum gases.
Working in the continuum allows for natural representation of quantum states and dynamics, free from the discretization artifacts sometimes introduced by ad-hoc lattice models. E.g., continuous space preserves rotational and full translational symmetries and supports all plane wave solutions, which simplifies the analysis of free fermions and facilitate the extraction of physical parameters from experimental data. Furthermore, learning in continuous space avoids complications such as fermion doubling and lattice-induced boundary effects, making it particularly well-suited for modeling systems where spatial resolution and smooth potential landscapes are essential. One potential application is the characterization of the charge number and position of ions in continuous space, ranging from a single test nucleus or ion to a Fermi gas approximation. Due to the ionic charge, the system is described by a Coulomb potential as follows:

\displaystyle h=-\Delta+V

(1)

Additionally, one encounters analytical challenges such as the regularity constraints imposed by the Laplacian as well as the Coulomb potential and the need to solve a highly nonlinear system to access the position of the Coulomb potential. Addressing these difficulties requires nonlinear numerical algorithms as well as a careful perturbation analysis of the Coulomb potential.

Beyond the Coulomb case, we also consider broader classes of potentials, including smooth functions, hyperbolic functions, and polynomials. All of these models can be addressed within a unified data acquisition framework using our modularly designed algorithm. Here, parallelization exponentially reduces the sample complexity of the algorithm and allows for an efficient approximation of the considered potential. This requires novel information-propagation bounds

With respect to the Coulomb potential, we start with a single Coulomb center and then provide an algorithm to extend to multi-Coulomb potentials. The basic idea is to first estimate the position of the particle, and then consider four appropriately chosen points in its neighborhood to approximate the charge number and position up to a given precision

Here, the lack of any information-propagation bounds due to the singular behavior of the Coulomb potential always induces a quadratic dependence of the sample complexity on the number of Coulomb centers. Owing to the free-fermion assumption of the model, we therefore generate the data sequentially to avoid errors in the free-fermion approximation. For the multi-Coulomb potential, the charge numbers can be estimated by a diagonally dominant linear system after a rough estimation of the positions. Then, the mutual influence on the output data is estimated and iteratively improved. In this way, the single-Coulomb case can be applied to the corrected and normalized data to find the positions and charge numbers up to a precision
\varepsilon

If we restrict our attention to the charge numbers, the reconstruction problem reduces to inverting the overlap matrix between the Coulomb potentials and the prepared states. This motivates the study of a broad class of linear systems. In particular, our modular algorithm is capable of learning not only Coulomb interactions, but also any smooth function, trigonometric function, or Lagrange polynomial. To achieve a good approximation, many states in different regions of space are required. To obtain an efficient algorithm, we parallelize our learning algorithm using novel novel information-propagation bounds
Hinrichs et al. 2024
in combination with regularity assumptions on the input states. These regularity assumptions are also related to an interesting observation — namely, Heisenberg’s uncertainty relation affecting the sample complexity.

Learning the Coulomb potential

Theorem A.8

Second Quantization Formalism and Rigorous Definition of the Hamiltonian

In this section we briefly recall the second quantization (Fock space) formalism in the continuum and provide a rigorous definition of our non-interacting fermionic many-body Hamiltonian in (

(

The Hilbert space for our model is the fermonic Fock space over
L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
,

\mathfrak{F}\coloneqq\mathbbm{C}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}(L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}))^{\wedge k}=\mathbbm{C}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}L^{2}_{\mathsf{a}}(\mathbbm{R}^{d\cdot k})

where
\wedge k

k

-space where the anti-symmetrization in each summand occurs over the
k

d
-dimensional variables. Elements

can be written as sequences

\psi=(\psi^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}_{0}},\qquad\psi^{(0)}\in\mathbbm{C},~\psi^{(k)}\in L^{2}_{\mathsf{a}}(\mathbbm{R}^{d\cdot k}),~k\geq 1.

The vacuum vector is defined as
\Omega=(1,0,0,0,\ldots)
.

Let
a(f)
,

be the fermionic annihilation operator

with

\{a(f),a(g)\}=\{a^{*}(f),a^{*}(g)\}=0,\quad\{a(f),a^{*}(g)\}=\braket{f,g}\qquad\mbox{for all}\ f\in L^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d}).
