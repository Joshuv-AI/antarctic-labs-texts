A dual functional mucus- and cell-penetrating peptide enhances mRNA lipid nanoparticle delivery to the lung
Authors: Sahana Kumar, Gregg A Duncan
Published: 2024

Main text

For the past several decades, gene delivery via the inhaled route has been explored for the treatment of chronic lung diseases like cystic fibrosis (CF).1 While CF impacts tissues throughout the body, complications associated with CF lung dysfunction are the primary cause of death. CF is caused by a mutation of genes coding for CF transmembrane conductance regulator protein (CFTR), which is responsible for the transport of chloride and sodium ions across airway epithelial cells.2 These mutations cause the dysfunction or complete lack of expression of CFTR, leading to impaired ion transport and water exchange and, consequently, causing dehydrated, thick, and viscous mucus on the airway epithelium. This thick mucus is not cleared by the cilia efficiently, which breeds bacterial infections, neutrophilic inflammation, and mucus build-up within the airways.2,3 Gene therapy for a monogenic disease like CF would be highly advantageous and, with the use of gene editing strategies targeting CFTR, could be potentially curative for individuals suffering from this disease.

However, crossing the CF mucus barrier and entering disease-relevant lung cell populations (e.g., secretory cells, basal cells, ionocytes) remains a significant obstacle to successful inhaled CF gene therapy.4 Moreover, many of the past clinical trials for CF gene therapy have been conducted using viral vectors (e.g., adenovirus, adeno-associated virus [AAV]) given their intrinsic capacity to deliver genetic cargo to target cells.1 However to address the shortcomings of viral vectors (e.g., limited genetic cargo capacity, immunogenicity), non-viral delivery strategies with comparable efficacy would be highly valuable. Typically, non-viral gene delivery vectors are formulated with muco-inert and stealth coatings such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) to improve their penetration through the mucus barrier.4 While this helps with traversing the mucus barrier, PEG functionalization can hinder gene vector uptake in the airway epithelium. To address this challenge, Soto et al. leveraged phage display technology to identify mucus- and cell-penetrating peptides to incorporate into mRNA lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations for pulmonary gene delivery.5

In order to identify leading candidate peptide sequences using a high-throughput screening approach, T7 phage libraries expressing unique peptides were generated and panned against primary human bronchial epithelial cells (pHBECs) that were collected from patients with CF. This is a highly relevant in vitro model that mimics disease phenotype with secretion of thickened mucus. To identify the most desirable candidate, the investigators subjected the phages to multiple rounds of selection pressures, such as shortening the incubation time and increasing the total volume of mucus within pHBEC cultures. To verify that these candidates also enabled cellular uptake, only phages that were internalized in pHBECs were collected. By implementing these strategies, peptide sequences possessing both mucus- and cell-penetrating properties were successfully identified. Based on the chemical properties of the peptides, they found that the subset of peptides identified had a net-positive charge and were hydrophilic. This is an interesting observation, as it is conventionally thought that a net-positive surface charge would lead to adhesion to net-negatively charged mucins in the mucus barrier.4 Among eligible candidates, a leading candidate was selected (identified as peptide C) for further testing and incorporation into mRNA LNPs.

To determine if peptide C could enhance non-viral gene delivery to airway cells, an LNP formulation was generated that was like that used in Moderna’s COVID-19 mRNA LNP vaccine but with the addition of peptide C-functionalized lipids (∼6% of the total lipid content). Peptide C-modified LNPs carrying nanoluciferase mRNA were used to assess transfection efficiency in differentiated pHBECs, where significantly higher luminescence was achieved as compared to controls. The study was repeated in THP-1-derived macrophages, and no significant difference in luminescence was observed in comparison to controls. These data would suggest that the dual mucus- and cell-penetrating functions of peptide C are the most beneficial in enhancing gene delivery to the lung epithelium. To confirm that peptide C-LNPs could successfully mediate mRNA delivery in the lung, peptide C-LNPs with Cre mRNA were delivered intratracheally into tdTomato reporter mice having LoxP stop cassettes flanking the tdTomato gene. If gene delivery is successful, Cre recombinase will excise the stop codons and enhance tdTomato expression. Following local delivery of peptide C-LNPs, enhanced tdTomato expression was observed in lung epithelial cells (∼20%) compared to other cell types, including immune and endothelial cells, which may help to limit any off-target effects.

Overall, these studies establish an approach to enhance LNP delivery to the lung using peptides to overcome the mucus barrier and facilitate lung epithelial uptake. Their findings are significant given these peptides have been optimized for delivery using patient-derived CF models for screening and demonstrate in vivo the potential to preferentially deliver mRNA to the lung epithelium. In future work, it will be important to further assess the specific lung cell populations accessed using peptide-C-functionalized LNPs. For example, successful delivery to basal cells in the airways will be necessary to achieve a durable therapeutic effect. Testing of peptide C functionalization on viral gene delivery vectors, like AAV, would also be worth pursuing to determine if this provides similar enhancements in lung gene delivery.
