Associations Between Parenting Stress, Family Support, Emotion Regulation, and Parental Burnout in Parents of Adolescents with Mood Disorders: A Cross-Sectional Structural Equation Modeling and Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis Study
Authors: Huili He, Wenwen Lu, Feng Zhang, Lili Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimParents of adolescents with mood disorders are at high risk of parental burnout. Existing research has examined risk and resource factors in isolation, with limited attention to the role of emotion regulation in relation to parental burnout. The present study integrates the Balance between Risks and Resources Theory and the Emotion Regulation Process Model and aims to examine both the net effects and potential configurations of parental burnout.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between July and October 2025 using a consecutive sampling strategy. A total of 212 parents of adolescents diagnosed with mood disorders were recruited from psychiatric outpatient and inpatient settings. Data were collected using the Short Parenting Stress Scale, the Family APGAR Index, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and the Chinese version of the Short Parental Burnout Assessment. Structural equation modeling (SEM) and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) were used for data analysis.ResultsThe SEM demonstrated good model fit (χ2/df = 1.071, CFI = 0.982, IFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.981, GFI = 0.858, RMR = 0.043, RMSEA = 0.018). Parenting stress and family support were indirectly associated with parental burnout via expressive suppression (β = 0.130, 95% CI [0.054, 0.237]; β = −0.116, 95% CI [−0.223, −0.052]), whereas cognitive reappraisal was not significantly associated with parental burnout in the model. FsQCA identified three configurations linked to high parental burnout. Low cognitive reappraisal emerged as a core condition in two configurations. The most prominent configuration was characterized by high parenting stress, unemployment, low family support, low cognitive reappraisal, and high expressive suppression.ConclusionThis study identified multiple patterns of association in parental burnout among caregivers of adolescents with mood disorders. Expressive suppression may represent an important emotional correlate linking risk–resource imbalance and parental burnout. Resource deprivation across economic, social, and psychological domains characterized a typical high-burnout configuration. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal. Enhancing family support and promoting adaptive emotion regulation strategies may help mitigate parental burnout, particularly among families with limited economic, social, and psychological resources.

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of mood disorders among adolescents has increased globally, posing a major public health challenge.1 Depression is a common and persistent mental health disorder that typically emerges in childhood and adolescence and may have long-term consequences for psychological, social, and academic functioning.2 A meta-analysis of Chinese children and adolescents aged 6–15 years reported a prevalence of depressive symptoms of 17.2%.3 In China, the incidence of bipolar disorder among adolescents increased from 22.92 per 100,000 in 1990 to 23.17 per 100,000 in 2021, with the highest rate observed in the 15–19 age group (33.14 per 100,000).4 Bipolar disorder is also associated with elevated suicide risk and substantial psychosocial impairment during adolescence.5 Collectively, these findings highlight the high prevalence and clinical severity of adolescent mood disorders, as well as the growing burden on healthcare and educational systems.

The impact of adolescent mood disorders extends beyond the individual level to the family, placing a substantial psychological burden on parents. Evidence shows that 34.8% of caregivers of adolescents with depression experience anxiety symptoms and 41.8% experience depressive symptoms, often accompanied by sleep disturbances, somatic complaints, and feelings of helplessness.6 A qualitative study further indicated that caring for individuals with mood disorders not only affects caregivers’ mental health but is also associated with changes in physical health, diet, and exercise behaviors.7 As primary caregivers, parents play a crucial role in maintaining family functioning and supporting their children’s recovery.8 However, current medical and social support systems remain insufficient to meet their needs, leaving many parents without timely and effective support under substantial caregiving stress.

Against this background, parental burnout has emerged as an important public health concern. It is characterized by emotional exhaustion, reduced parenting efficacy, and emotional distancing from children resulting from prolonged parenting stress.9 Parents of adolescents with mood disorders face greater challenges than those of typically developing adolescents, including long-term uncertainty in disease management, social stigma, financial strain, and limited access to support resources. These conditions place them at elevated risk of parental burnout.10 Previous studies have shown that parental burnout is associated with severe psychological distress, marital conflict, and an increased risk of child neglect or abuse.11 Although research on parental burnout has increased in recent years, most studies have focused on general parent populations. Evidence regarding parents of adolescents with mood disorders remains limited. This group not only experiences persistent caregiving stress but also faces distinct psychosocial burdens, suggesting that the mechanisms underlying parental burnout may differ from those observed in other populations. Therefore, systematically examining the developmental pathways of parental burnout in this population is both theoretically and practically warranted.

International research has examined the correlates of parental burnout. However, studies focusing on parents of adolescents with mood disorders remain limited. Existing evidence suggests substantial inconsistency in the effects of demographic and psychosocial variables. It indicates that parental burnout is likely driven by complex interactions among multiple conditions. Structural equation modeling (SEM) estimates net effects among variables but is limited in capturing configurational complexity and equifinal causal pathways. In contrast, fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) enables the identification of different combinations of demographic and psychosocial conditions that lead to the same outcome. Accordingly, this study integrates SEM and fsQCA to provide a more comprehensive analysis of parental burnout among parents of adolescents with mood disorders.

Literature Review on the Antecedents of Parental Burnout

Demographic Factors

Findings regarding demographic correlates of parental burnout have been inconsistent across studies, suggesting a lack of stable and generalizable effects. Across multiple demographic indicators, including parental age, gender, education level, employment status, and family structure, existing evidence does not converge on consistent patterns of association with parental burnout. Overall, younger parents have been reported to be more vulnerable to burnout due to limited parenting experience and psychosocial resources,12 although other studies have found no significant or only weak associations between parental age and burnout.13 Similar inconsistencies are observed for gender, with some studies reporting no significant differences,14,15 while others indicate higher burnout among mothers,16–18 particularly in contexts where caregiving responsibilities are disproportionately borne by women.

Education and employment status also show mixed findings. Higher education may serve as a protective factor by enhancing coping capacity and access to social and informational resources,19 whereas it may also increase burnout risk through stronger parenting ideals and elevated self-imposed expectations.20 In addition, differences across educational levels have been found to be non-significant under certain conditions, such as during the COVID-19 pandemic.21 Regarding employment status, some studies report no significant association with parental burnout,12,22 while others identify unemployment as a significant risk factor,23 particularly among mothers.24 Family structure variables, such as single parenthood and increased caregiving burden, are more consistently associated with higher levels of burnout,12,25 although effect sizes remain variable across studies. These findings suggest that demographic characteristics do not exert uniform effects on parental burnout. Instead, their influence appears to be contingent on broader psychosocial and contextual conditions, indicating that demographic factors function as distal and context-dependent risk indicators rather than independent predictors.

Psychosocial Factors

Emotion Regulation

Emotion regulation refers to the psychological process through which individuals influence the generation, experience, and expression of emotions to meet environmental demands.26 It includes antecedent-focused strategies (eg, cognitive reappraisal) and response-focused strategies (eg, expressive suppression). Cognitive reappraisal involves reinterpreting emotional events to reduce negative affect, whereas expressive suppression refers to the inhibition of emotional expression after emotions are generated.26,27 A meta-analysis has shown that cognitive reappraisal is negatively associated with parental burnout, whereas expressive suppression is positively associated with it.28 Similar patterns have been reported in caregiving populations, particularly among caregivers of patients with schizophrenia, where adaptive emotion regulation is linked to better psychological outcomes.29 Longitudinal evidence further supports the protective role of cognitive reappraisal over time.30 However, another meta-analytic findings suggest that these effects may vary across cultural and contextual conditions.31 Taken together, emotion regulation appears to play a complex and context-dependent role in parental burnout.

Parenting Stress

Parenting stress is widely recognized as one of the most robust predictors of parental burnout.32 Traditionally, parenting stress is conceptualized as arising from an imbalance between perceived parenting demands and available coping resources.33 Numerous studies have demonstrated its positive association with parental burnout.14 However, recent findings suggest that the relationship between parenting stress and burnout may not be uniformly direct. Instead, high levels of parenting stress may influence parental burnout through intermediate psychological processes, particularly emotion regulation strategies such as increased expressive suppression and reduced cognitive reappraisal.34 In addition, the strength of this association appears to vary across different family contexts, levels of social support, and cultural expectations regarding parenting roles.35,36 These findings suggest that parenting stress is not merely an isolated risk factor, but a core element of a broader risk–resource imbalance system interacting with emotional and contextual variables.

Family Support

Family support is consistently identified as a critical protective factor against parental burnout, yet its effects are not entirely uniform across empirical studies. In general, higher levels of family support are associated with lower parental burnout, as it provides emotional comfort, instrumental assistance, and informational resources that reduce caregiving burden.37 However, the strength and mechanisms of this protective effect vary across studies. Some research suggests that family support exerts its influence indirectly by enhancing adaptive emotion regulation strategies, such as increasing cognitive reappraisal and reducing reliance on expressive suppression.38 In other contexts, particularly where caregiving demands are extremely high, the buffering effect of family support appears to be weakened.39 This indicates that the protective role of family support is context-dependent rather than universal, and may be moderated by parenting stress and other contextual constraints. These mixed findings suggest that family support should not be viewed as a simple linear protective factor. Rather, it functions as a contextual resource that interacts with both stress exposure and emotion regulation processes, jointly shaping parents’ vulnerability to burnout.

Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses Development

This study was guided by the Balance between Risks and Resources (BR2) theory40 and the Emotion Regulation Process (ERP) model.41 Several theoretical frameworks have been used to explain burnout, including Job Demands-Resources theory,42 Conservation of Resources theory,43 and the Stress–Strain–Coping model.44 These frameworks generally emphasize the dynamic interplay between demands, resources, and adaptation. However, BR2 theory was developed specifically within the parental burnout literature and has become one of the most influential frameworks for understanding parental burnout. According to BR2 theory, parental burnout results from a chronic imbalance between parenting-related risks and protective resources. The theory proposes two complementary processes. The first is a health damage process, in which intensive caregiving demands progressively deplete parents’ physical, emotional, and cognitive resources. The second is a motivation process, in which internal and external resources promote adaptation and resilience. Compared with broader stress theories, BR2 highlights the unique nature of the parenting role. Unlike occupational roles, parenting responsibilities cannot be easily abandoned or withdrawn from. As a result, prolonged imbalances between risks and resources may increase vulnerability to parental burnout. In the present study, parenting stress was conceptualized as a demand factor, whereas family support was conceptualized as a resource factor.

BR2 theory explains how imbalances between risks and resources may contribute to parental burnout. However, it does not explain the psychological processes underlying this association. The present study further drew upon the ERP model, which focuses on two key emotion regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression.41 Within the integrated framework, emotion regulation reflects parents’ capacity to manage emotional responses to caregiving-related stress.

In addition, demographic characteristics were conceptualized as contextual risk factors that may increase vulnerability to parental burnout under specific circumstances. The exploratory aims were: (a) to identify whether multiple distinct configurations are associated with high parental burnout in this population, and (b) to examine how demographic factors combine with psychosocial conditions in non-linear ways.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Sample

A cross-sectional study design was adopted. Data were collected using self-reported questionnaires through both online and offline approaches from October 2025 to December 2025. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling strategy from psychiatric outpatient and inpatient departments in several hospitals in Shanghai. The study population consisted of parents of adolescents aged 10–19 years with mood disorders. Adolescents’ diagnostic information and age were verified through hospital medical records. Psychiatric diagnoses, including depressive disorders and bipolar disorders, were confirmed by attending psychiatrists based on ICD-10 criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being the primary caregiver of an adolescent diagnosed with a mood disorder; (2) co-residing with the adolescent for at least six months per year; and (3) providing informed consent to participate. The exclusion criteria included: (1) a self-reported history of severe mental disorder, cognitive impairment, or acute psychological distress; and (2) experiencing major life events such as bereavement, serious illness, or psychological trauma during the study period.

A total of 248 parents were initially assessed for eligibility. After excluding 15 parents who did not meet the inclusion criteria and 12 who declined participation, 221 eligible parents were invited to complete the questionnaires. Among them, 217 questionnaires were returned. Five questionnaires were further excluded because of substantial missing data (n = 3) or invalid/patterned responses (n = 2). Ultimately, 212 valid questionnaires were included in the final analyses, yielding a valid response rate of 95.9%. No missing values remained in the final dataset.

The adequacy of the final sample size was evaluated based on established methodological guidelines. For SEM, previous literature suggests that a sample size of approximately 200 is generally adequate for models of moderate complexity under conditions of acceptable data distribution.45 Therefore, the final sample of 212 participants was considered sufficient for the SEM analysis. For fsQCA, traditional statistical power analysis is not applicable, as the method is case-oriented rather than variable-oriented. Instead, sample adequacy is assessed based on case coverage and configurational stability. According to Rihoux,46 meaningful fsQCA solutions can typically be obtained with 50–100 cases. In addition, Greckhamer et al,47 recommend a case-to-condition ratio of at least 10–20 per condition to ensure stable configurational analysis In this study, 212 cases and seven conditions were included, yielding a case-to-condition ratio of approximately 30:1. This exceeded the recommended threshold and supported the robustness of the configurational results.

Measurement

Demographic Information

To summarize the participants’ characteristics, a self-designed questionnaire was developed to collect sociodemographic information, including gender, age, and educational level.

Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ)

The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) was developed by Gross and John in 200341 to assess the habitual use of emotion regulation strategies in daily life. The scale was translated and revised into Chinese by Wang Li et al in 2007.48 The Chinese version has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in Chinese populations, supporting the assessment of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression as two distinct emotion regulation strategies.48 The Chinese version was used in this study to measure participants’ emotion regulation strategies in parenting contexts. The ERQ consists of two subscales with a total of 10 items, including six items for cognitive reappraisal and four items for expressive suppression. Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). Higher scores indicate a greater frequency of using the corresponding emotion regulation strategy. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression subscales were 0.733 and 0.720, respectively.

Family APGAR Index

The Family APGAR Index was originally developed by Smilkstein in 1978.49 The Chinese version was introduced and validated by Lv and Gu in 1995.50 Previous studies have demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity of the Chinese version in assessing perceived family functioning and support among Chinese populations. This tool assesses an individual’ s perceived level of family support across five dimensions: Adaptability, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve. It consists of five items, each corresponding to one dimension, and uses a three-point scoring system (0 = almost never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often). The total score ranges from 0 to 10, with 0~3 indicating severe family dysfunction, 4~6 indicating moderate dysfunction, and 7~10 indicating good family functioning. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α value for this scale was 0.714.

Parenting Stress Index-Short Form-15 (PSI-SF-15)

The Parenting Stress Index-Short Form-15 (PSI-SF-15) was used to assess the level of parenting stress. It was revised by Luo et al,51 based on the original Parenting Stress Index and consists of 15 items Previous validation studies among Chinese parents confirmed its three-factor structure (Parental Distress, Parent-Child Dysfunctional Interaction, and Difficult Child) through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses and demonstrated satisfactory construct validity.51 The scale uses a five-point Likert format ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). A higher total score indicates greater parenting stress. The scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.911).

Chinese Short Version of Parental Burnout Assessment (CS-PBA)

The Chinese Short Version of the Parental Burnout Assessment (CS-PBA) was used to measure participants’ levels of parental burnout. It was revised by Chinese scholar Wang et al,52 based on the original Parental Burnout Assessment.9 The CS-PBA is a single-factor scale consisting of seven items. Previous validation studies supported its single-factor structure through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses and demonstrated satisfactory criterion-related validity in Chinese parents.53 Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of parental burnout. The Cronbach’s α value of CS-PBA was 0.836.

Data Collection

Before data collection, all research staff involved in the survey received standardized training. Prior to questionnaire administration, participants were informed of the study objectives, content, estimated completion time, and confidentiality principles, and written informed consent was obtained. Both online and offline questionnaires were used to improve accessibility and reduce recruitment barriers. This combined approach was adopted to ensure broader coverage of eligible participants and reduce potential selection bias. Offline questionnaires were administered to parents recruited from inpatient wards and outpatient clinics during hospital visits, while online questionnaires were provided to those unable to complete the survey on site. The questionnaire content, item order, response options, and instructions were identical across both modes. For offline administration, standardized explanations were provided for items that participants did not understand. For the online version, guidance and contact information were provided for inquiries. All questionnaires were collected on site or submitted immediately upon completion via the online platform. The research team reviewed all questionnaires for completeness to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

Statistical Analysis

Given that all variables were collected using self-reported questionnaires, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess potential common method bias.54 Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to examine associations among psychological variables. A SEM was then constructed using maximum likelihood estimation.55 Model fit was evaluated using χ2/df, RMSEA, IFI, CFI, TLI, and GFI. Descriptive and correlation analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. SEM analyses were conducted using AMOS 24.0. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

For fsQCA, raw data were calibrated into fuzzy-set membership scores to reflect the degree of set membership for each case. Following Rihoux and Ragin,46 three calibration anchors were applied: full membership (1.0), full non-membership (0.0), and the crossover point (0.5). A necessity analysis was first conducted, with conditions exceeding a consistency threshold of 0.90 considered necessary.56 A truth table was constructed, and consistency and frequency thresholds were set to generate configurational solutions. Complex, parsimonious, and intermediate solutions were obtained, with the intermediate solution used as the basis for interpretation. Core conditions were identified by their presence in both parsimonious and intermediate solutions, while peripheral conditions appeared only in the intermediate solution.56 Raw coverage indicated the proportion of cases explained by each configuration.56 Unique coverage reflected the explanatory power of individual configurations.56 Consistency assessed the reliability of each configuration in explaining the outcome.56

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 212 valid samples were collected. The majority of participants were aged 31 years or older, which is consistent with the demographic characteristics of parents raising adolescents aged 10–19 years. The gender distribution was relatively balanced. Nearly half of the participants (49.1%) were employed full-time. More than half of the families (54.7%) reported shared parenting responsibilities between both parents. Most participants (56.1%) had one child. Univariate analyses showed that parental burnout differed significantly according to marital status and employment status (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed across other demographic characteristics. Preliminary Analysis

Prior to SEM analysis, assumptions of multivariate normality, linearity, and the absence of extreme outliers were examined. The absolute values of skewness and kurtosis for all continuous variables were within acceptable ranges. Mardia’s test indicated no significant departure from multivariate normality (P > 0.05). Scatterplots supported the assumption of linear relationships among the continuous psychological variables. No extreme univariate or multivariate outliers were identified based on z-scores and Mahalanobis distance statistics.

To examine potential mode effects, key study variables were compared between participants who completed the questionnaire online and those who completed it offline. The results showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). Harman’s single-factor test identified seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first unrotated factor explained 28.59% of the total variance. This value was below the 40% threshold, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern in this study.

Before conducting the main analysis, pairwise correlations among the psychological variables in the model were examined. As shown in Supplemental Material 1, the mean parental burnout score was (27.00 ± 5.47). Parental burnout was positively correlated with parenting stress (r = 0.485, P < 0.01) and expressive suppression (r = 0.433, P < 0.01). It was negatively correlated with family support (r = −0.391, P < 0.01) and cognitive reappraisal (r = −0.436, P < 0.01).

Structural Equation Modeling

The SEM demonstrated a good model fit: χ2/df = 1.071, CFI = 0.982, IFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.981, GFI = 0.858, RMR = 0.043, and RMSEA = 0.018. Table 2 presents the main path analysis results of the model. Both parenting stress and family support significantly predicted different emotion regulation strategies. Parenting stress exerted a significantly negative influence on cognitive reappraisal (β = –0.422, P < 0.001) and a significantly positive influence on expressive suppression (β = 0.367, P < 0.001). Family support had a significant positive effect on cognitive reappraisal (β = 0.489, P < 0.001) and a significant negative effect on expressive suppression (β = –0.328, P < 0.01). According to conventional benchmarks for standardized path coefficients, these effects can be interpreted as moderate in magnitude.57 When examining the effects of all antecedent variables on parental burnout, expressive suppression showed a significant positive effect (β = 0.355, P < 0.001). FsQCA

Calibration

For continuous variables, because no established theoretical or clinical cutoffs were available, calibration was performed using the direct method proposed by Pappas and Woodside.58 Following commonly adopted practices and the empirical characteristics of the data, the 75th, 50th, and 25th percentiles were used as the thresholds for full membership, crossover, and full non-membership, respectively. All variables were then calibrated into fuzzy sets with membership scores ranging from 0 to 1. For categorical variables, binary (or ternary) coding was applied, treating them as crisp sets (ie, membership scores of 0 or 1). A small constant (0.001) was added to fuzzy-set membership scores to prevent cases with a value of exactly 0.5 from being automatically excluded.59 Supplement material 2 presents the calibration thresholds for each variable.

Sufficiency Assessment of Configuration Conditions

Following recommendations in the fsQCA literature, a minimum consistency threshold of 0.80 was specified for sufficiency analysis.60 Given the sample size of 212 cases, a frequency threshold of four was adopted, which is consistent with recommendations for medium-to-large samples.60 After applying the frequency criterion, the truth table was sorted according to raw consistency. The fsQCA software identified 0.82 as the natural consistency breakpoint, which was subsequently used to generate the intermediate solution. The fsQCA results revealed three significant configurations associated with high parental burnout. The overall solution coverage was 0.408, indicating that these three configurations jointly explained approximately 40.8% of the cases with high parental burnout. The overall solution consistency was 0.879, which exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.8.

Configuration 1 consisted of single, employed parents with high parenting stress and low cognitive reappraisal. Configuration 2 consisted of single, employed parents with high parenting stress and high expressive suppression. Configuration 3 consisted of unemployed parents with high parenting stress, low family support, low cognitive reappraisal, and high expressive suppression. Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the identified configurations to calibration choices, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using thresholds based on the 95th percentile for full membership, the 50th percentile for the crossover point, and the 5th percentile for full non-membership.

As shown in Supplemental Material 3, the alternative calibration yielded five configurations. The three core configurations identified in the original solution were consistently retained under the alternative calibration, indicating stability of the main configurational structure. Two additional configurations emerged; however, these showed limited robustness. One configuration fell below the consistency threshold (0.806 < 0.82), while the other exhibited very low unique coverage (0.0417), suggesting limited empirical relevance. Overall solution coverage increased from 0.408 to 0.491, while solution consistency decreased slightly from 0.879 to 0.866. Taken together, the stability of the core configurations and the marginal nature of the additional solutions suggest that the findings are robust to alternative calibration specifications.

Integration of SEM and fsQCA Results

The SEM and fsQCA analyses provided complementary insights into parental burnout. SEM identified expressive suppression as a key psychological factor associated with parental burnout. Parenting stress and family support were indirectly associated with parental burnout through expressive suppression. In contrast, cognitive reappraisal showed no significant direct or indirect associations in the SEM model. However, fsQCA results indicated that low cognitive reappraisal emerged as a core condition in two high-burnout configurations, suggesting its relevance within specific combinations of contextual and psychological conditions.

Discussion

Research Findings

This study integrated the BR2 theory and the ERP model to examine parental burnout among parents of adolescents with mood disorders. Using a combination of SEM and fsQCA, the findings provide complementary evidence on both variable-centered associations and configurational pathways.

The SEM results indicated that parenting stress and family support were indirectly associated with parental burnout through expressive suppression. No significant direct associations with parental burnout were observed. This pattern is broadly consistent with the integrated BR2 and ERP framework.40 Within this framework, parenting stress and family support represent relatively distal contextual factors. In contrast, emotion regulation reflects a more proximal psychological process through which parents respond to caregiving demands.28 After emotion regulation was taken into account, the direct associations between parenting stress, family support, and parental burnout were substantially attenuated. These findings suggest that expressive suppression may represent an important psychological factor associated with how caregiving demands and resources relate to parental burnout.61 Although the indirect effects were small in magnitude, they may still be practically relevant given the chronic and cumulative nature of caregiving demands experienced by parents.

Cognitive reappraisal was not significantly associated with parental burnout. One possible explanation is that expressive suppression and cognitive reappraisal differ in their regulatory demands. Expressive suppression is an immediate strategy that targets emotional expression, whereas cognitive reappraisal requires greater cognitive effort and flexible reinterpretation of stressful situations.62 For parents facing recurrent illness episodes and persistent caregiving demands, regulating emotional expression may therefore be more feasible in daily caregiving contexts. Consequently, expressive suppression may be more relevant to day-to-day caregiving experiences and show a stronger association with parental burnout.63 The null finding for cognitive reappraisal may also be related to measurement. The ERQ assesses general tendencies rather than parenting-specific reappraisal in response to caregiving stress. Parenting-specific cognitive reappraisal may therefore be more closely associated with parental burnout.

The fsQCA findings further supported this interpretation. Configuration 2 was characterized by high parenting stress and high expressive suppression. This suggests that expressive suppression may be particularly relevant under conditions of elevated caregiving stress. Sociocultural factors may further reinforce this pattern. In Chinese culture, openly expressing negative emotions is often viewed as a sign of poor self-control or personal vulnerability.64 Among parents of adolescents with mood disorders, concerns about mental illness stigma and family reputation may discourage emotional expression and encourage the suppression of anxiety, helplessness, and distress.65 Consequently, expressive suppression may become a more common coping strategy.66 However, persistent reliance on suppression may hinder emotional processing and contribute to emotional exhaustion, thereby being associated with higher levels of parental burnout.

FsQCA identified low cognitive reappraisal as a core condition in two of the three high-burnout configurations, highlighting its configuration-dependent role in parental burnout. For example, Configuration 1 (covering 17.1% of high-burnout cases) was characterized by single, employed parents experiencing high parenting stress and low cognitive reappraisal. This pattern may reflect cumulative cognitive burden. Single parents often lack a partner to share caregiving responsibilities, while employment introduces competing work demands. Combined with high parenting stress, these circumstances may reduce the cognitive resources available for adaptive emotion regulation, making cognitive reappraisal less accessible within this specific context. The apparent discrepancy between the SEM and fsQCA findings may also reflect their different analytical perspectives. SEM estimates the net association of individual variables while controlling for other factors, whereas fsQCA identifies combinations of conditions associated with an outcome.58

The fsQCA revealed a dual role of employment status. Configurations 1 and 2, employment co-occurred with high parenting stress, low family support, and low cognitive reappraisal in high-burnout configurations. Balancing work and caregiving responsibilities may increase role overload and emotional exhaustion, consistent with work–family conflict theory.67 This finding contradicts the results of Wu et al,68 They suggested that employment may alleviate burnout through contextual shifts or a sense of professional accomplishment, particularly serving as a protective factor for mothers. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in sample characteristics, including cultural context, child illness severity, and measurement approaches to parental burnout. By contrast, Configuration 3 identified unemployment, together with low family support, high parenting stress, and poor emotion regulation, as another pathway associated with high parental burnout. This pattern may reflect simultaneous limitations in economic, social, and psychological resources. It suggest that the association between employment status and parental burnout depends on the broader psychosocial context rather than employment status alone.

Strength, Limitation and Further Research

This study established an integrative framework for explaining parental burnout by combining the BR2 theory and the emotion regulation process model. The framework views parental burnout as an imbalance among demands, resources, risks, and emotion regulation. It provides a clearer and more dynamic understanding of its development in parents of adolescents with mood disorders. The combined use of SEM and fsQCA answered not only which factors are important but also how they work together, making the conclusions more robust and comprehensive.

However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships among these variables. Participants were recruited from several hospitals in Shanghai, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should include samples from different regions, cultural contexts, and socioeconomic backgrounds to enhance external validity. Multiple testing corrections were not applied, and several potentially relevant clinical and family-related factors were not assessed, including illness severity, duration of illness, history of hospitalizations, adolescent suicidal risk, and parental history of mental health problems. Although the overall solution coverage of the fsQCA analysis was acceptable, approximately 59% of high-burnout cases were not explained by the identified configurations. Future studies should incorporate additional contextual, familial, and clinical conditions to better characterize configurations associated with high parental burnout.

Conclusion

This study integrated SEM and fsQCA to examine associative patterns of parental burnout among parents of adolescents with mood disorders. SEM results indicated that parenting stress and family support were indirectly associated with parental burnout through expressive suppression, whereas cognitive reappraisal showed no significant direct or indirect effects. FsQCA suggested that low cognitive reappraisal functioned as a core condition in certain high-burnout configurations. Three high-burnout configurations were characterized by limited economic, social, and psychological resources. This suggests that parental burnout is associated with multiple configurations of contextual risk and resource deprivation. Given the cross-sectional design and hospital-based recruitment, causal inferences cannot be made. The relatively low solution coverage in fsQCA further suggests that additional contextual, familial, and clinical factors may be involved. Future longitudinal studies with more diverse populations are needed to clarify temporal relationships and contributing factors.
