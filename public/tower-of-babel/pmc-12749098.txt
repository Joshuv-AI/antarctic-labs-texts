Rapid surgical intervention for acute spinal subdural hematoma triggered by ruptured spinal dural arteriovenous fistula: illustrative case
Authors: Abdul Naeem, Frank Mazza, Abdalla Shamisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) are rare vascular malformations that typically cause progressive congestive myelopathy. Hemorrhagic presentations of dAVFs, such as subdural hematomas (SDHs), are exceedingly rare and can lead to acute neurological deterioration.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present a case of spinal SDH secondary to a ruptured spinal dAVF. A 46-year-old woman presented with rapidly progressing paraparesis to complete paralysis within hours, without a history of trauma, anticoagulation, or infection. Thoracic spine MRI revealed an intradural, ventral extramedullary lesion from T4 to T7, consistent with a compressive hematoma. The patient underwent a thoracic laminectomy for evacuation. Intraoperative indocyanine green angiography identified a spinal dAVF near the left T7 nerve root sleeve, which was successfully clipped. At the 9-month follow-up, the patient had significantly recovered with 4/5 strength in her lower extremities.LESSONSThis is the fourth documented instance of a spinal dAVF presenting as a spinal SDH. Clinicians should consider this diagnosis in patients with acute paraplegia, back pain, and sphincter dysfunction, especially without trauma or coagulopathy. Diagnosis may be complicated by angiographically occult lesions due to a hematoma mass effect, making intraoperative imaging essential. The spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF) is a rare vascular shunt between arteries and veins in the dura mater, having an annual incidence of 5–10 cases per million.1 Hemorrhagic manifestations in dAVF in the root sleeve are notably infrequent, making the occurrence of spinal subdural hematomas (SDHs) in this context exceptionally rare, with only 3 other documented cases.2–4 Contrary to the sporadic hemorrhagic events, dAVFs more typically precipitate congestive myelopathy due to venous hypertension and resultant spinal cord ischemia.5

We present a case of spinal SDH caused by a ruptured spinal dAVF, along with a review of the relevant literature to provide insights into this rare condition.

Illustrative Case

A 46-year-old woman presented to our emergency department by ambulance after experiencing a precipitous onset of paraparesis that escalated to complete motor paralysis within hours. The extensive medical evaluation revealed no history of anticoagulation therapy, trauma, or infection that might account for her presentation. Neurological assessment confirmed bilateral lower extremity paralysis and a sensory deficit at the T6 dermatomal level. Furthermore, she exhibited signs of autonomic dysfunction, as evidenced by an acute loss of bowel and bladder control (American Spinal Injury Association [ASIA] grade A, modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 5).

Diagnostic thoracic spine MRI (Fig. 1) revealed an intradural extramedullary lesion positioned ventral to the spinal cord, extending from T4 to T7, and imposing significant compressive forces on the spinal cord. A thoracic laminectomy from T4 to T7 was performed to evacuate an SDH causing mass effect on the spinal cord. Abnormal vasculature adjacent to the nerve root was noted after hematoma evacuation. This prompted advanced intraoperative imaging with indocyanine green (ICG) angiography, which played a crucial role in real-time delineation of the spinal dAVF fed by a prominent arterial vessel draining into a dilated and serpiginous vein associated with the left T7 nerve root sleeve. Through precise microsurgical intervention, this vascular malformation was effectively clipped and disconnected. ICG was administered as a rapid intravenous bolus of 25 mg (0.3 mg/kg), followed by a 10-mL saline flush. The first run was obtained after dural opening and evacuation of the SDH with exposure of the suspicious arterial vessel draining into the dorsal spinal veins prior to fistula disconnection. This first run thus delineated arterial feeders and the arterialized draining vein. A second run was performed immediately after clipping and ligation to confirm elimination of early venous filling, with additional runs obtained as needed with a 5- to 10-minute washout period between injections. A video of the procedure accompanies the case report (Video 1). Postoperative spinal angiography revealed no residual spinal vascular malformations.

At the 9-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated remarkable recovery, with motor strength in the lower extremities improving from dense paralysis preoperatively to 4/5 bilaterally in all the major muscle groups (lower extremity ASIA grade E, mRS score 2). The patient regained independence in activities of daily living, reflecting significant recovery.

Literature review of hemorrhagic manifestations in spinal dAVF

Observations

Each case report in this literature review involves female patients at different stages of life. While this suggests a possible sex susceptibility to this rare pathology, the data are insufficient to draw definitive conclusions. The clinical presentations, however, were heterogeneous, encompassing a spectrum of neurological deficits from severe back pain to paraplegia including sphincter dysfunction. Two of the 3 published cases presented with only back pain,2,3 while 1 patient presented with paraparesis and sphincter dysfunction.4 In contrast to the cases reviewed, our patient exhibited dense paralysis.

All cases published in the literature review underwent surgical decompression via laminectomy, dural opening, and hematoma evacuation, followed by ligation of the fistula. This consistent approach underscores the potential efficacy of surgical intervention across diverse presentations and symptom severities. It is noteworthy that none of the patients received endovascular treatment for the spinal dAVF, an alternative therapy that might be considered in cases without significant cord compression.

Remarkably, each case documented in the literature review culminated in complete symptomatic resolution within 6–9 months—a notably positive outcome that extends to our patient, who initially presented with paraplegia. Our patient demonstrated significant postoperative recovery, achieving ambulatory independence with residual motor weakness graded at 4/5 and persistent urinary urgency. Her recovery plateaued at 9 months but highlights the potential for meaningful improvement. This knowledge of postoperative recovery can assist spine surgeons in crucial patient care discussions.

Lessons

Ultimately, diagnosing spinal dAVFs can be challenging when lesions appear angiographically occult on catheter-based spinal angiography due to the distorting effect of a spinal SDH, as reported in 2 of 3 prior cases.2,4 Spinal dAVFs typically present with congestive myelopathy, as hemorrhagic presentations are exceedingly rare. In this context, the SDH mass effect can hide vascular abnormalities and increase the likelihood of false-negative findings on preoperative studies, including CT myelography, MR angiography, and catheter angiography, which may delay treatment initiation. In the present case, patient history and examination excluded trauma and anticoagulation as etiologies. Other potential causes included spontaneous hemorrhage, vascular malformations, coagulopathy, and hemorrhagic neoplasm. Spinal dAVF was not suspected preoperatively due to its extreme rarity, consistent with the limited reports in the literature. Given the patient’s after-hours presentation of acute paralysis and incontinence, urgent surgery was performed in accordance with guidelines recommending intervention within 24 hours to preserve neurological function.6

The diagnosis of spinal dAVF was only ascertained intraoperatively using ICG angiography, which also confirmed complete ligation with no additional vascular anomalies observed. Nevertheless, ICG is limited to the exposed surgical field. Thus, a delayed angiogram was performed postoperatively and confirmed no additional vascular anomalies.

Several practical considerations accompany the use of ICG.7 Vessels covered by tissue or other products are subject to signal attenuation; thus, all vessels must be well exposed and within the microscope’s line of sight. Adequate hemodynamic stability is also required, as changes in cardiac output may alter ICG transit time and vascular filling patterns. Finally, guidelines limit a cumulative dose of ICG to < 5 mg/kg/day;7 our approach remained well below 1 mg/kg. Findings were interpreted in conjunction with preoperative imaging and intraoperative anatomical inspection to guide surgical decision-making. No adverse effects related to ICG administration were observed in this case.

In summary, the spectrum of clinical presentations for spinal dAVF can be broad, with acute spinal SDH being a noteworthy and extremely rare manifestation. Clinicians should maintain a high degree of suspicion for this entity when evaluating cases of acute paraplegia with back pain accompanied by sphincter dysfunction, as prompt treatment is required. The potential for some spinal dAVFs to be angiographically occult due to the distorting impact of a spinal SDH necessitates a diligent investigative approach, particularly when standard etiologies for spinal SDH such as coagulopathies or trauma are excluded. Published accounts corroborate that pronounced cord compression secondary to spinal SDHs demands surgical intervention, with a heightened alertness for concomitant vascular anomalies. Prompt surgical decompression and meticulous ligation of associated dAVFs have been shown to yield favorable neurological outcomes, a finding that is supported by the case delineated herein and corroborated by a comprehensive review of the literature.
