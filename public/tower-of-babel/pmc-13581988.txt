MYZAP promotes angiogenesis by targeting RND1 after myocardial infarction
Authors: Lina Xuan, Guangze Wang, Xiufang Li, Shengjie Wang, Yuman Gao, Jingheng Wang, Hui Zhang, Shuo Zhuang, Huishan Luo, Jianjun Guo, Mingyu Zhang, Xingmei Yang, Hailong Zhang, Qingqing Zhang, Yiyang Qiao, Kai Kang, Ying Zhang, Lihua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMyocardial infarction (MI) remains the leading cause of death due to cardiovascular disease worldwide. role of MYZAP in angiogenesis remains unclear. The expression of MYZAP in MI was significantly decreased. Endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP improved cardiac function, increased angiogenesis, and restored hindlimb blood flow levels in MI mice detected by MoorFLPIReview. The proliferation ability, migration, and invasion of endothelial cells were reduced after hypoxia, which were restored after overexpression of MYZAP or knockdown of RND1. The pro-angiogenic effect of MYZAP overexpression was reversed by RND1 overexpression. The proliferation ability, migration and invasion ability, and tube formation ability of endothelial cells were significantly reduced after knockdown of MYZAP. Direct interaction between MYZAP and RND1 was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and thermal stability assays. TRIM21 was identified as a potential E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for RND1 degradation. Our work revealed that endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP enhances angiogenesis by activating the VEGF/PI3K/AKT pathway in MI.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent the predominant cause of global mortality, with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) constituting a particularly critical manifestation.1 While timely reperfusion of ischemic myocardium is considered a key pathway for salvaging jeopardized cardiac tissue and improving clinical outcomes, significant challenges persist in AMI management.2 Despite many advances in therapeutic strategies, such as percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass, mortality after AMI remains high.3 This clinical predicament stems primarily from the formation of non-contractile scar tissue and the inherent inability of adult cardiomyocytes to regenerate.4 Consequently, there exists an urgent, unmet need for novel therapeutic interventions that can effectively mitigate myocardial damage and prevent the progression to heart failure (HF) following AMI.

MI can cause severe damage to the coronary microcirculation, leading to vascular collapse and capillary rarefaction in the infarct area.5 Accumulating evidence indicates that angiogenesis is essential for tissue repair or regeneration, including in MI, where the formation of new blood vessels can rescue cells from death and attenuate poor remodeling.6 Repair after MI is divided into three distinct but overlapping phases: the inflammatory phase, the proliferative phase, and the mature phase.7 Wound healing after MI involves a robust angiogenic response that commences in the border zone and extends into the necrotic infarct core. Revascularization through angiogenesis induces the sprouting and maturation of new microvessels within ischemic myocardium, orchestrating the development of robust collateral circulation networks. This endogenous bypass system effectively restores myocardial perfusion, attenuates ischemic injury, and enhances ventricular performance through improved oxygen and nutrient delivery to compromised cardiac tissue.8 Therefore, identifying key regulators of angiogenesis as therapeutic targets to promote blood flow recovery after MI is of great interest.

By targeting the key factors of angiogenesis after MI, it can regulate the repair of MI and produce improvement, which is a treatment method in the current treatment strategy. It has been found that MSNs/microRNA-21-5p injectable hydrogel has dual functions of immune modification and angiogenesis enhancement in the treatment of myocardial infarction in pigs.9 M2 macrophage-derived exosomes promote angiogenesis and improve cardiac function after MI.10 Endothelial cell HSPA12B and yes-associated protein cooperatively regulate angiogenesis following myocardial infarction.11

Angiogenesis after MI is regulated by a variety of factors, including mesenchymal stem cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and so on. A major component of MYZAP, which links the cytoplasmic patches of adhesion junctions (AJ) of endothelial cells in the blood and lymphatic vasculature of mammals, has attracted our attention. MYZAP is not only a component of cell-cell junctions in the myocardium, but is also present in different endothelial cells of the blood and lymph vascular systems of mammals, suggesting that this protein not only plays a specific role in the heart, but may also have a broader set of functions in the vessel systems.12 In cardiac tissue, MYZAP is localized to the ID and directly binds to desmoplakin and zonula occludens-1.13 Coding variants in MYZAP have been found to increase the risk of atrial fibrillation.14 MYZAP deficiency promotes adverse cardiac remodeling through differential regulation of MAPK/SRF and β-Catenin/GSK-3β signaling.15 The importance of MYZAP in the heart has been fully proved, and strict control of its expression and activity is essential for maintaining contractility and proper response to stress. Additionally, MYZAP serves as a key component of endothelial adherens junctions, contributing to endothelial cell function by regulating solute transport and cellular permeability.16 These findings suggest that MYZAP not only exerts specific roles in the heart but may also have broader functional significance in the vascular system.17

In our previous study, a marked reduction in MYZAP expression correlates with progressive cardiac dysfunction and compromised vascular regeneration following MI. Therapeutic upregulation of MYZAP not only ameliorates ventricular performance but also potentiates capillary formation, indicative of its dual role in coordinating cardiomyocyte-endothelial interactions during tissue repair. However, the role of MYZAP in ischemic heart disease has not yet been explored, so we explored the role of MYZAP in angiogenesis after MI. Therefore, this study employs endothelial cell-specific overexpression of MYZAP to investigate its role and underlying mechanisms in post-MI angiogenesis, aiming to elucidate its regulatory effects on vascular regeneration following MI.

Results

The expression of MYZAP was decreased in the myocardium after MI

MI models were established by ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery, and the relevant tests were carried out after MI on the 14th and 28th days (Figure 1A). It was found that ejection fraction (EF%) and shortening fraction (FS%) were significantly decreased on the 14th and 28th day after MI (Figure 1B). IB4 staining further revealed that the number of cardiac vessels in MI mice was significantly diminished at both 14 and 28 days post-infarction (Figure 1C). Based on this series of pathological phenomena, to evaluate the function of MYZAP, we detected the expression level of MYZAP in cardiac tissues after MI. MYZAP mRNA level was down-regulated, and its protein expression was significantly reduced on day 14 after MI, and both were further decreased on day 28 (Figures 1D and 1E). The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that MYZAP was expressed in the nucleus and cell membrane of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Figure 1F). To investigate whether endothelial cell-type specificity influences MYZAP regulation, we measured its expression in HUVECs and mouse aortic endothelial cells (MAECs) subjected to hypoxia. Interestingly, the protein and mRNA levels of MYZAP in HUVECs and MAECs in the Hypoxia group were significantly lower than those in the Ctl group (Figures 1G and 1H). Endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP significantly improves cardiac function and enhances angiogenesis in MI mice

To determine whether MYZAP improves cardiac function after MI, we constructed an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV-9) that specifically overexpresses MYZAP in endothelial cells to further clarify the role of MYZAP on endothelial cells after MI (Figure 2A). Throughout the 4-week observation period, no notable differences in general health status or housing conditions were detected among the experimental groups. We also detected the overexpression efficiency of MYZAP and found that the protein expression of MYZAP in the aorta of the mice in the MYZAP overexpression group was significantly increased (Figure S1). Co-staining of MYZAP and CD31 revealed a marked increase in MYZAP expression in the blood vessels of cardiac tissue as well (Figure S1C). Echocardiographic results showed that the ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%) were significantly lower at 14 and 28 days after MI than in the Sham group (Figures 2B and 2C). A marked increase in left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters (LVIDd/s) was observed, which was indicative of substantial cardiac dysfunction (Figure 2D). However, at 28 days after MI, these parameters were significantly improved in the MYZAP-overexpressing group (Figures 2B–2D). Considering that MYZAP serves as a predominant structural component within the cytoplasmic plaques of endothelial adhesion junctions in both blood and lymphatic vasculature across mammalian species. Subsequently, we examined the effect of MYZAP overexpression on angiogenesis after MI. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that, compared with the NC group, the positive signals of IB4 and CD31 in the infarct border zone of MI mice were significantly increased after overexpression of MYZAP, which indicated that angiogenesis was increased (Figures 2E and 2F). In addition, we performed Micro-CT experiments to visualize the cardiac microvasculature in mice with MI. Micro-CT analysis further revealed that MYZAP overexpression promoted angiogenesis in mice following myocardial infarction (Figure 2G). These results suggest that endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP can improve cardiac function in mice with MI and promote the repair of ischemic myocardium by increasing angiogenesis in the infarct border zone.

Overexpression of MYZAP increases vessel density

Von Willebrand Factor (vWF) is a profound angiogenic molecule that is selectively expressed in endothelial cells. vWF serves as a marker for angiogenesis, enabling the quantification of blood vessel formation. To validate the pro-angiogenic function of MYZAP, we performed immunofluorescence staining for the endothelial markers vWF and CD31 on cardiac tissue sections. The results demonstrated that overexpression of MYZAP significantly reverses the decreased expression of vWF and CD31 following MI (Figures 3A–3C). Similarly, overexpression of MYZAP in HUVECs restores the reduced expression of vWF and CD31 after hypoxia. These findings demonstrate that MYZAP possesses a specific pro-angiogenic capacity. Co-localization of vWF and CD31 by immunofluorescence shows that MYZAP promotes the expression of vWF and CD31 in HUVECs in vitro. A lower expression of vWF was observed in the hypoxia group than in the Ctl group, which was enhanced in the MYZAP group compared with the +NC group. MYZAP promotes angiogenesis in a mouse model of hindlimb ischemia

To further investigate the potential beneficial effects of MYZAP in vivo, hindlimb ischemia was surgically induced in C57BL/6 mice, and MoorFLPIReview was utilized to evaluate blood flow perfusion in mice treated with different groups, thereby validating the promotional effect of MYZAP on angiogenesis (Figure 4A). As illustrated in Figures 4B and 4C, compared to the Sham group, there was a significant reduction in hindlimb blood flow in the mice following surgery. On day 3, mice in the MYZAP overexpression group demonstrated marked improvement in blood flow recovery. Furthermore, blood flow in the left hindlimbs of the mice gradually recovered on days 5, 7, and 14 post-surgery. Quantitative analysis of the area under the curve (AUC) derived from MoorFLPI imaging further confirmed significantly enhanced blood flow recovery in the MYZAP group over the entire observation period (Figures 4D and 4E). The effects of MYZAP overexpression on the paracrine pathway post-MI in a cardiac AAV9-MYZAP-OE mouse model.

To confirm whether or not the paracrine pathway contributes to angiogenesis post-MI in cardiac AAV9-MYZAP-OE model, we performed ELISAs assay to detect secreted angiogenic factors VEGF from plasma of AAV9-ICAM2-MYZAP-treated mice. Our data showed that the VEGF level was decreased in plasma of MI mice, whereas MYZAP overexpression promotes VEGF paracrine in plasma in cardiac AAV9-MYZAP-OE mice model (Figure S3).

MYZAP overexpression accelerates angiogenesis in vitro

Angiogenesis is a very complex physiological process, which requires endothelial cells to have the ability to actively migrate, invade, and form tubes. Therefore, we constructed the MYZAP overexpression plasmid to further explore the effects of MYZAP on endothelial cells. qRT-PCR and western blot results showed that the expression levels of MYZAP mRNA and protein were significantly increased after transfection of the MYZAP plasmid (Figure S4).

The proliferation and migration ability of endothelial cells is an important index to evaluate their functional activity. Although hypoxia significantly reduced the proliferation of both HUVECs and MAECs, this effect was markedly reversed by MYZAP overexpression, which substantially restored their proliferative capacity (Figures 5A and 5B). Next, we further examined the effect of MYZAP on the migration and invasion ability of endothelial cells. Similarly, MYZAP overexpression significantly enhanced the migratory and invasive capacities of both HUVECs and MAECs under hypoxic conditions (Figures 5C and 5D). Subsequently, we investigated the effect of MYZAP on the ability of endothelial cells to form tubes. Following hypoxia, HUVECs exhibited a marked decrease in branch number and tube length, both of which were effectively rescued by overexpression of MYZAP (Figures 5E and 5F). MYZAP knockdown inhibits angiogenesis in vitro

To further clarify the role of MYZAP in endothelial cells, we knocked down MYZAP in endothelial cells and verified the efficiency of MYZAP knockdown by qRT-PCR and western blot. The mRNA and protein expression of MYZAP were significantly decreased in HUVECs compared with the NC group (Figure S6). Next, we further examined the function of MYZAP-knockdown endothelial cells. The results of the wound healing experiment showed that the proliferation and migration ability of HUVECs and MAECs in the si-MYZAP group was significantly lower than that in the NC group (Figures 6A and 6B). Transwell assays further revealed that MYZAP knockdown significantly attenuated the migration and invasion of HUVECs and MAECs (Figures 6C and 6D). Overexpression of MYZAP downregulates RND1 expression

In summary, MYZAP plays a crucial role in the functional activity of endothelial cells and angiogenesis. We aim to further investigate the mechanism by which MYZAP promotes angiogenesis. The expression of RND1 increases significantly after MI, whereas it decreases notably upon the overexpression of MYZAP (Figures 7A–7C ). Subsequently, we conducted a Co-IP experiment and discovered an interaction between MYZAP and RND1 (Figure 7D). Molecular docking results also revealed multiple binding sites between MYZAP and RND1 (Figure 7E). This suggests that MYZAP may play a role in promoting angiogenesis by targeting RND1. To demonstrate the underlying mechanism by which MYZAP downregulates RND1, we also tested whether this binding directly mediates RND1 degradation. Our results demonstrate that MYZAP overexpression significantly accelerates the degradation of RND1 protein, as evidenced by a shorter half-life of RND1 in MYZAP-overexpressing cells compared to controls. These findings support the conclusion that MYZAP downregulates RND1 at least in part by promoting its degradation. To delve into the specific mechanism of MYZAP in regulating RND1, we assess the effect of MYZAP on the thermal stability of the RND1 protein. The results showed that, compared with the NC group, overexpression of MYZAP significantly altered the melting curve of the RND1 protein (Figure 7F). Our proteomic screen using MYZAP pull-down identified TRIM21, a cytosolic and nuclear E3 ubiquitin ligase, as a potential interactor, and subsequent experiments confirmed that TRIM21 modulates RND1 ubiquitination. This finding offers a plausible mechanistic context for our nuclear MYZAP observation. Although TRIM21 is primarily known for antibody-mediated degradation, it can be ubiquitinate substrates involved in Rho GTPase signaling. We hypothesize that MYZAP may escort TRIM21 to RND1, possibly as a consequence of altered ubiquitin-proteasome dynamics. Notably, nuclear MYZAP might not possess direct transcriptional activity but could instead serve as a scaffold or modulator of nuclear E3 ligase functions (Figure 7G). RND1 and TRIM21 also have good binding (Figure S7).

Silencing RND1 promotes angiogenesis in vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of RND1 silencing in vivo, we administered endothelial-specific AAV9-sh-RND1 to mice subjected to MI. Echocardiographic analysis revealed that MI significantly impaired cardiac function, as evidenced by markedly reduced ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%) compared with the Sham group. Notably, endothelial-specific knockdown of RND1 effectively rescued cardiac function, with EF and FS values significantly improved in sh-RND1-treated mice relative to the sh-NC Ctl group (Figures 8A and 8B). We next investigated whether this functional recovery was associated with enhanced angiogenesis within the infarct zone. Immunofluorescence staining of cardiac sections revealed that the expression of the angiogenic markers vWF and CD31 was markedly reduced in the infarct area following MI, relative to Sham-operated controls. However, RND1 knockdown markedly upregulated both vWF and CD31 expression compared with the sh-NC group (Figures 8C and 8D), indicating increased vascular density in the infarct region. Consistent with these observations, molecular analysis revealed that silencing of RND1 in the vascular endothelium significantly upregulated the protein and mRNA expression of PI3K, as well as the mRNA levels of VEGF-A, compared with the NC group (Figures 8E–8H). To further validate the role of RND1 in post-MI angiogenesis, we performed RND1 overexpression in MYZAP-OE mice. Specifically, we constructed lentiviral vectors expressing RND1 and administered these vectors to MI mice with pre-existing MYZAP overexpression. Our results show that RND1 overexpression impairs post-MI angiogenesis marked by CD31 (Figure 9E) and cardiac function (Figure 9F). Moreover, in MYZAP-overexpressing mice subjected to MI, co-administration of RND1-overexpressing AAV9 attenuated the protective effects of MYZAP, further supporting that MYZAP acts through RND1.

Silencing RND1 promotes angiogenesis in vitro

We designed four siRNA sequences and conducted knockdown efficiency verification. It was found that the fourth siRNA sequence significantly reduced the expression of both mRNA and protein. Therefore, we selected this sequence for subsequent experiments. In HUVECs, knockdown of RND1 at the protein and mRNA level using si-RND1-286 was significant. In MAECs, knockdown of RND1 at the protein and mRNA level by si-RND1-567 was significant (Figure S9). The proliferation and migration ability of endothelial cells is an important index to evaluate their functional activity. After hypoxia, the proliferation of HUVECs and MAECs decreased, but knockdown of RND1 could significantly restore the proliferation of HUVECs and MAECs (Figures S10A and S10B). We next examined whether RND1 also participates in the regulation of endothelial cell function. Similar to the pro-angiogenic effects of MYZAP overexpression, knockdown of RND1 significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of HUVECs and MAECs under hypoxic conditions. These findings suggest that both MYZAP and RND1 act as positive regulators of angiogenesis, albeit through distinct mechanisms (Figures S10C and S10D). Subsequently, we investigated the effect of MYZAP on the ability of endothelial cells to form tubes. Hypoxic exposure decreased the branching and tube length of HUVECs, but RND1 knockdown significantly restored these parameters. (Figures S10E and S10F).

To further assess the effect of RND1 on angiogenic potential, we examined the expression of endothelial cell markers vWF and CD31 in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) under normoxic and hypoxic conditions following RND1 knockdown. Representative images further illustrated the enhanced co-localization of vWF and CD31 in si-RND1-treated cells, indicating promoted angiogenic activity (Figure S11B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that hypoxia alone significantly reduced the expression of both vWF and CD31 compared with normoxic controls. However, knockdown of RND1 markedly attenuated this effect, as evidenced by significantly increased fluorescence intensity of vWF and CD31 in RND1-silenced cells compared with negative control cells under hypoxic conditions (Figures S11B–S11D). These results demonstrate that RND1 knockdown promotes angiogenesis in HUVECs in vitro.

PI3K-AKT pathway is the key mechanism linking the MYZAP/RND1 axis

We next examined the effect of RND1 knockdown on the expression of VEGF-A and PIK3CA, key regulators of angiogenesis. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that hypoxia significantly reduced the expression of VEGF-A in HUVECs compared with normoxic controls. Notably, RND1 depletion effectively rescued this hypoxia-induced downregulation, as demonstrated by a significant increase in VEGF-A immunofluorescence intensity in RND1-silenced cells versus negative control cells under hypoxic exposure (Figures S12A and S12B). Similarly, PIK3CA expression was significantly downregulated under hypoxia relative to the Ctl group, whereas knockdown of RND1 led to a significant upregulation of PIK3CA expression compared with the +NC group under hypoxic conditions (Figures S12A–S12C). Representative immunofluorescence images further illustrated the enhanced expression of both VEGF-A and PI3K in RND1-silenced cells. These results indicate that RND1 negatively regulates VEGF-A and PI3K expression in endothelial cells under hypoxic conditions.

We further examined the effect of RND1 knockdown on the expression of VEGF-A and PI3K, key components of angiogenesis-related signaling pathways. Western blot analysis revealed that under hypoxic conditions, RND1-silenced endothelial cells exhibited significantly increased protein levels of both VEGF-A (Figures S13A and S13B) and PI3K compared with negative control cells (Figures S13A–S13C). Consistent with these findings, quantitative RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that RND1 knockdown also significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of PI3K and VEGF-A under hypoxic conditions (Figures S13D–S13E). Taken together, these results indicate that RND1 negatively regulates VEGF-A and PI3K expression at both the protein and transcriptional levels in endothelial cells.

To investigate the effect of RND1 on angiogenesis-related signaling, we examined the expression of VEGFR2 and AKT in endothelial cells following RND1 knockdown. Western blot analysis revealed that silencing RND1 significantly increased the protein level of VEGFR2, a key receptor mediating VEGF-A-induced angiogenesis. In addition, phosphorylation of AKT (p-AKT), a critical downstream effector of the PI3K pathway regulating cell proliferation, was markedly elevated in RND1-knockdown cells, whereas total AKT protein expression remained unchanged (Figure S14A). Under hypoxic conditions, quantitative analysis confirmed that p-AKT protein levels were significantly higher in RND1-silenced cells than in the si-NC group (Figure S14B). Consistently, VEGFR2 expression was also significantly upregulated in RND1-knockdown cells compared with control cells under hypoxia (Figure S14C). These results suggest that RND1 negatively regulates VEGFR2 expression and AKT phosphorylation in endothelial cells.

To identify whether activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway is the key mechanism linking the MYZAP/RND1 axis, the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 was used both in vitro and in vivo. We treated MYZAP-overexpressing endothelial cells with the specific PI3K inhibitor LY294002, and then assessed endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation. Our results demonstrate that LY294002 or si-PI3K treatment significantly reverses the enhanced proliferation (Figures 9A, 9B, S8A, and S8B), migration (Figures 9C and 9D), and tube formation (Figures 9E–9G) observed in MYZAP-OE ECs, confirming that PI3K activity is essential for the pro-angiogenic effects of MYZAP in vitro.

To validate these findings in the context of MI, we administered LY294002 to MYZAP-overexpressing mice subjected to MI. Functional assessments, including echocardiography and angiogenesis evaluation detected by CD31 immunostaining, revealed that LY294002 treatment largely abrogated the protective effects of MYZAP overexpression on post-MI angiogenesis (Figures 9H and 9I) and cardiac function (Figures 9J–9M). These in vivo data further support that MYZAP promotes angiogenesis and improves cardiac function primarily through activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway. Inflammation, ROS, and mitochondrial function were also detected (Figure S15). Compared with the Sham group, the expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS were significantly increased in the hearts of mice after MI; however, this upregulation was reversed by MYZAP overexpression, whereas the negative control (NC) treatment produced no such changes (Figure S15A). Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in myocardial tissues, as evaluated by dihydroethidium (DHE) staining, were significantly increased after MI relative to the Sham group; however, this elevation was attenuated by MYZAP overexpression, while NC treatment failed to elicit any significant changes (Figures S15B and S15C).

We next investigated whether RND1 knockdown affects endothelial cell survival under hypoxic conditions. Apoptosis analysis revealed that exposure to hypoxia for 24 h significantly induced apoptosis in HUVECs compared with normoxic controls (Figures S16A and S16B). However, this effect was markedly attenuated by RND1 silencing, as RND1-knockdown cells exhibited significantly lower apoptosis levels than negative control siRNA-treated cells under hypoxic conditions (Figures S16A and S16B). These results demonstrate that RND1 knockdown protects HUVECs from hypoxia-induced apoptosis.

Discussion

Myocardial infarction (MI) occurs due to acute or persistent reduction/cessation of coronary blood flow, leading to prolonged myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, ultimately resulting in ischemic necrosis of cardiomyocytes. Although current clinical treatments have significantly reduced patient mortality, critical complications such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and coronary restenosis post-treatment remain concerning. Therefore, identifying novel and effective therapeutic targets to preserve myocardial function and prevent heart failure progression is urgently needed.18,19

The expression of MYZAP was reduced in the myocardium of mice after MI and in hypoxic endothelial cells, and knockdown of MYZAP impaired the proliferation, migration, invasion, and tube formation of endothelial cells. Overexpression of MYZAP improves cardiac function of MI mice by regulating RND1 to enhance angiogenesis in MI, which was identified by the following experiments: (1) overexpression of MYZAP significantly increased left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening, decreased left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, and decreased left ventricular end-systolic diameter, increased IB4 and CD31 positive signals in the infarct border zone, and increased angiogenesis in MI mice compared to NC group. (2) The proliferation ability of endothelial cells was reduced after hypoxia compared with the NC group, which could be restored after overexpression of MYZAP or siRND1, which was reversed by overexpression of RND1 or by PI3K specific inhibitor LY294002. (3) The decreased migration and invasion ability of endothelial cells was restored after overexpression of MYZAP or siRND1. (4) Compared with negative controls, hypoxic endothelial cells exhibited a reduced capacity for vascular structure formation, which was significantly restored by MYZAP overexpression. However, this protective effect was abrogated by either RND1 overexpression or treatment with the PI3K-specific inhibitor LY294002. (5) The proliferation ability, migration and invasion ability, and tube formation ability of endothelial cells were significantly reduced after knockdown of MYZAP or RND1 overexpression. (6) Endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP down-regulated RND1 expression, and there was a direct interaction between MYZAP and RND1.20 (7) RND1 suppresses VEGF expression, PI3K/AKT signaling, and AKT phosphorylation in hypoxic endothelial cells. Knockdown of RND1 conversely activates angiogenic pathways, upregulates angiogenic markers, and reduces hypoxia-induced apoptosis, thereby improving post-MI cardiac function. (8) MYZAP scaffolds TRIM21 to RND1, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

MYZAP is a cardiomyocyte intercalated disc protein discovered through bioinformatics screening. The intercalated disc is a unique morphological feature of cardiomyocytes, whose complex junctional structures ensure mechanical and electrical coupling during contraction.21 MYZAP interacts closely with actin and desmosomal proteins at the plaque junctions, playing a crucial role in stabilizing cellular and tissue architecture as well as nuclear signaling. This finding aligns with Sebastian Pieperhoff’s report identifying MYZAP as a novel key component of endothelial adherens junctions in mammalian capillaries, arterioles, veins, and lymphatic vessels. Collectively, these observations point to a dual role for MYZAP, acting not only within cardiomyocytes but also exerting regulatory functions in the vascular compartment. Accordingly, our data revealed a significant decrease in MYZAP expression in the post-MI myocardium (Figure 1). Endothelial-specific overexpression of MYZAP significantly improves cardiac function and enhances angiogenesis in MI mice (Figure 2).

Endothelial cells play a central role in angiogenesis after MI. They form new blood vessels through proliferation and migration, improving blood supply to the ischemic myocardium and promoting tissue repair. Key mechanisms involve factors such as VEGF and FGF activating signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and Notch to regulate cellular behavior. Given the established centrality of PI3K/AKT in endothelial function, our observation that MYZAP overexpression enhances AKT phosphorylation and promotes neovascularization in the post-MI heart suggests that MYZAP may act as a novel upstream activator of this endothelial signaling axis. This neovascularization not only facilitates the clearance of necrotic debris and the delivery of inflammatory cells for granulation tissue formation, but also enhances local oxygen supply, nutrient diffusion, and metabolic waste removal. Beyond these classic roles, MYZAP may confer additional cytoprotective effects by modulating inflammatory responses (Figure S15), reducing ROS accumulation (Figure S15), thereby attenuating cardiac injury at the infarct border. Our data demonstrated that MI markedly upregulated the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS, concomitant with elevated intracellular ROS levels, whereas these effects were significantly attenuated by MYZAP overexpression. Mechanistically, MYZAP overexpression enhanced AKT phosphorylation in the post-MI myocardium, implicating MYZAP as a positive upstream modulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway. To substantiate this causal relationship, we employed the PI3K-specific inhibitor LY294002, which effectively abrogated both the cardioprotective and proangiogenic effects conferred by MYZAP overexpression. Collectively, these findings indicate that MYZAP protects against MI-induced inflammatory and oxidative injury through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, which in turn suppresses pro-inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species.

The pivotal role of PI3K/AKT signaling in post-MI pathophysiology is well documented. Activation of this pathway has been shown to attenuate the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS, thereby limiting excessive inflammatory infiltration and cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the ischemic myocardium.22 Concurrently, PI3K/AKT activation upregulates antioxidant enzymes and curtails intracellular ROS accumulation, a major contributor to oxidative damage following MI.23 Beyond its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions, the PI3K/AKT pathway also serves as a pivotal regulator of post-MI angiogenesis, critically promoting endothelial cell survival and tube formation under hypoxic stress.24 In line with these established roles, our work reveals that MYZAP overexpression activates PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby not only mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress but also fostering neovascularization in the infarcted heart. These observations position MYZAP as a novel upstream activator of PI3K/AKT that coordinates multifaceted cardioprotective programs, with particular relevance to angiogenesis, an essential process for long-term functional recovery after MI.

Although endothelial cells are essential for angiogenesis and anti-inflammatory responses, there are currently no targeted endothelial cell-based therapies for myocardial infarction. To elucidate the functional significance of MYZAP in endothelial cells, we conducted a series of in vitro assays, including scratch wound-healing, Transwell migration/invasion, and Matrigel-based tube formation. Our data revealed that MYZAP overexpression markedly reversed the hypoxia-induced deficits in proliferation, migration, invasion, and tube formation, with no discernible effect on cell viability (Figure 5). Conversely, knockdown of MYZAP markedly reduced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tube formation (Figure 6). While MYZAP is canonically described as a junctional protein, our reproducible observation suggests the possibility of context-dependent subcellular trafficking. We have no functional evidence for a transcriptional role of nuclear MYZAP. Nevertheless, given emerging examples of junctional proteinssuch as β-catenin acting as transcriptional co-regulators, we cannot exclude that MYZAP might similarly influence gene expression. Further studies using live imaging, biochemical fractionation, and chromatin assays are warranted to clarify the nature and function of nuclear MYZAP. These findings indicate that MYZAP plays a critical role in the dynamic regulation of angiogenic responses in endothelial cells.

Previous studies have demonstrated that MYZAP functions as a novel intercalated disc protein to maintain cardiac function and participates in the pathogenesis of cardiomyopathy.25 When MYZAP is deficient in vivo, ERK1/2 and SRF-signaling are inhibited, resulting in heightened biomechanical stress-induced maladaptation.26 The loss of MYZAP promotes adverse cardiac remodeling through differential regulation of MAPK/SRF and β-catenin/GSK-3β.15 These studies together identified that MYZAP deficiency was associated with cardiomyopathy, heart failure, and cardiac death. Our research further expands the role of MYZAP in the cardiovascular system, particularly in post-myocardial infarction angiogenesis. Overexpression of MYZAP further promotes endothelial cell proliferation and migration, thereby improving myocardial ischemic injury.

Notably, overexpression of MYZAP has been found to be effective in modulating endothelial cell behavior and promoting angiogenesis, which plays a crucial role in angiogenesis after MI. Our results indicated that overexpression of MYZAP promoted endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and the formation of tubular networks in vitro. Overexpression of MYZAP also promotes angiogenesis by increasing vWF and CD31 and restores blood flow in mice with left hindlimb ischemia in vivo. Growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor A and Akt1 control vascular permeability after MI. The mechanisms involved in angiogenesis after MI include copper loss, microRNA, Akt1, inflammation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondria, and the interaction between endothelial cells and pericytes. CXCL12-CXCR4, Wnt-β-catenin, VEGF, and RND1 signaling activity take part in angiogenesis after MI.27 RND1 was predicted to be a potential target of MYZAP, which was identified by a Co-IP experiment. Downregulation of RND1 promotes wound healing ability, migration and invasion, and tube formation (Figure 8).28 It has been demonstrated that endothelial notch signaling directly regulates the small GTPase RND1 to facilitate Notch suppression of endothelial migration in previous studies.29 Previous study identified that the hydrogel’s pro-angiogenic capability, achieved through programmed anti-oxidation and inflammation resolution, effectively promotes neovascularization. This vascular restoration is critical for salvaging the ischemic border zone, thereby enhancing cardiac function post-MI and establishing a foundation for sustained myocardial repair.30 The muscle-homing peptide BV2 enables targeted delivery of biotherapeutics to cardiac tissue, offering a powerful strategy for treating cardiomyopathy.31

A recent genome-wide screen and epigenomic analysis revealed that in primary cultured endothelial cells following VEGF stimulation, the NFAT transcription factor regulates endothelial cell activation by controlling RND1 expression, which modulates cell migration, tube formation, and barrier formation.32,33 Additionally, in human microvascular endothelial cells (HMVECs) pretreated with IL-1β, upregulated expression of RND1 was detected, suggesting that RND1 may be involved in vascular inflammation.34 Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis in primary human endothelial cells and neonatal mouse brains after induction of Notch signaling identified RND1 as a novel direct transcriptional target of Notch.29,35 It is rapidly and directly regulated by endothelial Notch signaling and participates in processes such as angiogenesis, endothelial migration, and RAS activity. In our study, we found that RND1 may interact with MYZAP to regulate angiogenesis. The findings establish RND1 as a novel negative regulator of angiogenic signaling and endothelial survival, with significant implications for cardiac repair following myocardial infarction. The observation that RND1 knockdown simultaneously upregulates VEGFR2, PI3K, and VEGF-A while enhancing AKT phosphorylation suggests that RND1 functions as a nodal point integrating multiple pro-angiogenic pathways. This is consistent with previous reports identifying RND1 as a direct transcriptional target of Notch signaling that suppresses endothelial migration and sprouting angiogenesis. Notably, RND1 has also been shown to be regulated by VEGF-inducible transcription factors NFATc1 and Egr, which bind to the RND1 locus to modulate endothelial activation, migration, and barrier function. These findings position RND1 as a convergence point for multiple angiogenic signaling pathways, including Notch, VEGF, and PI3K/AKT cascades. While we acknowledge the use of different siRNA sequences for HUVECs and MAECs due to species-specific sequence constraints, we consider this approach both methodologically justified and sufficient to support our functional conclusions. Notably, the consistent phenotypic outcomes observed in both cell types further reinforce that the pro-angiogenic effect of RND1 downregulation is unlikely to reflect cell-type-specific heterogeneity, but rather a conserved regulatory mechanism across endothelial cells.

The cytoprotective effect of RND1 knockdown against hypoxia-induced apoptosis is particularly relevant to the ischemic environment following MI, where endothelial cell survival is critical for preserving microvascular integrity and promoting neovascularization. Enhanced angiogenesis in the infarct zone following RND1 silencing likely contributes to improved cardiac function by facilitating oxygen and nutrient delivery, removing metabolic waste, and potentially supporting cardiomyocyte survival through paracrine mechanisms. The restoration of EF and FS in MI mice with endothelial-specific RND1 knockdown underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting endothelial RND1 to promote cardiac repair (Figure 8).

Translational implications

Our findings position MYZAP as a critical regulator of both human and murine endothelial cells, and we propose that combinatorial strategies promoting both angiogenesis and arteriogenesis may represent a particularly promising avenue for post-MI therapy. Activation of endothelial MYZAP to augment neovascularization thus holds tangible translational potential for patients with MI. However, the cell-type-specific effects of MYZAP and its contextual roles in other disease settings necessitate further exploration. Future investigations should also assess whether RND1 inhibition yields functional, durable vessels rather than aberrant vascular structures, and explore the feasibility of small molecule or RNA-based therapeutics for endothelial-restricted RND1 targeting. Given the emerging recognition of MYZAP and RND1 as a signaling hub integrating Notch, VEGF, and NFAT pathways in endothelial cells, therapeutic modulation of this axis may open avenues for enhancing post-ischemic tissue repair in cardiovascular disease.

From a translational standpoint, RND1 emerges as a compelling therapeutic target for ischemic heart disease. Unlike pro-angiogenic growth factors such as VEGF, which require exogenous delivery and are often associated with the formation of unstable or leaky vessels, pharmacological modulation of a negative regulator such as RND1 offers the potential to achieve more physiologically controlled and sustained angiogenesis. The endothelial-specific AAV9-mediated gene silencing strategy employed in our study provides evidence that targeted genetic manipulation within the vascular endothelium is both feasible and clinically adaptable.

Nevertheless, several critical issues must be addressed prior to clinical translation. First, the optimal timing of RND1 inhibition following MI remains undefined. It is unclear whether acute knockdown immediately after ischemia or delayed intervention during the remodeling phase would confer superior functional benefits. Second, the long-term consequences of sustained RND1 suppression on vascular homeostasis and potential off-target effects warrant rigorous evaluation, given the reported role of RND1 in maintaining endothelial barrier integrity. Equally important is the elucidation of the precise molecular machinery through which RND1 inhibits VEGFR2 and PI3K/AKT signaling. This mechanism may involve interactions with Rho GTPase regulatory proteins or modulation of receptor trafficking dynamics.

Although VEGF remains a potent angiogenic factor, its clinical utility is limited by off-target effects, including vessel leakage and hemangioma. In contrast, MYZAP targets the VEGF pathway upstream via RND1 suppression, potentially achieving effective angiogenesis with a more favorable safety profile. Whereas Notch signaling modulates angiogenesis indirectly through lateral inhibition and tip-stalk cell selection, MYZAP acts directly on endothelial cells to enhance proliferation, migration, and tube formation via the PI3K/AKT/HIF1α axis. This difference underscores a unique pro-angiogenic mechanism that bypasses the conventional sprouting regulatory circuitry. Unlike YAP/TAZ, which respond to mechanical cues and regulate transcriptional programs during angiogenesis, MYZAP is a junction-associated protein that translocates to the nucleus to promote RND1 ubiquitination and degradation. This reveals a post-translational regulatory layer not previously described in endothelial angiogenesis.

Limitations of the study

Although we have demonstrated a regulatory relationship between MYZAP and RND1, the exact binding mechanism still needs to be identified through further experiments. In addition, how MYZAP down-regulated RND1 to promote angiogenesis pathways requires more in-depth investigation in future studies. AAV-mediated endothelial-specific delivery remains challenging, as systemic administration may result in off-target transduction in other organs, and achieving sufficient EC coverage in the ischemic heart requires optimization of injection routes and dosages. Pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against AAV serotypes in the human population may limit therapeutic efficacy, and long-term expression of exogenous MYZAP raises potential concerns regarding oncogenic or off-target effects. The long-term stability of EC-specific MYZAP expression and whether chronic RND1 suppression might lead to adverse vascular remodeling or other unintended consequences remain unknown. Most preclinical data are derived from young, healthy animals; translation to aged patients with comorbidities requires further validation. The nuclear localization of MYZAP in HUVECs under normoxic conditions is intriguing and somewhat unexpected, given its well-established role as a junctional protein. This may be attributable to hypoxic stress or potential post-translational modifications. Furthermore, we cannot exclude the possibility that MYZAP exerts nuclear functions, such as transcriptional regulation, although this hypothesis requires further investigation beyond the scope of the current study

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (10-week-old, weighing 20–25 g) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature (24 ± 1°C), humidity (55 ± 5%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Harbin Medical University (Ethics Approval No. IRB3045723).

Cells

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, BNCC347734, purchased from BeNa Culture Collection) and mouse aortic endothelial cells (MAECs, PRI-MO-00018, purchased from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology) were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) containing 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B, and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement at 37°C with 5% CO2. All cell lines were provided with the identification report of biomarker fluorescence labeling method by the supplier, and the cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were routinely passaged at 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA treatment (3–5 min, 37°C). For hypoxic experiments, confluent cells were washed with PBS and incubated in D-Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution under controlled hypoxic conditions (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 24 h in a modular hypoxia chamber.

Method details

Establishment of myocardial infarction model

Adult male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with avertin (2,2,2-tribromoethanol, 0.2 g/kg, i.p.). Following anesthesia induction, surgical preparation included chest hair removal and supine positioning on a temperature-controlled platform with continuous ECG monitoring. After oral endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation initiation, a sterile left thoracotomy was performed at the 3rd-4th intercostal space to expose the heart. The LAD was permanently ligated 1–2 mm distal to the left auricle using 7–0 prolene suture, with successful occlusion verified by immediate ST-segment elevation. The thoracic cavity was closed in layers, and ventilation continued until spontaneous respiration resumed. Sham controls received identical procedures without LAD ligation.

Echocardiographic assessment

C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of avertin (2,2,2-tribromoethanol, 0.2 g/kg). Ultrasound gel was applied to the anterior thoracic region, and a high-frequency transducer was adjusted to acquire the left ventricular short-axis view. Cardiac functional parameters, including ejection fraction (EF%), fractional shortening (FS%), and other hemodynamic indices, were quantified using echocardiography analysis software.

Cardiovascular microfil perfusion assay

Anesthetized C57BL/6 mice received heparin (0.1 mL, Sanofi) before cardiac excision. Hearts were PBS-rinsed, then perfusion-fixed with 4% PFA (100 mmHg). Microfil MV-122 (Flow Tech) was infused retrograde (80–100 mmHg) through the aortic root. After polymerization (4°C, 15 min) and PBS stabilization, vascular networks were imaged using a Zeiss SteREO microscope (5–20×) with 3D reconstruction via ZEN software.36

Construction of endothelial-specific MYZAP-overexpressing, RND1-overexpressing and AAV-9-mTie-sh-RND1 vectors

The full-length MYZAP CDS with EGFP reporter was cloned into an AAV-9 backbone plasmid under the endothelial-specific ICAM-2 promoter. AAV-9 vectors (1.9 × 1011 GC/mL) were packaged, sequenced-verified, and stored at −80°C. The full-length sh-RND1 sequence with an EGFP reporter was cloned into an AAV-9 backbone plasmid under the control of the endothelial-specific mTie promoter. AAV-9 vectors were packaged and verified by sequencing (1.0 × 1013 GC/mL), and stored at −80°C. For in vivo delivery, thawed virus was diluted in DMEM and administered via tail vein injection (100 μL/mouse). RND1 was successfully cloned into the pLV3-CMV-3×FLAG-CopGFP-Puro vector, lentivirus (5 × 106 GC/mL) were packaged, the CMV promoter was used for RND1 overexpression lentivirus. Four weeks after viral delivery, MI was induced in mice using the surgical ligation protocol described above.

Endothelial transfection

For MYZAP knockdown, cells at 70–80% confluency were transfected with 50 μM MYZAP-specific siRNA or non-targeting scrambled siRNA (negative control, NC group) using Lipofectamine 2000 following the manufacturer’s instructions. For RND1 knockdown, parallel experiments were performed with 50 μM RND1-specific siRNA using X-tremeGENE siRNA Transfection Reagent. Transfection complexes were incubated with cells for 6–8 h in serum-free medium. After transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete endothelial cell medium supplemented with 5% FBS. Cells were cultured for 48 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2).

Matrigel-based tube formation assay

Thaw the Matrigel matrix (stored at −20°C) at 4°C. The matrigel was diluted with endothelial cell medium, which was pipetted 1 into pre-chilled confocal dishes. The matrix was polymerized at 37°C for 30 min in a CO2 incubator. HUVECs were seeded onto the polymerized Matrigel at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. HUVECs were incubated for 6–12 h to allow tube formation. The images were captured using an inverted phase-contrast microscope at 10× magnification. The images were analyzed with ImageJ software using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin. The tube structures were defined as closed luminal networks.

Transwell cell migration and invasion assay

100 μL of diluted Matrigel was applied to the upper surface of the Transwell membrane, which was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow polymerization. HUVECs or MAECs were harvested and resuspended in serum-free medium to a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL. The coated transwell chamber was placed into a 24-well plate. 200 μL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. 800 μL of complete medium was filled into the lower chamber. The cells were incubated for 36 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. 0.05% crystal violet was stained for 5 min. The images of migrated/invaded cells were captured using a microscope (10× magnification).

LY294002 treatment

For in vitro studies, endothelial cells were treated with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (HY-10108, 10 μg/mL) for 40 min prior to cell harvest. For in vivo studies, LY294002 was administered as a single intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 30 mg/kg, given 45 min before myocardial infarction (MI) induction.

Wound healing test

HUVECs and MAECs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well, and cultured for 24 h until the cell confluence reached approximately 80%. A sterile 10 μL pipette tip was used to make a longitudinal scratch perpendicular to the bottom of each well, using a ruler as a guide. After the scratch, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells, fresh culture medium was added, and the transfection reagent was introduced. Cells were then incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Images were captured under a 10× microscope at 0 and 24 h. Wound areas were measured using ImageJ software. The wound closure rate was calculated as the percentage of the difference between the wound areas at 24 h and 0 h relative to the initial area.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the control group and calculated as follows: Cell viability (%) = (OD_treatment/OD_control) × 100.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum VEGF-A levels were quantified by sandwich ELISA. Mice were bled via the retro-orbital sinus, and whole blood was allowed to clot at 4°C 2h. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until use. All samples were thawed only once to avoid freeze thaw cycles. VEGF-A concentrations were measured using a Mouse VEGF-A ELISA Kit (Elabscience Biotechnology; detection range 31.25–2000 pg/mL, sensitivity 18.75 pg/mL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of serum was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 90 min. Plates were washed, followed by incubation with biotinylated detection antibody at 37°C for 60 min. After three washes, streptavidin-HRP was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Following five washes, TMB substrate was added for color development in the dark at 37°C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of stop solution per well, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated from a four-parameter logistic standard curve.

Detection of reactive oxygen species

Intracellular ROS levels in myocardial tissues were evaluated using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. Fresh frozen sections of mouse left ventricles were prepared and rinsed with PBS to remove OCT residue from the tissue edges. Sections were then incubated with DHE working solution (DHE:PBS = 1:1000) at 37°C in the dark for 20 min, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. Excess dye was removed by PBS washing, and fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA extractions from mouse ventricular tissues and cells were performed using Trizol® Reagent (15596–026; Invitrogen, Gaithersburg, MD) and measured on a NanoDrop-2000 spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription based on the reverse-transcription kit instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed by SYBR Green Master (Roche, Switzerland). The primer sequences for MYZAP, RND1 were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) as shown in key resources table. GAPDH was selected as an internal control. The comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method (2-ΔΔCt) was applied to calculate the relative expressions of mRNAs. The Ct value of each sample was normalized to GAPDH, which was recognized as an internal reference. Then results were normalized against the internal control using the ΔΔ Ct method.

Thermal stability assay

Cells were harvested and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. After removing residual PBS, approximately 800 μL of PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail was added to each dish, and cells were scraped and collected. Cell suspensions were aliquoted into tubes containing 100 μL each. Nine tubes were labeled corresponding to the target temperatures ranging from 40°C to 70°C. Samples were heated in a dry bath heater at their designated temperatures for 5 min. Immediately after heating, tubes were transferred to room temperature and allowed to stand for 5 min, then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. To ensure uniform thermal equilibration across samples, cells were subjected to two freeze–thaw cycles by alternating between liquid nitrogen and a dry bath set at 25°C. Tubes were briefly vortexed following each thaw step. After the final thaw, cell lysates were kept on ice at 4°C. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants containing soluble protein fractions were carefully transferred to fresh tubes and maintained on ice. For SDS-PAGE sample preparation, 40 μL of clarified lysate was mixed with 20 μL of loading buffer in 0.2 mL PCR tubes. Samples were briefly vortexed, centrifuged, heated at 95°C for 10 min, vortexed again, and briefly spun down prior to electrophoresis.

Western blot

Proteins purified from cells or cardiac tissue were assayed for protein concentration using the BCA protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime). Protein samples were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (7.5%–10% polyacrylamide gels) and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The primary antibodies used included: anti-MYZAP antibody (1:300, PA5-21116, Thermo, USA), anti-RND1 antibody (1:1000, PA5-89374, Invitrogen, USA), anti-PI3K p110α antibody (1:3000, 82796-4-RR, Proteintech, USA), anti-VEGFA antibody (1:3000, 81323-2-RR, Proteintech, USA), anti-VEGFR2 antibody (1:1000, 26415-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), anti-phospho-AKT antibody (1:2000, 66444-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), and anti-AKT antibody (1:2000, 81323-2-RR, Proteintech, USA), and anti-TRIM21 antibody (1:2000, 91462S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). GAPDH (1:2000, TA802519, Origene), β-tubulin (1:20000, 66240-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), and β-actin (1:4000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) were used as internal controls for protein input. β-Actin was preferentially chosen for the majority of experiments due to its stable and consistent expression across our experimental conditions. BSA was dissolved in PBS to prepare a 5% BSA solution for antibody dilution. Western blot bands were imaged using the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LICOR Biosciences). The band intensity (area × OD) was measured in each group with Odyssey v1.2 software. The protein expression level was expressed as the gray value of the target protein band/GAPDH, β-actin or β-tubulin and further normalized to the average value of the control group.

Co-immunoprecipitations (Co-IP)

A total of 1000 μg protein from the primary cultured cardiomyocytes was cleaved by Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer and incubated with 1 μg primary antibody at 4°C overnight. After that, 35 μL protein A/G PLUS agarose beads (MCE) were mixed into the compound which continued to shake overnight. The beads were washed with ice-cold PBST buffer for 3 times after centrifugation. Next, 25 μL 1 × SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer was added to the precipitation, and after that the samples were boiled for 10 min and centrifuged. Finally, the supernatant was taken for Western blot analysis. Antibodies for co-immunoprecipitation were MYZAP (PA5-21116, Progen, Germany) and RND1 (PA5-89374, Invitrogen, USA), TRIM21 (91462S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA).

Co-IP protein mass spectrum

Anti-MYZAP (Invitrogen #P45-21116) and control IgG (Cell Signaling Technology #2729) antibodies were coupled to Protein A/G magnetic beads overnight at 4°C. Myocardial lysates in IP buffer were incubated with the beads overnight at 4°C. After TBS washing, bound proteins were eluted, reduced with 5 mM DTT (56°C, 30 min), alkylated with 11 mM IAA (dark, 15 min), and sequentially digested with trypsin at 1:50 and 1:100 (enzyme:protein) ratios. Desalted peptides were separated by EASY-nLC1200 UPLC and analyzed via Orbitrap Exploris 480 MS in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cell samples or tissue samples were rinsed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After rinsing with PBS, the samples were incubated with 0.5% Triton × −100 for 30 min at room temperature and then blocked with 10% normal goat serum at 37°C for 1 h. Next, α-actinin (1:200, A7811, Sigma) and MYZAP (1:200, PA5-21116, Thermo), anti-CD31 (1:100); Mouse anti-vWF (1:50); anti-IB4 (1:200) antibodies were added to each slide and incubated at 4°C overnight. After washing with PBS, the fluorescent secondary antibodies [Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, Mouse: A11029, Rabbit: A11034, Thermo) or Alexa Fluor 594 antibody (1:200, Mouse: A11032, Rabbit: A11037, Thermo)] were applied for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:3, DAPI, C0065, Solarbio). Immunofluorescence was analyzed under a confocal fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Quantification of the fluorescence was performed and processed with ImageJ to obtain Mean value (Mean value = Integrated Density/Area), and subsequently normalized with the average value of the control group.37

Masson staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene for 20 min, rehydrated through graded ethanol to water, and stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 8 min. After differentiation in acidic ethanol for 15 s and rinsing, sections were blued for 5 min and washed. Sections were then stained with Ponceau-fuchsin for 5 min, rinsed with weak acid, treated with phosphomolybdic acid for 1 min, rinsed, stained with aniline blue for 2 min, and rinsed again. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5–10 min. Excess stain was rinsed off with distilled water, and sections were differentiated in 1% acid alcoho, followed by immersion in distilled water to terminate differentiation. The sections were then counterstained with eosin for 1–3 min and rinsed with distilled water. Dehydration was carried out through a graded ethanol series: 85% ethanol, 95% ethanol, and 100% ethanol. Clearing was performed in xylene. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Establishment of a murine left hindlimb ischemia model

8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice received tail vein injection of endothelial-specific AAV9-MYZAP. Twenty-eight days post-injection, surgical induction of left hindlimb ischemia was performed under Avertin anesthesia with continuous body temperature maintenance at 37°C using a heating pad. Following hair removal, a 1-cm longitudinal incision exposed the femoral vasculature. The femoral artery was doubly ligated proximally and distally using 6–0 silk sutures, followed by complete excision of the intervening arterial segment. The incision was closed with absorbable sutures. Hindlimb perfusion was quantitatively assessed by laser Doppler imaging (PIMSoft v1.5, Perimed) at baseline (day 0) and postoperative days 3, 5, 7, and 14. Perfusion recovery was expressed as the ischemic-to-nonischemic limb blood flow ratio (ischemic/nonischemic flow × 100%).38

Micro-CT

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of Avertin and administered heparin. The heart was then rapidly excised and briefly rinsed in PBS. Subsequently, the heart tissue was fixed by transaortic perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, a Microfil solution-prepared by mixing Microfil MV-122 with its diluent buffer at a 1:1.25 ratio-was perfused through the vasculature. The perfusion process was maintained at 37°C to ensure proper viscosity and distribution of the contrast agent. After perfusion, the samples were placed on the sample stage of a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanner for imaging. Scanning parameters included a tube voltage of 50–70 kV, tube current of 100–200 μA, and a spatial resolution of 9–15 μm, which was optimized to clearly resolve fine structures such as ventricular walls, papillary muscles, and major coronary artery branches. Post-scanning, device-specific image reconstruction software was used to correct artifacts and generate three-dimensional tomographic images.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using SPSS and GraphPad Prism 10.0. Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, with Dunnett test or Tukey’s test for pairwise comparisons. All of the statistical details of experiments can be found in Figure legend. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
