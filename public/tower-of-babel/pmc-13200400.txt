Standalone commercial exergame training to improve balance in older adults in care facilities: a systematic review and meta-analysis of recent 10-year randomized controlled trials
Authors: Wilson Kin Chung Leung, Connie Yim Ching Yau, Bobo Ching Lam Chan, Yvonne Yuet Ying Wong, Janice Ngar Lam Chow, Simon Ching Lam, Lorna Kwai Ping Suen, Wendy Moyle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired balance is among the leading causes of injury, hospitalization, and death in older adults. Previous studies have shown that exergaming is a viable and well-accepted strategy for promoting optimal functional levels in older adults. Hence, this systematic review aimed to examine the effects of commercially available exergame interventions on balance performance among institutionalized older adults.MethodsThis study was a systematic review of recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in care facility settings published between 2016 − 2025. Four electronic databases (CENTRAL, PubMed, CINAHL, and SPORTDiscus) were searched. RCTs examining the effects of commercial exergame systems on balance performance among care facility residents aged 60 years and older were included. Random-effects models were used for meta-analyses. Risk of bias (RoB) and certainty of evidence (CoE) assessments were conducted using the Cochrane RoB2 and GRADE tools, respectively.ResultsA total of 14 RCTs (n = 724; age: 66.5─86.8 years) were included. Overall, our pooled data showed that exergame interventions significantly improved balance performance among older adults in care facilities [Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) (11 RCTs; n = 588): standardized mean difference (SMD)=-0.87 s, 95%CI -1.47 to -0.27 s, P = 0.004, I2 = 91%; Berg Balance Scale (BBS) (6 RCTs; n = 354): weighted mean difference (WMD) = 2.88, 95%CI 0.36 to 5.41, P = 0.03, I2 = 93%; Tinetti test (2 RCTs; n = 56): WMD = 4.58, 95%CI 2.64 to 6.51, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%]. By excluding studies having high RoB, our sensitivity analyses with lower heterogeneity consistently showed that exergame interventions improved TUG test time (6 RCTs; n = 280; SMD=-0.53 s, 95%CI -0.99 to -0.07 s, P = 0.03, I2 = 70%) and BBS test scores (5 RCTs; n = 228; WMD = 4.20, 95%CI 1.25 to 7.16, P = 0.005, I2 = 47%). Compared with usual care, exergame training also induced significant improvements in balance [TUG (6 RCTs; n = 194): SMD=-0.80 s, 95%CI -1.37 to -0.23 s, P = 0.006, I2 = 70%; BBS (4 RCTs; n = 131): WMD = 5.48, 95%CI 4.46 to 6.50, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%]. However, there was no significant difference when compared with other types of exercise training (e.g., conventional balance training, multicomponent exercises, and routine activities), suggesting that exergame training exhibited comparable effects on balance with conventional physical training. Further subgroup analyses showed that higher training doses (≥ 9 weeks) were more likely to result in improvements in balance control. Additionally, care facility types and exergame systems served as moderators in the subgroup analyses. No study-related adverse events or fall incidents were reported. The RoB ranged from “some concerns” to “high”, whereas the CoE was very low.ConclusionDespite very low CoE, exergaming can be considered as a therapeutic modality adjuvant to already existing options to enhance the comprehensiveness of services provided in care facilities. Introduction

Impaired balance is strongly related to an increased risk of falling and is among the leading causes of injury, hospitalization, and death in older adults. Recent survey data indicate that more than one-quarter of older adults fall at least once a year [1]. Specifically, nearly one-third of older adults living independently in the community and 60% care facility residents fall at least once per year [2–4]. In care facilities, over half of older residents exhibit varying levels of frailty that impairs their balance performance to perform basic daily tasks [5], and low levels of physical activity have been shown to be strongly associated with impaired balance in this population [6, 7]. Given that independent predictors for impaired balance include advancing age, female sex, impaired functional fitness, impaired visual acuity, medication side effects, gait and balance disorders, cognitive impairment, dizziness symptoms, and medical conditions (e.g., diabetes and Parkinson’s disease) [8–15], current strategies for improving balance advocate for a multifaceted approach, encompassing gait and balance assessment, multisensory stimulation training (vestibular, somatosensory, visual), fall arrest strategy training, strength and balance training, medication review, and vitamin D supplementation [8, 11, 16, 17].

A 2019 Cochrane systematic review reported that exercise, particularly multiple types, effectively ameliorated the risk of falling by 23% to 34% and the incidence of injurious falls by 27% to 39% in older adults [18]. Adherence rates to the exercise interventions were overall satisfactory yet varied widely (25% to 100%) [18, 19]. Low compliance was associated with older participants finding traditional exercise training not enjoyable [20, 21]. Notably, adherence was suggested to have significant moderating effects on the effectiveness of program treatment [20]. Systematic reviews have reported that exercise adherence to technology-enabled programs (> 90%) was much higher than conventional exercise programs (50% to 80%) in the geriatric population [22–24]. This implies that a technology-based approach may be appealing to older adults, primarily virtual experience-based devices, which can provide instant audio/visual feedback [25].

Exergaming (active, gamified video-based exercises) is a fun, engaging, and interactive form of exercising, where the real-time, augmented environment provokes motivation and enjoyment. A 2022 systematic review suggested that, due to high levels of enjoyment and satisfaction, active video games are viable and well-accepted methods for promoting optimal functional levels in older adults, with adherence rates ranging from 70% to 100% across 22 exergame interventions [25]. Compared with traditional physical training, Liu et al. also demonstrated that virtual reality-based exergame training conferred greater improvement in balance control performance in older adults concerning faster time to complete Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) test by 0.31 s and longer time in single leg stance with eyes open by 7.28 Sect [26]. Chen et al. also revealed that exergame interventions combined with traditional forms of physical training could be a promising strategy to improve balance in older adults, as exemplified by superior improvement in postural control than either exergaming or physical training alone [27]. The underpinning mechanisms for improved balance through exergaming were a progressive increase in task difficulty and repeated practice of meaningful physical movement to train the muscles involved in balance. Consistent findings of using exergame training as a rehabilitative strategy were also intensively observed in special populations, including frail older adults [28], individuals living with cognitive-motor disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease [29, 30], dementia or mild cognitive impairment [31, 32]), and post-stroke survivors [33, 34]. The treatment effectiveness can be attributed to the simultaneous challenge of multiple functional components, including working memory, attention, information processing, balance, strength, and motor coordination, which are closely related to balance control [27].

Recently, Chen et al., Leal et al., and Peng et al., conducted systematic reviews to meta-analytically examine the effects of virtual reality-based exergames on physical (including balance and fall outcomes), psychological (e.g., depression), and cognitive outcomes among older adults residing in care facilities [35–37]. Results demonstrated significant improvement in balance self-efficacy [95% CI (confidence interval) 0.20 to 0.78], mobility (95% CI -0.99 to -0.33), lower limb strength (95% CI 0.22 to 0.84), cognition (95% CI 0.06 to 0.90), the severity of depressive symptoms (95% CI -1.38 to -0.07), and physiological profile approach to falls risk assessment (95% CI 0.01 to 0.76) compared with the control groups receiving conventional exercise, physical therapy, usual care, or no treatment. More importantly, no adverse side effects of exergame training were reported, even in frail older adults [38, 39] or individuals at high risk of falls [40]. However, the systematic reviews considered both commercial exergame systems and self-made exergame consoles and the self-made systems were well designed to suit the study populations [35–37]. In this systematic review, the term “commercial exergame systems” was conceptualized as the exergame systems of which the purchasing information was easily accessible online. Since the self-made exergame systems were only customized to specific needs, the external validity of the findings generated and whether the systems could be readily available for the general population is largely questionable. Additionally, care facilities often have indoor space constraints (especially in Hong Kong), which is only suitable for small-scale, home-based exergame training (such as Wii and Kinect) [41]. Due to limited resources, the low monetary cost of most commercial systems (e.g., US$250 for the Wii versus a customized video game-based balance rehabilitation system that costs US$8,700 [42]) could also be a motivating factor to encourage people to engage in exergame training. Although Cieślik et al. studied the effects of commercially available exergame on balance and functional mobility in older adults [43], the systematic review only focused on community-dwelling older adults, who are generally healthier and more functionally independent than those admitted to care facilities. Moreover, Chen et al. [35] considered mixed populations (e.g., community senior centers and residential living centers) whose dynamic balance performance was not within the risk zone for falling [44]. This might also lead to the underestimated effect sizes of exergame training due to the presence of a ceiling effect regarding balance improvement among healthier, more independent, community-dwelling older adults who are high in their mobility functioning.

This systematic review sought to quantitatively examine the pooled effects of market-ready exergame interventions derived from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on balance performance among older adults residing in care facilities. Specifically, this systematic review aimed at answering the following research questions:

Did current evidence reveal the causal relationship(s) of commercial exergame interventions with balance outcomes among older adults in care facilities?

Did current evidence reveal the causal relationship(s) of commercial exergame interventions with balance outcomes among older adults in care facilities?

If yes, what were the key findings?

If yes, what were the key findings?

The findings generated will ultimately inform a targeted intervention to cater to the needs of older adults that may be unresolved by current recreational activity programs offered in care facility settings.

Methods

Study design

The study protocol was designed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The reporting of the review findings was also in line with the PRISMA 2020 updated guidance and exemplars for reporting systematic reviews [45]. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO registry (CRD42023469693). The electronic databases used were amended according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [46]. Specifically, the Cochrane Handbook suggests searching at least two databases to lower the risk of missing eligible records. The minimum databases to be covered are CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase (if Embase is available to the review author). For the research areas that are specialized, cross-disciplinary, or involve emerging technologies, additional databases should also be included. In this systematic review, we included two additional electronic databases, namely CINAHL (a nursing and allied health literature database) and SPORTDiscus (a bibliographic database for sports and sports medicine research) to ensure breadth of coverage. However, access to Embase was not available to us, so we did not include Embase in the literature search process. Given that a recent bibliometric analysis on the global research trends in exergaming indicated a steep rise in exergaming research since 2015, peaking during 2020 − 2021 [47], this systematic review also focused solely on studies published within the past 10 years (i.e., 2016 − 2025).

Studies conducted with participants recruited from other settings (e.g., community, outpatient clinics, and hospitals) or mixed-settings were excluded. Information sources

The selection of electronic databases for the literature search was primarily based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [46]. The Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), MEDLINE, and Embase (if available) need to be searched. As access to Embase was not available to us, the database was not employed in our literature search. To ensure comprehensive coverage and minmize the risk of missing relevant records, two additional electronic databases, CINAHL and SPORTDiscus, were also searched. Overall, four electronic databases, namely CENTRAL, PubMed (access to the MEDLINE database), CINAHL, and SPORTDiscus, were searched from 01/01/2016 until 06/03/2025.

Search strategy

Preliminary searches were conducted using different combinations of Text Word terms. With the Text Word terms used previously for care facilities [48], the PubMed database search yielded no search outcomes (i.e., “No results were found.”). So, studies conducted in care facilities were manually identified. The text word terms used in the database search were (older OR senior OR elder* OR aging OR ageing) for older people, (“virtual reality” OR exergam* OR “video gam*” OR Kinect OR Wii OR Nintendo) for exergame interventions, and (balance OR fall OR postur*) for balance and fall outcomes. The search queries for every electronic database are summarized in Supplementary File 1.

Selection process

A two-stage approach was adopted for the study selection process by initially screening titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment. If eligibility of the retrieved records could not be verified due to missing information during the initial screening, a full-text assessment would be carried out for the studies, along with other potentially eligible studies. To avoid missing any potentially eligible studies, the reference lists and the lists of included and excluded studies from three recently searched systematic reviews in care facility settings [35–37], and all included articles were also screened for additional potentially eligible studies. Duplicates were removed manually using an Excel spreadsheet. Meanwhile, librarians were involved in extracting articles from which the full-text versions were not publicly available. Both initial screening and full-text assessment were conducted by the first author (Leung WK) and independently verified by four co-authors (Chan BCL, Chow JNL, Wong YYY, and Yau CYC). Disagreements among the five authors were resolved by discussion with a third author (Lam SC).

Data collection and data items

Information, including publication details (authors, publication year, and countries or regions), participant characteristics, levels of care, intervention and control groups, training volume, attrition rates, and main findings, was extracted mainly from the method and result sections of the included studies, and summarized in a calibrated evidence table. Similar to our previous studies [48], the classification of care facilities was based on the levels of care provided. In short, intermediate-level care facilities (e.g., assisted living facilities or daycare centers) are aged care service units that provide a level of care higher than that of community-dwelling individuals but lower than skilled nursing care in nursing homes. Some care facilities (e.g., retirement communities) that provide both intermediate and high-levels of care to cater to a continuum of ageing care needs were considered to have mixed-care levels. The data extraction was completed by the first author (Leung WKC) and independently verified by four co-authors (Chan BCL, Chow JNL, Wong YYY, and Yau CYC). Disagreements among the five authors were resolved by discussion with a third author (Lam SC). All data in the table were also independently checked by all authors.

Study risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was used to assess the RoB across studies. The tool is structured into five domains that cover all types of bias that can affect the results of randomized trials, including bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. As blinding (especially participants and therapists delivering the interventions) was not easily implemented in exercise intervention trials [26, 49, 50], the overall RoB (low risk, some concerns, and high risk) for each included RCT were drawn based on the results of either all bias domains or the results excluding domain 2. The RoB assessment was completed by the first author (Leung WKC) and verified by two independent co-authors (Chan BCL and Yau CYC). Disagreements among the three authors were resolved through discussion with a third author (Lam SC).

Effect measures

For continuous outcomes examined using the same scale or different scales, data were represented as either the weighted mean difference (WMD) or the standardized mean difference (SMD), respectively.

Synthesis methods

Meta-analyses were conducted for balance or fall outcomes that were reported in at least two included studies. For RCTs, a pairwise meta-analysis of post-intervention data was conducted between the exergame and control groups (i.e., mean and standard deviation) using random-effects models, which accounted for possible variations in effect sizes across trials [51]. The Higgins I2 statistics evaluated the degree of heterogeneity across findings. The results of the I2 statistics in the 0–25%, 25–50%, and > 50% categories represented low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential moderators (e.g., types of exergame systems, types of comparison controls, and levels of care) while controlling for statistical heterogeneity. For the meta-analyses identified as having high heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies having high RoB. In the event of missing information, we contacted the study authors twice over two weeks. If the authors did not provide us with the required information, we would address the possible impacts of missing data on our synthesized evidence in the discussion section. According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, numerical data used for meta-analysis were also extracted from figures manually using a ruler if the included studies only presented the findings in graphical formats [52]. Data conversions were conducted using an online calculator (https://meta-converter.com/conversions) when the required data formats for meta-analysis were not available (e.g., converting 95% CI into standard deviation). All meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4) software.

Reporting bias assessment

Funnel plots were constructed to visually examine the publication bias given that at least 10 studies were included in the meta-analyses [45]. The asymmetry of the funnel plots suggests possible publication bias. Publication bias assessment was also performed based on our search of RCT study protocols and trial registry records to determine whether there might be unpublished studies [53]. The assessment for relevancy to our study aims of the searched RCT study protocols was conducted initially on their abstracts and titles, followed by their full texts or complete trial registry records. For selective reporting of results, the results of domain 5 (RoB in selection of the reported result) of Cochrane RoB 2 were also analysed and reported.

Certainty assessment

The Grading of Recommendation Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to examine the certainty of evidence (CoE) of the included studies. Evidence about the clinical endpoints was downgraded one or more levels if (1) the RoB was high (i.e., the majority of included trials have some concerns or high RoB) (2), there was evidence of imprecision [i.e., 95% CI fell within the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) or the effect estimate was derived from only one or two small studies] (3), there was evidence of unexplained inconsistency or high heterogeneity (i.e., I2 > 50%) (4), the directness of evidence was questionable in respect of the participants studied, intervention of interest, measurements of the outcome, and the method of the trial of a candidate intervention (i.e., depending on whether direct comparisons are available or not) (5), publication bias was detected (e.g., visual inspection of funnel plots and identification of unpublished studies through examining relevant RCT study protocols and trial registry records [53]). Based on distribution-based approaches, boundaries of an MCID were set as half of a standard deviation from the mean value in the control arm(s) of the included studies at baseline [54, 55].

For RCTs, the CoE was overall classified as very low, low, moderate, and high. The first author (Leung WK) conducted the CoE assessment, and the results were verified by two independent reviewers (Chan BCL and Yau CYC). Disagreements among the three reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third author (Lam SC).

Results

Study selection

By June 3, 2025, our electronic database search had retrieved a total of 1,909 published records. After removing duplicates, 1,466 records were initially screened for titles and abstracts. After the initial screening of titles and abstracts, 172 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Two additional articles [56, 57] were identified for full-text assessment through a manual search of the reference lists of three relevant systematic reviews in care facility settings [35–37]. The RCT by Lim et al. was excluded because it lacked an appropriate control group compared to the two Wii-Fit Plus exergame interventions [58]. Li et al. ’s study on Nintendo Switch exergames in rural care facility settings (daycare centers and nursing homes) was excluded because the study was retracted due to concerns over irregularities during the peer review process [59]. Finally, 14 studies were included for narrative review [39, 41, 56, 60–70]. We contacted the first author of an included study regarding the issue of raw data availability [68]. However, the author replied that they no longer had the raw dataset.

Therefore, only 13 studies with outcomes reported in at least two included studies and with raw data available were included for meta-analysis. Overall, there were no concerns about multiple reports or double result counting of the included RCTs because they were completely different from each other in terms of trial registration numbers, publication details (e.g., authors’ names and locations), participants’ baseline characteristics (e.g., age, sample sizes, and medical conditions), exergame interventions (e.g., types, length, frequency, duration, intensity, formats, attrition and adherence rates, etc.), and control groups. Risk of bias in studies

Overall, seven studies were rated as having high RoB [56, 61, 64–68], while the other seven studies were rated as having some concerns [39, 41, 60, 62, 63, 69, 70] (Fig. 2a). The major sources of bias were RoB in the selection of the reported result, with six studies rated as having high risk in this domain [56, 61, 64, 66–68] (Fig. 2b). Of note, all 14 studies had some concerns or high risk for the domain “RoB due to deviations from the intended interventions” where blinding of participants and therapists delivering the interventions was considered. Without considering this domain, one study was overall judged to have low RoB instead of some concerns [60]. The details of signaling questions guiding the RoB judgement for each bias domain and overall bias were summarized in Table S2.

Results of syntheses

Overall effects of exergaming on balance and fall outcomes

For the TUG test, 11 RCTs were included for assessing the effects of exergaming [39, 41, 56, 60–62, 64, 65, 67, 69, 70]. Collectively, 288 participants were in the exergaming group, and 300 participants were in the control group. The pooled analysis showed that the exergame interventions reduced the TUG test time by 0.87 Sect. (95%CI -1.47 to -0.27 s, P = 0.004, I2 = 91%) (Fig. 3a). Sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding five studies having high RoB [56, 61, 64, 65, 67]. The analysis consistently showed that exergame interventions reduced the TUG test time by 0.53 Sect. (95%CI -0.99 to -0.07, P = 0.03, I2 = 70%) (Fig. 3b).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions on the timed up and go test performance among older adults in care facilities

For BBS, six RCTs were included for examining the effects of exergaming [41, 60, 63, 67, 69, 70]. Altogether, 176 participants were in the exergaming group, while 178 participants were in the control group. With all exergaming interventions combined, the exergaming group showed a significant increase in the BBS total scores by 2.88 (95%CI 0.36 to 5.41, P = 0.03, I2 = 93%) (Fig. 4a). Sensitivity analysis was carried out by excluding one study having high RoB [67]. Consistently, the analysis showed that exergame interventions improved BBS test scores by 4.20 (95%CI 1.25 to 7.16, P = 0.005, I2 = 47%) (Fig. 4b).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions on the berg balance scale among older adults in care facilities

The pooled analysis also indicated that exergame interventions improved Tinetti test scores by 4.58 (95%CI 2.64 to 6.51, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 5). However, there was no significant difference between the exergame and control groups in the test performances of FRT, FABS, SLS, and FES (Figure S1).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions on the tinetti test among older adults in care facilities

Exergaming effects versus other types of exercise training

For TUG, six RCTs were eligible for pooling (exergame: n = 152; control: n = 150) [39, 41, 62, 64, 67, 70]. The control groups included conventional balance training [64, 70], multicomponent exercises (e.g., resistance, balance, and endurance training) [39, 62, 67], and routine exercises (e.g., jogging and table tennis) [41]. This subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in TUG test time between the exergame and control groups (SMD=-0.31, 95%CI -0.95 to 0.32, P = 0.33, I2 = 86%) (Fig. 6a). Further subgroup analysis, when compared with only conventional balance training, also demonstrated no significant difference (SMD=-0.28, 95%CI -0.79 to 0.22, P = 0.27, I2 = 29%) [64, 70] (Fig. 6b).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions when compared with other types of exercise training on the timed up and go and berg balance scales test performances among older adults in care facilities

For BBS, three RCTs were included for pooling, with 90 participants in the exergaming group and 91 participants in the control group [41, 67, 70]. Overall, the pooled data from the subgroup analysis demonstrated no significant difference in BBS total scores between the exergame and control groups (WMD = 3.00, 95%CI -0.81 to 6.82, P = 0.12, I2 = 95%) (Fig. 6c).

Exergaming effects versus non-exercise interventions

Apart from other types of exercise training, our included studies compared the effects of exergaming with usual care [60, 61, 63–65, 67, 69] and/or sedentary activities (e.g., board and card games) [56, 67]. Overall, the pooled data showed that exergame interventions improved the test performances of both TUG by reducing the test time by 0.80 Sect. (95%CI -1.37 to -0.23, P = 0.006, I2 = 70%) [60, 61, 64, 65, 67, 69] and BBS by increasing the total scores by 5.48 (95%CI 4.46 to 6.50, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) [60, 63, 67, 69] when compared with usual care (Fig. 7a and b). However, there was no significant difference in the TUG test time between the exergame and sedentary control groups (SMD=-5.06, 95%CI -15.37 to 5.25, P = 0.34, I2 = 99%) [56, 67] (Fig. 7c).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions when compared with non-exercise control groups (usual care and sedentary activities) on the timed up and go and berg balance scales test performances among older adults in care facilities

Exergaming effects in high-level versus mixed or intermediate-level care facilities

For TUG, five RCTs in high-level care facility settings (exergame: n = 172; control: n = 172) [41, 60, 64, 67, 70] and three RCTs in mixed/intermediate level care facility settings (exergame: n = 148; control: n = 148) [39, 62, 69] were eligible for pooling. For BBS, four RCTs in care facility settings (exergame: n = 148; control: n = 148) [41, 60, 67, 70] and two RCTs in mixed-level care facility settings (exergame: n = 28; control: n = 30) [63, 69] were included for the pooled analysis. Overall, the pooled effect size estimate showed that there was no significant difference in the TUG test time between the exergame and control groups in both high-level care facility settings (SMD=-0.41, 95%CI -1.03 to 0.21, P = 0.20, I2 = 87%) (Fig. 8a) and mixed or intermediate-level care facility settings (SMD=-0.40, 95%CI -1.04 to 0.24, P = 0.22, I2 = 59%) (Fig. 8b). However, exergame interventions significantly increased the BBS total scores among older adults residing in high-level care facilities by 3.07 (95%CI 0.28 to 5.85, P = 0.03, I2 = 95%) (Fig. 8c). However, there was no significant difference in the BBS total scores between the exergame and control groups (WMD = 1.87, 95%CI -3.22 to 6.96, P = 0.47, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 8d).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions on the timed up and go and berg balance scales test performances among older adults residing in high level care facilities versus mixed/intermediate care facilities

Exergaming effects using Nintendo Wii versus Microsoft Xbox Kinect

For TUG, nine RCTs (exergame: n = 259; control: n = 274) were eligible for pooling [39, 41, 56, 62, 64, 65, 67, 69, 70]. For BBS, five RCTs (exergame: n = 160; control: n = 163) were eligible for the pooled analysis [41, 63, 67, 69, 70]. Consistently, exergame interventions using the Nintendo Wii were shown to improve test performances in both the TUG (SMD=-0.74, 95%CI -1.35 to -0.14, P = 0.02, I2 = 66%) (Fig. 9a) and BBS (WMD = 2.87, 95%CI 1.74 to 4.00, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 9b). However, significant effects of exergaming using Microsoft Xbox Kinect were only observed in the TUG test time (SMD=-1.10, 95%CI -1.99 to -0.21, P = 0.01, I2 = 93%) (Fig. 9c), but not BBS scores (WMD = 2.96, 95%CI -0.58 to 6.51, P = 0.10, I2 = 96%) (Fig. 9d).

Forest plots examining effects of exergame interventions using nintendo wii versus microsoft xbox kinect on the timed up and go and berg balance scales test performances among older adults in care facilities

Exergaming effects at ≤ 8 weeks versus ≥ 9 weeks

For TUG, six RCTs were pooled for the ≤ 8-week interventions (exergame: n = 184; control: n = 185) [41, 60, 65, 67, 69, 70], whereas five RCTs were pooled for the ≥ 9-week interventions (exergame: n = 104; control: n = 115) [39, 56, 61, 62, 64]. Overall, the pooled data showed that significant effects of exergaming were only observed for the ≥ 9-week interventions (SMD=-1.68, 95%CI -3.01 to -0.35, P = 0.01, I2 = 94%) (Fig. 10b), but not ≤ 8-week interventions (SMD=-0.50, 95%CI -1.12 to 0.12, P = 0.11, I2 = 87%) (Fig. 10a).

Safety and adverse effects

Five studies reported that no study-related adverse events (e.g., musculoskeletal pain), fall incidents, or other adverse events occurred during and after exergame interventions [60–62, 64, 68]. Overall, exergame interventions were safe with no adverse side effects reported among older adults living in care facilities of different levels.

Reporting biases

A funnel plot was only constructed for the overall effects of exergaming on the TUG test time because there was only one meta-analysis involving 10 or more RCTs (Figure S2). Asymmetry of the funnel plot was apparently observed, indicating possible publication bias. Besides, we assessed the relevance of our searched RCT study protocols (n = 49) and trial registry records (n = 44) to determine if publication bias on this subject matter was present. The judgement for each individual study protocol and trial registry record was supplemented in Table S3 and Table S4. Primarily based on the asymmetry of the funnel plot, publication bias was overall “strongly suspected” for the subject matter that we studied.

For selective reporting of results, six included RCTs were found to have high RoB [56, 61, 64, 66–68] (Fig. 2 and Table S2). Although the results of the major balance or fall outcomes of the RCTs were fully reported at predetermined data collection timepoints, the results could be analyzed using multiple eligible ways (e.g., group x time interaction effects). Moreover, one RCT employed graphical representation of their findings that greatly hindered the reporting of true values at baseline and postintervention and hence impeded the data included for meta-analysis [67]. Overall, the RoB for the selective reporting domain was judged to be mostly high risk [42.9% (6/14)], followed by low risk [35.7% (5/14)] and some concerns [21.4% (3/14)].

Certainty of evidence

For TUG, BBS, Tinetti, FRT, FABS, SLS, and FES, the CoE was overall judged to be “very low” because it was downgraded by at least three levels for (1) very serious concern about the study quality (i.e., one or more studies had high RoB) (2), serious concern about imprecision (i.e., 95% CI falling within the MCID range, but not the harmful side), and (3) very serious concern about unexplained inconsistency (I2 > 50%), and/or (4) strongly suspected publication bias. Table S5 summarizes the results of different CoE domains and the overall judgement.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the effectiveness of market-ready, virtual reality-based exergame training on balance and falls among older adults in care facility settings. Overall, the pooled results of 13 studies included in the meta-analyses showed that exergame interventions significantly improved the balance performance of care facility residents as measured by the TUG, BBS, and Tinetti. To further investigate the moderating factors that governed the exergaming effects, multiple subgroup analyses in respect of control groups (exercises versus non-exercises), levels of care (high versus mixed or intermediate), exergame systems (Nintendo Wii versus Microsoft Xbox Kinect), and intervention durations (≤ 8 weeks versus ≥ 9 weeks) were also conducted. Since only three studies reported the results for fall-related outcomes (e.g., FES) [39, 41, 67], the effects of commercial exergaming may be underpowered to detect a significant change.

Compared with other types of exercise training (balance, multicomponent, and routine sports), exergame interventions demonstrated comparable effects on balance performance among older adults in care facilities. The findings were concordant with two systematic reviews evaluating the effectiveness of virtual reality-based exergame interventions versus traditional physical therapy (e.g., Tai Chi, conventional balance training, and strength training) on balance performance, functional mobility, and fall prevention among healthy older adults [26, 27]. Their findings consistently showed that exergaming conferred greater improvements on dynamic balance [TUG (-0.19 to -0.31 s)], but mixed findings were reported regarding the exergaming effects on static balance (BBS and SLS). Despite similar exergaming effect directions (i.e., beneficial side), the slight differences in the meta-analytic results between ours and theirs were mainly attributed to the studied participants; our systematic review solely focused on institutionalized older adults, who are generally frailer with high dependency. Therefore, higher exergame training dosages may be required to further improve the balance performance among institutionalized older adults than community-dwelling, healthy, independent-living older adults. This was supported by our findings that exergame interventions at ≥ 9 weeks conferred greater improvement in the TUG test performance than that at ≤ 8 weeks, and the training dosage was higher than that recommended for healthy older adults (8 weeks) [26].

In respect of BBS, exergaming induced greater improvements in balance performance among older adults living in high-level care facilities (e.g., nursing homes) than those attending mixed or intermediate-level care facilities (e.g., daycare centers). This may be due to a lower initial level of conditioning among high-level care facility residents, implicating a lower training dosage that would be sufficient to confer improved physical functioning among frailer older adults in nursing homes. The lower initial state of conditioning among high-level care facility residents, explicitly shown in Table 2, that they generally had worse balance and mobility performances as evidenced by longer TUG test times, lower BBS scores, and shorter FRT distances.

Given high heterogeneity and generally limited number of studies included for the meta-analyses (Fig. 9), we could not have a definite answer for the selection of an appropriate exergame system for improving balance among older adults in care facility settings. However, the potentially beneficial effects of the two commercial exergame systems (Wii and Kinect) might be explained by the fact that the exergames of both systems (e.g., “Sports Pack 1 and 2”, “Your Shape Fitness Evolved, 2012 Aging with Grace”, “Your Shape: Fitness Evolved” and “Kinect adventures!”) were designed based on an important motor-learning principle that emphasizes repetitive, varied practice of meaningful tasks and sensory feedback pertaining to the task, which is considered the best exergame strategy for improving balance in older adults [26].

Adhering to exercise protocols and training is strongly associated with treatment effectiveness [20]. Our findings added new information regarding the adherence rates of 55% to 100% for commercially available exergame training (e.g., Wii and XBox) in care facility settings, whereas nearly 60% of our included studies showed no dropouts in the exergame groups [41, 61–64, 66, 67, 70]. According to the Capability, Opportunity and Motivation psychological model of behavior change, Buyle et al. Revealed a causal relationship between motivational elements and the exergaming effectiveness in ameliorating balance performance in older adults [71]. The motivational elements included auditory and visual feedback, enjoyment (e.g., music), and autonomy over selecting levels of difficulty. Given that participants also treasured social interaction and additional progress feedback provided by health professionals (e.g., physiotherapists) [71], a coach-led, personalized exergame program in a group-based format, along with individualized goal setting, should be guaranteed to address their varied balance capability needs. Recently, Fidan et al. Supported the use of group-based exergame training format, rather than individual training as evidenced by more profound beneficial impacts on balance and functional mobility in older adults [72]. The authors explained that group exergame training could enhance participants’ motivation and adherence, social interaction, and provide a competitive training environment.

In the field of sports physiotherapy, Sherrington et al. mentioned that blinding of people receiving interventions and blinding of therapists/exercise coaches delivering interventions were not easily implemented in most exercise- or sports-related trials [49]. Therefore, in the present study, the overall RoB was drawn based on both the overall results and the results excluding domain 2, which is about whether participants or people delivering the interventions have knowledge about the assigned intervention during the trial. Given no obvious baseline differences between intervention and control groups (i.e., “No” or “Probably no” for the signaling questions 1.3 of the Cochrane RoB 2 tool), the intergroup comparisons at post-intervention in our meta-analysis were believed to be trustworthy.

The present review was a rigorous methodological design, in line with the best reporting guidelines, and based on a pre-specified protocol. However, there were several limitations in this study. First, although our meta-analysis results showed that the effects of exergame interventions on balance performance and fall-related indicators (TUG, BBS, and Tinetti) were significant (P < 0.05), their 95% CIs fell within the MCID ranges. Given the high adherence rates with no adverse effects reported, we herein shed light on its adjunctive roles to readily available interventions for improving balance performance in care facility settings. Second, relevant results across studies were not stratified by sex among participants. Given apparent gender disparity in recreational activity preferences in care facilities [73], an improved understanding of the gender-related activity preferences, motivational determinants, and their associations with exergaming effectiveness can facilitate the design of individualized programs for older men and women having multifaceted needs that may not be met by current activity offerings. Third, some of our meta-analysis had limited power to examine exergaming effects due to a small number of relevant included and raw data unavailable (e.g., post outcomes and SD). This led to a challenge to provide comprehensive subgroup comparisons (e.g., supervised training versus self-practice, group versus individual training, etc.). Fourth, marked heterogeneity, especially in several key meta-analytic models (Figs. 3a and 4a) with I2 of 91–93%, was observed. This might be due to a variety of exergame systems, measurement tools, training volumes, comparison controls, and care facility types across our included studies. In light of this, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies having high RoB (Figs. 3b and 4b). The I2 statistics were considerably lowered (47–70%) and the improvement in both TUG and BSS was maintained, substantiating a potentially beneficial effect of exergaming on balance performance in older adults in care facilities. The lower heterogeneity also suggested that the results of our sensitivity analyses were more reliable than the overall pooled results. Fifth, there was language bias as studies only published in English were included. Given that authors from non-English speaking countries were more likely to submit their negative or null findings to local journals not published in English, inclusion of journal articles merely published in English may lead to an increased risk of publication bias [53]. Sixth, qualified librarians were not involved in generating the search terms for our literature search. Given that the first author (Leung WKC) had rich experience in conceiving and conducting systematic and scoping reviews [48, 74–81], we strongly believe that our search strategy for electronic databases was trustworthy and sensitive enough for comprehensive coverage of the available literature as evidenced by a retrieval of 1,909 records and less than 1% of studies (n = 14) included for review and meta-analysis. Seventh, Embase which contains a large number of rehabilitation, gerontology, and physiotherapy-related RCTs not indexed in PubMed or CENTRAL was not included for our literature search. However, to ensure breadth of coverage and reduce the risk of missing relevant records, we screened the reference lists and the lists of included and excluded studies from three relevant systematic reviews in care facility settings [35–37] and all included studies.

Conclusion

Recently, technology-enabled fitness activities have emerged as very popular globally; wearable technology, mobile exercise apps, and fitness programs for older adults are the top three popular worldwide fitness trends in 2025 [82]. Our results show that commercial exergame training was superior to usual care or sedentary activities, but not superior to conventional exercise among older adults in care facilities. Therefore, commercial exergame training can be considered as a therapeutic modality adjuvant to already existing options to enhance the comprehensiveness of services provided in care facilities because the effects of exergame training have been empirically proven significant in balance training with low cost, high feasibility, and essential motivational elements (e.g., music and sensory feedback). However, our findings should be interpreted cautiously because of a small number of studies included in most of our meta-analyses, very low CoE for all the outcomes, and considerable heterogeneity for some key meta-analytic models. Thus, the present study will eventually inform future interventions with rigorous design to validate our findings and, more importantly, to further realize the potentiality of exergame training (e.g., usability, acceptability, user experience, and co-design) that is crucial for stakeholders’ consideration in terms of therapeutic activity program planning, development, and implementation in care facility settings.
