Experimental evaluation of fly ash and lime stabilization on the geotechnical characteristics of expansive clay for road subbase applications
Authors: Nnadi Ezekiel, Josephine Najjibyo, Deepa Krishnan, Emmanuel Izere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansive clay soils pose significant challenges in road construction due to their high plasticity, swelling potential, and low bearing capacity. This study investigates the combined use of fly ash, lime, and waste tyre granules as sustainable stabilizing agents, addressing a gap in existing research. Laboratory tests were conducted to assess the geotechnical properties of treated and untreated soils. The results revealed that the natural soil was classified as A-6 according to AASHTO, with 86.8% fines, indicating poor suitability for subgrade applications. Stabilization with 5% combined lime and fly ash (by dry soil weight) reduced the plasticity index from 14.37% to 2.01%, representing an 86% reduction in swelling potential. It also improved the maximum dry density to 1.85 kg/m3 at an optimum moisture content of 8.62%. The CBR value increased significantly from 2 to 6% (untreated soil) to 16%, indicating an approximate 167–700% improvement in load-bearing capacity. In contrast, a higher additive content of 10% led to reduced performance, suggesting over-stabilization effects due to excessive binder content. The addition of waste tyre granules (2% to 6% by weight) increased the optimum moisture content and reduced the dry density, indicating their influence on compaction behavior, while also enhancing the ductility and energy absorption capacity of the stabilized soil matrix. Overall, this study demonstrates that a mixture of 5% lime and fly ash, combined with controlled tyre content, significantly improves subgrade performance. Furthermore, the integration of industrial by-products and waste tyre materials shows strong potential for sustainable construction through waste valorization, reduced environmental impact, and alignment with circular economy principles. Introduction

In roadway and pavement engineering, expansive clay soils pose a significant issue because of their weak strength properties, limited bearing capacity, and high shrink-swell potential. When exposed to moisture variations, these soils experience large volume changes that frequently result in pavement cracking, settling, and structural instability of road infrastructure. Road networks are sometimes built on weak subgrade soils without sufficient stabilization measures, which is a widespread concern in many developing regions. According to recent research, ensuring long-lasting and reasonably priced road infrastructure requires enhancing the engineering performance of expansive clay soils1, 2. Soil stabilization has gained popularity in geotechnical engineering as an effective approach to improve the strength, durability, and load-bearing capacity of problematic soils. Due to its effectiveness and sustainability benefits, chemical stabilization—which uses lime and industrial byproducts like fly ash—has attracted more attention than alternative stabilization methods. Fly ash adds additional cementitious chemicals that improve soil strength and stiffness, while lime treatment increases soil workability and reduces plasticity through cation exchange and pozzolanic reactions3, 4. Fly ash and lime together improve the geotechnical environment, like the CBR, unresisted tensile force, and shear strength, which makes stabilized soils more suitable for use as road subgrade. After 2020, more recent studies have shown how well lime-fly ash stabilization works to improve expansive clay soils. For example, Singh et al.,5 found that adding fly ash and lime to clayey subgrade soils for road building significantly increased soil strength and decreased flexibility. Similarly, Pratikso et al.,6 discovered that stabilized soils’ bearing capacity and durability were greatly enhanced by lime–fly ash combinations. The combined stabilization technique improves cohesiveness, compressive strength, and overall subgrade performance, according to studies by Hangge et al.,7 and Bhoi et al.,8. Despite these advances, more experimental analysis is needed to determine the optimal fly ash to lime ratios and the combined effects on expansive clay soils under different curing conditions. This work investigates the experimental effectiveness of lime and fly ash in stabilizing the geotechnical properties of expanding clay soils, specifically for their application in road subbase construction. Expansive clay soils remain a major geotechnical challenge in pavement engineering due to their susceptibility to volumetric changes, leading to cracking, differential settlement, and premature pavement failure. Despite the availability of conventional stabilization techniques, many developing regions, such as Rwanda and Uganda, continue to experience road failures due to inadequate subgrade treatment. Although lime and fly ash stabilization have been extensively studied, limited research has explored their combined interaction with waste tyre granules under controlled laboratory conditions. Furthermore, there is insufficient comparative evaluation with other emerging sustainable stabilizers such as pond ash, brick kiln dust, and RBI Grade 81. Therefore, this study aims to fill this research gap by experimentally evaluating the combined effects of lime, fly ash, and waste tyre granules on expansive clay soils, while positioning the findings within the broader context of sustainable and waste-based soil stabilization techniques.

Literature review

Overview of chemical stabilization of expansive soils

Nwamgbowo et al.9 and Ezemerihe et al.10 demonstrate that optimizing local earth materials and stabilizing weak soils enhance road performance, reduce costs, and lessen environmental impact. Additionally, the use of chemical additives improves the engineering properties of expansive clay soils, significantly increasing strength and load-bearing capacity, as supported by studies from Puppala1, Consoli et al.,3, Tang et al.,2, and Sharma and Sivapullaiah4, which confirm the effectiveness of chemical treatments in reducing swelling potential and improving soil stability.

Lime stabilization of expansive soils

Lime stabilization is one of the most widely adopted techniques for improving expansive soils due to its ability to modify soil plasticity and enhance strength through pozzolanic reactions. Chen et al.,11 and Phummiphan et al.,12 demonstrated that lime treatment significantly increases soil strength and improves pavement performance. Similarly, Avinash et al.,13 reported improvements in soil workability, bearing capacity, and reductions in pavement thickness and construction costs. Earlier studies by Amadi and Okeiyi14 and Latifi et al.,15 confirmed that lime stabilization reduces plasticity, enhances compressibility characteristics, and improves long-term durability. These improvements are largely attributed to cation exchange, flocculation–agglomeration, and cementitious reactions that occur between lime and clay minerals. Consequently, lime remains a reliable and cost-effective stabilizer for subgrade applications, particularly in regions with highly expansive soils.

Fly ash stabilization and pozzolanic materials

Fly ash has been extensively studied as an effective pozzolanic material for soil stabilization due to its silica and alumina content. Kalyani et al.,16 investigated the combined effects of fly ash and lime on geotechnical properties, including specific gravity, particle size distribution, and Atterberg limits. Their results showed that varying proportions (4%, 6%, and 8%) significantly improved soil consistency and strength, particularly with increased curing duration. Supporting these findings, Hangge et al.7 and Lillian17 reported improvements in bearing capacity, shear strength, and California Bearing Ratio (CBR). Khan et al.,18 and Soltani et al.,19 further demonstrated that fly ash stabilization reduces volumetric changes, decreases plasticity, and enhances compressibility behavior. Abbaszadeh et al.,20 also emphasized improvements in durability and mechanical performance. Ahmad et al.,21, along with Laureano and Shehata22, showed that coal-based ash materials significantly enhance compressive strength and reduce shrink–swell potential. These results align with findings from Yadav and Tiwari23 and Basha and Hashim24, which advocate for the use of industrial by-products in sustainable soil stabilization. In addition, combined stabilization approaches have demonstrated enhanced performance. Pratikso et al.,6 reported that mixtures of cement, lime, and fly ash significantly improve CBR and load-bearing capacity while reducing the plasticity index. Abdelbaset et al.,25 also confirmed that fly ash–lime systems are particularly suitable for low-traffic and rural roads. Recent studies have expanded the application of fly ash with other industrial by-products. Gupta et al.26 demonstrated that incorporating brick kiln dust and pond ash improves permeability and durability. Gupta et al.,27 further showed that such approaches reduce lifecycle costs and carbon emissions. Additionally, Gupta et al.,28 and earlier studies29, 30 revealed that pond ash combined with fiber reinforcement enhances long-term pavement performance and resistance to cyclic loading. These findings suggest that fly ash-based systems can be effectively integrated with other materials to improve sustainability and engineering performance31.

Waste Tyre inclusion in soil stabilization

The reuse of waste tyre granules in geotechnical engineering has gained increasing attention due to environmental concerns associated with tyre disposal. Horpibulsuk et al.,32 and Yadav et al.,33 found that the inclusion of tyre particles reduces soil density while enhancing ductility and energy absorption capacity. However, excessive tyre content may reduce soil strength due to weak bonding between rubber particles and soil matrices. Despite these limitations, waste tyre inclusion offers significant benefits in terms of flexibility and resistance to deformation. Notably, there is limited research on the combined use of waste tyre granules with traditional stabilizers such as lime and fly ash, particularly for road subgrade applications. Most existing studies focus on single or binary stabilization methods, leaving a gap in understanding the behavior of composite stabilization systems involving three or more materials.

Research gap and contribution

From the reviewed literature, it is evident that lime and fly ash stabilization techniques are well established and widely applied in subgrade improvement. However, studies focusing on the integration of waste tyre materials with these conventional stabilizers are scarce. Furthermore, comparative sustainability assessments involving multiple industrial by-products are limited. This study addresses these gaps by investigating a triple stabilization system incorporating lime, fly ash, and waste tyre granules. It experimentally determines the optimal proportions required to enhance subgrade strength, durability, and sustainability. The findings are expected to contribute to the development of cost-effective and environmentally sustainable road construction practices.

Methodology and materials

The soil used in this study came from Mbarara, Uganda, and is classified A-6 in adherence to AASHTO. The fly ash used came from Bushienyi and was Class F fly ash, which means it had a lot of silica and alumina in it. The lime used was hydrated lime (Ca(OH)₂) with a lot of calcium in it. Waste tyre granules were made from old tyres that were broken down into pieces that were 2 to 6 mm in size. The study utilised a methodical laboratory approach to examine the geotechnical characteristics of expansive clay soil and to assess the effectiveness of stabilisation through the use of lime, fly ash, and waste tyre granules (Fig. 1). The process started with taking samples of the soil and getting it ready, and then it moved on to sieve analysis to figure out the size of the particles.

Results and discussions

This section presents laboratory test results aimed at assessing soil properties and classification for the specified subgrade, along with discussions of the findings.

Sieve analysis test results

A soil sample of 2000 g was washed and yielded 265 g, revealing 86.8% fine soil content. The fineness modulus (FM) was calculated at 0.242, indicating a clay and silt classification. Uniformity coefficient (CU) and coefficient of curvature (CC) were both determined to be 0.00, indicating poorly graded soil, confirming that Cu values below 4 indicate the need for stabilization. With 86.8% passing the No. 200 sieve, the soil is mainly fine-grained, making further calculation of Cu and Cc unnecessary. Hydrometer analysis is suggested for detailed particle size distribution assessment of fine soils. The grading curve of the soil is presented in Fig. 2.

Atterberg limit test

Figure 3, represents the flow curve for Liquid Limit using Atterberg Limits Test in the laboratory. The soil exhibits an average liquid limit (LL) of 34.18% and a plastic limit (PL) of 19.81% based on the Atterberg limits test. The result in Fig. 2, indicates a plasticity index (PI) of 14.37%, suggesting moderate plasticity. According to AASHTO standards, the soil is categorized as type A-6; 86.8% of it passes through the No. 200 sieve, and its Group Index (GI) of 14 indicates extremely low subgrade quality. As previously stated by Zeinali et al.,34 and Zhang et al.,35, these results emphasized the significance of Atterberg limits in evaluating soil strength and behaviour in geotechnical engineering.

The Atterberg limits test revealed a liquid limit (LL) of 14.0% and a plastic limit (PL) of 11.97%, yielding a plasticity index (PI) of 2.01%, which indicates low plasticity and low swelling potential of the soil. Based on the measured Atterberg limits and particle-size distribution, the soil classification was evaluated according to the AASHTO Soil Classification System. The natural soil was identified as A-6. The stabilized soil showed better consistency after treatment with 5% lime and fly ash. The addition of stabilizing agents reduced moisture susceptibility and improved the plastic behavior of the clay soil, thereby enhancing its load-bearing capacity and suitability for road subgrade applications, as similarly reported by Zhang et al.,35 and Akinwumi et al.,36.

California bearing ratio results

This section depicts the CBR test results for both stabilized and non-stabilized samples.

CBR test of expansive soil before stabilization

Untreated expansive soil exhibits a low California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of 2% to 6%, dry densities of 1.272 to 1.43 g/cm3, and compaction levels ranging from 87% to 98%. These numbers show that the soil is not appropriate for pavement subgrade without stabilization due to its low load-bearing capacity and significant susceptibility to deformation. Figure 7, presents detailed CBR test results, corroborating findings from Zhang et al.,40 and Lillian et al.17, that highlight the need for stabilization in road construction applications for expansive soils.

Variation of California bearing ratio (CBR) with dry density for untreated soil.

CBR test results of soil stabilized with 5% and 10% fly ash and lime

From Fig. 8, the observed increase in CBR values (5%–16%) and dry density (1.703–1.802 kg/m3) with the addition of 5% lime and fly ash is primarily attributed to physicochemical stabilization mechanisms rather than mere densification. Initially, the introduction of lime induces cation exchange and flocculation–agglomeration, where calcium ions (Ca2+) replace weaker exchangeable cations on clay surfaces. This reduces the thickness of the diffuse double layer, leading to particle aggregation and a more stable, friable soil structure. As a result, workability improves and compaction becomes more effective, explaining the increase in dry density. With time and adequate moisture, pozzolanic reactions become dominant. Silica (SiO₂) and alumina (Al₂O₃) present in both the soil and fly ash react with calcium from lime to form cementitious compounds such as calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) and calcium aluminate hydrate (CAH). These compounds act as binding agents that fill voids and create a rigid soil matrix, thereby significantly enhancing load-bearing capacity, which is reflected in the increased CBR values. The trend of increasing CBR with stabilizer content indicates that the combined lime–fly ash system provides both short-term modification (flocculation) and long-term stabilization (pozzolanic bonding). The peak CBR value of 16% suggests an optimal level of reaction products that maximize interparticle bonding without causing excessive brittleness or unreacted material. Beyond this, any marginal gains may depend on curing duration and the availability of reactive silica and alumina. These findings are consistent with Arulrajah et al. who reported that lime–fly ash blends improve subgrade strength through similar mechanisms, particularly in fine-grained soils used in pavement applications.

Variation of California bearing ratio (CBR) with dry density for fly ash–lime stabilized soil.

CBR test on soil stabilized with 10% lime and fly ash

Figure 9 shows the CBR graphic result for unstabilized soil. Stabilization of soil using 10% fly ash and lime resulted in CBR values between 4% and 10%, with dry densities of 1.530 to 1.702 g/cm3, consistent with Agarwa et al. (2024). Although the CBR improvement compared to untreated soil was noted, it was slightly less than that achieved with 5% stabilization due to possible excess additive affecting compaction efficiency. Pham et al.41 indicated that over-stabilization might hinder dry density increases, but overall, lime-fly ash treatments substantially improve soil expansiveness in road construction.

Discussion of findings

The expansive soil examined in this study displayed poor initial engineering properties, characterized by low California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values (2–6%), a high liquid limit (34.18%), and a plastic limit (19.81%). Sieve analysis confirmed that 86.8% of the soil is fine-grained, classifying it as A-6 (silt-clay) with a high Group Index (GI = 14), indicating very poor subgrade quality. Stabilization with 5% lime and fly ash resulted in the formation of beneficial calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) and calcium aluminate hydrate (CAH), improving soil bonding and strength, while decreasing flexibility This aligns with findings by Zeinali et al.,34 and Zhang et al.,35, noting that untreated expansive clays have low bearing capacity and are prone to significant volumetric changes. At this stabilization level, maximum dry density (MDD) reached 1.85 g/cm3, optimum moisture content (OMC) was 8.62%, and CBR rose to 16%, demonstrating significant enhancements in soil properties.

However, increasing the additive level to 10% diminished compaction efficiency, yielding an MDD of 1.709 g/cm3, OMC of 12.5%, and a reduced CBR of 4–10%, aligning with findings from previous studies by Tuan et al.41, Arulrajah et al.,42, and Zhang et al.,40 indicating adverse effects of over-stabilization. The findings indicate that utilizing industrial by-products and waste materials is an effective, environmentally sustainable approach to improving weak subgrade soils. The study highlights earlier research by Kolie et al.,43 and Asha and Lavanya (2025), emphasizing the need for further investigations into effective stabilizer combinations and field tests. It reveals that incorporating waste tyre granules (2–6%) improves optimum moisture content (OMC) and reduces maximum dry density (MDD), thereby enhancing ductility and deformation resistance. Recent studies highlight the importance of waste tyre and solid waste in soil stabilization and pavement engineering. Research by Zhang et al.44 shows that acid-activated geopolymer binders improve the strength and durability of dredged soils. Wang et al.45 found that lime and lignin stabilization boosts the shear strength and deformation resistance of clay soils. Li et al.46 reported that rubber particle and clay mixtures improve dynamic behavior and energy absorption through the skeleton void ratio mechanism. Chen et al.47 observed that rubber and soil mixtures enhance deformation control and protective performance for buried pipes under dynamic loading conditions. However, a high content of tyre granules compromised load-bearing capacity due to inadequate interfacial bonding. Soil treated with 5% additives conforms to AASHTO standards for subgrade layers in low to medium-traffic roads, demonstrating its viability for construction, as supported by Horpibulsuk et al.32 and Yadav et al.33. Furthermore, the incorporation of tyre granules illustrated a sustainable recycling approach in geotechnical applications, supporting the transformation of weak expansive clay into viable subgrade material for road construction, thus achieving the study’s objectives and improving soil performance under compaction.

Conclusion

This study investigated the stabilization of expansive A-6 clay soil using a combination of lime, fly ash, and waste tyre granules. The results confirm that the integrated stabilization approach significantly improves the geotechnical performance of the soil. At an optimal stabilizer content of 5%, the soil exhibited peak performance, achieving a maximum dry density (MDD) of 1.85 g/cm3 and a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of 16%. This represents a substantial improvement in load-bearing capacity, with CBR increasing from 2 to 6% (untreated soil) to 16%, equivalent to an approximate 167–700% enhancement. Additionally, the plasticity index decreased by 86%, from 14.37% to 2.01%, indicating a significant reduction in swelling potential and improved workability. The inclusion of waste tyre granules (2–6%) modified compaction behavior by increasing the optimum moisture content (OMC) and reducing MDD, while enhancing ductility and energy absorption capacity. However, excessive stabilizer content beyond the optimum resulted in reduced strength and compaction efficiency, highlighting the importance of proper mix design. From a sustainability perspective, the reuse of fly ash and waste tyre materials demonstrates strong potential for reducing environmental impact through waste valorization, decreased landfill burden, and lower dependence on conventional construction materials. This approach supports circular economy principles and promotes sustainable road infrastructure development.

Future work directions

Future studies should focus on full-scale field implementation of the proposed stabilization method under real traffic loading and environmental conditions. This will help verify laboratory findings, evaluate constructability, and assess in-situ performance over time. Additional research is needed to investigate the durability of the stabilized soil under cyclic environmental stresses, including repeated wetting–drying and freeze–thaw cycles. Such studies are essential to understand performance degradation, resilience, and service life in varying climatic conditions. Advanced characterization techniques such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) are recommended to examine the formation of cementitious compounds (e.g., CSH and CAH) and to better understand the bonding mechanisms and evolution of soil structure at the microscopic level. Further parametric studies are required to optimize the proportions of lime, fly ash, and waste tyre granules. This includes identifying the optimum tyre content that achieves a balance between strength, ductility, and energy absorption without compromising compaction characteristics. Future work should evaluate the behavior of the stabilized soil under cyclic and dynamic loads (e.g., traffic-induced stresses) to better simulate real pavement conditions and assess fatigue performance.

Comprehensive life-cycle analysis (LCA) should be conducted to quantify the environmental benefits of using industrial by-products and waste tyre materials, including reductions in carbon footprint, energy consumption, and landfill disposal31.
