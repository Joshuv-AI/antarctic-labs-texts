Predictors of morbidity and oncological benefit after liver resection with vascular reconstruction: international collaborative study from expert centres

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver resection with vascular reconstruction is a highly complex procedure for advanced hepatobiliary tumours. This study evaluated the postoperative and oncological outcomes of this patient cohort.MethodsThis retrospective multicentre study included patients undergoing liver resection with vascular reconstruction between 1990 and 2024 across 21 expert centres. Reconstructions involved the inferior vena cava, hepatic veins, portal vein, or hepatic artery. Primary endpoints were postoperative morbidity and 90-day mortality. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and predictors of complications. Multivariable logistic regression, Kaplan–Meier, and Fine–Gray analyses were performed.ResultsIn all, 532 patients were included and classified according to surgical approach as in situ (455, 85.3%), ante situm (48, 9.2%), or ex situ (29, 5.5%). The most frequent indications were colorectal liver metastases (162, 30.4%), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (139, 26.1%), and perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (106, 19.9%). The median operative time was 390 minutes. Clavien-Dindo ≥ IIIa occurred in 217 patients (40.8%), and the 90-day mortality rate was 9.8% (52). Portal vein reconstruction independently predicted post-hepatectomy liver failure, ascites, infectious complications, and major morbidity, whereas inferior vena cava reconstruction showed a more favourable perioperative profile. Hepatic vein reconstruction was associated with greater transfusion requirements and longer operative time. Vascular graft-related complications occurred in 22.3% (58) of reconstructions and were not associated with graft type. After excluding patients with perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (426), ante situm and ex situ procedures were associated with higher rates of Clavien-Dindo ≥ IIIa than in situ procedures (67.3% (33) and 64.3% (18) versus 35.5% (124), respectively; P < 0.001), longer intensive care unit stay (6.5 and 5 versus 3 days, respectively; P < 0.001), and higher mortality (16.3% (8) and 17.9% (5) versus 8.3% (29), respectively; P = 0.060). DFS was superior for hepatocellular carcinoma (P = 0.011), whereas perihilar cholangiocarcinoma showed a higher risk of recurrence (P = 0.022). Five-year OS was highest for sarcoma (68.8%) and hepatocellular carcinoma (42.7%).ConclusionLiver resection with vascular reconstruction is feasible in specialized centres but remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. The in situ approach showed the most favourable perioperative outcomes, although oncological benefit varied according to tumour biology, highlighting the importance of careful patient selection.Keywords: tertiary referral centres, advanced hepatobiliary surgery, surgical outcomes

Introduction

Hepatectomies requiring vascular reconstruction represent one of the greatest challenges in modern hepatobiliary surgery due to their high technical complexity and associated risks. These interventions are indicated in patients with advanced primary liver tumours or liver metastases involving critical vascular structures, offering, in some cases, the only curative-intent therapeutic option1. However, the need to preserve an adequate volume of functional liver parenchyma, along with prolonged ischaemia times and the inherent complexity of vascular reconstruction, entails a high risk of postoperative complications, including liver failure, haemorrhage, infections, and vascular graft thrombosis2. These factors ultimately determine postoperative mortality, which has historically been reported as prohibitively high.

In recent decades, advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management have significantly improved the outcomes of these procedures. Techniques such as ex situ, ante situm, and in situ approaches have been implemented in specialized centres with variable results3–6. Similarly, the integration of advanced strategies such as hepatic perfusion and the use of extracorporeal bypass have been adopted by different centres, with potential benefits in reducing postoperative complications and protecting liver parenchyma during prolonged ischaemia periods7–10. Nevertheless, the implementation of these strategies remains non-standardized, resulting in variability in outcomes and hindering objective comparison between surgical approaches.

Another major challenge in this context is the selection of candidates for these procedures. Currently, there are no universally accepted criteria to accurately determine which patients would benefit most from extreme liver surgery with vascular reconstruction. This has led to decision-making being largely based on the experience of the surgical team and the resources available at each centre. The lack of studies with significant case series and homogeneous data on long-term oncological and functional outcomes hamper the optimization of surgical strategies and the development of clear guidelines for patient selection.

In this context, there is a growing need for a multicentre analysis to consolidate knowledge in this field of liver surgery and to establish clinical guidelines based on robust evidence. Standardization of surgical techniques and perioperative strategies could help reduce complications and improve oncological outcomes, as well as strengthen the capacity of surgical teams to perform these procedures in high-complexity settings.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective international multicentre study that included patients from 21 hospitals worldwide who underwent hepatectomy with vascular reconstruction due to primary or secondary hepatobiliary tumours between January 1990 and June 2024. The institutional review board at the Clinic and University Virgen de la Arrixaca Hospital (Murcia, Spain), which served as the central ethics committee for the study, approved the study (Internal Protocol Code: 2025-1-1 HCUVA).

Adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with tumour infiltration of major vascular structures requiring vascular resection and subsequent reconstruction to achieve complete tumour removal and restore adequate hepatic inflow or outflow were included. Eligible procedures comprised hepatectomy with vascular reconstruction involving the inferior vena cava (IVC), right, middle, or left hepatic veins (HVs), the hepatocaval confluence, the portal vein (PV), or the hepatic artery (HA). The final surgical approach (ante situm, ex situ, or in situ) was determined according to intraoperative findings and the preference and expertise of the surgical team. A minimum postoperative follow-up of 6 months was also required. Patients with end-stage liver disease or pre-existing severe liver failure were excluded, as were those who underwent surgery with purely palliative intent.

Tumour data were collected across participating centres using multiple sources for the study period. For earlier patients, data were retrieved from institutional surgical registries, prospectively maintained hepatobiliary databases, operative reports, discharge summaries, and archived medical records that were subsequently reviewed and digitized. For more recent patients, comprehensive electronic medical records were used. Only variables that could be reliably and consistently assessed across centres and time periods were included in the analysis, and patients with insufficient documentation to accurately evaluate key variables or outcomes were excluded.

Endpoints

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing liver resection with vascular reconstruction for primary or secondary hepatobiliary tumours. Secondary aims included analysis of complication rates across different surgical approaches, the impact of vascular graft types and reconstruction sites, comparisons between perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (PHC) and other tumour entities, the identification of predictors of morbidity and mortality, and the evaluation of oncological outcomes at 1, 3, and 5 years.

Study outcomes

The variables assessed included demographic data, co-morbidities, tumour characteristics, vascular involvement, preoperative treatments, surgical details, postoperative complications, and short- and long-term clinical outcomes. Demographic variables included age, sex, body weight and height, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists grade, the Charlson co-morbidity index, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status. Co-morbidities evaluated were diabetes, severe cardiac disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease (stage ≥ 3). Tumour-related variables included tumour type, location, the size of the largest lesion, and the number of tumours. Vascular infiltration was assessed by identifying the structures involved, including the IVC, HV, PV, and HA.

Preoperative treatments included chemotherapy (number of cycles), and locoregional therapies such as transarterial chemoembolization or radioembolization. Surgical variables included the approach (in situ, ante situm, ex situ), the use of hepatic perfusion and the type of preservation solution, the type of hepatectomy, the method of vascular occlusion (total, partial, or Pringle manoeuvre), the use of extracorporeal bypass, and the vascular reconstruction strategy, including graft type. Intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirements, and operative time were recorded. Liver resections were classified according to the Brisbane 2000 Terminology of the International Hepato-Pancreato-Biliary Association (IHPBA)11.

Postoperative morbidity was assessed up to 90 days after surgery to capture clinically relevant complications beyond the index hospitalization. Complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification within 90 days and included bile leak, postoperative liver failure as defined by the International Study Group of Liver Surgery12, ascites, bleeding, and pulmonary, infectious, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and late biliary complications. The length of hospital stay (LOS) and 90-day readmission rates were recorded when reliably available. Follow-up data were obtained from institutional surgical databases, electronic medical records, and structured outpatient follow-up at participating tertiary referral centres, including patients referred from other institutions. Prophylactic and postoperative anticoagulation was also analysed. Oncological outcomes were evaluated through overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), with time-to-recurrence analyses performed only in patients with malignant disease and stratified by primary tumour type; patients with non-malignant indications were excluded from oncological analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the median with interquartile range (i.q.r.), and categorical variables are summarized as absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test; however, Fisher’s exact test was used when expected frequencies were too low to meet the assumptions of the χ2 test.

Simple logistic regression analyses were conducted, and results are reported as log-odds coefficients along with their corresponding P values. For multivariable analysis, logistic regression models were constructed using a backward stepwise selection approach based on the Akaike information criterion. Variables that did not improve model fit were sequentially excluded, yielding a parsimonious final model. Statistical significance was defined as a P < 0.050.

The Fine–Gray model was used for survival analysis in the presence of competing risks, because mortality from unrelated causes could prevent the observation of the event of interest. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were generated to illustrate time-to-event distributions. Survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were calculated for both OS and DFS. Differences in survival were examined according to tumour type, and associations were further assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression.

Results

Overall cohort

Data for the 532 patients, classified according to the surgical technique used (in situ, 85.3% (454 patients); ante situm, 9.2% (49); ex situ, 5.5% (29)) are presented in Table S1. The median age of the overall cohort was 63 years and 50.4% (268) were female. The most common indications were colorectal liver metastases (CRLM; 30.3%, 161 patients), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA; 26.5%, 141), PHC (19.9%, 106), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; 8.6%, 46). The most frequently performed surgical procedures were extended right hepatectomy (24.8%, 132), right hepatectomy (23.9%, 127), and extended left hepatectomy (15.4%, 82). The median tumour size was 65 (i.q.r. 35–90) mm, with bilobar involvement in 38.7% (199) of patients. Liver hypertrophy techniques were required in 20.3% (108) of patients.

The median vascular occlusion time was 85 minutes (40-110 min), and 23.9% of patients required extracorporeal bypass. Intraoperative blood transfusion was needed in 50.7% of patients, with a median operative time of 390 (range 300–493) min. Major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa) occurred in 40.6% (n=216) of patients, and the overall 90-day mortality rate was 9.6% (52). The median LOS was 13 days, and the 90-day readmission rate was 17.2% (88).

Infiltrated vascular structure

The most frequently tumour-involved vascular structure was the retrohepatic IVC, followed by the HV, PV, and HA. Figure 1 shows the different combinations of tumour-involved vascular structures. In the multivariate analysis, IVC repair remained associated with a lower incidence of bile leakage, postoperative bleeding, pulmonary complications, and late biliary complications, although it was also linked to a greater need for intraoperative transfusion. HV reconstruction was independently associated with higher transfusion requirements, longer operative time, prolonged LOS and intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and a higher 90-day readmission rate. Conversely, PV reconstruction was significantly associated with higher rates of post-hepatectomy liver failure, ascites, postoperative infection, Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa complications, and prolonged operative time. HA reconstruction was associated with an increased risk of postoperative ascites, cardiovascular complications, and prolonged surgical time (Table 1).

PHC versus primary/secondary tumours

When comparing the 106 patients with PHC to the 426 patients undergoing surgery for other indications (Table 2), significant differences were identified in many clinical and surgical variables. Patients with PHC were older (median age 67.5 versus 62 years; P = 0.001) and had a higher prevalence of severe heart disease (34% (36) versus 3.8% (16); P < 0.001), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (10.4% (11) versus 1.9% (8); P < 0.001), and chronic kidney disease (13.2% (14) versus 1.9% (8); P < 0.001) than patients undergoing surgery for other indications. The PHC group underwent lymphadenectomy far more frequently (95.3% (101) versus 33.3% (140); P < 0.001) but required the use of extracorporeal bypass considerably less often (1.9% (2) versus 29.3% (125); P < 0.001). In addition, bile leak (41.5% (44 ) versus 15% (64); P < 0.001) and postoperative ascites (33% (35) versus 22.1% (94); P = 0.026) were more common in the PHC group. However, no significant differences were observed between the PHC and other surgical indications groups in terms of major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa; 38.7% (41) versus 41.1% (175), respectively; P = 0.734), mortality (8.5% (9) versus 9.9% (42), respectively; P = 0.807), LOS (13.5 versus 13 days, respectively; P = 0.518), or 90-day readmission rates (18.2% versus 17%, respectively; P = 0.894; Table 2).

Table 3 includes data only for the 426 patients with primary liver tumours or metastases (excluding PHC). In this subgroup of patients, the in situ surgical approach was the most common (81.9%, 349), followed by ante situm (11.5%, 49) and ex situ (6.6%, 28). Ante situm/ex situ approaches were associated with younger age (P < 0.001), larger tumours (P < 0.001), higher rates of bilobar disease (P < 0.001), and extracorporeal bypass (P < 0.001). Consequently, these approaches had greater blood loss (P < 0.001), transfusion needs (P < 0.001), and longer operative times (P < 0.001). The ante situm and ex situ groups, compared with the in situ group, had significantly higher postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa; 67.3% (33) and 64.3% (18) versus 35.5% (124), respectively; P < 0.001) and ICU stays (6.5 and 5 versus 3 days, respectively; P < 0.001). Mortality was also higher in the ante situm and ex situ groups than in the in situ group (16.3% (8) and 17.9% (5) versus 8.3% (29), respectively; P = 0.060). In this patient cohort (Table S2), multivariate analysis showed that the in situ approach was associated with a lower incidence of biliary and pulmonary complications, as well as a shorter operative time. Conversely, hypothermic perfusion of the remnant liver was associated with a higher rate of late biliary complications and major complications in general. Both partial and total vascular occlusion was associated with increased postoperative bleeding, Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa complications, and the need for intraoperative transfusion. Finally, the use of extracorporeal bypass was associated with increased blood loss requiring intraoperative transfusion, longer operative time, prolonged ICU stay, and higher hospital readmission rates. Vascular grafts

In all, 296 patients (55.6%) underwent vascular graft placement. The most frequently used grafts were synthetic (60.5%, 192), followed by autologous grafts (21.3%, 63) and xenografts (7.8%, 23). Synthetic grafts were more commonly used for IVC repair (P < 0.001), whereas autologous grafts and allografts were preferred in HV reconstructions (P < 0.001).

Graft-related complications were observed in 58 patients (22.3%; Table S3). Postoperative infectious complications were significantly associated with graft-related complications (40.3% (25) versus 23.8% (62) in the group without graft-related complications; P < 0.001), and bile leakage occurred more frequently among those with than without graft complications (42.1% (16) versus 14.6% (38), respectively; P = 0.003). Graft-related complications were more frequent in patients requiring PV (P = 0.019) or HA reconstruction (P = 0.010). Therapeutic postoperative anticoagulation (36% (40) versus 48.5% (111); P < 0.001), and prophylactic anticoagulation (10.2% (17) versus 68.7% (n = 167); P = 0.003) were less common in patients with than without graft-related complications.

Temporal comparisons of surgical and postoperative outcomes

When comparing the two study periods (1990–2010 versus 2011–2024), several statistically significant differences were observed (Table S4). In the more recent period, patients presented with a significantly smaller median largest tumour size (60 versus 80 mm; P < 0.001) and a markedly higher use of liver hypertrophy techniques (23.6% (106) versus 2.4% (2); P < 0.001). Surgical strategy also evolved, with increased use of the Pringle manoeuvre (P < 0.001) and a shift in vascular reconstruction techniques towards primary suture repair (47.1% (212) versus 29.3% (24); P < 0.001), alongside reduced use of synthetic grafts. Intraoperative blood loss (700 versus 1000 ml; P = 0.007) and transfusion requirements (48.3% (211) versus 67.2% (43); P = 0.003) were significantly lower in the more recent period. Changes in anticoagulation practice were evident, with a reduction in the use of prophylactic anticoagulation (63% (273) versus 96% (72); P < 0.001) and an increase in the use of therapeutic anticoagulation (54.6% (208) versus 12% (6); P < 0.001) from 1990–2010 to 2011–2024. After surgery, the more recent period was associated with higher rates of Clavien–Dindo IIIa complications (P = 0.036), but no significant differences were observed in Clavien–Dindo IIIb complications, mortality, ICU stay, LOS, or 90-day readmission rates between the two periods.

Oncological outcomes

Compared with the other indications, DFS according to the Kaplan–Meier method was significantly superior for HCC (P = 0.011), whereas according to the Fine–Gray model PHC was associated with a higher risk of recurrence in the presence of the competing risk of death (P = 0.022). Overall, respective DFS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 61%, 28%, and 24% for CRLM; 80.8%, 45.8%, and 45.8% for HCC; 69.5%, 34.7%, and 26.2% for iCCA; 51.3%, 22.4%, and 22.4% for non-CRLM; 52.1%, 19.1%, and 11% for PHC; and 63%, 44.1%, and 31.5% for sarcoma (Fig. 3a). Respective OS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 87.1%, 48.2%, and 29.7% for CRLM; 82.5%, 60.3%, and 42.7% for HCC; 84.3%, 53.3%, and 30.2% for iCCA; 75%, 46.7%, and 37.4% for non-CRLM; 76.2%, 36.3%, and 22.4% for PHC; and 87.4%, 87.4%, and 68.8% for sarcoma (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

This international multicentre study represents the largest and most comprehensive series to date evaluating the outcomes of extreme liver resections with vascular reconstruction in patients with advanced primary or metastatic hepatobiliary tumours involving major vascular structures. The findings confirm that these highly complex procedures are technically feasible and reproducible when performed by an experienced multidisciplinary team, although they remain associated with considerable morbidity and mortality.

There has been a longstanding debate over which surgical approach offers the best balance of safety and efficacy when hepatic resection with vascular reconstruction is required3. The present results support the preferential use of the in situ approach whenever technically feasible. In this cohort, in situ resections were associated with better perioperative outcomes, including lower rates of major complications, reduced blood loss and transfusion requirements, and shorter operative time and ICU stay compared with ante situm and ex situ procedures. The 90-day mortality observed for ante situm and ex situ procedures was higher than that reported in recent series from highly specialized centres13,14. These differences likely reflect the highly centralized expertise of such institutions, where these complex procedures are performed more frequently. In contrast, the present study represents a broader multicentre experience, suggesting that outcomes of extreme liver surgery procedures may be closely related to institutional experience and procedural volume.

Multiple vascular reconstruction techniques have been described in oncological liver surgery2,15. Synthetic grafts were predominantly used in IVC repairs, whereas autologous and allogeneic grafts were more frequently used for HV reconstruction. Synthetic grafts were considered not very suitable for HV and PV reconstructions, compared with IVC reconstructions, due to their rigidity. Despite these differences in anatomical application, no statistically significant association was observed between the type of graft material and the incidence of complications. This suggests that technical factors, patient-related variables, and the complexity of the vascular reconstruction itself likely play more critical roles in determining outcomes than the graft material used16. These data provide reassurance that graft selection and postoperative anticoagulation strategies can be adapted to individual patients and surgical characteristics without compromising safety.

Vascular involvement had a variable impact on surgical complexity and outcomes. IVC reconstruction, although most frequent, showed a more favourable perioperative profile and appeared technically more manageable with adequate vascular control. In contrast, HV reconstruction was more demanding, being associated with higher transfusion requirements, longer operative time, and prolonged hospital stay17–19. These findings suggest that the anatomical position of the HV, especially in proximity to the hepatocaval confluence, poses a greater technical challenge, increasing the likelihood of intraoperative bleeding and postoperative complications20,21.

PV reconstruction was associated with the worst intra- and postoperative outcomes, likely due to a higher risk of thrombosis and liver failure. However, this should not be interpreted as a direct causal effect, because PV involvement often reflects greater surgical complexity, including extensive hepatectomy, reduced future liver remnant, and possible biliary reconstruction. Therefore, PV reconstruction likely indicates a particularly high-risk surgical setting, highlighting the need for careful planning and thorough patient risk stratification22,23.

PHC poses significant management challenges, particularly when associated with vascular invasion24,25. These findings underscore that PHC requiring vascular reconstruction constitutes a clinically and surgically distinct entity compared with other liver tumours and metastases. Consequently, in situ approaches are favoured and complex perfusion strategies are generally unnecessary. Despite these anatomical and procedural differences, postoperative outcomes such as major complications, mortality, LOS, and readmission rates were comparable to those observed in other tumour types. However, the higher incidence of bile leaks and ascites in this group underscores the ongoing technical complexity of biliary with vascular reconstruction.

The role of liver graft perfusion during extreme liver surgery has traditionally been considered a protective measure to reduce ischaemic injury, particularly in prolonged resections7,26. However, the present study challenges this assumption. In subgroup analyses, hepatic perfusion was not associated with reduced late biliary complications or postoperative liver failure and was instead linked to higher rates of late biliary complications and major morbidity (Clavien–Dindo ≥ IIIa). This likely reflects its use in more complex, high-risk procedures rather than a true protective effect. Moreover, perfusion during ante situm resections is often suboptimal, frequently warmer than intended and without adequate oxygenation. With the development of flexible devices for hypothermic oxygenated perfusion, the impact of proper liver oxygenation warrants further evaluation27,28.

Similarly, the utility of extracorporeal bypass remains debatable16,29–31. Although extracorporeal bypass aims to maintain haemodynamic stability and reduce ischaemic injury during complex resections, this study showed its use was associated with higher transfusion requirements, longer operative time, prolonged ICU stay, and increased readmission rates. These findings question its routine use and support a more selective, individualized approach, reserving bypass and hepatic perfusion for patients where alternative vascular control is not feasible or safe.

A central question in the context of extreme liver surgery with vascular reconstruction is whether the morbidity and mortality rates associated with these high-risk procedures are justified by their oncological benefit32,33. In the present study, OS and DFS varied substantially across tumour types, reflecting both the heterogeneity of tumour biology and the limitations of resection-based strategies in certain settings. The most favourable long-term oncological outcomes were observed in HCC and sarcoma patients, with 5-year OS reaching 42.7% and 68.8%, respectively. These results suggest that, for select patients with good performance status and controlled disease burden, extreme liver resection can offer a meaningful chance of prolonged survival and may represent the best or only curative option when transplantation is not feasible.

In contrast, patients with CRLM and PHC showed poorer long-term DFS and OS, despite aggressive surgical management. For example, 5-year OS in CRLM patients was only 29.7%, and DFS dropped to 24%, raising legitimate concerns about the oncological durability of these procedures in this subgroup. Although PHC showed acceptable postoperative safety, its high recurrence rate and poor 5-year DFS raise concerns regarding the long-term benefit of resection, particularly when vascular reconstruction is required. However, recent evidence indicates that, despite the negative impact of vascular resection on OS compared with standard resections, it still provides a striking survival advantage over non-resection, suggesting that, in experienced hands, this approach may be justified for its oncological benefit34.

These findings reignite the discussion in the emerging field of transplant oncology35. In select tumours (particularly HCC, iCCA, PHC, and carefully selected CRLM), liver transplantation may provide better disease control and long-term survival while avoiding the burden of extreme surgery. These findings emphasize the importance of patient selection, tumour biology, and multidisciplinary decision-making. As transplant oncology evolves, studies comparing resection and transplantation will be essential to refine indications and optimize outcomes.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including potential selection bias and heterogeneity in surgical techniques, perioperative management, and reporting standards across centres. The absence of centralized pathology review and standardized follow-up may have introduced variability in outcome assessment. Given the long inclusion period, advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care also may have influenced outcomes. A predefined time-based subanalysis was therefore performed to account for temporal changes in practice. Despite these limitations, this study represents the largest data set to date on extreme liver resections with vascular reconstruction, providing a comprehensive analysis of surgical complexity, postoperative outcomes, and long-term survival.

This international multicentre study shows that liver resection with vascular reconstruction is feasible but carries significant morbidity and mortality even in specialized centres. The choice between in situ, ante situm, and ex situ techniques may also reflect the experience and technical preference of each surgical team. These highly complex procedures are often tailored to the expertise of the operating centre and the familiarity of the surgical team with a given technique. Therefore, although the present study supports the preferential use of the in situ approach whenever technically feasible, the final choice of technique ultimately depends on the intraoperative findings and the preference and experience of the surgical team.

From an oncological perspective, outcomes largely depend on tumour biology. Although some tumour types derive limited benefit from resection, this may still exceed that of non-surgical management, underscoring the value of surgery despite its risks. These findings highlight the need for individualized patient selection and consideration of liver transplantation in select patients as transplant oncology advances. Prospective studies comparing resection and transplantation are required to define optimal strategies for advanced liver tumours with vascular involvement.
