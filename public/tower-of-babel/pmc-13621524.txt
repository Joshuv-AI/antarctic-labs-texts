Solid‐State Fermentation of Legumes: Multi‐Scale Structural Modifications to Unlock Techno‐Functional Properties
Authors: Deepak Subramani, Nicholas Horlacher, Alice Marciniak, Maria G. Corradini, Iris J. Joye, Biniam Kebede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLegumes are increasingly used as functional ingredients in plant‐based foods. However, their compact endogenous structure restricts techno‐functional properties. Conventional fractionation methods that isolate proteins further impair these properties and generate co‐products. Solid‐state fermentation (SSF), which involves growth of microorganisms on moist solid legumes for in situ enzymatic modification, is a gentle and sustainable strategy to enhance techno‐functionality. However, the mechanistic understanding of structural changes underpinning functional modifications in legume ingredients remains limited. This review critically evaluates SSF as a multi‐scale structural modification strategy across cellular, microstructural, and molecular scales to improve the techno‐functionality of legume‐based ingredients. Factors influencing the SSF process are briefly discussed. Key research gaps are identified, and future directions are proposed to advance understanding of process–structure–function relationships in SSF‐derived legume ingredients. SSF‐induced process–structure–function relationships in legume ingredients are established within a multi‐scale structural framework. Cellular breakdown during SSF liberates starch and protein, overcoming hydration restrictions. Under the microstructural scale, breakdown of protein aggregates improves solubility and interfacial behavior, while surface erosion and crystalline rearrangement of starch granules modulate pasting and thermal properties. At the molecular scale, hydrolysis of multimeric proteins into smaller peptides; protein unfolding that increases surface hydrophobicity, charge, and sulfhydryl content; and alterations in amylose–amylopectin ratio of starch underpin improvements in water and oil binding, interfacial, gelling, and pasting properties. Integrating multi‐scale structural insights with microbial growth, enzyme activity and metabolomics will enable reverse engineering of SSF for targeted design of functional legume ingredients.

Introduction

Foods are complex soft materials whose physical structure is characterized by the spatial organization of biopolymers across multiple length scales, from nanometers to millimeters (Mengucci et al. 2022). Composed of molecular entities such as proteins and carbohydrates, these biopolymers form hierarchical structures through supramolecular assemblies that are governed by covalent and non‐covalent interactions. In this context, nature can be regarded as a sophisticated food designer that manifests the complex structural architecture of legumes. Legumes such as soybean, pea, and faba bean possess thick and rigid polymeric seed coats that encapsulate cotyledons containing densely packed cellular structures. The fibrous cell wall matrix surrounding starch granules and protein bodies, together with their compact molecular organization, makes legumes challenging to process into functional ingredients suitable for diverse food applications (H. Li et al. 2021; Patel et al. 2025).

Conventionally, the production of legume‐based ingredients has largely relied on fractionation approaches that prioritize protein concentration and purity (Lie‐Piang et al. 2024). Wet fractionation methods, such as alkaline extraction followed by isoelectric precipitation (IEP), are widely used to produce highly purified protein isolates (> 80%–90% protein content; Harasym et al. 2025). However, exposure to extreme pH conditions during extensive processing steps leads to irreversible protein denaturation and aggregation. These structural changes reduce protein solubility, which is critical for many functional properties, while requiring substantial resource inputs (e.g., water and chemicals) and generating large volumes of low‐value starch‐ and fiber‐rich co‐products (Eze et al. 2022). Dry fractionation methods, such as air classification, separate milled legumes into protein‐rich and starch‐rich fractions without the use of water or chemicals (Patel et al. 2025). While dry fractionation provides a more sustainable alternative that better preserves native protein structures, the resulting ingredients often contain residual antinutrients and off‐flavor compounds, and their techno‐functional performance may be constrained by complex interactions within the multi‐component matrix (Lie‐Piang et al. 2023; Schutyser et al. 2025). These limitations have stimulated growing interest in functionality‐driven ingredient design strategies that rely less on extensive purification and more on targeted modification of endogenous legume structures (Van der Goot et al. 2016; Lie‐Piang et al. 2024). In this context, solid‐state fermentation (SSF) has emerged over the past 5 years as a gentle and sustainable bioprocessing strategy capable of simultaneously modifying flavor, techno‐functional properties, and antinutritional factors through microbial enzyme‐driven transformations of legume structures (Canoy et al. 2024).

SSF involves the growth of microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, and fungi) on moist solid substrates in the absence of free‐flowing water, enabling in situ enzyme production that drives structural, compositional, and functional modifications (De Villa et al. 2023). Unlike widely used submerged fermentation (SMF), SSF consistently yields several‐fold higher levels of extracellular enzymes due to conditions that better mimic the natural growth environment of many microorganisms, while also requiring minimal resources such as water (Sandhya et al. 2005; Londoño‐Hernandez et al. 2020). The higher enzyme yields frequently observed in SSF are attributed to minimal free water content, which may restrict bulk diffusion of readily metabolizable nutrients and generate local concentration gradients, thereby preventing sustained exposure of microbes to high sugar concentrations and alleviating catabolite repression (suppression of enzyme gene expression by readily available sugars; Kumar et al. 2021; Viniegra‐González and Favela‐Torres 2006). Moreover, greater oxygen availability within inter‐particle void spaces supporting aerobic respiration, and the extensive spread of fungal hyphae directly across the substrate surface in SSF may also contribute to enhanced extracellular enzyme induction (Barrios‐González 2012; Téllez‐Téllez et al. 2008). Enzymatic hydrolysis by a diverse cocktail of microbial enzymes, including protease, amylase, cellulase, xylanase, pectinase, and lipase, induces transformations in proteins, starch, cell‐wall polysaccharides, and lipids (De Villa et al. 2023; Y. Zhang et al. 2022). While hydrolytic reactions are well established, oxidative reactions may also be initiated by fungal oxidative enzymes (e.g., laccases and peroxidases), leading to oxidation of phenolic moieties in cell‐wall polymers (e.g., lignin) and aromatic amino acids and thiol groups in proteins (Contreras et al. 2023; Kumla et al. 2020; H. Zhang et al. 2024; Mokoonlall et al. 2016). Structural changes spanning multiple overlapping structural length scales—cellular scale (≈50–500 µm, covering cell integrity and tissue organization), microparticulate scale (≈0.1–100 µm starch or protein particles and cellular fragments), and molecular scale (≈0.1–50 nm, biopolymer assemblies) can enhance solubility, water‐ and oil‐holding capacity, foaming, emulsifying, gelling, and pasting properties of legume‐derived flours, protein isolates, and concentrates (Ayllón‐Parra et al. 2025; Ali et al. 2022; Zahir et al. 2020; Vilgis 2015). Such functionally enhanced fermented ingredients have demonstrated improved water‐ and oil‐retention, appearance, network strength, texture profile, and reduced cooking loss in applications such as plant‐based meat analogues (Ou et al. 2023; Patria et al. 2024; Maung et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2025; Razavizadeh et al. 2021).

With the expanding body of research on SSF for functional legume ingredient development, comprehensive and critical reviews are needed to identify trends and facilitate further progress. Existing SSF‐related reviews primarily focus on compositional changes linked to improvements in bioactivity in grains (including legumes) and by‐products (Green et al. 2024; De Villa et al. 2023; Martins et al. 2011; Kaur and Purewal 2023; Harahap et al. 2025). Canoy et al. (2024) explored SSF broadly for improving flavor, texture, nutrient composition, and digestibility in plant protein sources. Feng et al. (2024) addressed the effects of SSF on the molecular structure and composition of plant proteins, primarily in relation to digestibility and bioactive outcomes. Sakandar et al. (2023), Álvarez et al. (2025), and Gbedo et al. (2025) mainly compiled studies on the degradation of antinutrients (i.e., phytic acid, trypsin inhibitors, raffinose) in legumes during SSF. Reviews that have partly addressed techno‐functionality and reduction of beany off‐flavor in legumes are generic and do not specifically focus on SSF (Senanayake et al. 2023; Garrido‐Galand et al. 2021; Fan et al. 2025; Emkani et al. 2022; Du et al. 2024; Coronel‐León et al. 2025; Chinma et al. 2025; Alrosan et al. 2023). Overall, studies on the techno‐functional impact of legumes during SSF remain scarce, and the underlying multi‐scale structural modifications and microbial enzyme‐driven bioconversion mechanisms have not yet been systematically reported. Understanding structural changes across multiple scales is crucial for achieving predictable fermentation outcomes and enabling the rational design of SSF‐derived ingredients, as techno‐functionality emerges from coupled hierarchical structural features such as particle morphology and molecular unfolding (Grossmann 2024; Yan et al. 2025). However, existing SSF studies remain fragmented, largely focusing on either compositional or molecular‐level changes, which limits their ability to fully explain or correlate structural transformations with techno‐functional outcomes (Khorsandi et al. 2024; Alrosan et al. 2023; Kumitch et al. 2020). This knowledge gap makes the optimization of SSF strategies for tailoring and predicting the techno‐functional performance of legume‐based ingredients challenging.

Therefore, the present comprehensive review, unlike previous studies, examines SSF from a multi‐scale structural modification perspective, spanning cellular, micro‐, and molecular length scales, and systematically linking structural changes with techno‐functional properties relevant to plant‐based foods. Factors influencing SSF are briefly discussed to understand key variables and enable better process control. In addition to identifying research gaps, future directions for integrating process–structure–function relationships in SSF are discussed. Overall, this review aims to advance mechanistic understanding of SSF and enable rational design of structurally tailored legume ingredients with targeted techno‐functional properties.

SSF

SSF involves the growth of microorganisms on moist solid legumes without free‐flowing water to harness their in situ enzyme synthesis for structural, compositional, and functional modifications (De Villa et al. 2023). Microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, and filamentous fungi) function as cellular factories in which metabolic conversions occur with complex heat and mass transfer. The three contacting phases—solid legume, thin liquid layer, and air—provide a unique microenvironment for bacterial and fungal activities and their potential interactions (Jin et al. 2024). SSF produces functional legume ingredients through a series of steps (Figure 1), including substrate moisture adjustment, sterilization, and inoculation of the prepared legumes with a starter culture, incubation for a defined period, and termination of fermentation by heat treatment, drying, and milling into a fine powder (Gbedo et al. 2025). Although SSF involves relatively simple operational steps, its mechanistic complexity necessitates a clear understanding of the factors influencing SSF (Figure 1), including substrate properties, starter culture characteristics, and fermentation conditions. These factors strongly influence microbial growth patterns and enzyme activity, thereby governing the progression of structural modifications in legumes. The effects of substrate properties, starter culture characteristics, and fermentation conditions on SSF performance are discussed in the following sections.

Substrate Properties

A wide range of legumes, such as oilseeds (e.g., soybean, peanut) and pulses (e.g., chickpea, faba bean, lentil, lupin), are suitable for SSF (Álvarez et al. 2025). These substrates can be subjected to different pretreatments (e.g., dehulling, milling, thermal processing, defatting, and fractionation) to obtain materials with varying structural integrity, including whole or dehulled grains, boiled grains, defatted cakes, grits, flours, gelatinized matrices, cotyledon cells, dry‐fractionated concentrates, or protein isolates (Feng et al. 2024; Emkani et al. 2022). Dehulling and milling remove endogenous structural barriers such as the outer hull and cotyledon cell walls that restrict microbial access to nutrients, whereas thermal processing (e.g., starch gelatinization) enhances substrate diffusibility, enzymatic accessibility, and microbial uptake (Agarwal et al. 2025). Fractionation of legumes into protein‐ or starch‐rich isolates and concentrates allows modulation of the carbon‐to‐nitrogen (C/N) ratio (Xing et al. 2020). Depending on legume type and pretreatment, chemical composition (e.g., C/N ratio), physical properties (e.g., hardness, particle size, porosity), and endogenous biological activity (e.g., native enzymes) vary substantially, thereby influencing SSF performance (J. Wang et al. 2023). Carbon‐rich substrates primarily support microbial energy metabolism, whereas nitrogen‐rich substrates favor biomass enrichment. In addition to nutrient supply, the substrate provides the physical environment for microbial growth. Reduced particle size increases surface area and enhances fermentation kinetics by enhancing nutrient and oxygen accessibility. However, excessively fine particles reduce porosity and aeration. Substrate porosity is particularly important in fungal SSF, enabling oxygen diffusion and hyphal penetration (Agarwal et al. 2025). Pretreatments such as soaking and boiling reduce legume hardness and improve fermentability (Di Stefano et al. 2019).

Microbial Culture

Legumes can be fermented with a diverse range of microorganisms. Since SSF resembles the natural habitat of fungi with a low moisture content (40%–60%), filamentous fungi are particularly well suited for SSF (Thomas et al. 2013). Fungi such as Aspergillus spp. (e.g., Aspergillus oryzae, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus sojae), Rhizopus spp. (e.g., Rhizopus oligosporus, Rhizopus oryzae), and edible mushrooms (e.g., Pleurotus ostreatus) are commonly cultivated on legume substrates (J. Wang et al. 2023). Fungal mycelia grow laterally as aerial hyphae on the surface and penetrate the substrate matrix to access nutrients. Although SSF generally maintains a high‐water activity (aw > 0.9), the absence of free‐flowing water and the heterogeneous microenvironment can restrict the growth of many pathogens and spoilage microorganisms by limiting nutrient diffusion and utilization (Jin et al. 2019). However, certain lactic acid bacteria (e.g., Lactiplantibacillus plantarum) and Bacillus spp. (e.g., Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus natto, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens) can adapt to solid substrates, and their rapid proliferation enables their use as starter cultures in legume‐based SSF (Green et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2024). Yeasts (e.g., Saccharomyces spp.) with rapid growth rates and low oxygen requirements are also suitable (Zhao et al. 2024). Microbial selection is therefore critical and should be based on safety, compatibility with the legume matrix, and the intended SSF outcome. The ability of starter cultures to metabolize legume substrates depends on their enzymatic capability (De Villa et al. 2023). Microorganisms involved in SSF synthesize diverse hydrolytic and oxidative enzymes, including cell wall‐degrading enzymes (e.g., cellulases, xylanases, pectinases, laccases, polygalacturonases, pectin methyl esterases, and β‐glucosidases), starch‐degrading enzymes (e.g., α‐amylase, β‐amylase, amyloglucosidase, and pullulanase), and proteolytic enzymes (endo‐ and exo‐proteases; De Villa et al. 2023). Beyond enzymatic hydrolysis, microbial metabolites also contribute to substrate modification. Organic acid production, particularly by lactic acid bacteria, lowers substrate pH, suppresses pathogenic and competing microorganisms, and alters biopolymer structure and stability (Anumudu et al. 2024). Certain microorganisms (e.g., Lp. plantarum, B. natto, A. oryzae) produce exopolysaccharides such as dextran, β‐glucans, and poly‐γ‐glutamic acid that modify the functional properties of legume ingredients (Osemwegie et al. 2020). When monocultures are insufficient for substrate utilization, co‐culture fermentation can be advantageous, with microbial dynamics influenced by inoculum size, inoculation strategy, and strain ratios. Accordingly, microbial selection should align with the desired structural or functional modifications (Green et al. 2026; J .Wang et al. 2023).

Process Variables

Regardless of mono‐ or co‐culture systems, SSF dynamics and kinetics are strongly influenced by process variables, including incubation temperature, fermentation time, inoculum size, bioreactor configuration, substrate bed depth, aeration, agitation mode, moisture content, relative humidity, and pH (J. Wang et al. 2023). Legume fermentations are typically conducted at 30°C–37°C, with fungi favoring lower temperatures (25°C–30°C) than bacteria (30°C–37°C; J. Wang et al. 2023). However, optimal growth conditions do not always coincide with those required for targeted structural or functional modifications, as specific enzyme expression may be enhanced under suboptimal stress conditions. Fermentation time governs the extent of modification and typically ranges from hours to days but rarely exceeds 1 week to avoid spoilage (Yan et al. 2025). Inoculum size influences colonization kinetics, microbial competition, and the uniformity of SSF. Insufficient inoculum levels prolong the lag phase, increasing susceptibility to contaminating organisms and thereby posing a significant food safety risk. Excessive inoculation, by contrast, may lead to rapid nutrient depletion and mass transfer limitations that can result in non‐homogenous fermentation outcomes (Cano‐González et al. 2026; Egbune et al. 2023). The optimum inoculum levels for filamentous fungi (105–107 spores/g substrate) and bacteria and yeast (105–107 CFU/g substrate) typically fall within the same order of magnitude (Batbayar et al. 2023; Stone et al. 2024; Cano‐González et al. 2026). Aeration is essential for heat dissipation and CO2 removal but can also promote moisture loss, necessitating moisture control (J. Wang et al. 2023). A thin substrate bed layer (2–5 cm) is desirable as it improves aeration and minimizes the formation of oxygen and temperature gradients. While static bioreactors are commonly used industrially, they present challenges in achieving homogeneous fermentation and aeration. Agitated or rotary systems improve oxygen exposure but may damage fragile fungal hyphae (Arora et al. 2018). Moisture contents of 50%–60% are generally considered optimal for SSF as low moisture restricts nutrient diffusion and turgor pressure (J. Wang et al. 2023). However, moisture levels exceeding the water‐holding capacity of the substrate (often > 70%, depending on substrate characteristics) can result in free water accumulation within interstitial spaces, thereby compromising substrate structure and aeration needed for microbial growth (Ramesh and Lonsane 1990; J. Wang et al. 2023; Chutmanop et al. 2008). As the substrate is typically spread over a large surface area, a high ambient relative humidity (85%–95% RH) should be maintained to prevent moisture loss during fermentation (De Villa et al. 2023). While pH is highly dynamic during SSF, the initial pH can be adjusted to favor the growth of inoculated microorganisms and promote their dominance (Bellon‐Maurel et al. 2003). Filamentous fungi generally grow optimally under mildly acidic conditions, whereas lactic acid bacteria are more acid‐tolerant and can continue to grow as pH decreases due to organic acid production (Green et al. 2025; J. Wang et al. 2023). However, substrate heterogeneity and the absence of a continuous free‐water phase limit effective pH control during SSF, as pH can vary locally within the solid matrix and added acid or alkali cannot be uniformly distributed throughout the substrate without disturbing its structure or disrupting fungal hyphae (Borkertas et al. 2025).

Collectively, substrate characteristics, microbial type, and process variables govern SSF dynamics and kinetics, thereby influencing the enzymatic transformation of the multi‐scale structure of legume matrices. These structural transformations, including cell wall damage, starch granule erosion, disruption of protein aggregates, unfolding of proteins, and hydrolysis of biopolymers in legumes, underpin the functional modifications observed during fermentation. The following section examines SSF‐induced structural modifications at the cellular, microstructural, and molecular levels.

Multi‐Scale Structural Modifications

Legume seeds consist of different parts, such as cotyledons and an embryo protected by a rigid outer seed coat. The complex structural architecture of legumes governs their hardness, milling behavior, fractionation efficiency, and techno‐functionality of derived ingredients (Kigel et al. 2015). Their structural organization can be described across three hierarchical scales: (1) cellular structure (≈50–500 µm), corresponding to intact or partially disrupted plant cells; (2) microstructure (≈0.1–100 µm), encompassing starch granules, protein aggregates, and cell fragments; and (3) molecular structure (≈0.1–50 nm), representing proteins, polysaccharides, and their molecular attributes and interactions, each of which is progressively modulated during SSF (Figure 2; Aguilera 2012; Di Stefano et al. 2019).

Cellular Structure

The cotyledon tissue of legumes is characterized by densely arranged, rigid cells of spherical to ovoid morphology, typically around 100 µm in size (Xiong et al. 2022). The cells act as a natural capsule for starch granules and protein bodies. This cellular encapsulation acts as a diffusion barrier, restricting hydration, enzyme accessibility, and mass transfer of intracellular constituents.

Across legumes, SSF consistently weakens the cellular structural barrier. For example, in yellow peas fermented by Lp. plantarum (48 h, 37°C), rupture of cell walls and depletion of intracellular starch granules and protein bodies were observed (Di Stefano et al. 2019). Structural alterations began from the seed coat, where macrosclereid and osteosclereid layers exhibited swelling and loosening, and the waxy cutin‐rich linear lucida layer was degraded. Removal of these protective seed coat layers enhances water imbibition and facilitates enzymatic penetration into inner cotyledon tissues. Once microorganisms gain access to intracellular tissues, the pectin‐rich middle lamella that “glues” the parenchyma cells together is hydrolyzed to loosen the matrix, eventually also degrading primary cell wall structure and cell membrane integrity. This exposes starch granules and protein bodies, resulting in their depletion, although starch accessibility would require disruption of the surrounding protein matrix. Access to intracellular nutrients shifts the microbial metabolism from cell wall hydrolysis to predominantly starch and protein assimilation for energy and biomass accumulation (Di Stefano et al. 2019).

Cellular disintegration during SSF, however, could vary by legume source due to differences in endogenous structural chemistry like lignification pattern of the seed coat, its thickness, and the porosity of cotyledon cells, which determines the local resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis (Di Stefano et al. 2019). Unlike yellow pea, lentils fermented by Lp. plantarum under the same fermentation conditions (48 h, 37°C) caused thinning of the macrosclereid layer of the seed coat due to delignification. Importantly, starch and protein depletion occurred only in subsets of cotyledon cells (Di Stefano et al. 2019). This heterogeneity suggests that enzyme diffusion and microbial colonization are spatially constrained by the compact structural assembly of intact lentil seeds. However, pre‐treatments such as dry milling and wet fractionation can reduce particle size and overcome the spatial constraints typical of intact seeds. Although the critical particle size distribution for microbial colonization has not yet been systematically established for legumes and likely varies among microorganisms, very fine particles (< 100 µm) may reduce aeration due to dense packing and limited inter‐particle void space, whereas larger particles (> 1 mm) may restrict microbial accessibility because of their lower specific surface area (Schmidt and Furlong 2012; Pandey 1991). Following wet fractionation of cotyledon cells and cell clusters (50–500 µm), SSF by Saccharomyces cerevisiae (60 h, 40°C) caused a pronounced increase in cell wall porosity, allowing better penetration of dextran probes corresponding to the molecular size of fungal amylase (Zahir et al. 2020).

The progression of cellular breakdown during SSF is also strongly time‐dependent and closely linked to microbial growth dynamics. In kidney beans, R. oligosporus hyphae progressively enveloped cotyledon cells, with limited structural disruption at early stages (17–29 h), restricted to regions in close proximity to hyphal growth, followed by widespread cell wall rupture after prolonged fermentation (35 h; Sun et al. 2023). This progression was consistent with ergosterol measurements, which showed a sharp 16‐fold increase between 17 and 29 h followed by a plateau, indicating rapid fungal biomass accumulation during early stages of fermentation. The gap between the cell wall and the protein–starch matrix grew over time due to the release of starch and proteins through the cracks formed on the cell wall. Similar time‐dependent changes in cellular integrity have been observed in chickpea seeds (kabuli type), where R. oryzae induced substantial cell wall disruption predominantly between 36 and 72 h of fermentation (Kim et al. 2025). In chickpea grits (type not specified), R. oligosporus hyphae preferentially colonized the pectin‐rich middle lamella, where cell loosening may have resulted not only from enzymatic hydrolysis but also from the mechanical stress exerted by extending hyphae (Paredes‐López et al. 1991). Edible mushrooms such as Pleurotus ostreatus may require longer fermentation times (10–14 days, 25°C–28°C) to induce cell wall disintegration than R. oligosporus (3 days, 25°C–28°C; Badia‐Olmos et al. 2024). Although fungi generally exhibit greater cellulolytic expression, bacteria display more rapid but less spatially organized cellular hydrolysis. Soybean meal fermented by B. subtilis (12–24 h, 37°C) showed an earlier onset of cellular disruption, compared with fungal‐fermented kidney beans (Wang et al. 2024). This accelerated breakdown likely reflects faster bacterial proliferation and improved accessibility due to the relatively smaller particle size of soybean meal. Unlike directional hyphal invasion, medium degradation by bacteria is primarily diffusion‐driven, leading to faster but substrate‐dependent cellular collapse. These observations indicate that the spatio‐temporal uniformity of cellular disintegration during SSF is dictated by microbial growth dynamics and kinetics.

A great diversity of filamentous fungi, bacteria, and yeasts has been shown to significantly alter the cellular structure of legumes during SSF. Such structural modifications can alleviate hard‐to‐cook phenomena in legumes, as cellular disruption during SSF of chickpea (kabuli) by R. oryzae contributes to a nearly two‐fold reduction in post‐cooking hardness (Kim et al. 2025; Li et al. 2019). This also enhances protein extraction yield by up to 130% in B. amyloliquefaciens subsp. plantarum‐fermented soybeans and may contribute to improved functionality by influencing starch and protein hydration, as well as their molecular interactions (J. Zhu, Liu et al. 2024; Khorsandi et al. 2024). Despite these advances, investigations of cellular‐level transformations during SSF remain limited, and most studies rely on destructive imaging techniques that cannot capture spatiotemporal structural dynamics. Future application of non‐destructive techniques, such as hyperspectral microimaging and X‐ray tomography, could enable a more comprehensive understanding of cellular structural changes and fungal hyphae penetration into legume matrices.

Microstructure

Cellular‐level modifications induced by SSF are underpinned by changes in the microstructure of the cell wall and middle lamella (Figure 2). Cell wall microstructure refers to the spatial organization and packing density of lignocellulose–pectin complex domains at the subcellular to micron scale (Johnson et al. 2018). Loosening of this network and weakening of crystalline cellulose domains during SSF reduce structural rigidity and intercellular adhesion. However, direct microstructural investigations of fermented legume cell walls remain limited, and most interpretations rely on indirect evidence from molecular‐level transformations of wall components (Cosgrove 2016; Di Stefano et al. 2019). As cellular barriers are progressively weakened, encapsulated starch granules and protein bodies become exposed, and their microstructural reorganization becomes evident (Dai et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2017).

In native legume cotyledons, storage proteins exist as dense, compact particulate assemblies confined within protein storage vacuoles (Campbell et al. 2011; Robinson et al. 2005). Thermal processing of legumes (e.g., heat sterilization) further increases particle size by denaturation‐induced aggregation of proteins (Bogahawaththa et al. 2019). SSF disrupts protein microstructure, resulting in reduced aggregate size across diverse microbial systems. For example, mesophilic and thermophilic Bacillus strains (5 days, 37°C–55°C) reduced soybean protein particle size (D50) from approximately 1 to ∼0.5 µm (Lu et al. 2022). Mesophilic bacteria, likely owing to greater extracellular enzyme expression than thermophiles, produced a narrower and smaller particle size distribution (Lu et al. 2022). A similar reduction in particle size has been observed in Aspergillus awamori (96 h, 30°C) fermented kidney bean, Lp. plantarum and S. cerevisiae fermented mash bean flours, and B. subtilis (24 h, 37°C) and Bacillus licheniformis (50 h, 55°C) fermented soybean meal (N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025; Ali et al. 2024; Dai et al. 2022). Co‐culture SSF with microorganisms exhibiting complementary enzymatic activities can enhance protein aggregate breakdown, compared to monoculture systems, as demonstrated in peanut proteins fermented (36 h, 37°C) by Lp. plantarum and B. natto co‐cultures (Pi et al. 2022). Generally, a longer fermentation time results in greater breakdown of protein aggregates (N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025). However, process‐induced shifts in pH toward the isoelectric point and partial protein unfolding can instead promote aggregation, depending on substrate characteristics and microbial activity. In faba bean flour, particle size increased from 67 µm to nearly 500 µm after SSF with A. oryzae (72 h, 30°C) and R. oligosporus (48 h, 28°C; Gautheron et al. 2024), indicating that protein aggregation was favored over fragmentation under those conditions.

When the protein matrix becomes discontinuous and exposes the starch granules, changes to the starch granular morphology also occur. Native starch granules in legumes are smooth, oval to polygonal particles with a broad size distribution (3–50 µm) (Guo et al. 2017). Exposure to microbial amylolytic enzymes during SSF generates surface indentations, pores, and fissures, which intensify with prolonged fermentation (Figure 2). In kidney bean flour fermented by A. awamori, shallow surface indentations observed in early stages (48 h, 30°C) developed into pronounced pores and channels when fermentation time was extended to 96 h (N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025). Microbial species‐dependent differences in starch granule morphology have also been reported. A. sojae (7 days, 25°C) created deeper perforations, whereas Rhizopus azygosporus induced laterally extending surface modifications under similar conditions (Purewal et al. 2024). During spontaneous fermentation of jack bean flour, starch granules have exhibited aggregation due to cross‐linking (Arise et al. 2022). However, starch granule changes during SSF can be influenced by heat pre‐treatment of legumes, as pre‐gelatinization alters native granular porosity and structural integrity, thereby modulating further susceptibility to enzymatic changes.

Beyond granular morphology, SSF affects the semi‐crystalline structure of starch. Legume starch typically exhibits a C‐type crystalline structure, representing a mixture of A‐type (dense, less hydrated orthorhombic packing) and B‐type (open, hydrated hexagonal packing) polymorphs resulting from the lamellar arrangement of amylose and amylopectin (Figure 2; Guo et al. 2017). In mungbeans fermented (6 h, 37°C) by B. subtilis, transformation toward a B‐type crystalline pattern was observed, attributed to rearrangement of starch chains following partial hydrolysis (Lyu et al. 2024). Interestingly, a substantial rise in relative starch crystallinity has also been observed for mungbeans fermented by B. subtilis and soybean pulp fermented (10 days, 25°C) by Ganoderma lucidum (Lyu et al. 2024; Cen et al. 2024). As amorphous regions are preferentially hydrolyzed by amylase, the relative proportion of ordered domains rises, leading to apparent crystallinity enhancement (Guo et al. 2025). However, prolonged fermentation may also introduce defects in starch crystallites, reducing crystallite size and relative crystallinity, as observed in certain cereals (Xiao et al. 2025; Li et al. 2019).

Collectively, SSF of legumes breaks protein aggregates, erodes starch granules and increases their crystallinity. These modifications strongly depend on interactions among fermentation time, the microorganism employed, substrate type, and other process variables. With limited research that explored legume microstructure, particularly cell wall microstructural assembly, starch crystallinity, and native or aggregated protein structures, the interpretation of SSF for micro‐scale transformations remains, however, challenging. An in‐depth analysis of fundamental molecular structures is essential to better explain the mechanisms underlying cellular‐ and micro‐scale modifications.

Molecular Structure

The molecular structure of legumes is primarily defined by its biopolymer constituents, including cell wall polysaccharides, starch and proteins. Cotyledon cell walls consist mainly of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin, whereas the seed coat further contains lignin (C. Li et al. 2021). During SSF, these structural polysaccharides undergo depolymerization, the extent and products of which depend on the microbial enzymes involved. Endo‐acting cellulase and xylanase randomly cleave internal glycosidic bonds, converting high‐molecular‐weight insoluble fiber into lower‐molecular‐weight soluble fractions. For example, soybean residues fermented by G. lucidum (12 days, 26°C) showed increased soluble fiber content, reflecting partial lignocellulosic breakdown (Cen et al. 2024). Simultaneously, exo‐acting cellulases and hemicellulases release reducing sugars from the terminal reducing ends of polymer chains. In soybean meal fermented by B. subtilis (24 h, 37°C), cellulose and hemicellulose contents decreased by 39% and 22%, respectively, accompanied by a significant rise in reducing sugars (Wang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2018). Comparable fiber reductions have been reported in chickpea (type not specified), grasspea, jack bean, and pigeon pea fermented by filamentous fungi such as R. oligosporus and R. oryzae (Cen et al. 2024; Arise et al. 2022; Paredes‐López et al. 1991; Toor et al. 2022). However, this depends strongly on microbial species, substrate structure and fermentation duration. For instance, lentils fermented by A. sojae and Aspergillus ficuum (7 days, 30°C) showed no significant changes in cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin contents (Olukomaiya et al. 2020), indicating structural resistance or limited enzymatic activity. Beyond cellulose–hemicellulose networks, pectin in the middle lamella also undergoes molecular weight reduction of its homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan domains, along with demethylation of ester groups (van den Brink and de Vries 2011). The pectinolytic enzymes are predominantly produced during fungal fermentation (Christiaens et al. 2016).

As cell wall barriers are progressively hydrolyzed, storage proteins become increasingly accessible for enzymatic modifications during SSF (Figure 2). Legume proteins are predominantly globulins (60%–70%) and albumins (10%–20%) organized into primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures (Du et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2022). SSF consistently reduces high molecular weight multimeric legume proteins into monomeric proteins and smaller peptides across legumes, including soybean, chickpea (type not specified), peanut, kidney bean, and faba bean. This reportedly decreases molecular weight from approximately 20–75 kDa to below 20 kDa (Feng et al. 2024; Dai et al. 2022; Pi et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023; Xiao et al. 2015). The degree of hydrolysis (DH) of protein is dependent on fermentation time and incubation temperature (Khorsandi et al. 2024; Das et al. 2025). During spontaneous SSF, proteolysis of faba bean and chickpea (type not specified) protein isolates was less pronounced at 37°C, compared to 30°C, while lentil proteins were extensively degraded at both temperatures (Das et al. 2025). In chickpea (type not specified) flour fermented by B. subtilis (12 h, 37°C), 12%–44% DH was observed, although protein subunit‐specific degradation patterns were reported (Li and Wang 2021). 7S vicilin α‐subunits of chickpea protein degraded earlier than other subunits (7S vicilin β‐subunits, 11S legumin acidic, and basic subunits) during SSF, reflecting preferential hydrolysis by microbial proteases secreted in the early stages of SSF. Similarly, in soybean meal fermented by B. amyloliquefaciens, B. subtilis, or Geobacillus stearothermophilus, β‐conglycinin was readily degraded, whereas the more compact glycinin exhibited partial resistance (Chi and Cho 2016; Dai et al. 2022). This substrate specificity of microbial enzymes is well demonstrated in white lupin flour fermented by R. oligosporus, which produces proteases that preferentially cleave peptide bonds close to aromatic and aliphatic hydrophobic residues, such as phenylalanine and leucine (Tahmasian et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2025). Variations in the abundance and accessibility of these residues among proteins can render some proteins more susceptible to proteolysis, while others exhibit greater resistance.

Other than the primary structure of proteins, SSF also alters their higher‐order secondary conformations (Figure 2). SSF of peanut proteins by B. natto and Lp. plantarum (36 h, 37°C) reduced β‐sheet content while increasing α‐helix and β‐turn structures (Pi et al. 2022). Conversely, soybeans fermented by B. subtilis (48 h, 37°C) showed a decreased α‐helix content with increased β‐turn and random coil structures (Miao et al. 2023). Although differences arise among legumes and microorganisms, the available SSF studies consistently indicate a pattern of structural rearrangements toward overall less ordered secondary structure conformations, which increases their molecular flexibility (Cen et al. 2024; Dai et al. 2022). This is attributed to the destabilization of hydrogen bonds between peptide sequences, resulting from proteolysis and pH modifications during SSF.

The tertiary structure of legume proteins arises from the spatial organization of secondary structural elements into a compact conformation with sequestered hydrophobic regions. During SSF, the tertiary structure of globular proteins unfolds by altering intramolecular electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions (Figure 2). For example, intrinsic fluorescence bathochromic shifts in B. natto and Lp. plantarum fermented peanut protein (36 h, 37°C) suggest exposure of previously buried hydrophobic residues from protein unfolding during SSF (Pi et al. 2022). The degree of protein unfolding is dependent on the applied microorganism and fermentation time, alongside further attributing factors (pH, protein type and temperature). In soybean, mesophilic B. subtilis (24 h, 37°C) produced greater protein unfolding, compared to thermophilic B. licheniformis and G. stearothermophilus (50 h, 55°C) (Dai et al. 2022). The stability of tertiary protein structure is also governed by intramolecular disulfide bridges. An increase in free sulfhydryl groups in soybean meal fermented by B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, and G. stearothermophilus suggests cleavage of disulfide bridges (Dai et al. 2022). The oxido‐reductive environment during SSF enables free sulfhydryl groups to undergo oxidation and form new disulfide bridges within or between protein molecules, with intermolecular bond formation potentially leading to aggregation. Such unfolding of protein structures alters surface charge and surface hydrophobicity. Higher surface hydrophobicity and charge have been reported in Lp. plantarum and B. natto fermented peanut proteins and A. oryzae fermented pea protein isolates (36 h, 37°C; Pi et al. 2022; Khorsandi et al. 2024) than their untreated isolates. These changes shift the intermolecular balance between electrostatic repulsion and hydrophobic attraction, thereby influencing aggregation behavior at the microscale. Moreover, as legume proteins often exist as insoluble phytic acid–protein and phenol–protein complexes through covalent and non‐covalent interactions, microbial phytase and esterase activities during SSF can release bound proteins. This further modifies protein surface properties (Emkani et al. 2022).

The breakdown of starch–protein complexes improves the accessibility of individual starch molecules to amylase enzymes and enhances structural degradation. SSF alters the amylose–amylopectin ratio and their chain length distribution (Figure 2). When soybeans containing 27% amylose were fermented by R. oligosporus for 72 h at 32°C, amylose content was reduced by half (Olanipekun et al. 2009). Since amylose is primarily located in the readily accessible amorphous regions of starch, its preferential degradation decreased the amylose–amylopectin ratio, although prolonged fermentation (> 72 h) may also promote amylopectin hydrolysis. Beyond compositional changes, SSF affects the chain length (A, B, and C clusters) of branched amylopectin, thereby influencing starch crystallinity (Yan et al. 2025; K. Zhang et al. 2024). The crystalline structure of legume starch depends on molecular interactions between single helical amylose and double helical amylopectin, and these conformations can be substantially modified during SSF. Fermentation of chickpea (kabuli type) by R. oryzae (72 h, 33°C) hydrolyzed starch into smaller, less branched chains that interact via enhanced hydrogen bonding, increasing molecular order through formation of double helical structures in B. subtilis‐fermented mung beans (Kim et al. 2025). However, this hydrogen bonding–mediated ordering depends on fermentation time. While 24 h of SSF at 30°C increased hydrogen bonding in kidney bean flour fermented by A. awamori, 72 h reduced hydrogen bonding stabilization (N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025).

Collectively, SSF alters the molecular structure of cell wall polysaccharides, pectin, starch, and storage proteins in legumes. However, compared to proteins, the molecular organization and compositional changes of cell wall polysaccharides and starch during SSF remain insufficiently characterized. Advanced chromatographic, spectroscopic, mass spectrometric, and solid‐state NMR approaches are needed to achieve precise insights into molecular structure. In addition, changes in molecular interactions between biopolymers, phytochemicals and other degradation products of SSF in legumes remain poorly understood and warrant systematic investigation.

Despite these research gaps, structural transformations across molecular, microstructural, and cellular scales remain central to the rational design of functional legume ingredients, as their techno‐functional performance is inherently structure‐dependent. Since each functionality requires specific multi‐scale structural attributes, Section 4 systematically examines these structure–function relationships.

Techno‐Functionality

Solubility

Solubility is a crucial attribute of proteins, influencing other downstream functional attributes such as emulsifying and foaming properties (Feng et al. 2024). It is governed by competitive protein–protein and protein–solvent interactions. These interactions are significantly influenced by multi‐scale structural changes induced by SSF, often improving protein solubility in legumes (Table 1). Although breakdown of legume cells by microbial cellulase and xylanase improves the accessibility and extractability of intracellular proteins to the solvent (Sun et al. 2023), protease‐induced microstructural disruption and molecular structural transformations are the primary determinants of protein solubility, as illustrated in Figure 3.

Structure–function relationships of solid‐state fermented legume ingredients for targeted food application.

At the microstructural scale, the hydrolytic breakdown of large protein aggregates into smaller fragments during SSF improves their solubility by increasing the exposed surface area available for hydration, thereby facilitating dispersion of protein particles in aqueous systems (Pi et al. 2022). This is particularly relevant for proteins that have been aggregated due to prior heat treatment, which is commonly applied in the food industry as a preprocessing step for safety and storage stability, and may serve as a pretreatment before SSF. Pi et al. (2022) reported that the soluble protein content of peanuts was significantly reduced by heat treatment, but subsequent bacterial fermentation (B. natto and Lp. plantarum, 36 h, 37°C) partially recovered the lost solubility. This was linked to a hydrolysis‐mediated reduction in particle size of proteins from nearly 800 to 200 nm, weakening protein–protein associations formed during heat‐induced denaturation. Compared to the monoculture of Lp. plantarum, the mono‐ and co‐culture fermentation with B. natto resulted in greater solubility improvement due to their significant particle size reduction. A similar reduction in particle size was reported by N. D. Patil, Bains et al. (2025) following SSF of kidney bean flour by A. oryzae (up to 96 h, 30°C). Although fermentation decreased the pH from 6.8 to 4.7 (after 48 h), approaching the isoelectric point (pH 4.5) where protein aggregation would generally be favored, the observed reduction in particle size suggests that molecular structural attributes, such as increased zeta potential, may have played a predominant role in preventing aggregation.

Beyond particle‐level disruption, the changes in molecular structure attributes such as molecular weight, DH, surface hydrophobicity, surface charge, and isoelectric point are often associated with the solubility improvement in SSF studies. Khorsandi et al. (2024) observed that SSF by A. oryzae (48 h, 30°C) increased the solubility of pea protein isolate threefold at pH 7, whereas the solubility of navy bean protein isolate was least affected. While surface hydrophobicity increased in both isolates post‐fermentation, net surface charge significantly increased only for the pea protein isolate. Although increased surface hydrophobicity may promote protein–protein hydrophobic interactions that reduce solubility, moderate exposure of previously buried hydrophobic regions during SSF may indicate partial unfolding, which can increase molecular flexibility and contribute to increased solubility when accompanied by adequate surface charge (R. Wang et al. 2023). Similarly, Stone et al. (2024) found that SSF (48 h, 30–37°C) increased the solubility (at pH 7) of protein isolates of chickpea (type not specified), lentils, and faba bean from 10%–20% to 22%–53% depending on the microbial species (A. oryzae, A. niger, and Lp. plantarum). Lp. plantarum produced a greater degree of protein hydrolysis (up to 15%), resulting in smaller peptides, a greater hydratable surface area, and increased surface charge, giving maximum solubility. However, the molecular structure of protein and consequently its solubility are highly dependent on solvent pH and ionic strength, although most SSF studies report solubility only under a single fixed condition. SSF of soybean meal by Monascus for 21 days at 30°C demonstrated improved solubility across both acidic (pH 3–5) and neutral–alkaline (pH 7–9) conditions (Lim et al. 2010). At pH 3–5, the solubility of fermented proteins was even higher than that of raw soybean proteins since the peptide mixture does not share a single isoelectric point, preventing a sharp solubility minimum. This may confer greater stability to acidic beverages.

Conversely, certain studies have reported decreased protein solubility following SSF, although the lack of multi‐scale structural characterization limits mechanistic interpretation of these findings. For example, Kumitch et al. (2020) reported that SSF of pea protein concentrate by A. niger and A. oryzae (2–6 h, 40°C) reduced nitrogen solubility at pH 3, 5, and 7 by 24%–38%, 6%–16%, and 7%–17%, respectively. Similar observations were reported by Batbayar et al. (2023), who found a slight reduction in solubility (at pH 4 and 7) of pea protein concentrate fermented (48–72 h, 30°C) with fungi (A. oryzae, R. oryzae, and R. oligosporus) and bacteria (Lp. plantarum and B. subtilis), with no significant differences among the microbial species. Interestingly, the reduced solubility observed in both studies occurred despite an increase in surface charge, reduction in surface hydrophobicity, and decrease in molecular weight, all of which would typically favor protein solubility. This consistency suggests that microstructural changes, particularly irreversible protein aggregation, may have contributed substantially to the observed reductions in solubility by reducing protein dispersibility and outweighing the positive effects of these molecular modifications. However, protein aggregation was not evaluated in either study, preventing definitive mechanistic conclusions. It can be hypothesized that during SSF, organic acid production lowers the pH and shifts the system toward the isoelectric point. At the isoelectric point, reduced net charge minimizes electrostatic repulsion, allowing attractive hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions to dominate, resulting in protein aggregation and decreased solubility (J. Zhang et al. 2022).

Overall, SSF generally improves legume protein solubility through disruption of protein aggregates and molecular‐scale modifications that favor protein–water interactions over protein–protein interactions. However, when hydrophobic groups exposed promote protein aggregation near the isoelectric point, reductions in solubility may occur. Enhanced solubility expands the application potential of fermented legume proteins, particularly in beverage systems, where improved dispersion reduces haziness, enhances mouthfeel, and contributes to storage stability (Tripathi et al. 2026). Future studies should evaluate protein solubility across a broader range of pH and ionic strength conditions to better reflect its performance in diverse food applications. Solubility outcomes should be supported by comprehensive structural characterization across multiple length scales. In addition, substantial variability exists in analytical methods (e.g., Dumas, Lowry, Bradford), experimental conditions (e.g., centrifugation parameters) and reporting metrics (nitrogen and protein solubility) used for solubility (J. Zhu, Lu et al. 2024; Kumitch et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2023). Standardization of protocols would enable more reliable comparison of solubility outcomes across studies.

Water and Oil Binding Abilities

Water holding capacity (WHC) and oil holding capacity (OHC) reflect the ability of legume matrices to absorb and retain water or oil against external forces. They are critical for texture, mouthfeel, and structural stability in food applications such as plant‐based cheese and meat and bakery products (Ma et al. 2022). SSF has been shown to improve the water‐ and oil‐holding capacity of legumes (Table 1; Gautheron et al. 2024; C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). Figure 3 illustrates the underlying multi‐scale structural transformations.

The evaluation of cellular structure contributions to WHC and OHC remains limited across existing SSF studies, likely because conventional milling used to produce powdery ingredients extensively disrupts cellular organization. However, this becomes relevant in coarsely milled flour and wet‐fractionated cellular flours containing clusters of intact or partially disrupted cells and their fragments (Coda et al. 2015; Badia‐Olmos et al. 2024). In such systems, breakdown of cellular structures during SSF may relieve physical constraints on protein hydration and starch swelling, thereby enhancing WHC (Noordraven et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2023).

At the microstructural scale, modifications in protein aggregates and starch granules further influence WHC and OHC. While SSF is often associated with disruption of protein aggregates, it may also promote aggregation through pH shifts toward the isoelectric point and partial protein unfolding (Gautheron et al. 2024). The resulting aggregates can form irregular particulate networks with capillary spaces capable of entrapping water and oil. For example, SSF of faba bean by R. oligosporus (48 h, 28°C) and A. oryzae (72 h, 30°C) increased WHC and OHC alongside increased particle size and reduced protein solubility, suggesting that protein aggregation contributed to enhanced liquid retention (Gautheron et al. 2024). Similarly, Guraya et al. (2025) found that acid‐precipitated protein isolates obtained from A. niger‐fermented (96 h, 35°C) yellow pea flour exhibited substantially higher WHC and OHC under acidic conditions, consistent with enhanced protein aggregation near the isoelectric point. Although Xing et al. (2020) did not characterize the microstructural changes underlying the increased WHC of chickpea (kabuli) flour and dry‐fractionated starch‐ and protein‐rich fractions following SSF with Pediococcus pentosaceus and Pediococcus acidilactici (72 h, 37°C), the consistent improvement in WHC across substrates with different C/N ratios suggests that similar structural modifications may occur and that these effects are not limited to fungal but also extend to bacterial SSF systems. Moreover, the reduction in pH to approximately 4.5 across all substrates suggests the possibility of protein aggregation, although the latter is unlikely to be the sole determinant of water and oil binding. This is supported by N. D. Patil, Kumar et al. (2025), who reported that SSF of chickpea (type not specified) protein isolate by A. awamori (96 h, 30°C) reduced aggregate size while still increasing both WHC and OHC. Instead, increased protein content together with modulated molecular‐scale surface properties of proteins may also contribute to enhanced liquid retention. Such differences among studies may partly reflect the distinct endogenous structural characteristics of different legume substrates, which influence their response to SSF. In addition to protein restructuring, enzymatic erosion of starch granules during SSF may increase surface porosity and facilitate water penetration and swelling. Although not confirmed in legumes, increased starch porosity during spontaneous fermentation of pearl millet was associated with improved WHC (Adebiyi et al. 2016). Moreover, fungal mycelium, whose microstructure is characterized by thin and elongated networks, may physically entrap water and oil within fermented matrices (Gautheron et al. 2024).

Beyond microstructural changes, molecular structure modifications such as protein unfolding and controlled hydrolysis modify the balance between surface‐exposed hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues, directly influencing water and oil binding affinity. For instance, two‐stage SSF (87 h, 55°C) of soybean meal by B. licheniformis increased WHC and OHC following degradation of high‐molecular‐weight proteins into shorter peptides exhibiting elevated surface charge and hydrophobicity without excessive aggregation at the microscale (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). Similar trends were reported for A. awamori‐fermented kidney bean flour (96 h, 30°C) and chickpea (type not specified) protein isolate (96 h, 30°C; N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025, N. D. Patil, Kumar et al. 2025). These observations are further supported by Batbayar et al. (2023), who reported that SSF by different fungal (A. oryzae, R. oryzae, and R. oligosporus) and bacterial (Lp. plantarum and B. subtilis) species produced a comparable increase in WHC and OHC when the degree of protein hydrolysis reached 10%. These changes were accompanied by a similar increase in zeta potential and decrease in surface hydrophobicity. Together, these findings suggest that the extent of protein hydrolysis has a greater influence on molecular surface properties and water‐ and oil‐binding capacity than the choice of fermenting microorganism. In addition to protein modifications, conversion of insoluble to soluble dietary fiber during SSF may improve WHC, as observed in R. oligosporus‐ and A. oryzae‐fermented faba bean flour (Gautheron et al. 2024).

Overall, WHC and OHC generally increase after SSF due to multi‐scale structural modifications in legumes. While many studies (Gautheron et al. 2024; C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023; N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025; Khorsandi et al. 2024; Kumitch et al. 2020) report the effect of process variables on WHC and OHC, these provide only partial insight into the structure of legume components. Molecular structure of proteins, characterized by surface hydrophobicity and charge, should be interpreted together with protein aggregate microstructure determined by particle size analysis and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to adequately justify observed changes in WHC and OHC. Moreover, the contribution of non‐protein compounds such as starch, fiber, microbial exopolysaccharides, and mycelial structures at micro and molecular scales to water and oil binding must be elucidated, particularly as the industry transitions toward minimally refined legume ingredients.

Emulsion Properties

Emulsions are colloidal systems of two immiscible liquids (oil and water) widely used in sauces, dressings, spreads, cheese, and bakery products. Amphiphilic legume proteins adsorb at the oil–water interface, reducing interfacial tension and aiding emulsion formation and stability (Ma et al. 2022). Proteins with low molecular weight, high solubility, surface hydrophobicity, and conformational flexibility show superior emulsifying properties (McClements et al. 2022). Although legume proteins do not meet many of those criteria and generally exhibit poorer emulsifying performance than animal proteins, SSF‐induced molecular and microstructural modifications can improve their emulsion properties (Table 1).

Breakdown of large protein aggregates at the microscale increases protein solubility and diffusion toward the oil–water interface, enabling more rapid adsorption and formation of a continuous interfacial layer around oil droplets (Vogelsang‐O'Dwyer et al. 2022, Vogelsang‐O'Dwyer et al. 2023). This improved interfacial coverage contributes to the development of stronger viscoelastic films that resist droplet coalescence and enhance emulsion stability. Reduced particle size from 504 to 56 nm in B. licheniformis–fermented soybean meal significantly improved emulsion activity and stability, despite the lower viscosity that is generally less favorable for stability (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). Similarly, SSF of peanut proteins by co‐culture of B. natto and Lp. plantarum (36 h, 37°C) produced peptides with greater emulsifying ability than those produced with a monoculture SSF due to their relatively larger particle size reduction (Pi et al. 2022).

In systems where proteins have not aggregated extensively, SSF‐induced molecular modifications strongly influence emulsion properties. Low‐molecular‐weight peptides (< 20 kDa) formed during SSF of peanut protein by B. natto and Lp. plantarum could exhibit increased diffusion toward the oil–water interface, while higher surface hydrophobicity enhances interfacial adsorption, together improving emulsifying ability (Pi et al. 2022). The conversion of highly ordered β‐sheet structures into less ordered α‐helix increases conformational flexibility, allowing better rearrangement and stronger anchoring at the interface (Pi et al. 2022). Higher surface charge of peptides in A. awamori‐fermented kidney bean flour resulted in improved emulsion stability due to electrostatic repulsion between droplets, which reduces droplet aggregation and coalescence during storage (N. D. Patil, Bains et al. 2025). Microbial exopolysaccharides produced during SSF may enhance continuous‐phase viscosity and provide steric hindrance against droplet coalescence. However, their specific contribution in SSF‐derived pulse ingredients remains unexplored.

Conversely, reduced emulsion ability and stability have also been reported. Gautheron et al. (2024) observed a seven‐fold increase in protein aggregate size in faba bean flour after SSF with R. oligosporus (48 h, 28°C) and A. oryzae (72 h, 30°C), which was associated with faster phase separation of emulsions stabilized by these aggregates. However, protein aggregates may not always be detrimental to emulsion properties, as appropriately sized aggregates can enhance stability by acting as soft Pickering‐type particles that adsorb irreversibly at the oil–water interface, forming a strong physical barrier against droplet coalescence (Chang et al. 2023). Khorsandi et al. (2024) reported reduced emulsion stability following extensive hydrolysis at longer fermentation times, which may be related to the generation of very short peptides with insufficient chain length and suboptimal distribution of hydrophobic residues required for effective interfacial adsorption and film formation. In addition, starch degradation‐induced reductions in continuous‐phase viscosity may further compromise emulsion stability.

Overall, SSF generally improves emulsifying ability and emulsion stability through microstructural disruption of protein aggregates, which enhances protein diffusion and interfacial coverage, and molecular‐scale modifications that increase protein flexibility, interfacial adsorption, and droplet–droplet repulsion. However, excessive aggregation or hydrolysis of proteins can impair the formation of stable interfacial films and reduce emulsion stability. As most studies (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023; Pi et al. 2022; Khorsandi et al. 2024; Toor et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2025; Stone et al. 2024) have not reported emulsion droplet size distribution or microstructural characteristics, the mechanisms of destabilization remain unclear, and emulsion properties do not always correlate solely with protein characteristics. Future research should examine interfacial rheology and emulsion microstructure to clarify differences in interfacial viscoelasticity and film strength. In addition, non‐protein components such as starch, soluble fibers, microbial polysaccharides, phenolics, saponins, and phospholipids may influence emulsion properties by modifying viscosity, steric interactions, and interfacial behavior. The impact of their structural changes during SSF on emulsion characteristics warrants further investigation.

Foaming Properties

Foaming capacity refers to the ability of ingredients to incorporate air into an aqueous medium and is determined by the surface area generated during whipping or stirring (Ma et al. 2022). It is important in aerated foods such as bakery products, whipped cream, ice cream, and related desserts. Similar to emulsifying ability, foaming requires proteins with high solubility and adequate surface hydrophobicity. Foaming stability represents the retained foam volume over time and depends on interfacial and bulk rheology of the continuous phase (Ma et al. 2022). SSF can improve foaming properties (Table 1) of legumes through multi‐scale structural modifications (Figure 3).

When cellular structures remain intact in pulse ingredients, they may act as physical barriers that limit the solubilization and diffusion of surface‐active proteins toward the air–water interface. Badia‐Olmos et al. (2024) reported that P. ostreatus fermentation (14 days, 28°C) of lentil flour failed to disrupt intact cells, resulting in nearly negligible foaming capacity, compared to unfermented fine‐milled flour, where cell rupture allowed protein release and diffusion. These findings suggest that with appropriate microbial strains and processing conditions, SSF‐induced cellular disintegration may enhance foaming ability.

At the microstructural level, breakdown of protein aggregates enhances solubility and diffusion to the air–water surface. N. D. Patil, Bains et al. (2025) observed increased foaming capacity and stability in kidney bean flour after SSF with A. awamori due to reduced aggregate particle size. A similar inverse relationship between particle size and foaming capacity was reported for soybean meal fermented by B. licheniformis (87 h, 60°C) and B. natto and Lp. plantarum‐fermented (36 h, 37°C) peanut proteins (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). From a molecular perspective, reduced molecular weight and increased surface hydrophobicity of fermented soybean meal could have favored rapid diffusion of soluble proteins or peptides to the air–water interface, lowering surface tension (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). Similar structural changes in A. oryzae‐fermented (48 h, 30°C) pea protein isolate and B. natto‐ and Lp. plantarum‐ fermented (36 h, 37°C) peanut proteins resulted in increased foaming capacity and stability (Khorsandi et al. 2024; Pi et al. 2022). The shift from rigid β‐sheet structures toward more flexible α‐helix and β‐turn conformations in fermented peanut proteins may have facilitated protein rearrangement at the air–water interface, further enhancing foaming properties (Pi et al. 2022).

In contrast, reduced foaming properties have been reported due to opposing structural modifications. Gautheron et al. (2024) observed decreased foaming capacity and stability after SSF with R. oligosporus (28°C, 72 h), attributed to microscale protein aggregation that increased particle size and reduced solubility. Although SSF of soybean meal by B. licheniformis improved foaming capacity, stability declined, likely due to reduced viscosity from polysaccharide and protein breakdown, which promotes liquid drainage from lamellae (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023). Since foams are more sensitive to structural changes than emulsions, an increased negative surface charge after SSF may have enhanced electrostatic repulsion between adsorbed proteins, weakening lamellar cohesion within the thin liquid lamellae separating air bubbles and reducing stability (C. Dai, Yan et al. 2023).

Overall, SSF‐induced multi‐scale structural modifications have been shown to enhance foaming capacity and stability in several studies. Future research should systematically evaluate interfacial properties, including surface tension, adsorption load, and interfacial film viscoelasticity, of foams stabilized by fermented ingredients to better elucidate structure–function relationships. In addition, microbial exopolysaccharides may form soluble conjugates with hydrolyzed proteins, potentially contributing to improved foaming properties, and this mechanism requires further investigation.

Thermal Properties

Thermal transitions of legumes reflect stability to heating and the minimum temperatures for starch gelatinization and protein denaturation (Elgadir et al. 2009). Multi‐scale structural changes brought by SSF could modulate thermal properties typically characterized using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).

Cellular structures act as barriers to heat and mass transfer, limiting water diffusion and starch swelling. SSF‐mediated disruption of cell walls and release of starch granules may increase water accessibility, lowering gelatinization temperature and enthalpy (Noordraven et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2023). At the microstructural scale, the effect of SSF on thermal transitions depends on the extent of starch degradation and structural reorganization. For example, SSF of black gram flour by S. cerevisiae (72 h, 30°C) and Lactobacillus spp. (48 h, 30°C) reduced onset gelatinization temperature and enthalpy owing to starch granule disruption and enhanced water absorption, although the magnitude of change depended on heat pretreatment and microbial strain (Ali et al. 2022). In contrast, SSF of cowpea flour by lactic acid bacteria consortia (48 h, 35°C) increased the onset gelatinization temperature from 70°C to 82°C, while simultaneously reducing gelatinization enthalpy, suggesting selective disruption of amorphous regions together with reorganization of ordered starch domains (Kewuyemi et al. 2024). These observations indicate that SSF may either increase or decrease gelatinization temperature depending on the balance between starch granule degradation and structural reorganization. The influence of processing conditions is further demonstrated by SSF of mung beans by B. natto (6 h, 37°C), where thermal transitions depended on the type of solid‐state bioreactor and agitation mode. Unlike static and shaking‐flask SSF, fermentation in a soft elastic tubular reactor (SETR) reduced onset (47°C to 33°C) and peak gelatinization temperatures because of combined mechanical disruption of starch granules and enzyme‐induced defects within crystalline domains, despite an increase in relative crystallinity (Lyu et al. 2024). At the molecular scale, amylolytic depolymerization of starch molecules lowers gelatinization enthalpy. Likewise, protein unfolding and controlled proteolysis during SSF may decrease protein denaturation temperature and enthalpy, thereby benefiting gel‐based systems such as plant‐based cheese.

Overall, SSF alters thermal properties through multi‐scale structural reorganization, although studies remain limited. Overlapping starch and protein endotherms complicate interpretation in minimally refined flours. Optimizing fermentation time and enzyme activity can tailor thermal transitions for specific applications, such as lower temperatures for cheese analogues or higher stability for clear protein beverages.

Gelation Properties

Gelation of fermented legume ingredients depends on the formation of three‐dimensional networks that immobilize water (Figure 3). The least gelation concentration (LGC) and gel strength are governed by multi‐scale protein structure and environmental factors (pH, temperature, ionic strength), while starch and cell wall polysaccharides also contribute to flour systems (Hui et al. 2025).

At the cellular scale, intact cell walls restrict hydration, mobility, and intermolecular interaction of proteins. SSF of faba bean flour by P. ostreatus did not disrupt cells and resulted in a high LGC (18%), compared with 8% in dry‐milled flour where cell rupture enabled better interaction of free biopolymers (Badia‐Olmos et al. 2024). Nevertheless, with appropriate strain selection and process optimization, gelation could be improved.

At the microstructural level, controlled aggregation plays a central role in determining gelation behavior. SSF of faba bean flour by A. oryzae and R. oligosporus reduced LGC from 24% to 17%–18% protein, associated with increased aggregate size and reduced solubility (Gautheron et al. 2024). Fungal mycelial networks observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were stated to further contribute to space‐spanning structures. Gel strength and transparency depend not only on the amount of protein aggregates but also on specific aggregate structures, including linear, branched, or fractal morphologies. Chickpea (type not specified) protein isolate fermented by P. pentosaceus formed a more defined porous network of aggregates with a higher storage modulus, though porosity may increase syneresis (Zhang et al. 2025).

At the molecular scale, aggregation during SSF is driven by protein unfolding, moderate proteolysis, enhanced hydrophobic interactions, and exposure of sulfhydryl groups that promote disulfide bond formation, strengthening gel networks. SSF of chickpea (type not specified) protein isolate by P. pentosaceus reduced the LGC from 14% to 8% and the critical gelling temperature from 87°C to 62°C, while significantly increasing storage modulus and resistance to deformation (Zhang et al. 2025). These improvements were associated with elevated free sulfhydryl groups, partial hydrolysis of high‐molecular‐weight proteins, and increased flexibility of protein secondary structure. Similarly, cowpea flour fermented by a lactic acid bacteria consortium showed earlier development of elevated storage moduli during heating, higher storage moduli throughout thermal treatment than untreated cowpea flour, and reduced loss tangent, indicating improved viscoelastic properties (Kewuyemi et al. 2024). These changes were attributed to increased low‐molecular‐weight proteins and their interactions with starch. High‐amylose starch capable of retrogradation upon cooling may further contribute to gel strengthening. Although microbial exopolysaccharides and pectin modifications may influence gelation (Xu et al. 2019), their specific contributions in SSF systems remain unclear.

In contrast, excessive protein hydrolysis can significantly impair gel formation. SSF of pea protein isolate for 72 h by various bacteria (Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus lactis, Lactobacillus delbrueckii, Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides, P. pentosaceus, Weissella confusa, Tetragenococcus halophilus), yeasts (S. cerevisiae, Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, Kluyveromyces marxianus), and A. oryzae significantly reduced the elastic and viscous moduli of heat‐set gels (Shi et al. 2025). Although molecular weight distribution was not reported, the DH (5%–15%) suggests substantial peptide bond cleavage and formation of shorter peptide fractions, which likely weakens the protein network required for gel structuring.

Overall, SSF‐induced structural modifications show strong potential to tailor gelling characteristics of legume ingredients by reducing LGC and enhancing gel strength. However, excessive proteolysis may impair gel formation, although a lower gelling tendency may benefit high‐protein clarified beverages subjected to heat treatment. Compared with other techno‐functional attributes, the gelling behavior of SSF‐derived legumes, particularly rheological properties, remains underexplored, and the potential for cold gelation via pH modification has not been investigated. Future studies should integrate gel rheology with microstructural analysis (CLSM, SEM) to relate network structure and component distribution to multi‐scale SSF‐induced transformations. As large‐deformation properties such as fracture behavior correlate more closely with texture than small‐deformation rheology, texture profile analysis should complement rheological measurements to better predict performance in foods such as extrudates and plant‐based cheese.

Pasting Properties

Pasting properties describe changes in the viscosity of fermented flour suspensions during heating and cooling, reflecting starch swelling, gelatinization and granular breakdown, while interactions with proteins and soluble fiber may also contribute to these changes. SSF‐induced multi‐scale structural modifications in legumes markedly alter pasting profiles (Yan et al. 2025).

Intact cellular structures in legume ingredients restrict starch hydration, granule swelling, and amylose leaching, reducing paste viscosity (Noordraven et al. 2021). SSF‐induced cellular disintegration may enhance viscosity by releasing starch from cells and protein matrices, although this effect has not been clearly elucidated.

At the microscale, moderate pore formation in starch granules through amylolytic erosion enhances hydration and swelling, although weakening of granules results in premature breakdown, decreasing viscosity upon shearing and heating. Nevertheless, some SSF studies have reported an increased peak viscosity. Cowpea flour fermented (48 h, 35°C) with a lactic acid bacteria consortium showed increased peak and final viscosities (Kewuyemi et al. 2024). Similarly, SSF of raw and pre‐gelatinized black gram flour by S. cerevisiae and Lactobacillus spp. (72 h, 30°C) increased peak and final viscosities (Ali et al. 2022). While this response may not be attributed to starch erosion alone, microbial exopolysaccharides, conversion of insoluble to water‐binding soluble fibers, and enhanced starch swelling may have collectively contributed to the observed increase in viscosity.

At the molecular level, changes in amylose–amylopectin composition strongly influence pasting behavior. Spontaneous SSF of germinated pigeon pea flour (24–48 h) significantly reduced peak, final, breakdown, and setback viscosities (Sobowale et al. 2024). Although the amylopectin proportion increased after SSF due to preferential hydrolysis of amylose‐rich amorphous regions, depolymerization of amylopectin likely contributed to reduced viscosity. Decreased amylose content (28% to 18%) was associated with lower setback viscosity, reflecting limited gelation during cooling. This indicates a reduced tendency for starch retrogradation, which could potentially mitigate staling in baked products and syneresis in frozen or chilled foods, although such effects were not assessed during storage. Similarly, starch isolated from R. oligosporus‐fermented soybean flour (12–72 h, 32°C) showed progressive reductions in peak, final, breakdown, and setback viscosities with decreasing amylose content (27% to 14%), confirming that starch degradation, rather than protein changes, governed the viscosity reduction (Olanipekun et al. 2009).

Overall, SSF‐induced multi‐scale structural modifications demonstrate strong potential to modulate pasting properties. Higher viscosity may benefit bakery products, sauces, and dressings, whereas lower viscosity is desirable in beverages and foods formulated for elderly individuals with swallowing difficulties. However, studies linking multi‐scale structural transformations during SSF to pasting behavior remain limited, and the contribution of fungal exopolysaccharides to viscosity development upon hydrothermal treatments requires further investigation.

Challenges and Industrial Perspectives

Although SSF has considerable potential for tailoring the techno‐functional properties of legume ingredients, several challenges must be addressed for industrial implementation. Process monitoring and control remain major hurdles due to the heterogeneous nature of solid substrates. Inherent local variations in moisture, oxygen, nutrient availability, and temperature cause batch‐to‐batch variability in microbial activity, structural modifications, and ingredient functionality (Jin et al. 2026). Improved reproducibility of fermentation outcomes requires real‐time monitoring of key process parameters and spatio‐temporal heterogeneity through direct sensing approaches, including thermistors, capacitive sensors, respirometry systems, pressure‐drop meters, and gas sensors, as well as non‐invasive approaches such as hyperspectral imaging, thermal imaging, and infrared spectroscopy, coupled with predictive modeling (Bellon‐Maurel et al. 2003; C. Dai, Xu et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2016).

Besides process monitoring, appropriate bioreactor designs are important to allow an effective process control. Tray bioreactors and incubation rooms remain widely used for commercial SSF products like tempeh and koji because of their simplicity and low cost, although limitations in aeration, heat transfer, and scalability restrict large‐scale application (Krishania et al. 2026). Rotating drum reactors with intermittent agitation and improved packed‐bed systems have received increasing attention for enhancing aeration, heat removal, mixing, and product yields (Mitchell et al. 2023). Evaporative cooling can alleviate heat dissipation challenges, while intermittent drum rotation, which improves heat transfer and mixing, causes less damage to fungal mycelia than continuous agitation (Arora et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the complexity of SSF, particularly in fungal systems, necessitates greater integration of computational modeling, to optimize reactor design and operating conditions for specific legumes, microorganisms, and targeted functionalities.

Microbial strain selection represents another critical consideration. Different microorganisms, and even different strains within the same species, can exhibit substantial variation in growth kinetics, enzyme secretion profiles, and metabolic activity (Xing et al. 2020). Autochthonous strains isolated from legumes may offer advantages through an improved adaptation to the substrate matrix and shorter lag phases, while co‐culture systems combining complementary enzymatic activities may enable more targeted structural modifications (Xing et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2025). However, systematic strain screening and genomic or transcriptomic characterization remain resource‐intensive, limiting rapid industrial optimizations (Banks et al. 2022).

Microbiological safety and regulatory compliance are important aspects for SSF‐derived food ingredients. Commonly used microorganisms, including lactic acid bacteria, yeasts such as S. cerevisiae, and fungi such as A. oryzae, R. oligosporus, and R. oryzae, have established histories of safe use in foods, with several species and strains classified as GRAS (generally recognized as safe; Chai and Chen 2025). Nevertheless, exclusion of toxin‐producing fungal strains (i.e., certain variants of A. niger and Fusarium spp.) and routine monitoring for potential mycotoxin contamination is essential (Tian et al. 2022). The solid‐state substrate with limited free water can restrict the growth of many pathogens, while starter culture dominance can further inhibit undesirable microorganisms through the production of organic acids and antimicrobial compounds (Álvarez et al. 2025). However, additional contamination risks may arise during substrate preparation for SSF. For example, acidification or the intentional use of lactic acid bacteria during legume soaking, as often recommended in tempeh production, can suppress undesirable microbial growth (Ahnan‐Winarno et al. 2021). In general, adoption of good manufacturing practices (GMP), hazard analysis and critical control point (HACCP) systems, and robust quality assurance protocols will be critical for ensuring product safety and regulatory acceptance (Mukherjee et al. 2022). Existing safety evaluation frameworks such as the GRAS system of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) framework established by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) provide structured approaches for evaluating microorganisms intended for food applications, thereby expediting the authorization and licensing of strains and supporting consumer assurance of product safety (Niyigaba et al. 2025). However, differences between jurisdictions, limited SSF‐specific guidance, and the absence of harmonized validation procedures for microbial identity, fermentation processes, and final product safety create challenges for ingredient commercialization (Mukherjee et al. 2022).

From an economic perspective, SSF offers several advantages, including low water requirements, reduced wastewater generation, lower chemical consumption, and the opportunity to valorize low‐value legumes and agricultural side streams (Krishna 2005). However, the economic viability of large‐scale SSF depends strongly on capital and operational costs associated with bioreactor and downstream processing requirements (e.g., sterilization, drying, and milling) needed to obtain ingredients with consistent quality (Arora et al. 2018). Integrated bioreactor designs that combine multiple functions, such as sterilization, mixing, fermentation, and drying, may help reduce expenditure, particularly for small‐ and medium‐scale enterprises (Arora et al. 2018). Since SSF is a relatively slow process, often requiring 3 to 10 days depending on the microorganism and substrate, and is commonly operated in batch or semi‐continuous configurations, productivity may be lower and labor requirements higher, compared with conventional legume processing approaches (Liu et al. 2004). As SSF studies for legume ingredient production remain largely at laboratory scale, pilot‐scale demonstrations supported by life‐cycle assessment and techno‐economic analysis are required to establish commercial feasibility (Cano‐González et al. 2026).

Future Directions

Designing legume ingredients with enhanced techno‐functional properties for targeted food applications requires understanding, establishing, and steering process–structure–function relationships in SSF. This necessitates a comprehensive characterization of the multi‐scale structural changes underlying techno‐functional properties, which remains insufficiently addressed in current studies. Fungal hyphal penetration into the legume matrix, gene expression, and enzyme synthesis are fundamental mechanisms driving structural modifications across scales. Characterizing these mechanisms enables process optimization based on mechanistic understanding rather than purely empirical relationships. The effects of these mechanisms on structural transformations, in relation to the initial endogenous structural integrity of the legume substrate matrix, particle size, microbial species, mono‐ versus co‐culture systems, incubation temperature, and fermentation time must be systematically elucidated.

Multi‐scale characterization of fermented legume structure requires integrated qualitative and quantitative analytical approaches. Cellular structural changes and spatio‐temporal hyphal distribution within the legume matrix can be assessed using advanced techniques such as X‐ray tomography, hyperspectral imaging, and three‐dimensional fluorescence imaging. The microstructure of protein bodies and starch granules should be examined using CLSM, SEM, X‐ray diffraction, and light scattering methods, while molecular structures of legume components (e.g., pectin, starch, protein), fungal exopolysaccharides, and metabolites should be characterized using chromatography, FT‐IR, and related physicochemical techniques. These approaches should be complemented with multi‐omics analyses and enzyme activity profiling to elucidate metabolic pathways governing SSF.

As these analyses generate extensive datasets, quantitative linkage between structural descriptors and techno‐functional outcomes becomes imperative. The structural insights of the fermented ingredient cannot be meaningfully linked to functional properties without simultaneously accounting for the structural characteristics of the model systems tested (e.g., gels, emulsions, foams) under relevant conditions such as pH and temperature. Therefore, the micro‐ and meso‐structure of these model systems should also be characterized using appropriate analytical techniques. Even qualitative 2D or 3D structural images must be subjected to systematic feature extraction and refinement through image analysis, followed by quantitative modeling to discriminate structural differences across samples.

Given the complexity and volume of data generated, reliance on empirical trial‐and‐error optimization is inadequate. A systematic transition toward data‐driven predictive modeling is required, supported by robust databases built on quantitative process–structure–function understanding. In such frameworks, SSF process variables serve as inputs, microbial growth kinetics, enzyme activities, and multi‐scale structural descriptors function as intermediate variables, and techno‐functional properties are defined as outputs. Machine learning approaches, including artificial neural networks, support vector machines, and ensemble methods, can capture nonlinear interactions among variables (De Farias Silva et al. 2022; Du et al. 2026). These predictive models can enable reverse engineering of SSF conditions to achieve targeted techno‐functional attributes tailored to specific food applications. To further strengthen predictive capability, these data‐driven approaches should be coupled with physics‐based modeling frameworks that explicitly account for mass and heat transfer phenomena within SSF systems. Incorporating mechanistic descriptions of diffusion, moisture migration, thermal gradients, and substrate heterogeneity will enable models to characterize and predict the spatial and temporal evolution of the fermentation environment. Such hybrid (physics–ML) models can bridge molecular‐scale transformations, for example, enzymatic reactions and metabolite formation, with macro‐scale performance, stability, and functionality.

Conclusion

This review highlighted SSF as a sustainable and versatile strategy for improving the functionality of legume‐based ingredients through controlled multi‐scale structural modification. At the cellular level, SSF disrupts cotyledon cell integrity, and the consequent liberation of intracellular starch and proteins facilitates improved hydration and solubility. At the microstructural level, breakdown of protein aggregates, surface erosion of starch granules, and alterations in crystalline arrangement contribute to improved solubility, enhanced interfacial properties (foaming and emulsification), and altered pasting behavior. Although pH reduction to the isoelectric point and protein unfolding during SSF may sometimes promote aggregation depending on substrate and process conditions, such aggregation can still be advantageous for gelation and water‐holding properties.

At the molecular level, proteolysis, modifications in secondary and tertiary protein structure that define surface characteristics, starch depolymerization, and changes in the amylose‐to‐amylopectin ratio collectively govern functional outcomes. These structural changes are interdependent, and their dynamics vary with legume type, microbial strain, and fermentation parameters such as incubation time and temperature. However, no single SSF condition can optimize all techno‐functional attributes simultaneously, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of process–structure–function relationships. In this context, artificial intelligence, physics‐based and data‐driven tools, integrated with advanced spatio‐temporal structural characterization and multi‐omics approaches, offer strong potential to unravel these complex interactions. Despite the rapid growth of SSF research, systematic insight into multi‐scale structural evolution, fungal penetration, and enzymatic synthesis driving structural reorganization in legumes remains limited. Addressing these knowledge gaps will enable improved control of SSF processes and support the rational design of functional legume‐based ingredients.
