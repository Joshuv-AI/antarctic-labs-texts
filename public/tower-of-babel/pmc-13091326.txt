Initiating Care for Low Back Pain in the Emergency Department of the Veterans Health Administration, Impact on the Location of Subsequent Visits
Authors: Charles W Penza, Brian C Coleman, Erica A Abel, Cynthia A Brandt, Anthony J Lisi, Tessa Runels, Andrew K Flack, Alcia Williams, Joseph L Goulet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOur study objective was to evaluate the location and timing of subsequent Veterans Health Administration care for low back pain (LBP) based on the initial clinic. Veterans can initiate care for LBP through different entry points such as primary care, emergency department (ED), urgent care, or other service line clinics. We examined how different starting points impacted the location and timing of the next LBP encounter.MethodsVeteran Affairs (VA) electronic health records were used to identify veterans with an initial visit for LBP, defined as a visit with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) LBP-related code after a 365-day period without such a visit, between October 1, 2015, and September 30, 2016. Primary and secondary clinic stop codes were used to identify the initial entry point and stratify the clinics into 3 groups: ED/urgent care, primary care, and all other service lines grouped into “other.” The location of the first subsequent visit within 365 days with an ICD-10 code for LBP, if any, was similarly identified. Logistic regression was used to assess the impact of entry point on the location of the first subsequent LBP visit and identify demographic and clinical characteristics of veterans whose first subsequent LBP visit was in the ED/urgent care clinic.ResultsWe identified 55,612 veterans, 88% of whom were male, 61% of whom identified as White, 54% of whom were married, and 9% of whom had co-occurring neck pain, who presented to VA care settings for an initial visit for LBP. Of those, 44,956 (80.8%) were initially seen in primary care, 4652 (8.4%) in the ED/urgent care, and 6004 (10.8%) in other clinics. First subsequent LBP visits occurred in primary care clinics (39%), other clinics (37%), no subsequent LBP visit within 365 days (21%), or ED/urgent care clinics (3%). The odds of the first subsequent LBP visit occurring in the ED/urgent care clinics were lower for veterans initially seen in the primary care clinic (odds ratio [OR], 0.12; 95% CI, 0.11-0.14) or other clinics (OR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.09-0.15) compared with veterans initially seen in the ED/urgent care settings. Pain, a history of a serious LBP diagnosis, and prescription of opioids were associated with the first subsequent LBP care in the ED settings.ConclusionIn the VA care setting, 1 in 10 initial LBP visits occur in the ED/urgent care setting. This initial site of care is associated with an 8-fold increase in the odds that the next medical visit for LBP will also occur in the ED/urgent care setting. Future research is needed to explore patient and system factors contributing to the first subsequent LBP care occurring in the ED/urgent care setting after their initial LBP visit.Keywords: veterans, analgesics, opioids, logistic models, low back pain, ambulatory care, emergency service

The Bottom Line

Among veterans seeking care for low back pain in the Veterans Affairs (VA) care system, where they start matters. Most begin in primary care, but about 1 in 10 start in the emergency department (ED) or urgent care setting. Those who start in the ED/urgent care setting are 8 times more likely to return there for their next visit compared with those starting in primary care or other clinics. Pain severity, opioid prescription, and serious back conditions were linked to ED return. However, our exploratory model showed modest ability to explain repeat ED visits, highlighting the need to identify additional factors driving ED recidivism.

Introduction

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is highly prevalent among veterans1 and is 1 of the most common reasons for them to seek medical care.2 Veterans with LBP in the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Healthcare System may choose to begin care through a variety of clinic entry points, including the emergency department (ED),3 In the VA care system nationally, LBP represents 3.8% of ED visits annually,3 compared with 2.3% in non-VA health care settings.4 Despite these encounters, there has been little examination of the impact of entry point on the location and timing of subsequent care. The VA care system provides a unique setting to evaluate the impact of the initial access point into the health care system on subsequent care, as it represents a nationally distributed health care delivery system that serves a diverse, medically complex, and often understudied patient population.5 VA is also a largely “closed system” of care, which allows for detailed tracking of patient pathways and service utilization over time. In addition, the VA care system has a robust integrated electronic health record (EHR), which helps facilitate observational health services research.

Importance

In 2009, the Department of VA adopted and customized the patient-centered medical home model of care as the foundation of its health care delivery system and branded it the Patient Aligned Care Team. Patient-centered medical home models of care feature 3 major principles: patient-centered care, coordination of care, and access to care. Many agencies, including the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, have outlined these models.6 Within VA’s team-based primary care model, there are multiple entry points into the health care system for veterans seeking medical care for LBP. This observational study aimed to provide an overview of the location and timing of subsequent health care services for LBP and the extent to which the initial entry point may have an impact. Understanding system-wide variation in follow-up care can be an important initial step for allocating appropriate resources to ensure care is accessible, timely, and coordinated.

Goals of This Investigation

Our study objectives were 3-fold. First, we aimed to quantify the relative frequency of 3 entry points: (1) ED/urgent care setting, (2) outpatient primary care setting, or (3) all other outpatient services, chosen by veterans who initiate care for LBP in the VA care setting. Second, we aimed to evaluate the influence of the initial point of care on both the timing and setting of the first subsequent LBP encounter within 1 year. Third, we aimed to describe the association between our available demographic and clinical variables and ED recidivism among veterans whose initial LBP encounter occurred in ED/urgent care settings.

Methods

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines7 to report this observational cohort study. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the VA Connecticut Healthcare System.

Study Design, Data Source, and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study with a 12-month observation period. The cohort in this study is a subset of the larger musculoskeletal diagnosis (MSD) cohort, a longitudinal cohort of veterans with musculoskeletal diagnoses who received VA care between January 1, 2000, and September 30, 2017 (n = 7,133,669).8 A detailed description of the MSD cohort has been published previously.1 The following is a brief description of the MSD cohort. Veterans were included on the basis of a search of VA EHR data sources for the presence of at least 1 of a possible 1685 International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Edition (ICD-9) musculoskeletal diagnoses or 27,221 ICD-10 musculoskeletal diagnoses received during ≥2 outpatient visits within 18 months or ≥1 inpatient stay(s).1 Veterans Health Administration, the largest integrated health care system in the United States of patients 18 years old and older, was our study setting. The exposure period to identify the initial location of an LBP visit was October 1, 2015, to September 30, 2016, followed by a 12-month observation period that ended September 30, 2017. The observation period was utilized to identify the timing and location of the first subsequent medical encounter for LBP after the initial (index) visit. This time period corresponds with the first 2 years after the change to the ICD-10 that occurred on October 1, 2015.9 This change resulted in an expansion from 14,000 diagnostic codes in the ICD-9 to nearly 70,000 in ICD-10. To define LBP of musculoskeletal origin, we used ICD-10 diagnostic “M-codes,” which represent “Diseases of the Musculoskeletal System and Connective Tissue.” Codes specific to LBP (M54.x) were selected to represent a medical encounter where the health care provider’s diagnosis is consistent with LBP of musculoskeletal origin.

An LBP visit was defined as an outpatient visit where an ICD-10 diagnostic code, consistent with our definition of LBP of musculoskeletal origin, was present in the EHR corresponding to that specific medical encounter. We defined an initial LBP visit as a medical encounter occurring between October 1, 2015, and September 30, 2016, where no other visit with a corresponding ICD-10 code for LBP occurred in the prior year. A combination of both ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes corresponding to LBP was used to identify whether a medical visit that occurred in our exposure window (ie, from October 1, 2015, to September 30, 2016) had a medical encounter in the preceding year for LBP. Veterans with an outpatient medical encounter meeting our definition of an LBP visit, who were subsequently admitted to the hospital (identified through administrative data and medical coding that differentiate outpatient and inpatient services), were excluded. For example, a medical encounter that occurred in an ED with an ICD-10 M-code used for LBP and a subsequent hospital admission on the same date were removed.

Exposure and Outcomes: Defining Clinic Location, Subsequent LBP Visits, and Timing

The primary outcome variables included the clinic location of the initial LBP visit, the location of the first subsequent LBP visit within 12 months, and the timing of that visit. The type of clinic (location) where the initial outpatient medical encounter with our defined LBP ICD-10 M-code took place was identified using VA administrative primary and secondary clinic Decision Support System identifiers, also known as “stop codes.” Because VA stop code conventions sometimes do not yield precise information on clinic location, a rule-based method was used to conditionally resolve primary and secondary stop codes into a single clinic location. Clinic locations were stratified into “primary care,” “ED/urgent care,” and all other service lines grouped into “other” for the initial LBP visits.

Following the initial LBP visit, the EHR was searched for any additional outpatient medical encounters with our defined ICD-10 M-code over the 12-month observation window. If multiple outpatient medical encounters occurred over the next 12 months, only the first subsequent LBP encounter was included in the analysis. The same method was used to identify the type of clinic (location) where the first subsequent outpatient health care encounter took place. For the first subsequent outpatient LBP visit, clinic locations were stratified into “primary care center,” “ED/urgent care center,” “physical therapy center,” “physical medicine and rehabilitation (PMR&R) physician,” “chiropractic clinic,” “pain clinic,” and all other service lines grouped into “other.” A category, “none,” was used when no additional LBP visits were identified in the 12-month observation period after the initial LBP encounter.

The timing of the first subsequent LBP visit was measured in days from the initial visit and analyzed as a categoric outcome. For categoric analysis, we defined 5 intervals: “1 to 7 days,” “8 to 30 days,” “31 to 90 days,” and “181 to 365 days.” These intervals were selected to reflect clinically meaningful timeframes—ranging from early follow-up (within 1 week) to delayed follow-up (up to 1 year).

Variables

Demographic characteristics

Age, sex (male or female), marital status (married or not), and race/ethnicity were used to describe the sample. Race/ethnicity was based on EHR data from veteran self-reports at VA registration and grouped into non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, or “other race/ethnicity” based on the most frequently reported race. Other races/ethnicities included responses of American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, ≥2 races, unknown by patient, declined to answer, or missing. Sex (male was used as reference), race/ethnicity (White was used as reference), and marital status (not married was used as reference) were included in the logistic models as covariates.

Comorbidities

The presence of certain medical and mental health conditions was identified based on diagnosis codes present in the medical record at any time. Medical and mental health comorbidities, including serious/treatable LBP, neck pain, major depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, traumatic brain injury, alcohol use disorder, and drug use disorder, were identified based on the presence of diagnosis codes in the medical record using previously described methods.1 We defined a subset of LBP diagnosis codes representing serious and/or treatable conditions, such as fracture or spinal infection. These LBP diagnosis codes were viewed as distinct from the ICD-10 M-codes used to define LBP of musculoskeletal origin and were not used when defining an initial or subsequent medical encounter for LBP. In other words, we identified a subset of veterans who had a history of a serious and/or treatable LBP condition.

Pain intensity ratings

The pain intensity numeric rating scale (NRS) is routinely charted in the medical record within the VA care setting. We obtained pain intensity NRS scores when they corresponded to the initial LBP visit, ie, were charted on the same day as the initial LBP visit. When multiple NRS were recorded on the index date, we retained only the highest. An invalid NRS (eg, 99) was considered as missing. We categorized NRS scores into 4 groups: 0, no pain; 1 to 3, mild; 4 to 6, moderate; and ≥7, severe. We reported missing as a separate category.10 Pain intensity ratings were included in the logistic models as a covariate, and the reference group was no pain (NRS = 0).

Body mass index

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on height and weight values recorded in the EHR and categorized into “underweight” (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), “healthy weight” (BMI = 18.5-24.9 kg/m2), “overweight” (BMI = 25.0-30.0 kg/m2), “obese” (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2), or missing.11 BMI was included in the logistic models as a covariate, and the reference group was “healthy weight” (BMI = 18.5-24.9 kg/m2).

Charlson comorbidity index

The Charlson comorbidity index (CCI)12,13 was calculated for all veterans based on the presence of ICD codes appearing in their medical records and categorized into 4 groups (CCI = 0, CCI = 1, CCI = 2, and CCI ≥3). The CCI was included in the logistic models as a covariate, and the reference group was CCI = 0.

Opioid prescription

Opioid use (excluding methadone and/or tramadol use) was categorized based on any prescription filled within 30 days before the index visit, any prescription filled at the index but not in the 30 days prior, and no prescription fills. Opioid use was included in the logistic models as a covariate, and the reference group was no prescription (none).

Statistical Analysis

Three distinct analyses were carried out, 1 analysis for each of the 3 objectives. Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical variables and stratified by initial LBP visit location. For aim 1, frequency statistics were used to describe how often the initial entry point into the health care system took place across the 3 different clinical locations: “ED/urgent care setting,” “primary care setting,” and “other care services.”

For aim 2, frequency statistics were used to describe how often and when the first subsequent visit for LBP occurred in “primary care center”, “physical therapy center,” “chiropractic clinic,” “pain clinic,” “PM&R physician,” “ED/urgent care setting”, “other care settings”, or “none”. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to examine the association between the location of the initial LBP visit (categorized as “ED/urgent care,” “primary care,” or “other” care services) and the adjusted odds that the first subsequent LBP visit occurred in the ED/urgent care setting (yes/no). The outcome was dichotomized as ED/urgent care setting vs all other locations. The reference group for the exposure variable was the ED/urgent care setting. All covariates described above (demographic characteristics, comorbidities, pain intensity, BMI, CCI, and opioid prescription) were included in the model to adjust for potential confounding. Variables were selected based on the authorship team’s perceived clinical relevance and availability within an EHR.

For aim 3, the analysis was exploratory and restricted to veterans whose initial visit occurred in the ED/urgent care setting. A multivariate logistic regression was used to assess the association between demographic and clinical characteristics and the odds of returning to the ED/urgent care setting for the first subsequent LBP visit among veterans whose initial visit was also in the ED/urgent care setting. The outcome was again dichotomized as ED/urgent care setting vs all other locations. Variables were selected based on the authorship team’s perceived potential clinical relevance and availability within an EHR.

All analyses used a complete-case analysis. For variables that were incomplete, such as pain intensity and BMI, a “missing” category was used to retain participants. Logistic regression model fit was assessed using Akaike’s information criterion, Schwarz’s Bayesian information criterion (BIC), −2 log likelihood, likelihood ratio, score, and Wald tests. Model discrimination performance was evaluated using percent concordant/discordant, Somers’ D, and the c statistic (area under the curve [AUC]) values. All analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc), and the visualization was created using R v4.2.214 and the ForestPlot package.

Limitations

Although the MSD cohort is a rich resource of data, there are limitations that are inherent to retrospective analyses using administrative data. First, data are derived from an EHR that was not primarily intended for research and may be subject to the errors inherent in all clinical and administrative data, including missing or miscoding of diagnoses, procedures, and medications. Despite these potential inaccuracies, the study proceeded under the assumption that evaluating healthcare staff who assign M54.x codes do so with the intent of identifying LBP of musculoskeletal origin. This assumption extends to the expectation that serious or treatable LBP conditions (spinal infection, fracture, or disc herniations creating significant neurologic deficits that require emergent treatment) would be accurately reflected in EHR coding, thus maintaining the integrity of the data used in our analysis. In addition, VA care facilities across the nation have acute care settings classified as an ED, whereas others use urgent care designations. Although this reflects an “acute care” setting, this limits the ability to definitively distinguish between the 2 settings from an administrative coding perspective. Second, subsequent LBP-related visits occurring outside the VA care system were not captured. Therefore, the study may underestimate the overall health care utilization for LBP and/or misclassify subjects into the “none” category when care occurred externally. Third, there is the inability to capture a cohort member’s lifetime diagnostic and treatment history for LBP. Although this limits conclusions about the impact of the true initial health care visit, cohort members do represent patients who are seeking care for LBP after a period of time (at least 1 year) without seeking/receiving care. Therefore, these data are still valuable as a resource for understanding systemic variability and utilization patterns across entry points within the VA care system. Fourth, there may be limitations in the generalizability of findings to non-VA care systems. However, the size and variation in the cohort suggest that similar findings might be observed in other populations. The veteran population served by the VA care settings may not be similar to the populations served by other health care systems. For example, the veteran population served by VA care settings has a large disparity in male-female gender and has a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, including mental health and substance use disorders, compared with the US population.15

Discussion

This descriptive study provided an overview of where veterans initiate care for LBP and how that initial entry point influences the timing and setting of subsequent care for the same condition. The results suggest that most veterans’ initial care location was in an outpatient setting, whereas 1 in every 10 veterans’ initial care location was in the ED/urgent care clinics. Roughly 20% of veterans do not seek subsequent care within a year after the initial visit. Only 4% of subsequent visits occur in the ED/urgent care clinics; however, initiating care in the ED/urgent care clinics increased the odds of this occurring by a factor of 8. Roughly 12% (95% CI, 11.4%-13.3%) of veterans, whose initial location of care was the ED/urgent care clinics, returned to the ED clinic within 30 days. Our results are similar to ED clinic recidivism rates of 14% (11%-17%) of patients presenting with LBP published by Friedman et al,16 2012, but lower than the 26% reported by Ginsberg et al,17 2021, in non-VA emergency health care settings. Our 3-month return rate for LBP was slightly lower than the return rates for all conditions published in an older veteran population and multiple urban academic and university-affiliated hospital emergency departments.18 One important factor to consider is that we excluded veterans who presented for treatment within a year of the observation period. This criterion may exclude veterans with high health care service utilization in general and potentially frequent ED visits for lower back pain.

We found that, holding all exploratory variables equal, veterans who were prescribed an opioid at the initial ED/urgent care setting visit had higher adjusted odds of returning to the ED/urgent care setting for subsequent LBP care. This result is consistent with Ginsberg et al (2021), who also found a similar magnitude and directional relationship between opioid prescription and ED recidivism rate. Although this association is consistent across both manuscripts, our model was not designed to determine total or direct associations. The higher proportion of returns among veterans treated with opioids may be related to a variety of factors, including opioids’ lack of efficacy for LBP, the habit-forming nature of opioids, or the compounding risk for overlapping pain diagnosis and opioid prescription for frequent ED use in VA.19 We also found that, holding all exploratory variables equal, a history of a serious/treatable LBP diagnosis increased the adjusted odds that a veteran presented to the ED/urgent care setting for their initial LBP visit and increased the adjusted odds of returning to the ED/urgent care setting for the first subsequent visit. This may suggest that having a prior experience of being diagnosed with a condition that requires immediate medical attention, such as a fracture or infection, creates a behavioral response to seek immediate medical attention in the future. Similarly, the association between serious/treatable LBP history and ED/urgent care setting utilization should not be interpreted as a causal or predictive relationship, but rather a reflection of observed patient behaviors around clinically complex conditions.

Although LBP is often considered a nonurgent condition, 1 in 10 initial LBP visits and 4% of subsequent visits occur in the ED/urgent care clinics. When the initial location of care for LBP is the ED/urgent care clinics, the odds that the next visit for LBP would occur in the ED/urgent care clinics increased by a factor of 8. Although the retrospective nature of the study does not allow us to identify why patients returned to the ED/urgent care clinics for their subsequent health care, advice to seek emergency services by a health care practitioner has been identified as a critical factor. However, our results do not show an expected higher percentage of ED/urgent care clinic visits occurring within the first week after the primary care clinic visit. Longitudinal data of patients presenting to the ED clinics for LBP care suggest roughly 48% (95% CI, 44%-52%) continue to experience functional impairment 3 months after the index visit.15 Our EHR data does not include longitudinal, structured patient-reported functional limitations attributed to LBP; thus, we could not address the impact of effective or ineffective treatments on future health care utilization. Consistent with recommended reporting practices, our multivariable models estimate adjusted associations rather than causal effects or independent predictive contributions of individual covariates. In aim 2, the exposure of interest was the initial visit location, with other variables included for confounding control. In aim 3, the analysis was exploratory within the subgroup of veterans whose initial visit occurred in ED/urgent care clinics and included the aim 2 variables to provide a broad, descriptive view of characteristics associated with ED return. The aim 3 model’s modest discrimination performance (AUC value = 0.63) underscores that there is significant room for improvement, and additional research is needed to explore patient and system factors contributing to the first subsequent LBP care occurring in the ED/urgent care clinic after their initial LBP care visit.
