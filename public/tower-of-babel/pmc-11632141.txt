Neuroarchitecture of the Central Complex in the Madeira Cockroach Rhyparobia maderae: Tangential Neurons
Authors: Stefanie Jahn, Vanessa Althaus, Ann‐Katrin Seip, Saron Rotella, Jannik Heckmann, Mona Janning, Juliana Kolano, Aurelia Kaufmann, Uwe Homberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNavigating in diverse environments to find food, shelter, or mating partners is an important ability for nearly all animals. Insects have evolved diverse navigational strategies to survive in challenging and unknown environments. In the insect brain, the central complex (CX) plays an important role in spatial orientation and directed locomotion. It consists of the protocerebral bridge (PB), the central body with upper (CBU) and lower division (CBL), and the paired noduli (NO). As shown in various insect species, the CX integrates multisensory cues, including sky compass signals, wind direction, and ego‐motion to provide goal‐directed vector output used for steering locomotion and flight. While most of these data originate from studies on day‐active insects, less is known about night‐active species such as cockroaches. Following our analysis of columnar and pontine neurons, the present study complements our investigation of the cellular architecture of the CX of the Madeira cockroach by analyzing tangential neurons. Based on single‐cell tracer injections, we provide further details on the internal organization of the CX and distinguished 27 types of tangential neuron, including three types of neuron innervating the PB, six types of the CBL, and 18 types of the CBU. The anterior lip, a brain area unknown in flies and highly reduced in bees, and the crepine are strongly connected to the cockroach CBU in contrast to other insect species. One tangential neuron of the CBU revealed a direct connection between the mushroom bodies and the CBU.Keywords: central complex, insect brain, neuroanatomy, Rhyparobia maderae, tangential neurons

Introduction

Navigation is an essential ability for nearly all animals and plays an important role in finding food, mating partners, and shelter, when escaping from predators or invading new, unknown territories. In insects, various navigational strategies have been observed depending on ecological needs. These include straight line orientation in dung beetles while rolling a dung ball away from a dung pile (Dacke et al. 2021), seasonal long distance migrations in monarch butterflies (Reppert, Guerra, and Merlin 2016) and desert locusts (Homberg 2015), and path integration in ants (Wehner 2003) and bees (Srinivasan 2015) during homing behavior. Complex navigational abilities during climbing behavior (Watson, Ritzmann, and Pollack 2002), odor‐guided orientation (Seelinger and Gagel 1985; Willis and Avondet 2005), and escape (Ritzmann 1984) have, likewise, been demonstrated in different cockroach species. Rivault and Durier (2004) showed that the German cockroach Blattella germanica uses olfactory cues and visual landmarks to find back to a shelter after an outbound trip. In addition, B. germanica can use path integration for homing even in complete darkness (Durier and Rivault 1999). All of these navigational strategies require a computation of body orientation with respect to the environment. A midline spanning neuropil assemblage plays an important role during this process—the central complex (CX).

The CX is a highly conserved brain region and consists of four midline spanning neuropils: the protocerebral bridge (PB), the central body with an upper division (CBU, fan‐shaped body [FB] in Drosophila melanogaster) and a lower division (CBL, ellipsoid body in Drosophila), and the paired noduli (NO). Internally, the PB and CB are subdivided into 16 or 18 vertical columns. Horizontally, the CB and NO are segmented species‐specifically into layers (Pfeiffer and Homberg 2014; Pfeiffer 2023). Studies on different insect species revealed that the CX plays an important role in directed locomotion and the transformation of multimodal inputs including external cues and internally generated signals of the animal into goal‐directed behavior (Heinze 2023). In addition to sun compass cues, information on wind direction, as well as self‐motion cues, such as translational and rotational velocity during flight or walking, is integrated in the CX (Heinze 2023; Honkanen et al. 2019; Pfeiffer 2023). As shown in flies and locusts, neurons form a topographic compass‐like representation of heading directions (Heinze and Homberg 2007; Seelig and Jayaraman 2015). Computational modeling suggests that the CX houses the neuronal network underlying vector computation used for path integration (Stone et al. 2017), as well as travel and goal direction (Beetz, Kraus, and el Jundi 2023; Lu et al. 2022; Lyu et al. 2022; Mussells Pires et al. 2024; Westeinde et al. 2024). The role of the CX in motor control has been impressively demonstrated in the cockroach Blaberus discoidalis (Bender et al. 2010; Guo et al. 2014; Martin et al. 2015). Here, electric stimulation of CX neurons in freely walking animals produced consistent walking trajectories and turning movements, and neurons showed strikingly different activity profiles during climbing behavior (Martin et al. 2015).

Neurons of the CX can be classified into three broad categories: tangential neurons, pontine neurons, and columnar neurons. Their morphology has been analyzed especially in the monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013), the fruit fly (Hanesch, Fischbach, and Heisenberg 1989; Hulse et al. 2021; Wolff, Iyer, and Rubin 2015; Wolff and Rubin 2018), the bumble bee (Sayre et al. 2021), the honeybee (Hensgen et al. 2021; Homberg 1985), the desert locust (Heinze and Homberg 2008; Müller, Homberg, and Kühn 1997; von Hadeln et al. 2020), and two species of dung beetles (el Jundi et al. 2018). Tangential neurons connect diverse areas outside the CX to all columns of a CX neuropil or, in the PB, only to selected columns. They are the principle input elements of the CX. Neurons with dendrites in the bulbs provide sun compass and landmark information, input from the lateral accessory lobes (LAL) and wedge information on self‐generated optic flow and wind direction, and tangential neurons with ramifications in the superior medial and intermediate protocerebrum various contextual information such as odor valence (Heinze 2023). Pontine neurons connect columns of the CBU/FB across the brain midline (hΔ neurons in Drosophila) or between different layers of the CBU (vΔ neurons in Drosophila). hΔ neurons provide vector computations that are, for example, important for encoding the actual direction of motion relative to heading direction (Lu et al. 2022; Lyu et al. 2022). Columnar neurons are the major output elements of the CX. Their axonal projections extend to various areas outside the CX, including the LAL, the crepine (CRE), and small adjacent areas like the gall or rubus (Heinze 2023). While projections to the gall and rubus appear to be involved in feedback pathways, columnar projections to the LAL are connected to descending pathways and generate steering signals during locomotion (Rayshubskiy et al. 2020; Westeinde et al. 2024).

While most data on the neural organization of the CX originate from studies on day‐active species, we have recently reported some unique features of the CX in the night‐active cockroach Rhyparobia maderae based on the organization of pontine and columnar neurons (Jahn et al. 2023). The present study focuses on the tangential organization of the CX and complements the catalogue of CX neurons in the cockroach brain.

Material and Methods

Animals

Madeira cockroaches (R. maderae, Blattodea: Blaberidae) were obtained from crowded colonies at the Department of Biology, Philipps University Marburg. Animals were kept at a temperature of 25–26°C, relative humidity of 50%, and a light/dark cycle of 12:12 h.

Neurobiotin injections

Animal preparation and Neurobiotin injections were done as described by Jahn et al. (2023). Briefly, animals were cold‐anesthetized at 4°C. They were fixed to a metal holder using dental wax, the head capsule was opened, and fat tissue and trachea above the brain were removed. The brain was kept moist with insect saline (4.088 g NaCl, 0.186 g KCl, 0.102 g MgCl2 × 6 H2O, 0.168 g NaHCO3, 0.4125 g NaH2PO4, 0.801 g glucose in 0.5 L H2O; pH 7.25). The neural sheath was removed to facilitate the insertion of a glass electrode. To minimize movement, a wire loop was positioned posterior to the brain. Tracer injections were performed using sharp glass microelectrodes (borosilicate capillaries, 0.75 mm inside diameter, 1.5 mm outside diameter; Hilgenberg) created with a Flaming/Brown horizontal puller (P‐97; Sutter Instrument). The tip of the electrode was filled with 4% Neurobiotin (Vector Laboratories) in 1 mol⋅L−1 KCl, while the shank was filled with 1 mol⋅L−1 KCl. The electrode was inserted into the brain by using a micromanipulator (Scientifica). Electrode resistance in the tissue was 50–150 MΩ. Signals were amplified 10× (BA‐01X amplifier; npi electronic instruments). Penetration of neuronal cell membranes was indicated by recording of action potentials that were visualized with an oscilloscope (Hameg) and displayed acoustically through an audio monitor. By applying a positive current (0.5–1.5 nA) for 1–10 min, Neurobiotin was injected into the neuron.

After tracer injection, brains were dissected, fixed in Neurobiotin fixative solution (4% paraformaldehyde, 0.25% glutaraldehyde, and 0.25% saturated picric acid in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 phosphate‐buffered saline [PBS]) or 4% paraformaldehyde in sodium phosphate buffer (NaPi; 28.04 g Na2HPO4 × 2 H2O and 5.244 g NaH2PO4 × H2O in 2 L H2O) overnight at 4°C. The fixed brains were washed 4 × 15 min in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBS and subsequently incubated for 3 days with streptavidin‐cyanin‐3 (Strep‐Cy3; Dianova, 1:1000, RRID: AB_2337244) in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBT (PBS with 0.3% Triton X‐100; TrX; Sigma). After incubation, they were washed 2 × 30 min in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBT followed by 3 × 20 min in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBS. The preparations were dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series, transferred to a 50:50 solution of methyl salicylate and ethanol, cleared with methyl salicylate (30 min), and mounted in Permount medium (Fisher Scientific).

Immunolabeling of Rehydrated Neurobiotin‐Injected Brains

To examine the morphology of individual neurons in more detail, selected brains were rehydrated and sectioned at 130 µm in the frontal plane. Permount‐embedded brains from 2.2 were transferred to xylene (Roth) for several hours followed by rehydration through an ascending ethanol series. After washing in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBS and embedding in albumin/gelatin (12% ovalbumin, 4.8% gelatin in demineralized water), brains were fixed overnight in 6% formaldehyde in PBS in a refrigerator. The embedded brains were sectioned with a vibrating‐blade microtome (VT 1200 S; Leica Biosystems) followed by several washing steps (2 × 10 min in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBS followed by 4 × 10 min in 0.1 mol⋅L−1 PBT) and preincubation overnight at 4°C in 5% normal goat serum (NGS; Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 005‐000‐121, RRID: AB_2336990). To visualize neuropil borders and layers, the brains were incubated for 5 days in primary antibody against synapsin (mouse monoclonal, SYNORF1, 1:50; kindly provided by Drs. E. Buchner and C. Wegener, Würzburg, Germany; RRID: AB_2315402). After washing and incubation in secondary antibody solution consisting of Strep‐Cy3 and goat‐anti‐mouse IgG antiserum conjugated with cyanin‐5 (GaM‐Cy5, 1:300; Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 115‐175‐146, RRID: AB_2338713) with 1% NGS in PBT the sections were washed, dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series and cleared with methyl salicylate. Finally, the sections were mounted in Permount medium. A detailed procedure of the protocol has been described by Jahn et al. (2023).

Image Acquisition

Scans of the labeled whole‐mount preparations and sections were obtained with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, TCS SP5; Leica Microsystems) with a 20× oil immersion objective (HC PL APO 20×/0.75 lmm Corr CS2) and a 40× objective (HCX PL APO 40×/1.25‐0.75 Oil). The images were captured with a scanning frequency of 400 Hz and a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels in the xy‐plane (pixel size: 0.75 × 0.75 µm). The step size in z‐direction varied between 0.5 and 1 µm. Cy3 fluorescence was excited by a diode‐pumped solid‐state laser (561 nm) and Cy5 fluorescence with a helium–neon laser (633 nm).

Image Processing and Anatomical Reconstruction

Images were processed using AMIRA 6.5 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Image stacks from the same preparation were merged using the merge‐module. Three‐dimensional (3D) reconstructions of neuropils and neurons were obtained from selected preparations by using the skeletonize plugin (Evers et al. 2005; Schmitt et al. 2004) for AMIRA 5.6 (ThermoFisher Scientific) for neurons and the segmentation editor in AMIRA 6.5 for neuropils. Neuropil reconstruction was based on synapsin staining. Different layers in all three planes marked manually resulted in a 3D grid. The wrap‐module generated a polygonal surface. The modules GenerateSurface and SurfaceView were used to visualize the neuropil surface. Neuron reconstruction was obtained by manual tracing with Snaxels (spheres within a 3D space) with a customizable diameter. Digital 2D reconstructions of single neurons were created using Affinity Photo (Serif). Neurites were traced from series of image stacks obtained from AMIRA 6.5. To illustrate the branching patterns, maximum‐intensity visualizations of the image stacks were produced using the ImageOrthoProjection module. Affinity Photo was used to compile final figures and optimize images. Comparisons of tangential neurons with corresponding cell types in the fruit fly were made by accessing the Drosophila hemibrain version 1.2.1, publicly available at https://neuprint.janelia.org.

Results

Neurobiotin‐labeled neurons in 43 brains were evaluated for this study. While most experiments revealed single fluorescently labeled neurons, some preparations showed more than one neuron or even mass labeling of numerous cells, probably owing to electrode penetration of neighboring cells caused by the movement of the brain during injection. Depending on innervation areas outside and inside the CX, 27 different cell types could be distinguished. Many of these neurons correspond to particular cell types in the desert locust. Accordingly, the nomenclature of cell types follows the terminology used by von Hadeln et al. (2020) for the desert locust. The terminology of Ito et al. (2014) is used for neuropils and the nomenclature of Althaus et al. (2022) for brain areas specific to the cockroach. All axes refer to the body axis of the animals.

The somata of TB1 neurons (n = 8, diameter: 14 µm) are located posterior lateral to the PB and dorsal to the posterior optic commissure. Cell body fibers run anteriorly and bifurcate (Figure 1). One neurite extends to the posterior optic tubercle (POTU) and sends additional smooth endings further ventrally to the posterior slope (PS, Figure 1a, inset ii). Each TB1 neuron has varicose endings in two adjacent columns within each PB hemisphere. The columns housing varicose endings are separated by seven columns in between (Figure 1c). At least three adjacent columns of each PB hemisphere house smooth ramifications, but columns next to varicose projections are free of innervations (Figure 1c). The arborization patterns encountered in the labeled TB1 neurons are, like in the locust, consistent with a system of four bilateral pairs of TB1 neurons, termed TB1a, TB1b, TB1c, and TB1d, that together connect the columns in the two PB hemispheres in a highly regular manner (Figure 1c).

The third type of TB neuron found in the cockroach brain is a TB8 neuron (Figure 2b). Its soma (diameter: 17 µm) is located medial to the antennal lobe. The cell body fiber runs in dorso‐posterior direction closely following the posterior face of the brain and splits up into two arms. One arm runs laterally but could not be traced further owing to weak staining (Figure 2b, yellow arrowhead). The other neurite increases in diameter to about 8 µm and runs medially. It sends fine side branches into the PS (Figure 2b, magenta arrowheads) and continues via the great commissure across the brain midline. A single collateral in each hemisphere projects dorsally, ramifies further, and innervates one arm of the PB and surrounding neuropil of the posterior slope (Figure 2b, insets i and ii). As other neurons invading the PB were co‐labeled in this preparation, the innervation of all PB columns could not be determined with certainty. In the contralateral hemisphere, a further collateral invades the PS with wide varicose ramifications (Figure 2b, inset iii).

Tangential Neurons of the CBL

The CBL of the cockroach CX shows an organization unlike that of any other insect studied. It is subdivided into two types of columns, an array of nine columns called “teeth” interdigitating another set of eight columns termed “cones” (Althaus et al. 2022; Figures 3 and 4). Within the cones, a head region (CO‐H) can be distinguished form a neck region (CO‐N; Figure 3f), while the teeth can be differentiated into core (TH‐C) and rim (TH‐R; Figure 3b). Tangential neurons of the CBL (TL neurons) in the cockroach can, accordingly, be divided into two categories. One type innervates the nine teeth, the other type the intersecting eight cones of the CBL. Because the correspondence of teeth and cones to the layers of the CBL in other insects is unclear, the suffix “‐TH” for teeth or “‐CO” for cones was added to the neurons’ names. Four different types of TL neurons with ramifications in the teeth and three types with arborizations in the cones were found in the cockroach brain (Table 1). TL neurons divide the bulb (BU) into two subunits, the inferior bulb (IBU) and the superior bulb (SBU). The IBU covers the ventrolateral part of the bulb near the isthmus tract, and the SBU lies dorsal to the IBU. Mass staining revealed that TL‐TH neurons (Figure 3a,b) largely innervate the IBU but partly also the SBU (Figure 3c,d). Arborizations of TL‐CO neurons, on the other hand (Figure 3e,f), are confined to the dorsal part of the bulb, the SBU (Figure 3g,h, Table 1). Mass stainings of TL neurons with innervated cones and teeth (Figure 4a,b) showed ramifications in the SBU and IBU on one side (Figure 4) or both sides of the brain.

TL‐TH Neurons

TL1 neurons with ramifications in the TH (TL1‐TH) were labeled in two experiments (Figure 5). Their somata (Figure 5a, diameter: 13 µm) lie ventral to the lateral complex (LX) and medial to the antennal lobe near the vest corresponding to TL1 neurons in the locust. The primary neurite runs posteriorly around the LAL. While continuing in dorsal direction, it splits up into two arms. One arm joins the isthmus tract (IT) that runs posterior to the CBL and anterodorsal to the lower unit of the noduli (NOL). It gives rise to nine side branches (1–9 in Figure 5b) that send varicose endings to the rim areas of each tooth of the CBL (Figure 5a, inset i, white arrowheads and 5c). The other arm runs anteriorly and gives rise to dense arborizations with smooth endings in the anterior upper lateral accessory lobe (ULAL, Figure 5a, inset ii, green arrowheads) and the gall (GA, Figure 5a, inset ii, white arrowheads). Scattered endings also reach the anterior lower lateral accessory lobe (LLAL, Figure 5a, inset ii, magenta arrowheads). The rubus is free of innervation.

Since correspondence of the SBU and IBU to the lateral and medial bulbs of other insects (locust: von Hadeln et al. 2020; honeybee: Hensgen et al. 2021) was not obvious, we did not differentiate between TL2 and TL3 neurons and named these neurons collectively TL2,3. TL2,3a‐TH neurons (n = 4) connect the IBU of the LX to the CBL (Figure 6a,b). Their somata (diameter: 12–13 µm) are located ventral to the LX. The main neurite runs dorsally and joins the IT. The neurons send smooth processes to small clusters of microglomeruli in the IBU (Figure 6a, inset ii, and 6b, inset ii). The axon splits up into several arms that further divide into a total of nine neurites, one running to each tooth of the CBL (Figure 6a,b). TL2,3a‐TH neurons tend to have a slightly variable course of their main neurite and branching pattern in the teeth of the CBL but generally innervate core and rim of the teeth (Figure 6b, inset i).

TL‐CO Neurons

Three types of TL‐CO neurons could be distinguished. TL2,3a‐CO (Figure 7a, n = 1) and TL2,3b‐CO neurons (Figure 7b, n = 1) have their somata (diameter: around 13 µm) ventral to the LAL and medial to the antennal lobe like TL2,3‐TH neurons. Their primary neurites run dorsally and pass through the LX within the IT. The main neurite of the TL2,3a‐CO neuron sends smooth endings organized in clusters to the SBU (Figure 7a, inset ii). The axonal fiber runs in medial direction and sends varicose arborizations to the cones, invading their entire volumes (Figure 7a, inset i). TL2,3b‐CO neurons, in contrast, do not innervate the upper parts of the cone heads (Figure 7b, inset i, white arrowheads).

Tangential Neurons of the CBU

Tangential neurons of the CBU (TU neurons) are the most diverse group of tangential neurons in the CX. They have arborizations outside the CBU in various areas of the protocerebrum (Table 2). As in the desert locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020), TU neurons were classified based on the position of their soma. As a result, we encountered eight different classes of TU neurons, each class likely comprising neurons that are clonally related. Based on morphological characters such as innervated brain areas or course of their main fiber, we distinguished in total 15 different neuron types, some comprising two or three subtypes (Table 2).

TUVES Neurons

Four different types of TUVES neurons were found in the cockroach brain, termed TUVES1 ‐ TUVES4 with TUVES4 neurons consisting of three subtypes. All neurons have their somata near the vest and share their soma position with TL1 neurons. Four of these types, TUVES1, TUVES2, TUVES3, and TUVES4a, closely correspond to TUVES types in the locust, while the other two (TUVES4b and TUVES4c) have no correspondence with neurons in the locust. All TUVES neurons enter the CBU through the IT.

TUVES1 neurons (n = 2, soma diameter: 11 × 14 µm) connect the LAL, anterior lip (ALI), and crepine (CRE) to all three layers of the CBU. On its way from the soma to the CB, the neurite sends several side branches into the ULAL and LLAL (Figure 8a, inset iii). Strikingly similar to the TUVES1 neuron in the locust, these arborizations wrap around the medial lobe of the mushroom body (Figure 8a’) and further innervate the CRE anterior and medial to the medial lobe (Figure 8a, inset ii). Ventral to the medial lobe, another side branch is given off. It sends smooth arborizations dorsally into the ALI. As the main neurite projects along the ventral face of the CBU, it sequentially gives off eight branches that innervate the CBU. Ramifications are particularly dense in posterior parts of layer I (Figure 8a, inset i).

We found three subtypes of TUVES4 neurons. Like the locust TUVES4 neurons they have characteristic ramifications in the CRE and an axonal neurite crossing the brain midline posterior from the CB and dorsally from the NO, but in contrast to the locust neurons also ramify sparsely in the LAL. TUSLP Neurons

Tangential neurons of the CBU with somata near the superior lateral protocerebrum (SLP), close to the lateral calyx of the mushroom body, were termed, as in the locust, TUSLP. The cell body fibers of all neurons extend anterior‐medially in a common fascicle along the posterior‐medial face of the vertical lobe of the mushroom body. Main fibers continue toward the CB through the anterior bundle (AB). Near the CB, it splits up into two fascicles. One of these corresponds to the anterior bundle 1 (AB1) in the locust, and the second was termed AB2 and is probably a combination of AB2 and AB3 in the locust (Figure 10f). Unlike AB2 and AB3 in the locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020), it passes between the ALI and CBL ventrally around the CBL before the neurons enter the CBU. Four different types of TUSLP neurons could be distinguished based on innervated areas inside and outside the CB. Three of them, TUSLP3, TUSLP4, and TUSLP5, correspond to a TUSLP type in the locust.

TUPS Neurons

We found two types of TU neurons with somata in the PS of the cockroach brain, termed TUPS neurons. One of these closely resembles TUPS11 neurons in the locust. The TUPS11 neuron was stained together with a TUCRE11 neuron in the same brain (Figure 11b). The soma of the TUPS11 neuron (diameter: 16 × 21 µm) is located in the posterior pars intercerebralis, dorsal to the PS. Its main neurite passes by the lateral arm of the PB. It continues ventrally and sends smooth endings to the inferior clamp and the LAL. An axonal fiber projects to the CBU and sends eight side branches to layer II of the CBU (Figure 11c, magenta). The terminals in layer II are concentrated in eight tapered domains dorsal to the cones of the CBL (Figure 11b, inset i, white arrowheads). A small process continues from the main fiber at the base of the CBU to the contralateral LAL. Owing to the nearby arborizations of the TUCRE11 neuron in the CBU, not all endings could be assigned clearly to one of the two neurons.

TUCRE, TULAL, and TLU Neurons

Two TU neurons with somata near the LAL, termed TULAL, and one neuron with soma near the CRE, termed TUCRE11, were labeled. The TUCRE11 neuron is highly similar to TUCRE11 neurons in the locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020). Its soma (diameter: 14 µm) lies anterior in the brain, lateral to the vertical lobe (Figure 11b, yellow). The neurite runs dorsally and innervates the anterior ventrolateral protocerebrum (AVLP, Figure 11b, inset ii) before it bypasses the vertical lobe of the mushroom body posteriorly and sends smooth endings to the CRE. The main neurite runs along the lateral border of the ALI and innervates the posterior part of layer II of the CBU from ventral direction. Three arms that ramify further innervate the regions between the tapered arborization domains of the TUPS21 neuron.

The somata of the TULAL neurons (n = 2) lie dorsally to the somata of TUVES neurons, anteroventral to the LAL together with the somata of TL2,3 and TL4 neurons. The neurons appear to be variants of TULAL1 neurons in the locust and were termed TULAL1a and TULAL1b. Both TULAL neurons share common arborization areas in the LAL, ALI, and CRE, but TULAL1b has an additional small projection to the contralateral LAL (Figure 12b, inset i). The soma of the TULAL1a neuron (diameter 18 × 20 µm) is slightly smaller than that of TULAL1b (diameter: 20 × 24 µm). Its cell body fiber runs dorsally to the LAL and sends side branches to the LAL and CRE before it passes along the anterior face of the CB and gives off at least 10 side branches to layers I and II of the CBU (Figure 12c). Small additional fibers run in the opposite direction and innervate the ALI (Figure 12a, yellow). Owing to co‐labeling of a TLU1 neuron in the same brain, the terminal arborizations within the CBU could not be assigned to a specific neuron (Figure 12a, inset ii, gray innervations).

TU‐MB Neurons

One type of TU neuron with soma in the pars intercerebralis (n = 2) has no correspondence to TU neurons encountered in the locust. Its soma has a diameter of about 15 µm. From the soma, the cell body fiber runs ventrally and enters the brain neuropil laterally from the PB. TUN Neuron

One neuron innervating the CBU as well as the two noduli was encountered and termed TUN1 neuron, based on its similarity to a TUN1 neuron in the locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020). Its soma is located near the vest together with those of TUVES and TL1‐TH neurons. The neuron connects the CBU and the LAL with both NO (Figure 13c). Arborizations in the CBU are concentrated in layer III and have a varicose appearance. Due to a weak labeling of the neuron, endings in layer II cannot be excluded. Terminals in the NO are varicose and innervate layers II and III of the NOU (Figure 13c, inset iii).

Discussion

This study complements the recently published catalogue of columnar and pontine neurons innervating the CX in the cockroach brain (Jahn et al. 2023) with 27 types of tangential neuron (Tables 1 and 2). The analysis of these neurons provides important insights into the internal organization of the cockroach CX, as well as brain areas connected to the CX (Figure 14) and reveals some striking differences to other insect species like the desert locust Schistocerca gregaria (von Hadeln et al. 2020) or D. melanogaster (Hulse et al. 2021). Tangential neurons from three different categories were analyzed, namely TB, TL, and TU neurons. In contrast to all other insects studied, the CBL (ellipsoid body in Drosophila), the input stage to the compass network of the CX, is not organized into layers but into intercalated systems of eight and nine columnar domains termed teeth and cones. The functional consequences of this particular organization are at present difficult to assess but show that the neuronal organization of the insect CX may be more diverse than hitherto assumed (Heinze 2024). While the teeth are connected by columnar neurons to the PB, likely establishing a compass‐like representation of space around the animal as in other species, the postsynaptic columnar partners of TL neurons innervating the cone volume have not been identified (Jahn et al. 2023). An organization of intercalated eight and nine columnar domains was also observed in parts of the CBU (this study; Jahn et al. 2023), suggesting that two distinct columnar systems exist throughout the CB. Nevertheless, many types of TB‐, TL‐, and TU neurons of the CX have obvious counterparts in other species such as bees, locusts, and flies. The strong connectivity of TU neurons to the ALI, however, appears as a unique feature of the cockroach CX.

Tangential Neurons of the PB

Three different types of tangential neuron of the PB were found in the cockroach brain (Figure 1). All of them have counterparts in other insect species like the desert locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020). Homologues to the cockroach TB1 neurons also exist in the monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013), the dung beetle (el Jundi et al. 2018), and Drosophila (termed Δ7 neurons; Wolff, Iyer, and Rubin 2015), although in the fly and the dung beetle, these neurons lack connections to POTUs. In contrast to other studied insect species, TB1 neurons in the cockroach have smooth endings beyond the POTU in the PS, and their varicose endings occupy two adjacent columns in each PB hemisphere (Figure 1c) instead of one as in the locust or monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013). The dung beetle is the only other insect studied so far with some TB1 neurons sending varicose endings to adjacent PB columns (el Jundi et al. 2018). Cockroach TB1 neurons are serotonin‐immunoreactive (Homberg et al. 2023). Their connectivity scheme is similar to those in the locust (Beetz et al. 2015) and monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013) revealing four different TB1 subtypes (a–d). The innervation of all PB columns by Δ7 neurons in flies differs strikingly from the patterned innervation of only certain columns in cockroaches, beetles, locusts, and butterflies and might, thus, be a highly specific feature of flies probably not found in non‐dipteran insects. As shown in flies (Turner‐Evans et al. 2020) and locusts (Heinze and Homberg 2007), TB1/Δ7 neurons play a key role in the head direction network of the CX, but the role of TB4 neurons is still unclear. The morphology of the TB8 neuron could not be completely revealed in the cockroach as well as in the locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020) because the targets of a large laterally projecting neurite could not be determined. Their likely counterpart in Drosophila, termed OA‐AL2i1 (Busch et al. 2009; Table 1), sends a long laterally projecting neurite into the optic lobe with large‐field beaded arborizations in all neuropils except the lamina. The TB8 and OA‐AL2i1 neurons are likely octopaminergic (Busch et al. 2009; Homberg et al. 2013) and might, thus, set arousal levels both in the optic lobe and the PB, perhaps for the processing of navigation relevant stimuli.

Tangential Neurons of the CBL

The cockroach CBL consists of nine cones and eight teeth (Jahn et al. 2023) and at first sight lacks an obvious layering. TL neurons (ER neurons in Drosophila) innervate either the cones or the teeth and were, therefore, grouped into TL‐TH and TL‐CO neurons, according to their branching scheme. A horizontal layering may be present within the cones but not in the teeth. While TL4‐CO neurons had ramifications confined to the cone necks, TL2,3b‐CO neurons invaded the necks plus lower half of the cone heads. A third type of TL‐CO neurons with ramifications confined to the cone heads (Figure 3f) may exist, but was not found in single‐cell impalements. In contrast, the teeth could be divided into rim and core. TL1‐TH neurons innervate the rim, TL2,3b‐TH neurons the core, and TL2,3a‐TH neurons both parts (Table 1).

All cockroach TL neurons except for TL4‐CO neurons innervate parts of the BU. Their ramifications divide the BU into a superior (SBU) and an inferior part (IBU). The IBU is only innervated by TL‐TH neurons, the SBU by TL‐CO neurons, and the TL1‐TH neuron that has broad ramifications in the LX (Table 1). In Drosophila, the BU is also divided into a superior (BUs) and an inferior part (BUi), but any correspondence to SBU and IBU in the cockroach remains to be seen. Hulse et al. (2021) showed that ER2 neurons arborize in the BUs and ER3 neurons in the BUi. This might indicate a correspondence of TL‐TH neurons in the cockroach with ER3 neurons in Drosophila (likely homologous with TL3 neurons in the locust; von Hadeln et al. 2020) and TL‐CO neurons with ER2 neurons (likely homologous with TL2 neurons in the locust; von Hadeln et al. 2020). TL2 and TL3 neurons were encountered in various insect species, for example, in the desert locust (Homberg et al. 1999), a dung beetle (el Jundi et al. 2015), two species of bees (Held et al. 2016; Hensgen et al. 2021; Stone et al. 2017), the field cricket (Sakura, Lambrinos, and Labhart 2008), and the monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013; Heinze and Reppert 2011). These neurons are GABA‐immunoreactive (Homberg et al. 2018), and in many species, GABA immunostaining shows a layered organization of the CBL (sphinx moth, honey bee, desert locust, flies) in contrast to the unique arrangement of cones and teeth in the cockroach.

TL2/TL3 neurons in locusts, crickets, butterflies, and dung beetles, like ER2/ER3 neurons in flies, provide visual input to the CX, largely celestial compass information that is used by downstream neurons to compute a compass‐like representation of head directions in the CX network (Heinze and Reppert 2011; Sakura, Lambrinos, and Labhart 2008; Seelig and Jayaraman 2013; Takahashi et al. 2022; Vitzthum et al. 2002). Homologues of TL4 neurons in Drosophila, termed ER1, represent the input stage of information on wind direction to the compass network of the CX (Okubo et al. 2020). Based on these data, TL neurons in the cockroach are, likewise, strong candidates to provide visual and mechanosensory input to the CBL as demonstrated by extracellular recordings (Ritzmann, Ridgel, and Pollack 2008; Varga et al. 2017).

Tangential Neurons of the CBU

TU neurons form the most diverse group of CX neurons in the cockroach, consistent with findings in other insect species (dung beetle: el Jundi et al. 2018; monarch butterfly: Heinze et al. 2013; fruit fly: Hulse et al. 2021; flesh fly: Phillips‐Portillo and Strausfeld 2012; desert locust: von Hadeln et al. 2020). Following their nomenclature in the locust, neurons were named according to their soma position resulting in eight different groups of TU neurons. This suggests that neurons within each group are clonally related. A comparison of clusters of TU neurons in the locust with clones of FB neurons in Drosophila, indeed, revealed striking topographic similarities and, thus, provides strong support for this hypothesis (Kandimalla et al. 2023). Although most of the encountered TU neurons are homologues to TU neurons in the desert locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020), and FB neurons in the fly (Hulse et al. 2021; Kandimalla et al. 2023), some differences exist especially in the main branching areas outside and inside the CX (Table 2, Figure 14).

TUVES neurons form the largest group of TU neurons with four different types and three subtypes (Figures 8 and 9). All of them have ramifications in layer II of the CBU. Main branching areas outside the CX are, like in TULAL neurons (see below), the ALI, CRE, ULAL, LLAL, and the prong of the LX (Table 2). Several of these areas, notably the ALI, ULAL, prong, and CRE, are prominent projection sites of columnar output neurons of the CX (Jahn et al. 2023). TULAL and TUVES neurons may, therefore, be involved in various feedback circuits perhaps mediating gain control and gating of CX outputs. A characteristic feature of TUVES neurons is the columnar appearance of their innervation domains in layer II, which are usually innervated by one fiber each. TUVES1, 2, 3, and 4a neurons have homologues in the locust brain (von Hadeln et al. 2020), and TUVES2 and 4 were also described in the brain of the honey bee (Hensgen et al. 2021). Owing to the large number of tangential neurons of the FB in Drosophila, the lack of an ALI, and differences in the layering of the CBU and FB (Hulse et al. 2021), neurons corresponding to TUVES neurons of the cockroach are more difficult to identify. TUVES neurons correspond to tangential neurons of the BAMmv1 lineage of the fly (Kandimalla et al. 2023). Striking similarities in cell body position, and dendritic and columnar ramifications exist between the TUVES1 neuron and the FB4G and FB4H neurons in the fly, which show arborizations around the medial lobe that are highly characteristic of TUVES1 neurons in the cockroach and locust (Table 2). Likewise, similarities also exist between the TUVES2 neurons and the fly FB2D neurons, the TUVES3 neurons and the FB4I neurons, and between the TUVES4 subtypes and the fly FB1A, FB1B, FB1F, FB6O, and FB6P neurons (Table 2).

The second large group are the TUSLP neurons with four different types (Figure 10). All of them have ramifications in the CRE. TUSLP3 neurons in the cockroach were described before in Timm et al. (2021) as tyrosine hydroxylase‐immunoreactive DC/PPL1 neurons. TUSLP3 and TUSLP4 neurons are counterparts of TU neurons in the desert locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020), the monarch butterfly (TU‐pdl‐(I), TU‐pdl‐(II), Heinze et al. 2013) and the honey bee (Hensgen et al. 2021). In the fruit fly, TUSLP neurons correspond to neurons of the large CP2d lineage with more than 130 cell types innervating nearly all layers of the FB (Kandimalla et al. 2023) and include cell types FB2F, FB2G, FB5Y, FB6A, FB6B, and FB6E with similarity to the branching patterns of TUSLP neurons in the cockroach (Table 2). The stained TUSLP3 neuron is the only neuron with ramifications confined to the cap region of the CBU (Figure 10a,f). It is, therefore, a strong candidate for contacting CU4 columnar neurons, the only columnar neurons with innervations in this layer that were traced so far (Jahn et al. 2023). CU4 neurons connect the CBU, CBL, ALI, and CRE. The TUSLP6 neuron (Figure 10b) is the only neuron outside the fruit fly that provides a direct connection between the AOTU and the CX and could, thus, provide visual input to the CBU, perhaps related to spatial information. In the fly, two cell types, FB6B and FB6E, share many similarities with the TUSLP6 neuron. In addition to ramifications in the superior medial protocerebrum, both neurons send a small fiber toward the AOTU, which, however, apparently ends before reaching the tubercle.

Two subtypes of TUPS and TULAL neurons were identified (Figures 11 and 12). The morphologies of the two TUPS neurons are very different, and they only share one arborization area outside the CX, the ICL (Table 2, Figure 14). A special characteristic of TUPS11 neurons is the innervation pattern in the CBU. The endings form tapered domains dorsal to the cones of the CBL (Figure 11b, inset i, white arrowheads) that divides layer II of the CBU into a system of eight columns (tapered domains) intersected by nine areas between the tapered domains, highly similar to the organization of cones and teeth in the CBL. The endings of the co‐labeled TUCRE11 neuron exactly fill up these nine domains (Figure 11b), which lie dorsally to the teeth in the posterior part of layer II of the CBU. Based on its characteristic projection pattern, the labeled TUPS11 neuron is most likely a member of the large group of tyrosine hydroxylase immunolabeled DP2/PPM3 neurons (in Drosophila also termed FB4L) that likely provide dopaminergic input to the CBU and occur in all dicondylian insects studied (Timm et al. 2021). As a result of their branching scheme, columnar candidate neurons that might connect to TUPS11 neurons are CPU1 or CPU5 neurons that innervate eight columns of layer II of the CBU (Figures 6 and 7 in Jahn et al. 2023). Therefore, CPU5 neurons are likely to receive information from TUPS11 neurons. CU2a‐ or CU2c neurons instead occupy regions innervated by TUCRE11 neurons within the CBU (Figure 9 in Jahn et al. 2023). Their arborization areas form a ninefold system and therefore might be connected to TUCRE11 tangential neurons.

The TUPS33 neuron (Figure 11a) is one of two TU neurons that enter the CBU from its dorsal face. Its arborizations in the CBU cluster around the fiber tracts running through layer II (Figure 11a, inset i). Corresponding neurons in Drosophila are the FB7C and FB7M types of neurons. A counterpart in the locust also enters the CBU from dorsal but innervates the LAL bilaterally. TULAL neurons seem to have the densest and most extensive arborizations of all TU neurons in the CBU (Figure 12). They innervate the CBU with many densely packed terminals (Figure 12b, insets iii and iv). Candidate corresponding neurons in the fly are the FB3B neurons, which however, unlike TULAL1a, also innervate the ellipsoid body, and the FB5A type with input ramifications wrapped around the medial lobe as in TULAL1b (Table 2).

As already shown for columnar neurons (Jahn et al. 2023), co‐innervation of the CBU and CBL has also been found among tangential neurons. The TLU1 neuron fulfills this characteristic by sending side branches into the cone heads of the CBL as well as into layers I and II of the CBU (Figure 12a, insets i and iii). Co‐innervation of both CB subdivisions by tangential neurons was not found in the honey bee (Hensgen et al. 2021), dung beetle (el Jundi et al. 2018), and monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013) but has been shown in the locust (TCX neurons; von Hadeln et al. 2020) and Drosophila (several ExR neurons, most prominently ExR3; Hulse et al. 2021; Kandimalla et al. 2023). Interestingly, the ExR3 neurons are serotonergic (Omoto et al. 2018) and correspond to the serotonin‐immunoreactive PM1 neurons in the cockroach (Homberg et al. 2023). The PM1 neurons show striking similarity with the TLU1 neuron: both innervate parts of the CBU as well as the cone heads and have their somata in the pars intercerebralis. Since these neurons show slightly different innervation patterns in the LAL, they might represent two subtypes of the same group.

Only single neurons of the TUCRE group, the TU‐MB and TUN type, were encountered. FB4B neurons in the fly closely correspond to the cockroach TUN1 neuron (Kandimalla et al. 2023). The TU‐MB neuron connects parts of the mushroom body directly with the CX (Figures 13a and 14). The endings of all arborization areas have the same appearance so that no clear input and output areas could be defined. In Drosophila, numerous output neurons of the mushroom body (MBONs) make connections with the dendrites of tangential neurons of the FB, but neurons with ramifications in both brain areas are rarer (Hulse et al. 2021; Li et al. 2020). The FB4K neurons share some similarity with the TU‐MB neuron (Kandimalla et al. 2023). They connect parts of the medial lobes to the FB, but most arborizations lie around rather than inside the medial lobes. A neuron similar to TU‐MB connecting the mushroom body lobes and CBU was also reported in the monarch butterfly (Heinze et al. 2013). Since staining of that neuron was incomplete, a further comparison of the innervation areas is not possible. The beaded appearance of all ramification areas of the TU‐MB neuron suggests that it might have some function in synchronizing the activities in the mushroom‐body lobes, ALI and CBU.

Internal Organization of the Upper Division of the Central Body

Together with the catalogue of columnar and pontine neurons, the layering of the CBU can be further refined (Figure 15). Each of the three layers, established by Jahn et al. (2023), consists of two sublayers (a and b). Layer Ia is free of innervations from most neuronal cell types but exclusively houses ramifications from TU‐MB neurons (Figure 13b) in addition to innervations from CU4 neurons (Figure 10 in Jahn et al. 2023). Layer Ib is innervated by many different cell types namely TUVES1 (Figure 8c), TUSLP5 (Figure 10f), TUPS33 (Figure 11c), both TULAL neurons, and the TLU1 neuron. Additionally, ramifications from pontine neurons, CPU1a‐1 and CPU2 neurons, and nearly all CU neurons except for CU2c and CU4 (Figures 9 and 10 in Jahn et al. 2023) are located in this layer. Layer II is divided into a ventral layer IIa and a dorsal layer IIb. This subdivision was only found for tangential neurons as ramifications of columnar neurons and the TUCRE11 neuron (Figure 11c) do not show a division into dorsal and ventral sublayers but rather support a division into anterior and posterior parts without clear borders (Jahn et al. 2023). Tangential neurons that show a clear border in their ramification area of layer II are TUPS33 (Figure 11c), TUSLP4 (Figure 10f), and TUVES4c neurons (Figure 9b). Two tangential neurons, two types of pontine neurons and three types of columnar cell types, namely TuVES1 (Figure 8c), TUSLP6 (Figure 10f), POU3, POUv, CPU1a‐3, CPU4b, CU1, and CU2a‐2 (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 in Jahn et al. 2023), indicate a subdivision of layer III in an anterior part IIIa and a posterior part IIIb (Figure 15). A correspondence to the CBU layers in the locust remains to be established as most neurons with comparable innervation areas outside the CX ramify in different layers of the CBU except for TUSLP4 neurons. This TU type has ramifications in layer IIb of the CBU in the cockroach and the locust.

Functional Considerations

Many tangential neurons that have been encountered in the cockroach were already known from other insect species like the desert locust (von Hadeln et al. 2020) or the fruit fly (Hulse et al. 2021). This suggests a similar functionality of the CX and provides a hypothesis on the cellular basis of spatial orientation control in the cockroach brain. As shown in several species, notably flies, bees, crickets, and locusts, tangential neurons to the CBL (TL/ER neurons) provide and combine celestial compass input, wind‐direction input, and possibly visual environmental input to the CX. Through a ring attractor mechanism, these inputs are transformed into a sinusoidal activity pattern across the columnar arrays of the CX which allow for efficient vector computations (Hulse et al. 2021; Wilson 2023). Neurons targeting the noduli, not yet identified in the cockroach, provide visual and mechanosensory angular velocity input during self‐rotation (Heinze 2023). This leads to corresponding shifts in the activity peak across the arrays of columnar neurons, and thus a head‐direction signal modulated by the rotational movements of the animal (Heinze 2023). Columnar neurons, finally, generate a goal‐directed signal that controls locomotion. In contrast to well‐characterized input pathways to the CBL, less is known about inputs to the PB and CBU, especially in non‐dipteran insects. In the cockroach, like in the locust, the POTU and surrounding areas of the PS receive prominent input from efferent neurons of the circadian clock in the accessory medulla that likely transfer time information important for time‐compensation during long‐distance locomotion or homing (Held et al. 2020; Homberg and Würden 1997; Petri et al. 1995).

The most diverse group of tangential CX neurons, TU neurons, receives information from numerous surrounding brain areas (Table 2, Figure 14). In the cockroach, the most prominent arborization areas of these neurons outside the CX are the CRE, LX, and ALI (Table 2, Figure 14). Although little is known about the role of TU neurons, they might act as modulators for context‐dependent behavior, integrate information about state dependency (Pfeiffer 2023), and have been proposed to be instrumental for setting navigational goals (Heinze 2023). Work in Drosophila has provided first specific insights. Distinct subsets of tangential neurons have been shown to provide such diverse inputs as the presence of an attractive odor to the FB, by input from either the mushroom body or lateral horn (Matheson et al. 2022), the need for sleep (Donlea, Pimentel, and Miesenböck 2014), or nutritional‐state‐dependent information to control foraging decisions (Goldschmidt et al. 2023). The latter two neuron types correspond to TUSLP neurons in the cockroach.

With their rich behavioral repertoire and sophisticated navigational performance (Canonge et al. 2009; Rivault and Durier 2004; Ritzmann et al. 2012; Watson, Ritzmann, and Pollack 2002; Willis and Avondet 2005), cockroaches are promising insect species for further analysis of navigation decision‐making and its underlying neuronal substrates (Varga et al. 2017). Several studies have impressively demonstrated that monitoring and manipulating neural activity in the CX are feasible in freely moving cockroaches (Guo et al. 2014; Martin et al. 2015). The present study together with our earlier report on the neuronal composition of the cockroach CX (Jahn et al. 2023) and brain (Althaus et al. 2022) provides an important basis for a cellular analysis of navigation centers in the cockroach brain and may shed further light on modifications of navigation circuitries in insects adapted to a nocturnal life style.
