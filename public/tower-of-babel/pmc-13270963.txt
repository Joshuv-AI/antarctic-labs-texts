Primary Mitral Valve Sarcoma Mimicking Infective Endocarditis
Authors: Jingzhi Huang, Shuo Chen, Hongwen Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Cardiac sarcoma; Mitral valve; Infective endocarditis; Misdiagnosis, Echocardiography

Introduction

Primary cardiac tumors represent a rare yet clinically significant group of diseases. Among these, cardiac angiosarcoma is the most prevalent malignant type, followed by fibrous histiocytoma and leiomyosarcoma. Primary cardiac liposarcoma only accounts for approximately 13% of primary malignant cardiac tumors with the potential for infiltration and metastasis.1,2 Notably, the presentation of a cardiac sarcoma involving the mitral valve (MV) can bear a striking resemblance to infective endocarditis (IE), leading to potential diagnostic challenge and delayed recognition. Due to their insidious growth and varied clinical manifestations, accurate and timely differentiation is critical.

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old woman presented with a 2-week history of exertional palpitations and dyspnea, accompanied by dizziness and near syncope. Symptoms worsened with climbing 2 flights of stairs. Initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) at a local hospital revealed hyperechoic, mobile masses attached to both the anterior and posterior leaflets of the MV, interpreted as vegetations in the context of underlying rheumatic heart disease. The patient received empiric therapy for IE and heart failure. For further diagnosis and treatment, the patient was transferred to our hospital. Vital signs revealed a temperature of 38.1°C, regular pulse at 97 beats per minute, and blood pressure of 98/63 mm Hg. Physical examination was significant for an apical grade 4/6 holo-systolic murmur. The patient was referred for a TTE, which revealed left atrial (LA) enlargement (43 × 51 × 63 mm; LA volume index, 49 mL/m2) and thickening of both the anterior and posterior MV leaflets. Frond-like slightly hypoechoic attachments were observed on the LA side of both the anterior and posterior MV leaflets (approximately 13 × 5 mm and 10 × 6 mm in size, respectively; Figure 1A, 1C, Videos 1 and 2). Color-flow Doppler showed a central jet occupying over 50% of the LA area, with a vena contracta width of 7 mm (Figure 1B and 1D, Videos 3 and 4 ). Two-dimensional tracing indicated an MV orifice area of 1.2 cm2 (Figure 2A, Video 5). Continuous-wave Doppler revealed increased flow velocity, with a maximum velocity of 2.6 m/s and a mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 17 mm Hg (Figure 2B). Moderate tricuspid regurgitation was observed (vena contracta width, 5 mm), and the regurgitant flow spectrum estimated a right ventricular systolic pressure of 68 mm Hg (Figure 3, Video 6). A small pericardial effusion was noted posterior to the right ventricle. Cardiac computed tomography (CCT) corroborated MV leaflet thickening, enlargement of the left atrium, slight widening of the pulmonary artery, and a small amount of pericardial effusion but could not definitively characterize the mass (Figure 4). Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (white blood cell count 11.23 × 109/L), elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein 15.96 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 71 mm/h), elevated anti-streptolysin O 266 IU/mL (reference range <200 IU/mL), and increased N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (2,006 pg/mL). The workup for systemic autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus and vasculitis, yielded negative results. Despite the negative blood cultures, IE superimposed on underlying rheumatic valvular disease was considered the most likely diagnosis. This clinical judgment was based on the patient's history of preceding antibiotic therapy (a known risk factor for culture-negative IE), serological evidence of streptococcal infection, and supportive echocardiographic findings on TTE. The patient's symptoms of dyspnea, dizziness and near syncope showed no improvement. Considering the severity of the patient's MV pathology and the associated risk of potential embolic complications, the treatment team expedited the surgical intervention. Intraoperatively, the MV was found to be severely stenotic (valve area ≈1.0 cm2) with thickened, myxoid leaflets and fused chordae (Figure 5A). The patient underwent mechanical MV replacement, tricuspid valve repair, and excision of the mass. Histopathology analysis reported atypical lipomatous tumor or dedifferentiated liposarcoma with positive immunohistochemistry for Vimentin(+++), CDK4(+), MDM2(+++), and Ki67 (30%+; Figure 5B). The patient recovered well postoperatively and has remained in good condition during the 3-month follow-up period. Adjuvant radiotherapy was recommended given the confined mass and the chemotoxicity of chemotherapy.

Discussion

Liposarcoma, a malignant mesenchymal tumor, most commonly arises in the retroperitoneum or extremities, with rare occurrences in the thoracic cavity or mediastinum. Histologically, it is classified into 3 main subtypes: myxoid (low-grade), dedifferentiated, and pleomorphic (both highly aggressive).3 Primary cardiac involvement is exceptionally rare, with reported cases in the left atrium, right ventricle, and pericardium.4, 5, 6 Symptoms typically result from mechanical obstruction of blood flow—causing dyspnea and edema—or nonspecific systemic effects such as fever, chills, fatigue, arthralgia, and myalgia.

This case presents a diagnostic challenge in which a rare primary cardiac liposarcoma mimicked IE. Unlike prior reports emphasizing tumor-related valvular obstruction, here the tumor exclusively involved the MV, causing severe stenosis and regurgitation without mass-like occlusion. The clinical picture—a young woman with fever and valvular vegetations—initially supported an infectious etiology. However, several inconsistencies emerged: persistently negative blood cultures, young patient age, a hemodynamic lesion of severity disproportionate to the duration of symptoms, and a notably brief febrile course. This case underscores that not all valvular masses are infectious and highlights the necessity of a systematic, multimodal diagnostic approach in atypical presentations.

Initial diagnosis relies on noninvasive modalities such as TTE, which provides information on mass location, morphology, size, and mobility. Transesophageal echocardiography offers improved spatial resolution for assessing tumor dimensions and extent and differentiating primary from metastatic lesions. However, both TTE and transesophageal echocardiography have limited capability in tissue characterization. In this case, typical features suggestive of malignancy—such as broad-based attachment, local infiltration, or associated pericardial effusion—were absent or nonspecific. Advanced imaging techniques can enhance tissue characterization and assess metabolic activity, aiding in distinguishing benign vegetations from malignant tumors.7 Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) with late gadolinium enhancement and tissue mapping could have provided preoperative evaluation of tumor vascularity, composition, and invasion extent.8 However, CMR may be limited by motion artifacts and tumor size, which can degrade image quality and compromise accurate tissue analysis. Cardiac computed tomography is valuable for detecting myocardial infiltration, chamber compression, pericardial or great vessel involvement, intratumoral calcification, fat content, and mass vascularity.9 Given the inherent limitations of echocardiography, CMR, and CCT in confirming malignancy, we recommend incorporating positron emission tomography when clinical suspicion of a malignant tumor is high.

A conclusive diagnosis was ultimately established only through histopathological analysis. This highlights an essential clinical lesson: For surgical cases of culture-negative suspected endocarditis displaying atypical characteristics, the utilization of intraoperative frozen section analysis or rapid turnaround histopathology is highly advisable. Such an approach directly informs surgical decision-making, particularly regarding resection margins. Unlike procedures for infectious foci, the management of sarcomas necessitates prioritizing complete excision with wide margins, even when this entails significant myocardial resection.

The prognosis for primary cardiac sarcomas remains poor, primarily due to frequent late-stage diagnosis. Historical outcomes show a median survival of 8 months, with overall survival rates of 48.1% at 2 years and 10.1% at 5 years.10 Treatment strategy depends on tumor location and stage, with complete surgical resection representing the cornerstone of therapy and being associated with survival benefits up to 3 years.11 Notably, left-sided cardiac sarcomas demonstrate better survival outcomes than right-sided tumors, likely due to their less aggressive biological behavior—typically exhibiting less local invasion at diagnosis and delayed metastatic spread compared to right heart counterparts. Clinically, left heart sarcomas more commonly present with heart failure symptoms.12 For young patients with localized, unresectable disease and no distant metastasis, artificial heart implantation and cardiac transplantation have emerged as promising recent therapeutic options. However, chemotherapy demonstrates limited efficacy in altering disease progression, emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary evaluation to tailor treatment strategies based on institutional experience and individual patient profiles. Dedicated clinical trials and pharmacological advances are urgently needed to improve therapeutic approaches for cardiac sarcomas.13

Conclusion

This case illustrates that primary cardiac liposarcoma can closely mimic IE both clinically and echocardiographically. In young patients with atypical features, negative blood cultures, or rapid clinical deterioration, malignancy should be considered in the differential diagnosis. A multidisciplinary approach involving cardiac oncology, cardiac surgery, and pathology is essential for timely diagnosis and appropriate management.
