Aqueous fraction of Alternanthera philoxeroides as a biodegradable corrosion inhibitor of mild steel in sulphuric acid solution
Authors: Gayatri Maiya Koju, Anju Kumari Das, Sanjay Singh, Dipak Kumar Gupta, Nabin Karki, Amar Prasad Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aqueous extract of Alternanthera philoxeroides effectively inhibited the corrosion of mild steel in 1.0 M H2SO4, as tested using gravimetric and electrochemical methods. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis identified phytochemical components with functional groups that interact with the metal surface. The aqueous extract showed very good anticorrosive inhibition activity. Inhibition efficiency increased with inhibitor concentration and reached its optimum at 3 h immersion time. The decrease in inhibition efficiency at higher temperatures suggested partial desorption of adsorbed inhibitor molecules. The Langmuir isotherm model described the adsorption of the inhibitor on the mild steel surface, and the free energy of adsorption (ΔGads° = −33.18 kJ mol−1) indicated mixed-mode adsorption, involving both physical and chemical adsorption. The formation of an efficient protective layer that minimized damage and roughness on the metal surface was observed during surface characterization using field emission scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FE-SEM/EDX) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis indicated the adsorption of C-, O-, and N-containing species that formed bonds with iron atoms, thereby confirming the interaction of inhibitors with the metal surface. Consequently, this study demonstrated the effectiveness of the aqueous fraction of Alternanthera philoxeroides as an eco-friendly inhibitor for mild steel in an acidic environment.

Introduction

Acid solutions are widely employed in petrochemical processes, acid pickling, boiler cleaning, acidization of oil wells, and the removal of rust and scale.1,2 Due to the overall aggressiveness of acid solutions, inhibitors are generally applied to reduce the corrosive attack on metal materials. Chromates and other inorganic inhibitors have been banned due to their harmful effects on health and the environment.3 However, organic compounds containing oxygen, phosphorus, sulphur, and nitrogen atoms demonstrate good corrosion inhibition.4,5 Corrosion inhibition can be accomplished by utilising organic compounds that adsorb onto the metal surface. This process helps prevent corrosion reactions and reduces the overall corrosion rate. The adsorption process may occur through electrostatic attractions between charges and the metal surface or by interactions between π-electrons and metal orbitals. Factors that determine the effectiveness of the protective film include functional group availability, molecular structure, aromatic character, steric effect, electron–donor property, corrosion medium, metal surface charge, and molecular orbital interactions of the inhibitor with the d-orbitals of iron.6,7

The inhibitory action of organic compounds has been confirmed by several studies; however, they also have limitations. They are found to be hazardous to human beings and the environment during synthesis and use. A few organic compounds have been found to be eco-friendly green inhibitors, but their application is limited due to high costs and poor economic feasibility.2

Many studies have focused on developing efficient and environmentally friendly corrosion inhibitors. Plant extracts and natural products, such as essential oils, are potential substitutes for traditional corrosion inhibitors. These substances can act as effective corrosion inhibitors due to their active compounds, which include alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols, terpenoids, carbohydrates, and proteins.8 There are many reasons why plant extract-based corrosion inhibitors can serve as effective alternatives to toxic and costly corrosion inhibitors. These reasons include their biodegradability, renewability, nontoxicity, environmental friendliness, availability, and affordability.2,8–10

There are several studies demonstrating the efficacy of plant extracts as corrosion inhibitors, including Tinospora crispa extracts,11Canna indica flower extract,12Berberis asiatica,13Acalypha chamaedrifolia leaves extract,14 extracts of Chamaerops humilis L. fruits,15 extract of Eucalyptus globules16etc.; have been reported as green corrosion inhibitors.

Extensive research has been carried out on the effectiveness of aqueous extracts from various plants in inhibiting corrosion. Such studies have included aqueous extracts of Allium sativum,17 olive leaves,18 and zallouh roots.19 Similarly, the aqueous extracts of curcumin, pasley, and cassia bark were also studied.20

The corrosion inhibition properties of the aqueous fraction of Alternanthera philoxeroides (AAP) were evaluated for industrial steel applications. The evaluation was carried out using both gravimetric and electrochemical techniques. The assessment of inhibitor effectiveness in electrochemical analysis required impedance and polarization measurements.

Experimental

Collection of plants and extraction of aqueous fraction

Alternanthera philoxeroides was collected from Byasi-2, Bhaktapur, Nepal (27.68° N, 85.43° E) (MCGG + CXG, Bhaktapur, 44 800, Nepal). The identification of the plant sample as Alternanthera philoxeroides (Mart.) Griseb. Was carried out by the National Herbarium and Plant Laboratories, Godawori, Lalitpur, Nepal.

The plant was collected and washed with distilled water to remove the dust. It was dried in the shade at room temperature, then ground to form a fine powder. The powder was treated with 25% ammonium hydroxide to convert the salts of basic phytochemicals into their free bases. The mixture was left for 4 hours and dried. It was macerated with methanol for 72 hours. The filtrate was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator (MVP 10 B SO22). The concentrated mixture was then treated with 5% hydrochloric acid to maintain a pH of 3–4. This converted the basic phytochemicals into salts. The mixture was then partitioned between a dichloromethane (DCM) solution, in which the salts remained in the aqueous portion, and the rest of the phytochemicals went into the DCM layer.

The salts were then converted into free bases by adjusting the pH to 11–12 using 25% ammonium hydroxide. The mixture was then partitioned with a DCM solution. The aqueous portion was collected and evaporated to obtain a crude aqueous fraction that was used in this study as a corrosion inhibitor. The aqueous fraction was also characterized using UV-vis, FTIR, and GC-MS. The resulting paste was used as the corrosion inhibitor.

Characterization of aqueous fraction

Spectroscopic techniques were used to characterize the functional groups and compounds present in the AAP. The UV-vis spectroscopy results for the APP were obtained by scanning from 200 to 800 nm using a 5 mm quartz cell and a SHIMADZU UV-3600. KBr pellets were used with a BRUKER TENSOR 27 FTIR. GC-MS was used to confirm the constituents of the AAP.

Preparation of metal sample and inhibitor solutions

Mild steel (MS) was used as the test material for corrosion experiments. For gravimetric analysis, the sample size was 3 × 3 × 0.15 cm3, and for electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and polarization experiments, the size was 2 × 2 cm2. The steel plates were successively polished with silicon carbide (SiC) papers of 100 to 2000 grit. After the polishing process, the steel plates were cleaned by washing with hexane, ultrasonication in ethanol for 15 minutes, and finally dried with a blower.

The 1.0 M H2SO4 solution was prepared by diluting concentrated H2SO4 with distilled water. A stock solution of 1000 ppm of AAP was prepared using 1.0 M H2SO4. The desired concentrations of the inhibitors were obtained by appropriately diluting this stock solution. The AAP concentrations investigated ranged from 100 ppm to 350 ppm.

Corrosion inhibition study

Gravimetric and electrochemical methods were used to study the corrosion inhibition efficiency. In the gravimetric section, the weight of the metal was measured before and after immersion, both with and without an inhibitor solution. The experiments were conducted with varying inhibitor concentrations, immersion periods, and temperatures. In the electrochemical section, a standard three-electrode system with a Gamry Instruments Interface™ 1010 potentiostat was used.

Gravimetric method

The dimensions of the mild steel samples were measured using digital Vernier calipers, and the mass was measured using a four-digit balance. The experiment involved corrosion of the samples by immersing them in 75 mL of test solution for specific time periods at particular temperatures with the required amount of inhibitors.

After the immersion period elapsed, the specimen samples were withdrawn from the solution, cleaned to remove any corrosion products that had formed, and then washed with acetone. The samples were dried and placed in a desiccator to prevent moisture damage. This process was carried out according to the standard protocol as ASTM G31. The mass loss of the samples was determined from the weights before and after experimentation. Electrochemical methods

Gamry Instruments Interface™ 1010 was used to study the electrochemical methods. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) were performed in a three-electrode system. Mild steel was used as the working electrode, a platinum electrode as the counter electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode. Before the experiment, the mild steel electrode was immersed in the test solution for 3 hours to get a stable open-circuit potential (OCP). After that, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and polarization resistance tests were conducted at the OCP, according to standard protocol ASTM G3 and ASTM G59, respectively.

Impedance measurement was performed at the stable open-circuit potential value under the AC perturbation with an amplitude of 10 mV. Surface analysis

The MS samples immersed in acid with and without AAP were washed with distilled water, dried in air, and stored in a desiccator. FE-SEM and AFM were carried out for surface morphology, and EDX and XPS for elemental composition.

Adsorption isotherm

An adsorption isotherm is the relationship between the amount of substance adsorbed and the concentration of the molecules at constant temperature and pH. Isotherm models describe the nature of the adsorption surface and the strength of the adsorbate–surface interaction. Isotherm models can be used to identify the adsorption mechanism. Adsorption isotherms are very important in corrosion inhibition studies.24 It is largely influenced by the nature of the metal and its charge, solvent molecules, and other ions' adsorption, the electronic properties of the metal, the temperature of the reaction, and the electrochemical potential of the interface between the solution and the metal.25 The mode and extent of interaction between inhibitors and the metal surface can be explained by understanding a few key thermodynamic parameters, i.e., adsorption free energy, activation energy, enthalpy, and entropy. In an aqueous medium, water molecules are first absorbed on the metal surface through dipole attractions. As a result, the adsorption of inhibitors takes place through the replacement of the adsorbed water dipoles, leading to quasi-substitution adsorption and is given by eqn (6) and (7).26,276(In h)sol + XH2Oads ↔ (In h)ads + XH2Osol7Fe2+ + (In h)ads ↔ [Fe − (In h)]ads2+

Since the inhibitor AAP is a crude aqueous extract comprising a mixture of organic constituents, each component may contribute either positively or negatively to the overall inhibition performance. Nevertheless, for adsorption isotherm analysis, the inhibitor concentration was estimated using the average molar concentration of the principal compounds presumed to be primarily responsible for the observed corrosion inhibition.13,19 The measured compounds present in the extract are shown in Fig. 2. The adsorption followed the Langmuir adsorption isotherm. The Langmuir plot (Fig. 6) shows a linear relationship with a high correlation coefficient (R2) and a slope close to unity (0.999), indicating the monolayer adsorption of the inhibitor molecules on the mild steel surface. The Langmuir adsorption isotherm implies unimolecular interaction between the adsorbed molecules. The value of the adsorption equilibrium constant kads was obtained from the intercept of the linear plot of Cinh/θ vs. Cinh, as given by the Langmuir adsorption isotherm eqn (8).288

The adsorption constants Kads were obtained from the intercepts of the straight lines when plotted Cinh/θ versus Cinh. The Kads values were then used in eqn (9) to obtain the standard free energy of adsorption (ΔG°).299ΔGads0 = −RT(ln 55.5 Kads)where 55.5 is the molar concentration of the solution in water, R is the gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature.

The standard free energy of adsorption, ΔG°, indicates the spontaneity and strength of adsorption of AAP molecules onto the mild steel surface. A high adsorption equilibrium constant, kads, indicates effective and rapid adsorption of the inhibitor onto the metal surface, hence increasing the inhibition efficiency. The strong adsorption characteristics of AAP are due to the presence of heteroatoms, including nitrogen and oxygen, as well as π-electrons present in the AAP extract molecules, which are responsible for donor–acceptor interactions with the mild steel surface.

The ΔG° value of −33.18 kJ mol−1 indicates that the adsorption of AAP extract on the MS surface is a spontaneous process, and the adsorption process involved both physical adsorption and chemical adsorption (mixed type of adsorption). It is agreed that if the values of ΔG° are equal to or less than −40 kJ mol−1, it is indicative of chemisorption, while if the values fall in the range −20 kJ mol−1 or higher, it is indicative of physisorption.30

An Ea of 20 kJ mol−1 or lower is an indication of physical adsorption, while an Ea of 80 kJ mol−1 or higher is an indication of chemical adsorption.26 The result obtained in this study indicated both physical and chemical adsorption.

The diameter of the semicircles increases with increasing inhibitor concentration. The presence of a single time constant in the Nyquist plot at high and medium frequencies indicates that the corrosion of mild steel in 1 M acid is dominated by a charge transfer mechanism.28 The Bode plots of magnitude and phase angle (Fig. 10a and b) indicate one time constant, indicating that there is only one capacitive loop from the Nyquist plots. It is therefore evident that the rate-determining step in the corrosion process for the mild steel sample in the acidic environment is charge transfer. The phase angle diagram also indicates that a protective film formed on the surface of the metal.34,35

It is noteworthy that varying the concentration of AAP extracts did not affect the shape of impedance curves. This indicated that the inhibition mechanism is not altered during the inhibition process. The Nyquist plots (Fig. 8b) show a depressed semicircular response in both the uninhibited and inhibited systems; this can be explained as surface inhomogeneity. Thus, it is more appropriate to replace the capacitance with a constant phase element.11,36

Eqn (12) and (13) describe the CPE's impedance function and the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) derived from it.12ZCPE = Y0−1(jw)−n13Cdl = (Y0Rctn−1)−nwhere Y0 is the CPE constant, w is the wave modulation angular frequency, j2 = −1 is the imaginary number, and n = α/(π/2) in which α represent the phase angle of CPE and n, i.e., the CPE exponent (−1 ≤ n ≤ 1), quantify the deviation from the ideal capacitive behavior and shows the surface non-homogeneous or roughness. The CPE represents a pure resistor, capacitor, and inductor when n is 0, −1, and +1, respectively.12,37

The diameter of this semicircle is given by the polarization resistance (Rp), which is the sum of all resistances in the system. Without an inhibitor, Rp consists of the charge-transfer resistance at the metal-solution interface (Rct), the diffusion-layer resistance (Rd), and the accumulation of corrosion products at the interface (Ra). However, in the presence of an inhibitor, Rp consists of the film resistance (Rf), which occurs at the adsorbed organic layer at the metal surface and possible complexes of organics and metals, such that Rp = Rct + Rd + Ra + Rf.21

The polarization resistance (Rp) values increased progressively with increasing AAP concentration, reflecting the improved corrosion resistance of mild steel in the acidic solution. The increase in Rp is due to the adsorption of AAP molecules and the formation of a protective layer. CPE values decrease with increased AAP concentrations due to the displacement of water molecules at the metal-water interface by AAP molecules. As a result, there is a decrease in corrosion rate.21

All Nyquist plots revealed an inductive loop at low frequencies. In an acidic environment, this inductive loop is normally associated with the adsorption of intermediate corrosion products, such as [FeSO4(ads)2−] and [FeOH]−. As the concentration of AAP increased, the inductive loop became more pronounced, signifying increased metal–AAP interactions on the mild steel surface. It indicated that the adsorption and desorption of AAP molecules were under a dynamic equilibrium.38 The maximum inhibition efficiency was attained at 94.61% at 300 ppm. The inhibition of corrosion by aqueous extracts from various plants has been studied extensively in the literature. The aqueous extract of olive leaves had an inhibition efficiency of 93.0% at 900 ppm.18 The same efficiency of 92.0% was observed from Allium sativum extract,17 and Zallouh root extract recorded an inhibition efficiency of 93.00% at 900 ppm.19 In addition, curcumin, parsley, and cassia bark extracts recorded inhibition efficiencies of more than 80.0% at 500 ppm.20

In this study, the AAP extract showed an inhibition efficiency of 95.65% at a very low concentration of 300 ppm, indicating that it has much better results than many other extracts reported earlier. This high inhibition efficiency at such a low concentration proves the strong ability of AAP extract to inhibit corrosion of mild steel in acid solutions.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS analyses were also carried out to determine the composition of the inhibited MS after 3 h of immersion in 1.0 M H2SO4 with 300 ppm of AAP (at this concentration, the highest inhibition efficiency was achieved). The obtained XPS spectra (C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and Fe 2p) were assigned to corresponding species through a deconvolution fitting procedure. The C 1s spectrum (Fig. 14a) of MS with AAP can be fitted into 3 components with different intensities. The major component at a binding energy of −285 eV can be attributed to C–C, CC, and C–H aromatic bonds. The second component at 286 eV can be attributed to C–N and CN. The last component at a higher binding energy of 188 eV can be represented by the CO bond and/or due to the C–N+ and CN+.41

XPS deconvolution of C 1s, O 1s, N 1s and Fe 2p spectrum.

The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 14b) can be fitted into three peaks. The first peak at binding energy 530 eV could be related to oxygen atoms bonded to Fe3+ as FeO and/or Fe2O3. The second peak at binding energy 532 eV is ascribed to OH− as FeOOH and Fe(OH)3. The third peak at binding energy 533 eV is assigned to the bonding of oxygen atoms with carbon (CO) and physically adsorbed water molecules on the metal surface.42

Fig. 14c represents the N 1s spectrum. The first peak at binding energy 400 eV can be attributed to the Fe–N bond. The second component, at a binding energy of 399 eV, could be attributed to the C–N bond. The AAP extract could be physically or chemically adsorbed in the presence of the Fe–N bond.36

As shown in Fig. 14d, the Fe 2p spectra consist of two sharp peaks at around 711 eV and 724 eV, corresponding to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, respectively. The Fe 2p3/2 spectrum can be deconvoluted into two distinct peaks at nearly 707 eV for Fe0 (metallic iron) and 711 eV for Fe3+ (FeO, Fe2O3, and FeOOH). The binding energy at 724 eV can be ascribed to the satellite of the ferric compounds.35

XPS analysis revealed nitrogen- and oxygen-containing compounds on the mild steel surface, indicating the adsorption of AAP compounds on the surface of mild steel. Under the action of the acid medium, there is an initial oxidation of the surface of mild steel. Then, AAP compounds interact with the surface of mild steel and get adsorbed, thus creating a protective layer.

Corrosion inhibition mechanism

Thermodynamic parameters show that the inhibitive effect of the AAP extract is due to adsorption on the mild steel surface through a mixed adsorption mechanism. In acidic environments, the mild steel surface is positively charged, which favors the adsorption of SO42− ions on the metal surface. Protonation of heteroatoms occurs when AAP molecules are exposed to an acid medium, producing positively charged inhibitors. This leads to the electrostatic attraction of protons towards the negatively charged areas on the surface of the mild steel, which become adsorbed in the cathodic regions. This formed layer prevents hydrogen ions from accessing the surface, hence minimizing the hydrogen evolution reaction rate.43

The AAP molecules possess lone pairs of electrons in their HOMO orbitals, which are available for donation to the unoccupied orbitals present on the metal surface. The formation of coordinate bonds occurs due to this donation of lone pairs of electrons. The adsorption occurs mainly at the anodic points, which helps to prevent the dissolution of the metals.44

During this process, electrons accumulate on the metal surface, and the mild steel gets a negative charge. In order to balance the charges, electrons are back-donated (retro-donated) to the LUMO of the AAF molecules by the 4s orbitals of the metal.26

Conclusion

The aqueous fraction of Alternanthera philoxeroides exhibits excellent corrosion inhibition performance for mild steel in 1.0 M H2SO4, achieving a maximum inhibition efficiency of 95.65% at 300 ppm and 303 K. The inhibition efficiency increased with inhibitor concentration but decreased with increasing temperature, indicating partial desorption of the adsorbed species at elevated temperatures. Adsorption of the inhibitor followed the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, and the calculated free energy of adsorption suggested a mixed adsorption mechanism involving both physisorption and chemisorption. Thermodynamic and kinetic analyses indicated that the corrosion process is activation-controlled and proceeds through a unimolecular reaction pathway. Electrochemical measurements revealed that the inhibitor effectively suppresses the corrosion rate without altering the underlying corrosion mechanism. Surface analyses by FE-SEM, AFM, and XPS confirmed the formation of a protective adsorbed film composed of carbon-, nitrogen-, and oxygen-containing species on the mild steel surface. These findings demonstrate that the aqueous fraction of Alternanthera philoxeroides is a promising, environmentally friendly corrosion inhibitor for mild steel in acidic media.
