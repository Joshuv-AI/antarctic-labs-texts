Mindfulness and academic engagement among medical students: the roles of psychological capital and self-concept clarity
Authors: Aamer Aldbyani, Afnan Alhimaidi, Zhang Chuanxia, Jie Leng, Zaid Alhadoor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-concept clarity and mindfulness have gained increasing attention in educational and psychological research, as they are considered important variables in explaining differences in students’ levels of academic engagement. However, the moderating role of psychological capital in this relationship remains insufficiently understood.ObjectivesThis study examines the mediating role of self-concept clarity in the association between mindfulness and academic engagement, and the moderating role of psychological capital in this pathway.MethodsA total of 503 medical students from Shandong Xiehe University, Jinan, China, participated in the study and completed the Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale–Revised (CAMS-R), the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S), the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS), and the Student Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ).ResultsAnalysis revealed that self-concept clarity partially mediated the relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement. The mediation model explained 11.0% of the variance in academic engagement (R2 = .1102, F(5, 497) = 12.31, p < .001), and the interaction term accounted for an additional 1.2% of explained variance (ΔR2 = .0123, p = .007). In addition, psychological capital moderated the relationship between mindfulness and self-concept clarity, thereby strengthening this indirect pathway and contributing to higher levels of academic engagement.ConclusionThese findings provide evidence for the psychological mechanisms underlying student engagement and offer practical implications for medical education, including the development of mindfulness-based interventions and strategies to enhance psychological capital among medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03760-3.

Introduction

Medical education represents a high-stakes environment characterized by intense workloads, emotional strain, and frequent exposure to stressful situations [14, 40]. These pressures make medical students particularly susceptible to disengagement and burnout, underscoring the importance of identifying psychological factors that support their engagement and resilience [1, 37]. Moreover, engagement among this group has direct implications for academic achievement, clinical competence, and patient care outcomes. Low levels of academic engagement among medical students have been linked to reduced academic performance, increased burnout, and lower clinical competence. These challenges not only affect students’ well-being and professional development but may also have long-term implications for the stability and quality of healthcare delivery. Strengthening academic engagement is therefore a critical educational and public health priority, particularly in medical education, where students’ sustained involvement may directly be connected to future workforce readiness.

Therefore, understanding the psychological mechanisms that support medical students' engagement in learning is essential for both academic and clinical training contexts. The psychological mechanisms that support medical students' engagement in learning are therefore essential. Recent work has emphasized the relevance of mindfulness as a dispositional resource that promotes attentional stability and emotional balance [7]. Mindfulness, defined as sustained receptive attention to present-moment experience with openness and non-judgment [24], has been identified as a key psychological resource for regulating attention and emotion. At the same time, the concept of self-concept clarity—defined as the extent to which individuals hold clearly articulated and consistent beliefs about themselves [8]—has gained attention for its role in supporting adaptive functioning and academic persistence. Both constructs have been independently linked to students’ ability to sustain engagement in demanding educational settings.

Emerging evidence further suggests that these factors are interconnected. Cross-sectional studies consistently report positive associations between mindfulness and self-concept clarity [6, 13, 20, 36], indicating that receptive attention to present experience may foster more coherent self-beliefs. Psychological capital may amplify the association between mindfulness on academic engagement by enhancing students’ ability to sustain attention, regulate emotions, and remain motivated in demanding learning contexts. This is consistent with evidence that psychological resources strengthen the effects of positive psychological states on performance and engagement [39, 50]. In parallel, research has documented those higher levels of self-concept clarity are associated with greater academic engagement, reflecting more sustained cognitive, behavioral, and emotional investment in learning [10, 28, 45, 51]. These findings suggest that self-concept clarity may function as an intermediate mechanism linking mindfulness to academic engagement.

In addition to these pathways, psychological capital refers to a set of positive psychological resources that enable individuals to maintain motivation and adapt effectively in challenging contexts [30]. Studies have shown that psychological capital can enhance the positive effects of mindfulness [39, 50], and it has also been identified as a mediator in the relationship between mindfulness and other adaptive outcomes, such as creativity [22]. Yet, its potential role in moderating the link between mindfulness and self-concept clarity has not been systematically investigated.

Collectively, these observations highlight the need for an integrative model that examines how mindfulness relates to academic engagement through self-concept clarity and how psychological capital may influence this pathway. Although numerous studies have examined the direct relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement [11, 15, 33], most have focused on documenting this association rather than explaining the underlying processes and boundary conditions. The present study extends this line of research by examining the mediating role of self-concept clarity and the moderating role of psychological capital. This approach clarifies how and under what conditions mindfulness contributes to students’ engagement, thereby addressing an unresolved gap in the literature.

Accordingly, this study contributes to advancing theoretical and practical understanding by clarifying how mindfulness is associated with academic engagement through self-concept clarity, while highlighting the role of psychological capital as a facilitating factor in this relationship. This addresses a critical gap in the existing literature and enhances the applied value of the findings in medical education contexts.

To provide a solid theoretical foundation for this model, the study draws on multiple perspectives. Monitor-and-Acceptance Theory explains how mindful awareness fosters more coherent self-representations, clarifying the mechanisms that link mindfulness to self-related psychological processes. Self-Determination Theory emphasizes how these self-related processes support autonomous motivation and sustained academic engagement. Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory offers a broader integrative framework that connects mindfulness, self-concept clarity, and psychological resources, providing theoretical grounding for the proposed conceptual model. Together, these perspectives establish a coherent rationale for the study and align the theoretical background with its objectives. Therefore, our study addresses this gap by testing a moderated mediation framework in a sample of medical students, aiming to clarify the combined roles of these psychological resources in sustaining engagement in demanding academic environments.

Mindfulness and self-concept clarity

Mindfulness is defined as sustained, receptive attention to present-moment experiences with an attitude of openness and non-judgment [7, 24]. It is often described as a multifaceted disposition involving attentional regulation and the quality of awareness brought to current experience [4, 7].

Self-concept clarity refers to the extent to which an individual’s beliefs about the self are clearly defined, internally consistent, and relatively stable over time [8, 46]. Individuals with higher self-concept clarity report a more coherent and organized sense of identity, whereas lower levels reflect fragmented or unstable self-beliefs.

Cross-sectional research consistently shows a positive association between mindfulness and self-concept clarity. Dummel [13] reported that individuals with higher mindfulness scores also reported higher levels of self-concept clarity, indicating that these two constructs tend to covary. Borgdorf et al. [6] emphasized the conceptual relevance of self-concept clarity when considering the psychological correlates of mindfulness. Further studies have found positive correlations between these variables [20, 36]. Moderate positive associations between mindfulness and self-concept clarity have been observed across different samples [32, 36, 42]. Additional evidence indicates that the two constructs are functionally related in various psychological contexts [44], and that specific facets of mindfulness—particularly present-moment awareness and non-judgment—show reliable positive associations with self-concept clarity [49]. Other work suggests that mindfulness is related to self-concept clarity as part of broader patterns of self-regulation [5]. Building on this foundation, the present study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1: Mindfulness is positively correlated with self-concept clarity.

Self-concept clarity and academic engagement

Academic engagement is commonly understood as a multidimensional construct that reflects students’ cognitive, behavioral, and emotional involvement in their learning. Astin [3] characterized it as the amount of physical and psychological energy that students invest in academic experiences. Subsequent work expanded this perspective by emphasizing sustained effort, persistence, and active participation in both academic and related social contexts [25, 34]. Engagement is generally described in terms of cognitive readiness to address challenging tasks, behavioral participation in instructional activities, and emotional involvement reflected in interest, pride, and a sense of belonging [18]. It is widely regarded as a motivational and affective process that underpins achievement and persistence, with higher levels of engagement consistently associated with improved academic performance and greater satisfaction with the educational experience [2, 37].

Recent research has documented consistent associations between self-concept clarity and academic engagement in student populations. In a cross-sectional study with secondary-school students, Liu et al. [28] reported that higher levels of self-concept clarity were associated with greater engagement in learning activities. Similarly, Zhou et al. [51], using a school- and university-based sample, found positive correlations between self-concept clarity and key components of engagement, including vigor, dedication, and absorption. Evidence from another cross-sectional investigation in a performance-oriented learning context showed that self-concept clarity was positively related to indicators of engagement such as energy and commitment [45]. Additional findings from studies of adolescents and university students have also described positive links between self-concept clarity and academic motivation or persistence, both of which are central elements of engagement (e.g., [23, 35]).

Collectively, these findings suggest that students with a clearer and more coherent sense of self tend to show higher cognitive, behavioral, and emotional engagement. Building on this foundation, the present study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2: Self-concept clarity is positively correlated with academic engagement.

H2: Self-concept clarity is positively correlated with academic engagement.

H2: Self-concept clarity is positively correlated with academic engagement.

Self-concept clarity as a mediator

Recent research suggests that self-concept clarity functions as an intermediate dimension linking mindfulness and academic engagement, as present-moment awareness and non-judgmental attention have been associated with more coherent and stable self-beliefs [7, 13]. In turn, higher self-concept clarity has been consistently related to stronger cognitive, behavioral, and emotional involvement in learning contexts [28, 51].

To our knowledge, no previous research has directly tested the mediating role of self-concept clarity in the association between mindfulness and academic engagement. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicates that self-concept clarity is often involved in the statistical associations linking mindfulness to various psychological and behavioral outcomes. For example, Mai et al. [32] reported that self-concept clarity statistically mediated the association between mindfulness and meaning in life, reflecting that greater clarity of self-beliefs was linked to higher levels of perceived purpose. Stenhaug and Solem [42] similarly found that both self-concept clarity and cognitive flexibility accounted for part of the association between mindfulness and self-esteem, suggesting that similar processes may also be observed in the context of academic engagement.

In addition, Law et al. [26] documented that participant in mindfulness-based programs showed higher levels of self-concept clarity, which was statistically related to improvements in emotion regulation, indicating that self-related processes may explain part of the link between mindfulness and adaptive outcomes. Parallel findings have been reported in academic contexts,for instance, Suhadianto et al. [43] found that academic self-concept, a construct conceptually related to self-concept clarity, accounted for the association between mindfulness and academic engagement, while Ljubin Golub and Gajšek [29] reported that academic flow played a similar mediating role. Further evidence shows that self-concept clarity was a significant mediator of the association between mindfulness and reduced attitudinal ambivalence, underscoring its relevance as an intermediate construct in diverse domains [13].

These associations can be contextualized within complementary frameworks. Monitor-and-Acceptance Theory proposes that receptive monitoring coupled with nonreactivity facilitates more stable self-representations [27], consistent with the reperceiving account in which metacognitive awareness helps consolidate self-knowledge [41]. In addition, the reperceiving model proposed by Shapiro et al. [41] provides a conceptual framework for understanding how mindfulness influences self-related processes. This model suggests that mindfulness facilitates a metacognitive shift in awareness, allowing individuals to observe their internal experiences with greater objectivity. This shift contributes to the development of more coherent and stable self-representations, which in turn supports the formation of clearer self-concept clarity. Including this perspective clarifies the theoretical link between mindfulness and self-concept clarity. Self-Determination Theory links mindful attention with autonomy and integrated regulation—conditions associated with clearer self-definition and sustained involvement—while control-theoretic models and the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory describe attention-guided feedback and broaden-and-build appraisal processes that align with connections from mindfulness to coherent self-views and academic engagement [9, 12, 19, 38].

Accordingly, our third hypothesis is as follows:

H3: Self-concept clarity mediates the relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement.

Psychological capital as a moderator

Psychological capital is a higher-order positive psychological construct comprising four core components: self-efficacy, optimism, hope, and resilience. It represents an individual’s positive appraisal of circumstances and capacity to mobilize the necessary cognitive, motivational, and emotional resources to succeed [30]. Within educational contexts, psychological capital has been shown to support adaptive functioning, sustain motivation, and enhance well-being. By fostering these personal resources, psychological capital contributes to individuals’ ability to manage challenges and leverage opportunities for personal and professional growth.

Drawing on Conservation of Resources Theory [53], we posit that psychological capital functions as a key personal resource that amplifies the benefits of mindfulness. Individuals with higher levels of psychological capital (self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience) possess a greater 'resource reservoir' to draw upon. This enriched reservoir is hypothesized to enhance their capacity to translate mindful awareness into a more coherent and stable self-concept. In other words, when a mindful individual also possesses high psychological capital, their confidence, hopefulness, and resilience likely provide the motivational and emotional foundation necessary to successfully integrate mindful insights into a clearer sense of self.

The moderating role of psychological capital is theoretically grounded in resource-based and self-regulatory perspectives. Mindfulness facilitates awareness and emotional regulation, but the translation of this awareness into a coherent and stable self-concept depends on the availability of psychological resources. Individuals with higher psychological capital—characterized by self-efficacy, optimism, hope, and resilience—are more capable of transforming mindful insights into stable self-representations. In this sense, psychological capital strengthens the mindfulness–self-concept clarity link because it provides the motivational and emotional foundation necessary for integrating moment-to-moment awareness into enduring self-knowledge. This moderation is less theoretically relevant for other links in the model, as the relationship between self-concept clarity and engagement reflects an outcome process rather than a resource-dependent transformation.

While previous research has often focused on psychological capital as a mediator (e.g., [22, 50]), its role as a moderating resource that strengthens the pathway from a state-like awareness (mindfulness) to a trait-like self-structure (self-concept clarity) remains novel and theoretically grounded in resource-based models of human functioning. However, to date, no study has specifically examined the interaction between psychological capital and self-concept clarity within the context of mindfulness. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4: Psychological Capital moderates the relationship between mindfulness and self-concept clarity.

Theoretical framework and conceptual foundations

This study employs Self-Determination Theory and Conservation of Resources Theory as the guiding frameworks to explain the proposed mediating and moderating mechanisms linking mindfulness, self-concept clarity, psychological capital, and academic engagement. Self-Determination Theory emphasizes that individuals engage more deeply in academic tasks when their sense of autonomy and integrated regulation is supported. Mindfulness facilitates these processes by promoting stable and coherent self-representations that strengthen internal motivation and sustained engagement [12, 38]. From this perspective, self-concept clarity is not only a psychological outcome of mindful attention but also a central mechanism through which individuals maintain academic focus and persistence.

Conservation of Resources Theory complements this explanation by highlighting how personal resources enable individuals to preserve and build adaptive capacities [53]. Psychological capital—comprising self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience—functions as a resource reservoir that strengthens the impact of mindfulness on self-concept clarity. Students with higher psychological capital are better positioned to translate mindful awareness into coherent self-beliefs, thereby enhancing their engagement in demanding academic contexts. This theoretical alignment provides a structured rationale for examining both mediation and moderation in the present model.

Review of core constructs

Mindfulness

Mindfulness is characterized by sustained and receptive attention to present-moment experiences with openness and non-judgment [7, 24]. Prior research has linked mindfulness to improved attentional regulation, emotional balance, and adaptive functioning in academic settings.

Self-concept clarity

Self-concept clarity refers to the degree to which self-beliefs are clearly defined, internally consistent, and stable [8]. Empirical studies have documented positive associations between mindfulness and self-concept clarity [13, 20], indicating that receptive attention supports the development of coherent self-representations. Such clarity is associated with stronger cognitive and motivational engagement in academic contexts [28, 51].

Psychological capital

Psychological capital reflects a set of positive psychological resources that support adaptive functioning and persistence. It has been linked to enhanced motivation, well-being, and learning outcomes, and recent findings indicate that it can amplify the benefits of mindfulness in educational settings [39, 50].

Positioning the current study

Existing research has addressed the associations between these constructs individually, but their integration within a unified theoretical framework remains limited. Previous studies have established that mindfulness contributes to academic functioning and that self-concept clarity is associated with engagement; however, the mechanisms linking these constructs, as well as the potential moderating role of psychological capital, have not been systematically examined. By applying Self-Determination Theory and Conservation of Resources Theory, the current study provides a structured explanation of how mindfulness is associated with academic engagement through self-concept clarity and how psychological capital strengthens this indirect effect. This theoretical integration addresses a specific gap in the literature and situates the study within established psychological and educational frameworks.

Method

This cross-sectional study proposed and tested a moderated mediation model to explain the relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement. Specifically, we hypothesized that self-concept clarity would mediate this relationship. Furthermore, we hypothesized that psychological capital would moderate the first stage of the mediation model, such that the positive effect of mindfulness on self-concept clarity would be stronger for individuals with higher levels of psychological capital.

Participants

Participants were 503 undergraduate medical students from Shandong Xiehe University in China. All participants provided informed consent, and confidentiality and anonymity were ensured throughout the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Academic Committee of Shandong Xiehe University, and the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Recruitment was conducted through announcements on the university’s internal platforms and voluntary sign-ups during the first semester of 2025. The questionnaire was administered online over three weeks in September 2025. Of the 520 students invited, 503 provided valid responses, resulting in a response rate of 96.7%. To ensure data quality, attention-check items were included, and incomplete or duplicate responses were excluded before analysis. The data used in this study were part of a larger dataset collected for a broader research project. Portions of this dataset have been or will be analyzed in separate manuscripts that address different research questions and use distinct theoretical frameworks and analytic methods. Measurements

Mindfulness

The Chinese version of the Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale–Revised (CAMS-R; [17]) comprises 12 items and has been validated as a reliable measure in Chinese populations [47]. Responses are provided on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always). In the present study, the scale showed satisfactory internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79.

Self-concept clarity

The Chinese version of the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS; [8]) consists of 12 items and has been validated as a reliable measure in Chinese populations [52]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In the present study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82.

Psychological capital

The Chinese version of the University Student Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ; [31]) consists of 29 items and has been validated as a reliable instrument in Chinese contexts [48]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all consistent) to 5 (completely consistent). In the present study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77. The official permission letter for the use of the Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ) is provided in the supplementary materials.

Academic engagement

The Chinese version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S; [40]) consists of 17 items and has been validated as a reliable measure in Chinese contexts [16]. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). In the present study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80.

Data analysis

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed to examine the bivariate relationships among the study variables. All regression assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, and normality of residuals) were examined. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis, were examined to assess normality. All skewness and kurtosis values fell within the acceptable range of ± 2, indicating no serious violations of normality. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values ranged between 1.09 and 1.31, well below the recommended threshold of 5, confirming the absence of multicollinearity. No serious violations were detected. Outliers were screened using standardized residuals (>|3.0|), and less than 2% missing data were handled via listwise deletion. To test the hypothesized mediation and moderated mediation models, we used the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Version 5.0; [21]). Specifically, Model 4 was used to test the simple mediation of self-concept clarity in the relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement. Subsequently, Model 7 was used to examine whether psychological capital moderated the first stage of the mediation model (i.e., the path from mindfulness to self-concept clarity). Before creating the interaction term (Mindfulness × Psychological Capital), all continuous predictors were mean-centered to reduce multicollinearity. Conditional effects were probed at ± 1 SD of psychological capital and verified with the Johnson-Neyman technique. In addition to unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β) were calculated for interpretability using the ratio of the standard deviations of the predictor and outcome variables. The significance of the indirect effects was assessed using bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals based on 5,000 bootstrap samples.

Mediation effects

A mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS macro (Model 4) to examine whether self-concept clarity mediated the association between mindfulness and academic engagement, controlling for gender, age, and academic level.

The model predicting self-concept clarity explained 9.99% of the variance (R2 = 0.0999), and the model predicting academic engagement explained 11.02% of the variance (R2 = 0.1102).

Mindfulness was positively associated with self-concept clarity (B = 0.323, SE = 0.049, t = 6.56, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.227, 0.420]). Age was also positively associated (B = 0.163, SE = 0.056, t = 2.91, p = 0.004, 95% CI [0.053, 0.273]), whereas academic level showed a small but significant negative association (B = –0.088, SE = 0.041, t = –2.17, p = 0.031, 95% CI [–0.167, –0.008]); gender was not significant (p = 0.096).

For academic engagement, both mindfulness (B = 0.184, SE = 0.049, t = 3.74, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.087, 0.280]) and self-concept clarity (B = 0.180, SE = 0.043, t = 4.18, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.095, 0.264]) were significant predictors. Among the covariates, only academic level was significant (B = 0.182, SE = 0.039, t = 4.67, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.105, 0.258]); gender and age were not significant (p = 0.517 and p = 0.743, respectively). Moderation effects

A moderated mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS macro (Model 7) to test whether the indirect effect of mindfulness on academic engagement via self-concept clarity varied as a function of psychological capital (PCQ), controlling for gender, age, and academic level.

The interaction term between mindfulness and psychological capital predicting self-concept clarity was significant (B = 0.138, SE = 0.041, t = 3.37, p = 0.001, ΔR2 = 0.018), indicating that the positive association between mindfulness and self-concept clarity was stronger at higher levels of psychological capital.

The model predicting self-concept clarity accounted for 16.72% of the variance (R2 = 0.1672). Conditional effects showed that mindfulness predicted self-concept clarity at both levels of psychological capital: low PCQ (–1 SD; B = 0.152, SE = 0.058, t = 2.64, p = 0.009, 95% CI [0.039, 0.266]) and high PCQ (+ 1 SD; B = 0.290, SE = 0.055, t = 5.29, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.182, 0.398]).

The conditional indirect effect of mindfulness on academic engagement via self-concept clarity was also significant at both levels of PCQ: B = 0.027 (Boot SE = 0.013, 95% Boot CI [0.007, 0.054]) for low PCQ and B = 0.052 (Boot SE = 0.017, 95% Boot CI [0.022, 0.088]) for high PCQ.

The index of moderated mediation was significant (Index = 0.025, Boot SE = 0.011, 95% Boot CI [0.006, 0.049]), indicating that the indirect association between mindfulness and academic engagement via self-concept clarity increased as psychological capital increased. Discussion

This study examined the associations among mindfulness, self-concept clarity, psychological capital, and academic engagement in a sample of 503 medical students. The findings demonstrated significant positive associations between mindfulness, self-concept clarity, and academic engagement. This pattern is consistent with previous empirical evidence showing that individuals with higher levels of mindfulness tend to display a clearer and more coherent self-concept [6, 13, 20, 36]. The present results also align with research linking self-concept clarity to higher academic engagement, including greater vigor, dedication, and persistence [28, 45, 51]. In addition, they support previous findings indicating that mindfulness contributes to students’ motivation and involvement in learning activities through attentional regulation and emotional stability [29, 43].

Beyond confirming these associations, the present study extends the literature in several theoretically meaningful ways. First, it identifies self-concept clarity as a partial mediator in the relationship between mindfulness and academic engagement. This finding suggests that the effect of mindfulness on academic engagement operates partly through its influence on how clearly and consistently students define their self-concept. Mindful awareness may be associated with a greater capacity to observe their thoughts and emotions objectively, leading to more coherent self-representations. In turn, a stable and clearly articulated sense of self is associated with stronger cognitive, behavioral, and emotional investment in learning. This interpretation is consistent with the Monitor-and-Acceptance Theory [27] and the reperceiving model [41], both of which posit that mindfulness facilitates metacognitive awareness, thereby supporting more integrated self-knowledge and adaptive functioning.

The observed relationships can also be interpreted through the frameworks of Self-Determination Theory and Conservation of Resources Theory. From a self-determination perspective, mindfulness enhances autonomous motivation and self-regulation, fostering coherent self-knowledge and sustained engagement. From a resource-based perspective, psychological capital operates as a meta-resource that amplifies the effects of mindfulness by enabling individuals to direct attentional and emotional regulation capacities toward constructing a stable sense of self. Together, these mechanisms clarify why psychological capital specifically moderates the pathway from mindfulness to self-concept clarity: this link represents a process of transforming momentary awareness into enduring self-structure, which depends on the availability of personal psychological resources. Consequently, higher psychological capital strengthens this transformation, resulting in greater self-concept clarity and, subsequently, higher academic engagement.

Second, the study provides new evidence for the moderating role of psychological capital. The results indicate that psychological capital significantly strengthens the positive association between mindfulness and self-concept clarity. Students with higher psychological capital—characterized by higher levels of self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience—showed a stronger linkage between mindful attention and self-concept clarity. Psychological capital may function as a resource associated with stronger mindfulness–self-concept clarity associations. This moderating effect is consistent with the Conservation of Resources Theory [53], which proposes that individuals with greater psychological resources can more effectively leverage other adaptive capacities. It also aligns with perspectives in Positive Organizational Behavior [54], which emphasize psychological capital as a factor that amplifies the benefits of personal strengths.

Third, these findings provide a clearer justification for the inclusion of self-concept clarity as a mediator and psychological capital as a moderator. Self-concept clarity is theoretically linked to cognitive and motivational processes that sustain engagement. A clear sense of self supports goal-directed behavior, stable motivation, and the regulation of effort, which are central to engagement in demanding academic contexts. Psychological capital, in contrast, represents a set of personal resources that enhance resilience, optimism, and confidence. These characteristics appear to strengthen the effect of mindfulness by enabling students to integrate mindful attention into coherent self-beliefs more effectively. This interaction underscores the importance of considering individual differences in personal resources when examining psychological mechanisms of engagement.

The interpretation of these effects contributes to the refinement of theoretical models linking mindfulness and academic engagement. By demonstrating that the pathway from mindfulness to academic engagement is mediated by self-concept clarity and moderated by psychological capital, this study integrates self-related processes and resource-based perspectives. It also reconnects the findings to the theoretical models outlined in the introduction, particularly Monitor-and-Acceptance Theory, the reperceiving model, Self-Determination Theory [12, 38], and the Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory [19].

From a practical perspective, the results have direct implications for educational interventions. Mindfulness-based programs designed for medical students may enhance self-concept clarity, thereby supporting sustained engagement in academic work. Furthermore, initiatives aimed at strengthening psychological capital—such as structured interventions to build self-efficacy, optimism, and resilience—may increase the impact of mindfulness on students’ self-concept and engagement. Combining these approaches may be particularly beneficial in medical education, where students face continuous academic and clinical demands that require stable motivation and psychological resources.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exclusive reliance on self-report measures may have introduced response biases. Future studies should incorporate multi-source and behavioral assessments to increase measurement validity. Second, the study relied on specific instruments for the measurement of key constructs. Further research should examine whether similar patterns emerge using alternative validated measures. Third, the sample was limited to medical students from a single institution, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Expanding the study to other disciplines and cultural contexts would enhance external validity. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to examine the temporal dynamics and mechanisms underlying the observed effects.

Finally, the observed means for all study variables were relatively low, which may indicate a conservative response style among participants or reflect the high-stress environment of medical education, potentially restricting the range of scores. This, coupled with the modest variance explained by the models (R2 = 0.11 for academic engagement), suggests that while the hypothesized relationships are statistically significant, other unmeasured factors play a substantial role in academic engagement. Future research should include a broader set of predictors to build more comprehensive models.

In summary, this study advances theoretical understanding by clarifying how mindfulness relates to academic engagement through self-concept clarity and how psychological capital strengthens this relationship. It also highlights practical directions for supporting student engagement through the combined development of mindfulness and psychological resources.

Conclusion

This study provides new evidence on the psychological mechanisms linking mindfulness and academic engagement among medical students. By identifying self-concept clarity as a mediator and psychological capital as a moderator, the study advances conceptual understanding of how personal resources and self-related processes interact to influence engagement. This model extends existing frameworks by integrating metacognitive and resource-based perspectives, offering a more comprehensive account of the pathways through which mindfulness operates in academic contexts.

Beyond its theoretical contribution, the findings indicate that enhancing both mindfulness and psychological capital may effectively support student engagement in demanding medical education environments. Future research should examine these relationships using longitudinal or experimental designs to clarify causal pathways, include students from diverse disciplines and cultural contexts, and employ multi-method assessments to improve measurement validity. This approach will help refine theoretical models and inform the design of evidence-based educational interventions.
