Homogenisation of a Passive Scalar Transported by Locally Supported White Noise
Federico Butori, Avi Mayorcas, Silvia Morlacchi

Abstract
Stochastic perturbations of transport type are a common and widely accepted way of representing turbulent effects in fluid dynamics models. In many known examples, it even leads to improved solution theory, a phenomenon known as regularization by noise . A common thread in the recent literature on the topic is the so-called Itô-Stratonovich diffusion limit . By selecting Stratonovich transport noise with carefully arranged vector fields, one can show that the solution of certain SPDEs are close, in an appropriate topology, to an effective, deterministic, equation with a new effective second order elliptic operator, linked to the Ito-Stratonovich corrector. In this work, we deal with a passive scalar model with molecular diffusivity κ \kappa . Starting from the results in [Flandoli et al. , 2022, Philos. Trans. Roy. Soc. A , 380(2219)], we consider a transport noise made by a sum of independent and compactly supported vector fields. This setting is relevant for models of stratified turbulence which naturally occur in boundary layers and Boussinesq models. Due to the anisotropic nature of the noise, the identification of the limit equation is not straightforward as in all other examples known in literature, as the Ito-Stratonovich corrector is a generic second order elliptic operator with non-constant coefficients. Using tools from Homogenisation theory, we obtain a representation for the limiting effective diffusivity matrix. Exploiting this representation, we study asymptotics, in the κ → 0 \kappa\rightarrow 0 regime, of the effective diffusivity across a number of vector field regimes parametrised by the radius of their support. Finally, we provide a careful numerical analysis of the effective diffusivity, discovering a nonlinear behavior for κ → 0 \kappa\rightarrow 0 , in some regimes.



1
Introduction

We study scaling limits, as
N\to+\infty
, of solutions to the following stochastic passive scalar equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
,

(1.1)

du^{N}_{t}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\grad u^{N}_{t}\circ dW_{t}^{k}+\kappa\Delta u^{N}_{t}dt,

where
\sigma^{N}_{k}
are radial divergence free vector fields, centred at lattice points
\nicefrac{{k}}{{N}}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and support contained within balls of radius proportional to
N^{-1}
(see
Subsection 2.1
, Assumption
A1
). We refer to
\sigma_{k}^{N}
as vorticity
patches
. Finally, we introduce a parameter
c>0
which governs the overlap of adjacent patches: for small values of
c
the patches are sparse, with little to no overlap, while for larger values they cover the space domain multiple times.
We are especially interested in the behaviour of the limiting equation (
N\to\infty
) in the small molecular diffusivity (
\kappa\ll 1
) and sparse (
c\leq\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
) regime. The treatment of the sparse regime, in particular, is one of the main novelties of this work (
c.f

2.5
), which we can treat thanks to some tools coming from homogenization theory, never employed before in this context.
The precise set-up of the noise and vector fields are given below.

The SPDE (
1.1
) is a simple model of a passive scalar diffusing at rate
\kappa>0
in a stochastic environment which is spatially varying, Gaussian and white in time. The stochastic transport is motivated on the one hand as a simplified model of a turbulent fluid (see
[
Kra68
,
Kra94
,
CF96
,
FMV98
,
MK99
,
CGH+03
,
Sre19
]
, and also
[
FP22
,
DP24
]
) and on the other from the perspective of regularisation by noise
[
MK99
,
FGP10
,
BFM16
,
GLN25
,
RT24
,
FGL21a
,
FL21
,
CM24
,
GGM24
,
Agr24a
]
. Our particular choice of noise is motivated, in part, by models of eddy turbulence generated by a fluid moving in a bounded domain,
[
FGL22
,
FL22
]
. While we treat (
1.1
) on the torus, the locally supported vector fields are analogous to those considered in
[
FGL22
]
which allowed the authors to represent boundary layer turbulence which forms when a viscous fluids moves through a bounded domain. The model considered in
[
FGL22
]
is variant of the Boussinesq model for heat conductance in a bounded domain where the vector fields
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
vanish at the boundary, in order to model a no-slip boundary condition on the turbulent fluid and
u^{N}
represents a scalar field transported by the noise and diffusing within the domain. In that work the authors show that for an appropriate choice of vector fields and
N
sufficiently large, solutions to (
1.1
) are arbitrarily close, in mean square, to solutions of an
enhanced
divergence form parabolic equation, depending on
N
. A similar analysis is given, for example, in
[
MK99
, Sec. 4.1]
.

Scaling limits of stochastic transport equations were first considered in
[
Gal20
]
, leading to a large number of works (of which we only mention a selection)
[
FGL21b
,
FGL21a
,
FGL24
,
FL21
,
FLL23
,
BFL24
,
BL25
,
Agr24b
,
BFLT24
]
, considering scaling limits of linear and non-linear equations similar to (
1.1
). The backbone of thess works lies in rewriting the Stratonovich SPDE in its (formally equivalent) Itô form, introducing a corrector term, which usually takes the form of a dissipative second order operator, and designing a sequence of transport coefficients, with high spatial frequency, such that the martingale term in the equation becomes infinitesimal in negative topologies, while the corrector term stays of order one (we give more a technical discussion in
Subsection 2.3
).

However, all the results mentioned above are concerned with transport noise defined such that the Stratonovich corrector can directly be seen to be strictly dissipative, most commonly (with the only exceptions of
[
FGL22
,
FL22
]
) being a constant multiple of the Laplacian. By considering anisotropic vector fields (i.e. with local support), the nice form of the Stratonovich corrector is lost, and it becomes a general second order elliptic operator with non-constant coefficients, depending on the scaling parameter
N
. This leads us to apply tools of homogenization theory to pass to a unified scaling limit in both the martingale noise and second order operator.

More specifically, the scaling argument shows that, for
N
large enough, solutions to the stochastic equation (
1.1
) are close, in an appropriate weak sense, to solutions of the parabolic PDE with
modified
diffusivity

\partial_{t}\tilde{u}^{N}=\operatorname{div}((\kappa I+A^{N}(x))\nabla\tilde{u}^{N}_{t}).

The matrix
A^{N}
is positive semi-definite everywhere and is determined by the choice of vector fields
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. However, in the case we are mostly interested, where the vector fields are sparse (
c\leq\sqrt{5}/2
),
A^{N}
is not uniformly strictly positive definite, thus making unclear whether the second order operator appearing in the PDE is ‘more elliptic’ than the original laplacian (i.e. the
effective diffusivity
is larger than
\kappa
). We refer again the reader to the discussion in
Subsection 2.3
.

The first goal of this work is to complete the scaling argument in
[
FGL22
]
, by identifying the limit parabolic equation for
N\rightarrow\infty
; this is the content of
Theorem 2.7
. We do so employing, for the first time in this context, tools from Homogenization theory, which readily gives us a way of quantifying the turbulent effective diffusivity of the limiting model. To the best of our knowledge, even if heuristically the
Itô-Stratonovich diffusion limit
has always been interpreted as a sort of homogenization technique, this is the first time in which this parallel is rigorously made explicit.
Moreover, this allows us to consider more general choices of the vector fields
\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
(i.g. the sparse
c\leq\sqrt{5}/2
case) with respect to the existing literature, even if not all of them will be compatible with a form of enhancement of dissipation.
This leads to our second and main motivation for this work, to investigate the behaviour of the effective diffusivity of the homogenized model, with respect to our main parameters, the radius of the patches
c
and the initial molecular diffusivity
\kappa
; this is the content of
Theorem 2.8
. This analysis is not straightforward in the sparse case (
c<\sqrt{5}/2
), due to the lack of uniform ellipticity of the Statonovich corrector
\operatorname{div}(A^{N}\nabla\cdot)
(a property always assumed in all the above mentioned works, see
2.5
)

The analysis is based on variational tools from homogenization theory, which we have access to from the first part. At the end of the work, we present also numerical results which complete the analysis presented in the second part. The numerical results aim to clarify the exact asymptotic behaviour of the effective diffusivity for low values of
\kappa
, and to show how, depending on
c
, there are clear transition between different regimes.

The manuscript is arranged as follows: in
Section 2
we introduce the setting and we define the basics objects of our analysis; in
Section 3
we prove the main scaling limit, identifying the limit
homogenized
PDE, proving
Theorem 2.7
. In
Section 4
we introduce the variational setting for the Homogenization problem of the preceding section and use it to deduce bounds on the
effective diffusivity matrix
, proving
Theorem 2.8
. Finally in
Section 5
we present some numerical computations to fill the gaps of our analysis and deduce precise asymptotics of the effective diffusivity for low values of
\kappa
, across several patches density regimes, depending on
c
.

1.2
Elements of Homogenization Theory

We briefly recall the main setting of the elliptic Homogenization Theory, which we shall employ later.
Let
M(x)
be a smooth field of symmetric, uniformly elliptic matrices on the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and for any
N>1
and
f^{N}\in\mathcal{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
consider the elliptic problem, understood in its weak formulation

(1.2)

\nabla\cdot(M(Nx)\nabla u^{N}(x))=f^{N}(x),\qquad u^{N}\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

Setting
e_{1}=(1,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1)
we let
\phi_{i}
(for
i=1,2
), be the solution of the elliptic PDE problem, still understood in its weak formulation

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\Big(M(x)e_{i}+M(x)\nabla\phi_{i}(x)\Big)=0\\
\phi_{i}\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}_{per}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\end{cases}

which is well-posed, thanks to the uniform ellipticity of
M(x)
, (
c.f.

[
Eva10
, Sec. 6.2.1]
).

We define the matrix with constant entries
\bar{M}
as

(1.4)

\bar{M}_{ij}=(\bar{M}e_{j})\cdot e_{i}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(M(x)(e_{j}+\nabla\phi_{j}(x)))\cdot e_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left(M_{ij}(x)+(M(x)\nabla\phi_{j}(x))\right)\cdot e_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Then, informally, the matrix
\bar{M}
, describes the effective diffusivity of (
1.2
) in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
. More precisely, provided that
f^{N}\rightarrow f
in
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then the solution
u^{N}
to (
1.2
) converges in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to the unique solution
u(x)\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
of

(1.5)

\nabla\cdot(\bar{M}\nabla u(x))=f(x)

We refer the reader to
[
BLP78
, Chapter 1]
for a precise statement and for a proof and further discussion.
In this manuscript we will mostly be concerned with the parabolic problem rather than the elliptic one, however since
M
will always be time independent in our setting, the homogenization of the parabolic problem reduces to homogenization of the elliptic one (see
[
BLP78
, Ch. 2, Rem. 1.6]
). Specifically, the solution of

(1.6)

\partial_{t}u^{N}(t,x)=\nabla\cdot(M(Nx)\nabla u^{N}(t,x)),\qquad u^{N}(0,\cdot)=u_{0}^{N}\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\quad u^{N}\in C(0,T;\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).

converges in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
to the unique solution
u(t,x)\in C(0,T;\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
of

(1.7)

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\nabla\cdot(\bar{M}(x)\nabla u(t,x)),\qquad u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

provided that
u^{N}_{0}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

In our setting, we give an analogous version of this statement in
3.6
.

Finally, we will need the following properties of the homogenized matrix
\bar{M}
, whose proofs, for the reader’s convenience, are recalled in
Appendix A
.

Lemma 1.1

Let

be a smooth and periodic field of symmetric matrices and let

(1.8)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}|M(x)\xi|\leq\Lambda\xi\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}M(x)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

ii)

For every

,

(1.10)

\bar{M}\xi\cdot\xi\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2}.

iii)

The matrix

Proof.

See

2
Setting

We deal with the stochastic passive scalar equation (
1.1
), posed on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
The noise coefficients
\sigma_{k}
are spatially localized vorticity
patches
,
\left\{W_{t}^{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of i.i.d. standard Brownian motions and
\kappa>0
is the
molecular diffusivity
.
In this section we give the definition of the noise and of the main objects of our analysis.

2.1
Definition of the Noise

We begin by constructing a
family of locally supported vortex
patches
. Let
\Lambda^{N}
be the integer lattice of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with resolution
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
for some integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
. That is,

(2.1)

\Lambda^{N}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}\times\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}\right).

We enumerate
\Lambda^{1}
by
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and then set

(2.2)

\Lambda^{N}\ni x^{N}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{k}{N},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

We will centre each vortex patch on the nodes of this lattice. The vorticity will be constructed by defining its stream function. Given a constant
c\in(0,+\infty)
, fix
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
with
\text{supp}(\psi)\in B(0,c)
and such that there exists a smooth scalar function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
for which

(2.3)

\psi(x)=f(|x|).

Then, fixing a family of sequences
\{\theta^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};\mathbb{R})
, a radius
r>1
, integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we set

\sigma^{N}_{k}(x)\coloneqq\theta^{N}_{k}\frac{1}{r}(\grad^{\perp}\psi)\left(\frac{x-x^{N}_{k}}{r}\right)=\theta^{N}_{k}\frac{1}{r}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x^{N}_{k}|}{r}\right)\frac{(x-x^{N}_{k})^{\perp}}{|x-x^{N}_{k}|}.

Note that since
c>0
is finite, there exists some
n(c)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.4)

\text{supp}\left(\sum_{|k|\geq n(c)}\sigma^{N}_{k}\right)\cap\mathbb{T}^{2}=\emptyset.

The time fluctuations are modelled as time varying stochastic weights multiplying each vortex patch, which we choose to be Brownian motions. To keep the noise periodic on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
these weights must agree for nodes
x^{N}_{k},\,x^{N}_{k^{\prime}}\in\Lambda^{N}
such that
x^{N}_{k}-x^{N}_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. To do so we introduce a sequence of
\mathbb{R}
valued Brownian motions
\{W_{t}^{k}\}_{k}
such that

\big[W^{k},W^{k^{\prime}}\big]_{t}=\begin{cases}t,\qquad\text{if }x_{k}-x_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
0\qquad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Denote by
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
the filtration generated by the collections of these Brownian motions.
For the same reason, we also fix the real sequence
\{\theta^{N}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
to be such that

\theta_{k}^{N}=\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{N}\qquad\text{ if }x_{k}-x_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

Now it is easy to check that our random velocity field is periodic. Letting
L=(l_{1},l_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a given shift and defining
k^{\prime}:=k-NL
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}(x+L)W_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\theta_{k}^{N}\frac{1}{r}(\grad^{\perp}\psi)\left(\frac{x+L-k/N}{r}\right)W_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\theta_{(k^{\prime}+NL)}^{N}\frac{1}{r}(\grad^{\perp}\psi)\left(\frac{x-k^{\prime}/N}{r}\right)W_{t}^{k^{\prime}+NL}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{N}\frac{1}{r}(\grad^{\perp}\psi)\left(\frac{x-k^{\prime}/N}{r}\right)W_{t}^{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}(x)W_{t}^{k}.

Next we introduce another important object, the symmetric, correlation matrix

(2.5)

A^{N}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}(x)\otimes\sigma_{k}^{N}(y).

This matrix field coincide with the spatial covariance of the noise provided that
r<(2c)^{-1}
. Indeed, if
k^{\prime}=k-NL
for some
L\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{0}
as above, then for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
either
\sigma_{k}(x)
or
\sigma_{k^{\prime}}(x)
is zero, since it cannot be the case that both vectors
x-x_{k}
and
x-x_{k^{\prime}}
lie in
B(0,rc)
. Thus, in this case, the correlation between
\sigma_{k}(x)W_{t}^{k}
and
\sigma_{k^{\prime}}(x)W_{t}^{k^{\prime}}
is zero, even if
[W_{t}^{k^{\prime}},W_{t}^{k}]=t
.

From now on we always assume
r<(2c)^{-1}
. Since, in the end, we will send
r\rightarrow 0
while keeping
c
fixed, this condition is no restriction at all.

By a slight abuse of notation we will often omit the superscript
N
when
N=1
and write

(2.6)

A^{N}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}(x)\otimes\sigma_{k}^{N}(x),\qquad A(x)\coloneqq A^{1}(x).

We end this subsection by introducing the main scaling assumptions we will use throughout the work. Let us introduce some compact notation. Given any function
f
and a scaling parameter
r>0
we define

(2.7)

(f)_{r}(\,\cdot\,):=\frac{1}{r}f\left(\frac{\cdot}{r}\right).

for which we readily have the identity

(2.8)

\left\|(f)_{r}\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}(r\mathbb{T}^{2})}\,=\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

As a result, it is straightforward to observe the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

If

(A0)

\theta_{k}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},

then

(2.9)

A^{N}(x)=(A(Nx))_{Nr}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(\nabla^{\perp}\psi)_{Nr}({Nx-k})\otimes(\nabla^{\perp}\psi)_{Nr}({Nx-k}).

In particular, if

(A1)

\theta^{N}_{k}=r=\frac{1}{N}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},

then

(2.10)

A^{N}(x)=A^{1}(Nx)

Proof.

By direct computation, in the first case when
\theta^{N}_{\,\cdot\,}=\frac{1}{N}
, we have

\displaystyle A^{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{N^{2}r^{2}}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\left(\frac{x-k/N}{r}\right)\otimes\nabla^{\perp}\psi\left(\frac{x-k/N}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{Nr}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\left(\frac{Nx-k}{Nr}\right)\right)\otimes\left(\frac{1}{Nr}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\left(\frac{Nx-k}{Nr}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(\nabla^{\perp}\psi)_{Nr}({Nx-k})\otimes(\nabla^{\perp}\psi)_{Nr}({Nx-k}),

which concludes the proof of (
2.9
). It is then clear to see that (
2.10
) holds if

∎

2.2
Solution Concepts and Basic Properties

In order to analyse (
1.1
) and its scaling limit, we interpret it in its Itô form, replacing the Stratonovich integral with an Itô integral plus the Itô-Stratonovich corrector. On a formal level, the corrector is given by the covariation

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}d[\sigma_{k}\cdot\nabla u^{N},W^{k}]_{t}.

To formally compute this quantity, we appeal to the divergence free condition on
\sigma_{k}
, to get

d(\sigma_{k}\cdot\nabla u)=\operatorname{div}(\sigma_{k}du)=dV_{t}+\sqrt{2}\operatorname{div}((\sigma_{k}\otimes\sigma_{l})\nabla u)dW_{t}^{l}

where
V_{t}
is a process with bounded variation. Inserting this expression in the covariation above, and assuming that
r\leq(2c)^{-1}
, we see that the Itô formulation reads

(2.11)

du^{N}_{t}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\grad u^{N}_{t}dW_{t}^{k}+\Big(\nabla\cdot(A^{N}\nabla u^{N}_{t})+\kappa\Delta u_{t}^{N}\Big)dt

Owning to this formal computation, we are motivated to work with the following notion of a solution, working directly with the Itô form.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
u_{0}\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
T>0
, we say that a process
u^{N}\in

C_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) on
[0,T]
if, for every
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
it holds that

(2.12)

\left\langle u_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{0},\phi\right\rangle+\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle u^{N}_{s},\nabla\cdot(\kappa I+A^{N})\nabla\phi\right\rangle ds+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2}\left\langle u^{N}_{s},\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\nabla\phi\right\rangle dW^{k}_{s},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Remark 2.3

(Mean Free Test Functions)
.

Note that since (
1.1
) and (
2.11
) are in divergence form, it directly follows that any weak solution, in the sense of
2.2
satisfies the identity

\langle u^{N}_{t},1\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}=\langle u_{0},1\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}.

Hence, it is not restriction to additionally require the test function

in

to be mean free. We will do this consistently from now on.

An application of the Itô formula gives the following (see for instance
[
FGL22
, Thm. 1.2]

[
FL22
, Thm. 1.2]
and
[
Fla95
, Thm. 5.24]
):

Proposition 2.4

(Energy Estimate)
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and

. Then, there exists a unique solution to (

) in the sense of
2.2
.
Moreover it holds that

\|u^{N}_{T}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}+2\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{N}_{s}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

2.3
Discussion of the Scaling Limit

The first goal of this work is to find suitable scaling regime, in terms of the coefficients
\{\theta^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
and the scaling parameter
r\coloneqq r(N)>0
, such that any sequence of solutions
\{u^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
to (
1.1
) has a meaningful limit as
N\to\infty
. As
N\to\infty
the distance between the patch centres converges to zero and so we must send both their effective radii
r
and their intensities,
\{\theta^{N}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
to zero. By scaling these quantities in an appropriate way (
c.f.
Assumption
A1
), we will obtain an effective description of the stochastic system in terms of a deterministic parabolic one, in the large
N
limit. This is done in
Section 3

The second goal of this work is to investigate the effective diffusivity of this limiting parabolic equation, in particular its behaviour as the molecular diffusivity
\kappa
is sent to
0
.
In order to shorten the notation, introduce the elliptic operator

\mathcal{L}^{N}_{\kappa}f=\operatorname{div}\left((\kappa I+A^{N}(x)\nabla)f\right).

As has been well understood in previous works,
[
Gal20
,
FGL21a
,
FL21
,
FGL22
]
, there are two main quantities, derived from parameters of the noise which govern any suitable scaling limit.

•

The quantity
\varepsilon_{N}
that governs the stochastic fluctuations of the solution, which has to be made small (
c.f.

3.3
)

(2.15)

\varepsilon_{N}:=\sup\left\{\int\int_{\mathbb{T}^{4}}(\nabla v(x))^{t}A^{N}(x,y)\nabla v(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y:\ v\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\,\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}=1\right\}.

•

The quantity
\pi_{N}^{\kappa}
, which is the first eigenvalue of the operator
-\mathcal{L}^{N}_{\kappa}
and governs the dissipation of the deterministic part of the Itô equation

(2.16)

\pi_{N}^{\kappa}\coloneqq\inf\left\{\frac{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\nabla v)^{\top}(x)(\kappa I+A^{N}(x,x))\nabla v(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|v(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}:\ v\in\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{1},v\neq 0\right\}.

To have a non trivial scaling limit, as in
[
Gal20
,
FGL22
]
, we need to keep
\pi_{N}^{\kappa}
of fixed magnitude while
\varepsilon_{N}\searrow 0
(
c.f.

Section

3.2
).

Remark 2.5
.

It is clear that if we had access to a uniform ellipticity inequality, of the form

(2.17)

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\inf_{\xi\neq 0}\frac{\xi^{T}A^{N}(x)\xi^{T}}{|\xi|^{2}}\geq M

then we would immediately have
\pi_{N}^{\kappa}\geq C(\kappa+M)
, where
C
is the Poincaré constant of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, all previous works concerning models of this type have been set-up such that (
2.17
) holds,
[
Gal20
,
FGL21a
,
FGL24
,
FGL22
]
. One useful consequence of our approach via homogenization theory is that it allows us to also study cases when (
2.17
) does not apply, see
4.1
and
4.2
.

Remark 2.6
.

Finally, we have to keep in mind that for fixed
N
, even if the new operator was uniformly elliptic, or more in general if it holds
\pi_{N}^{\kappa}>m\geq 0
, it would not be evident a priori that the noise in (
1.1
) increases the dissipation rate of the
L^{2}
norm of the solution. Indeed, applying Itô’s formula to
\|u^{N}_{t}\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}
, the conservative nature of the Stratonovich transport noise gives the same energy balance as for deterministic heat equation
\partial_{t}u=\kappa\Delta u
. We do not deal with the problem of the convergence of the kinetic energy profile of the solution, which was only very recently tackled in
[
Agr24b
]
with tools from stochastic maximal
L^{p}
-regularity theory; we content ourself with a weaker mixing estimate, identifying a limit
effective diffusivity
coefficient
\pi^{\kappa}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{N}^{\kappa}
and giving a comprehensive analysis of the behaviour of this coefficient across different regimes, hoping in future to close the gap, proving also convergence of the energy profile.

3
The Scaling Limit

In this section we prove our first main result

and

except

,

.

Proposition 3.2

For

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim_{T}\,e^{-\pi^{\kappa}_{N}t}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\quad\text{for all }\,\phi\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),

iii)

Proof.

See collected results of
[

, Sec. 7.2]
. The equality between the weak and the mild form, that is, identity (

[

∎

Before continuing, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let us introduce the following bi-linear form

(3.6)

L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\otimes 2}\ni(v,w)\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{N}(v,w)\coloneqq\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{4}}v(x)^{\top}A^{N}(x,y)w(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\in\mathbb{R}.

so that recalling (

\varepsilon_{N}=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{H}^{1},\|\nabla v\|=1}\mathcal{A}^{N}(v,v)

Given the definition of
A^{N}
in (
2.5
) and assumption (
A0
) we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{N}(v,v)=\,\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{4}}v(x)^{\top}A^{N}(x,y)v(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{N^{2}r^{2}}\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}v(x)\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi\left(\frac{x-k/N}{r}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right]^{2}

Note that since

The following lemma, relying fundamentally on the assumption of compact support of

is proportional to the scaling constant

and the

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume (
A0
). For all
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})

(3.8)

\varepsilon_{N}=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}^{N}(v,v)\lesssim_{c}r^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}.

Proof.

Recalling that
supp(\psi)\subset B(0,c)
, we begin by applying Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\mathcal{A}^{N}(v,v)\leq N^{-2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B(k/N,cr)\cap\mathbb{T}^{2})}\|r^{-1}\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\cdot/r)\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}

Recalling the notation (
2.7
), it is easy to see that
\|r^{-1}\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\cdot/r)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|_{L^{2}(B(0,c))}^{2}
.
Using again the assumption on the supports of
\psi
we see that for
k\neq k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\psi(\frac{x-k/N}{r})

have non empty intersection if and only if

Thus, as
N\to+\infty
and

, for any

the number of overlaps of the supports of the

at

\#\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:|x-k/N|\leq cr\}

is bounded, up to a constant, by

.
This means that there exists a constant
C(c)>0
for which, using the additivity of the

norm with respect to union of sets, we can estimate the sum

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|v(x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B(k/N,cr)\cap\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq CN^{2}r^{2}\|v(x)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Putting all together we arrive at the claimed bound.
∎

The uniform bound of

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume (

Defining
D^{N}_{t}\coloneqq u^{N}_{t}-\tilde{u}^{N}_{t}

) from (

Hence, by Itô’s isometry, we have

(3.9)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle D^{N}_{t},\phi\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left\langle e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\left(\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\nabla u^{N}_{s}\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\right].

Define

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|\left\langle e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\left(\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\nabla u^{N}_{s}\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|\left\langle\sigma_{k}^{N},\nabla u^{N}_{s}\phi_{t,s}\right\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left\langle\sigma_{k}^{N},\nabla u^{N}_{s}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma_{k}^{N},\nabla u^{N}_{s}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{4}}\left(e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\right)(x)\left(\nabla u^{N}_{s}\right)^{\top}\hskip-5.0pt(x)A^{N}(x,y)\nabla u^{N}_{s}(y)\left(e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\right)(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|\left\langle e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\left(\sigma_{k}^{N}\cdot\nabla u^{N}_{s}\right),\phi\right\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(c)r^{2}\Big\|\nabla u_{s}^{N}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\Big\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}.

Thus, appealing to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle D^{N}_{t},\phi\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{c}r^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\nabla u_{s}^{N}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\kappa}}\phi\Big\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\Big]ds

Once we have this result, given any

Recalling the quantity

In the setting of

Then, using the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u^{N}(t,x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\right]

The quantity

In this section, under the scaling regime (

The fact that the homogenized diffusivity matrix is a multiple of the identity is a crucial result of this section (

Let us introduce the notation

So that we rewrite the equation (

Assume (

We refer the reader to

Let us introduce the Frechet space
\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\coloneqq\bigcap_{\alpha>0}\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

(\|\|_{\dot{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}})_{\alpha>0}

In the above setting it also holds that

3.3

In the previous sections we have shown that the solution

4
Analysis of Homogenised Diffusivity

Theorem 3.9
and
3.8
shows that our stochastic equation (
2.11
) is arbitrarily close to an heat equation (
3.14
) with diffusivity
C(c,\kappa)
. The goal of the rest of this paper is to give quantitative estimates on
C(c,\kappa)
. Recall that
1.1
shows that the homogenized diffusivity
C(c,\kappa)
satisfies

(4.1)

C(c,\kappa)\geq(\kappa+\lambda_{A})

where the factor

\lambda_{A}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\sup_{\xi\neq 0}\frac{\xi^{T}A(x)\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}

is the uniform ellipticity constant of
A(x)
. This additional factor, as shown in the previous sections, is due to the spatial structure of the noise, and can be made arbitrarily large, as recalled in the introduction, by letting the supports of the vector fields
\sigma_{k}^{N}
overlap sufficiently and have large enough intensities (
c.f.

4.4
). However, we additionally consider situations in which
\lambda_{A}=0
and demonstrate that, in some regimes, some enhancement of dissipation (
C(c,\kappa)\geq(\kappa+m)
) is still possible. Secondly, we investigate the asymptotics of
C(c,\kappa)
as
\kappa\rightarrow 0
, identifying different regimes based on the level of overlap of the vortex patches.
See
[
FP94
]
for a similar situation with a carefully constructed cellular transport.

Before proceeding with this analysis we study finer properties of the matrix
A^{N}(x)
for varying
c>0
. This is the content of the next subsection.

4.1
Properties of the covariance Matrices

We discuss some properties of the matrices
A^{N}(x)
.
We remark that if
c\leq 1
, that is each vortex patch has support contained in the unit ball, then the only non-zero summands in the definition of
A(x)
are those indexed by
k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}=\{(0,0),\ (1,0),\ (0,1),\ (1,1)\}
. On the other hand, if
c\geq 1
, then there are contributions from vector fields with centres outside the unit square. However, in both cases
A(x)
corresponds to the periodization of one single vortex patch of radius
c>0
.

In order to understand ellipticity properties of
A(x)
, we begin by studying its degeneracies. For every
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the matrix
A(x)
is positive semidefinite by definition, so we are interested in the regions where it fails to have full rank. We also keep in mind that when
c\leq 1
,
A(x)
has zeroes at the lattice points
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as a consequence of smoothness radial symmetry and the support of
\psi
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the supports of
A(x)
for selected
c\in\{0.25,\,0.5,\,0.75,\,1,\,1.25,\,1.5\}
. Darker shading represent multiple overlaps.

Lemma 4.1
.

The following situations all hold.

1.

If
c=1/2-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
then

\Big\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:\min(|x_{1}-1/2|,|x_{2}-1/2|)\leq 2\delta\Big\}\subset\text{supp}(A)^{c}.

2.

If
1/2\leq c<\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}
, then letting
\delta=\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}-c
, it holds that

\Big\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:\min_{k\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}}(|x-k|)\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\delta\Big\}=\text{supp}(A)^{c}.

3.

If
\sqrt{2}/2\leq c\leq 1
then letting
\delta=1-c
it holds that
A
has a null eigenvalue in the region

\Big\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\,:\,|x-k|\geq 1-\delta\text{ for exactly three $k$ out of }(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\Big\}

and on the edges of the square
\partial([0,1]^{2})
, while it has full rank in the rest of
[0,1]^{2}
.

4.

Let
1\leq c\leq\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
, then setting
\delta=\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}-c
it holds that
A
has full rank outside of the region

(4.2)

\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\,:\,\exists\,a\in B\bigg(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\sqrt{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}+\delta^{2}-\sqrt{5}\delta}\bigg)\,\,\text{s.t. }\,x\in\{(a,0),\,(a,1),\,(0,a),\,(1,a)\}\,\,\bigg\}.

Furthermore, in this region it has one null eigenvalue.

5.

If
c>\sqrt{5}/2
then
A
is strictly positive definite everywhere on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Proof.

Before proving each statement it is useful to picture the configuration of the patches
\sigma_{k}
. We have one patch on each integer lattice point
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. As we already noted, at each location
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
only a finite number of vortices are active, each of which has support exactly equal to the ball of radius
c
. For instance, if
c<1
it is enough to consider only four patches located at the corners of the square. We will derive our statements using geometric considerations of the intersections of the patches supports. The regions where
A\equiv 0
are those that lie outside of the support of all vortices, so they can only be present when the torus is not fully covered by balls of radius
c
located at integer lattice points.

1.

By our previous considerations, it is straightforward to see that the region in the first statement is made of two strips that separate the supports of the patches
\sigma_{k}
located at the corners of the square.

2.

In the second situation, the supports of the patches do overlap but the radius
c
is too small to reach the centre of the square, leaving out the diamond shaped region described in the statement, where
A\equiv 0
.

3.

In this situation the torus is fully covered. The matrix field is again given by

(4.3)

A(x)=\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\nabla^{\perp}\psi({x-k})\otimes\nabla^{\perp}\psi({x-k}).

The matrix
A
is the sum of orthogonal projections along
\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Notice that the region described in the statement of the lemma is exactly the region where only one patch out of the four is active, thus
A
is a single projection, and cannot have full rank. In the remaining region, either two three or four patches are active. Recall that
\psi
is radial, and thus, for every
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)
is parallel to
(x-k)^{\perp}
. In the region where only two vortices are active,
x-k_{1}
and
x-k_{2}
are linearly independent unless
x
lies in the span of
k_{1}-k_{2}
, which happens only on the edges of the square. Where either three or four patches are active, we argue in the following way. Suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{0\}
such that
w\cdot A(x)w=0
. This would mean that
\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}|\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\cdot w|^{2}=0
but this can happen if and only if
\omega\perp(x-k)
for all active
k
. We conclude that if the
k
are not all collinear, we must have
w=0
.

4.

In this case we reason as above, but we can prove that the system of vectors
\{\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\}_{k\in\Box^{2}_{1}}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for every
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
except on the edges of the square. Since
c>1
, at each point
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
there are at least two or three active patches who’s centres are not co-linear. Since
A(x)
must have full rank wherever there are more than two active vortices we are left with the region where only two vortices are active. Reasoning as in the previous case,
A(x)
has rank
1
in this region only along the segment connecting the centres of the blobs. This region is a subset of the edges of the square described as in the statement.

5.

Finally, in the last situation, at each point of the square there are active at least three active vortices with centres that are not collinear and hence
A
has full rank everywhere.

∎

C(c,\kappa)=\xi\cdot\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}H_{\kappa}(x)(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi}(x))\ \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where
\phi_{\xi}
solves the corrector equation (
3.12
) with
\xi
in place of
e_{i}
. Using the fact that
\phi_{\xi}
solves the cell problem (
3.12
), and integration by parts, we can write

(4.9)

C(c,\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|H^{1/2}_{\kappa}(x)(\xi+\nabla\phi_{\xi}(x))|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

For any
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
we introduce the convex functional,

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\ni u\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|H^{1/2}_{\kappa}(x)(\xi+\nabla u)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

we deduce from the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
, that one has

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(\phi_{\xi})=\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(u)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{\xi}=\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(u).

That is, the unique minimizer of the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
coincides with the solution of the cell problem in a given direction
\xi
and the energy associated to this minimizer, as a function of
\xi
, defines the quadratic form associated to
\overline{H}_{\kappa}
.

4.3
Characterisations of Additional Diffusivity

Recall from
4.1
that ellipticity of the matrix
A
depends on the value of
c
in particular, for
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}})
the support of
A
is of measure strictly less than that of the torus. At the other extreme, for
c>\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
the matrix
A
is strictly positive definite everywhere on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Furthermore, it was shown in
4.4
that in this latter regime, there is a quantifiable lower bound on the ellipticity of
A
. Therefore, due to
1.1
we have two direct conclusions; for
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}})
the matrix
\bar{H}_{\kappa}-\kappa I
is non-negative definite for all
\kappa>0
, while for
c>\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
the matrix
\bar{H}_{\kappa}-\kappa I
is positive definite with a quantifiable lower bound. Beyond these direct conclusions, however, we are interested in obtaining finer properties of the quantity

(4.10)

\nu_{\kappa}\coloneqq C(c,\kappa)-\kappa=\left(\bar{H}_{\kappa}\xi\cdot\xi-\kappa\right),\qquad\text{for any }\,\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

in the limit
\kappa\to 0
. Recall that this quantity does not depend on the choice of
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We refer to the quantity
\nu_{\kappa}
as the
additional diffusivity
in the homogenized limit. This section will give a proof of
Theorem 2.8
, whose statement we now recall.

Theorem 4.5
.

Under the same assumptions of the previous theorem the following statements hold:

i)

If
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
(i.e. the vorticity patches are completely separated) there exists a constant

, depending only on

\kappa\leq C(c,\kappa)\leq L\kappa\quad\text{ for all }\kappa>0.

\psi
.

ii)

If If

\kappa\leq C(c,\kappa)\leq L^{\prime}(\kappa+\kappa^{1-\frac{1}{n}})\quad\text{for all }\kappa>0.

iii)

If

, then there exists a constant

Moreover, in this case, the constant

(see Remark

.

In the setting of Theorem
4.5

iii)

.

a)

If
\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}<c

-homogeneous in

and

.

4.3.1
The regime
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]

In this regime, the matrix
A
is not uniformly elliptic, i.e. there exist a set of positive measure in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
where
A\equiv 0
, therefore the trivial lower bound from
1.1
gives that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\kappa>0
,

(4.14)

C(c,\kappa)\geq\kappa\quad\implies\quad\nu_{\kappa}\geq 0.

In particular,
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\nu_{\kappa}(\xi)\geq 0
. We are interested in constructing a commensurate upper bound on
\nu_{\kappa}(\xi)
. We will achieve this by constructing approximate solutions to the cell problem for the unit vector
e_{1}=(1,0)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
. Then,

•

for
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
there exists a constant
L>0
depending on
c>0
alone such that

(4.15)

\nu_{\kappa}\leq L\kappa.

•

for
c=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and any
n\geq\nicefrac{{7}}{{2}}
there exists a
p\coloneqq p(n)\geq 1
and a constant
L^{\prime}\coloneqq L^{\prime}(c,\|\psi\|_{W^{p+1,\infty}_{x}})>0
such that

(4.16)

\nu_{\kappa}\leq L^{\prime}\left(\kappa+\kappa^{1-\frac{1}{n}}\right).

Proof.

By the definition of
H_{\kappa}
and
C(c,\kappa)
(recall (
4.9
)) and
3.8
, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{H}_{\kappa}\xi\cdot\xi-\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left[\kappa|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\nabla\phi+2(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\xi+\xi A(x)\xi\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x;\,\right\},

So that for any
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

(4.17)

\nu_{\kappa}\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left[\kappa|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\nabla\phi(x)+2(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\xi+\xi A(x)\xi\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Furthermore, observing that

(4.18)

(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\nabla\phi(x)+2(\nabla\phi(x))^{\top}A(x)\xi+\xi A(x)\xi=\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\left|(\nabla\phi(x)+\xi)\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\right|^{2},

we write (
4.17
) in the more compact form, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,

(4.19)

\nu_{\kappa}\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left[\kappa|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\left|(\nabla\phi(x)+\xi)\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{k}(x-k)\right|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Therefore, setting
\xi=e_{1}=(1,0)
and

(4.20)

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}-x_{1},&x_{1}\in[0,c],\\
\frac{2c}{1-2c}x_{1}-\frac{c}{1-2c},&x_{1}\in[c,1-c],\\
1-x_{1},&x_{1}\in[1-c,1].\end{cases}

for which,

(4.21)

\nabla\phi(x)=\begin{cases}-e_{1},&x_{1}\in(0,c)\cup(1-c,1)\\
\frac{2c}{1-2c}e_{1},&x_{1}\in(c,1-c)\end{cases}

we find the estimate

(4.22)

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}\,\leq\,

\displaystyle\kappa\left(2\int_{0}^{c}\int_{0}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}+\int_{c}^{1-c}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{2c}{1-2c}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\right)

(4.23)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\int_{B(k,c)}\left|(-e_{1}+e_{1})\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 2\kappa\left(c+\frac{4c^{2}}{(1-2c)^{2}}\right)

which concludes the proof for
c\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
.

Since the estimate (
4.24
) degenerates as
c\to\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
we are required to adapt our argument in this case. To this end we define a family of approximate competitors. For
\delta\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
we set

(4.25)

\phi^{\delta}(x)=\begin{cases}-x_{1},&x_{1}\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta],\\
\frac{1-2\delta}{2\delta}x_{1}+\frac{2\delta-1}{4\delta},&x_{1}\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\delta],\\
1-x_{1},&x_{1}\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\delta,1].\end{cases}

Therefore, by symmetry of the sum and radial symmetry
\psi
, for every
\delta\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}\leq

\displaystyle\,\kappa\left(2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\delta}\left|\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\int_{B(k,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap([1/2-\delta,1/2+\delta]\times[0,1])}\left|\left(\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right)e_{1}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 2\kappa\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta+\frac{(1-4\delta)^{2}}{8\delta}\right)

\displaystyle+4\int_{B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap([\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1])}\left|\left(\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right)e_{1}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Since we assume
D^{a}\nabla^{\perp}\psi|_{\partial_{B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})}}=0
for all
a\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\nabla^{\perp}\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
we can Taylor expand to arbitrary order around any point
x\in\partial B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
. With the same
\delta\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
as above, let us define

B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap\{x_{1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta\}\eqqcolon x_{\delta}=\Big(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}\,\Big),

for which

|x_{\delta}-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},0)|=\sqrt{\delta},

Hence, for any
x\in B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap([\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1])
and
p\geq 0
, we obtain

(4.26)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)|=

\displaystyle\,\left|\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)-\sum_{|a|\leq p}\frac{1}{a!}D^{a}\nabla^{\perp}\psi((\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},0))(x-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},0))^{a}\right|

(4.27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\sup_{x\in B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1]}|x-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},0)|^{p+1}\max_{|a|=p+1}\|D^{a}\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,|x_{\delta}-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},0)|^{p+1}\max_{|a|=p+1}\|D^{a}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

(4.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\delta^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\max_{|a|=p+1}\|D^{a}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

(4.30)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,\delta^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|_{W^{p+1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})},

Furthermore, one directly sees

(4.31)

\big|B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1]\big|\leq\delta\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}\lesssim\delta^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}.

Putting all of this together, we find that for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{B(k_{1},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap[\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1]}\bigg|\left(\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right)e_{1}\cdot

\displaystyle\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)\,\bigg|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right|^{2}\sup_{x\in B(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\cap([\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\delta,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]\times[0,1])}|\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\delta^{\frac{p+4}{2}}\left|\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right|^{2}\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|^{2}_{W^{p+1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

Hence, we find

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}\,

\displaystyle\leq\,2\kappa\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta+\frac{(1-4\delta)^{2}}{8\delta}\right)+2\delta^{\frac{p+4}{2}}\left|\frac{1-4\delta}{8\delta}+1\right|^{2}\|\nabla^{\perp}\psi\|^{2}_{W^{p+1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\|\psi\|_{W^{p+1,\infty}}\right)\left(\kappa+\frac{\kappa}{\delta}+\delta^{\frac{p+4}{2}}\right).

Now, let us set
\delta=\kappa^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, to write

\nu_{\kappa}\lesssim_{\|\psi\|_{W^{p+1,\infty}}}\kappa+\kappa^{1-\alpha}+\kappa^{\frac{\alpha(p+4)}{2}}.

Since we are free to choose
\alpha>0
we obtain the best result when

1-\alpha=\frac{\alpha(p+4)}{2}\quad\iff\quad\alpha=\frac{2}{p+6}.

Plugging this in we see that

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}\lesssim_{\|\psi\|_{W^{p+1,\infty}}}\kappa+2\kappa^{1-\frac{2}{p+6}}.

Hence, for any
n\geq\nicefrac{{7}}{{2}}
there exists a
p\coloneqq p(n)\geq 1
(in fact
p(n)=2n-6
) such that

\displaystyle\nu_{\kappa}\lesssim_{\|\psi\|_{W^{p(n)+1,\infty}}}\kappa+\kappa^{1-\frac{1}{n}}.

which concludes the proof.

∎

Remark 4.8
.

Note that in the case
c=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
we crucially used the assumption that
\psi
was smooth to take Taylor expansions of arbitrarily high order. However, since
\psi
is compactly supported, it cannot be analytic and so we cannot take the limits
p(n),\,n\to+\infty
. This fact is reflected in our numerical experiments (see
Figure 3
) where we observe that the exponent drops from being consistent with linear dependence on
\kappa
for
c<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
to being strictly less than one for
c=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

4.3.2
The regime
\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}<c<\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
:

In this subsection we analyse the constant

in the regime
\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}<c<\nicefrac{{\sqrt{5}}}{{2}}
. We prove that in this case

for a constant

independent of

, uniformly in

.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be given and define the affine space

\mathcal{V}_{\xi}=\left\{F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}):\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}F=\xi,\ \nabla^{\perp}\cdot F=0\right\}.

We write
V_{\xi}
for the closure of
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Let
c>1
, introduce on
V_{\xi}
the quadratic functional

\|F\|_{A}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}F^{\top}(x)A(x)F(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|F\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Lemma 4.10
.

The infimum of
\|\cdot\|_{A}
on
V_{\xi}
coincides with the infimum on
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
.

Proof.

Since
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{A}
is a convex functional, there exists a sequence
\{F^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
be a sequence such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|F^{n}\|_{A}=\inf_{F\in\mathcal{V}_{\xi}}\|F\|_{A}
, and for each
\delta>0,\,n\in\mathbb{N}
let
F^{n,\delta}
be a smooth approximation to
F^{n}
such that
\|F^{n}-F^{n,\delta}\|_{L^{2}}=\delta
. This approximation can be constructed noticing that
F\in V_{\xi}
implies
F=\xi+\nabla u
for some
u\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
, and then approximating
u
. Since
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
we have

\displaystyle\|F^{n,\delta}\|_{A}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|F^{n}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)|^{2}+|(F^{n,\delta}-F^{n})\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\,\Box^{2}_{1}}\|F^{n}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|(F^{n,\delta}-F^{n})\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|F^{n}\|_{A}+2\delta\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}\ ,

where we used the subadditivity of the square root. Finally, taking the infimum over

yields the result since
\delta
was chosen arbitrarily.
∎

From now on, we will keep
\xi
fixed and drop the subscript
\xi
in
V_{\xi}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
.
For the next lemma we regard
F
as a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and perform integrations over
[0,1]^{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{V}
,
a\in[0,1]
and define the curves

\gamma_{H}(t)=(t,a),\quad\gamma_{V}(t)=(a,t)

and the functions

\quad g_{H}(x)=x_{2}-a,\quad g_{V}(x)=-x_{1}+a.

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}F_{1}(t,a)dt=\int_{\gamma_{H}}g_{H}Fd\tau=\xi_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{1}F_{1}(a,t)dt=\int_{\gamma_{V}}g_{V}Fd\tau=-\xi_{2}.

Proof.

Consider the vector fields
v_{1}(x)=e_{1},\ v_{2}(x)=e_{2}
. It is readily seen that
v_{i}(x)=\nabla^{\perp}g_{i}(x)
with
i\in\{H,V\}
. For
F\in\mathcal{V}
we have

\xi_{i}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}F_{i}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}F\cdot\nabla^{\perp}g_{i}=\int_{\partial[0,1]^{2}}g_{i}F\cdot d\tau

For the sake of the computation fix

. Using the periodicity of

and the symmetries of

the integration reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\partial[0,1]^{2}}g_{H}F\cdot d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x_{2}F_{2}(1,x_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}+\int_{0}^{1}F_{1}(x_{1},1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}-\int_{0}^{1}x_{2}F_{2}(0,x_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{2}

to obtain the case for general

, replace
F
by
F(x_{1},x_{2}-1+a)
. The case

is analogous.
∎

and

\inf_{F\in V}\|F\|_{A}>0.

Recalling

5
Numerical Simulations

As anticipated in the previous sections, the behaviour of the total effective diffusivity
C(c,\kappa)
as
\kappa\to 0
is not completely understood. To address this issue, a numerical estimate of the total effective diffusivity
C(c,\kappa)
can be performed by solving equation (
3.12
).

5.1
Numerical setup

We numerically solve the equation on a two-dimensional lattice
[0,1]\times[0,1]
, discretized on a
n\times n
grid of grid-space
d=1/n
, and
H_{\kappa}(x)=\kappa I+A(x)
is a
2n\times 2n
matrix.
In our numerical setup, the orthogonal gradient of
\psi
is taken as:

(5.1)

\mathbb{T}^{2}\ni x\mapsto\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x)=\frac{x^{\perp}}{|x|}\frac{\varphi(|x|)}{\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}},

In order to be able to compare different values of the parameter
c
, the function
\varphi(x)
is normalized, so that the following condition holds:

(5.2)

\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}_{x}}=1.

The following choice of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni r\mapsto\varphi(r)\in\mathbb{R}
is used, in order to satisfy conditions (
2.3
) :

(5.3)

\varphi(r)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\exp{\left(-\frac{a_{1}}{r^{2}}\right)}\exp{\left(-\frac{a_{2}r^{2}}{|r-c|}\right)}

with
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
parameters.

We define the second order elliptic operator
T
in divergence form as:

(5.4)

T\phi_{e}:=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{-}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}H_{ij}D_{j}^{+}\phi_{e}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}H_{ij}D_{j}^{-}\phi_{e}\right)\right)\ ,

where
D_{i}^{-}
and
D_{i}^{+}
are, respectively, the forward and backward difference operators. The two directions are denoted by the index
i=1,2
. This numerical approximation is consistent up to second order, see
[
Gro07
]
. In this framework, the corresponding divergence operator is defined as:

\text{div}f:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{i}^{-}+D_{i}^{+})f_{i}.

We solve the two linear systems

(5.5)

T\phi_{1}=\text{div}\left(A\cdot e_{1}\right)\ ;\quad T\phi_{2}=\text{div}\left(A\cdot e_{2}\right)

employing the direct solver implemented in MATLAB.

After solving the linear systems, we compute the total effective diffusivity, (
4.8
), for different values of the parameter
c
. The total diffusivity is obtained from the real part of the first eigenvalue of the
2\times 2
matrix obtained by averaging on the whole grid. The computation of the total diffusivity from the other eigenvalue leads to consistent results, as the
2\times 2
matrix is (almost) diagonal. The compatibility of the imaginary part of both the eigenvalues with zero was checked.

In all cases, the data obtained at different values of the molecular diffusivity
\kappa
were computed for different values of the gridstep
d
:
d=0.0100,0.00222,0.00167,0.00125
. Then, the final estimate for the total diffusivity was obtained by extrapolating for
d\to 0
.

We check that a compatible result is obtained by plugging our solution into the definition of the total diffusivity in the variational setting, (
4.9
). This second quantity is also extrapolated for
d\to 0
.

Introduce the matrix

The matrix

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\tilde{R}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
be such that
\tilde{R}x=R(x-j)+j
where

and
R

A(\tilde{R}x)=R^{J}A(x)(R^{J})^{t},

and analogously

A(\tilde{R}^{-1}x)=(R^{J})^{t}A(x)R^{J}.

Proof.

Noticing that for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, there exists a unique
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
k=\tilde{R}k^{\prime}
, we write

\displaystyle A(Rx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\tilde{R}x-k)\otimes\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\tilde{R}x-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\tilde{R}x-\tilde{R}k^{\prime})\otimes\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\tilde{R}x-\tilde{R}k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\nabla^{\perp}\psi(R(x-k^{\prime}))\otimes\nabla^{\perp}\psi(R(x-k^{\prime}))

Now since
\nabla^{\perp}\psi(|x|)=\frac{x^{\perp}}{x}f^{\prime}(|x|)
, and
|Rx|=|x|
, we have

\nabla^{\perp}\psi(R(x-k^{\prime}))=\frac{\left(R(x-k^{\prime})\right)^{\perp}}{|x-k^{\prime}|}f^{\prime}(|x-k^{\prime}|).

By the properties of the matrix

\nabla^{\perp}\psi(R(x-k^{\prime}))=JRJ^{t}\left(\nabla^{\perp}\psi(x-k^{\prime})\right).

Now we observe that for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
B\in M_{2\times 2}

(Bv\otimes Bv)_{lm}=B_{lj}v_{j}B_{mi}v_{i}=B_{lj}(v_{j}v_{i}B^{t}_{im})=B_{lj}(v\otimes vB^{t})_{jm}=(B(v\otimes v)B^{t})_{lm}

Putting all together we obtain the desired identity.
∎
