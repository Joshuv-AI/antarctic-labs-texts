Transplantation outcomes in patients with Down syndrome‐associated acute lymphoblastic leukaemia: Implications for treatment intensity and the use of novel therapies
Authors: Hisashi Ishida, Yasuhiro Okamoto, Hirotoshi Sakaguchi, Yasuyuki Arai, Tomoaki Ueda, Shunsuke Yamamoto, Mio Yano, Tomoko Yokosuka, Keiko Okada, Maho Sato, Shuhei Karakawa, Ryoji Kobayashi, Keisuke Kato, Katsuyoshi Koh, Toshihiro Fujiki, Kumiko Goi, Shoji Saito, Junko Takita, Takako Miyamura, Yuhki Koga, Nao Yoshida, Atsushi Sato, Moeko Hino, Ken Tabuchi, Yuki Arakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDown syndrome‐associated acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (DS‐ALL) is associated with inferior outcomes compared with non‐DS‐ALL; however, data on haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in DS‐ALL remain limited. We analysed nationwide data of patients aged <30 years with B‐cell precursor ALL who underwent first allogeneic HSCT between 2000 and 2022 in Japan. In total, 56 patients with DS‐ALL and 3873 with non‐DS‐ALL were identified. The incidences of neutrophil engraftment, grade II–IV acute graft‐versus‐host disease and chronic graft‐versus‐host disease were comparable between groups. The 4‐year event‐free survival (EFS) was lower in DS‐ALL than in non‐DS‐ALL (40.3% vs. 55.2%), but was similar when stratified by disease status at HSCT. The 4‐year EFS rates in first and second complete remission (CR) were 62.7% and 48.2% in DS‐ALL and 69.5% and 56.0% in non‐DS‐ALL respectively. Relapse, rather than non‐relapse mortality (NRM), was the leading cause of treatment failure in DS‐ALL. Among patients with DS‐ALL undergoing HSCT in CR1/2, myeloablative conditioning (MAC) showed a trend towards superior EFS compared with reduced‐intensity conditioning (RIC), which was associated with a higher incidence of NRM. Accordingly, patients in CR1/2 who are unable to tolerate MAC are considered good candidates for emerging novel therapies rather than RIC‐HSCT.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with Down syndrome (DS) are at higher risk of developing acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukaemia than those without DS. 1 Most patients with DS‐associated ALL (DS‐ALL) exhibit a B‐cell precursor (BCP) immunophenotype, and T‐cell ALL is extremely rare. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Previous studies have revealed that DS‐ALL is associated with inferior outcomes compared with non‐DS‐ALL. This is because patients with DS‐ALL are at increased risk of relapse and have a higher non‐relapse mortality (NRM) rate. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Further, post‐relapse survival in DS‐ALL has been dismal. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 Therefore, establishing an appropriate treatment strategy that decreases treatment‐related complications while preventing relapse in patients with DS‐ALL has been significantly challenging.

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is a standard treatment for children with high‐risk ALL. 13 , 14 , 15 However, its role in DS‐ALL remains unclear. Previous studies evaluating HSCT in DS‐ALL have been limited by small sample sizes—including only 11, 11 and 27 patients—as well as the inclusion of earlier treatment eras and heterogeneity in disease status at transplantation. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 In addition, disease stage‐specific analyses were rarely performed. As a result, the clinical characteristics and outcomes of HSCT in DS‐ALL remain poorly defined.

In recent years, several treatment strategies including targeted immunotherapies (such as inotuzumab and blinatumomab) and cellular therapies (such as chimeric antigen receptor [CAR] T‐cell therapy) have been introduced to children with ALL. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Although the treatment outcomes of these novel therapies in patients with DS‐ALL have been promising, the number of patients included in these case series remains limited. Thus, to develop optimal treatment strategies for patients with DS‐ALL, it is important to properly understand the characteristics and treatment outcomes of each therapeutic strategy including HSCT.

In the present study, a retrospective analysis of clinical data on HSCT in children with DS‐ALL was conducted using a national database in Japan. This study aimed to assess the clinical characteristics of HSCT and the clinical factors associated with outcomes in children with DS‐ALL.

METHODS

Patients

The data were collected from the Transplant Registry Unified Management Program (TRUMP), which is sponsored by the Japanese Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy and the Japanese Data Center for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation (JDCHCT). 26 Patients or their parents provided a written informed consent for transplantation and the use of medical records for research in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The current study was approved by the Data Management Committee of the JDCHCT and the Institutional Ethics Committee of Okayama University (2407‐041).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with BCP‐ALL, (2) those aged <30 years at diagnosis, (3) those who had no previous history of HSCT and (4) those who received allogeneic HSCT between 2000 and 2022. Patients with missing data on survival or disease recurrence were excluded from the analysis. All included patients were classified into two groups: those with DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL. Performance status (PS) was assessed according to established criteria. 27 A myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimen was defined as total body irradiation at a dose of >8 Gy, melphalan dose >140 mg/m2 or busulfan dose ≥9 mg/kg. All other regimens were considered as reduced‐intensity conditioning (RIC). 28 Disparities in human leucocyte antigen (HLA) were assigned at the genetic level for HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C and ‐DRB1 for bone marrow and peripheral blood transplantation, and at the serological level for HLA‐A, ‐B and ‐DRB1 for cord blood transplantation. Assignment at the serological level for HLA‐A, ‐B and ‐DRB1 was also permitted for related donors. Diagnosis and clinical grading of acute and chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD) were performed according to established criteria. 29 , 30

Statistical analysis

Probabilities of overall survival (OS) and event‐free survival (EFS) were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier estimates. EFS was defined as survival in continuous remission after HSCT. Relapse was defined as the recurrence of clinical or haematological leukaemia. NRM was defined as death from any cause other than relapse. The competing events were death without engraftment for neutrophil engraftment, death without relapse for haematological relapse, haematological relapse for NRM and death without GVHD or haematological relapse for GVHD. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as an absolute neutrophil count ≥0.5 × 109/L for three consecutive days. Fisher's exact test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare categorical and continuous variables respectively. Univariate analysis was performed using the log‐rank test. In the univariate analysis, the effects of acute and chronic GVHD on survival and events were analysed with the landmark method. In particular, the effects were analysed among patients surviving without events at 60 or 180 days after transplantation for acute and chronic GVHD respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.54.; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (version 4.2.2.; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 31 A p value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences for all analyses.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the patients

In total, 56 patients with DS‐ALL and 3873 with non‐DS‐ALL were included in the study (Table 1). The median follow‐up periods among survivors were 3.8 (range: 0.4–17.3) years and 7.3 (range: 0.0–23.5) years in patients with and without DS respectively. The median ages at diagnosis were 8 (range: 2–27) and 13 (range: 0–29) years in children with and without DS respectively. The distribution of disease subtypes differed significantly between children with DS and those without, as most children with DS lacked common genetic/cytogenetic alterations (ETV6::RUNX1, hyperdiploid, TCF3::PBX1, BCR::ABL1 or KMT2A rearrangements). Children with DS received HSCT at less favourable disease status than those without DS; 44.6% of HSCT was performed during third or later complete remission (CR) or non‐CR for those with DS‐ALL and 28.0% of patients with non‐DS‐ALL did so. Further, children with DS‐ALL more frequently received RIC (41.1% vs. 8.9%, p < 0.001) compared with children without DS. The year of HSCT also differed between the two groups, as HSCT for DS‐ALL was more frequently performed in more recent years (Table 1). The cumulative incidence rates of neutrophil recovery by day 42 were 96.4% (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 83.9%–99.3%) and 95.2% (95% CI: 94.5%–95.8%) in patients with DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL respectively (p = 0.490). The details of pre‐HSCT treatment were largely unavailable. Among patients with DS‐ALL, one patient was reported to receive CAR‐T therapy before HSCT, and no patient was reported to receive blinatumomab before HSCT.

Incidence of acute and chronic GVHD

The cumulative incidence rates of grades II–IV or III–IV acute GVHD by day 100 were 43.6% versus 40.5% (p = 0.528) and 21.4% versus 14.1% (p = 0.065) in patients with DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL, respectively (Figure S1A,B). The cumulative incidence rates of chronic or extensive chronic GVHD at day 365 were 23.3% versus 25.5% (p = 0.963) and 9.0% versus 13.0% (p = 0.778) in patients with DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL respectively (Figure S1C,D).

Transplantation outcomes: Comparison between patients with and without DS

The 4‐year EFS rates were 40.3% (95% CI: 26.9%–53.3%) in those with DS and 55.2% (95% CI: 53.6%–56.8%) in those without DS (p = 0.018). The 4‐year OS rates were 48.0% (95% CI: 33.4%–61.1%) in those with DS and 61.7% (95% CI: 60.1%–63.3%) in those without DS (p = 0.010). The outcomes were similar between the two groups when stratified by disease status (Figure 1); the 4‐year EFS rates for patients with and without DS were 62.7% (95% CI: 30.7%–83.2%) and 69.5% (95% CI: 67.3%–71.6%) in those who received HSCT in CR1 (p = 0.941), 48.2% (95% CI: 20.8%–71.2%) and 56.0% (95% CI: 52.6%–59.2%) in those in CR2 (p = 0.544), and 20.0% (95% CI: 7.3%–37.2%) and 30.5% (95% CI: 27.7%–33.3%) in those with more advanced disease (CR3 or later, or non‐CR) respectively (p = 0.205). The 4‐year OS rates were 65.1% (95% CI: 28.4%–86.4%) and 77.4% (95% CI: 75.3%–79.3%) in those who received transplantation in CR1 (p = 0.775), 68.6% (95% CI: 35.9%–87.0%) and 62.0% (95% CI: 58.6%–65.2%) in those in CR2 (p = 0.661), and 24.0% (95% CI: 9.8%–41.7%) and 35.4% (95% CI: 32.5%–38.4%) in those with more advanced disease for patients with and without DS respectively (p = 0.120).

The cumulative incidence rates of relapse at 4‐year were 36.8% (95% CI: 24.0%–49.6%) and 29.4% (95% CI: 27.9%–30.8%) (p = 0.168), and the cumulative incidence rates of NRM at 4‐year were 23.0% (95% CI: 12.5%–35.4%) and 15.4% (95% CI: 14.3%–16.6%) (p = 0.210) in patients with and without DS respectively. The main cause of treatment failure across all disease statuses for both DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL was relapse (Figure S2A–C). The most frequent cause of death was NRM in both groups, accounting for 55.2% of deaths in patients with DS‐ALL and 54.7% in patients with non‐DS‐ALL. The causes of NRM were similar between the two groups (Table S1).

Transplantation outcomes: Focusing on patients with DS‐ALL receiving HSCT in CR1/2

Next we first focused on patients with DS‐ALL who received HSCT in the first or second CR (n = 31). The cumulative incidence rates of relapse at 4‐year were 34.5% (95% CI: 17.7%–52.0%) and 22.2% (95% CI: 20.6%–23.8%) (p = 0.086), and the cumulative incidence rates of NRM at 4‐year were 8.6% (95% CI: 1.4%–24.7%) and 12.8% (95% CI: 11.5%–14.1%) (p = 0.300) for patients with and without DS respectively (Figure 2).

The results of the univariate analysis of these patients are shown in Table 2. Donor–recipient sex mismatch was associated with worse EFS, with male recipients of female donors having the worst outcomes, followed by female recipients of male donors. Meanwhile, sex‐matched pairs had the best EFS. Moreover, patients who received the MAC regimen exhibited a trend towards a better EFS compared with patients who received the RIC regimen (4‐year EFS: 70.2% vs. 27.4%; p = 0.114; Figure S3). Among patients receiving the MAC regimen, the 4‐year EFS rates were 71.4% (95% CI: 40.6%–88.2%; n = 14) in patients who received TBI‐based MAC and 64.3% (95% CI: 15.1%–90.2%; n = 7) in those who received chemotherapy‐based MAC, with no significant difference between them (p = 0.831). As shown in Table 2, no clear difference was observed in terms of EFS between younger and older patients, or between those who received HSCT with a better PS and those with a worse PS.

Transplantation outcomes: Focusing on patients with DS‐ALL receiving HSCT in advanced disease status

Next, we focused on patients with DS‐ALL who received HSCT in third or later CR (n = 3) or in non‐remission (n = 22). The cumulative incidence rates of relapse at 4‐year were 40.0% (95% CI: 20.6%–58.8%) and 47.9% (95% CI: 44.9%–50.9%) (p = 0.472), and the cumulative incidence rates of NRM at 4‐year were 40.0% (95% CI: 20.5%–58.9%) and 21.6% (95% CI: 19.2%–24.2%) (p = 0.042) for those with and without DS respectively (Figure S2C). In addition, those with a lower PS score exhibited a trend towards a better EFS compared with those with worse PS (4‐year EFS, 27.8% vs. 0.0%; p = 0.063, Table S2).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to date focusing on the clinical outcomes of HSCT in patients with DS‐ALL. Based on the current study, HSCT for children with DS‐ALL was generally performed safely. The outcomes observed in this cohort appear to be more favourable than those reported in earlier studies. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 In addition, a trend towards improved outcomes in more recent cases suggests advances in transplantation practices and supportive care.

In the current study, children with DS‐ALL had inferior EFS and OS compared with children with non‐DS‐ALL when all the disease statuses were combined. This result is consistent with that of previous reports, in which patients with DS‐ALL had increased rates of both relapse and NRM. 5 , 7 , 8 Conversely, the EFS was similar between patients with DS‐ALL and those with non‐DS‐ALL, particularly among those who received HSCT in CR1/2 (Figure 1). Notably, this study showed that children with DS‐ALL who underwent HSCT in CR2 had a relatively favourable EFS (48.2% at 4 years). In contrast, reports from the United States or the United Kingdom have shown OS rates of <20% in patients with relapsed DS‐ALL. 8 , 10 Although the proportion of patients achieving CR2 among all relapsed patients must be considered, these results indicate that HSCT in CR2 may be a viable treatment strategy for children with relapsed DS‐ALL in terms of survival.

In general, HSCT for DS‐ALL has been performed safely. Children with DS‐ALL and those with non‐DS‐ALL had a similar cumulative incidence of engraftment, grade II–IV acute GVHD and chronic GVHD. Thus, the cumulative incidence of NRM was also similar between the two groups, particularly among those who received HSCT in CR1/2 (Table 2; Figure S2A,B). This suggests that HSCT for DS‐ALL has become safer with more recent, refined HSCT procedures, even in patients with more advanced diseases. Consequently, relapse, rather than NRM, was the main cause of treatment failure among patients with DS‐ALL across all disease statuses, which is also consistent with previous reports. 17 , 18

In this study, the frequencies of overall NRM and the distributions of the causes of NRM were similar between patients with DS‐ALL and non‐DS‐ALL (Figure S2; Table S1). This finding was in contrast to that of previous reports indicating that children with DS had increased susceptibility to lung complications and infections. 5 , 9 , 16 Moreover, individuals with DS are in a state of heightened inflammatory activity, 32 , 33 which may place them at increased risk for treatment‐related complications. 34 While the frequency of NRM was not elevated as compared to non‐DS patients in this study, it should be noted that several studies have revealed that patients with DS who survive acute leukaemia are at a substantially higher risk of late mortality than non‐DS survivors and DS controls in the long term. 35 , 36 , 37 Thus, long‐term follow‐up studies are warranted to further refine the treatment strategy for patients with DS‐ALL.

Notably, this study showed a trend towards inferior outcomes with RIC compared with MAC among patients with DS‐ALL who received HSCT during CR1/2. Importantly, patients who received RIC also had a higher incidence of NRM—not only relapse—than those who received MAC (Table 2). This finding suggests the presence of selection bias, whereby children who could not tolerate MAC because of poor systemic condition or severe comorbidities were preferentially treated with RIC‐HSCT. By contrast, novel therapeutic agents for BCP‐ALL have emerged in recent years, and they are not only safe for use in patients with DS‐ALL but are also highly effective. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Thus, based on our results, when MAC‐HSCT is not feasible due to systemic conditions or other complications, alternative treatment strategies including novel agents, rather than RIC‐HSCT, should be considered. Nonetheless, this study was not designed to evaluate the outcomes of RIC‐HSCT in patients for whom RIC was intentionally selected based on favourable disease conditions, rather than as a necessity due to inability to tolerate MAC.

Based on these findings, we propose the following considerations for HSCT in DS‐ALL. HSCT performed in CR1/2 yielded relatively favourable outcomes in this study, suggesting that it remains a feasible treatment option in this setting. However, in the light of emerging novel therapies such as inotuzumab, blinatumomab and CAR T‐cell therapy, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 these results should be interpreted in the context of and compared with outcomes achieved with newer treatment modalities to define the optimal therapeutic strategy. Notably, even among patients who achieved CR, a substantial incidence of NRM was observed following RIC, indicating that RIC‐HSCT should not be selected solely on the basis of poor general condition or as a less intensive alternative. Furthermore, patients in CR3 or non‐CR exhibited high NRM, suggesting that these populations—along with fragile patients in CR1/2—may be more appropriate candidates for novel therapeutic approaches.

This study has several limitations. First, as the number of included patients with DS‐ALL was limited despite the long study period, we were unable to perform multivariate analyses. Moreover, several important prognostic factors such as minimal residual disease status or details of pre‐HSCT therapy were not available in this registry; therefore, potential confounding due to differences in baseline characteristics between groups could not be adequately adjusted for. Thus, results regarding ‘comparable’ outcomes in this study must be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the small sample size increases the risk of type II error—that is, the study may fail to detect true differences in outcomes between groups. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of equivalence. Second, the follow‐up duration of patients with DS‐ALL (3.8 years) was shorter than that of patients with non‐DS‐ALL (7.3 years). This might have resulted in an underestimation of late events in the DS‐ALL group, potentially leading to an overestimation of treatment outcomes in this population. Third, although we analysed the frequencies and causes of NRMs in this cohort, information on late adverse events was largely unavailable and, therefore, could not be analysed. Future studies incorporating detailed late‐effect data should be performed to completely evaluate the long‐term efficacy and safety of HSCT for DS‐ALL. Finally, the retrospective nature of this study represents an important limitation. Selection bias, as exemplified by the RIC‐HSCT group, may have been present and could have limited the ability to make direct comparisons between groups. Despite these limitations, we believe that the current study adds important insights, particularly regarding the current status and limitations of HSCT for DS‐ALL, and provides a basis for comparison with recently emerging therapeutic modalities.

CONCLUSION

This nationwide retrospective study showed that the EFS of children with DS‐ALL was similar to that of children with non‐DS‐ALL when stratified by disease status at the time of HSCT. Our findings suggest that patients who could not tolerate MAC might be more appropriate candidates for emerging novel therapies rather than RIC‐HSCT. Physicians should cautiously consider how to integrate HSCT and novel therapies by balancing short‐term efficacy with long‐term survivorship.
