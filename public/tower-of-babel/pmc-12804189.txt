Salivary short chain fatty acids serve as biomarkers of periodontal inflammatory burden
Authors: Kazu Takeuchi-Hatanaka, Yasushi Shirahase, Toshiyuki Yoshida, Mari Kono, Naoki Toya, Kenji Konishi, Kazuhiro Omori, Shogo Takashiba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory condition associated with systemic diseases. Early detection and intervention are crucial; however, conventional diagnostic methods require specialized dental procedures. Therefore, we aimed to develop a noninvasive saliva-based screening method that can be easily performed outside dental clinics. This cross-sectional pilot study evaluated three periodontal indices—probing depth, Periodontal Inflamed Surface Area (PISA), and periodontal epithelial surface area—in relation to short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bacterial profiles in the saliva. Saliva samples collected during the day exhibited stronger correlations with periodontal indices than waking time samples, demonstrating a significant association with periodontal pathogens, protease activity, and elevated levels of butyric acid. The diagnostic thresholds for PISA were 300 mm2 and 600 mm2. Multivariate logistic regression and likelihood ratio analyses identified the combination of enzymatic SCFA markers and dipstick-based occult blood or leukocyte detection as a promising biomarker pair. Combining enzymatic SCFA markers with occult blood demonstrated a positive likelihood ratio of 3.4 and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.19 for PISA ≥ 600 mm2, with a post-test probability of 77%, sensitivity of 86%, and specificity of 75%. These findings suggest that combining salivary enzymatic and dipstick-based biomarkers provides a simple, cost-effective, and moderately informative screening strategy for periodontitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31364-9.Keywords: Periodontitis, Screening, Saliva, Short-chain fatty acid, Periodontal inflamed surface area, Cross-sectional studies

Introduction

According to the WHO Global Oral Health Report 20221, periodontal disease affects approximately 3.5 billion people worldwide and is recognized as one of the most prevalent chronic inflammatory conditions. In addition to tooth loss and impaired oral function, periodontal disease contributes to systemic inflammation and is associated with a wide range of chronic disorders, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and preterm birth2–7. These associations highlight the urgent need for effective population-level strategies for early detection and prevention.

However, current diagnostic approaches for periodontal disease rely on specialized clinical procedures, such as probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and bleeding on probing (BOP), all of which require skilled dental professionals. Although advanced indices such as Periodontal Inflamed Surface Area (PISA) and Periodontal Epithelial Surface Area (PESA) provide valuable quantitative assessments8,9, they are time-consuming and impractical for large-scale screening. These limitations hinder early detection, particularly in settings where dental professionals are not readily available. Consequently, there is a growing demand for a simple, cost-effective, and reproducible screening method that can be implemented by non-dental healthcare providers or the patients themselves.

Saliva is an ideal diagnostic medium for this approach because it is non-invasive, easy to collect without specialized equipment or training, and reflects both local oral and systemic health. Previous studies have identified several salivary biomarkers, such as cytokines, enzymes, bacterial virulence factors, and metabolic byproducts, as potential indicators of periodontal disease10–13. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly butyric acid (C4), have been implicated in epithelial damage, immune dysregulation, and disease progression14–21. Notably, considering the multifactorial nature of periodontal pathogenesis, the diagnostic value of these biomarkers may be enhanced when assessed together. Similar to matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP-8), which can be measured by enzymatic or immunoassay methods without mass spectrometry22, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) can also be quantified enzymatically, providing a simple and practical approach for clinical applications23.

In this study, we aimed to develop a practical saliva-based screening tool for periodontal disease that could be applied to large populations regardless of the clinical setting or examiner expertise. We retrospectively analyzed clinical data as a pilot study using EnzC36 (an enzymatically assayed value of [C3: propionate + C4 + IC4: iso-butyrate + C5: pentanoate + IC5: iso-pentanoate + C6: hexanoate]) to examine the relationship between salivary biomarkers and established periodontal indices. Specifically, we sought to identify the optimal biomarker combinations, including SCFAs, proteolytic enzymes, and microbial components, that most accurately reflect disease severity, thereby establishing a robust diagnostic model suitable for routine screening.

Association between SCFA levels and periodontal severity

SCFA concentrations tended to increase with increasing periodontal disease severity (Fig. 1). Levels were approximately three to six times higher during waking than during daytime. A severity-dependent increase in SCFAs was noted in the PISA, although the increase was less than twofold during daytime sampling. These findings warrant further analysis focusing on the relationship between SCFA levels and PISA.

Distribution of oral bacteria by PISA severity

The bacterial genus composition and mean total bacterial counts varied with PISA severity at both waking time and daytime samples (Fig. 2A). In healthy individuals (PISA-1), salivary bacterial counts were 139,000 counts/µL at waking time and 27,000 counts/µL during the day; daytime counts were approximately 20% of waking levels. However, bacterial counts during the daytime increased with the severity of PISA. Additionally, 10 main bacterial genera were detected in each PISA severity during waking time and daytime.

Therefore, we compared the bacterial genera in PISA-1 and PISA-4. Differences in the bacterial composition were observed between the two groups (Fig. 2B). In the waking time samples, genera such as Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, Parvimonas, Treponema, and Neisseria increased in severity (PISA-1 vs. PISA-4), whereas Prevotella, Leptotrichia, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, and Rothia decreased. Multivariate logistic analysis of single biomarkers for screening

Using the pooled dataset of 43 cases (25 from 2019 and 18 from 2021; Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S1), logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the relevant biomarkers in daytime saliva (Table 4). Individual biomarkers were evaluated as explanatory variables, along with age, sex, and number of teeth present. When using PISA Level 3 as the threshold, significant associations were found between C4, C5, C6, and the dipstick items ALB, LEU, and protein (PRO). When using PISA Level 4 as the threshold, C4, IC4, C5, C6, ALB, and occult blood (BLD) levels were significant. C4, C5, C6, and ALB levels were consistently recognized as relevant across both severity thresholds.

Multivariate and likelihood ratio analyses of two-biomarker combinations

Based on the single-factor logistic regression results, two biomarker combinations were evaluated after adjusting for age and sex. Combinations of EnzC36/C2 and one of the dipstick biomarkers (ALB, BLD, or LEU) were assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) metrics (Table 5A, B). For PISA Level 4 (Table 5B), the combination of EnzC36/C2 and BLD yielded the highest diagnostic performance, with an IDI of 0.339 (2p = 0.0019; 95% reliability interval CI]: 0.125–0.551) and a significant NRI of 1.21 (2p < 0.001). Although the AUC was 0.841 (p = 0.053), it was not statistically significant because the 95% CI included zero.

To further evaluate the diagnostic utility, a likelihood ratio analysis was performed using risk probabilities derived from the logistic regression model, assuming a pre-test probability of 50% (Table 5C). For PISA Level 3, the EnzC36/C2 and BLD combination demonstrated the highest post-test probabilities, yielding a positive likelihood ratio (PLR) of 2.5, negative likelihood ratio (NLR) of 0.36, and post-test probability of 72%. For PISA Level 4, the PLR increased to 3.4, NLR decreased to 0.19, and post-test probability reached 77%.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the use of salivary biomarkers for periodontitis screening, focusing on both waking time and daytime samples. Saliva collected during waking time exhibited markedly higher concentrations of bacterial metabolites, such as butyric acid (C4), which was approximately four times more concentrated than daytime saliva (Table 2). Similarly, the bacterial counts in the waking time samples were approximately five times higher (Fig. 2A). Despite these elevated levels, only a few biomarkers from waking time saliva were associated with clinical indices such as PISA and PESA, whereas numerous correlations were observed in daytime saliva (Table 3). This suggests that daytime samples, which better reflect the current inflammatory and epithelial status, are more appropriate for evaluating periodontitis severity using these indices.

The bacterial profile of daytime saliva from patients with periodontitis revealed increased levels of Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, and Rothia, which together accounted for approximately 30% of the elevated bacterial count (Fig. 2). There was a correlation between the PISA Level-specific bacterial increase in Porphyromonas, Streptococcus, Rothia, Fusobacterium, and Neisseria and the C4 concentration in saliva (Fig. 3A). This aligns with previous reports showing that Porphyromonas and Fusobacterium synergistically enhance their metabolic activity by up to 20-fold when they coexist in periodontal biofilms24. In contrast, waking time samples showed an increased abundance of Butyricimonas, Cardiobacteriaceae, and gut-associated bacteria capable of producing SCFAs, such as C425,26, but not typically implicated in periodontitis. This shift may result from altered carbon source availability during sleep due to reduced salivary flow, leading to mucin degradation by oral bacteria in a manner analogous to gut microbial activity27. However, the low abundance of Bifidobacterium, a known mucin-degrader28, suggests that the exact origin of SCFAs in waking time saliva remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Salivary nitrite provides physiologically active nitric oxide, which is essential for vascular endothelial cell function and blood pressure regulation29–31. In this study, salivary nitrite concentrations decreased as periodontal inflammation worsened at PISA-4 (Fig. 3B). Haemophilus and Neisseria increased in number, while Actinomyces declined (Fig. 3B); these bacteria are reportedly among the top four oral species (including Veillonella) that convert nitrate to nitrite and nitric oxide32. Although the increase (Neisseria: strong reducer) and decrease (Actinomyces: moderate reducer) of nitrate-reducing bacteria were observed, salivary nitrite concentrations decreased at PISA-4. Other factors, such as bacteria on the tongue, the effects of mouthwash, and certain foods, may influence nitrite levels. Larger studies with well-defined groups may better identify the cause.

In addition, strong associations between PESA and ALB suggest that PESA reflects tissue damage and protein leakage. In contrast, PISA is associated with oral bacterial infection and inflammatory markers, including Kgp, MMP-8, C4, C5, C6, and LEU (Table 3). However, assays of Kgp and MMP-8 need enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) systems, and assays for C4, C5, and C6 need mass spectrometry. Combining the automatic biochemical analyzer tests with dipstick tests (Table 4), may further enhance its screening accuracy.

We performed multivariate logistic regression and likelihood ratio analyses to assess the clinical utility. Although ROC-AUC and NRI alone showed moderate performances (Table 5), the likelihood ratio analysis provided clearer insights (Table 5). For the EnzC36/C2 and BLD combination for PISA Level 4, the PLR and NLR improved to 3.4 and 0.19, respectively, yielding a post-test probability of 77%. These values were within a moderately informative range. The sensitivity (86%) and specificity (75%) were well balanced, suggesting that the test may have clinical utility for triage, especially in intermediate-risk populations. Although a negative result may help rule out disease in individuals at moderate to high risk (for example, with a pre-test probability of ≥ 30–50%), a positive result should prompt additional diagnostic evaluation (including imaging and detailed clinical examinations) or a specialist referral, as the PLR is not sufficient for standalone confirmation. Therefore, although the test itself does not confirm the diagnosis, it significantly contributes to clinical decisions when combined with patient symptoms, risk factors, and other findings.

Although the sample size was limited to 43 participants and no attention was given to systemic conditions, this study highlights the potential of a multi-marker salivary screening approach. By integrating enzymatic and dipstick methods, this test can be adapted into simple, equipment-free formats such as dipsticks or biosensor devices, making it feasible for use in clinical, community, or remote settings. Measuring individual SCFAs (C3–C6) usually requires mass spectrometry; however, EnzC36/C2 serves as a practical surrogate in this case. We can provide EnzC36/EnzC2, since our C2 enzymatic reagent aligns with the C2 detected by the mass spectrometer23, indicating the potential for using the EnzC36/EnzC2 test in future studies. The dipstick-based chemical method for BLD showed a stronger association with PISA than the immunological method for hemoglobin protein (Hb pro). Future studies with larger sample sizes should focus on optimizing marker combinations and enhancing posttest probabilities. Overall, this diagnostic model offers moderate but meaningful clinical utility, especially when integrated into broader screening and decision-support strategies for periodontitis.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a practical saliva-based screening method for periodontitis using daytime samples. Among the tested indices, PISA was strongly associated with inflammatory markers, SCFAs, and bacterial composition. The combination of the automatic biochemical analyzer-based EnzC36/EnzC2 with dipstick-based BLD or LEU yielded moderate diagnostic accuracy and may serve as a useful tool for early detection. Given its simplicity and non-invasiveness, this approach has the potential for application in non-dental settings. Further validation in larger cohorts is required to confirm its clinical utility and optimize biomarker combinations.

Materials and methods

Reagents

The EnzC2 and EnzC36 reagents were prepared according to a previously reported method23. Salivary Hb pro, LDH, and TP assay kits were procured as previously described23. The Human Gingipain K (KGP) and Human Gingipain R (RGP) ELISA Kits were purchased from MyBioSource Inc. (San Diego, CA, USA). A salivary MMP-8 activity assay kit, comprising both the activated and non-activated forms (MMP-8 Act. and MMP-8 non-Act), was purchased from QuickZyme Biosciences (Leiden, Netherlands). A urine qualitative test strip (MEDITAPE UC-12S) was used to detect salivary BLD, ketones (KET), GLU, PRO, pH, nitrite, LEU, and ALB. Test strips were obtained from Sysmex Corp. (Kobe, Japan).

Saliva collection

Saliva samples were obtained from Japanese men and women aged ≥ 20 years who had at least 15 teeth. All daytime samples were collected at least two hours after eating, drinking, or tooth brushing. The participants were instructed to rinse their mouths with 20 mL of saline solution to reduce oral debris, which may disturb the dipstick measurement, and chew gum for 5 min to stimulate full salivary secretion33. This study was approved by the Okayama University Ethics Review Committee using data from 30 cases collected in 2019 (Approval No. 1807–025) and 22 cases collected in 2021 (Approval No. 2009–053) (Supplementary Fig. S1), from which nine cases were excluded for the analysis in this study because of lack of data and withdrawal of consent. In the 2021 cohort, waking time samples were also collected immediately after awakening and chewing gum as described above. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study protocol. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, as well as the Declaration of Helsinki.

Periodontal disease indices and clinical examination

The periodontal status was evaluated using PD, PISA, and PESA. Each index was classified into four severity levels (Table 1). PD was classified as follows: PD-1 (< 4 mm), PD-2 (4–6 mm), PD-3 (≥ 6 mm in one tooth), and PD-4 (≥ 6 mm in two or more teeth)23. PISA classifications were based on clinical thresholds: PISA-1 (< 100 mm2), PISA-2 (< 300 mm2), PISA-3 (< 600 mm2), and PISA-4 (≥ 600 mm2), referencing reports that cited PISA values of 450 mm2 (vs. salivary cytokines)34 and 500 mm2 (vs. serum hsCRP)35 as disease thresholds. The PESA classification was adjusted to align with PISA-based distributions: PESA-1 (< 1,150 mm2), PESA-2 (< 1,300 mm2), PESA-3 (< 1,800 mm2), and PESA-4 (≥ 1,800 mm2) (Table 1). Eighteen cases from 2021 were grouped using all three indices, whereas all 43 cases were staged using PISA alone (Supplementary Table S1).

A well-trained examiner (K T-H), who is the JSP Board-Certified Periodontist, performed the clinical examination. However, some additional data was collected by our institute’s clinical staff under K T-H's supervision (less than 20% of the data). The data sets of the 2019 and the 2021 cohorts were summarized in the Supplementary Data Set file.

Measurements

EnzC36, Hb pro, LDH, and TP levels were measured as previously described23. The ratio of EnzC36 to C2 (EnzC36/C2) was calculated by dividing the EnzC36 value by the C2 value and multiplying by 1,000. The EnzC36 data obtained in 2019 were normalized to the mass spectrometry method. Consequently, the EnzC36/C2 ratio was calculated using the C2 values derived from mass spectrometry. Salivary SCFAs fraction concentrations and bacterial flora were analyzed according to previously published protocols23. Total bacterial counts were obtained using the microbial detection function of a fully automatic urine formed element analysis device UF-5000 (Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan) after a five-fold dilution with sterile saline. Genus-level bacterial counts were calculated by multiplying the 16S rRNA occupancy by the total bacterial count. The qualitative test strip (MEDITAPE UC-11A) was analyzed using a fully automated urinary chemistry analyzer UC-3500 (Sysmex Corp.) after a five-fold dilution of saliva with sterile saline. The reflectance values were used without further conversion. However, the nitrite concentration in Fig. 3 was converted to reflectance absorbance instead of reflectance, and the reflectance absorbance of a normal person was set to 100% (Reflectance absorbance = −Log10(reflectance/100)).

Statistical analysis

Although PD is not a continuous variable, severity stages (1–4) were treated as continuous for statistical purposes. For correlation analysis, the data were log-transformed (common logarithm (base 10)), and Pearson’s correlation coefficients and risk ratios were calculated (Table 3). Logistic regression analysis revealed abnormal odds ratios following log transformation; thus, standardized values (z-scores) were used instead. Binary variables based on cutoff values for PISA Levels 3 and 4 were established, and multivariate logistic regression was conducted using age, sex, number of remaining teeth, and individual biomarkers as predictor variables (Table 4). EnzC36/C2, ALB, BLD, and LEU were identified as strongly associated biomarkers. TP and PRO were excluded from the multivariate models because of their potential multicollinearity with ALB. The LDH and Hb pro combination, which is commonly used for periodontal screening in Japan, was used for comparative purposes (Table 5). Multivariate logistic models incorporating the two combined biomarkers were also assessed while controlling for age and sex. The screening performance was evaluated using three indices: ROC-AUC, NRI, and IDI (Table 5). Owing to concerns regarding the reliability of the AUC, the NRI and IDI were selectively applied. As IDI reflects enhancements in both sensitivity and specificity, it was prioritized in the interpretation36,37. Multicollinearity was not observed among the variables (Table 5). A 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed based on the disease probability derived from logistic regression (Table 5), assuming a pre-test probability of 50%, and likelihood ratio analysis was performed38.

Statistical analyses were conducted using EZR software (version 1.66). The NRI and IDI analyses were conducted using R commands through the EZR interface. The “rms” package from The Comprehensive R Archive Network was installed in the EZR library (compatible with Rcmdr version 2.9–1). The likelihood ratio test was performed by constructing 2 × 2 contingency tables based on the predicted risk probabilities from logistic regression models, allowing for comparison of model fit.
