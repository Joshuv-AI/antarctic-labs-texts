Artificial intelligence for postoperative plain radiographic assessment after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: current evidence, clinical readiness, and limitations
Authors: Seok Won Chung, Sung-Jin Park, Yu Sung Yoon, Dong-Hyun Kim, Chul-Hyun Cho, Jun-Young Kim, Jong Pil Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostoperative radiographic follow-up after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) plays an essential role in the early detection of implant-related complications and longitudinal risk stratification. However, interpretation of plain radiographs remains limited by interobserver variability and low sensitivity to subtle or progressive mechanical changes. This review was intended to survey the current evidence regarding artificial intelligence (AI) applications for postoperative plain radiograph–based assessment after RTSA, with a focus on clinical readiness, validated performance, and existing limitations. A narrative review was conducted with a specific focus on AI studies relating to shoulder arthroplasty and postoperative plain radiographic analysis. Applications outside shoulder arthroplasty or those based primarily on computed tomography were excluded. Among AI applications in RTSA imaging, implant identification and automated measurement of glenosphere orientation demonstrated the highest level of clinical readiness, with reproducible accuracy reported in multiple studies. In contrast, AI-based detection of scapular notching progression, component loosening, baseplate migration, and acromial or scapular spine stress reactions remains exploratory, with limited shoulder-specific validation. Across these domains, the principal barrier to clinical translation is not algorithmic capability but the lack of high-quality, longitudinally annotated RTSA radiographic datasets. Current AI applications in postoperative RTSA radiographs primarily serve to augment existing radiographic assessment, rather than replace established clinical interpretation. While select tasks are approaching clinical usability, broader adoption will require shoulder-specific longitudinal data, validated outcome-linked thresholds, and integration into routine clinical workflows.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Shoulder arthroplasty, Prosthesis failure, Radiography, Scapula

INTRODUCTION

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) is widely used for the treatment of complex shoulder conditions, demonstrating reliable improvements in pain relief and function [1-3]. Despite these advances, postoperative mechanical complications, such as malalignment, scapular notching, component loosening, and stress-related fractures, remain clinically relevant and often evolve gradually over time [4-6]. Plain radiographs continue to serve as the primary imaging modality for routine postoperative surveillance after RTSA owing to their wide availability, low cost, and feasibility for longitudinal follow-up [7,8]. However, their radiographic interpretation is inherently limited by interobserver variability, sensitivity to patient positioning, and difficulty in quantifying subtle changes at the implant–bone interface [4,9,10]. These limitations complicate early detection of progressive mechanical failure and reduce the reproducibility of radiographic assessment across institutions [11,12].

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning–based image analysis, has emerged as a potential tool to standardize and automate specific aspects of radiographic interpretation in musculoskeletal imaging [13-15]. Recent bibliometric analysis suggests that global research interest in AI applications, especially in shoulder and elbow surgery, has been rapidly increasing, reflecting both opportunities and limitations in clinical translation [14,16]. Moreover, task-specific deep-learning approaches have been used to automatically measure key positional features on postoperative shoulder radiographs, achieving high segmentation accuracy and strong human–AI agreement [15,17]. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, AI-based methods are intended to improve measurement consistency, reduce subjective variability, and enable objective longitudinal comparison of serial radiographs [18]. However, meaningful implementation in RTSA imaging depends not only on algorithm performance but also on the availability of large, well-curated, shoulder-specific radiographic datasets and robust external validation [19].

The purpose of this review was to summarize the current state of evidence for AI applications in postoperative plain radiographic assessment after RTSA to distinguish clinically mature applications from exploratory concepts. Moreover, the review was intended to highlight the key limitations that currently constrain broader clinical implementation. This review, which was a narrative survey, reviewed the English-language literature on AI applied to shoulder arthroplasty and postoperative plain radiographic assessment. The survey drew from major electronic databases, including PubMed/Medline. Priority was given to studies most relevant to clinical readiness and to shoulder-specific applications, while research addressing applications unrelated to shoulder arthroplasty or based primarily on cross-sectional imaging was not emphasized. Consistent with the present narrative scope, a formal systematic search protocol with predefined screening counts was not applied; sources were instead selected to represent the current state, illustrative methods, and principal limitations of the field.

To make the readiness gradations explicit, three readiness tiers are applied throughout this review. Near-clinical readiness denotes tasks supported by reproducible, shoulder-specific (or closely transferable) validation, demonstrated agreement with expert assessment, and a natural fit to existing radiographic workflows, broadly corresponding to technology readiness levels 6–7. Exploratory or investigational denotes methodological plausibility without shoulder-specific external validation and frequent extrapolation from adjacent joints or imaging modalities (levels 4–5). Conceptual denotes proof-of-principle or theoretical approaches that lack shoulder-specific data (levels 2–3). This type of staging is consistent with those of frameworks proposed for the phased clinical evaluation of medical AI, such as developmental and exploratory clinical investigations of decision support systems driven by artificial intelligence (DECIDE-AI). Analysis of Glenosphere Orientation

Among AI applications in RTSA imaging, automated measurement of glenosphere orientation represents the most mature and reproducible use case. These systems are typically based on deep learning–driven anatomical landmark detection, which enables consistent identification of humeral and scapular reference points on plain radiographs.

Crutcher et al. [20] demonstrated that a convolutional neural network could localize key landmarks with a sub-2-mm error, allowing automated calculation of clinically relevant alignment parameters, including glenosphere tilt and inclination. More recently, task-specific models have shown strong agreement with expert measurements and reduced interobserver variability when applied to postoperative shoulder radiographs [15].

From a clinical perspective, this application aligns well with existing workflows, as it automates measurements already performed manually rather than introducing novel or unvalidated metrics. Consequently, AI-based glenosphere orientation analysis can be considered nearly clinically ready, particularly as a tool for standardization and documentation. However, external validation across different implant designs and radiographic acquisition protocols remains limited. Widespread clinical deployment will require further multicenter evaluation.

Analysis of Baseplate Migration and Loosening

AI-based assessment of baseplate micromotion is technically feasible but currently under-explored in shoulder-specific datasets. Accurate detection of baseplate migration requires robust registration of serial radiographs using stable bony landmarks combined with geometric normalization to account for changes in projection and patient positioning.

While an AI model has yet to demonstrate validated performance on plain radiographs for detecting baseplate migration after RTSA, related image registration techniques have shown high spatial precision in other arthroplasty contexts [21,22]. In theory, such methods could enable objective detection of clinically relevant migration thresholds (vertical displacement ≥2 mm), which are difficult to reliably assess by visual inspection alone.

Notably, the advantage of AI in this domain lies not in enhanced visual perception but in mathematical alignment and comparison of serial images, which reduce the measurement noise introduced by radiographic variability. Presently, however, the absence of longitudinal, shoulder-specific training data and outcome-linked validation limits this application to a conceptual or proof-of-principle stage.

Analysis of Scapular Notching

Scapular notching remains a clinically important but challenging complication to assess using conventional radiography. The widely used Sirveaux classification demonstrates only fair interobserver reliability and provides a categorical description that inadequately reflects the continuous nature of bone loss [4]. Meanwhile, AI-based approaches have been proposed to augment, rather than replace, existing classification systems by enabling automated segmentation of the scapular neck and quantification of the erosion area or volume [23]. It should be noted, however, that this supporting evidence derives from computed tomography-based segmentation. Whether comparable quantification is achievable on plain radiographs—given the projection overlap and absence of cross-sectional depth—remains to be demonstrated. Such quantitative metrics may nonetheless offer greater sensitivity to change than categorical grading alone. For example, volumetric analysis could detect progressive osteolysis despite an unchanged Sirveaux grade. However, the clinical relevance of detecting small quantitative changes remains uncertain. Increased sensitivity raises the risk of overinterpretation or overdiagnosis in the absence of validated thresholds linked to clinical outcomes. While longitudinal studies have shown that progression to advanced notching precedes late glenoid loosening [6], it has not yet been established whether earlier detection of subtle progression alters management or outcomes.

Accordingly, AI-based scapular notching analysis should currently be viewed as an investigational adjunct that may improve consistency and enable research into progression dynamics, rather than as a standalone clinical decision tool.

Analysis of Humeral Component Loosening

Early detection of humeral component loosening on plain radiographs is notoriously difficult owing to overlapping cortical bone, metallic artifacts, and subtle radiolucent lines [24]. AI-based approaches have therefore focused on radiographic texture analysis, in which convolutional neural networks learn the pixel-level patterns associated with early interface changes rather than relying on visually apparent radiolucencies [25]. Although shoulder-specific evidence remains sparse, studies in hip and knee arthroplasty have reported that AI models can detect aseptic loosening with high sensitivity, in some series exceeding that of human observers [26-28]. These findings establish methodological plausibility rather than expected shoulder-specific performance; the distinct implant geometry and the metal-overlap problem particular to RTSA mean that comparable accuracy on shoulder radiographs cannot be assumed.

Moreover, extrapolation from other joints must be cautiously interpreted. Differences in implant geometry, bone quality, and loading conditions limit direct transferability, and no validated AI model currently exists for detecting humeral loosening after RTSA on plain radiographs. Therefore, this application remains exploratory and is highly dependent on future availability of shoulder-specific longitudinal datasets.

Analysis of Acromial and Scapular Spine Stress Reactions

AI-based detection of acromial and scapular spine stress reactions is at an early conceptual stage. Early radiographic signs, such as subtle cortical thickening or trabecular remodeling, are difficult to recognize even for experienced clinicians, and they are often missed prior to fracture [5,7]. Developing AI models for this task would require precisely annotated, longitudinal radiographs from patients with confirmed progression to stress fracture, a type of dataset that is currently scarce. While large musculoskeletal datasets have enabled fracture detection at a study level [29], they lack the temporal and regional annotations necessary to model early stress reactions.

From a technical standpoint, AI may eventually offer advantages through quantitative analysis of trabecular texture and density patterns [25,30]. To date, however, these approaches remain theoretical, and no clinically applicable AI tool exists for early stress reaction detection after RTSA.

Analysis of Screw Loosening and Breakage

Detection of screw loosening or breakage represents the least mature AI application in RTSA radiographic analysis. These events are rare, resulting in a severe class imbalance that limits effective model training. Additionally, radiographic signs are subtle and often obscured by implant geometry. Conceptual AI strategies include quantitative analysis of pixel intensity gradients at the screw–bone interface for loosening [31], and anomaly detection methods for early identification of structural discontinuities associated with fatigue-related breakage [32,33]. However, these approaches have not been validated in shoulder arthroplasty datasets and remain experimental.

In summary, AI applications for postoperative RTSA radiographs demonstrate asymmetric clinical maturity. Automated measurement tasks, particularly glenosphere orientation, are approaching clinical usability, whereas applications targeting complication detection remain constrained by data scarcity, low event prevalence, and limited longitudinal validation. At the present time, AI should be regarded primarily as a tool for measurement standardization and research augmentation, rather than as a comprehensive solution for complication surveillance.

TECHNICAL AND CLINICAL GAPS IN AI DEVELOPMENT

Despite growing interest in AI-based analysis of postoperative RTSA radiographs, the primary obstacles to clinical translation are not algorithmic limitations; rather, they are the structural and clinical constraints inherent to the available data and imaging workflow [11,24,34]. The following gaps explain why many AI applications remain exploratory despite demonstrated technical feasibility.

Challenge of Low-Prevalence, High-Impact Complications

One of the most fundamental limitations in AI development for RTSA imaging is the low prevalence of clinically critical complications, such as screw loosening, hardware failure, liner dissociation, and catastrophic glenoid loosening. These events are rare but carry high clinical impact, creating a pronounced class imbalance in available datasets [24,35].

From a machine-learning perspective, a severe class imbalance limits an algorithm’s ability to learn meaningful representations of abnormal patterns. In the absence of sufficient positive examples, even well-designed models tend to default to the dominant “normal” class, resulting in low sensitivity for rare events [12,35]. Notably, this limitation reflects data scarcity rather than insufficient model complexity and it cannot be resolved through algorithmic refinement alone [35].

Clinically, this challenge is compounded by the subtle radiographic appearance of early failure patterns, which are often indistinguishable from normal postoperative variation. Consequently, no AI model to date has demonstrated validated, reliable detection of these low-prevalence, high-risk complications on plain RTSA radiographs [24,34].

Detecting Subtle Bony Changes: Stress Reactions and Early Osteolysis

Early bony changes, including acromial or scapular spine stress reactions and incipient osteolysis, represent another major gap in AI development. These processes manifest as diffuse, low-contrast alterations in cortical or trabecular structure, rather than discrete radiographic signs [5,11]. Training AI models to recognize such changes requires high-resolution, region-specific annotations and longitudinal imaging data linked to confirmed clinical outcomes, such as subsequent fracture or implant failure. In RTSA, such datasets are exceedingly rare [11,36]. Most available musculoskeletal datasets are cross-sectional, labeled at the image or study level, and lack the temporal granularity necessary to model progressive biomechanical injury [29]. As a result, while AI-based texture analysis and density modeling are theoretically attractive, current applications in this domain remain conceptual rather than clinically actionable [11,25].

Limitation of Static Image Analysis in Longitudinal Clinical Problems

Perhaps the most important conceptual gap in current AI applications for RTSA radiographs is the reliance on static, single-timepoint image analysis. Most models are designed to classify abnormalities or measure parameters from an isolated radiograph, whereas clinical decision-making in shoulder arthroplasty is inherently longitudinal [37]. In practice, surgeons are less concerned with the absolute presence of a finding than with its trajectory over time, such as the rate of scapular notching progression or incremental baseplate migration. Static models are inherently incapable of capturing these progression dynamics, which limits their relevance for timing intervention or modifying follow-up strategies [37,38].

Although temporal modeling frameworks exist in other domains, their application to RTSA imaging has been limited by the lack of large, consistently acquired serial radiographic datasets. Consequently, the absence of progression-aware models represents a data infrastructure problem rather than a modeling failure [36,37].

Variability in Imaging Acquisition and Clinical Context

Plain radiographs are subject to substantial variability in patient positioning, beam angle, and image quality across institutions and even within the same patient over time. While experienced clinicians implicitly account for these factors, AI models are sensitive to such inconsistencies unless explicitly trained to accommodate them [19,39]. This variability poses two related challenges. First, it reduces the generalizability of models trained on single-center data. Second, it complicates external validation, as the performance may degrade when models are applied to radiographs obtained under different acquisition protocols. Without standardized imaging practices or robust normalization strategies, AI outputs risk being inconsistent across clinical settings [19,39]. Notably, several of these sources of variability are at least partly controllable at the point of acquisition today. Specifically, standardized anteroposterior and axillary projection protocols, the use of calibration markers to support scale normalization, and a consistent source-to-image distance can each reduce inter-study variability before any model-side processing is performed, representing immediately actionable steps for departments adopting AI-based surveillance.

Absence of Clinically Validated Thresholds and Actionability

Finally, even when AI models generate precise quantitative outputs, their clinical utility remains limited without validated thresholds linked to actionable decisions. For example, identifying a small increase in notching volume or micromotion does not inherently inform whether closer surveillance, advanced imaging, or surgical intervention is warranted [40,41].

Currently, most AI studies report technical performance metrics rather than outcome-linked clinical thresholds. This disconnect underscores the need for prospective studies that integrate AI-derived measurements with patient outcomes, enabling translation from quantitative analysis to decision support [41-43]. In aggregate, the technical and clinical gaps in AI development for RTSA radiographic analysis reflect a misalignment between algorithmic capabilities and the realities of orthopedic imaging data and clinical decision-making. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated efforts in data curation, longitudinal study design, and clinical validation, rather than incremental improvements in model architecture alone [19,34].

ROADMAP FOR PRACTICAL CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION

Redefining Labeling: from Static Categories to Dynamic Vectors

To overcome the current limitations of AI and translate its potential into tangible clinical value, a comprehensive approach is required that extends beyond algorithm development to include data design, result interpretation, and clinical workflow integration (Fig. 2). The performance of an AI model is dictated by the quality of its training data, particularly the labeling methodology [44].

Many current datasets rely on simple binary classifications such as “loosening present/absent,” which fail to capture the gradual and progressive nature of mechanical failure after RTSA. Such static labeling schemes inherently limit an algorithm’s ability to learn clinically meaningful trajectories of change. Future data-labeling strategies must therefore shift from static categories toward continuous, time-aware vectors that explicitly encode longitudinal progression [37,38].

For example, glenosphere orientation should be recorded not only as a static value (e.g., “5° superior tilt”), but also as a progression vector (e.g., “+3° over six months,” Δ°). Baseplate micromotion should be quantified as displacement in millimeters along defined axes (Δmm), rather than as a binary judgment. Similarly, scapular notching may be best represented using a hybrid approach that combines the traditional Sirveaux grade with quantitative measures such as erosion depth (mm) or area (cm²), enabling both clinical familiarity and numerical precision [45].

In more concrete terms, consider a hypothetical patient whose glenosphere tilt is unchanged between the 1- and 2-year postoperative radiographs, such that a conventional static reading records “no interval change.” A progression-aware analysis might nonetheless reveal that the scapular notching area increased from approximately 0.8 to 1.2 cm² over the same interval (Fig. 3). Although this value remains within a single Sirveaux grade, the implied progression velocity could reasonably prompt a shortened surveillance interval or advanced imaging rather than routine annual follow-up. It is precisely this trajectory information—invisible to single-timepoint classification—that dynamic, vector-based labeling is designed to capture.

Addressing Data Constraints through Advanced Learning Strategies

The structural data limitations described earlier—single-center bias, rare-event class imbalance, and small labeled datasets—have motivated technical strategies that complement, rather than replace, continued data collection (Fig. 4). Federated and multi-institutional learning allows models to be trained across institutions without centralizing patient images, directly mitigating single-center bias while preserving privacy. Synthetic data augmentation using generative adversarial networks or diffusion models can enrich the representation of rare failure patterns, partially offsetting the class imbalance intrinsic to low-prevalence complications. Furthermore, transfer learning, self-supervised pretraining, and emerging foundation-model approaches are well suited to the small labeled-set regime typical of RTSA imaging, enabling models pretrained on large general or musculoskeletal datasets to be adopted with limited shoulder-specific annotation. Together, these paradigms constitute the natural technical complement to the data-infrastructure investments emphasized throughout this review.

Building Trust: Explainability and Uncertainty Quantification

For clinicians to trust and utilize AI-generated results in their decision-making, the “black box” problem must be addressed [40,42]. AI outputs must be presented in a clinically interpretable format. For example, vector overlays illustrating angular changes, heatmaps visualizing scapular contact zones, or trend graphs showing changes over time can provide clinicians with intuitive insights.

Furthermore, for safe clinical application, uncertainty quantification is essential. An AI model must be able to self-assess its confidence in its analysis and signal when it encounters an ambiguous case that requires further review [43]. This process would serve as a critical safety mechanism, controlling for the possibility of AI error and ensuring that the final decision-making authority remains with the clinician.

Final Hurdles: Workflow Integration and Regulatory Barriers

Even technically robust and interpretable AI models offer limited clinical value if they cannot be seamlessly integrated into routine practice. Effective deployment requires interoperability with existing Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) as well as electronic health records, enabling real-time access to AI analyses within established clinical workflows [39,45,46].

Beyond technical integration, medical–legal and regulatory considerations represent significant barriers to adoption. Presently, no AI system designed specifically to analyze RTSA-related radiographic metrics has received regulatory approval from agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [47]. For AI-derived analyses to influence high-stakes clinical decisions, such as revision surgery, they must be validated through prospective studies demonstrating a measurable impact on patient outcomes. Ultimately, the clinical utility of AI-generated quantitative data depends on its linkage to validated, actionable thresholds. Identifying a numerical change is insufficient unless it informs a specific clinical response, such as altered surveillance intervals, advanced imaging, or surgical intervention [41]. Achieving this level of integration will require a reciprocal relationship between AI development and clinical research, in which evolving technology informs guideline refinement, and clinical practice in turn defines meaningful targets for AI systems.

The path toward practical clinical implementation of AI in RTSA radiographic surveillance lies not in increasingly complex algorithms, but in more effectively aligned data structures, transparent output design, and rigorous clinical validation. By redefining labeling strategies, prioritizing explainability and uncertainty awareness, and addressing workflow and regulatory constraints, AI tools may evolve from experimental measurement aids into reliable partners in longitudinal patient management [19,34,39].

CONCLUSION: TOWARD AN ERA OF AUGMENTED RADIOGRAPHIC SURVEILLANCE

AI applied to postoperative RTSA radiographs is among the most promising developments in contemporary orthopedic imaging. As this review has outlined, AI has already achieved clinically meaningful, reproducible accuracy in foundational tasks, such as automated component alignment measurement and implant identification, which closely align with existing clinical workflows [13,15,20]. AI remains limited, however, in its ability to reliably detect the most clinically consequential failure modes, including screw loosening, liner dissociation, and progressive scapular notching. This gap reflects not an inherent shortcoming of current algorithms but an absence of the high-quality, shoulder-specific longitudinal datasets required to model rare, subtle, and progressive complications, as summarized in the proposed clinical readiness map (Table 2) [34,36].

In the short term, the most realistic clinical impact lies in measurement standardization, reduction of interobserver variability, and improved efficiency in postoperative assessment and surgical planning [17,45]. The expectations that AI will imminently replace clinical interpretation or serve as a standalone diagnostic tool for complication detection are not currently supported by available evidence. In the longer term, the value of AI will depend on its evolution from static measurement tools to progression-aware, predictive systems, which will require coordinated investment in longitudinal, multi-institutional data, dynamic labeling strategies that encode change over time, and prospective validation linking AI-derived metrics to patient outcomes [37,38,45].

Ultimately, successful integration of AI into RTSA care will depend not on algorithmic precision alone but on a broader system of regulatory trust, clinician engagement, and seamless workflow integration. Under these conditions, AI is best positioned not to replace clinical expertise but to augment it—improving consistency and enabling earlier recognition of failure patterns in shoulder arthroplasty care [19,34,39].
