Venlafaxine for the Prevention of Postherpetic Neuralgia in Patients with Acute Herpes Zoster: Protocol for a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Yan Xing, Minying Liu, Fei Ren, Xiaobo Feng, Chunmei Zhao, Fang Luo, Dasheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostherpetic neuralgia (PHN) affects approximately 30% of individuals with herpes zoster (HZ), with approximately 80% of cases occurring in individuals aged 50 years or older. PHN represents a major clinical challenge due to its chronicity and impact on quality of life. Current treatments for acute HZ pain have limited efficacy in preventing PHN, highlighting the need for effective preventive strategies targeting early pathophysiological mechanisms.Study DesignMulticenter, prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded-endpoint controlled trial.Methods and AnalysisA total of 832 patients with acute HZ within 30 days of rash onset and moderate to severe pain (NRS ≥4) will be randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either conventional therapy alone or venlafaxine plus conventional therapy. Venlafaxine will be initiated at 75 mg once daily and titrated based on pain response and tolerability. If pain persists, the dose will be increased to 150 mg once daily during the second week and subsequently to a maximum of 225 mg once daily. Treatment will continue until pain resolves or until 90 days after rash onset. After assessment of the primary endpoint at day 90, participants who develop PHN will receive guideline-based treatment according to their clinical condition. All participants will receive standardized analgesic management following the World Health Organization pain ladder. The study duration will be 52 weeks. The primary outcome is the incidence of PHN at 90 days after rash onset, defined as clinically meaningful persistent pain in the affected dermatome with an average NRS score ≥3. Secondary outcomes include pain intensity, proportion of patients achieving pain reductions, analgesic consumption, Herpes Zoster Severity of Illness Index, 12‑item Short‑Form Health Survey, Medical Outcomes Study Sleep Scale, Neuropathic Pain Scale, and adverse events. Participants and treating physicians will be aware of the assigned treatment, whereas outcome assessors will remain blinded to group allocation.Ethics and DisseminationThis study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (KY2025-369-02). The trial will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentation at scientific conferences.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07361796. Registered on January 2026 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07361796?term=NCT07361796).

Introduction

Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), the most prevalent complication of herpes zoster (HZ), is typified by a persistent pain that lasts for at least 90 days and follows a dermatomal distribution.1 The pain is often depicted as a persistent burning sensation or an electric shock-like pain, frequently accompanied by allodynia or hyperalgesia. These symptoms result from nerve damage induced by the inflammatory response triggered by viral replication within the affected nerve.1 Affecting approximately 30% of individuals with HZ, PHN predominantly affects older populations, with 80% of cases occurring in individuals aged 50 years or older, and its prevalence increases markedly with advancing age.2 Beyond advanced age, established risk factors for PHN include severe prodrome symptoms, extensive rash, intense acute zoster pain, ophthalmic zoster involvement, immunosuppression, and chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus and systematic lupus erythematosus.3,4 PHN imposes a considerable clinical and socioeconomic burden, contributing to sleep disturbances, impaired physical function, reduced quality of life, and increased rates of depression and anxiety.5

Preventive and pre-emptive management has emerged as a promising approach to reduce the incidence of PHN.6 Vaccination against varicella-zoster virus is the cornerstone of primary prevention and has been shown to reduce the risks of both HZ and PHN in adults aged 50 years and older.7,8 However, vaccination does not address patients who have already developed HZ. In these individuals, antiviral agents remain the standard of care.9 Antiviral agents can reduce the severity and duration of acute pain, accelerate cutaneous healing, and potentially limit sensory nerve injury by inhibiting viral replication.10 Nevertheless, current evidence suggests that antivirals alone do not substantially reduce the risk of PHN.11 Likewise, systemic corticosteroids may alleviate acute HZ pain and improve quality of life, particularly in patients with severe pain or site-specific complications such as neurologic or ophthalmologic involvement.12,13 However, their effect on lesion healing and PHN prevention remains unclear.14,15 Opioids, while effective for severe pain management, also fail to prevent PHN.16 Gabapentinoids and other anticonvulsants are effective for PHN, but their relatively slow onset and insufficient evidence in the acute HZ setting limit their value as preventive agents for PHN.17 Collectively, these findings underscore a critical gap in current therapeutic strategies. Beyond the vaccine, there is little evidence for interventions that effectively reduce the incidence of PHN following varicella-zoster virus reactivation.

Interventional pain management strategies have also attracted increasing interest during the HZ. Evidence from randomized trials and a systematic review suggests that repeated or continuous nerve blocks, including epidural and paravertebral techniques, may shorten the duration of acute zoster related pain and reduce the incidence of PHN, whereas the preventive effect of a single epidural injection appears less consistent.18–20 Early stellate ganglion block has been associated with reduced acute pain and a lower incidence of PHN in patients with craniofacial HZ.21 A recent case report also described sustained pain resolution without subsequent PHN following stellate ganglion blockade, although confirmatory studies are required.22 Pulsed radiofrequency targeting the affected sensory ganglia has likewise demonstrated analgesic effects and possible preventive benefits, but the available evidence is derived largely from small or observational studies.23 Although these interventional approaches are promising, the limited and heterogeneous evidence has not yet established their role in routine PHN prevention. This uncertainty underscores the need for broadly applicable preventive strategies targeting the early pathophysiological mechanisms that drive the transition from acute HZ pain to PHN.

The transition from HZ to PHN is believed to be driven by neural sensitization, including peripheral and central sensitization, as well as deafferentation of all fiber classes during the acute phase of HZ.24 Clinical manifestations such as thermal and vibratory hypesthesia, pressure hyperalgesia, and increased dynamic mechanical allodynia reflect enhanced spinal synaptic strength and activation of nociceptive pathways.25,26 These mechanisms provide a rationale for early pharmacologic interventions aimed at modulating neural sensitization and preventing the development of PHN.

Amitriptyline, a conventional antidepressant, has provided early proof of concept for preventive treatment. In a placebo-controlled trial, low-dose amitriptyline initiated soon after HZ diagnosis and continued for 90 days significantly reduced pain prevalence at 6 months after rash onset, although no significant benefit was observed at 3 months.27 However, its clinical use is often limited by poor tolerability, particularly in older adults, due to anticholinergic effects (eg, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention), sedation, and potential cardiovascular risks such as orthostatic hypotension and conduction abnormalities.28 More recently, our multicenter, randomized, open-label, blinded-endpoint trial showed that duloxetine, a newly antidepressant that enhances descending pain inhibition through increased serotonin and norepinephrine availability, reduced pain severity at week 4, 8, and 12 when initiated within 72 hours of HZ reactivation.29 Nevertheless, a small proportion of patients discontinued treatment early because of intolerable adverse events (AEs), highlighting the need to consider tolerability when selecting preventive therapy. Venlafaxine, another SNRI, shares a similar mechanism of action with duloxetine but may offer superior safety profile. Venlafaxine acts predominantly on serotonin reuptake at lower doses and exerting dual serotonin-norepinephrine effects at higher doses. Evidence from patients with major depressive disorder suggests that venlafaxine may be associated with fewer discontinuations due to AEs than duloxetine, although the applicability of this finding to patients with acute HZ remains uncertain.30 Taken together with the findings for duloxetine in acute HZ, these pharmacological and tolerability considerations provide a rationale for evaluating venlafaxine for the prevention of PHN. However, to date, no published studies have specifically evaluated venlafaxine in this setting.

Therefore, we hypothesize that venlafaxine initiated within 30 days of rash onset may reduce the incidence of PHN without significantly increasing AEs. To rigorously evaluate the effect of venlafaxine premedication for PHN in acute HZ, a prospective, randomized, multi-center study is warranted.

Methods

Study Design

This study is a multicenter, prospective, randomized, open‑label, blinded‑endpoint (PROBE) trial designed to evaluate whether venlafaxine, when added to conventional therapy within acute phase of HZ rash onset reduces the incidence of PHN at 90 days after rash onset. Participants will be recruited from Department of Pain Management, Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, and Jilin Province People’s Hospital. The trial has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University following the criteria required by the Declaration of Helsinki, and has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07361796) before enrollment begins. Recruitment and Informed Consent

Experienced attending physicians in the Department of Pain Management will identify and enroll eligible patients with acute HZ. Candidates will receive comprehensive information covering the study aim, interventions, benefits, possible risks, and risk responses. A consideration period of at least one hour will be granted to decide whether to participate. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Importantly, participants will be explicitly informed of their right to withdraw at any time without prejudice to their ongoing clinical care.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the venlafaxine arm or the control arm using a computer‑generated blocked randomization schedule (block size = 4 or 6) stratified by site. Allocation sequences will be prepared by an independent statistician and concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The designated envelope will be opened only after eligibility confirmation and consent.

The study is an open-label trial, so neither the researchers nor the patients will be blinded. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group assignments. And independent of treatment procedures, and outcomes will primarily from standardized patient-reported measures and daily pain diaries. Assessors will be instructed to avoid discussing treatment allocation with participants. The open‑label design ensures pragmatic titration of venlafaxine while the blinded assessment minimizes detection bias.

Interventions

Treatment Before 90 Days After Rash Onset

Participants will record daily pain in pain diaries for the duration of the trial. All participants will receive conventional therapy for HZ, consisting of antiviral agents when indicated and concomitant analgesic management. This approach is consistent with previous trials evaluating treatments for the prevention of PHN.19,20,29 Antiviral therapy will be initiated as early as possible, preferably within 72 hours of rash onset, according to predefined indication criteria.31 For participants who have not received antiviral therapy before enrollment and remain eligible for treatment, oral valacyclovir 1000 mg three times daily for 7 days will be the preferred regimen. If valacyclovir is considered unsuitable, famciclovir 500 mg three times daily or acyclovir 800 mg five times daily may be administered for 7 days, and the reason for using an alternative agent will be documented. Participants who have already initiated or completed antiviral therapy before enrollment will be eligible only if the regimen meets the protocol requirements for the antiviral agent, dose, and treatment duration. Those receiving an ongoing protocol compliant regimen will continue the original treatment until completion. Antiviral doses will be adjusted according to renal function. The antiviral agent, dose, timing of initiation relative to rash onset, and treatment duration will be documented. Concomitant analgesic treatment will be administered according to a standardized stepwise protocol based on the three-step World Health Organization pain ladder and guided by pain severity assessed using the NRS.32 For mild pain (NRS ≤3), non-opioid analgesics, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, will be used as first-line therapy. For moderate pain (NRS 4–6), or when pain remains inadequately controlled despite optimized non-opioid treatment, a weak opioid in combination with a non-opioid analgesic will be permitted. For severe pain (NRS ≥7), or when pain persists despite the previous step, treatment may be escalated to a strong opioid combined with non-opioid analgesics. Opioid escalation will be allowed only when prespecified criteria are met, including persistent inadequate analgesia despite the preceding treatment step or clinically significant breakthrough pain. Rescue medication will be limited to protocol-specified short-acting analgesics and will be used only for breakthrough pain. As rescue analgesia, patients may take 1000 mg acetaminophen for mild pain, with a maximum daily dose of 4000 mg. For more severe pain, oxycodone and acetaminophen tablets (containing 5 mg oxycodone hydrochloride and 325 mg paracetamol per tablet) are permitted up to four times daily. Throughout the trial, procedural therapies, such as nerve blocks or pulsed radiofrequency, are forbidden. However, any such treatment received because of inadequately controlled pain or personal preference will be documented as a protocol deviation.

The control group will receive conventional therapy alone. Participants in the venlafaxine group will receive venlafaxine (Venlafaxine Hydrochloride Sustained-Release Capsules, Pfizer, Co Kildare, Ireland) in addition to conventional treatment. Venlafaxine will be orally initiated at 75 mg once daily after breakfast. If the patient’s pain score reaches 0 within the first week, venlafaxine will be discontinued. Otherwise, the dose will be increased to 150 mg once daily during the second week. If the pain score reaches 0 within the second week, venlafaxine will be tapered to 75 mg once daily for 1 week and then discontinued. If pain persists, the dose will be further increased to 225 mg once daily and maintained until a pain score of 0 is achieved or until 90 days after rash onset. If pain resolves before day 90, venlafaxine will be gradually tapered and discontinued according to the current dose. For patients who developed intolerable AEs, the dose will be adjusted to the last tolerable level. If AEs are intolerable even at the initial dose, the medication will be discontinued, and the participant will be withdrawn from the trial.

Treatment at and After 90 Days Following Rash Onset

After 90 days from rash onset, treatment will be standardized across both groups. Participants who develop persistent pain consistent with PHN will receive guideline-based management, including gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and topical agents such as lidocaine or capsaicin, as clinically indicated.33 All PHN-related treatments and any dose adjustments will be systematically recorded.

Follow-Up

The study schedule includes nine visits over 52 weeks, including screening on day 0, follow-up at weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 52, and a separate assessment at 90 days after rash onset. Visits at weeks 1 and 2 and 90 days after rash onset will be conducted by telephone, whereas the remaining visits will be conducted in person. Unblinded research staff will manage dose titration, review participants’ pain and medication diaries, and document adherence to the prescribed regimen, including treatment interruptions, dose modifications, discontinuation, and the reasons for these events. Blinded outcome assessors, who will have no access to allocation or treatment records, will conduct the scheduled efficacy and safety assessments, including the administration of questionnaires and scales. The primary endpoint, the incidence of PHN, will be assessed by a blinded outcome assessor during the telephone visit at 90 days after rash onset. This visit will be scheduled according to the date of rash onset rather than the date of randomization. Reasons for withdrawal of consent will be recorded, and no further data will be collected after complete withdrawal of consent.

Data Management and Quality Control

All study data are recorded by trained investigators using standardized case report forms developed specifically for this protocol. To ensure data accuracy and integrity, information is entered into a centralized electronic database (EpiData Manager, Version 4.6.0.6; EpiData Association, Denmark) using an independent double-data entry method. Any discrepancies between the two entries are resolved by verification against source documents.

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee provides oversight of the trial. The committee meets twice yearly to review cumulative safety data and assess trial validity. Based on these reviews, the committee makes recommendations to the Steering Committee regarding continuation, modification, or early termination of the study.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in developing the research question, designing the study, or selecting outcome measures. Participants are recruited through physician referrals at participating multicenter sites.

After study completion, findings will be disseminated through presentations at international conferences and publications in peer-reviewed journals. A plain-language summary of the results will also be made available to participants upon request.

PHN will be defined as persistent pain in the affected dermatome at 90 days after rash onset that is clinically meaningful, operationalized as an average pain score ≥ 3 on the NRS.1 Rash onset is defined as the date on which the characteristic HZ rash was first noticed by the patient or caregiver.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size is calculated based on the primary endpoint, the incidence of PHN at 90 days after rash onset using PASS 11.0 software (NSCC, LLC, USA). Recently, our multicenter, randomized, open-label, blinded-endpoint trial evaluated duloxetine initiated within 72 hours of HZ reactivation and found that, when clinically meaningful persistent pain at week 12 was defined as a VAS score ≥3, the incidence was 17.6% in the duloxetine group and 28.3% in the control group (risk ratio 0.6, 95% confidence interval 0.5–0.8, P = 0.001).29 Based on this finding, we hypothesized that a PHN incidence of 28.3% in the control group and 18.3% in the venlafaxine group in our study, with a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 90% power, 374 participants are required per group. Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, the final target sample size is 416 participants per group (832 in total).

All statistical analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (V27.0) and R software (V4.4.1) where appropriate. Analyses will follow the intention‑to‑treat principle primarily, including all randomized patients. A per-protocol analysis may also be performed as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

Continuous variables will be summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and compared between groups using Student’s t‑test or the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test.

The primary outcome, PHN incidence at 90 days after rash onset, will be compared between groups using the chi-square test and further analyzed with multivariable logistic regression to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals after adjustment for prespecified covariates, including age, sex, time from rash onset to enrollment, study site, and baseline pain intensity. Continuous secondary outcomes will be analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for treatment group, time, and their interaction and random intercepts for participants. Binary secondary outcomes will be analyzed using logistic regression. Missing primary-outcome data will be handled using multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption, with sensitivity analyses based on complete cases and conservative imputation strategies. Furthermore, pre-specified subgroup analyses of the primary outcome will be performed based on age (<65 vs ≥65 years), sex, time from rash onset to enrollment, and baseline pain intensity. Relevant antiviral treatment characteristics will also be considered in these analyses. A P-value < 0.05 for the interaction term will be considered statistically significant to suggest a subgroup effect. These subgroup analyses will be considered exploratory.

All tests will be two sided, and P < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this context, the present trial is designed to evaluate whether early venlafaxine treatment can reduce the risk of clinically meaningful PHN following HZ reactivation. As a prevention study, its primary endpoint is the incidence of PHN at 90 days after rash onset. The endpoint is particularly relevant because it reflects the clinically important transition from acute HZ pain to persistent neuropathic pain, which remains the primary therapeutic concern in patients with acute HZ.

Pain occurring within 30 days after rash onset is commonly classified as acute HZ-related pain.31 Although previous PHN prevention trials generally initiated treatment within the first several days or 2 weeks after rash onset,19–21,29 we adopted a pragmatic 30-day inclusion window to encompass the broader spectrum of acute-phase patients encountered in routine pain clinic practice, including those referred later in the disease course. Because this broader window may introduce heterogeneity in treatment response, the time from rash onset to enrollment will be examined as a potential modifier of the effect of venlafaxine in prespecified exploratory analyses.

Previous studies have often defined PHN as the presence of any pain at 12 weeks or later, a highly sensitive approach that may overestimate the burden of persistent pain by including patients with minimal or clinically insignificant symptoms.29,39 In the PROCESS trial, PHN was defined as the presence of any pain at week 12 based on visual analogue scale (VAS), under which no significant between-group difference was observed. However, a significant reduction in persistent pain emerged when a more clinically meaningful threshold (VAS ≥3) was applied.29 Evidence from natural-history and assessment studies further suggests that defining PHN as any residual pain may overestimate clinically relevant symptoms, whereas thresholds around 30/100 on a VAS may better capture persistent pain.39,40 More recent studies have defined PHN as persistent pain with an average VAS, Zoster Brief Pain Inventory score, or NRS ≥ 3 at least 90 days after rash onset.35,41–43 This definition was selected to enhance clinical relevance and improve the interpretability of preventive efficacy. Accordingly, PHN in this trial will be defined as clinically meaningful persistent pain in the affected dermatome at 90 days after rash onset, operationalized as an average NRS score ≥ 3.

The interpretation of the findings will depend on the clinical effects observed. A reduction in PHN with acceptable tolerability would be consistent with, but would not prove, the hypothesis that enhancing descending pain inhibition during acute HZ may limit pain chronification. If venlafaxine relieves acute pain without reducing PHN, this would suggest that symptomatic analgesia alone may be insufficient to prevent the transition to chronic pain. Conversely, limited efficacy or poor tolerability would not support its routine use for acute HZ. Because neural sensitization is not directly assessed in this trial, any mechanistic interpretation should remain cautious.

The PROBE design was chosen to balance methodological rigor with clinical pragmatism. While a double-blind design is the gold standard, it can be restrictive in real-world titration scenarios. The PROBE approach mitigates potential assessment bias by ensuring that investigators responsible for outcome measurements remain blinded to group allocation, thereby strengthening the internal validity of the trial.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the open-label nature of the intervention may introduce performance or ascertainment bias, although the blinded-endpoint assessment is intended to counter this bias. Any between group differences in analgesic consumption will be considered when interpreting the findings. Although the permitted antiviral regimens use protocol specified doses and treatment durations, differences in the antiviral agent and timing of treatment initiation before enrollment may introduce residual treatment heterogeneity. Second, because the study will enroll patients with onset of HZ rash less than 30 days, the findings may not be generalizable to patients presenting in subacute HZ. Third, patients with advanced chronic kidney disease are excluded, which may further limit the external applicability of the results to broader HZ populations. Finally, the exclusion of previously vaccinated individuals may limit the generalizability of the findings to patients who develop breakthrough HZ after vaccination.

Further studies would be needed to confirm the effect, determine the optimal treatment window and dosing strategy, evaluate tolerability in broader patient populations, and clarify how venlafaxine could be integrated with existing antiviral and analgesic treatment. The present trial may therefore provide evidence to guide subsequent confirmatory studies and future evaluation of venlafaxine as an adjunctive option for patients with acute HZ.
