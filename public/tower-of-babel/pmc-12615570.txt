The association of anticoagulant use and hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke among older adults aged 75 or older: A propensity score-matched study
Authors: Seigo Mitsutake, Koki Ono, Masahiro Akishita, Takumi Hirata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation-based studies on the association between anticoagulant use and the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke in older adults are limited. We examined the association between anticoagulant prescription and hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke and whether this association varies between warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).MethodsThis propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study used medical insurance claim data from Hokkaido, Japan. Older adults aged ≥ 75 years receiving medical care between April 2016 and March 2017 (baseline period) were included. The exposure variable was prescription of anticoagulants during the baseline period and outcome variable was hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke between April 2017 and March 2020. A one-to-one matched design adjusted for covariates (age, sex, copayment rate, comorbidities, annual health check-up, and care needs certification) compared the incidence of hospitalization between patients prescribed and those not prescribed anticoagulants.ResultsAmong 717,097 patients, 66,916 (9.3%) were prescribed anticoagulants. After propensity score matching, the incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke was higher in patients prescribed anticoagulants (383.2 per million person-month) than in those who were not prescribed anticoagulants (252.2 per million person-month) (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.64; 95% Confidence interval [CI], 1.39–1.93). Moreover, among patients prescribed one anticoagulant (n = 61,556), after matching, the incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke was significantly higher in patients prescribed warfarin than in those prescribed DOACs (HR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.39–2.01).ConclusionsThe findings of this study highlight the need for careful consideration when prescribing anticoagulants, particularly warfarin, to older adults to minimize the risk of hemorrhagic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03234-x.Keywords: Anticoagulants, Bleeding, Older adults, Population-based study, Propensity score matching

Background

The number of adults aged 80 years or older is expected to triple globally between 2020 and 2050 [1]. In older adults, anticoagulants are commonly prescribed to reduce the risk of thrombosis and ischemic stroke (IS) [2–4]. However, the use of oral anticoagulants increases the risk of major bleeding, especially in adults aged 80 years or older [5, 6]. Hemorrhagic stroke is a major bleeding event associated with anticoagulant use that leads to high mortality rates and severe functional impairment among older adults [7, 8]. In aging societies, the use of anticoagulants requires careful consideration because of potential adverse events.

Several criteria and guidelines regarding Potentially Inappropriate Medications (PIMs) indicate an association between anticoagulant use and the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke [9–11]. Although a guideline of medical treatment and safety for older adults from the Japan Geriatric Society includes anticoagulants as PIMs [11], studies examining the risk of anticoagulants use on the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke through the population-based study of older adults aged ≥ 75 years in Japan are limited. While several prior studies investigated the association between anticoagulants and the inc

idence of bleeding using registry data from multi-medical institutes, including older adults aged ≥ 75 years [12, 13], few Japanese population-based studies have examined this association among older adults aged ≥ 75 years. As the effect of anticoagulant use on the incidence of bleeding varies depending on race and age, evidence of the association of anticoagulants with hemorrhagic stroke through a Japanese population-based study among older adults aged ≥ 75 years would be crucial for guidelines on PIMs in Japan.

The Beers Criteria and STOPP/START guidelines recommend avoiding the use of warfarin as an initial therapy for the treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF) or venous thromboembolism (VTE), except in specific situations such as long-term warfarin use, because warfarin use is associated with a higher risk of bleeding [9, 10]. Several meta-analyses have shown that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) reduce the risk of hemorrhagic stroke compared to warfarin among patients with AF or VTE [8, 14, 15]. Moreover, a guideline of the Japan Geriatric Society, updated in 2025, and Japanese study using clinical-based registry data of patients with AF indicated the higher risk of warfarin use for bleeding than DOACs [11, 16]. Although population-based data among patients who underwent tooth extraction have compared the bleeding risk between warfarin and DOACs use [17], there is no population-based studies among older adults aged ≥ 75 years in Japan. Therefore, comparing the risk of hemorrhagic stroke between warfarin and DOACs can inform guideline for medical treatment and ensure safe use in older adults.

Aims

The current study examined the association between anticoagulant prescriptions and hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke among all older adults aged ≥ 75 years in Hokkaido, Japan. Additionally, we compared the association between hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke and warfarin and DOACs prescriptions.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study linked large-scale anonymized medical claims, registry, and annual health checkup data from April 2016 to March 2020 in the Hokkaido Prefecture obtained from the Hokkaido Association of Medical Care for Senior Citizens (MCSO). Hokkaido is the northernmost of Japan’s four main islands. The population spread over an area of 83,424 km2, consisted of 5,376,211 residents in 2016, with approximately 14.1% aged ≥ 75 years [18]. In Japan, all citizens are mandated to enroll in an MCSO on their 75th birthday [19]. In addition, adults aged between 65 and 74 years who are certified as having specific disabilities, such as those resulting from a stroke, are also eligible for enrollment [19]. Several prior studies used the database of MCSO from Hokkaido and Tokyo to assess the associations between hypnotics use and mortality and hip fracture admission. In addition, these studies investigated the effect of dental visits on occurrence of acute hospitalization for systemic diseases and described the common 3-way disease patterns in adults aged ≥ 75 years [20–23].

Medical claims data included patient-level sociodemographic characteristics, medical and dental care treatments, medical facilities used, prescribed drugs, and diagnoses made during clinical encounters. Prescribed drugs were recorded using a 9-digit code for which prices were listed [24]. Diagnoses were recorded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Moreover, the registry data included patient-level sociodemographic data, day of birth and death or migration out of Hokkaido, and day and level of care needs certification from the long-term care insurance (LTCI) system in Hokkaido. The Japanese LTCI is a type of mandatory public social insurance that pays for LTCI services and differs from the medical insurance system [25]. All citizens aged 40 and older pay LTCI premiums, and those aged 65 or older (and those aged 40–64 with aging-related diseases, such as terminal cancer) can be certified for LTCI service needs. In addition, we linked annual health checkup data from the fiscal year (FY) 2016 (i.e., April 1, 2016, to March 31, 2017) to assess whether patients had undergone an annual health checkup.

Study patients

Older adults who registered as beneficiaries of the MCSO in Hokkaido and received medical care at any medical facility between April 2016 and March 2017 (the baseline period) were identified. Exclusion criteria during the baseline period included adults aged 74 years or less, those who lost eligibility for the MCSO in Hokkaido (e.g., death or moved out of the study area), and those who were hospitalized for hemorrhagic stroke.

Exposure variable

The prescription of anticoagulants was identified using the National Health Insurance (NHI) drug code linked with a 9-digit drug code in the claims data through the medical drug master from the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare [26]. According to previous studies and guidelines for medical treatment and safety in older adults from the Japan Geriatric Society, five anticoagulants were identified during the baseline period: warfarin, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban (Supplemental Table 1) [4, 11, 15, 16, 27]. Additionally, we categorized warfarin prescriptions separately from other drugs as DOACs.

Outcome measure

The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of hospitalization due to hemorrhagic stroke between April 2017 and March 2020 (the follow-up period), which was identified using the primary diagnosis of hemorrhagic stroke (ICD-10: I60, I61 and I62) recorded in the medical claim data [27, 28].

Covariates

We collected data on the following characteristics: sex, age, number of Charlson comorbidities, prevalence of hypertension, IS or transient ischemic attack (TIA), VTE, AF, annual health checkups, and care needs certification. Covariates were identified in the literature and were available in the database of the current study [5, 27]. Information on patient sex (male and female) and age was obtained from the registry data. Charlson comorbidities were identified using diagnoses from the baseline period from medical claim data [29]. The number of Charlson comorbidities was categorized as nil, 1, and ≥ 2 comorbidities [29]. Additionally, four comorbidities recognized in the literature as covariates for the prescription of anticoagulants and occurrence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke were also individually identified. These four were: hypertension (ICD-10: I10, I11, I12, I13, and I15), IS/TIA (ICD-10: I63, I64, G45(except G45.4), H34.1, H34.2), VTE (ICD-10: I80.1, I80.2, I80.3, I80.8, I80.9, I82.8, I82.9, I26.0, I26.9), and AF (ICD-10: I48) [23, 27, 30]. Cerebrovascular disease identification was excluded from the number of Charlson comorbidities to avoid collinearity between Charlson comorbidities and IS/TIA. We used health checkups in FY 2016, with health checkup data as a covariate of health behavior [20]. The MCSO recommends that beneficiaries undergo annual health check-ups designed to identify individuals at high risk of developing and worsening lifestyle-related diseases. Additionally, care needs certification during the baseline period was identified from the registry data.

Statistical analysis

Patients with and without anticoagulant prescriptions were matched 1:1 on propensity score (PS) to adjust for covariates (sex, age, number of Charlson comorbidities, prevalence of hypertension, IS/TIA, VTE, annual health checkups, and care needs certification). AF was excluded from the PS model because its near-deterministic relation with anticoagulant prescriptions can inflate PS variance, distort covariate balance, and reduce statistical efficiency [31]. AF was instead included as a covariate in the Cox proportional hazards model to adjust for residual confounding after matching. Moreover, using sensitivity analyses stratified by patient with and without AF, we performed separate 1:1 PS matching patients with and without anticoagulant prescriptions, followed by Cox proportional hazards models comparing anticoagulant users with non-users for the risk of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke. PS was set as the probability that a patient would be prescribed anticoagulants during the baseline period and was estimated using a logistic regression model with or without anticoagulant prescription as the dependent variable and all covariates as independent variables. Moreover, among patients who were prescribed one anticoagulant, we compared the risk of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke between patients receiving warfarin and those administered DOACs using 1:1 PS matching to adjust for all covariates (sex, age, number of Charlson comorbidities, prevalence of hypertension, IS/TIA, VTE, AF, annual health checkups, and care needs certification). The C-statistic was calculated to evaluate the model’s goodness of fit (a C-statistic of 0.5 indicates no predictive ability beyond random chance, whereas a C-statistic of 1.0 indicates perfect predictive power). PS matching was performed without replacement using a caliper width set at 20% of the standard deviation [20, 32]. After matching, patient characteristics were compared between the two cohorts using absolute standardized differences (ASD). An ASD >0.1 indicated a significant imbalance in a covariate based on the suggestion of a prior study [33]. Survival analysis was conducted using the log-rank test and Cox proportional hazards model to estimate the effects of anticoagulant prescriptions on hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke during the follow-up period. We generated Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the PS-matched cohort to describe the occurrence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals before and after PS matching. Patients who died or moved out of the study area during the follow-up period were excluded. The hazard ratio (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

In addition, to prevent immortal time bias, we performed Cox proportional hazards models with first anticoagulant prescription as a time-dependent covariate. This covariate was adjusted for all covariates to assess the association between anticoagulant prescriptions and hospitalization among patients who were not prescribed anticoagulants during baseline period as sensitivity analyses [34]. Additionally, we conducted a 1:1 PS matched analysis among patients without baseline anticoagulant use, defining time zero as the month of the first warfarin or DOAC prescription in treated patients; we then estimated hazard ratios for hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke during follow-up. PS matching, absolute standardized difference calculations and the Schoenfeld residual test were performed using Stata software (version 17.0; Stata Corp LLC, TX, USA). All other analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA)[35].

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology Research Ethics Committee (No.2020-44). Because all data were fully anonymized before being provided to the authors, the Ethics Committee granted a waiver of informed consent. This study was conducted following the Japanese government’s research ethics guidelines for medical and health research involving human participants.

Results

A total of 798,877 candidates who received medical care at any medical facility at baseline were identified (Fig. 1). We excluded 33,044 patients aged 74 or younger, 46,341 patients who were no longer eligible for MCSO in the study area, and 2,395 patients who were hospitalized for hemorrhagic stroke during the baseline period. The final sample comprised of 717,097 patients before PS matching. Among these, 66,916 (9.3%) were prescribed anticoagulants during the baseline period. The C-statistic of the PS model was 0.731 (95% CIs: 0.730–0.733). After 1:1 PS matching, the two cohorts (prescribed and nonprescribed anticoagulants) comprised 66,258 matched pairs. Moreover, a total of 61,556 patients were prescribed one anticoagulant among 66,916 prescribed anticoagulant during baseline period. Among these, 24,011 (39.0%) and 37,545 (60.1%) patients were prescribed warfarin and DOACs, respectively. The C-statistic of the PS model was 0.607 (95% CIs: 0.603–0.612). After 1:1 PS matching, the two cohorts (prescribed warfarin and DOACs) comprised 19,713 matched pairs. Plots of PS before and after matching are shown in the Supplemental Fig. 1, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by the median of PS before matching are shown in the Supplemental Fig. 2.

Table 2 shows the incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke during the follow-up period among patients who were and were not prescribed anticoagulants. After matching, 790 patients who were prescribed anticoagulants were hospitalized for hemorrhagic stroke (383.2 per one million person-months) and 531 patients who were not prescribed anticoagulants were hospitalized for hemorrhagic stroke (252.2 per one million person-months). Using the Schoenfeld residual test, no evidence to reject the proportional hazards assumption for prescription of anticoagulants with the occurrence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke after PS matching (P = 0.64) was found. The incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke was significantly higher in patients prescribed anticoagulants than in those who were not prescribed anticoagulants (log-rank test, P < 0.001, HR = 1.64; 95% CI, 1.39–1.93) (Fig. 2). Using sensitivity analyses stratified by AF, although after PS matching, the ASD between both groups was within the margin of 0.1 for the covariates used to calculate PS among patients without AF, age, number of Charlson comorbidities, prevalence of hypertension, health checkup, and PS were higher than 0.1 of ASD (Supplemental Table 2). Therefore, we have performed Cox hazard model adjusted by age, number of Charlson comorbidities, prevalence of hypertension, annual health checkups, and PS among patients with AF. Both incidences of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke among patients with or without AF were significantly higher in patients prescribed anticoagulants than in those not prescribed anticoagulants (Supplemental Table 3). In addition, among 650,181 patients not prescribed anticoagulant during baseline periods, the sensitivity analysis of the Cox proportional hazards models with the first anticoagulant prescription month specified as a time-dependent covariate indicated that the incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke was significantly higher in patients prescribed anticoagulants during follow-up period than in those not prescribed anticoagulants (HR = 2.72; 95% CI, 2.45–3.02).

Discussion

This population-based study of older adults aged ≥ 75 years found that the prescription of anticoagulants was associated with a higher incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke. Among patients prescribed anticoagulants, warfarin was associated with a higher incidence of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke than DOACs. These findings suggest that anticoagulants, especially warfarin, should be used with caution in older adults aged ≥ 75 years, and may improve the clinical guidelines for medical treatment and safety for older adults in Japan.

The finding that older adults prescribed anticoagulants were more likely to be hospitalized for hemorrhagic stroke than those who were not prescribed anticoagulants is consistent with previous studies and clinical guidelines on PIMs in older adults [5, 6, 9–11]. This population-based study of older adults aged ≥ 75 years contributes to strengthening the existing evidence that the use of anticoagulants increases the risk of hemorrhagic stroke in older adults, especially in Japan, where population-based studies among older adults ≥ 75 years are limited. Although anticoagulants can prevent thromboembolic events, disruption of hemostatic function caused by anticoagulant use increases the risk of bleeding, including hemorrhagic stroke, particularly in older adults [5, 6]. As the prescription of anticoagulants increases in aging societies [2–4, 36], ensuring their safe use is critical.

The current study also found that patients prescribed warfarin had a higher risk of hemorrhagic stroke than those prescribed DOACs. This finding aligns with previous meta-analyses [8, 14, 15], which consistently reported a higher risk of hemorrhagic stroke associated with warfarin use. Warfarin requires frequent monitoring and dosage adjustment during the administration period, and it is more affected by food and drug interactions than DOACs, which increases the risk of hemorrhagic stroke [8, 14, 15]. Although the magnitude of this increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke associated with warfarin use compared with DOACs use was smaller in the current study than in previous studies (approximately two-fold), this discrepancy may be due to differences in the study population. The differences in race and national healthcare contexts may have influenced the observed effect size. A Japanese registry study of patients with AF reported a risk similar to that observed in this study [17]. In addition, among newly prescribed anticoagulants, the incidence of hospitalization for intracerebral hemorrhage was higher with warfarin than with DOACs; however, the difference was not statistically significant. This might reflect limited power due to the small number of events after matching; a further study is needed to compare the incidence of hemorrhage stroke between warfarin and DOACs using longer cohorts. However, the main results of the current study and previous meta-analyses indicated that the risk of hemorrhagic stroke associated with warfarin use was higher compared with the risk associated with DOACs [8, 14, 15], the elevated risk remains clinically crucial and warrants careful consideration in treatment decisions. Therefore, the Japan Geriatric Society may consider revising its clinical guidelines to recommend against the prescription of warfarin as an initial therapy for not only AF but also other diseases like VTE, except in patients who have been on long-term warfarin without adverse effects, similar to the clinical guidelines in Western countries [10].

The strength of this study is that it is a population-based study using all citizens aged 75 years or older who received medical care in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan. The findings are highly representative of citizens aged 75 years or older living in Hokkaido, because we included data from approximately 90% of Hokkaido citizens in that age group during the study period (888,389 citizens were insured by the MCSO in Hokkaido in April 2016). However, this study has several limitations. First, although we identified hospitalizations due to hemorrhagic stroke using insurance claims data, we could not validate the diagnostic accuracy. Nevertheless, most cases of hemorrhagic stroke were captured as hospitalizations for hemorrhagic stroke in this study because hemorrhagic stroke generally requires inpatient care. Second, we could not confirm whether the patients who were prescribed anticoagulants actually took anticoagulants, as claims data included prescriptions but not medication adherence. Therefore, the number of patients using anticoagulants may have been overestimated. In addition, we could not include disease severity as a covariate due to lacking information on disease severity in our claims data. Although we adjusted the covariates which were unbalanced between patients prescribed warfarin and those receiving DOACs after matching, the fit of the PS model for patients prescribed warfarin and DOACs needs to be improved in future studies. Third, the diagnoses for Charlson comorbidities could not be validated. Despite the efforts to minimize the underestimation of the identification of comorbid conditions using claims data, including outpatient and inpatient data during the study period, comorbidities are likely to be underestimated. Finally, our findings may not be directly generalizable to other countries because the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke varies depending on race and healthcare systems. Despite these limitations, this study has important implications for the healthcare of older adults.

Conclusions

In this population-based study of older adults aged ≥ 75 years in Japan, anticoagulant prescriptions were associated with an increased risk of hospitalization for hemorrhagic stroke. Among the patients prescribed anticoagulants, warfarin was associated with a higher risk than DOACs. These findings highlight the need for careful selection and monitoring of anticoagulant therapy in older adults and may help improve the clinical guidelines for medical treatment and safety for older adults in Japan.
