Mitigating nickel-induced toxicity: the protective role of native probiotic strains on oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways in mice lung tissues
Authors: Fatemeh Haririzadeh Jouriani, Elham Haj Agha Gholizadeh Khiavi, Niloofar Rezaie, Shokufeh Beglari, Shadi Aghamohammad, Mahdi Rohani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNickel exposure is a recognized environmental and occupational hazard that contributes to pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation, potentially leading to chronic respiratory conditions. Probiotics, known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, offer a promising strategy to combat oxidative stress caused by nickel exposure. This research will investigate how native probiotics can reduce inflammatory responses and oxidative damage in lung tissues, improving strategies for lung protection against heavy metal toxicity.MethodsIn this study, male NMRI mice were subjected to Nickel exposure to induce oxidative stress and inflammation for a duration of 60 days, after which they received probiotic treatment with a concentration of 1.6 * 109 CFU/ml. To assess the impact of these interventions on the antioxidant system and inflammatory responses, Real-Time PCR analysis was performed to evaluate the gene expression profiles in the lung tissue of the mice.ResultsThe study revealed that native probiotic strains significantly upregulated antioxidant gene expression while concurrently enhancing genes linked to the inflammatory signaling pathway. Although Nickel exposure diminished the expression of these genes, the administration of probiotics after Nickel exposure led to a marked increase in their expression levels.ConclusionThis research highlights the harmful impact of Nickel, a heavy metal, on lung health, while simultaneously examining the beneficial properties of probiotics, particularly their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Given the significant risk associated with heavy metal exposure, the incorporation of probiotics emerges as a promising strategy to mitigate oxidative stress and prevent a range of pulmonary disorders, including those linked to inflammation.Keywords: Antioxidant system, Probiotics, Oxidative stress, Lung, Nickel, Inflammation

Introduction

Nickel (Ni) is a metal abundant in the Earth’s crust and is widely utilized in various industrial applications [1]. While nickel is essential in trace amounts, chronic human exposure to this heavy metal poses significant health risks, particularly affecting vital organs such as the lungs, liver, and kidneys. In other words, the toxic effects of nickel are primarily mediated through oxidative stress, which can lead to severe conditions, including lung diseases, liver dysfunction, and kidney damage [2]. However, these harmful effects can be controlled and contained by antioxidants.

Oxidative stress is characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidants, resulting in cellular damage and disruption of redox signaling pathways. This phenomenon has been linked to numerous respiratory diseases, including cystic fibrosis and respiratory distress syndrome, where elevated oxidative stress levels contribute to tissue damage. In contrast to oxidative stress, different groups of antioxidant enzymes form an antioxidant network in conjunction with the support system [3].

The inflammatory response plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of oxidative stress-related diseases. Central to this response is the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which is activated by various stimuli, including pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [4, 5]. The canonical NF-κB pathway involves the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, composed of IKKα, IKKβ, and the regulatory subunit NEMO (IKKγ). Upon activation, IKKβ phosphorylates IκB proteins, leading to their degradation and the subsequent release of NF-κB dimers, primarily RELA (p65) and p50, which translocate to the nucleus to promote the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. This pathway is crucial for mediating inflammatory responses and has been implicated in various diseases characterized by chronic inflammation [6].

ROS levels are modulated by antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GR), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and by non-enzymatic antioxidants such as reduced glutathione (GSH) [7]. A large number of respiratory diseases, including cystic fibrosis and respiratory distress syndrome, are associated with increased oxidative stress [8]. ROS/RNS released by phagocytes are involved in lung tissue damage in interstitial lung diseases [9]. Several factors affect the antioxidant network and the improvement of enzyme activities; one of these factors may be probiotics [10]. Probiotics are a group of beneficial bacteria that enter the digestive tract through food or water and promote gut and human health by improving the internal microbial balance. Probiotic microbes produce bacteriocins, siderophores, lysozymes, proteases, and hydrogen peroxide, and prevent the growth of harmful pathogens [11].

The gut is now considered one of the most important foundations for maintaining the function of other organs [12]. There is evidence that the human gut microbiome is connected to other organs. This connection may be via nerves, the portal vein, or the intestinal epithelial barrier. In recent years, studies have improved our understanding of the gut-liver axis, the gut-kidney axis, the gut-brain axis, and the gut-lung axis [13]. Disorders related to gut function or the imbalance of gut flora can lead to gut diseases such as IBD or to disorders and diseases in other organs [14]. The gut and lungs have different functions, but the potential connections and complex pathways involving the microbiota make the existence of the gut-lung axis more likely, which is less researched than other gut-organ axes. Disruption of the gut-lung axis may be associated with an increased likelihood of involvement in respiratory diseases and infections, including allergies. The importance of the gut-lung axis is more apparent in patients with chronic gastrointestinal disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which have a higher prevalence than lung disease [15, 16]. In this article, we aimed to investigate the antioxidative effect of probiotics in nickel-induced oxidative stress in the lung.

Materials and methods

In vitro stage

Preparation of nickel solution and probiotic cocktail for treatment

The preparation process of the 400 mM Nickel chloride solution (NiCl2. 6H2O) involved the dilution of a concentrated stock solution of Nickel chloride, which was then subjected to an autoclave for sterilization purposes prior to being stored. This particular solution has been specifically formulated for the purpose of treating mice that have been exposed to Nickel. Before the actual implantation process takes place, the Nickel solution undergoes thorough testing to ensure that there are no potential toxic effects or allergic reactions that could arise from its use.

The ongoing research involves the utilization of four strains of probiotics that were previously identified. These particular strains, including Lactobacillus brevis 205, Lactobacillus mucosae 226, Lactobacillus casei 375, and Bifidobacterium infantis 1001, are considered the native potential probiotic strains being studied [17–19]. The first step in the process was the culturing of the bacterial strains in De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) broth, followed by an overnight incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently, the bacterial cells underwent a meticulous procedure of three rounds of washing with PBS buffer, after which they were centrifuged at a speed of 6000 rpm for a duration of 5 min at a temperature of 4 °C. The resulting pellets from this centrifugation were then carefully reconstituted in an appropriate volume of PBS buffer. To ensure consistency, the concentration of bacterial cells in the suspension of each strain was precisely adjusted to achieve an optical density of 600 nm at 2, which corresponds to a concentration of 1.6 * 109 CFU/ml. Moving on to the next step, for the preparation of the probiotic cocktail, a precise amount of 500 µl from the suspension of each strain was meticulously combined in a sterile conical tube. This specially prepared probiotic cocktail was then used for the probiotic treatment administered to the mice in the study.

In vivo stage

In the current study, thirty male NMRI (Naval Medical Research Institute) mice, weighing between 25 and 35 g and approximately two months in age, were acquired from the Pasture Institute of Karaj, Iran. These mice were divided into four groups. In this study, the Negative Control group (PBS), Positive Control (Nickel) group, and Nickel-Probiotic group (Ni + Pro) each consisted of 8 mice (n = 8). The probiotic group included 6 mice (n = 6). The stainless steel cages where they were housed provided optimal conditions, with humidity levels, lighting, and temperatures carefully maintained within the range of 55% to 65%, a balanced 12-hour light and 12-hour dark cycle, and a consistent temperature of 24 ± 1 °C. The mice had unrestricted access to a nutritionally balanced diet of Mazuri® Rat & Mouse Diet as well as fresh water. All experimental procedures conducted on the mice strictly adhered to the ethical standards set forth by the Ethics Committee of the Science and Research branch of the Islamic Azad University of Tehran, Iran, as evidenced by the approved code IR.IAU.SRB.REC.1399.106.

Treatment of mice with nickel and probiotics

In the present investigation, we utilized two distinct controls, namely Positive and Negative Controls, to ensure the validity of our experimental procedures. The Negative Control group (PBS), consisting of mice treated with PBS, was exclusively exposed to 40 µl of distilled water (PBS) for a duration of 60 days. This approach was crucial in establishing a baseline for comparison with the groups subjected to Nickel chloride, such as the Positive Control (Nickel) group, and the Nickel chloride-probiotic cocktail regimen, known as the Nickel-Probiotic group. Within the Positive Control group (Nickel), each mouse underwent daily oral administration of 40 µl of NiCl2 through gavage over the course of 60 days. On the other hand, the Nickel-Probiotic group (Ni + Pro) received a probiotic cocktail orally, comprising 100 µl with a concentration of 1.6 * 109 CFU/ml, approximately one hour after the oral administration of 40 µl nickel chloride via gavage for the same 60-day period. In addition, we examined a probiotic group that received a 50 µl probiotic cocktail solely to investigate the effects of our native probiotic strains within a normal, non-oxidative environment. This approach enabled us to systematically compare various treatments under differing conditions, thereby elucidating the potential benefits and interactions of the employed probiotic strains.

Upon the completion of the specified treatment duration, the animals were euthanized using cervical dislocation while under anesthesia. The anesthesia protocol involved administration of ketamine/xylazine at doses of ketamine 80 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally. Subsequently, their lung samples were collected, thoroughly washed with PBS, rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen, and then stored at a temperature of -80 °C to facilitate the subsequent extraction of RNA from the specimens.

RNA extraction, DNA synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from brain tissues using the RNA extraction kit from Roche, United Kingdom. The extracted RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the cDNA synthesis kit from Yekta Tajhiz in Iran, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Genes related to antioxidant systems and also genes involved in the inflammatory signaling pathway were chosen for Real-Time PCR reactions, using specific primers detailed in Table 1. Real-time PCR analysis was carried out with cDNA samples and the primers, utilizing a specialized master mix (SMbio with Rox). Each sample was evaluated with biological and experimental replicates, and the relative mRNA levels were determined using the comparative ΔΔCT technique [20]. Additionally, the gapdh gene was used as the internal control in parallel qPCR reactions [21].

Statistical data analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc, CA, USA). The one-way test ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for normal data, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normal data. The results are presented as the mean ± standard error. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

The effects of our native probiotic strains on antioxidant genes

The antioxidant effects of our native probiotic strains are illustrated in Fig. 1. The comparison of CAT gene expression revealed that the use of our probiotic strains resulted in an increase in expression levels (p < 0.05) compared to the PBS treatment, which served as the Negative Control. Conversely, the use of Nickel led to a notable decrease in expression levels (p < 0.01). When compared to the Negative Control, the combination of our native probiotic treatment and Nickel resulted in a statistically significant increase in expression levels (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the comparative analysis between our native probiotic, Nickel, and Positive Control groups demonstrated that the combination of our probiotic with Nickel (Ni + Pro) significantly enhanced CAT gene expression in mice following 60 days of Nickel chloride consumption (p < 0.0001).

The present study evaluated the antioxidant effects of our native probiotic strains against Nickel chloride as an oxidant agent. Treatment with probiotics was associated with an increase in GPX gene expression levels (p < 0.05). In contrast, Nickel chloride exposure resulted in a significant decrease in GPX expression (p < 0.01) compared to the Negative Control. When the native probiotic strains were combined with Nickel, GPX gene expression showed an increase relative to both Positive and Negative Control groups (p < 0.0001).

Our findings revealed that the expression level of the HO-1 gene was enhanced by our native probiotic cocktail in mice (p < 0.05). Conversely, the presence of Nickel chloride as an oxidative factor led to a decrease in HO-1 expression level (p < 0.05). Moreover, mice treated with Nickel chloride and probiotics exhibited an increase in HO-1 gene expression level (p < 0.0001) compared to the Positive and Negative Controls.

Based on our results, probiotic treatment did not cause significant changes in the expression levels of the antioxidant gene KEAP. In the Nickel group, KEAP expression was significantly reduced compared to the Negative Control (p < 0.05). However, co-administration of the native potential probiotic blend with Nickel was associated with an increase in KEAP gene expression compared to both the Nickel-treated group (Positive Control) and the PBS group (Negative Control).

The native probiotics did not change the Nrf2 expression in mice compared to the Negative Control group. When Nickel was compared with the Negative Control group, the results showed that this oxidant agent led to a decrease in the expression level (p < 0.01). Mice treated with Nickel chloride and probiotics for 60 days showed a significant increase in Nrf2 expression levels (p < 0.0001) compared to the Positive Control group (p < 0.001) and the Negative Control group.

The findings demonstrated changes in SOD expression levels associated with treatment using our native probiotic strains. Our results showed a statistically significant increase in SOD expression in the probiotic-treated group compared to the Negative Control (p < 0.01). In the Positive Control group, Nickel exposure resulted in a reduction in SOD expression relative to the Negative Control (p < 0.05). Additionally, the combination of our native probiotic strains with Nickel chloride after 60 days led to a significant increase in SOD gene expression compared to other groups (p < 0.0001).

The expression level of the antioxidant gene TRX was influenced by the treatment with our native probiotic strains. According to the results, the native probiotic mixture was associated with an increase in TRX expression levels (p < 0.05), while mice treated with Nickel showed a decrease in expression. Moreover, co-treatment with the native probiotic blend and Nickel resulted in a statistically significant increase in TRX gene expression compared to the controls (p < 0.0001).

Conversely, mice treated with our native strains in combination with Nickel showed a decrease in expression level compared to the Positive Control group (p < 0.01).

Discussion

Heavy metals are widely present in diverse sources such as industrial activities, agricultural practices, healthcare, and cosmetics, resulting in considerable environmental dispersion [22]. Nickel, as a prominent heavy metal, raises substantial health concerns. While the exact mechanisms of its toxicity are not fully understood, studies indicate that Nickel can negatively affect oxidative stress, alter DNA functions, and disrupt essential signal transduction pathways. Exposure to Nickel via food and drinking water is particularly alarming, as its environmental presence is associated with oxidative stress, linked to various detrimental health effects [23, 24]. The lung is significantly impacted by oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which arise from both its inherent physiological functions and various pathological conditions. Increased ROS levels can lead to compromised lung function, resulting in inflammation and impaired gas exchange, thereby worsening respiratory diseases [25, 26]. This disruption poses not only risks to individual health but also broader public health concerns, highlighting the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies to address oxidative damage in pulmonary tissues [27]. The adverse effects of oxidative stress on lung health necessitate the use of protective agents, particularly those with antioxidant capabilities. Probiotics, as live microorganisms, emerge as a promising and safe intervention, capable of exerting antioxidant activities and enhancing the body’s natural defenses, potentially mitigating oxidative stress and fostering overall lung health [28, 29]. In the current study, we examined the adverse effects of Nickel exposure and hypothesized that our native probiotic strains possess the potential to mitigate these detrimental impacts. By leveraging the natural resilience of these probiotics, we aimed to explore their efficacy in counteracting the physiological challenges posed by heavy metal toxicity, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of their protective mechanisms.

In the present research, we sought to elucidate the effects of Nickel on oxidative stress and the antioxidant capabilities of native probiotic strains by examining its influence on genes associated with the Nrf2 pathway, including CAT-1, HO-1, GPX, KEAP, NRF-2, SOD, and TRX. The study explored the effects of our native probiotic strains in a non-oxidative environment by administering probiotic treatments alongside Nickel and Nickel-probiotic treatments. The results showed that treatment with our native probiotic strains was associated with an increase in the expression levels of all the genes examined compared to the PBS group. These strains demonstrated antioxidant activity even before the induction of oxidative stress, suggesting a potential role in cellular protection. The use of Nickel as an oxidative agent was associated with a significant decrease in the expression levels of all examined genes. This reduction brought the gene expression levels close to zero, suggesting that Nickel may have a suppressive effect on the regulatory mechanisms of these genes. In accordance with our research, Wu et al. conducted a study on the detrimental effects of Nickel, specifically its influence on oxidant and antioxidant enzyme activities. Their research revealed that the administration of NiCl2 at a concentration of 1.6 mg/ml (0.25 ml) led to an increase in the levels of MDA. Concurrently, there was a significant reduction in the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including GSH-Px, GR, SOD, and CAT [30]. In a study conducted by Iqbal et al., the effects of Nickel nanoparticles were examined, revealing a significant increase in MDA and LPO levels, accompanied by a decrease in CAT and GSH levels [31]. These findings underscore the adverse impact of nickel on oxidative stress mechanisms in biological systems.

On the other hand, our study showed that co-administration of native probiotic strains with Nickel was associated with an increase in gene expression levels. The results indicated a statistically significant rise in expression when these probiotics were given alongside Nickel. This suggests that the native probiotic strains may help mitigate some of the effects caused by Nickel exposure through their antioxidant-related activity. These findings suggest that our native probiotic strains could offer enhanced protective benefits under conditions of oxidative stress. Research indicates promising roles for probiotic strains in mitigating the effects of heavy metals, although further investigation is necessary. According to Goyal et al., Bacillus species demonstrate potential for bioremediation, specifically in addressing heavy metals such as Cadmium and Nickel through mechanisms like biosorption and bioaccumulation. Additionally, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have been shown to alleviate the toxicity of these metals in eukaryotic cell lines and murine models, highlighting the need for continued exploration of probiotics as a viable solution to heavy metal contamination. Recent studies have highlighted the antioxidant properties of specific probiotic strains [32]. Notably, research by Dahi et al. demonstrated that Lactobacillus casei NCDC19 and Lactobacillus acidophilus NCDC14 effectively inhibited lipid peroxidation while maintaining the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including GPx, SOD, and catalase CAT. Additionally, Lactobacillus plantarum AS1 has been shown to possess antioxidant characteristics that contribute to the modulation of DMH-induced colon carcinogenesis in rat models, underscoring the potential health benefits of probiotics in cancer prevention [32].

From another perspective, in the present research, we aimed to elucidate the effects of Nickel on inflammation, along with the anti-inflammatory potential of native probiotic strains. Our investigation focused on the influence of Nickel exposure and native probiotic strains on key genes associated with the NF-κB pathway, specifically RELA, IKKA, and IKKB. By analyzing these genetic responses, we sought to deepen our understanding of the interplay between Nickel and inflammatory processes, as well as the potential therapeutic implications of probiotics in modulating these effects. The study investigated the impact of native probiotic strains in a non-inflammatory environment, comparing probiotic, Nickel, and Nickel-probiotic treatments. Results indicated that the administration of our native probiotics significantly elevated the expression levels of the RELA and IKKA genes. Importantly, the probiotics displayed anti-inflammatory properties prior to any inflammatory induction (Probiotic treatment), underscoring their proactive role in modulating inflammatory responses. However, the use of Nickel as an inflammatory agent has been shown to markedly elevate the expression levels of key regulatory genes such as RELA, IKKA, and IKKB. This pronounced increase suggests a potential detrimental impact on the regulatory mechanisms of these genes, ultimately leading to inflammatory responses. Notably, the expression levels diminished nearly to 2, indicating that Nickel may significantly enhance inflammatory pathways, thereby contributing to the exacerbation of inflammation. Hongrui Guo et al. demonstrated that NiCl2 induces inflammatory activation through several key signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPKs, IRF3, and the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway by elevating the transcription levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-18, TNF-α, and IFN-β [33]. Our research demonstrated a marked decrease in gene expression levels following the co-administration of native probiotic strains and Nickel. This interaction resulted in enhanced activation of genes associated with the inflammatory pathway, suggesting that these native probiotics may play a crucial role in mitigating the adverse effects of Nickel exposure through their anti-inflammatory properties. Angélica Vincenzi et al. demonstrated that probiotics play a crucial role in modulating primary cell signaling pathways associated with inflammation. Their research highlights the inhibition of both degradation and phosphorylation of IκB, mechanisms that are pivotal in the regulation of the NF-κB pathway [34]. Shadi Aghamohammad et al. demonstrated that a thorough evaluation of probiotics’ molecular effects on signaling pathways elucidates their role in modulating and reducing inflammation. Their research highlighted the anti-inflammatory properties of Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus brevis, and Lactobacillus reuteri in HT-29 cells, specifically through the modulation of JAK/STAT and NF-kB pathways [35].

The findings of this research suggest that heavy metals, especially Nickel, may pose risks to human health, particularly by affecting the respiratory system through oxidative stress and inflammation. The native probiotic strains showed potential to reduce some of these effects, especially in lung tissue. However, this study was limited to molecular assessments of the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of the native probiotic strains, without including histopathological evaluation of the impacts of Nickel or the probiotics. Future studies will focus on higher doses of probiotics and longer treatment durations to better understand the histopathological effects of Nickel exposure and native probiotic application.

This study has several limitations. Primarily, the investigation focused on molecular assessments of gene expression related to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways without incorporating histopathological or biochemical analyses to evaluate the tissue-level effects of Nickel exposure or probiotic treatment. Additionally, the sample size was limited to thirty male mice of a single strain, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. The study also did not explore different doses of Nickel or probiotics, nor longer treatment durations, which could provide more comprehensive insights into the dose-response relationship and long-term effects. Future research should address these limitations by including histological evaluations, varying dosages, and larger, more diverse animal cohorts to better elucidate the therapeutic potential and mechanisms of native probiotic strains against Nickel toxicity.

Conclusion

The present research aims to uncover significant findings regarding the detrimental effects of Nickel, a heavy metal, on the respiratory system, as well as the potential benefits of native probiotic strains. It is essential to investigate how these probiotics may mitigate the harmful impacts of heavy metals, particularly in relation to pathways that influence oxidative stress and inflammation. Given the heightened risk associated with heavy metal exposure, leveraging probiotic strains presents a promising strategy to counteract adverse outcomes, such as oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby helping to prevent the development of various pulmonary disorders, including inflammation-related diseases.
