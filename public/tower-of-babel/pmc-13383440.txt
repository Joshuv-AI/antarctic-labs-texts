Spontaneous bone formation after sinus graft infection control: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Jiaxin Luo, Shengyuan Wang, Xingchao Zeng, Fuchun Chen, Shiyong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus is a rare phenomenon, commonly attributed to multiple factors that influence the space between the sinus floor and mucosa, thereby promoting the formation of blood clots in this region. Nevertheless, these factors are not yet fully elucidated.Case presentationIn January 2022, a 20-year-old male patient underwent bone-added osteotome sinus floor elevation with simultaneous implant placement at the maxillary left first molar. However, 10 days after surgery, the patient presented with left infraorbital pain, peri-implant suppuration, and a loose implant. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images showed sinus opacification and reduced density of the peri-implant bone and graft materials. The implant and graft materials were then removed, and the socket was thoroughly irrigated with copious chlorhexidine solution and saline, then meticulously covered with iodoform gauze. The pain symptoms were alleviated a week after implant removal. The maxillary sinus remained opaque on CBCT images, with thinning of the lateral sinus wall and reduced bone height. After 3 months, clinical examination revealed no significant abnormalities in the soft tissues. Radiographic evaluations showed new bone formation within the maxillary sinus, accompanied by mucosal thickening and the disappearance of opacities. 2 years later, CBCT images displayed spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus. Finally, an implant was placed, and the restoration was completed successfully.ConclusionsThis case report underscores the occurrence of spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus after resolution of a postoperative sinus graft infection, ultimately enabling successful reimplantation at the same site. In analogous cases, prolonging the healing period may be beneficial to promote natural self-healing.

Background

Odontogenic infection and alveolar bone resorption following tooth loss often cause a substantial reduction of the residual bone height (RBH) in the posterior maxilla, constraining standard implant placement [1]. Maxillary sinus floor elevation (MSFE) is a bone augmentation procedure to enhance the bone height in the posterior maxilla by forming new bone in the space created under the elevated sinus membrane [2]. Bone-added osteotome sinus floor elevation (BAOSFE) can be considered more conservative than the lateral approach, particularly when the RBH exceeds 4–5 mm [3, 4]. Its merits comprise reduced morbidity, shorter surgical time, and less postoperative discomfort [5, 6]. Although the underlying mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, it is obvious that the lifting of the membrane probably triggers a series of events, encompassing blood and fibrin clot formation, cell migration and differentiation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis [7]. However, during transcrestal sinus floor elevation, the force required to detach the membrane increases as the elevated area enlarges. Perforation or tearing may occur when this force exceeds the elastic limit of the sinus membrane, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative infection [8].

Postoperative infection refers to an infection of the graft and/or the sinus. It is more likely to result from an infection of the graft rather than a true sinus infection [9]. The following have been suggested as potential etiologic factors: periapical or periodontal lesions, preoperative sinusitis, bacterial contamination of the graft, membrane perforation, graft migration into the sinus cavity, or blockage of sinus drainage after membrane elevation due to the presence of a mucous retention cyst or an excessively thickened sinus membrane [10]. Before considering the complete or partial removal of the graft, pharmacological treatment with antibiotics should be administered first [11]. However, if symptoms (such as tenderness, nasal obstruction, pain, purulent nasal discharge, suppuration, etc.) persist for more than 3 weeks despite pharmacological treatment, surgery involving partial or total removal of the graft materials, via oral or nasal approach, is recommended [12].

Postoperative infections commonly result in bone resorption, requiring secondary bone grafting for implant placement. This procedure is more complex for the surgeon, more traumatic for the patient, and less predictable for osteogenic outcomes [10, 13]. Unexpectedly, we encountered a clinical case of a patient in whom spontaneous osteogenesis occurred within the maxillary sinus following the resolution of the infection. The newly formed bone is sufficient for the subsequent implant placement. While a limited number of cases of spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus have been reported, to the best of our knowledge, spontaneous osteogenesis following the control of postoperative sinus graft infection has not been documented. To expand the current understanding of spontaneous bone formation after intra-maxillary sinus infection and its underlying pathophysiological processes, this report describes a case of postoperative infection following BAOSFE and discusses its etiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis, management, and prognosis. A comprehensive literature review of spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus was also conducted. This case report was reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines [14].

Case presentation

In January 2022, a 20-year-old man presented for treatment of an edentulous site at tooth #26. The patient lost the tooth 4 months prior because of deep caries and a vertical root fracture. The patient was in good general health and denied any history of medication use or smoking. Clinical examination showed no signs of mucosal redness or swelling, although poor oral hygiene and soft plaque accumulation were observed. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) evaluation revealed a slight thickening of the sinus membrane and approximately 5 mm of RBH at the #26 site (Fig. 1A, B). The following treatment plan was proposed: BAOSFE with simultaneous implant placement. The patient was informed of all reasonable risks and asked to review and sign the informed consent.

Intraoperatively, midcrestal and sulcular incisions around the adjacent teeth were made with a No. 15c scalpel blade under local anesthesia using articaine 4% with epinephrine 1:100,000. A full-thickness flap was elevated. A round carbide bur with diameters of 1.4 mm and 2.3 mm was used to mark the implant site. A CAS drill (Osstem, Korea) with a diameter of 2.8 mm and 3.6 mm, along with a 4 mm stopper, was utilized to prepare an osteotomy 1 mm away from the sinus floor. An osteotome with a 3.5 mm tip was employed to gently fracture the sinus floor with a mallet, and the membrane was elevated by approximately 2 mm. The Schneiderian membrane was continuously elevated via the osteotome by gently pushing the bovine xenograft bone (Bio-Oss, Geistlich, Switzerland) upwards with a mallet until an adequate vertical height was achieved. Then, a dental implant (4.8 mm in diameter, 10 mm in length, regular neck; Straumann, Institute Straumann AG, Switzerland) was placed, achieving a primary stability of 25 Ncm. A healing abutment was connected to the fixture with a hand driver, and 5 − 0 nylon sutures were used to stabilize the soft tissue. Postoperative CBCT revealed an intact elevation of the sinus membrane of approximately 6 mm high (Fig. 1C, D). The patient was prescribed an antibiotic (cefuroxime axetil tablets, 250 mg twice daily) and an anti-inflammatory drug (dexamethasone, 1.5 mg three times daily) for 3 days, along with nitrofurazone nasal drops (1 to 2 drops, three times daily) for 1 week, and 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthrinses twice daily for 2 weeks.

Ten days postoperatively, the patient reported pain in the left infraorbital region. Intraoral examination revealed peri-implant suppuration and implant loosening. CBCT images showed sinus opacification and a reduction in density of peri-implant bone and graft materials (Fig. 1E, F). It is important to note that the patient did not adhere to the prescribed post-surgery medication regimen.

Based on the patient’s clinical symptoms and radiographic findings, removal of the implant at position #26 was indicated to control the infection. During the procedure, the implant was removed, and the socket was debrided and irrigated with chlorhexidine and saline via a syringe until the effluent ran clear. A substantial amount of dark brown blood clots and infected graft material was flushed out. Iodoform gauze was then placed in the socket and secured in situ with sutures. The patient was prescribed antibiotics, including cefuroxime axetil (250 mg twice daily) and metronidazole (400 mg three times daily) for 6 days, nitrofurazone nasal drops (1 to 2 drops, three times daily) for 1 week, along with 0.2% chlorhexidine mouth rinse twice daily for 2 weeks.

One week after implant removal, the wound showed uneventful healing, with no obvious redness, swelling, or tenderness in the left infraorbital region. The iodoform gauze was removed, and the site was irrigated again with chlorhexidine solution. The effluent was clear, with no clinical signs of infection. To prevent an oroantral fistula and promote soft tissue healing, a collagen plug was then inserted into the defect and secured with absorbable sutures. CBCT images demonstrated that the maxillary sinus remained opaque, exhibiting signs of thinning of the lateral sinus wall and a reduction in RBH (Fig. 2A, B).

A thorough intraoral examination was taken 3 months later, and the soft tissues exhibited a pink color and normal texture. CBCT images revealed new bone formation at the socket and maxillary sinus floor, accompanied by mucosal thickening and the disappearance of opacities (Fig. 2C, D). Afterwards, to meet the patient’s personal needs and maintain the restorative space, a removable partial denture was provided.

2 years later, the patient revisited with no symptoms of pressure pain and no significant abnormalities in the mucosa. As shown in the CBCT images, a notable increase was observed in both the RBH and the thickness of the lateral sinus wall (Fig. 2E, F). The floor and lateral wall of the maxillary sinus exhibited signs of the formation of cortical bone layers. In the coronal view (Fig. 2F), the lateral wall displayed a distinct multiple cortical bone layers, and there is a marked thickening of the buccal cortical bone when compared to earlier images (Fig. 2D). DICOM files were automatically superimposed by aligning the same hard tissue landmarks across three time points (preoperatively, 10 days after removal of the implant, and two years after removal of the implant) (Fig. 3A, B). Despite a reduction in alveolar ridge bone width at site #26 compared to preoperative images, a substantial augmentation in bone height and lateral sinus wall thickness was observed (Fig. 3C-E).

Finally, an implant (4.1 mm in diameter, 10 mm in length, bone level; Straumann, Institute Straumann AG, Switzerland) was placed at the tooth #26 site (Fig. 4A-E). Upon flap elevation, corticated alveolar bone was observed (Fig. 4B). Case summary

Discussion

Infection is a rare but severe early complication following MSFE, with a reported incidence of approximately 2%-5% [15]. In most cases, it involves infection of the grafted material rather than primary sinusitis and may result from contamination of graft materials, bacterial entry through sinus membrane perforation, or exacerbation of pre-existing asymptomatic sinus pathology by postoperative inflammatory changes [10, 16, 17]. In this case, the patient developed left infraorbital pain and discomfort 10 days after BAOSFE, accompanied by peri-implant suppuration and implant loosening. CBCT images showed sinus opacification and a decreased density of the peri-implant bone and grafted area (Fig. 1E, F). These findings suggest an early postoperative infection that contributed to implant failure.

Based on the analysis of the etiology of postoperative infection, it is likely due to bacterial infection, surgical, and patient-related factors. Firstly, bacteria and infectious agents from the adjacent teeth and mouth may lead to the contamination of graft materials. In this case, although preoperative cleaning was performed, the limited mesial-distal distance of the edentulous site made it more likely for plaque from the adjacent tooth to contaminate the graft material. To address this issue, the optimization of methods and equipment for graft material delivery, including the use of injectable instruments for grafts, may be a viable approach [15]. Secondly, the initial bone height at the implant site was measured at 5 mm. After surgery, although the augmented sinus had a dome-shaped appearance, the sinus floor was elevated to a maximum height of approximately 6 mm and filled with an excess of graft materials (Fig. 1C, D, and Fig. 5A, B). This excess stress on the sinus membrane increased the risk of sinus membrane perforation or tear [18, 19]. Furthermore, as previously reported, disparities in anatomical structures, nasal pathologies, and systemic conditions may result in divergent postoperative edema reactions in the sinus mucosa [20]. Analysis of the preoperative CBCT images revealed mild thickening of the Schneiderian membrane. This is usually a sign of altered sinus physiology, which may have resulted in diminished membrane vasculature and elasticity, secondary necrosis, and reduced resistance during elevation. It also led to an elevated risk of membrane perforation and postoperative infection [21, 22]. To reduce the risk of graft contamination, preoperative dental cleaning, local disinfection with mouthwash, intraoperative saline irrigation and suction, postoperative antibiotic administration, and avoidance of excessive graft filling during bone augmentation are recommended [22]. However, in this case, the patient did not comply with the prescribed postoperative medication regimen, highlighting the importance of considering medication adherence.

Several studies have confirmed new bone formation following maxillary sinus lift, with the space being initially filled solely by blood [23–25], which subsequently transforms into a callus that promotes bone formation [26]. The blood clot seems to serve as an adequate filler and excellent medium for bone formation [27, 28]. In the present case, early postoperative infection led to implant removal, followed by thorough irrigation with saline and chlorhexidine solution, during which graft materials and dark brown blood clots were flushed out. CBCT imaging indicated considerable resorption of the bone wall at the implant site (Fig. 2A, B). However, two years postoperatively, unexpected bone formation was observed in the maxillary sinus floor, lateral wall, and implant socket (Fig. 2E, F). This spontaneous osteogenesis extended well beyond the original grafted area and, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in the literature. Changes in the space between the sinus mucosa and the sinus floor after surgery are considered to be the underlying cause of the observed spontaneous osteogenesis (Fig. 5C, D). Postoperative hemorrhage and edema may result in additional stripping of the sinus membrane and alterations in the space between the membrane and sinus floor [29]. Furthermore, irrigation during the postoperative infection control phase may also contribute to passive stripping of the sinus membrane, thereby expanding the space beneath the sinus floor.

Evidence across the literature suggests that spontaneous bone formation within the maxillary sinus is primarily driven by the creation of a secluded space and the innate osteogenic potential of the sinus environment (Table 2). Lundgren et al. [30] initially documented this phenomenon following the removal of intrasinus cysts and simple membrane elevation, concluding that surgical trauma and the maintenance of a protected compartment between the bone surfaces and the mucosa are sufficient to trigger autogenous bone reformation. Supporting this mechanism, Jung et al. [31] demonstrated that when an extraction socket is in proximity to the sinus, the blood clot within the socket serves as a vital matrix, facilitating new bone growth that fills the gap between the sinus floor and the elevated membrane. Further histological findings reported by Scarano et al. [32] after removal of migrated implants demonstrated newly formed bone with active vascularization, absence of inflammatory infiltrate, and areas of mature cortical bone. The authors noted that blood clot formation induced by surgical trauma may stimulate the release of multiple growth factors, which are believed to regulate bone tissue repair. Additionally, Liu et al. [33] highlighted that the Schneiderian membrane possesses significant regenerative capacity even when indirectly stimulated through guided bone regeneration in adjacent extraction sockets, resulting in measurable vertical bone gain under a closed, stable environment. Collectively, these findings indicate that the maxillary sinus possesses a robust inherent capacity for bone regeneration, provided a stable, blood-clot-filled environment is maintained.

Recent studies have demonstrated that elevating the Schneiderian membrane alone can induce bone formation in the maxillary sinus [34–36]. The membrane has been reported to contain mesenchymal osteoprogenitor cells capable of expressing osteogenic markers, undergoing osteogenic differentiation, and contributing to new bone formation [37, 38]. Previous studies suggested that the bony walls of the maxillary sinus serve as the principal source of osteogenic cells, whereas the Schneiderian membrane plays a secondary role in this process [39, 40]. Surgical trauma and mechanical stimulation may activate these cells, while bleeding can generate a fibrin scaffold that supports osteogenesis beneath the elevated membrane [41] (Fig. 5C-F). Clinical studies using platelet-rich fibrin as the sole grafting material have further demonstrated substantial bone gain and high implant survival rates, supporting the concept that a stabilized blood clot or fibrin matrix can function as a natural osteogenic chamber within the sinus cavity [42, 43]. In addition, infection-related inflammation may also stimulate periosteal new bone formation, typically presenting as layered bone deposition on an existing cortical surface [44–46].

Based on the analysis of osteogenic conditions, it is postulated that the space formed between the maxillary sinus bony wall and the Schneiderian membrane, together with subsequent blood clot formation induced by surgical trauma, provides a stable environment for osteogenesis. This environment, in conjunction with the osteogenic capacity of the bone wall and Schneiderian membrane, ultimately contributes to the initiation of bone formation. Furthermore, inflammatory stimuli can induce subperiosteal ossification, which may also play a role in the osteogenesis within the maxillary sinus. However, it should be explicitly acknowledged that no histological evidence was available in this case, which prevents a definitive evaluation of the newly formed bone observed in both radiographic and clinical examinations and limits interpretation of its underlying etiology. In future cases, timely collection of tissue samples is essential to enable accurate diagnosis and etiological assessment.

Conclusion

This report presents a case of spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus after the removal of both the implant and graft, with subsequent infection resolution, which ultimately resulted in the successful reimplantation at the same site. This report contributes new insights into spontaneous bone formation in the maxillary sinus, particularly following the successful management of maxillary sinus graft infection. In similar cases, it may be advisable to extend the healing period to promote natural self-healing.
