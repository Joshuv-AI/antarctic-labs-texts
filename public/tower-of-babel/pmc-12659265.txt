Lactar bonito (breastfeeding artfully): knowledge, attitudes, and practices among the Nasa indigenous people in Colombia
Authors: Leisy Cruz Rodríguez, Diego Fernando Rosero Medina, Cristina María Mejía Merino, Luz Nidia Finscué Pete, Juan Camilo Calderón Farfán, Jennifer Marcela López Ríos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding (BF) among indigenous people is considered a natural and socio-cultural practice that stems from worldviews and ancestral knowledge aimed at strengthening bonding and affection and transmitting history and language for cultural survival. Our study aimed to describe the knowledge, attitudes, and practices about BF under the Nasa cosmovision of an indigenous people in southern Colombia.MethodsThis qualitative study is part of a project focusing on Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR). The photovoice technique was used with beneficiary mothers of Community Care Units (CCUs) attached to the Intercultural Early Childhood Program administered by the Nasa Paez Huila Indigenous Reservation. Fourteen CCUs participated, and 27 photographs and stories about BF in the community were collected. Data analysis was conducted using Grounded Theory analytic tools and ATLAS.ti software.ResultsBF under the Nasa cosmovision, women are seen as protagonists who, by breastfeeding, influence cultural scenarios such as their homes, community, ancestral settings (house, tul,tulpa, rivers, trees), symbolic elements (chumbe), and knowledge (medicinal plants and foods) that contribute to the care of mothers and newborns in connection with Uma Kiwe (Mother Earth). Positive and affirming attitudes (lactar bonito) toward BF associated with cultural survival and care for the community and Mother Earth are emphasized to the point of elevating BF to the status of a children’s right. Traditional and Western practices coexist to prepare women in puerperium through traditional medicine practices to improve breast milk production, quality, and duration.ConclusionsNasa’s knowledge of BF incorporates indigenous beliefs about the interconnectedness and interdependence of caring for women, children, and Mother Earth. The attitudes involve the feelings of different family and community members who are prepared to take care of both the mother and the ‘new being’, which implies the revitalization of the food and ancestral culture of the indigenous people. Finally, BF practices are implemented from pregnancy to puerperium and aim to improve milk production, infant feeding, and nutrition. Some alternative uses of breast milk were also identified, as well as synergies between traditional and Western practices that promote BF, and the tensions and risks associated with BF in the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08352-y.

Introduction

Breastfeeding (BF) is the natural process of feeding an infant with breast milk, which provides the necessary nutrients and protection against infectious, allergic, and autoimmune diseases for a child’s normal growth and development [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) of newborns for the first six months of life and complementary feeding for up to two years of age [3]. To this end, the WHO has set a target of increasing the BF rate to at least 50% in the first six months of life by 2025 [4].

In addition to the benefits for children, BF promotes maternal health by reducing postpartum bleeding, increasing uterine involution, and reducing the risk of breast and ovarian cancer [4]. It helps babies adapt to the world by promoting their emotional, psychological, and motor development, increasing their IQ (Intelligence quotient), and stimulating their visual, auditory, and communicative development [1, 2]. Breast milk is, therefore, the ideal food for children during the first two years of life, as its high caloric content guarantees the proper development and nutrition of the child.

In 2019, the global rate of EBF in the first six months of life was low (43%), consistent with figures from a survey of 194 countries in which only 23 countries reported EBF above 60% [5–7]. In Colombia, 95% of children born through institutional delivery (56 out of every 100) begin BF within the first few hours of their lives [8]. In 2015, the rate for children under six months was 36.1%, lower than that reported in Peru in 2014 (68.4%) and Bolivia in 2012 (64.3%). In this regard, it was also found that indigenous children have a higher rate of BF (78.1%) compared to non-indigenous children (51.9%) and are 50% more likely to continue BF up to one year compared to mestizo children [9].

A study conducted in an indigenous reservation in Cauca, Colombia, found that 11.9% of mothers with infants aged 0–6 months were giving EBF, while the majority (88.1%) had already started complementary feeding. Therefore, while BF has been recognized as a healthy practice, the numbers show that weaning and introducing solid foods before the recommended time is associated with health problems such as infant malnutrition [10, 11].

In the Department of Huila, the prevalence of EBF in 2015 was 58.5% in children under six months, with a decrease as the child’s age increases: 1 month (74.8%), 2–3 months (56.4%), and 4–5 months (31.3%). Such a decrease is accompanied by introducing foods other than breast milk, such as liquids or semi-solid or solid foods, into the infant’s diet [12].

In the light of the above, BF plays a significant role in the sociocultural sphere, which makes it pertinent to address the personal and social knowledge, attitudes, and practices woven around it. This approach is based on recognizing BF as an intercultural practice that allows for the reinforcement of knowledge, the strengthening of bonds and affection, and the transmission of history and language, thus promoting cultural survival. Similarly, BF is a protective practice against child malnutrition in which indigenous women play a fundamental role, integrating values, knowledge, and teachings without neglecting their own knowledge and practices [10, 13, 14]. For these reasons, this study aimed to describe the knowledge, attitudes, and practices about the BF under the Nasa cosmovision in an indigenous community in southern Colombia.

Methods

This qualitative and descriptive study was conducted as part of a broader research project using Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR). Due to the subject matter of the research, the photovoice technique was used as part of CBPR [15]. The study population comprised mothers of children aged 0–6 years from the Community Care Units (CCUs) attached to the Intercultural Early Childhood Program “Semillas de Vida” (Seeds of Life) administered by the Nasa Páez Huila Indigenous Reservation. The community is part of the Nasa people who represent 12.76% of Colombia’s indigenous population and are primarily concentrated in the departments of Cauca and Huila. This community accounts for 18.49% of the population of the municipality of Iquira (Huila). This population originates from the reservation with the same name, located in the Tierradentro region of Cauca, and relocated following the Páez River flood in 1994. The reservation comprises ten villages situated near the rural municipality of Río Negro, where cultural traditions, such as agricultural practices, spiritual ceremonies, and community governance is preserved.

Participants were selected based on the following criteria [16]: Mothers over 14 years of age with children between 0 and 6 years of age who were beneficiaries of one of the fourteen CCUs of the Nasa Paez Huila Indigenous Reservation. A total of 27 participants were enrolled, including pregnant and BF mothers aged between 17 and 45 years.

The information-gathering process was carried out using the photovoice technique, which allowed social reality to be captured through photographs and enhanced the power of narrating events through the voices of the community. The photovoice technique has been used and is congruent with the CBPR approach [17].

A photovoice contest was coordinated with the Semillas de Vida program, in which each CCU took two photographs related to BF from the view of the Nasa cosmovision. This activity was carried out in August 2022 as part of the celebration of World Breastfeeding Week (WBW), with the active participation of the mothers enrolled in the program and the support of educators and community communicators of the program Vientos de Comunicación (community communicators of the Regional Indigenous Council of Huila [CRIHU]).

In addition, the following criteria were established to evaluate the photographs entering the contest: (1) creativity (innovative photographs that presented the content in an original way); (2) representation of one’s own (aspects or ancestral and traditional practices of the community); (3) sharpness and technique (sharp photos allowing to appreciate all the details); (4) achievement of the objective (representation of meanings, experiences or practices related to BF), and 5)inspiration and emotionality (ability to convey emotions and retrieve the Nasa feeling).

After the photographs were submitted, the stories associated with each picture were recorded during a joint meeting with the mothers, the Semillas de Vida program staff, and the research team members. For the presentation of the photographs, a display board was set up with the names of each CCU in Spanish and Nasa Yuwe (indigenous language) so that the photographs could be placed and shown as the participants explained their meanings. These stories were told primarily in their mother tongue, with the assistance of a community interpreter to share them in Spanish. In addition, with the consent of the participants, a total of 27 stories associated with the photographs were recorded and later transcribed verbatim in Spanish.

The information analysis was carried out in two phases using analytical tools of Grounded Theory as described by Strauss and Corbin [18]. In the first phase, line-by-line open coding of the transcribed texts was performed, identifying initial codes and creating descriptive categories. In the second phase, the codes were grouped into broader categories through axial coding, resulting in analytical categories from which relational maps were created in response to the proposed objective and emerging themes. These processes were supported by ATLAS.ti 8 qualitative data analysis software with an educational license.

This research project was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Faculty of Public Health of Universidad de Antioquia, with Minutes Number 21030002-0082−2021, Session 264 of May 21, 2021.

Results

Based on the information analysis, the knowledge, attitudes, and BF practices from the view of the Nasa cosmovision were described. Three related categories emerged: (1) breastfeeding in connection with Mother Earth, (2) lactar bonito (breastfeeding artfully) for Nasa survival, and (3) breastfeeding practices: tensions between traditional and Western practices (Fig. 1). Each category is described below:

Breastfeeding under the Nasa cosmovision

Under the Nasa cosmovision, women have a leading role in protecting and caring for the survival of the indigenous people in their land; an example of this is the care of newborns and children. From this perspective, the mother figure is seen as a hardworking and daring woman committed to the permanence and survival of her culture and people.

“A Nasa woman doesn’t fuss over anything. She doesn’t say, ‘Who do I leave the baby with while I go get my food?’ She doesn’t say, I can’t! There we see a warrior woman (Fig. 2), not only with the baby, but also with the family, with the land.” (Mother, CCU 7).

The act of breastfeeding the baby has a direct connection with Uma Kiwe (Mother Earth) because in both contexts, the mother nourishes and cares for the baby, establishing relationships of balance and interdependence between the care of a mother, a child, and Mother Earth:

“Mother Earth and breastfeeding are closely related. If we do not feed Mother Earth, the water dries up, if we do not sow, if we do not take care of the land, the trees dry up and she does not produce water. The same would happen with mothers, if we do not feed ourselves, if we are not fed, our milk dries up.” (Mother, CCU 9).

Knowledge: breastfeeding in connection with mother earth

Spaces like home represent a warm environment, full of love and care, made by the hands of their ancestors, with clay foundations and full of stories. It is an appropriate place to recreate the past to remember and strengthen cultural roots. Inside it is thetulpa (three-stone fire), where traditional medicine practices are performed. These practices include baths in the heat of the fire to stimulate breast milk production and rituals to “open the way” (projecting processes) that symbolize purification and spiritual renewal, fostering the mother-child bond and its connection with the land in both the present and the future. Such practice is regarded as a ritual of connection with Mother Earth and is considered essential during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. Another important practice is the sowing of the baby’s navel (umbilical cord) performed to ensure protection and take root in their land. A further meaningful space is the tul lecxkwe, or vegetable garden, where women fertilize, sow, and tend vegetables and medicinal herbs to promote the family’s buen vivir (good living) with food and traditional medicine.

“We do it there (breastfeeding) next to the tulpa, there where the remedy is cooked, and we wash our breasts for the respective ritual or remedy.” (Mother, CCU 1).

“We do it there (breastfeeding) next to the tulpa, there where the remedy is cooked, and we wash our breasts for the respective ritual or remedy.” (Mother, CCU 1).

“We represent the vegetable garden, that is, what we, the indigenous women, grow: onions, tomatoes, or rather, everything […] and there we are nursing our children…”. (Mother, CCU 13)

“We represent the vegetable garden, that is, what we, the indigenous women, grow: onions, tomatoes, or rather, everything […] and there we are nursing our children…”. (Mother, CCU 13)

There are also the Nasa cultural symbols related to BF, including water, trees, the spiral, and the chumbe. Water represents history, strength, and, above all, life, which in turn represents mother’s milk: water that flows through rivers like milk through a mother’s breasts (Fig. 3). A tree, in turn, is an essential element of nature and represents a mother’s breast and mammary gland anatomy. The tree’s branches are the ducts and lobules that carry nourishment to the babies.

Just as water and trees give life to nature and living beings, mother’s milk gives life and strength to infants, the community’s future leaders.

The chumbe is a profoundly symbolic Nasa item. It is a belt woven by women and essential during maternity for its multiple functions: mothers’ abdominal binder, front or back baby wrap carrier, and baby sling to hold the baby during BF, among others. When Nasa babies are born, they are wrapped in a chumbe woven by their mothers, and while they are wrapped, their mothers tell the story depicted in the chumbe and initiate the transition to BF (Fig. 4). In this way, the ancestral writing of the Nasa’s history is recovered from each woven figure. They believed wearing a chumbe protects the baby from evil spirits and goblins. In addition, children are girdled with a chumbe to strengthen their physical appearance, so they do not suffer from physical problems. The weaving of a chumbe implies wisdom and concentration and symbolizes strength, intelligence, and protection to the new generations for survival in time.

Lactar bonito (breastfeeding artfully) for Nasa’s survival

This category includes the meanings of the practice of BF to mothers, families, and the community. In principle, they consider BF a right that should be promoted and encouraged among all generations for the cultural survival of the Nasa people. Among the benefits of breast milk, mothers consider it to be the ideal food, to be given exclusively up to 6 months of age and accompanied by complementary foods up to 2 years of age, even at older ages:

“It’s essential up to six months; it’s pure milk. They say after six months it becomes complementary, and compotes, broths and those little things are included. The baby continues to suckle milk until he’s two years old. My doctor told me to do this until he [the baby] wants to.” (Mother, CCU 2).

“It’s essential up to six months; it’s pure milk. They say after six months it becomes complementary, and compotes, broths and those little things are included. The baby continues to suckle milk until he’s two years old. My doctor told me to do this until he [the baby] wants to.” (Mother, CCU 2).

The benefits of BF for both mother and baby are recognized from the moment of birth through skin-to-skin contact, which allows for proper adaptation to the outside world and strengthens the deep physical and emotional bond with the mother. This bond that begins at birth is maintained throughout life, forged with love and affection that creates security. Some of the care provided by the mothers includes speaking kind words to the babies, telling stories about their ancestors, and holding them in the chumbe they wove (Fig. 5), which conveys well-being and confidence.

Breastfeeding practices: Tensions between the traditional (cuido) and the Western (care)

Among Nasa people, BF as a natural process and social practice occurs amid tension between traditional and Western visions. Ancestral practices have traditional medicine and the Nasa cosmovision as a cross-cutting theme, an important aspect closely related to community empowerment. Medicinal plants have multiple functions in maternity care and parenting: protection, harmonization, healing, and prevention through baths and rituals.

“Our three of us got together and agreed that traditional medicine should always be present in the process of caring for and raising our babies, because it’s a beautiful process where we’re in balance with Mother Earth (Fig. 9). We also take care of our body; by using plants, we strengthen it.” (Mother, CCU 10).

“Our three of us got together and agreed that traditional medicine should always be present in the process of caring for and raising our babies, because it’s a beautiful process where we’re in balance with Mother Earth (Fig. 9). We also take care of our body; by using plants, we strengthen it.” (Mother, CCU 10).

Nasa women’s preparation for BF is a process that begins during pregnancy and involves cultural actors (midwives, grandmothers, and mothers), institutional actors (Colombian Institute of Family Welfare [ICBF]), and community programs (Semillas de Vida). They guide and support women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium, teaching and caring for the newborn.

“When our mothers prepared us by telling us that, when we have our babies, we have to feed them, and also my grandmother had told me that after six months I have to feed them this way and so on. And the program has also been there to encourage the mommies. Every month there is a meeting, and they explain to us how to take care of our babies and how to breastfeed our children.” (Mother, CCU 8).

“When our mothers prepared us by telling us that, when we have our babies, we have to feed them, and also my grandmother had told me that after six months I have to feed them this way and so on. And the program has also been there to encourage the mommies. Every month there is a meeting, and they explain to us how to take care of our babies and how to breastfeed our children.” (Mother, CCU 8).

In the puerperium (also known as “the diet”), special care is provided during the 40 days postpartum. During this time, the mother-child bond is strengthened, and the mother is cared for to prevent illness. The mother should stay at home and avoid ‘the chill’ (an indigenous illness not included in the classification of Western diseases), eat a special diet, and engage in some traditional health practices:

“Of course, because we’re weak afterward, so they tell us that it’s for the womb. I even think that when you have a baby, you have to get your belly rubbed so that the womb can be put back in place. Hot baths are for that very reason, so that you don’t take the ‘chill’, because when you become an old lady, you’ll suffer a lot. […] we women are more prone to getting sick because of the whole baby process.” (Mother, CCU 4).

“Of course, because we’re weak afterward, so they tell us that it’s for the womb. I even think that when you have a baby, you have to get your belly rubbed so that the womb can be put back in place. Hot baths are for that very reason, so that you don’t take the ‘chill’, because when you become an old lady, you’ll suffer a lot. […] we women are more prone to getting sick because of the whole baby process.” (Mother, CCU 4).

Most of the care or cuido of the diet focuses on the nutrition provided to mothers to initiate BF and, through them, nutrients to the babies. Within the recommended diet for this care, the use and consumption of plants and hot foods stand out, such as rue, leaves of arracacha, leaves of the orange tree, leaves of the mango tree, hens (preferably black-feathered), mutton, arracacha, garlic, and chocolate, among others. In addition, cold foods that may cause discomfort when consumed, such as onions, rice, cassava, avocado, cabbage, and citrus fruits, are avoided.

“Then she focuses more on the importance of the cuido or care that we indigenous women should have in our diet, also in the good food that we should consume before and after childbirth. Depending on how we feed ourselves, we can provide food for our baby during the diet.” (Mother, CCU 12).

“Then she focuses more on the importance of the cuido or care that we indigenous women should have in our diet, also in the good food that we should consume before and after childbirth. Depending on how we feed ourselves, we can provide food for our baby during the diet.” (Mother, CCU 12).

Some practices to improve milk production include ritual washing, massaging, or drinking beverages derived from medicinal plants, animals (fish or crabs), or objects (combs) that help improve breast milk production:

“That’s why they do the rituals. For breastfeeding, when they just give birth and go on the diet, they don’t get milk letdown for the first few days. What’s recommended is to make washes with papaya or pumpkin leaves, they wash themselves with the buds and start making their washes or they put roasted salt in chocolate or in broths, and always, until they come out of the diet. The main thing they give her is broth so that the baby has plenty of breast milk.” (Mother, CCU 9).

What’s recommended is to make washes with papaya or pumpkin leaves, they wash themselves with the buds and start making their washes or they put roasted salt in chocolate or in broths, and always, until they come out of the diet. The main thing they give her is broth so that the baby has plenty of breast milk.” (Mother, CCU 9).

When the mother’s diet ends, food is not restricted; the better the mother feeds, the better the quality of milk she produces. Therefore, a mother often consumes some foods to favor this process, including chocolate, agua panela (panela drink), chayote squash, mote, arracacha, and chicken or crab broth. Fluid intake is increased so that breast milk is always plentiful. However, when milk is abundant and a baby does not consume it, there are practices such as extraction and disposal in running waters. Breast milk is not stored or exposed to animals. These practices keep the flow of breast milk plentiful and healthy for a baby.

“To give good breast milk to children, women need to feed well, because if they do not feed well, their milk production decreases. You need to make an effort and eat right so that there’s a greater amount of milk production.” (Mother, CCU 2).

“To give good breast milk to children, women need to feed well, because if they do not feed well, their milk production decreases. You need to make an effort and eat right so that there’s a greater amount of milk production.” (Mother, CCU 2).

In addition, alternative uses of breast milk for infant care have been identified, including bathing, nasal irrigation for colds, ophthalmic use to reduce yellowing of the sclera (white part of the eye), and post-vaccination use.

Despite the persistence of ancestral practices, tensions with Western practices are evident. Prenatal visits expose pregnant women to Western teachings on BF, including recommended BF positions, duration of EBF, and introduction to complementary feeding, all of which are reinforced by the Semillas de Vida program. At the same time, some Nasa mothers also turn to Western medicine, for instance, to suppress lactation when BF becomes painful.

“It [nipple] cracked and a piece fell off and I couldn’t stand it. I couldn’t stand it, and I didn’t give him milk. When I was like that, I went to the drugstore and got a shot to dry up the milk.” (Mother, CCU 12).

Discussion

According to the results, BF is positioned as an identity and socio-historical practice, given its contribution to the transmission of memory, stories, values, and ways of life of different indigenous people. International organizations such as the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) place a high value on traditional foods, recognizing them as a nutritional strategy of incalculable benefit to these people [19].

This study shows that mothers recognize breast milk as the best nutritional option for infants, which is consistent with the recommendations of the WHO and ECLAC reports [19]. These findings are in line with studies conducted in Ecuador and Guatemala, where approximately 70% of indigenous mothers breastfeed, a higher percentage compared to non-indigenous mothers (40%). It is noteworthy that indigenous mothers continue to breastfeed their children between six and nine months, with 9 out of 10 indigenous mothers breastfeeding their children up to 15 months, and more than half up to 23 months, showing between 10% and 41% higher rates of BF respectively than non-indigenous mothers [19]. In these contexts, BF does not require training, as it is learned through observation of other women in the family or community and the advice from midwives and community elders [20].

BF is seen as a fundamental practice for the survival of the Nasa culture, allowing for the transmission of knowledge and the upbringing of future community leaders. It is also seen as the spiritual protection of the child provided by human nature. Knowledge is transmitted from the womb through rituals at every moment of life and integrates the mother tongue (Nasa Yuwe) throughout the stages of sowing (conception), germination (birth), and maturation of the seeds (sons and daughters) [20].

Strengthening BF is a protective aspect against child malnutrition, and the role of women stands out as a protagonist in ethnic communities [13]. As part of the food practices for the survival of indigenous knowledge of families and descendants, BF is important in different domestic scenarios, including the home, the tulpa, and the tul (vegetable garden) for preserving ancestral traditions.

In the context of weakening traditional practices [21], community programs such as Semillas de Vida, within the framework of autonomy and self-determination, support BF by promoting traditional practices such as sowing the navel and protection with the chumbe. Other experiences emphasize that once the seed has germinated (baby’s birth), the midwife cuts the umbilical cord and sows it in the earth, inside the house, near the tulpa, or outside in the tul [20], to connect children with the earth, to promote attachment, rooting, and a desire to cultivate it.

Under the Nasa cosmovision, water gives life. It nourishes nature just as breast milk provides the necessary nutrients for the healthy growth and development of a baby physically and psychologically [13, 22]. Breast milk provides strength and guidance and strengthens the mother-child bond in several indigenous people [1, 13, 22, 23] and the general population [1–3, 24, 25]. Semillas de Vida program and the indigenous health care provider through indigenous health staff promote EBF up to six months of age to favor the healthy development of the children, recognizing the EBF benefits [1, 3, 24]. Likewise, international societies and consensuses specializing in pediatric complementary feeding practices recommend introducing foods other than breast milk or formula at four months of age and not earlier [26, 27]. Doing it earlier carries physiological consequences such as kidney damage, immunobiological immaturity, risk of chronic degenerative diseases, and risk of food allergy [28].

Finally, the close relationship between BF, indigenous women, and the care of Mother Earth is highlighted. BF is thus framed as a sustainable cultural practice and a form of resistance to the Western worldview and hegemonic economic systems that accelerate environmental degradation through the overexploitation of natural resources [29].

Limitations

As with any qualitative study, certain limitations must be acknowledged. In this case, the research team’s limited proficiency in the Nasa Yuwe language represented a challenge; however, this was mitigated by the support of community members who acted as interpreters, ensuring the reliability of the information obtained.

Conclusions

BF under the Nasa cosmovision places the woman at the center, and not only the feeding of the child but also the survival of the Nasa culture depends on her. By BF, the Nasa woman establishes a connection and a cycle of interdependence between her own care, that of the child, and that of Mother Earth.

The cultural practice of BF plays a fundamental role in transmitting knowledge, wisdom, and stories, fostering identity and cultural rootedness across generations. BF takes place in specific spaces and incorporates important symbolic elements such as home, tulpa, tul, rivers, trees, the spiral, and the chumbe, which revitalize and strengthen women’s connection to Mother Earth and position BF as a vital natural practice for the survival of the Nasa people.

Lactar bonito integrates knowledge, feelings, practices, and different family and community actors who prepare for the care and protection of the mother and the new Nasa being. This implies the revival of food and ancestral culture as an indigenous people to enjoy buen vivir (good living).

For the Nasa people, BF is a natural process that takes place in the tension between traditional and Western practices. Traditional practices, from pregnancy to the puerperium, are mediated by traditional medicine and the Nasa cosmovision. Such practices aim to improve breast milk production/overproduction, infant feeding and nutrition, and some alternative uses of breast milk. In this sense, synergies are identified between traditional and Western practices that promote BF, but also tensions and risks, such as the use of medications to treat BF problems, which compromises the Nasa ways of life and survival.

Using participatory research methods and alternative techniques, such as photovoice with communities and indigenous peoples, facilitates information collection and allows for identifying community perceptions on health issues such as BF.

It is recommended that community interventions to promote BF be considered to control morbidity due to infectious agents and reduce malnutrition levels in the infant population. These interventions should promote EBF up to six months of age and complementary feeding up to two years, based on the integration of the Nasa cosmovision, traditional medicine, and family and community members, and in coordination with community programs. The goal is to strengthen ancestral knowledge and practices for the buen vivir (good living) and cultural survival of the Nasa indigenous people.
