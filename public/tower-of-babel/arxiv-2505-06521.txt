Implementing Errors on Errors: Bayesian vs Frequentist
Satoshi Mishima, Kin-ya Oda

Abstract
When combining apparently inconsistent experimental results, one often implements errors on errors. The Particle Data Group’s phenomenological prescription offers a practical solution but lacks a firm theoretical foundation. To address this, D’Agostini and Cowan have proposed Bayesian and frequentist approaches, respectively, both introducing gamma-distributed auxiliary variables to model uncertainty in quoted errors. In this Letter, we show that these two formulations admit a parameter-by-parameter correspondence, and are structurally equivalent. This identification clarifies how Bayesian prior choices can be interpreted in terms of frequentist sampling assumptions, providing a unified probabilistic framework for modeling uncertainty in quoted variances.

Introduction

The combination of inconsistent experimental results is a recurring challenge in particle physics, particularly when managing and interpreting uncertainties. This issue arises when discrepancies between experimental data exceed the quoted statistical and systematic uncertainties, necessitating the implementation of additional uncertainty corrections.

The Particle Data Group (PDG) addresses this by introducing a phenomenological scale factor applied to the combined uncertainty:

\displaystyle S:=\max\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left(1,\,\sqrt{\chi^{2}\over N-1}\right),

(1)

where
N
denotes the number of experimental results being combined and
\chi^{2}
is the chi-squared value for the combination
[
1
]
. While this method is practical and widely used, it lacks a clear theoretical foundation, leaving room for more principled statistical treatments.

In Refs.
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
, D’Agostini and Cowan independently proposed approaches to address the so-called “errors on errors”—uncertainties in the quoted systematic uncertainties—based on Bayesian and frequentist inference, respectively. Both methods extend the standard profile likelihood by introducing auxiliary variables to model the uncertainty in the quoted errors, typically using gamma distributions.

While these ideas are individually known, the structural correspondence between the Bayesian and frequentist formulations has not been explicitly established in the literature.
In this Letter, we show that the two approaches can in fact be mapped onto each other parameter by parameter. In particular, we demonstrate that a specific parameter choice in the Bayesian prior—often presented as an ad hoc recommendation—can be naturally understood as arising from a frequentist sampling model involving virtual repetitions of each experiment. This correspondence not only clarifies the origin of the so-called “democratic skepticism” advocated by D’Agostini, but also provides a concrete rationale for adopting symmetric gamma priors with shape and rate parameters set equal.

Our aim is not to introduce a new statistical model, but rather to clarify, connect, and unify existing approaches by revealing their structural equivalence.
By identifying a parameter-by-parameter correspondence between the Bayesian and frequentist formulations, we provide a coherent probabilistic framework for modeling uncertainty in quoted variances.
This perspective helps elucidate the rationale behind specific prior choices in Bayesian methods and may provide useful insight into empirical practices such as uncertainty rescaling in data combinations.

3.1
Cowan’s sampling scheme

In the Bayesian approach, the “true value”
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\mu}}
is treated as a
theoretical
random variable that follows a prior distribution
f_{\circ}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\mu}\right)
and is updated to a posterior distribution
f\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\mu}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\boldsymbol{d}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\boldsymbol{s}}\right)
upon observing data.
In contrast, the frequentist approach considers
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\mu}
to be the fixed “true value”, while the observed variables fluctuate around it according to Eq. (
5
).

To handle the “errors on errors” within the frequentist framework, Cowan provides a way to treat each estimated variance
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}
as an
experimental
random variable fluctuating around a so-to-say “true variance”
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}
.
To implement this idea, Cowan introduces
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
(virtual) repetitions for each real experiment
i
, resulting in
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
random variables for each:
7
7

7

In principle,
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
may vary across experiments, resulting in an
i
-dependent
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n_{i}}
. However, this dependence is typically neglected in practice
[
5
,
6
]
, corresponding to D’Agostini’s notion of democratic skepticism; see footnote
4
. Here we also omit the
i
-dependence, as it can be trivially recovered if needed.

\displaystyle\Set{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{d}_{i\,\textsf{j}}}}_{\textsf{j}=1,\dots,{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}}.

(33)

These are assumed to follow normal distributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}d_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\mu},{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}\right).

(34)

Here and hereafter, the Roman typewriter-style index
j
runs from 1 to
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
, indexing the virtual repetitions associated with each real experiment
i
.
The sample mean and the unbiased sample variance of these outcomes are defined as usual:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\overline{\hat{d}_{i}}}

\displaystyle:={1\over{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}}\sum_{\textsf{j}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{d}_{i\,\textsf{j}}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}

\displaystyle:={1\over{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1}\sum_{\textsf{j}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{d}_{i\,\textsf{j}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\overline{\hat{d}_{i}}}\right)^{2}.

(35)

Given the normal distributions (
34
), the random variable

{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}

follows a chi-squared distribution with
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1
degrees of freedom, which then can be written in terms of the gamma distribution (
15
) as:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2},{1\over 2}\right).

(36)

That is, the PDF for the variance is obtained from the probability elements as

\displaystyle p\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}\right)\text{d}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}

\displaystyle=g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2},{1\over 2}\right)\text{d}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2},{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}\right)\text{d}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}},

(37)

where we used the scaling property (
16
).
It follows that

\displaystyle\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}},

\displaystyle\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle={2\over{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\over{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1}\,{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}.

(38)

3.2
Errors-on-errors parameter

Cowan defines what is called the errors-on-errors parameter:
8
8

8

{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon}
used in Ref.
[
7

{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}r}
in Ref.

3

{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}r_{i}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:={1\over 2}{\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]\over\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]}={1\over\sqrt{2\left({\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\right)}}.

(39)

The spirit behind this definition can be explained as follows
[
3
]
:
For
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}}:={\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}

\Delta{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\simeq 2{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}s_{i}}\Delta{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}s_{i}}

Cowan then expects the analogous relation between the standard deviations:

\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]\simeq 2\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]

If one were to approximate

\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]\sim\E^{2}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]
,
then one would vaguely expect
9
9

9

Note, however, that this naive treatment leads to
\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]\sim 0
, implying
\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]\sim 0
.
This limitation can also be seen more explicitly: It suggests that

{1\over 2}{\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]\over\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Braket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}^{4}}}}{\Braket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}}}^{2}}-1}

{\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]\over\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]}=\sqrt{\frac{\Braket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}^{2}}}}{\Braket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}}^{2}}-1},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon}\sim{\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]\over\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{s}_{i}}\right]}.

(40)

In this sense,
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon}

Regardless of how it was initially motivated, the errors-on-errors parameter (

{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
in terms of an intuitive measure of relative uncertainty:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{2{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(41)

The resultant PDF reads

\displaystyle p\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}},1+{1\over 2{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{1\over 4{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}},{1\over 4{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}\right),

(42)

and the variance and standard deviation of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}

\displaystyle\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=4{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]=\sqrt{\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\right]}

\displaystyle=2{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}.

(43)

4
Comparison between D’Agostini and Cowan

This section presents the main findings of the Letter. We compare D’Agostini’s Bayesian and Cowan’s frequentist approaches to uncertainty in quoted variances, focusing on the structure of their respective probabilistic formulations.

D’Agostini’s Bayesian approach treats
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}s_{i}^{2}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}
as the experimental deterministic value and the
theoretical
random variable, respectively, whereas Cowan’s frequentist approach treats
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}
as the
experimental
random variable and the deterministic theoretical value.

Here we point out, on physical grounds, that the ratio
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}s_{i}^{2}}/{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}
corresponds to the ratio
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}/{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}
:

\displaystyle\underset{\text{D'Agostini's Bayesian}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\omega}_{i}}={1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{r}_{i}^{2}}}={{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}s_{i}^{2}}\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{correspond}}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\underset{\text{Cowan's frequentist}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\hat{w}_{i}}\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}.

(44)

In Cowan’s Eq. (
36
) or (
37
), the probability element reads

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\right)\text{d}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2},{{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2}\right)\text{d}{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}w_{i}}\over{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}v_{i}}},

(45)

where we have again used the scaling property (
16
).
This can be directly compared with D’Agostini’s gamma distribution in Eq. (
15
):

\displaystyle g\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\omega_{i}}\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}\middle|\mathchoice{\;}{\;}{\,}{\,}{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k},{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}\right)\text{d}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\omega_{i}}.

(46)

The shape and rate parameters can thus be identified as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}={{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\over 2}={1\over 4{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(47)

We see that the choice
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}
, as suggested in Eq. (
25
), provides a better match with the result from Cowan’s frequentist approach.
10
10

10

Cowan also touches on related Bayesian approaches in his talk
[
8
]
, noting that choosing
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}
yields a gamma distribution with unit expectation and adjustable variance.
His reference to the inverse gamma distribution, in comparison with D’Agostini’s approach, suggests a perspective not fully aligned with the formulation considered here, which relies exclusively on the gamma distribution.

The corresponding PDFs are shown in Fig.

5
Summary and discussion

In this Letter, we have clarified the structural correspondence between D’Agostini’s Bayesian and Cowan’s frequentist approaches to implementing errors on errors. Both methods enhance the conventional treatment of experimental uncertainties by modeling auxiliary random variables with gamma distributions. Although their starting points differ—Bayesian priors versus frequentist sampling models—we have established a parameter-by-parameter mapping between them.

In particular, we have demonstrated that the Bayesian assumption of a symmetric gamma prior with shape and rate parameters
\left({\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k},{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}\right)
corresponds naturally to Cowan’s frequentist model with
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}
virtual repetitions, where
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}=\left({\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}n}-1\right)/2=1/4{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\varepsilon^{2}}
. This identification provides a theoretical rationale for the prior choice
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}
, ensuring
\E\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\omega}_{i}}\right]=1
and
\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\omega}_{i}}\right]=1/{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}
, and provides a basis for choosing specific values such as
{\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}k}={\color[rgb]{0.03,1,0.25}\lambda}=1
, which satisfy
\V\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\omega}_{i}}\right]=\E^{2}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0.2,0}\hat{\omega}_{i}}\right]
as our version of D’Agostini’s recommendation.

Our aim has not been to propose a new statistical model, but rather to unify existing approaches by revealing their underlying equivalence. This correspondence offers a principled perspective that sheds light on empirical procedures such as uncertainty rescaling, and clarifies how specific prior choices in Bayesian methods can be interpreted in terms of frequentist assumptions about sampling variability.

We hope that the parameter-by-parameter identification presented here serves to bridge the conceptual gap between Bayesian and frequentist perspectives, and provides a coherent probabilistic framework that may serve as a guiding principle for future work involving uncertainty modeling and data combination strategies.
