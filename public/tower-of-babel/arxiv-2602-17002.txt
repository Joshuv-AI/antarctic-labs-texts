The Kinematics and Dynamics Theories of a Total Lagrangian Finite Element Analysis Framework for Finite Deformation Multibody Dynamics
Zhenhao Zhou, Ganesh Arivoli, Dan Negrut

Abstract
This work presents a Total Lagrangian (TL) finite element formulation for deformable body dynamics. We employ the TL-FEA framework to simulate the time evolution of collections of bodies whose motion is constrained by kinematic constraints and which mutually interact through contact and friction. These bodies experience large displacements, large deformations, and large rotations. A systematic approach is proposed for classifying and posing kinematic constraints acting between the bodies present in the system. We derive the governing equations for ANCF beam, ANCF shell, and tetrahedral elements, and present hyperelastic material models including St. Venant–Kirchhoff and Mooney–Rivlin formulations with their corresponding internal force contributions and consistent tangent stiffness matrices. A finite-strain Kelvin–Voigt viscous damping model is incorporated in the TL-FEA formulation for numerical stability.

1
Background and Related Work

Large-deformation deformable-body dynamics can be posed naturally within a continuum mechanics setting by describing kinematics through the deformation map
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{X},t)
and its associated deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)=\partial\mathbf{r}/\partial\mathbf{X}
. In a Total Lagrangian (TL) formulation, all strain measures, stress measures, and constitutive responses are expressed with respect to the reference configuration, leading to a conceptually clean separation between geometry (reference domain), kinematics (through
\mathbf{F}
and its invariants), and material response (through a strain energy density and/or rate-dependent extensions). This deformation-gradient-centric viewpoint is standard in nonlinear finite elements for solids and structures and provides a universal interface for accommodating hyperelasticity, finite-strain viscous regularization, and multi-field extensions while retaining a consistent linearization and tangent structure
Bonet2008nonlinear
.

Here,
\mathbf{X}
denotes generic reference/material coordinates. In element-level derivations, we parameterize the reference domain with local coordinates
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
, so that locally
\mathbf{X}\equiv\mathbf{u}
.

Within flexible multibody dynamics, the Absolute Nodal Coordinate Formulation (ANCF) introduced by Shabana was a key step toward combining large rigid-body motion with large deformation in a non-incremental finite-element setting
Shabana1996
. The defining feature of ANCF is the choice of nodal coordinates: instead of interpolating rotations, ANCF employs absolute position vectors and their spatial gradients as nodal degrees of freedom. This coordinate selection yields a polynomial representation of the position field that remains valid under arbitrarily large rotations and deformations, and it enables a constant (configuration-independent) consistent mass matrix for many element families
Shabana1998
. In Shabana’s original presentation and in the standard ANCF literature that followed, the position field is written in the familiar FE form
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{e}(t)
, where
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{X})
is a block-structured shape-function matrix and
\mathbf{e}(t)
stacks nodal positions and slopes into a generalized coordinate vector
shabanaYakoub2001Part1
;
Shabana2023Overview
;
RecueroNegrut2016ANCF
.

A large body of work has since expanded ANCF element technology and clarified its modeling tradeoffs. Early developments addressed the construction of beam and cable elements that admit large axial and bending deformation while capturing geometrically nonlinear coupling effects without resorting to rotation interpolation
berzeri2000
. Subsequent research introduced shear-deformable and higher-order elements and investigated numerical pathologies such as shear and membrane locking, together with formulation-level remedies that retain ANCF kinematics while improving element accuracy and robustness
GarciaVallejo2007LockingFree
. Comprehensive surveys document these developments and highlight a recurring theme: although ANCF elements come in many variants (beams, plates/shells, solid-like continua), their kinematics can be consistently viewed as a continuum mapping whose primary computational artifact is the deformation gradient and its derived strain measures
Gerstmayr2013
;
Otsuka2022RecentAdvances
;
Shabana2023Overview
.

The present work is built on this lineage but reorganizes the kinematic interpolation to make the deformation-gradient structure explicit. Rather than embedding the spatial basis in a shape-function matrix
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{X})
acting on a generalized-coordinate vector, we express the position field as
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{N}(t)\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X})
, where
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X})
collects scalar basis functions and
\mathbf{N}(t)
stores the nodal unknowns in a matrix form. This representation is algebraically equivalent to the classical ANCF interpolation (it corresponds to a different arrangement of the same degrees of freedom), yet it changes where the shape functions “live” and, crucially, yields the factorization

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)=\mathbf{N}(t)\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{X})
with
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{X})=\partial\mathbf{s}/\partial\mathbf{X}
.
As a result, element kinematics, stress measures, and internal force contributions can be written uniformly in terms of
\mathbf{F}
(and, when needed,
\dot{\mathbf{F}}
), which aligns the ANCF description with standard TL continuum mechanics notation and facilitates a unified derivation across element topologies and material models
Bonet2008nonlinear
;
Shabana2023Overview
.

From the standpoint of formulation comparison, the deformation-gradient emphasis is consistent with the direction of modern ANCF literature: reviews stress that many apparent differences between ANCF element variants ultimately reduce to (i) the choice of interpolation spaces for
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{X},t)
and its gradients, (ii) the induced strain measures and their linearizations, and (iii) the treatment of cross-section deformation assumptions (e.g., Euler–Bernoulli, Timoshenko-like, or fully continuum)
Gerstmayr2013
;
Otsuka2022RecentAdvances
. By casting all element expressions through
\mathbf{F}
in a TL setting, the present framework is positioned as a universal, element-agnostic formulation layer: beams, shells, and solid elements differ primarily through their basis functions and reference-domain parameterizations, while the constitutive and variational machinery remains shared.

2
Formulation for Deformable Elements

In handling the time evolution of deformable bodies that experience large displacements, large deformations, and large rotations, one can use the Total Lagrangian (TL) or the Updated Lagrangian (UL) approaches. Here, we embrace the former, a methodology that is approximately five decades old
zienkiewicz-taylor-FEA2005
. Our goal is to establish a general TL-FEA framework that can naturally handle complex multibody systems, i.e., one can have multiple bodies, their motion can be constrained by kinematic constraints, and they can mutually interact through contact and friction.

Throughout,
\mathbf{X}
denotes generic reference/material coordinates. Within an element, we parameterize the reference domain by local coordinates
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
, so that locally
\mathbf{X}\equiv\mathbf{u}
.

2.1
Key Element Configurations

The elements discussed here are volumetric elements. The nodal unknowns can be either positions only, or, just as in ANCF
shabana01
, they can be a combination of positions and position gradients.

Figure 1:
Key element configurations.

The reference configuration of an element is that in which the element experiences no strain and no stress. Typically, this is the undeformed configuration of the element, as produced by a mesher, e.g.,
Gmsh

GeuzaineRemacle2009Gmsh
or
TetGen

Si2015TetGen
. Associated with this reference configuration there is a reference frame
Ouvw
, whose position and orientation are fixed in space. A point
P
in the reference configuration of coordinates
(u,v,w)
is mapped at time
t
into a different point, which has coordinates
\mathbf{r}(u,v,w;t)=[x(u,v,w;t),y(u,v,w;t),z(u,v,w;t)]^{T}
in a global reference frame
Oxyz
. In this work, the deformation map is represented by the position field
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)
, where
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
. The initial configuration is the configuration of the element at time
t=0
. Finally, the parent (or canonical) configuration is used to carry out the numerical integration over the volume of the element. The elements can be iso-parametric, or, for simple cases, the mapping between the parent and the reference configuration can be an affine transformation.

2.2
Interpolation Across the Element

Since the deformation map cannot be produced analytically, the FEA method uses an interpolation approach to approximate a field variable using the nodal values. Here, the “field variable” of interest is the position
\mathbf{r}
of a point that in the reference configuration is at coordinates
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
. The interpolation is carried out using the matrix of nodal unknowns
\mathbf{N}(t)=[\mathbf{e}_{1}(t),\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n_{u}}(t)]\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times n_{u}}
and the array of shape functions
\mathbf{s}(u,v,w)=[s_{1}(u,v,w),\ldots,s_{n_{u}}(u,v,w)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(u,v,w;t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{N}(t)\,\mathbf{s}(u,v,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\mathbf{e}_{i}(t)\,s_{i}(u,v,w)\;.

This leads to
\dot{\mathbf{r}}(u,v,w;t)=\dot{\mathbf{N}}(t)\,\mathbf{s}(u,v,w)
,
\ddot{\mathbf{r}}(u,v,w;t)=\ddot{\mathbf{N}}(t)\,\mathbf{s}(u,v,w)
, and the deformation gradient

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{N}(t)\,\mathbf{H}(u,v,w)

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\mathbf{e}_{i}(t)\,\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}(u,v,w)\;,

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}(u,v,w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial s_{i}(u,v,w)}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\;,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(u,v,w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial\mathbf{s}(u,v,w)}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\;.

3
Augmented Lagrangian Formulation

Start by assuming the position-level kinematic constraint equations read:

c_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{n+1},\mathbf{A}_{n+1},t_{n+1})=0\;,\quad i=1,2,\ldots,m\;,

(12)

and they combine to form the vector of constraints
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}_{n+1},\mathbf{A}_{n+1},t_{n+1})=\mathbf{0}
.
For compactness, define
c_{i}^{n+1}\coloneqq c_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{n+1},\mathbf{A}_{n+1},t_{n+1})
.
ALM starts with the Lagrangian function:

\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\rho}}(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1},\bar{\bm{\omega}}_{n+1},\bm{\lambda})=\ell(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1},\bar{\bm{\omega}}_{n+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Bigl[\lambda_{i}c_{i}^{n+1}+\frac{\rho_{i}}{2}\left(c_{i}^{n+1}\right)^{2}\Bigr]\;,

(13)

where
\rho_{i}
are penalty parameters that control the weight of the quadratic penalty term, and
\lambda_{i}
are the Lagrange multipliers, i.e., the dual variables. The constraint functions
c_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{n+1},\mathbf{A}_{n+1},t_{n+1})
are evaluated at the position level and depend implicitly on the optimization variables
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1}
and
\bar{\bm{\omega}}_{n+1}
through the integration scheme used to compute the updated position
\mathbf{r}_{n+1}
and orientation
\mathbf{A}_{n+1}
.

The optimization approach, either first-order or second-order, can be used to minimize the cost functions in
Eqs.
13
and
14
.
For the velocity level approach to solving the constrained multibody dynamics problem, see Eq.
14
, the constraints are abstracted as

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}\;,\quad\text{where }\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1}\\
\bar{\bm{\omega}}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}\;.

The Augmented Lagrangian for this problem is

\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\rho}}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})=\ell(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[\lambda_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\rho_{i}}{2}\tilde{\nu}_{i}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right]\;,

(14)

where
\ell(\mathbf{x})
is the cost function,
\lambda_{i}
are the Lagrange multipliers, and
\rho_{i}>0
are penalty weights associated with each constraint.

In the proposed framework for solving the Total Lagrangian finite element analysis for multibody dynamics, we derive the equations of motion from the principle of virtual work.
The total virtual work is written as:

\delta W=\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}+\delta W_{\mathrm{applied}}+\delta W_{\mathrm{force\text{-}field}}+\delta W_{\mathrm{internal}}\;,

(15)

where
\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}
,
\delta W_{\mathrm{applied}}
,
\delta W_{\mathrm{force\text{-}field}}
, and
\delta W_{\mathrm{internal}}
denote the virtual works of the inertial terms, applied forces, mass-distributed force fields, and internal forces, respectively.
Imposing
\delta W=0
and factoring out the virtual generalized displacements yields an expression of the form:

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{q}_{i}^{T}\,\mathbf{g}_{i}=0\;,

(16)

where
\delta\mathbf{q}_{i}
is an arbitrary virtual displacement associated with the
i
th nodal unknown and
\mathbf{g}_{i}
is the corresponding residual contribution.
Since
\delta\mathbf{q}_{i}
are arbitrary, Eq. (
16
) implies
\mathbf{g}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
for all
i
, which yields the discrete equations of motion.

In the following sections, we derive the expressions for all contribution terms that make up Eq. (
15
). The inertial term is derived in Section
4
. The mass-distributed force fields are discussed in Section
5
, and the internal forces are discussed in Section
6
. Finally, the applied forces, which in the majority of problems come from friction and contact and external applied force loads, are discussed in Section
7
.

With the update
\mathbf{q}_{n+1}=\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\,\mathbf{v}_{n+1}
, the residual can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{n})+\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ff}}

(17)

\displaystyle+h\,\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})^{T}\bigl(\bm{\lambda}+\rho\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})\bigr)\;,

where
\mathbf{M}
is a preassembled and constant mass matrix of the dynamic system,
\rho
is the ALM penalty parameter, and
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})=0
denotes bilateral constraints with constraint Jacobian
\mathbf{C}_{q}=\partial\mathbf{c}/\partial\mathbf{q}
. Note that
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a constraint-space quantity and is mapped to the generalized space through
\mathbf{C}_{q}(\cdot)^{T}
in Eq. (
17
). We evaluate
\mathbf{C}_{q}^{T}(\cdot)
via a sparse representation of
\mathbf{C}_{q}^{T}
, and enforce constraints with an augmented-Lagrangian outer loop that alternates an inner solve for
\mathbf{v}
with multiplier updates until
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}
meets a prescribed tolerance.

In Eq. (
17
),
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})
denotes the assembled internal force vector induced by material deformation (including elastic and viscous contributions), while
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}
collects applied loads specified directly as external nodal forces (including point loads and contact/friction forces treated as external). The term
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ff}}
accounts for mass-distributed force fields (body forces) specified per unit mass, such as gravity; although it can be absorbed into
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}
, we keep it separate to emphasize its origin in the force-field virtual work component in Eq. (
15
).

The residual form in Eq. (
17
) can be interpreted as the first-order optimality condition of a scalar augmented objective in the velocity unknown.
Introduce the step map
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{v})\coloneqq\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\,\mathbf{v}
, and assume that the internal force operator admits an incremental potential
\Pi_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\!\bigl(\frac{1}{h}\Pi_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})\bigr)=\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})
.
Then the residual
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})
is obtained by differentiating the discrete augmented Lagrangian cost:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h}\,(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{n})^{T}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{n})

(18)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h}\,\Pi_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{T}\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ff}}^{T}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle+\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\,\bigl\|\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

Using
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})=h\,\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v})
and the chain rule, one verifies that

\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\Phi_{\rho}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})
.
Consequently, for fixed multipliers
\bm{\lambda}
, the inner solve
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v},\bm{\lambda})=\mathbf{0}
is equivalent to enforcing stationarity of the augmented cost (
18
) with respect to
\mathbf{v}
, while the outer ALM loop updates
\bm{\lambda}
to reduce the constraint violation
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}
.

4
Inertia Force Contribution in TL-FEA Formulation

The inertia contribution in Eq. (
15
) is

\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}=-\int_{V_{r}}\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})\,\delta\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)^{T}\ddot{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{u};t)\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}\;,

(19)

where
\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})
is the mass density expressed in the reference configuration and
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
are the material coordinates.
Using the TL interpolation
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\mathbf{e}_{i}(t)s_{i}(\mathbf{u})
, we obtain

\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}m_{ij}\,\ddot{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\;,\qquad m_{ij}=\int_{V_{r}}\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})\,s_{i}(\mathbf{u})\,s_{j}(\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}\;.

(20)

The coefficients
m_{ij}=m_{ji}
depend only on reference quantities (
\rho_{r}
and the shape functions), hence they are constant and can be precomputed.

It is convenient to introduce the element unknown vector

\mathbf{e}=[\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n_{u}}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n_{u}}\;.

Equation (
20
) can then be written as

\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}=-\delta\mathbf{e}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{e}\,\ddot{\mathbf{e}},\qquad\mathbf{M}_{e}=\bigl[m_{ij}\mathbf{I}_{3}\bigr]_{i,j=1}^{n_{u}}\;.

(21)

After standard FE assembly over all elements, Eq. (
21
) yields the global inertial virtual work

\delta W_{\mathrm{inertia}}=-\delta\mathbf{q}^{T}\mathbf{M}\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}}
, where
\mathbf{M}
is the global consistent mass matrix.
In particular, under the backward-Euler update used to obtain Eq. (
17
),
\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{n+1}=\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{n+1}\approx(\mathbf{v}_{n+1}-\mathbf{v}_{n})/h
, which leads to the inertial residual contribution
\frac{1}{h}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{n})
in Eq. (
17
).

5
Mass-Distributed Force Field Contribution in TL-FEA Formulation

The force-field component
\delta W_{\mathrm{force\text{-}field}}
in Eq. (
15
) accounts for mass-distributed loads specified as a force per unit mass.
Let
\mathbf{b}(P,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote such a field (e.g., gravity) evaluated at the current spatial position
P=\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)
of a material point with reference coordinates
\mathbf{u}=[u,v,w]^{T}
.
Its virtual work over a reference volume
V_{r}
is

\delta W_{\mathrm{force\text{-}field}}=\int_{V_{r}}\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})\,\delta\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)^{T}\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t),t)\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}\;,

(22)

where
\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})
is the reference density.
Using
\delta\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}s_{i}(\mathbf{u})
, Eq. (
22
) becomes

\delta W_{\mathrm{force\text{-}field}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i},\qquad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i}\coloneqq\int_{V_{r}}\rho_{r}(\mathbf{u})\,s_{i}(\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t),t)\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(23)

After element assembly, the force-field contribution is collected in
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ff}}
in Eq. (
17
).

If
\rho_{r}
is constant and the field is uniform,
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t),t)\equiv\mathbf{b}_{0}
, then

\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ff}}_{i}=\rho_{r}\,\mathbf{b}_{0}\,v_{i},\qquad v_{i}\coloneqq\int_{V_{r}}s_{i}(\mathbf{u})\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}\;.

(24)

Note that, although the integration is carried out over
V_{r}
, the field must be evaluated at the current position
P=\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)
; consequently,
\mathbf{b}
can vary in time as the body moves through space.

6
Internal Force Contribution in TL-FEA Formulation

The internal force contribution to the equations of motion (EOM) in the Total Lagrangian finite element analysis (TL-FEA) formulation arises from the material’s constitutive response to deformation. In the proposed framework, the internal forces are the result of two major components: the hyperelastic material response and the viscous damping effect. The former captures the elastic behavior of the material under finite deformation, while the latter provides numerical stability and models energy dissipation during dynamic simulations.

6.1
Hyperelastic Material Laws

6.1.1
Generic TL internal force expression

The contributions of the internal forces to the EOM for the ANCF beam element and the ANCF 3443 shell element are discussed and derived as follows:

Following the notation used in Eq.
2a
, the deformation gradient for the ANCF 3243 element is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{N}(t)\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{s}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{N}(t)\cdot\left[\begin{smallmatrix}s_{1,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{2,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{3,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{4,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{5,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{6,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{7,\mathbf{u}}\\
s_{8,\mathbf{u}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\mathbf{e}_{1}s_{1,\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{e}_{2}s_{2,\mathbf{u}}+\ldots+\mathbf{e}_{8}s_{8,\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\mathbf{e}_{i}s_{i,\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\;.

Following the notation used earlier, the deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}
for the 3443 shell element is:

\mathbf{F}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{16}\mathbf{e}_{i}\,s_{i,\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

where each
s_{i,\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 3}
.

6.1.2
St Venant–Kirchhoff (SVK) material

The Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff (SVK) material model is a fundamental hyperelastic constitutive model in finite element analysis (FEA), particularly suited to the total Lagrangian formulation, where it employs the Green–Lagrange strain tensor and the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress to capture moderate nonlinear deformations in compressible solids
Bonet2008nonlinear
. This model extends classical linear elasticity to finite strains by defining a quadratic strain energy density function
\Psi(\mathbf{E})
(see (
25
)), with
\lambda
and
\mu
denoting the Lamé constants, making it computationally straightforward for quasi-static and dynamic simulations of structures undergoing large rotations but limited stretches
Bonet2022limitations
. Popular in computational mechanics for its simplicity and compatibility with isoparametric elements, the SVK model finds broad use in beam and shell analyses, such as Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beams, biological soft tissues, and multifunctional laminates, although its popularity wanes in extreme compression due to unphysical softening beyond about
58\%
strain
Zur2023large
. Despite its limitations in large uniaxial compression, it remains a standard benchmark for validating more advanced hyperelastic models, such as neo-Hookean or Mooney–Rivlin formulations, in FEA software like Abaqus
Luo2020benchmark
.

The strain energy density function is quadratic in
\mathbf{E}
:

\Psi(\mathbf{E})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{E})\bigr)^{2}+\mu\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{E}^{2}\bigr),

(25)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé constants.

The second Piola–Kirchhoff stress follows from differentiation:

\mathbf{S}=\lambda\,\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{E})\,\mathbf{I}+2\mu\,\mathbf{E}.

(26)

From this, the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is:

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{S}=\lambda\,\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{E})\,\mathbf{F}+2\mu\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{E}.

(27)

Using
\mathbf{E}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{I}\bigr)
,
this can also be written as:

\mathbf{P}=\lambda\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F})-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)\mathbf{F}+\mu\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}-\mu\,\mathbf{F}.

(28)

The SVK model is only accurate for small to moderate strains,
although it can accommodate arbitrarily large rotations.
For large strains, it can give non-physical predictions,
such as negative stiffness in compression.

For hyperelastic materials, the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress satisfies

\mathbf{P}=\partial\Psi/\partial\mathbf{F}
; for the SVK model this is consistent with
Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
).

Using the definition of internal force in the Total Lagrangian setting, the internal force associated with
nodal unknown
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{i}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{i}}\int_{V_{r}}\Psi(\mathbf{E})\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}=\int_{V_{r}}\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{E})}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{r}}\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{E})}{\partial\mathbf{F}}:\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}=\int_{V_{r}}\mathbf{P}:\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}

(30)

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{r}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}.

(31)

which leads to the following result:

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\int_{V_{r}}\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

(32)

where
\mathbf{P}
is the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor and

\mathbf{h}_{i}:=s_{i,\mathbf{u}}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
is the reference-gradient of the shape function
s_{i}(u,v,w)
.
Note that this important relation holds for
any
hyperelastic
material model, not only for the SVK material model.
Plugging in the expression of
\mathbf{P}
for the SVK material model,
see (
28
), we get:

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\int_{V_{r}}\Bigl[\lambda\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F})-\tfrac{3}{2}\Bigr)\mathbf{F}+\mu\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}-\mu\mathbf{F}\Bigr]\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

(33)

and therefore, the expression of the virtual work of the internal force
over the element is given by:

\delta W_{\text{int}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{i}.

(34)

Second-order (Newton-type) methods require the consistent linearization
of the internal force vector, i.e., the Jacobian (tangent stiffness)
\partial\mathbf{f}_{i}/\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}
.
For the SVK material model, this Jacobian reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{r}}\Bigl[\lambda(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{E})\,(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}

(35)

\displaystyle+\mu(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}+\mu(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}

\displaystyle+\mu(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}-\mu(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}.

6.1.3
Mooney–Rivlin (MR) material

The Mooney–Rivlin material model is a widely adopted hyperelastic constitutive framework for finite element analysis (FEA) of rubber-like materials, formulated in terms of strain invariants
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
of the right Cauchy–Green tensor to accommodate the near-incompressible behavior of elastomers
Mooney1940theory
;
Rivlin1948large
. In this work, we employ the compressible two-parameter form in (
36
), which augments the isochoric Mooney–Rivlin response with a volumetric penalty term
Bonet2008nonlinear
. The model is effective for moderate to large strains and is widely used for elastomers and soft tissues due to its computational efficiency and compatibility with total Lagrangian formulations
Luo2020benchmark
.

It is expressed directly in terms of the deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}

and the invariants of the right Cauchy–Green tensor

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}
. It depends on three material parameters:
\mu_{10}
and
\mu_{01}
(shear response) and
k
(bulk modulus controlling compressibility).
The Mooney–Rivlin model reduces to the neo-Hookean model when
\mu_{01}=0
,
and to a purely incompressible form when
k\to\infty
with
J=1
.

The compressible Mooney–Rivlin strain energy density function is given by

\Psi(\mathbf{F})=\mu_{10}\,(\bar{I}_{1}-3)+\mu_{01}\,(\bar{I}_{2}-3)+\frac{k}{2}\,(J-1)^{2},

(36)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle\text{(right Cauchy--Green tensor)},

(37)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}),

(38)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C})^{2}-\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}^{2})\bigr],

(39)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\det\mathbf{F},

(40)

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=J^{-2/3}I_{1},

(41)

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=J^{-4/3}I_{2}.

(42)

The first Piola–Kirchhoff stress can be computed as:

\mathbf{P}=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

(43)

using the chain rule. First, the required derivative identities are:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{F},

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=2\bigl(I_{1}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C}\bigr),

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=J\mathbf{F}^{-T},

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{-m}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=-mJ^{-m}\mathbf{F}^{-T}.

(47)

Therefore, the computation of the derivatives of the isochoric invariants gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(J^{-2/3}I_{1})}{\partial\mathbf{F}}=J^{-2/3}\frac{\partial I_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}+I_{1}\frac{\partial J^{-2/3}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=J^{-2/3}(2\mathbf{F})-\frac{2}{3}I_{1}J^{-2/3}\mathbf{F}^{-T}

\displaystyle=2J^{-2/3}\mathbf{F}-\frac{2}{3}J^{-2/3}I_{1}\mathbf{F}^{-T}.

(48)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(J^{-4/3}I_{2})}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

(49)

\displaystyle=J^{-4/3}\frac{\partial I_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}+I_{2}\frac{\partial J^{-4/3}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}

\displaystyle=J^{-4/3}\bigl[2(I_{1}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C})\bigr]-\frac{4}{3}I_{2}J^{-4/3}\mathbf{F}^{-T}

\displaystyle=2J^{-4/3}(I_{1}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C})-\frac{4}{3}J^{-4/3}I_{2}\mathbf{F}^{-T}.

The derivative of the volumetric penalty term is:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{F}}\left[\frac{k}{2}(J-1)^{2}\right]=k(J-1)\frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf{F}}=k(J-1)J\mathbf{F}^{-T}.

(50)

After the final assembly based on the above presented equation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\mu_{10}\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}+\mu_{01}\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}+k(J-1)J\mathbf{F}^{-T}

(51)

\displaystyle=2\mu_{10}J^{-2/3}\mathbf{F}-\frac{2}{3}\mu_{10}J^{-2/3}I_{1}\mathbf{F}^{-T}

\displaystyle+2\mu_{01}J^{-4/3}(I_{1}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C})-\frac{4}{3}\mu_{01}J^{-4/3}I_{2}\mathbf{F}^{-T}

\displaystyle+k(J-1)J\mathbf{F}^{-T}.

Thus, the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress for the compressible
Mooney–Rivlin model is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{10}J^{-2/3}\bigl(\mathbf{F}-\tfrac{1}{3}I_{1}\mathbf{F}^{-T}\bigr)

(52)

\displaystyle+2\mu_{01}J^{-4/3}\bigl(I_{1}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{C}-\tfrac{2}{3}I_{2}\mathbf{F}^{-T}\bigr)

\displaystyle+k(J-1)J\mathbf{F}^{-T}

Using (
32
) together with (
52
), the internal force associated with the nodal unknown

\mathbf{e}_{i}
admits the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}_{1}-\mathbf{t}_{2}+\mathbf{t}_{3}-\mathbf{t}_{4}-\mathbf{t}_{5}+\mathbf{t}_{6},

(53)

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{10}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-2/3}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{t}_{4}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{01}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu_{10}}{3}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-2/3}I_{1}\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{t}_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\mu_{01}}{3}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}I_{2}\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{3}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{01}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}I_{1}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{t}_{6}

\displaystyle=k\int_{V_{r}}J\,(J-1)\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}.

Second-order (Newton-type) methods require the Jacobian of the internal force,

\mathbf{K}_{ij}:=\partial\mathbf{f}_{i}/\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.
Differentiating (
53
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{3}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{4}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{5}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{6}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}},

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{10}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-2/3}\Bigl[(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}-\frac{2}{3}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu_{10}}{3}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-2/3}\Bigl[(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})(2\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3}I_{1}(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}

\displaystyle-I_{1}(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{3}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{01}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}\Bigl[I_{1}(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}+(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})(2\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}I_{1}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{4}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{01}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}\Bigl[(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{I}+(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{h}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}-\frac{4}{3}\,\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{5}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\mu_{01}}{3}\int_{V_{r}}J^{-4/3}\Bigl[(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})\Bigl(2(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}(I_{1}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T})

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}I_{2}(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}\Bigr)-I_{2}(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{t}_{6}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{j}}

\displaystyle=k\int_{V_{r}}J\Bigl[(2J-1)(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})^{T}

\displaystyle-(J-1)(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{j})(\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{h}_{i})^{T}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}.

6.2
Viscous Damping Model in TL-FEA Formulation

6.2.1
Kinematics and Strain-Rate Measure in the Reference Configuration

In the Total Lagrangian setting, all kinematic quantities are expressed
with respect to the reference configuration. Let
\mathbf{X}
denote
a material point in the reference configuration and

\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{X},t)
its current position at time
t
. The
deformation gradient is

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X},t)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}}{\partial\mathbf{X}},\qquad J=\det\mathbf{F}.

(55)

The right Cauchy–Green tensor and Green–Lagrange strain tensor are
given by

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F},\qquad\mathbf{E}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I}).

(56)

The material time derivative of the deformation gradient is

\dot{\mathbf{F}}=\frac{\partial\dot{\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\mathbf{X}},

(57)

and the corresponding Green–Lagrange strain rate follows from

\dot{\mathbf{E}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\dot{\mathbf{C}}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\dot{\mathbf{F}}^{T}\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}^{T}\dot{\mathbf{F}}\bigr).

(58)

The tensor
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
constitutes the basic strain-rate measure
used to define viscous damping in the reference configuration.

For the ANCF 3243 beam and 3443 shell elements, the deformation gradient
at a given quadrature point can be expressed, following the notation in
the previous subsubsections, as

\mathbf{F}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\,\mathbf{s}_{i,\mathbf{u}},

(59)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the vector of nodal
unknowns (position and gradients) and
\mathbf{s}_{i,\mathbf{u}}
is the
row vector of shape-function derivatives with respect to the local
coordinates. The time derivative
\dot{\mathbf{F}}
is then

\dot{\mathbf{F}}=\sum_{i}\dot{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\,\mathbf{s}_{i,\mathbf{u}},

(60)

and
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
is obtained from (
58
).
For the T10 element, analogous expressions are obtained by replacing the
ANCF basis with the isoparametric T10 shape functions and their
reference gradients.

6.2.2
Finite-Strain Kelvin–Voigt Law in the Reference Configuration

The viscous damping model adopted in this work is a finite-strain
Kelvin–Voigt law formulated in the reference configuration, where the
viscous contribution to the stress depends linearly on the
Green–Lagrange strain rate
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
defined in
(
58
). This type of reference-configuration
viscoelastic extension of a hyperelastic backbone is widely used in
finite-strain constitutive modeling and finite element formulations,
see for example
Simo
Simo1987FiniteStrainViscoelastic
,
Reese and Govindjee
ReeseGovindjee1998FiniteVisco
,
Holzapfel
Holzapfel1996LargeStrainVisco
,
Holzapfel and Simo
HolzapfelSimo1996FiniteVisco
,
and Treutenaere et al.
Treutenaere2015FiniteStrainViscoDamage
.
The total second Piola–Kirchhoff stress
\mathbf{S}
is decomposed
into an elastic and a viscous contribution,

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}^{\text{el}}+\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}},

(61)

where
\mathbf{S}^{\text{el}}
is provided by the chosen hyperelastic
backbone (SVK or Mooney–Rivlin), and
\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}
is a
linear function of
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
of Kelvin–Voigt type.

In analogy with the small-strain isotropic Kelvin–Voigt model, the
viscous second Piola–Kirchhoff stress is chosen as

\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}=2\eta\,\dot{\mathbf{E}}+\lambda_{v}\,\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\mathbf{E}})\,\mathbf{I},

(62)

where
\eta\geq 0
and
\lambda_{v}\geq 0
are viscosity-like parameters
that play a role analogous to the shear and bulk Lamé constants in the
elastic law. The total first Piola–Kirchhoff stress then becomes

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{S}^{\text{el}}+\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}=\mathbf{P}^{\text{el}}+\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}},

(63)

with

\mathbf{P}^{\text{el}}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{S}^{\text{el}},\qquad\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}}=\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}.

(64)

This formulation is compatible with both SVK and Mooney–Rivlin
hyperelastic models, since only the elastic part

\mathbf{S}^{\text{el}}
changes when switching the constitutive
backbone, while the viscous Kelvin–Voigt contribution
(
62
) retains the same structure

Simo1987FiniteStrainViscoelastic
;
ReeseGovindjee1998FiniteVisco
;
Holzapfel1996LargeStrainVisco
.

6.2.3
Dissipation and

Thermodynamic Consistency

The viscous part of the stress should dissipate, rather than create,
mechanical energy. In the reference configuration, the power of
internal forces per unit reference volume associated with the viscous
stress is

\dot{W}_{\text{diss}}=\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}:\dot{\mathbf{E}}.

(65)

Using the Kelvin–Voigt law (
62
),

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{diss}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(2\eta\,\dot{\mathbf{E}}+\lambda_{v}\,\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\mathbf{E}})\,\mathbf{I}\bigr):\dot{\mathbf{E}}

(66)

\displaystyle=2\eta\,\dot{\mathbf{E}}:\dot{\mathbf{E}}+\lambda_{v}\,\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\mathbf{E}})\,\bigl(\mathbf{I}:\dot{\mathbf{E}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\eta\,\dot{\mathbf{E}}:\dot{\mathbf{E}}+\lambda_{v}\,\bigl[\operatorname{tr}(\dot{\mathbf{E}})\bigr]^{2}.

Both terms on the right-hand side are quadratic forms in
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
.
For
\eta\geq 0
and
\lambda_{v}\geq 0
one obtains

\dot{W}_{\text{diss}}\geq 0

(67)

for all admissible strain rates, so that the viscous model is
thermodynamically consistent in the sense that it cannot generate
mechanical energy. This type of quadratic dissipation potential and
non-negativity requirement is standard in finite-strain viscoelastic
theories
Simo1987FiniteStrainViscoelastic
;
ReeseGovindjee1998FiniteVisco
;
Holzapfel1996LargeStrainVisco
;
HolzapfelSimo1996FiniteVisco
;
Treutenaere2015FiniteStrainViscoDamage
.
In particular,
\dot{W}_{\text{diss}}
vanishes if and only if

\dot{\mathbf{E}}=\mathbf{0}
, corresponding to a configuration with
no strain evolution.

6.2.4
Element-Level Formulation with Viscous Damping

The derivation of the internal nodal forces for hyperelastic materials
in the Total Lagrangian formulation leads to

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\int_{V_{r}}\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},

(68)

where
V_{r}
is the element volume in the reference configuration,

\mathbf{P}
is the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress, and

\mathbf{h}_{i}
denotes the Jacobian (with respect to
\mathbf{X}
) of
the shape function corresponding to nodal unknown
\mathbf{e}_{i}
. This
expression holds for any hyperelastic constitutive model, as discussed
earlier.

When the viscous contribution (
63
) is
included, the internal force naturally splits into an elastic part and a
viscous part,

\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{el}}+\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{vis}},

(69)

with

\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{el}}=\int_{V_{r}}\mathbf{P}^{\text{el}}\,\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r},\qquad\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{vis}}=\int_{V_{r}}\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}}\,\mathbf{h}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}V_{r}.

(70)

Using
\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}
and
(
62
), the viscous force contribution at a
quadrature point is computed as follows:

1.

Assemble
\mathbf{F}
from the current nodal unknowns
(ANCF gradients or T10 displacements) and shape-function
gradients.

2.

Assemble
\dot{\mathbf{F}}
from the current nodal velocities
and the same shape-function gradients.

3.

Compute
\dot{\mathbf{E}}
using (
58
).

4.

Evaluate
\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}
by
(
62
).

5.

Form
\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{S}^{\text{vis}}
.

6.

Accumulate the contribution

\mathbf{P}^{\text{vis}}\mathbf{h}_{i}
into

\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{vis}}
at the quadrature point, multiply
by the geometric Jacobian and quadrature weight, and sum over
all quadrature points.

The total internal force vector for the element is the sum of the
elastic and viscous contributions. This procedure is identical for the
ANCF 3243 beam, ANCF 3443 shell, and T10 solid elements, differing only
in the specific definitions of the shape functions and their reference
gradients.

The corresponding contribution to the element tangent matrix (if an
implicit time integrator is employed) can be obtained by linearizing

\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{vis}}
with respect to the nodal velocities and
displacements. In the present work, the viscous term is primarily used
to regularize the explicit dynamics and to introduce physically
motivated damping; therefore, an explicit evaluation of

\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\text{vis}}
is sufficient.

7
External Force Contribution in TL-FEA Formulation

In the proposed TL-FEA formulation, the external force contribution to the equations of motion (EOM) in the majority of cases falls under two categories: a pointwise external force load, which is trivial, and friction and contact forces formulated as externally applied forces, which involve a friction and contact force model. In this section, we discuss the handling of both types of external forces.

7.1
Pointwise External Force Loads

When a pointwise external force load is applied to an element, it shall be distributed to the nodes of the element according to the shape functions. The external force contribution for this component is relatively trivial.

The pointwise applied-load component of
\delta W_{\mathrm{applied}}
in Eq. (
15
) accounts for forces concentrated at material points.
Consider an external force
\mathbf{f}_{P}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
applied at a point
P
of reference coordinates
\mathbf{u}_{P}=[u,v,w]^{T}
.
Its virtual work contribution is

\delta W_{\mathrm{applied}}^{P}=\delta\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u}_{P};t)^{T}\mathbf{f}_{P}(t)\;.

(71)

Using the TL interpolation
\delta\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u};t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}s_{i}(\mathbf{u})
, Eq. (
71
) becomes

\delta W_{\mathrm{applied}}^{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{u}}\delta\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{f}_{iP},\qquad\mathbf{f}_{iP}\coloneqq s_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{P})\,\mathbf{f}_{P}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(72)

Therefore, a pointwise force is distributed to the element nodal unknowns according to the shape functions evaluated at
\mathbf{u}_{P}
.
After assembly over all pointwise loads and elements, these contributions are collected in the external force vector
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}
in Eq. (
17
).

7.2
Friction and Contact Handling in TL-FEA Formulation

In the proposed TL-FEA formulation, we handle friction and contact using a penalty-based contact law with damping and history-dependent tangential friction.
Note that the model is piecewise (compressive-only normal response via
\max(0,\cdot)
and stick–slip branching) and is therefore nonsmooth at regime transitions; if a differentiable operator is required, one may replace
\max(0,x)
with
\tfrac{1}{2}(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}})
and adopt a regularized Coulomb law.
At each active contact, forces are evaluated from the penetration depth and relative velocity at the contact point, and assembled into the external force vector
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}
in Eq. (
17
).

We adopt a damped Hertz-type model for the normal response and a Mindlin history-dependent model for tangential friction.

7.2.1
Contact kinematics and activation

Consider two bodies
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
in contact at point
\mathbf{x}_{c}
.
Let
\mathbf{n}
be the unit normal pointing from
\mathrm{B}
to
\mathrm{A}
, and let
\delta
denote the penetration depth (contact active when
\delta>0
).
Define the relative velocity at the contact point as
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{c})
, with normal and tangential components

v_{n}=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}}\cdot\mathbf{n},\qquad\mathbf{v}_{t}=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rel}}-v_{n}\mathbf{n}.

(73)

The contact traction is applied in action–reaction form:
\mathbf{F}_{c,\mathrm{A}}=\mathbf{F}_{n}+\mathbf{F}_{t}
and
\mathbf{F}_{c,\mathrm{B}}=-\mathbf{F}_{c,\mathrm{A}}
.
When
\delta\leq 0
, we set
\mathbf{F}_{n}=\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{0}
and reset the tangential history variable (Section
7.2.3
).

7.2.2
Damped Hertz-type normal law

We use an effective elastic modulus
johnson1987contact

\frac{1}{E_{\mathrm{eff}}}=\frac{1-\nu_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{A}}}+\frac{1-\nu_{\mathrm{B}}^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{B}}}.

(74)

To accommodate both sphere-like and patch-based contacts, we introduce an effective contact radius
a
.
For smooth spheres,
a=\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}\delta}
with
R_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}=R_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}+R_{\mathrm{B}}^{-1}
; for a triangle/patch contact of area
A_{\mathrm{patch}}
, we use the equivalent disk radius
a=\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{patch}}/\pi}
.
With the stiffness scale
S_{n}=2E_{\mathrm{eff}}a
and coefficient
k_{n}=\tfrac{2}{3}S_{n}=\tfrac{4}{3}E_{\mathrm{eff}}a
, the elastic contribution is
k_{n}\delta
, which recovers the classical Hertz scaling
F_{n}\propto\delta^{3/2}
when
a=\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}\delta}
.

To model dissipation, we use a restitution-based damping parameter
Brilliantov_1998
;
tsuji1992lagrangian

\beta=\frac{\ln(e)}{\sqrt{\ln(e)^{2}+\pi^{2}}},\qquad e\in(0,1],

(75)

and define the (nonnegative) normal damping coefficient

\gamma_{n}=-2\sqrt{\frac{5}{6}}\,\beta\,\sqrt{S_{n}\,m_{\mathrm{eff}}}.

(76)

Here
m_{\mathrm{eff}}
is an effective mass at the contact; for scalar masses it reduces to
m_{\mathrm{eff}}=(m_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}+m_{\mathrm{B}}^{-1})^{-1}
.
The normal contact force is

\mathbf{F}_{n}=F_{n}\,\mathbf{n},\qquad F_{n}=\max\!\left(0,\,k_{n}\delta-\gamma_{n}v_{n}\right),

(77)

which enforces a compressive-only response and yields a repulsive damping contribution on approach (
v_{n}<0
).

7.2.3
Mindlin history-dependent tangential friction

We model tangential traction using a Mindlin-type incremental stiffness with a history (stiction) variable
mindlinCompliance1949
;
mindlin53
;
jonJCND2015
.
Let
\bm{\delta}_{t}
denote the stored tangential spring displacement for each contact.
At each step, we update and re-project it onto the current tangent plane,

\bm{\delta}_{t}\leftarrow\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}\right)\left(\bm{\delta}_{t}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_{t}\right).

(78)

We define the effective shear modulus as
G_{i}=E_{i}/(2(1+\nu_{i}))
and

\frac{1}{G_{\mathrm{eff}}}=\frac{2-\nu_{\mathrm{A}}}{G_{\mathrm{A}}}+\frac{2-\nu_{\mathrm{B}}}{G_{\mathrm{B}}}.

(79)

The tangential stiffness and damping are

k_{t}=8G_{\mathrm{eff}}a,\qquad\gamma_{t}=-2\sqrt{\frac{5}{6}}\,\beta\,\sqrt{m_{\mathrm{eff}}k_{t}},

(80)

and the trial tangential force is

\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}=-k_{t}\bm{\delta}_{t}-\gamma_{t}\mathbf{v}_{t}.

(81)

With a Coulomb limit
F_{\max}=\mu\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|
, we set

\mathbf{F}_{t}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}},&\|\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\|\leq F_{\max}\quad(\text{stick}),\\[1.99997pt]
\dfrac{F_{\max}}{\|\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\|}\,\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}},&\|\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{trial}}\|>F_{\max}\quad(\text{slip}).\end{cases}

(82)

In the slip regime, we rewind the history variable to keep
\mathbf{F}_{t}=-k_{t}\bm{\delta}_{t}-\gamma_{t}\mathbf{v}_{t}
consistent with the capped force:

\bm{\delta}_{t}\leftarrow-\frac{\mathbf{F}_{t}+\gamma_{t}\mathbf{v}_{t}}{k_{t}}.

(83)

This update reduces chatter by ensuring that the next step starts from the Coulomb boundary rather than from an over-extended tangential spring.

Finally, once
\mathbf{F}_{n}
and
\mathbf{F}_{t}
are evaluated at the contact point, they are treated as instantaneous external forces applied to the corresponding element(s) in action–reaction form. The resulting nodal load distribution follows the same procedure as for pointwise external force loads in Section
7.1
.

8
Kinematic Constraint Handling in TL-FEA Formulation

We summarize the representation and enforcement of bilateral kinematic constraints
in the proposed TL-FEA multibody setting. We employ a compact set of scalar
geometric primitives—dot-product 1 (DP1), dot-product 2 (DP2), distance (DIST),
and coordinate-difference (CD)—as building blocks for intermediate constraints
and engineering joints obtained through constraint composition. Practical applications may involve additional primitives and joint-specific
constraints beyond those listed here; we therefore restrict attention to these
four canonical forms to present the essential theory. The same point-evaluation
and linearization operators (
87
)–(
88
)
extend directly to richer constraint libraries. Each primitive is
posed as a scalar holonomic condition at the position level, and its first
variation provides the Jacobian contributions required by the velocity-level
augmented Lagrangian residual in Eq. (17). To keep the presentation focused, we
first define a unified constraint interface consistent with the step map

\mathbf{q}_{n+1}=\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\,\mathbf{v}
, then detail the four primitives in
TL-FEA notation, and finally outline joint construction by composition together
with enforcement within the ALM outer loop.

8.1
Constraint interface and TL-FEA evaluation operators

Let
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
denote the global vector of nodal unknowns
assembled over all deformable bodies. A set of
m
bilateral holonomic constraints
is posed at the position level as

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q},t)=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbf{c}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m},

(84)

with Jacobian

\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q},t)=\frac{\partial\mathbf{c}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{q}}.

(85)

In the velocity-level time stepping used to obtain Eq. (17), constraints are
evaluated at
\mathbf{q}_{n+1}=\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\,\mathbf{v}
, so that the constraint
residual and Jacobian entering the nonlinear solve are

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})
and

\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})
.

The constraint contribution appearing in Eq. (17) is written in augmented form as

h\,\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})^{\mathsf{T}}\!\left(\bm{\lambda}+\rho\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})\right),

(86)

where
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are Lagrange multipliers and
\rho>0
is
the ALM penalty parameter (scalar or componentwise). Constraints are scleronomic
if
\mathbf{c}
has no explicit time dependence and rheonomic otherwise (e.g., if
driven by a prescribed function
f(t)
); in the present discrete setting, time
dependence enters through evaluation of
\mathbf{c}(\cdot,t_{n+1})
.

Each primitive constraint is defined through the evaluation of points (and
directions built from point differences) on one or more deformable bodies. For
body
b
, element
E
, and material point
\mathcal{P}
with reference coordinates

\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}}
, the TL-FEA interpolation gives

\mathbf{r}_{bE}(\mathcal{P};t)=\mathbf{N}_{E}^{b}(t)\,\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}}).

(87)

For an element-level stacked unknown vector

\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}=[\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n_{u}}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n_{u}}
,
the corresponding linearization is

\delta\mathbf{r}_{bE}(\mathcal{P};t)=\left(\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right)\delta\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}.

(88)

Directions used by the primitives are constructed from point differences; for two
points
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
we define
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}
, where

\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathbf{r}(\mathcal{P};t)
and
\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\mathbf{r}(\mathcal{Q};t)
, so that

\delta\mathbf{a}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}
and
Eq. (
88

8.2
Primitive constraints in TL-FEA notation

The primitives DP1, DP2, DIST, and CD are scalar compositions of point evaluations

87

same chain-rule pattern. For compactness, we state each primitive as a scalar

8.2.1
Dot-product 1 (DP1)

DP1 prescribes the dot product between two directions, each defined by a pair of
points. Let
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}
lie on body
b
(elements
E,F
) and
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T}
lie on body
c

(elements

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}},\qquad\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{R}},

(89)

c_{\mathrm{DP1}}(\mathbf{q},t)=\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{b}-f(t)=0,

(90)

\delta c_{\mathrm{DP1}}=\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{b},\qquad\delta\mathbf{a}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}},\quad\delta\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{R}}.

\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DP1}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}}=-\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),

8.2.2
Dot-product 2 (DP2)

DP2 prescribes the dot product between a body-attached direction and a connector

\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{R}
lie on body
b
(elements

\mathcal{T}
lie on body
c

(element

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}},\qquad\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{R}},

(93)

where

is a direction on body

\mathbf{b}
connects point
\mathcal{R}
on

on body

(94)

with first variation

\delta c_{\mathrm{DP2}}=\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{b},\qquad\delta\mathbf{a}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}},\quad\delta\mathbf{b}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{T}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{R}}.

(95)

The only nonzero Jacobian blocks are associated with the hosting elements of

, and
\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DP2}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DP2}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{F}^{b}}

\displaystyle=+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{F}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DP2}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{G}^{b}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{G}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{R}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DP2}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{H}^{c}}

\displaystyle=+\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{H}^{c}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right).

(96)

8.2.3
Distance (DIST)

DIST prescribes the separation between two points. Let
\mathcal{P}
lie on body
b

(element
E
) and
\mathcal{Q}
lie on body
c
(element
F
), and define

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}
. We employ the squared-distance form

c_{\mathrm{DIST}}(\mathbf{q},t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{a}-f(t)^{2}\right)=0,

(97)

with
f(t)>0
. The first variation is

\delta c_{\mathrm{DIST}}=\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{a},\qquad\delta\mathbf{a}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}.

(98)

Therefore, the only nonzero Jacobian blocks are those for the hosting elements of

\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
:

\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DIST}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}}=-\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),\qquad\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{DIST}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{F}^{c}}=+\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{F}^{c}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right).

(99)

8.2.4
Coordinate-difference (CD)

CD constrains one Cartesian component of a point difference. Let
\mathcal{P}
lie on body

b
(element
E
) and
\mathcal{Q}
lie on body
c
(element
F
), and define

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Given a constant unit vector

\mathbf{d}\in\{[1,0,0]^{\mathsf{T}},[0,1,0]^{\mathsf{T}},[0,0,1]^{\mathsf{T}}\}
selecting
the
x
,
y
, or
z
component, CD enforces

c_{\mathrm{CD}}(\mathbf{q},t)=\mathbf{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{a}-f(t)=0.

(100)

The first variation is

\delta c_{\mathrm{CD}}=\mathbf{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\delta\mathbf{a},\qquad\delta\mathbf{a}=\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{Q}}-\delta\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}},

(101)

so that the only nonzero Jacobian blocks are

\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{CD}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{E}^{b}}=-\mathbf{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{E}^{b}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right),\qquad\frac{\partial c_{\mathrm{CD}}}{\partial\mathbf{e}_{F}^{c}}=+\mathbf{d}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{s}_{F}^{c}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{Q}})^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{3}\right).

(102)

Vector coincidence constraints are obtained by stacking three CD equations with

\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{e}_{x},\mathbf{e}_{y},\mathbf{e}_{z}
.

Since Eq. (17) requires the transpose action
\mathbf{C}_{q}^{\mathsf{T}}(\cdot)
, we
typically avoid forming
\mathbf{C}_{q}
explicitly. Instead, each scalar constraint

c_{i}
contributes a local scatter term of the form
\mathbf{C}_{q,i}^{\mathsf{T}}\eta_{i}

with
\eta_{i}=\lambda_{i}+\rho\,c_{i}
, which mirrors standard FE assembly and
integrates naturally with the element-level loops used for internal and external
force accumulation.

8.3
Joint construction by composition and enforcement within ALM

Engineering joints are obtained by stacking primitive constraints into a vector
constraint
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}]^{\mathsf{T}}
. This approach is convenient
in the TL-FEA setting because all defining points and directions are evaluated
through (
87
); attachments and body-attached axes therefore
evolve consistently with the underlying deformable discretization. A spherical
joint is realized by enforcing coincidence of two attachment points, implemented
by stacking three CD constraints to impose
\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{P}}-\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathbf{0}
.
A revolute joint is obtained by combining the spherical constraint with two
independent orientation constraints (DP1/DP2-type) enforcing axis collinearity,
thereby suppressing the two rotational degrees of freedom orthogonal to the hinge
axis. A fixed joint is obtained by combining point coincidence with three
independent orientation constraints that eliminate relative rotation. Other joints
(e.g., cylindrical, planar, prismatic) follow by the same composition principle
by combining coincidence or axis-alignment constraints with additional translational
or rotational suppression constraints, and are omitted for brevity.

Constraint enforcement is integrated into the velocity-level residual solve in
Eq. (17). At each time step, we evaluate
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})

and apply
\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v},t_{n+1})^{\mathsf{T}}
in sparse form
through the primitive-local scatter operations described above, while solving the
inner nonlinear system for
\mathbf{v}
with
\bm{\lambda}
held fixed. The
ALM outer loop then updates the multipliers until
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}
meets a
prescribed tolerance, using the standard update

\bm{\lambda}^{(k+1)}=\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}+\rho\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n}+h\mathbf{v}^{(k)},t_{n+1}).

(103)

At convergence, the multipliers
\bm{\lambda}
are the discrete dual
variables associated with enforcing
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q}_{n+1},t_{n+1})=\mathbf{0}
.
The corresponding generalized constraint reaction is recovered as

\mathbf{C}_{q}(\mathbf{q}_{n+1},t_{n+1})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}
(up to the adopted
sign convention); in the present velocity-level discretization,

h\,\mathbf{C}_{q}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}
represents the associated constraint
impulse over the time step. For coincidence constraints expressed by three CD
equations, the components of
\bm{\lambda}
admit a direct Cartesian
interpretation as the reaction intensity at the attachment.
