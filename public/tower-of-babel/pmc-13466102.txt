Molecular Diagnosis of Early Equivocal Alloimmune Injury After Imlifidase Desensitization in a Kidney Transplant Recipient
Authors: Loubna Hamdini, Valentin Goutaudier, Jean-Baptiste Gibier, Jessy Dagobert, Fariza Mezine, Isabelle Top, Vincent Elsermans, François Provôt, François Glowacki, Alexandre Loupy, Arnaud Lionet, Mehdi Maanaoui
Published: 2026

Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice for end-stage kidney disease patients,1 yet access remains limited for highly sensitized candidates because of preformed anti-HLA antibodies.2 In France, imlifidase enables transplantation in selected positive-crossmatch candidates,3 although rejection remains frequent.4 We hereby present the complex case of a patient who underwent transplantation with imlifidase, experiencing early acute kidney injury (AKI) suspected to be an antibody-mediated rejection (AMR). Banff criteria could not classify kidney allograft injuries into a definitive category and molecular diagnosis helped discriminate between possible diagnoses.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 42-y-old woman underwent a second kidney transplant using the imlifidase desensitization protocol. She has a history of kidney disease due to C3 glomerulopathy (C3G) with a previous deceased-donor kidney transplant complicated by recurrence of C3G and underwent allograft nephrectomy 10 y after transplantation.

With a calculated panel-reactive antibody of 99% and nearly 10 y on dialysis, she became eligible for the French imlifidase protocol3 and received a deceased-donor kidney. On the day of transplantation, multiple class I and II donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) were detectable, including anti-A3 (mean fluorescence intensity [MFI] 10 600), A11 (MFI 7100), B7 (MFI 5000), DR14 (MFI 2200), DR8 (MFI 4000), DQ4 (MFI 2700). Six hours after the imlifidase injection, only anti-A3 antibodies were still detectable (MFI 100). As a result, her calculated panel-reactive antibodies were virtually reduced to 0%, and the crossmatch was negative. Treatment followed the French protocol. Steroid therapy consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg on the day of transplantation, followed by 250 mg/d from day 1 to 3 before switching to oral prednisone 20 mg/d. Maintenance immunosuppression with tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil was initiated on the day of transplantation. Intravenous immunoglobulins (total dose 2 g/kg administered during 2 d) and thymoglobulin (1.5 mg/kg/d for 4 d) were started on day 4 posttransplant, followed by rituximab 375 mg/m2 on day 7 (Figure 1).

Serum creatinine rapidly decreased from 8.5 to 4.4 mg/dL on day 1. On day 3, the patient developed abrupt oliguria and AKI, with normal imaging and tacrolimus levels (8–12 ng/mL). A kidney allograft biopsy was performed on day 3, revealing histopathological findings of g1, i2, t1, v0, ptc0, and c4d0 and severe interstitial edema suggesting borderline lesions for acute T cell–mediated rejection (TCMR) and isolated glomerulitis (Figure S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A888). Immunofluorescence staining showed no deposits. DSA screening at this time was considered negative, apart from a persistent anti-A3 DSA at a very low level (MFI approximately 100). Treatment for suspected TCMR was initiated with steroids. This therapeutic approach initially led to an improvement in kidney function, with serum creatinine decreasing from 6 to 4 mg/dL; however, this was followed by worsening kidney function, with creatinine rising to 7 mg/dL on day 7. On day 9, marked rebound of DSA was observed, including anti-A3 (MFI 16 100), anti-A11 (MFI 13 400), anti-B7 (MFI 7300), anti-Cw4 (MFI 5700), anti-DR14 (MFI 11 900), and anti-DQ4 (MFI 4600). Repeat kidney allograft biopsy on day 9 showed lesions classified as g0, i1, t0, v0, ptc0, and c4d3 with no deposits detected on immunofluorescence (Figure S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A888). Although the Banff 2022 criteria for AMR were not met, we decided to treat as AMR based on high clinical suspicion with the start of plasmapheresis (6 sessions) followed by intravenous immunoglobulins (2 g/kg administered during 2 d) because of the immunological context and the diffuse deposition of C4d along peritubular capillaries.

We retrospectively performed a molecular diagnosis of each kidney biopsy. Paraffin-embedded formalin-fixed kidney allograft biopsies were sequenced at the same time using the NanoString nCounter platform.5 The analysis of the subsequent tissue-based transcriptomic data with the Banff Human Organ Transplant (B-HOT) gene panel-based automated HistoMx system6,7 showed for the first kidney biopsy a moderate probability of AMR (43.0%) and an unlikely diagnosis of TCMR (8.1%). The analysis of the second biopsy, however, revealed a high probability of AMR (75.4%) and a low probability of TCMR (12.8%), supporting treatment for AMR.

The patient’s clinical condition improved, and stable kidney function was observed 15 d posttransplantation with a creatinine level of 1.1 mg/dL. Later, she presented with AKI due to a compressive lymphocele. She also faced infectious complications, with 3 episodes of allograft pyelonephritis in the first year. At 1 y, the kidney graft showed excellent function, as evidenced by a creatinine level of 1.0 mg/dL and a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 15 mg/g.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates the challenges of diagnosing early alloimmune injury after imlifidase desensitization. Although Banff criteria for AMR were not fully met, the clinical course, diffuse C4d deposition, and marked rebound of preformed DSAs strongly suggested evolving AMR. Treatment was initiated on the basis of clinical suspicion before molecular results were available. Molecular assessment subsequently supported the diagnosis and reinforced the therapeutic strategy. Such tools may therefore help support earlier diagnosis in cases with equivocal histology or discordant immunologic findings.

HLA allosensitization remains a major obstacle to transplantation. Numerous studies have documented that desensitization increases access to transplantation and improves patient survival. Imlifidase cleaves all 4 subclasses of IgG, which offers an antibody-free window that enables transplantation in highly sensitized patients.8,9 A recent French real-world series reported the outcomes of the first nine French patients transplanted after imlifidase desensitization. Despite acceptable short-term graft function, early DSA rebound was frequent and associated with both clinical and subclinical AMR, highlighting the need for close immunologic monitoring and accurate rejection assessment.10

Standard histology remains the gold standard for diagnosing allograft rejection in kidney transplantation. The Banff classification helps with the diagnosis of kidney transplant pathologies using lesion scores. Within the Banff classification system, AMR differs from TCMR primarily by the localization of immune infiltrates (microvascular versus tubulointerstitial) and the presence or absence of DSA and complement C4d deposition. Notably, molecular analysis of kidney allograft biopsies was integrated into the Banff classification in 2013 to improve diagnostic accuracy.11 In 2019, the Banff Molecular Pathology Working Group introduced the B-HOT panel, a curated set of 770 genes strongly associated with key phenotypes in human allograft biopsies. This open-source panel encompasses core pathways involved in tissue injury, rejection, and the immune response and is commercially available (NanoString Inc, now Bruker Spatial Biology) as a standardized nCounter reagent kit. The assay is validated for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue,12 enabling molecular analysis on the same biopsy used for routine histology. The nCounter platform, approved for clinical use, ensures high reproducibility, decentralized testing, and remains cost-efficient across laboratories. Recent work has demonstrated that molecular diagnosis profiling using the B-HOT panel can significantly enhance the diagnosis of kidney allograft rejection. In a large multicenter cohort of nearly 1000 biopsies, gene expression signatures were used to build robust predictive models for both AMR and TCMR, achieving strong diagnostic performance across independent validation cohorts. Notably, this approach proved particularly useful for challenging or equivocal cases, where molecular probabilities helped refine histologic interpretations.6 However, molecular assessment should be considered complementary to clinical, immunologic, and histologic evaluation rather than a substitute for it. In the present case, molecular profiling did not guide the initial therapeutic decision but subsequently supported the clinical diagnosis of AMR.

In imlifidase-treated patients, Hruba et al13 reported that DSA-positive biopsies without histologic rejection shared transcriptomic signatures similar to those of active AMR, many of which later developed biopsy-proven rejection. This may be explained by the rapid rebound of donor-specific antibodies after imlifidase-mediated IgG cleavage, which can trigger early endothelial activation and alloimmune injury before the development of histologic lesions. These findings highlight that subclinical AMR activity may persist despite negative histology and early therapeutic intervention may be warranted before all diagnostic criteria are fulfilled.

To conclude, this case highlights the complex challenges encountered in kidney transplantation for highly sensitized patients. Although imlifidase represents a potentially paradigm-shifting advance in desensitization strategies, it does not eliminate the underlying risk of rejection. In this setting, prompt recognition and treatment of evolving AMR rely primarily on clinical judgment and immunologic monitoring, whereas molecular diagnostics may provide valuable complementary evidence when histologic findings remain equivocal.
