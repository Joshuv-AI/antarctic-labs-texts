Introduction

Deglutition or swallowing is a complex, coordinated sensorimotor process by which food, liquid or saliva is transported from the oral cavity to the stomach while protecting the airway. Dysphagia (difficulty in swallowing) is a detrimental condition that affects millions of people worldwide. It commonly arises from neurological disorders (such as stroke and Parkinson’s disease), structural and oncological conditions of the head and neck and age-related neuromuscular decline. Untreated dysphagia leads to serious complications including malnutrition, dehydration, aspiration pneumonia and increased mortality, substantially reducing quality of life for patients and caregivers.1–3

Current dysphagia assessment relies on a combination of clinical swallowing examinations (CSE) including the 100 mL water swallow test, standardised questionnaires such as the Gugging Swallow Screen (GUSS), Eating Assessment Tool (EAT-10), Mann Assessment of Swallowing Ability (MASA) and instrumental investigations like the videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS), Flexible Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing (FEES) and high-resolution manometry (HRM).4–7 These techniques are considered the reference standards for evaluating bolus flow, airway protection and swallowing biomechanics. However, their interpretation is inherently subjective, requires highly trained specialists and is subject to inter-rater variability. In many parts of the world, access to instrumental evaluation is limited by infrastructure, workforce shortages and cost, creating substantial inequities in dysphagia care.8–11

In addition to surgical interventions (such as cricopharyngeal myotomy), swallowing therapy is the mainstream treatment option for dysphagia. It is mainly comprised of compensatory strategies, rehabilitative exercises, preventive measures and adjunct modalities. Compensatory strategies involve immediate techniques such as postural adjustments, swallowing manoeuvres (like chin tuck, head tilt or turn) and diet modification to reduce aspiration risk without altering the underlying swallowing physiology. Rehabilitative exercises focus on improving the strength, coordination and range of motion of the swallowing musculature to promote long-term functional recovery. Preventive measures aim to maintain swallowing function and minimise secondary complications, particularly in individuals at risk of dysphagia due to neurological or medical conditions. Adjunct modalities potentially include additional therapeutic approaches such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation, biofeedback and sensory stimulation that are used alongside conventional therapy to enhance treatment outcomes.2

Artificial intelligence (AI), encompassing machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), computer vision and related techniques, has emerged as a powerful tool for analysing complex biomedical data and supporting clinical decision-making. In medical imaging, signal processing and predictive modelling AI systems have demonstrated the ability to detect subtle patterns beyond human perception, automate time-consuming tasks and generate individualised risk estimates.

Preliminary scoping of the literature revealed a growing number of publications at the intersection of AI and dysphagia. Studies from North America, Europe and Asia describe diverse applications, ranging from AI-assisted VFSS interpretation to ML-based risk prediction tools in stroke populations. For example, convolutional neural networks (CNN) have achieved high classification accuracy in swallow phase identification, and random forest models have been developed for aspiration prediction with promising results. AI offers the potential to automate interpretation including DL models for detecting aspiration in VFSS and FEES, ML algorithms for predicting post-stroke dysphagia, non-invasive and radiation free sensor-based systems for identifying swallowing events and airway invasion,12–15 support early screening in hospital settings and predict clinically meaningful outcomes such as aspiration risk, recovery of swallowing function or need for long-term enteral feeding.12 16–21

Despite the advancement and widespread adoption of AI across many areas of healthcare, the systematic exploration and integration of AI-based tools in the field of dysphagia remain limited.16 22–24 While other clinical domains have begun to embed AI-driven systems into routine practice, research in dysphagia continues to be characterised by fragmented efforts and a predominance of narrowly focused technical studies that are often disconnected from the broader clinical care pathway.16 22 24–28

Recent reviews have begun to explore the AI in dysphagia; however, existing syntheses have generally adopted a broad scope across age groups and clinical contexts.17 22 28 Given the substantial differences between paediatric and adult dysphagia in terms of aetiology, pathophysiology, a focused synthesis of AI applications in adult dysphagia is warranted.17 The present scoping review therefore aims to comprehensively map the use of AI tools across the adult dysphagia assessment and management.23 24 28

Objective

To explore current AI tools which have been adopted for the assessment and management of dysphagia.

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review included studies that involved adults aged 18 years and above with a confirmed diagnosis of dysphagia. Diagnosis required to be established through any validated clinical or instrumental methods, including the CSE, the 100 mL water swallow test, VFSS, FEES, or validated screening instruments GUSS, MASA or EAT-10.

Studies were excluded if they exclusively involved paediatric populations, healthy participants or simulated cases, as these differ substantially in pathophysiology, diagnostic considerations and management strategies compared with adults with dysphagia.

Concept

This review considered studies that investigated the application of AI in the screening, assessment, diagnosis, prognosis or management of dysphagia. Eligible AI approaches included, but were not limited to, ML, DL, neural networks, computer vision and related algorithmic techniques designed to support clinical decision-making. Studies were required to apply AI within a clinically relevant dysphagia context involving human participants.

Eligible outcomes included technical or model-performance outcomes (eg, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve, Dice coefficient), diagnostic accuracy outcomes, clinical process outcomes (eg, screening efficiency, workflow support and risk stratification) and patient-centred outcomes (eg, aspiration pneumonia, nutritional status, feeding tube dependence, functional oral intake and quality of life).

Studies were excluded if they focused solely on feasibility or usability testing without evaluating clinically relevant outcomes or described only theoretical model development.

Context

This review included studies conducted in clinical or therapeutic settings where dysphagia was assessed or managed, such as hospitals, rehabilitation centres, outpatient clinics and telehealth platforms. No restrictions were placed on geographical location, cultural or subcultural factors or socioeconomic context, in order to capture the global breadth of evidence on AI applications in dysphagia care. Both high-resource and low-resource settings were considered eligible.

Types of studies

This scoping review considered a wide range of study designs to capture the breadth of evidence on AI tool in dysphagia. Experimental and quasi-experimental designs, including randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs were eligible. Analytical observational designs such as prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies and analytical cross-sectional studies were also included. Descriptive observational designs, including case series, individual case reports and descriptive cross-sectional studies, were considered where they provided relevant insights into AI applications.

Qualitative studies were eligible if they focused on patient perspectives on AI in dysphagia care and systematic reviews that aligned with the inclusion criteria were also considered for inclusion where relevant.

Text and opinion papers were included if they addressed the clinical application of AI in dysphagia and contributed to conceptual understanding of the field.

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines29 30 (online supplemental file 1).

The study selection process is presented using a PRISMA flow diagram in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR reporting guideline.

The review protocol was prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/DYCE9) and was published in BMJ Open prior to data extraction. The published protocol detailed the eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening process and data extraction framework, and was followed without deviation throughout the review.31

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus, CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library, JBI Evidence Synthesis, ProQuest, and Google Scholar, covering the period from January 2000 to May 2025.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to:

Dysphagia, swallowing disorders, oropharyngeal dysphagia.

Dysphagia, swallowing disorders, oropharyngeal dysphagia.

Artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, computer vision

Artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, computer vision

The full search strategy was adapted to each database and peer-reviewed prior to execution (online supplemental material I). The literature search will be limited to only those published in English. Reference lists of included articles were also screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into Zotero for deduplication and then transferred to JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (SUMARI) for screening.32 33

Two independent reviewers (EVS and KT) screened all titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria. Studies deemed potentially relevant were retrieved in full text and assessed independently by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with consultation from a third reviewer (SIK) when required.

The search strategy was intentionally broad and included terms related to both oropharyngeal and oesophageal dysphagia to maximise sensitivity and ensure comprehensive literature retrieval. However, during full-text screening, the majority of studies focusing predominantly on oesophageal dysphagia were excluded, as they did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria centred on adult oropharyngeal dysphagia and its clinical assessment or management.

The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram (online supplemental material II).

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a prepiloted, standardised charting form developed in line with the JBI framework. Two reviewers independently extracted information from each included study. Data extraction table is attached along with the published protocol.31 Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus or by consultation with a third reviewer, and corresponding author was contacted where necessary to clarify missing or unclear data.

Data synthesis

Extracted data were analysed using descriptive and narrative synthesis. Quantitative variables, such as AI model type, clinical application and diagnostic modality, were summarised using frequency counts and tables. Narrative synthesis was used to identify patterns in AI applications, clinical use cases, performance trends and research gaps. Results were grouped into major domains including assessment, screening, prognosis and rehabilitation. A narrative synthesis was developed to describe how AI tools are currently being applied across the dysphagia care continuum and to identify areas requiring further research and clinical validation.

Extracted data given in online supplemental material IIIa,b.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this scoping review. The review synthesised evidence from published literature and did not involve the collection of primary data. Future studies examining the implementation and adoption of AI-based dysphagia technologies should incorporate stakeholder perspectives.

Results

Study selection

The search retrieved 4863 records (Scopus: 588; Cochrane: 38; CINAHL: 60; Google Scholar: 100; MEDLINE: 468; ProQuest: 3599; JBI Evidence Synthesis: 0, citation searching: 10). Four retracted articles were excluded. After removal of duplicates, 2522 unique records remained.

Following title and abstract screening, 62 studies were considered potentially relevant. After full-text review, 27 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final synthesis. List of excluded studies with reason and references given in online supplemental material IIIc.

Characteristics of included studies

The 27 included studies were published between 2013 and 2025 and originated in high-income countries (figure 1).

Across all studies, a total of 8842 participants included, representing a wide range of dysphagia aetiologies (online supplemental material IIId).

Dysphagia diagnosis was established using standard clinical and instrumental reference tests, including VFSS, FEES, 8-point Penetration Aspiration Scale (PAS), Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP), GUSS, EAT-10, HRM and validated water swallow tests.

Types of AI technologies

Traditional ML techniques were widely used for clinical prediction and screening tasks. Algorithms such as random forest, logistic regression, support vector machines (SVMs), XGBoost, AdaBoost and Bayesian networks were commonly applied to structured clinical datasets to perform functions including dysphagia screening, aspiration risk prediction, prognosis estimation, dehydration risk prediction and identification of patients at risk of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) dependence. These approaches typically relied on tabular clinical variables, patient characteristics or extracted physiological features to generate predictive models for clinical decision support.14 19–21 27 34–37

In contrast, DL methods were predominantly used for analysing imaging and video data obtained from instrumental swallowing assessments. Architectures such as CNN, CNN-long short-term memory (CNN-LSTM), convolutional recurrent neural networks, U-Net, three-dimensional CNN (3D-CNN), MoViNet and EfficientAT were used to analyse dynamic swallowing recordings. These models enabled automated interpretation of VFSS and FEES, including tasks such as airway invasion detection, aspiration identification and tracking of anatomical structures such as the hyoid bone.12 20 34–50

Several studies also employed computer vision techniques to analyse visual anatomical features from imaging data. These methods were used for facial landmark tracking, CT-based tongue segmentation and kinematic analysis of swallowing movements during VFSS, allowing objective measurement of biomechanical swallowing parameters.10 12 13 18 25 37 41 43 45 48–50

Artificial neural networks were applied in studies using physiological signal data, particularly HRM. These models were used for swallow classification and prediction of VFSS-derived swallowing parameters, demonstrating the potential of neural network approaches to interpret complex physiological signals associated with swallowing function.7 43 51 52

A smaller number of studies implemented multimodal AI systems, which integrated data from multiple sources such as video recordings, acoustic signals, HRM data, electromyography (EMG) and wearable sensors. These multimodal approaches aimed to capture the complex and multidimensional nature of swallowing physiology and may offer improved diagnostic accuracy by combining complementary data streams.7 15 19 43 47 51 52

Overall, DL-based image and video analysis emerged as the dominant methodological approach, particularly for the interpretation of VFSS and FEES recordings. This predominance likely reflects the availability of large volumes of imaging data in dysphagia assessment and the suitability of DL architectures for analysing complex visual and temporal patterns in medical imaging.12 35 37 38 40 41 46–50

Assessment and diagnostic applications

AI tools were applied across the full dysphagia care pathway, although most studies (n=21) concentrated on assessment and diagnosis. These applications included automated detection of aspiration and penetration,12 20 36 40 47–49 identification of pharyngeal residue,40 43 measurement of hyoid excursion,41 43 53 classification of upper oesophageal sphincter (UES) opening20 37 50 and prediction of swallow safety using established metrics such as the MBSImP and PAS.34 35 38 39 42 44–46 The majority of these studies used instrumental data from VFSS, FEES, HRM and high-resolution cervical auscultation, which were analysed using DL or ML models.

Screening and clinical risk prediction

A second major domain involved screening and clinical risk prediction, with models developed for hospital-wide screening (eg, Artificial Intelligence Massive Screening for Oropharyngeal Dysphagia (AIMS-OD)),54 post-stroke dysphagia prediction,38 42 aspiration risk prediction,12 35 36 40 47–49 radiation-induced dysphagia risk estimation45 55 and sarcopenic dysphagia screening.34

These models primarily used classical ML techniques, including SVMs, random forests, k-nearest neighbours, logistic regression, naïve Bayes classifiers, decision trees, gradient boosting and extreme gradient boosting algorithms,34 36 42 44 45 51 54 56 and demonstrated strong predictive performance, with reported Area Under the Curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.80 to 0.95, supporting their potential utility for early identification, triage, and preventive intervention.

Prognostic and management support applications

Prognostic and management-support applications were less common and focused on predicting 6-month swallowing recovery after stroke,42 identifying patients requiring PEG,56 estimating dehydration risk44 and guiding radiotherapy dose planning to minimise dysphagia-related toxicity,55 thereby enabling individualised risk stratification to inform clinical decision-making.42 45 55–57

Rehabilitation applications

Only one RCT evaluated an AI-driven therapeutic intervention, in which an AI-based video game-assisted swallowing therapy resulted in significant improvements in swallowing function (GUSS, FOIS), nutritional status (MNA-SF) and swallowing-related quality of life compared with standard therapy in post-stroke dysphagia patients.57

Other rehabilitation-focused studies were limited to exploratory AI-enabled biofeedback systems and digital platforms providing real-time feedback during swallowing exercises.

Imaging-based AI approaches

The predominance of AI applications in diagnostic and assessment domains likely reflects the availability of structured, high-dimensional data, such as imaging and physiological signals, which are well suited to computational analysis.

DL methods, particularly CNN, were the most frequently used architectures for imaging data and showed promising performance in detecting key swallowing events and identifying penetration or aspiration.12 20 36 40 47–49

Recurrent neural networks, LSTM models and 3D-CNN were employed in some studies to analyse sequential video frames and capture dynamic swallowing patterns.

Non-imaging modalities

Several studies also explored non-imaging modalities, including accelerometry, manometry, ultrasound, EMG and acoustic signals, suggesting that AI-enabled dysphagia assessment may extend beyond conventional radiological techniques.39 40 43 47 51

Such approaches may be especially valuable in resource-limited settings where access to VFSS or FEES is restricted, thereby supporting greater equity in dysphagia care.20 34 37 39 42 45 47 48 52 58

However, substantial heterogeneity in input modalities, outcome measures and validation strategies across studies limits direct comparability and synthesis of performance metrics.7 38 43 47 51 52

Outcomes and performance of AI systems

Across the included studies, reported outcomes primarily related to diagnostic accuracy, classification performance, predictive validity and feasibility of automated analysis. Most studies evaluated AI models using standard performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, AUC and F1 scores.

In diagnostic applications, deep learning models demonstrated high performance, with reported accuracy values exceeding 80–90% for classification of swallow safety or aspiration status.

In studies focusing on quantitative swallowing biomechanics, AI-based segmentation and landmark detection models produced objective measurements of anatomical movement, including hyoid displacement, laryngeal elevation and timing of swallow events, showing strong agreement with manually derived reference values.

Benefits of AI in dysphagia care

The main reported benefits of AI included improved objectivity, consistency and efficiency in dysphagia assessment. Automated systems reduced reliance on subjective clinician judgement and enabled standardised interpretation of complex imaging and sensor data.

Several studies reported reductions in analysis time compared with manual frame-by-frame review of VFSS recordings.12 20 36 40 47–49

AI-based screening systems also enabled non-invasive and low-cost identification of swallowing abnormalities, with potential applicability in non-specialist and resource-limited settings.

In therapeutic studies, AI-enabled biofeedback systems facilitated real-time monitoring of swallowing performance and provided immediate feedback during rehabilitation exercises.

Limitations of current evidence

Many studies were conducted using small sample sizes, retrospective designs and single-centre datasets.35 36 42–44 48 51–54 56

The majority of AI models were developed and validated using internal datasets, with limited external validation across different populations, clinical settings or imaging systems.47 50

Data heterogeneity, class imbalance (particularly for aspiration events) and inconsistent outcome measures further limited model generalisability and comparability.7 38 43 47 51 52

Only a few studies evaluated the impact of AI tools on clinical decision-making, workflow integration or patient-centred outcomes such as quality of life, nutritional status or long-term functional recovery.42 54–57

Diagnostic performance of AI systems

Across diagnostic studies, AI systems demonstrated high performance when compared with gold-standard clinical tools, with aspiration and penetration detection using VFSS and FEES achieving accuracies of 90–97% and AUC values up to 0.98.35 43 53

Swallow kinematic analysis (hyoid movement, UES opening and residue) reported Dice coefficients up to 98.6% and kappa values up to 0.80.20 37 43 48 53 54

Screening and prediction models showed AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.95, indicating that AI tools can reach high accuracy in several dysphagia-related tasks.34 36 42 44 45 51 54 56

The findings address the study objectives by identifying the types of AI technologies used in dysphagia care, the clinical settings in which they are applied and the reported outcomes, benefits and limitations. The review also highlights key gaps in the literature, particularly the limited focus on rehabilitation and real-world clinical implementation.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive synthesis of AI tools across the dysphagia care continuum, encompassing screening, diagnosis, prognosis and rehabilitation.

AI tools demonstrated strong potential to enhance the accuracy, accessibility and efficiency of dysphagia assessment and management. The dominant focus of existing research is on instrumental assessment, particularly VFSS and FEES.12 34 35 37–43 45 47–53 58

DL-based computer vision models consistently achieved high accuracy in detecting aspiration, penetration, pharyngeal residue, hyoid movement and UES function.12 20 34–50 This reflects AI’s natural advantage in analysing complex spatiotemporal imaging data, where subtle kinematic and bolus flow patterns are difficult for human observers to quantify reliably.

Beyond imaging, multimodal AI systems integrating wearable sensors, cervical auscultation, HRM, EMG and acoustic signals demonstrated that dysphagia can be evaluated using radiation-free, bedside and potentially home-based tools.39 40 43 47 51 These developments are particularly important for low-resource settings and for patients requiring repeated monitoring, such as those with neurodegenerative diseases or post-stroke dysphagia.20 34 37 39 42 45 47 48 52 58

AI also showed substantial promise in screening and risk stratification. Hospital-wide systems such as AIMS-OD and predictive models based on electronic health records, clinical variables or simple images (eg, neck photographs) achieved high sensitivity and good discrimination for identifying patients at risk of dysphagia, aspiration pneumonia, dehydration and prolonged tube feeding. These tools could allow dysphagia to be detected earlier, often before overt symptoms or complications occur, enabling preventive intervention and reducing hospital morbidity.34 35 38 39 42 44–46 54 56

Importantly, several studies moved beyond diagnosis toward prognostic and management-support roles, predicting long-term swallowing recovery after stroke, radiation-induced dysphagia or the need for prophylactic gastrostomy. These models support a shift toward personalised dysphagia care, where treatment intensity, feeding strategies and counselling can be tailored according to predicted risk and recovery trajectories.42 45 55–57

The majority of studies were retrospective, single-centre and relied on internal validation.35 36 42–44 48 51–54 56 Only one study evaluated an AI-based dysphagia rehabilitation intervention.54 Not only accuracy, AUC and kinematic precision were frequently reported, but also studies evaluated whether AI improved nutrition, pneumonia rates, quality of life or healthcare utilisation. Without evidence that AI alters meaningful clinical endpoints, adoption into routine care remains premature.37 41–44 50 51 53 56

Equity and accessibility also require attention. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, often in tertiary hospitals with advanced imaging and computing infrastructure.12 20 34 35 37–43 45–56 58 Very few studies addressed how AI might perform in rural, resource-limited or culturally diverse settings, despite dysphagia being a global problem.36 44 57

Only a minority of studies reported how data privacy, informed consent or algorithmic bias were addressed. As AI systems increasingly influence clinical decisions such as PEG placement or discharge planning, transparency, explainability and accountability will be essential for clinician and patient trust.56

Compared with diagnostic applications, only one study evaluated an AI-driven therapeutic intervention. This thematic imbalance leaves a significant gap in understanding AI applications in interventions and long-term management.57

Substantial heterogeneity observed across study populations, dysphagia aetiologies, AI methodologies, outcome measures and reporting practices, limiting direct comparison between studies.12 34 35 37–43 45 47–53 58

Relatively few studies evaluate model performance using independent external datasets.47 50 These methodological characteristics limit the generalisability, real-world applicability and definitive conclusions about the efficacy of AI in specific clinical settings.

The review was restricted to English-language publications, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Although this approach ensured methodological consistency with the predefined protocol, it may have limited the global representation of the evidence base.31

Although multiple biomedical and interdisciplinary databases were searched, dedicated engineering and computer science databases such as IEEE Xplore and ACM Digital Library were not included. This decision was consistent with the published review protocol and reflected the review’s focus on clinically relevant AI applications in adult dysphagia. Nevertheless, relevant engineering-focused studies may not have been identified, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the evidence base.31

Rapid pace of advancement in artificial intelligence, additional tools and applications may have emerged following the completion of the search.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that AI shows potential for transforming the assessment and management of dysphagia, with robust evidence supporting its application in instrumental interpretation, risk stratification and prognostic modelling at the technical level. DL-based analysis of VFSS and FEES has achieved high accuracy in detecting aspiration, penetration, pharyngeal residue and key swallowing kinematics, while ML models have shown strong potential for early screening, outcome prediction and personalised management planning. However, current evidence remains largely pre-implementation, with most studies being retrospective, single centre and internally validated. Before AI can be adopted into routine clinical care, large-scale prospective multicentre studies demonstrating clinical effectiveness and patient-centred outcomes are urgently needed.
