A novel EST-SSR fingerprint database development strategy for accurate identification of asparagus cultivars
Authors: Ze-Hui Yi, Ying Wang, Hui-Xia Song, Jing Zhao, Li-Ping Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGarden asparagus (Asparagus officinalis L.), a dioecious perennial crop, is highly valued for its nutritional and medicinal properties. The high similarity among cultivars and abundant intracultivar variation pose challenges to conventional cultivar identification methods. Leveraging the conserved parental genotype of ‘clone hybrid’ (CH) cultivars, this study employed a single-plant sampling strategy in which 20 individuals per cultivar were analysed to capture intracultivar variation. An innovative approach was further introduced for constructing a fingerprint database based on parental genotype data, which were reverse-inferred following Mendelian inheritance principles. Fifty EST-SSR markers, with an average polymorphism information content (PIC) of 0.6344, were selected to evaluate the genetic relationships among 62 cultivars. These cultivars were divided into three subgroups, revealing limited correlationt with their geographical origins and indicating their intricate genetic backgrounds. Furthermore, a core set of 7 EST-SSR markers effectively distinguished all cultivars except ‘Huamiao’ and ‘C5-115’, which showed high similarity, suggesting potential synonymy. Validation with three identified cultivars and two novel hybrid combinations further confirmed the stability and accuracy of this fingerprint database. This study not only offers a reliable and practical tool for identifying the authenticity and purity of asparagus seeds but also provides a valuable foundation for efficient breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07746-6.Keywords: Asparagus officinalis L., EST-SSR markers, Genetic relationship, Cultivar identification, Fingerprinting

Introduction

Garden asparagus (Asparagus officinalis L.), a significant vegetable crop in the Asparagus genus comprising over 300 species, is widely recognized as originating from the Mediterranean region [1]. This dioecious perennial plant has been cultivated for over two millennia and was introduced to China during the late 19th century [2]. Renowned for its extensive adaptability, significant economic returns, and remarkable health benefits, asparagus cultivation in China has expanded significantly in recent decades [3, 4]. Presently, China’s annual asparagus production accounts for approximately 50% of the global total [5]. As critical genetic repositories, crop cultivars play a deterministic role in agricultural productivity, quality, and stress resistance. Counterfeit cultivars not only cause severe agricultural losses but also seriously undermine the interests and enthusiasm of breeders [2, 5]. In garden asparagus, inherent intracultivar variation severely compromises morphometric cultivar discrimination, thereby facilitating the proliferation of counterfeit cultivars [6]. Consequently, it is imperative and urgent to develop standardized protocols for precise cultivar identification, which will not only safeguard breeders’ intellectual property but also enhance the registration, preservation, and utilization efficiency of germplasm resources.

Currently, distinctness, uniformity, and stability (DUS) testing remains the prevailing approach for plant cultivar discrimination despite limitations including time-consuming processes, labor-intensive efforts, high operational costs, and particularly the environmental sensitivity of quantitative traits [7, 8]. However, the DUS guidelines for garden asparagus encompass only 22 phenotypic descriptors [9], substantially fewer than those for eggplant (44) [10], peppers (55) [11], and french bean (49) [12]. This descriptor deficiency, coupled with intracultivar heterogeneity compromises the reliability of cultivar discrimination. Molecular marker-based DNA fingerprinting offers superior discrimination through DNA-level variation analysis [3, 13, 14]. Compared with other molecular marker systems, simple sequence repeats (SSRs) have the advantages of wide genomic distribution, high polymorphism, excellent reproducibility, co-dominant inheritance, and ease of detection [15]. These attributes have established SSR-based cultivar identification as the recommended technical standard by China’s Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs for many crop species, such as Chinese cabbage [16] and watermelon [17]. Unlike single-genotype cultivars, open-pollinated populations and polygenotypic cultivars frequently present multiallelic profiles at specific SSR loci, posing significant challenges for precise genotyping [6, 18, 19]. Overcoming these complexities requires thoughtful selection of molecular markers, sampling strategies aligned with propagation characteristics, and robust PCR data analysis methods [18–21]. Although two SSR-based fingerprint databases for asparagus have been reported [22, 23], they suffer from three critical limitations: (1) insufficient sampling density for allelic variation detection [6, 19]; (2) inadequate cultivar representation compromising diversity coverage [24, 25]; (3) potential scoring inconsistencies from manual electrophoretic analysis [26]. These limitations may undermine genotyping accuracy, particularly for closely related cultivars. Modern asparagus cultivation predominantly employs ‘clonal hybrid’ (CH) cultivars, which maintain genetic uniformity through sexual hybridization of clonally propagated parent lines [6]. Combined with strict pollen containment in isolated plots, this approach theoretically limits intracultivar alleles to ≤ 4 per locus, establishing an identification threshold. Based on this threshold, the strategic selection of molecular markers, sampling protocols, and PCR data analysis methods is expected to enhance asparagus DNA fingerprinting database reliability.

In this study, 62 asparagus cultivars from various institutions were analyzed using EST-SSR markers. To enhance accuracy, we employed a single-plant sampling strategy with 20 biological replicates per cultivar, enabling precise capture of intracultivar variation and fingerprint development. Initially, we assessed 200 primer pairs across nine phenotypically distinct cultivars, identifying 50 key EST-SSR markers based on amplification quality, polymorphism, Mendelian inheritance patterns, genome-wide distribution, and intracultivar alleles ≤ 4 per locus. These key markers were subsequently used to evaluate the genetic diversity of 62 asparagus cultivars. Then, following the principle of maximizing sample discrimination with minimal molecular markers, we selected core EST-SSR markers distinguishing nearly all 62 cultivars; Finally, we validated the developed DNA fingerprinting database with three identified cultivars and two novel hybrid combinations. This study aimed to (1) evaluate the genetic diversity and population structures of 62 asparagus cultivars and (2) construct a DNA fingerprint database for effective, reliable, and cost-effective cultivar/germplasm identification. Our results provide not only a practical tool for assessing cultivar authenticity and purity but also important genetic insights for the conservation, management, and utilization of asparagus cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

Sixty-two asparagus cultivars from various breeding institutions, along with two hybrid combinations (14 − 5 × 6 and 15 − 4 × 8), were selected for this study. Among them, forty-one cultivars were released in China, while the rest were introduced from other countries (Table 1). To minimize heterogeneity, a single-plant sampling strategy utilizing fresh leaves from 20 individuals per cultivar was employed to comprehensively capture intracultivar variation. Segregation analysis of EST-SSR markers was conducted using a cohort of 100 ‘Champion’ individuals to identify Mendelian-compliant markers. To validate the DNA fingerprinting database, three randomly selected identified cultivars (‘Champion’, ‘Purple Passion’, and ‘Grande’) and two novel hybrid combinations were subjected to confirmatory EST-SSR genotyping, requiring an additional 20 plants for each of these cultivars. All cultivars were planted at the Dongyang experimental base of Shanxi Agricultural University.

Information of 62 asparagus cultivars

DNA extraction and quality detection

Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB protocol [27]. Compared to the conventional method, this modified CTAB approach omits the selective precipitation and CsCl gradient centrifugation steps, resulting in enhanced time efficiency. DNA was solubilized in preheated CTAB extraction buffer containing high-concentration NaCl, followed by sequential purification through a series of centrifugation steps and phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol mixture (25:24:1, v/v) extraction, with final DNA recovery achieved via ethanol precipitation. DNA integrity was assessed through 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, with λ-DNA serving as a molecular weight marker. DNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following quantification, all DNA samples were stored at −80℃ for subsequent analyses.

DNA amplification, EST-SSR marker screening and genotyping

Two hundred EST-SSR markers were systematically selected from previous research [28]. Using nine morphologically distinct cultivars (‘Jinglv No.2’, ‘Zhefeng 891’, ‘Kangaicao No.2’, ‘Champion’, ‘CD115’, ‘Jinggang Wase’, ‘Grande’, ‘Huamiao’, and ‘Walk Pioneer’), a key set of 50 markers was screened based on the following criteria: (1) high amplification quality, (2) abundant polymorphisms, (3) ≤ 4 intracultivar alleles per locus, (4) Mendelian inheritance patterns, and (5) uniform genome distribution. These key markers were then used for genetic diversity analysis across all 62 cultivars. PCR reactions were conducted in 10 µL volumes containing 0.2 µL of DNA (50 ng·µL−1), 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µmol·L−1), 3 µL of 2×Taq PCR Mix, and 5.8 µL of sterile ddH2O. The protocol included an initial denaturation at 94℃ for 4 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94℃ for 30 s, annealing at specific temperatures for 30 s, and extension at 72℃ for 35 s, with a final extension step at 72℃ for 10 min. Amplified fragments were separated using 8% silver-stained polyacrylamide gels. Furthermore, a core subset of EST-SSR markers was refined from the key 50 EST-SSR markers based on their discriminatory power. Fluorescent FAM (6-carboxy-fluorescein) label was added to the 5’-end of the forward primer for each core EST-SSR marker to enable fluorescence-based detection (Supplementary Material 1). Both standard and fluorescent-modified primers were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Corporation Ltd. Amplification protocols for fluorescent capillary electrophoresis SSR analysis, including reagent concentrations and thermal cycling parameters, were maintained identical to conventional SSR procedures. The fluorescent-labeled PCR products were genotyped via an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyser and analyzed with Gene Marker 4.0 software (USA), referencing the internal marker LIZ 500.

Data statistics and analysis

Amplified fragments were scored as present (1) or absent (0) to generate a binary matrix for genetic analysis. Genetic parameters of 50 EST-SSR markers was assessed on 57 diploid cultivars through integrated analytical approaches. POPGENE v1.32 was employed to calculate fundamental genetic indices including: observed allele count (Na), effective allele count (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), major allele frequency (MAF), Shannon’s information index (I), Nei’s genetic distance (GD), F-statistics (Fst), and gene flow (Nm). Polymorphic information content (PIC) was determined using PowerMarker v3.25. For genetic relationship assessment across all 62 cultivars, binary matrices derived from 20 biological replicates per cultivar were subjected to dominant marker analysis. Genetic distances were calculated employing Nei’s coefficient in POPGENE v1.32, which subsequently informed multivariate analyses: principal component analysis (PCA) using NTSYS-pc 2.10 and UPGMA clustering via MEGA11. In parallel, exploratory population structure analysis was performed using Structure v2.3.4 [29]. To accommodate intraspecific variation, the two most frequent alleles per locus were strategically selected to meet diploid co-dominant marker criteria, while minor allele exclusion inevitably reduced resolution. The K values ranged from 1 to 10, with each K value replicated 10 times. The runs were performed with a burn-in period of 100,000 and 100,000 replicates of MCMC. The optimal K value was determined using the STRUCTURE HARVESTER website following the △K method and the lnP(K) values [30, 31]. Additionally, genotypic data from 20 diploid individuals per cultivar and dimensionality-reduced tetraploid datasets were analyzed using GenAlEx 6.51 to calculate F-statistics (Fst) and genetic identity (GI) between cultivars, with simultaneous evaluation of subgroup genetic variation. The molecular identification system was developed in three phases: First, locus-specific fingerprint data were generated by deducing parental genotypes from 20 individual profiles per cultivar, followed by concatenation to create SSR-based molecular fingerprints (Fig. 1). Second, core markers with complete discriminatory capacity were identified through polymorphism locus screening. Third, a digital fingerprint database was established by assigning numerical codes (1–9) to SSR alleles based on fragment length, and concatenating core marker codes in their designated order to generate unique molecular fingerprints. These fingerprints were further converted into QR codes as machine-readable molecular passports for cultivar identification. For tetraploid cultivars, fingerprints were developed using the four most frequent alleles per locus.

Results

Screening and polymorphism analysis of EST-SSR markers

Among the 200 screened EST-SSR markers, 199 (99.5%) generated clear amplicons matching the expected size. Of these, 182 (91%) followed classical Mendelian inheritance patterns, with 137 (73.5%) showing polymorphisms. Based on multiple criteria, 50 EST-SSR markers were selected as a key set for further study (Appendix A). Across 62 asparagus cultivars, this key set detected 211 alleles. The Na per marker ranged from 3 (SSR44, SSR232, et al.) to 9 (SSR1899), with a mean value of 4.22. The Ne averaged 2.8149, ranging from 1.9110 (SSR2113) to 4.0958 (SSR1131). The MAF values spanned from 0.3115 (SSR1131) to 0.6452 (SSR1118), with a mean of 0.4532. The Ho values varied between 0.2379 (SSR1131) and 0.5194 (SSR2113) (mean = 0.3604), whereas He values ranged from 0.4806 (SSR2113) to 0.7621 (SSR1131) (mean = 0.6396). The highest (1.6067) and lowest (0.7026) I values were obtained for SSR1899 and SSR2113, respectively, with an average of 1.1253. The Nm values fluctuated from 0.3863 for SSR543 to 1.1030 for SSR232, averaging 0.5761. Meanwhile, the PIC value averaged 0.6344, with extremes of 0.4767 (SSR2113) and 0.7558 (SSR1131). Furthermore, the PI values spanned from 0.0896 (SSR1899) to 0.3796 (SSR2113) (mean = 0.2018), whereas the PISibs values ranged from 0.3904 (SSR1899) to 0.6061 (SSR2113) (mean = 0.4805) (Table 2). Overall, these 50 EST-SSR markers demonstrated sufficient discriminatory power for genetic relationships analysis among the 62 asparagus cultivars.

This aggregate differentiation level was 12.5% lower than the angiosperm benchmark (mean FST = 0.2). The distribution of FST values revealed that 2.12% of the cultivar pairs exhibited minimal differentiation (FST < 0.05), with 11.90% (0.05–0.10), 49.55% (0.10–0.20), and 31.62% (0.20–0.30) showing progressively higher levels of differentiation, while 4.81% displayed substantial divergence (FST > 0.30) (Fig. 2A). Nei’s genetic identity (GI) ranged from 0.1907 (‘Jinggang Red’ vs. ‘Jewel Source 1’) to 1.0000 (‘Hyman’ vs ‘C5-115’), with an average value of 0.6777. Among all pairwise comparisons, 6.03% of the cultivar pairs had GI values < 0.4, 6.98% fell within 0.4–0.5, 13.59% within 0.5–0.6, 23.43% within 0.6–0.7, 31.09% within 0.7–0.8, and 18.88% showed high similarity with GI ≥ 0.8 (Fig. 2B). These results demonstrated that while many of the 62 asparagus cultivars exhibited genetic similarity, considerable genetic diversity was still observed among them.

Population structure analysis

Population structure analysis revealed that the maximum value of ΔK occurred at K = 4, with a secondary peak at K = 3 (Fig. 3A). However, the highest Ln P(K) was observed at K = 3, followed by K = 4 (Fig. 3B). Accordingly, the 62 asparagus cultivars were grouped into three and four subgroups, respectively. At K = 3, all five purple asparagus cultivars were clustered exclusively in subgroup Ⅰ, with Q values > 0.8. Subgroup Ⅱ contained 31 green asparagus cultivars, such as ‘WXS8001’, ‘Emerald’, and ‘TC’. Among these, 26 cultivars, with the exception of ‘Kangaicao no.2’, ‘Walker pioneer’, ‘Jinggang wase’, ‘H2−12’ and ‘US901’, exhibited Q values exceeding 0.8. Subgroup Ⅲ comprised 26 green asparagus cultivars, such as ‘UC green arrow’, and ‘Jinglv no.3’, with all but for ‘Fengdao No.1’, ‘Apollo’, ‘Jerim’, and ‘JX1915’ showing Q values > 0.8 (Fig. 3C). At K = 4, subgroups Ⅰ and Ⅳ corresponded to subgroups Ⅲ and Ⅰ at K = 3, respectively, whereas subgroup Ⅱ (K = 3) was further divided into two distinct clusters: a new subgroup II consisting of 20 green asparagus cultivars, and a new subgroup III containing 11 green asparagus cultivars (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate that cultivars developed by the same institutions didn’t consistently cluster into the same subgroup, indicating their intricate genetic backgrounds.

Cluster analysis

The phylogenetic tree, constructed using Nei’s unbiased genetic distance with the UPGMA method, categorized the 62 cultivars into three primary clusters. Cluster Ⅰ included all five purple cultivars from various regions. Cluster Ⅱ comprised 31 green cultivars, with the following distributions: 10 from Shandong Province (47.62% of its cultivars in this study), 8 from Zhejiang Province (88.89%), 3 from Jiangxi Province (42.86%), 9 from the United States (60.00%), and 1 from France. Except for ‘Optimus Prime’, the remaining 30 cultivars in this cluster were consistent with those in subgroup Ⅱ (K = 3) (Fig. 4). Cluster Ⅲ contained 26 green asparagus cultivars, distributed as follows: 9 cultivars from Shandong Province (42.86%), 3 from Jiangxi Province (42.86%), 5 from the United States (33.33%), 2 from the Holland, and 5 from other regions. With the exception of ‘US901’, all the other 25 cultivars were aligned with subgroup Ⅲ (K = 3) (Fig. 3). Further subdivision of cluster Ⅱ into cluster Ⅱ−1 and cluster Ⅱ−2 revealed notable distinctions between these subclusters and the subgroups identified at K = 4. (Supplementary Material 2). This evidence confirms that categorizing the 62 cultivars into three genetically distinct populations best reflects their biological relationships.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) corroborated the population structure and clustering patterns. The first two principal components explained 17.80% and 14.50% of molecular variance respectively, totaling 32.30% (Fig. 5). The 62 asparagus cultivars were categorized into three distinct categories: class Ⅰ included all five purple cultivars, while classes Ⅱ and Ⅲ consisted of 35 and 30 green cultivars, respectively. Although nine cultivars overlapped between classes Ⅱ and Ⅲ, most class Ⅱ members corresponded to subgroup Ⅱ (K = 3) and cluster Ⅱ, whereas class Ⅲ cultivars matched subgroup Ⅲ and cluster Ⅲ. Crucially, all overlapping cultivars showed Q < 0.8, indicating genetic admixture between subgroups Ⅱ and Ⅲ lineages. In summary, the PCA results agreed with population structure and cluster analyses, demonstrating the robustness of this study in assessing the genetic relationships among the 62 asparagus cultivars.

Genetic differentiation analysis of subgroups

Genetic differentiation among three subgroups of 62 asparagus cultivars was further examined using GenAlEx 6.51. The analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) revealed that only 7.116% of total genetic variation was attributed to differences among subgroups, whereas 92.27% occurred within subgroups (Table 3). Pairwise Fst comparisons quantified subgroup divergence levels: subgroup Ⅰ vs. Ⅱ (Fst = 0.119), I vs. Ⅲ (Fst = 0.141), and Ⅱ vs. Ⅲ (Fst = 0.028). These findings demonstrate a significantly closer genetic relationship between subgroups Ⅱ and Ⅲ (Fst < 0.03), consistent with population structure analysis results and suggesting shared ancestral origins (Fig. 6). Gene flow plays a crucial role in shaping the genetic differentiation of the germplasm resources. In this study, the Nm value between subgroups Ⅱ and Ⅲ was notably higher than those of the other comparisons, indicating enhanced genetic exchange, further corroborating the Fst findings (Fig. 6).

Establishment of DNA fingerprints for 62 asparagus cultivars and accuracy validation

To identify the most effective combination of EST-SSR markers for asparagus cultivar discrimination, PI and PISibs indices were analyzed (Table 2). A core set of seven EST-SSR markers spanning distinct chromosomes was systematically selected to establish cultivar-specific DNA fingerprints (Fig. 7), collectively detecting 48 alleles among 62 cultivars (Table 4). A novel molecular identification system was developed by concatenating standardized codes from these markers (SSR201-SSR510-SSR709-SSR857 -SSR1505-SSR1899-SSR2392) into unique digital identifiers, subsequently encoded as QR-code molecular IDs. Except for ‘Huamiao’ and ‘C5−115’, the remaining 60 cultivars were successfully distinguished by this system (Table 1). The complete genetic identity (GI = 1.0) between ‘Huamiao’ and ‘C5−115’, coupled with their highly similar agronomic traits, establishes them as putative synonymous cultivars. Validation tests with three identified cultivars (‘Champion’, ‘Purple Passion’, and ‘Grand’) showed 100% concordance between generated codes and reference fingerprints. For two novel hybrid combinations (‘14 − 5 × 6’, ‘15 − 4 × 8’), the codes ‘3535555522264545151533352323’ and ‘3535555533334545454513131333’ presented unique profiles that were distinguishable from all tested cultivars. These results confirm the technical robustness of the developed molecular identification system, which provides an effective solution for verifying the authenticity and purity of asparagus cultivars.

Discussion

Screening criteria and quality assessment of the 50 key EST-SSR molecular markers

SSR markers, widely used for targeted PCR amplification, exhibit several advantages including extensive genomic coverage, high polymorphism, co-dominant inheritance, exceptional reproducibility, and straightforward detection methods [15]. These attributes render them ideal for genotyping polygenotypic cultivars, even with frequent multiallelic variations at SSR loci [8, 22]. This study extended conventional screening criteria (amplification efficiency, polymorphism, and locus distribution) by introducing Mendelian segregation ratios and intracultivar alleles to ≤ 4 per locus. This innovation specifically addresses the genetic structure of the CH cultivar, an F1 hybrid originating from heterozygous parental clones, which exhibits a biological constraint of four maximum observable alleles per locus. First, eliminating SSR primers with non-unique amplification sites enhances SSR genotyping accuracy. Second, the threshold identifies cultivars exceeding four alleles at specific loci, typically resulting from foreign pollen contamination during seed production (common in counterfeit or substandard cultivars). This approach significantly improves both EST-SSR genotyping precision and cultivar identification reliability. In this study, the 50 EST-SSR markers exhibited superior genetic diversity parameters (mean Ne = 2.8149, He = 0.3604) compared to prior reports (Ne = 1.2499, He = 0.2486) [14], with a mean PIC value of 0.6344 exceeding published values of 0.41 (10 cultivars [32]) and 0.35 (24 cultivars [22]). Three main factors explain these discrepancies: (1) locus-specific polymorphism levels across 62 cultivars remained largely unaffected by intracultivar threshold, (2) expanded coverage of cultivar diversity, despite persisting limitations in representing landrace cultivar and wild genetic resource, and (3) single-plant sampling of 20 individuals. Previous approaches using 1–4 plants [14, 32] or bulk sampling [22] often failed to detect low-affinity alleles, primarily due to primer competition effects in multiplex amplifications. According to previous study, markers with a PIC value ≥ 0.5 were considered highly informative [33]. Therefore, the key set of 50 EST-SSR markers thus provides robust discriminative power for evaluating genetic relationships among 62 asparagus cultivars.

Genetic relationships of 62 asparagus cultivars

Accurate assessment of genetic relationships in germplasm resources is crucial for optimizing breeding efficiency [24]. Population structure, cluster, and PCA analysis provide complementary genetic perspectives to investigate these relationships [34]. Population structure analysis categorized the 62 cultivars into three subgroups, a pattern corroborated by cluster analysis for 60 cultivars with two exceptions (‘US901’ and ‘Optimus Prime’). This consistency underscores the practicability of genotype representation through major allele profiling in population structure inference, while acknowledging inherent limitations in capturing full genetic diversity. Crucially, implementing genome sequencing and systematic genotype-phenotype correlations analyses in subsequent research will be essential to validate and refine these findings. Notably, PCA analysis revealed nine cultivars exhibiting dual affiliations with Class Ⅱ and Class Ⅲ, suggesting potential historical inter-subgroup gene flow. Another factor is PCA’s focus only on the first two principal components (PC1: 17.8%, PC2: 14.5%), which might obscure biologically meaningful variation. Crucially, no significant correlation emerged between genetic distance/cluster affiliation and geographical origin among the 62 cultivars, highlighting their intricate genetic backgrounds. Similar findings have also been reported in other crops, such as cigars [35] and watermelon [36]. Two factors may explain this phenomenon. First, modern transportation and communication networks have facilitated germplasm exchange and cultivar introduction. For instance, cultivars such as ‘Grande’ and ‘Apollo’ from the United States and ‘Champion’ from Shandong Province, China, belong to subgroup Ⅲ owing to sharing genetic pedigree from ‘New Jersey’ [6, 37]. Second, obligate outcrossing in asparagus promotes gene flow among cultivars, eroding region-specific genetic signatures. Therefore, future breeding programs for garden asparagus should prioritize genetic relationships over geographical origins. This comprehensive analysis of the genetic architecture of 62 cultivars provides a valuable foundation for their effective utilization in breeding programs.

Genetic differentiation of three asparagus subgroups

Fst is a widely used metric for assessing population differentiation. According to Hartl and Clark’s [38] classification, Fst values below 0.05 indicate minimal genetic variation, while those between 0.05 and 0.15 suggest moderate differentiation. In this study, moderate differentiation was observed between subgroups Ⅰ and Ⅱ (Fst = 0.1193) and subgroups Ⅰ and Ⅲ (Fst = 0.1407), indicating significant genetic divergence between purple and green asparagus cultivars. Conversely, subgroups Ⅱ and Ⅲ exhibited minimal differentiation (Fst = 0.0280). These findings align with AMOVA results, which revealed that 92.88% of genetic variation occurred within subgroups, whereas merely 7.12% among subgroups. The Nm values of 1.8765 (subgroups Ⅰ vs. Ⅱ) and 1.5263 (subgroups Ⅰ vs. Ⅲ) exceeded Wright’s gene flow threshold (Nm < 1.0) [39], confirming restricted genetic exchange between purple and green asparagus cultivars. This reproductive isolation likely stems from ploidy divergence causing chromosomal pairing failure during meiosis. Given the rising popularity of purple asparagus, it is essential to broaden germplasm genetic diversity by biotechnological methods that overcome such reproductive isolation and facilitate genetic exchange with other asparagus germplasms. In contrast, subgroups Ⅱ and Ⅲ displayed frequent gene flow (Nm = 8.6867), which accounts for their limited genetic differentiation. The overutilization of limited elite germplasms in breeding programs has diminished cultivar genetic diversity, potentially hindering sustainable development in asparagus industry. To address this issue, incorporating local cultivars, wild germplasms, and closely related species with valuable agronomic traits into breeding programs is advisable. Future efforts should prioritize the genetic evaluation of these materials to facilitate their effective integration into breeding initiatives, thereby enhancing the genetic diversity of asparagus germplasm reservoirs and providing a robust foundation for cultivar improvement.

Fingerprint database construction for asparagus cultivars

Accurate cultivar identification is essential for protecting breeders’ intellectual property and preventing agricultural losses caused by counterfeit cultivars [7, 36]. Developing germplasm identification systems necessitates prioritizing accuracy while balancing cost-effectiveness, processing time, and operational feasibility. Although SSR markers have proven effective in developing fingerprint databases across multiple species [13, 22, 40, 41], their application in polygenotypic species faces technical challenges due to intrinsic genetic heterogeneity [6, 18, 19]. By leveraging the conserved parental genotypes in ‘clone hybrid’ (CH) cultivars, we developed a parental genotype-derived fingerprint database using seven core EST-SSR markers. Polyploidization of germplasm increases the technical complexity in SSR-based genotype identification and may compromise accuracy, particularly in species with intricate genetic architectures, due to the resultant proliferation of amplification bands in SSR markers that introduces ambiguity in allelic discrimination. To address this limitation, we implemented a genotype simplification strategy employing four predominant alleles, which enhanced identification reliability despite partial resolution trade-offs. The developed database showed unambiguous discrimination of all 60 cultivars, as validated by identified cultivars (‘Champion’, ‘Purple Passion’, and ‘Grand’) and novel hybrid combinations (‘14 − 5 × 6’ and ‘15 − 4 × 8’), confirming its high stability and accuracy. However, differentiation failed between ‘Huamiao’ and ‘C5-115’. Their identical SSR profiles across 50 markers and siniliar field phenotypes suggest these may closely related lineages or potential synonyms. Further verification through additional molecular markers or genomic sequencing is required to confirm this finding. Caruso et al. [6] used EST-SSR markers AAT1 and TC1 to distinguish ‘Depoli’ from 35 other cultivars, but these markers lack sufficient resolution for large-scale identification. Ahmad et al. [22] constructed the first fingerprint database for asparagus using three g-SSR markers, successfully differentiating all 24 tested cultivars. However, this study failed to report key methodological details, such as sampling type (single-plant or pooled-plants) and sample size, potentially constraining the database’s practical applicability. Li et al. [23] subsequently developed molecular fingerprints for 36 asparagus cultivars using 10 SSR markers, with each cultivar represented by 10 numerical allele codes. This result is likely due to strict marker selection protocols, with an additional contributing factor being sampling limitations, which may significantly compromise the reliability of the fingerprint database. Compared with previous efforts, our fingerprint database demonstrates improved reliability and broader applicability, attributable to three key improvements: First, a robust sampling strategy involving the analysis of 20 individuals per cultivar to capture intraspecific allelic variation, thereby mitigating errors caused by insufficient or pooled sampling. Second, high-resolution fluorescence capillary electrophoresis ensures precise band size detection, while parental genotype-derived fingerprint further enhance database stability. Third, molecular marker selection was based on adherence to Mendelian inheritance, high polymorphism, and absence of genetic linkage, which significantly improves the discriminative power of the marker combinations. Future research should encompass expanding both the quantity and genetic diversity of tested cultivars, while strategically increasing the core markers and integrating of field phenotypic data to comprehensively address potential resolution limitations.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the genetic relationships among 62 asparagus cultivars using a set of 50 high-resolution EST-SSR markers. The cultivars were grouped into three genetically distinct subpopulations, with no significant correlation to their geographical origins- highlighting the complexity of asparagus breeding backgrounds. Furthermore, a core set of seven EST-SSR markers was used to establish reliable DNA fingerprints for 60 cultivars, enabling accurate, cost-effective, and practical identification of cultivar authenticity and purity. These results provide a valuable foundation for germplasm management, breeding program optimization, and cultivar protection in asparagus.
