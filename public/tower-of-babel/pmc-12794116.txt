Cognitive disengagement syndrome and peer relationships of youth with critical congenital heart defects
Authors: Olivia G Ritchey, Eva L Darow, Amber T Riggs, Andrea Goard, Jamie L Jackson, May Ling Mah, H Gerry Taylor, Kathryn Kirkpatrick, Kathryn Vannatta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWorldwide, approximately 1 in 100 children are born with a congenital heart defect, with 25% of those being classified as critical and posing risks for neurocognitive deficits due to cyanosis. Cognitive Disengagement Syndrome (CDS) has yet to be examined in children with critical congenital heart defects (cCHD) despite calls for research in pediatric populations with acquired brain injury and associated deficits. Children with cCHD, ages 9–13, were identified through patient registries and comparison classmates CC were matched after completing sociometric surveys of peer relationships in schools. Caregiver ratings of CDS symptoms and children’s self-report of peer interaction and social adjustment were obtained at home visits with both groups (NcCHD = 108, NCC = 72). Children with cCHD exhibited greater CDS symptoms than CC (d = 0.31, p = .011). CDS symptoms were associated with peer and self-reports of social difficulty, in particular the experience of peer victimization and fewer reciprocated friendships at school relative to healthy peers. CDS symptoms partially accounted for higher self-reported victimization and social problems in cCHD survivors but did not account for group differences in peer-reported social difficulties. These indirect effects were no longer significant after controlling for parent reports of broader symptoms of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Future research is needed to better disentangle the comparative influence of CDS and ADHD symptoms on the psychosocial outcomes of children with cCHD and other chronic conditions involving brain injury and heightened neurodevelopmental risk.Keywords: Critical congenital heart disease, cognitive disengagement syndrome, sluggish cognitive tempo, social adjustment, peer relationships

Congenital heart defects are the most common type of birth defect worldwide and are diagnosed in approximately 1% of live births (Gilboa et al., 2016; Hoffman & Kaplan, 2002). Diagnoses of congenital heart defects range from simple to complex, with the more severe forms requiring one or more surgical procedures. Advanced screening and medical care have improved survival significantly (Gilboa et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023). Consequently, there are increasing efforts to address potential developmental and functional complications affecting individuals with these anomalies, particularly those with a critical congenital heart defect (cCHD) (Areias et al., 2018; Bonthrone et al., 2021; Chew et al., 2024; Feldmann et al., 2021).

Characterized by neonatal cyanosis, cCHDs often require surgical intervention soon after birth (Harold, 2014; Liberman et al., 2014) and comprise approximately 25% of diagnosed children (Hoffman & Kaplan, 2002; Mahle et al., 2009). A central feature of cCHDs is the substantial risk of impaired neurodevelopment attributed to structural defects and surgeries involving time on bypass that undermine oxygen perfusion (Areias et al., 2018; Naef et al., 2017; Peyvandi et al., 2019). Consequently, children with a cCHD acquire a variety of neurocognitive deficits, including in executive functioning, attention, and visuospatial processing, which are associated with anatomical severity and treatment intensity (Bean Jaworski et al., 2018; Cassidy et al., 2015; Schlosser et al., 2022). These deficits have documented neuroanatomical underpinnings due to brain tissue damage from low blood oxygen levels (Mahle et al., 2009). A growing body of research has documented deficits across cognitive capacities and self-regulatory capabilities in cCHD survivors (Ehrler et al., 2023; Jackson et al., 2021; Schlosser et al., 2022; Wotherspoon et al., 2020) and suggests that children with cCHD have higher rates of neurodevelopmental comorbid disorders including attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD (Hasan et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2024).

Cognitive disengagement syndrome (CDS) is defined by symptoms of daydreaming, mental confusion, and hypoactivity (Becker, 2025). This neurobehavioral profile is a related but separate construct from ADHD, identified by empirically distinct symptoms and unique neurological features (Barkley, 2014; Becker, 2025; Willcutt et al., 2014). CDS symptoms have been linked to adverse behavioral and social outcomes in typically developing children (Becker, 2014; Becker et al., 2019; Fredrick et al., 2022; Tamm et al., 2016). Indeed, CDS has been associated with psychosocial outcomes above and beyond ADHD symptoms (Becker et al., 2016; Darow et al., 2025; Fredrick et al., 2024).

There have been recent calls to consider the possible emergence of CDS symptoms in children with chronic medical conditions and whether CDS symptoms help explain psychosocial difficulties documented for these youth (Fredrick et al., 2024). Elevations in CDS symptoms have been documented for children with chronic medical conditions involving the brain and known to be at risk for adverse functional and developmental outcomes above what may be expected for typically developing youth (Darow et al., 2025; Peterson et al., 2022; Willard et al., 2013). Interestingly, elevated CDS symptoms partially account for depressive symptoms reported by pediatric brain tumor survivors and for greater peer problems and behavioral regulation difficulties in children with sickle cell disease (Darow et al., 2025; Hardy et al., 2022). Despite this growing literature, there are no existing studies which examine the prevalence or correlates of CDS symptoms in children with cCHD (Fredrick et al., 2024).

Recent findings identify difficulties in peer relationships for children with cCHD. Diminished peer acceptance, social support, and impaired quality of social life have been identified in children with cCHD (Abda et al., 2019; Clancy et al., 2020; Feldmann et al., 2021; Heusch et al., 2014; McCusker et al., 2013). Proposed mechanisms for these social deficits include impairment in social cognition and executive functioning due to cyanosis and acquired brain injury (Bellinger, 2008; Feldmann et al., 2021; Sanz et al., 2023). Since CDS has not been assessed in children with cCHD, it is unknown whether this might account for peer relationship difficulties for these youth. Interestingly, studies suggest that children with congenital heart anomalies may have comorbid parent-reported ADHD symptoms and social difficulties (Werninger et al., 2020).

CDS symptoms have demonstrated associations with diverse social difficulties, including peer rejection, withdrawn behavior, and social disinterest in children without known medical challenge (Becker, 2014; Becker, Vaughn, et al., 2025; Fredrick & Becker, 2023; Mayes et al., 2023; Willcutt et al., 2014). Specifically, symptoms of CDS are associated with more frequent exclusion by peers, while prior longitudinal work links CDS symptoms as a predictor of shyness and social withdrawal (Becker et al., 2019; Fredrick & Becker, 2023). Longitudinal findings from teens with spina bifida suggest that CDS symptoms assessed during childhood and adolescence predict adolescent social functioning including social acceptance and variability in social skills (Darow et al., 2023).

The current study had three primary objectives. First, we tested group differences between children with cCHD and healthy peers on symptoms of CDS. We expected that parents of children with cCHD would report that their children had significantly more CDS symptoms than parents of demographically similar classmates without a chronic illness. Second, we examined bivariate correlations of CDS symptom levels with peer and self-reported difficulties in peer interactions and social adjustment and expected to find evidence that higher CDS symptoms would be associated with diminished social competence. Third, we investigated whether CDS symptom levels would account for group differences in social outcomes. We expected that group differences in peer interactions and social adjustment would be at least partially explained by greater CDS symptoms for youth with cCHD (Figure 1). Finally, we considered post hoc whether CDS symptoms accounted for group differences in peer relationships when controlling for ADHD symptoms that might be expected to correlate with CDS.

Method

Procedure

A patient registry at a large pediatric hospital in the midwestern United States was used to identify eligible children who a) had been diagnosed with cCHD (e.g., single ventricle anatomy, tetralogy of Fallot), b) were enrolled in a 4th or 5th grade class, c) had been seen by a cardiologist within 3 years, and d) resided within 120 miles of the hospital. Children were excluded if: a) they had been diagnosed with a genetic syndrome with known neurodevelopmental sequelae (e.g., Down Syndrome, 22q11 deletion), b) they were home-schooled or did not spend academic time in a mainstream classroom, or c) they and at least one legal guardian were not able to consent and complete assessments in English. These requirements were structured to ensure that participants and their caregivers could provide consent and assent as well as complete self-report questionnaires.

Caregivers received a letter introducing the study from their child’s cardiologist, followed by a phone call from study staff detailing the two-phase study, including a school visit and a home visit. Staff obtained permission to contact the child’s school principals and administrators to provide information and obtain permission to work with the teacher of the child’s primary classroom. Teachers obtained consent forms from parents for group administration of sociometric measures to assess peer interactions, social acceptance, and friendships. To protect confidentiality, the study was described to classmates and parents as a study of children’s relationships without reference to the hospital or the child with a cCHD. Surveys assessing peer interactions and social adjustment were administered in one group session by study staff. After completion of school data collection, comparison classmates (CC) were recruited to complete home visits identical to those for the cCHD sample. A parent of the participating same-sex classmate closest in age to the child with cCHD was contacted by phone for recruitment. If they were unable to be contacted, were ineligible, or declined, recruitment was attempted for the same-sex classmate next closest in age, and so forth. CCs were not eligible if they had received subspecialist treatment for a chronic medical condition of more than 6 months duration. Screening for this was completed with parents by study staff.

Home visits were scheduled with families of children in the cCHD and CC groups. Alternative locations were offered if preferred by parents. During home visits, children completed questionnaires measuring peer interactions and social adjustment. Caregivers completed surveys regarding family demographics and ratings of behavioral adjustment, including CDS symptoms. All procedures were approved by the hospital’s institutional review board, written informed consent and assent were obtained during each study stage, and funding allowed schools to receive $50 and families $100 following completion of data collection.

Participants

Eligibility was confirmed for 206 children with cCHD of whom 134 (65%) agreed to participate. Classroom data collection was completed for 92 of these children. Schools declined participation for a variety of reasons, including disinclination to allocate time to research, lack of time, or restrictions put in place post COVID-19 shutdowns. In completed classrooms, 1865 (89%) of available classmates had parental consent and completed sociometric measures. Home visits were completed with a total of 108 families in the cCHD sample, including 66 with and 42 without completed school visits. Twenty-six recruited cCHD families did not complete home-visits due to scheduling difficulties or diminished interest following school data collection, and these data are not utilized in the current paper. Home visits were completed for 72 CC, with 25 (34%) being first choice, 11 (15%) being second choice, and 36 (51%) third choice or higher CC. As a result, parent and self-report data were available for 180 children (NCHD = 108, NCC = 72) and additional school sociometric data for a subset of 138 of these children (NCHD = 66, NCC = 72). Caregivers included 168 legal guardians of children with cCHD and 103 of CC. Demographic data are presented in Table 1 and discussed in Results.

Measures

Classroom visits

Extended Class Play (ECP; Rubin et al., 2006).

Children participating in the classroom visits were instructed to pretend they were “the directors of a play starring children in their class” and provided written nominations of one classmate who best fit the positive and negative behavioral attributes of each of the 33 roles in the “play.” Classmates received nominations whether or not they were participating, and nominations were restricted to the gender of the cCHD survivor in that class to minimize sex-role stereo-typing. Item scores, reflecting the number of nominations participants received for each role were summed to create scale scores. Item and scale scores were standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) within sex in each class to adjust for unequal class sizes, compositions, and participation rates, as well as to create scores on a common scale. Each subscale represents a dimension of peer interaction. The current study focused on two subscales as indicators of negative peer interactions that have been linked to CDS in studies with other populations (Becker et al., 2019; Fredrick & Becker, 2023; Yung et al., 2021): Shy-Withdrawn (4 items, α = .88; e.g., “Someone who would rather play alone than with others”) and Rejection-Victimization (8 items, α = .87; e.g., “Someone who is picked on by other children”).

Best Friend Nomination (Bukowski et al., 1994).

Participants recorded the names of their three best friends in their classroom. Each nomination was coded as to whether the friendship was reciprocated, and the number of Reciprocated Friendships were tallied for each participant and standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) within gender to adjust for unequal class sizes, participation rates, and gender composition.

Peer Acceptance Ratings (Asher et al., 1979).

Participants completed 5-point ratings of how much they liked each classmate (1 = Dislike a lot to 5 = Like a lot). Acceptance ratings received by each student were averaged and standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) within gender for each class.

Home visits

Child report measures

Social Experiences Questionnaire (SEQ; Crick & Grotpeter, 1996).

Children rated the frequency (1 = Never to 5 = All the time) of 15 peer behaviors directed toward them involving Overt Victimization (e.g., how often do you get hit by another kid at school?; α = .72 for 5 items), Relational Victimization (how often do other kids leave you out on purpose when it is time to play or do an activity?; α = .87 for 5 items), and Prosocial Acts (“how often does another kid try to cheer you up when you feel sad or upset?”; α = .79 for 5 items).

Classroom visitsYouth Self-Report (YSR; Achenbach, 2009).

The YSR is a validated self-rating of behavioral and emotional symptoms for children ages 11–18 years old. The Social Problems subscale provided a self-report of social adjustment, with ratings based on how children perceived their social relationships over the previous 6 months. A raw sum of item ratings (0–2) was used for analysis and T scores reported for descriptive purposes. A higher score indicates worse social adjustment. National norms for this 11-item subscale reports α = .71–.95.

Parent report measures

Demographic Information.

Caregivers provided demographic information at the home visit (e.g., family income, child race and ethnicity).

The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach, 2009).

The CBCL is a parent rating of child behavior and emotional symptoms observed over the prior 6 months. The CBCL Sluggish Cognitive Tempo syndrome scale was used to assess symptoms of CDS and includes four items which evaluate cognitive disengagement (i.e., “daydreams or gets lost in thoughts,” “stares blankly,” “underactive, slow-moving, lacks energy,” and “confused or seems to be in a fog”). These items were summed, and the raw scores used in analyses to retain full variability. Scores ranged from 0 to 8, with higher scores representing greater levels of cognitive disengagement symptoms. Cronbach’s α for the current sample was 0.64. Although this internal consistency is not optimal, this value is aligned with prior research (Cronbach’s α = .68 Burns & Becker, 2022). The ADHD symptoms subscale consists of seven items (e.g., “can’t sit still, restless, or hyperactive”). Cronbach’s α = .87 for seven items.

Analysis plan

Aim 1: Independent samples t-tests were conducted to investigate the hypothesis that CDS survivors would exhibit higher levels of parent-report CDS symptoms than CC. Aim 2: Pearson nondirectional correlation coefficients were calculated to demonstrate bivariate associations of CDS with peer- and self-report of peer relationships. Aim 3: The correlation of CDS and ADHD symptoms was calculated to verify the value using this subscale as a covariate to estimate the unique explanatory value of CDS symptoms. Linear regression was conducted using SPSS v29. The PROCESS v.4 macro (Hayes, 2022) was used to examine the significance of indirect effects of group (1 = cCHD, 0 = CC) on each of 8 peer relationship measures via parent-reported symptoms of CDS. Predictors were entered sequentially adding group and CDS symptom scores in Step 1 and group, CDS, and ADHD symptoms in Step 2. The significance of estimated indirect effects was tested using 10,000 repeated subsamples to construct bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals. Although this procedure is statistically equivalent to a simple mediation model (Model 4), with CDS symptoms mediating group differences in peer relationships, reference to “indirect effects” is more appropriate given the cross-sectional study design. Indirect effects are considered significantly different than zero when the 95% confidence interval does not include zero.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

Demographic characteristics for both cCHD and CC groups are presented in Table 1. The groups were similar in mean age and sex distribution, with the majority being male. Groups did not differ significantly on other demographic variables (e.g., family income, caregiver relation to child). Participants were primarily White and non-Hispanic. The most prevalent cCHD diagnosis in our sample was single ventricle anatomy.

Group difference in CDS symptoms

The cCHD group (M = 1.22, SD = 1.38) had higher levels of parent-reported CDS symptoms than the CC group (M = 0.84, SD = 1.03); t(175) = 2.14, p = .034, d = 0.31.

Correlations

Bivariate correlations examined the simple associations of CDS symptoms with peer- and self-report measures of peer interaction and social adjustment (Table 2). Elevated CDS symptoms were associated with higher levels of Rejection-Victimization and Shy-Withdrawn behavior from the peer-reported ECP and fewer Reciprocated Friendships as reported by self and peers. Higher CDS scores were also associated with higher self-reported ratings of Overt Victimization and Relational Victimization on the SEQ. Finally, higher CDS symptom levels were significantly associated with lower levels of self-reported social adjustment represented by the YSR Social Problems subscale. An unexpected positive association was found between higher CDS scores and self-report on the SEQ of receiving more Prosocial Acts from peers.

Regarding the association between CDS and ADHD symptoms reported by parents, a significant correlation was found (r = .47, p < .001). Thus, the ADHD subscale was added as a post hoc covariate to examine whether indirect effects involving CDS symptoms remained significant controlling for ADHD symptoms (Table 3). Discussion

The objectives of this study were to understand differences in CDS symptoms between children with cCHD and a control group of demographically similar classmates and to consider whether elevations in CDS symptoms might inform our understanding of peer relationship difficulties in cCHD survivors. This is significant as symptoms of CDS have yet to be examined in children with cCHD and experts have advocated for more attention to CDS in children with chronic health conditions (Fredrick et al., 2024). Furthermore, the existing research on social sequalae of neurocognitive difficulties in children with cCHD is limited (Bellinger & Newburger, 2010). Our findings demonstrate elevations in CDS symptoms for children with cCHD that are in turn linked to more negative peer interactions, e.g., peer victimization, and poorer social adjustment, including fewer mutually recognized friendships with classmates. These findings are notable as few studies in the CDS and cCHD literatures, respectively, have examined facets of peer relationships which may help characterize the social environments of children with CDS or cCHD, such as dyadic friendship and peer interactions as reported by peers (Becker, 2025; Bellinger & Newburger, 2010). There was preliminary evidence that elevated CDS symptoms partially account for victimization and poorer social adjustment reported by the cCHD survivors, but these effects became nonsignficant when ADHD symptoms were taken into account. These findings suggest that future research to disentangle the unique effects of CDS and ADHD symptomatology on social outcomes is warranted but would benefit from improved assessment approaches.

Within the existing literature on CDS in pediatric populations, our findings are consistent with some (Darow et al., 2025; Georgsdottir et al., 2013) but not all (Darow et al., 2023) studies of children with a chronic medical condition known to impact the central nervous system. Pediatric brain tumor and extremely low birth weight survivors demonstrate significantly heightened CDS symptoms compared to control groups without known medical conditions (Darow et al., 2025; Georgsdottir et al., 2013; Willard et al., 2013). Contrastingly, research on youth with spina bifida and idiopathic epilepsy found that parent-reported CDS symptoms were significantly lower, not higher, than typically developing children (Darow et al., 2023; Moreira et al., 2014). Such inconsistencies suggest that further research into the medical risks associated with the temporal development of CDS is needed. Identification of the neurobiological underpinnings of CDS may elucidate the links between ADHD and CDS and improve our understanding of the etiology of CDS in different populations (such as pediatric groups with acquired brain injury) (Becker, 2025). Studies which have explored this topic suggest that CDS may be linked to impaired connections in the default mode network and ventral and dorsal attention networks (Becker, Braimah, et al., 2024; Camprodon-Rosanas et al., 2019). Neuroimaging research in pediatric populations (including cCHD, pediatric brain tumor, and traumatic brain injury) has identified impairments in white matter integrity that are associated with deficits in higher order cognitive skills that may be linked to CDS (Becker, 2025), such as poorer processing speed (Glazer et al., 2024), working memory (Easson et al., 2023), and attention (Berger et al., 2023).

This study had several strengths in its methodological approach. Previous studies have confirmed variability in the detection of social adjustment difficulties by reporter, with caregivers reporting greater social difficulties than children with congenital heart defects themselves (Miatton et al., 2007; Spijkerboer et al., 2008). Peer report is a highly reliable source in studies of child development (Clemans et al., 2014), but self-report is also of significant concern in understanding the quality of life and internal experience of children contending with health risks and intensive medical procedures. Thus, we used multisource reports from cCHD survivors and demographically similar CC to assess peer interactions and social adjustment. This study is, to our knowledge, the first, or at least one of the first, within the CDS and cCHD literatures, respectively, which uses sociometric measures. Moreover, this study is the first to examine CDS in a sample of children with a congenital heart defect, despite hypotheses that CDS symptoms may be apparent in pediatric populations (Calderon & Bellinger, 2015; Fredrick et al., 2024).

There are limitations to this study which should be addressed in future research. First, the CBCL subscale of CDS (i.e., sluggish cognitive tempo) only incorporates four items. This tool may have limited scope and exclude symptoms included in more recent conceptualizations of CDS, such as mental fogginess (Becker, 2021). Utilizing a tool which assesses CDS more robustly and was developed to assess CDS as a distinct disorder, such as the Child and Adolescent Behavior Inventory (Burns et al., 2022) or the Child Concentration Inventory Second Edition (Becker et al., 2015), is recommended. Further, the interpretation of linear regression analyses presented in this article may be difficult due to the issue of multicollinearity that arises when using subscales from the same measure (CBCL Sluggish Cognitive Tempo and ADHD symptoms) and reporter (parents). While the CBCL is the most frequently used measure of CDS symptoms in pediatric populations and thus allows for comparison of findings, pediatric studies aiming to examine CDS should explore those symptoms (e.g., daydreaming, mental confusion, and hypoactivity) which show promising reliability and validity in the assessment CDS (Becker, 2025; Fredrick et al., 2024).

Second, incorporating longitudinal data into a study design would elucidate directionality and temporal effects of CDS on functional outcomes. Our data were cross-sectional and collected from a sample with restricted age range. Thus, we are unable to address developmental differences in CDS symptoms or the direction of effects. The age group of this sample (9–13 years old) aligns with important neurocognitive and psychosocial skill development. CDS symptoms have been found to increase slightly overtime, with CDS scores in childhood being strongly associated with scores in adulthood (Leopold et al., 2016; Sadeghi-Bahmani et al., 2025). These data from middle to late childhood are important for understanding the stability of CDS symptoms overtime, particularly as longitudinal work on CDS in cCHD has yet to be done.

Third, given the lack of published work on CDS and peer relationships of children with cCHD, we examined individual indicators rather than creating latent variables of negative peer-interactions and social adjustment. We made this choice in part to examine reporter differences but also because our modest sample size may be insufficient for structural equation modeling. Future work with larger samples might consolidate multiple indicators of CDS and peer relationships into fewer models. Finally, our findings may be limited by demographic homogeneity, as most participants identified as white and non-Hispanic, reflecting the patient population at our midwestern children’s hospital. Future studies should consider multisite collaboration and explore sampling techniques such as stratified sampling to purposefully recruit families that are representative of broader demographics.

In conclusion, the present study addresses a gap in our understanding of CDS in children with medical conditions (Fredrick et al., 2024). Consistent with work involving children with acquired brain injury, results from this study indicate that children with cCHD have elevated symptoms of CDS compared to healthy peers and higher levels of these symptoms associated with more difficulties in peer interactions and diminished social adjustment. Elevated levels of CDS symptoms may partially account for differences between children with cCHD and their peers in self-reported peer relationship difficulties, but this effect may not be unique from co-occurring ADHD symptoms. Further research is needed to disentangle these effects with enhanced assessments and longitudinal designs. In the meantime, clinical teams may benefit from awareness that children with cCHD may be at risk for elevated symptoms of CDS as well as ADHD. Screening batteries could be expanded to assess this risk. If not universally screened, CDS should be considered amongst other possible mechanisms explaining peer relationship problems for youth with cCHD.
