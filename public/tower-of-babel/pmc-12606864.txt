Patterns and association between smokeless tobacco consumption and hemoglobin level among 6 to 12 years children: analytical cross-sectional study
Authors: Zeeshan Ahsan, Aysha Almas, Romania Iqbal, Sheraz Ahmed, Shiyam Sunder Tikmani, Zehra Jamil, Fayaz Ahmed, Sadaf Jakhro, Khaliq Qureshi, Shareef Dars, Anum Rahim, Syed Asad Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSmokeless tobacco (SLT) use is a growing and significant public health issue in low- and middle-income countries. SLT consumption impairs nutrient absorption, particularly iron, leading to poor nutritional status and reduced hemoglobin levels. The study aimed to assess the association between SLT consumption and hemoglobin levels (Hb) among 6 to 12-year-old school-going children in rural Matiari, Pakistan.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted over 40 days (August–September 2024) in four schools in Matiari, Pakistan. Children aged 6–12 years from classes I–V who provided assent and parental consent were included. Those with hematological diseases, nutritional deficiencies, or a history of intestinal worm infestation were excluded. A systematic sampling strategy was employed to enroll at least 14 students per class and a minimum of 70 children per school. Data on SLT use, dietary habits, and health-related factors were collected using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire via REDCap software. SLT use was defined as consuming any SLT product for at least five minutes per day within the last 30 days. Hemoglobin (Hb) levels (g/dL) were measured using the HemoCue Hb 301 system device, while height (cm) and weight (kg) were recorded using a digital scale. Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0.ResultAmong 283 participants, 34 (12.01%) reported SLT use. The median hemoglobin level among SLT users was 10.8 g/dL (IQR: 9.8–11.7) compared to 11.2 g/dL (IQR: 10.1–12.2) among non-users. The difference in hemoglobin levels was not statistically significant (p = 0.17). 151 (53.36%) were boys, and the median age was 8.06 years (IQR: 7–9). In multivariable analysis, SLT use was associated with a − 0.40 g/dL change in hemoglobin (95% CI: − 0.97 to 0.17) (p = 0.28). Most of the children used SLT for 1–3 days in the past 30 days (79.41%).ConclusionSLT users had lower hemoglobin levels, although the results were not statistically significant. Overall, children have lower hemoglobin levels, and most of them used SLT for the first time before age 6 or between ages 7 and 8, which is a major public health concern.Keywords: Smokeless tobacco, Hemoglobin, Children, Public health, Pakistan

Introduction

The Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), in its Global Burden of Disease 2019 report, attributes an annual loss of 8.71 million lives to tobacco use, including 2.5 million Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), a metric representing years lost to ill-health, disability, or premature death, due to smokeless tobacco (SLT) alone [1, 2]. Globally, over 300 million individuals consume SLT, contributing to approximately 390,000 cases of oral and pharyngeal cancers annually [3]. The burden is especially high in the Southeast Asian region, where SLT use is more prevalent among boys than girls [4]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 27.1% of U.S. high school students report using some form of tobacco, with 5.9% consuming SLT products [5]. Common forms include areca nut, gutka, pan with tobacco, snuff, dip, and naswar; each is associated with significant health risks and rising morbidity and mortality [6].

In Pakistan, the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (2014–2017) reported an SLT prevalence of 8.6%, with initiation often occurring in early adolescence. Prevalence among boys is substantially higher than among girls [7]. Ethnic and geographic variations also exist, with Sindhi populations showing the highest SLT use (23.9%) and boys aged 11–20 years being the most affected group [8]. In regions like Lasbela, Balochistan, children as young as 7–11 years demonstrate daily gutka and supari addiction, correlating with a clinical anemia prevalence of 18.7% [9]. Although Pakistan imposed a nationwide ban on tobacco product use and storage in 2019, enforcement remains inconsistent, especially in rural settings [10, 11]. A recent survey in Karachi found that 42.6% of schoolchildren used SLT products, underscoring the urgent need for action [12].

SLT use among children is increasingly linked to adverse nutritional outcomes. The consumption of tobacco often displaces nutrient-rich foods, contributing to deficiencies in essential vitamins and minerals such as iron, vitamin C, and folate [13]. Areca nut and slaked lime—key ingredients in many SLT products—interfere with iron absorption and contribute to iron-deficiency anemia [14]. Chronic use is also associated with appetite suppression, gastrointestinal discomfort, food insecurity, and elevated risk of oral cancer [15, 16]. Moreover, SLT users exhibit heightened levels of plasma nitric oxide, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation, suggesting a pathophysiological link between nicotine exposure and systemic inflammation, anemia, and cardiovascular risk [17].

Despite the mounting evidence, limited research has focused on the impact of SLT use on hemoglobin levels and nutritional health among children in rural Pakistan. The issue is particularly concerning in school environments, where early behavioral patterns are formed and reinforced [10, 18]. SLT use in schools is not only a public health concern but also a missed opportunity for preventive education and early intervention [18]. Although studies have explored SLT use in adolescents, data are sparse on younger children aged 6–12 years, especially in underserved rural districts like Matiari, Sindh.

While previous studies in South Asia have linked SLT use to anemia and poor nutritional status primarily among women, limited data exist on this relationship in younger children aged 6 to 12 years, particularly in underserved rural populations. Additionally, few studies have explored the interaction between SLT use and dietary quality, including micronutrient intake. This study seeks to address these gaps by assessing the association between SLT use and hemoglobin levels in rural Pakistani schoolchildren, while accounting for dietary intake and sociodemographic factors. Findings from this age group can inform school-based interventions targeting early prevention of SLT use and nutritional anemia.

This study aimed to assess the association between smokeless tobacco (SLT) consumption and hemoglobin levels among 6- to 12-year-old schoolchildren in Matiari, Pakistan. Additionally, it sought to explore patterns of SLT use, including frequency, duration, and type of product consumed among children, to inform early preventive and school-based public health interventions.

Method

Study design The study employed an analytical cross-sectional design, utilizing the Standard for Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE; https://www.strobe-statement.org/checklists/) for reporting.

Study setting

The study was conducted in four co-educational primary schools (two public and two private) in Matiari, a rural district in southeastern Sindh, Pakistan (Appendix 1). Spanning 1,417 square kilometers, Matiari is situated along the Indus River and has a population of approximately 770,000 (Census 2017). The district hosts 918 primary schools, with variable infrastructure [19]. Schools were selected based on accessibility, permissions, and to represent both public and private sectors.

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised children aged 6–12 years enrolled in Grades I to V in selected schools in Matiari, Sindh. A systematic sampling technique was employed using a class attendance list. A minimum of 70 students per school (14 per class) were selected regardless of school size. The sampling interval (K) was calculated per class by dividing the total number of enrolled students (N) by the required sample size (n = 14). A random starting point was selected using a lottery method, and every Kᵗʰ student was enrolled. If a student declined or was absent, the next eligible student was approached.

Sample size The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi v3.01, assuming a mean hemoglobin difference of 1 g/dL (SD = 2), 95% confidence, 80% power, and a 1:9 SLT user-to-non-user ratio from previous studies in Pakistan. After adjusting for a 12% non-response rate, the required sample was 280 children; the final sample was evenly distributed across the four schools, with at least 70 students per school and 14 per class.

Eligibility criteria

Children aged 6–12 years with both parental consent and child assent were eligible. Exclusion criteria included a history of chronic hematologic conditions (e.g., thalassemia, leukemia), current iron/multivitamin supplementation, documented nutritional deficiencies, or worm infestation. These were based on self-report, as a confirmatory diagnostic test was not feasible.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was hemoglobin level (g/dL), measured using the HemoCue Hb 301 system, a validated, point-of-care device with 90% sensitivity and 80% specificity [20]. WHO defines anemia in children aged 6–12 years as Hb < 11.5 g/dL [21]. Daily device calibration was performed using control cuvettes provided by the manufacturer. There was no blinding of data collectors to SLT status during hemoglobin assessment.

Exposure variable

Smokeless tobacco use

SLT use was defined as the use of non-combusted tobacco products placed in the oral or nasal cavity, including gutka, zarda, khaini, snuff, naswar, and betel quid with tobacco. Consumption patterns were assessed using a structured questionnaire adapted from Readshaw et al. (2020), with additional questions on frequency and duration of use [10].

Dietary intake assessment

A 65-item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), modified to local dietary patterns and children’s comprehension, assessed dietary intake over the previous 4 weeks [22, 23]. Food items were grouped into cereals, dairy, meats, fruits, vegetables, pulses, snacks, and beverages. A food catalog aided recall. Daily nutrient intakes were calculated and cross-checked against WHO and national dietary reference values. Implausible values (>3 SD from the mean or exceeding physiological thresholds) were reviewed and excluded.

Data collection procedures

After obtaining school permissions, parent consent forms were sent home with the child. Those children who had provided filled parent consent forms were then given child assent before participation, and assent was obtained. Three trained research assistants, after a two-day standardized training, collected data and measurements using tablets on the REDCap application for real-time data entry and quality control. Data was collected in private rooms provided by the schools to ensure confidentiality.

Data collection tool (Questionnaire)

We conducted a pre-test of the questionnaire among children aged 6 to 12 years within the study population. During this phase, we identified difficulties and flow in understanding certain questions. Subsequently, we have made modifications to the questionnaire, refined the questionnaire, and enhanced the overall flow and understanding of questions. The data collection tool consisted of five sections. The first section consists of 11 questions that gather participants’ information, which was followed by another section that consists of 6 questions that include demographic information of participants, their homes, and family information. The third section included 19 questions related to SLT consumption, out of which 13 questions are adapted from Readshaw A. et al. (2020) [24], who developed these questions mirroring the question about smoking tobacco from the Global Youth Tobacco Survey. Two additional questions were added about the number of packets taken daily and the time during which SLT is kept in the mouth [25]. Four additional questions were added to assess the oral health condition of children. The fourth section involves a food frequency questionnaire, where participants were asked about their dietary intake [23]. The fourth section encompassed physical examinations and consisted of 3 questions, wherein height (cm), weight (Kg), and Hemoglobin Level (g/dl) were measured and recorded. The height was measured in centimeters using a portable stadiometer, which is a valid and reliable tool [26]. The weight was measured in kilograms using a weighing scale, which is a valid and reliable tool [27]. The Hemoglobin Level was measured in grams per deciliter using the HemoCue 301 system, which was a valid and reliable tool for measuring hemoglobin [20]. The questionnaire has been adopted from the study, which was used in the Pakistani population. After translation into the Sindhi language, pilot testing of the questionnaire was performed in 10% of the total sample population in a private and a public school in Matiari. Hemoglobin measurement was conducted immediately following questionnaire administration, which included SLT-related questions. As a result, data collectors were not blinded to the participants’ SLT status during hemoglobin assessment.

Bias minimization

To minimize the potential biases in the study, a set of systematic measures was implemented. A systematic sampling technique was used to minimize selection bias and ensure randomness. Information bias was minimized by employing a trained research assistant who utilized a pretested structured questionnaire for data collection. The use of the Calibrated HemoCue Hb 301 system, stadiometer, and digital weight scale further enhances precision, with daily checks to promptly identify and rectify any errors. The other confounding variables were controlled during the analysis phase. Pilot testing of the questionnaire tool was performed in 10% of the study sample. To minimize selection bias, schools were chosen to ensure representation across different sub-regions of Matiari, including both relatively urbanized and rural catchment schools.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA v17 [28]. Descriptive statistics included medians (Interquartile Range, IQR), and frequencies (percentage %). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test assessed differences in hemoglobin and nutrient intake. FFQ-derived nutrient data were merged with anthropometric files using participant ID. Nutrient composition was calculated using the USDA Food Composition Database. Multivariable linear regression was used to assess the association between SLT use and hemoglobin level, controlling for sociodemographic and dietary variables. Crude and adjusted beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were reported, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

The study received approval from the Ethical Review Committee of Aga Khan University (ERC No: 2024-9867-28546). Written parental consent and verbal & written child assent were obtained. Data confidentiality was ensured through password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Participation was voluntary, and withdrawal was allowed at any stage. A health education session on SLT was conducted for all students after the study completion.

Result

A total of 331 school-going children were approached during screening. Thirty-eight children were excluded based on the exclusion criteria. Parental consent and assent were obtained from 293 students, while 10 children withdrew before the study’s completion. Ultimately, 283 children remained in the study and were included in the final analysis, as shown in Fig. 1.

Discussion

The findings from our study showed that smokeless tobacco (SLT) users had lower mean hemoglobin levels compared to non-users, though the difference was not statistically significant. Additionally, among SLT users, most of them have their parents or friends who are also using SLT. Overall, children have lower hemoglobin levels, and most of them used SLT for the first time before age 6 or between ages 7 and 8 years. There was a significant interaction between gender and SLT use, where female SLT users had a decrease in hemoglobin levels compared to male non-users, even after adjusting for dietary intake, father’s occupation, and union council. This gender disparity may be influenced by early onset of menstruation, cultural differences in SLT type and frequency, or dietary disparities. Although noteworthy, these results should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger, stratified studies.

Our findings are consistent with recent systematic reviews that highlight the nutritional and hematologic risks of SLT use among children and adolescents, particularly in low-resource settings [29, 30]. They also align with studies from India, Bangladesh, and Nepal linking adolescent SLT use to anemia, growth faltering, and poor dietary intake [31–33]. For example, a school-based study in rural Bangladesh reported early SLT initiation among adolescents, strongly influenced by family and peers [32]. Similarly, Indian surveys have found widespread youth SLT use, with significant associations with lower hemoglobin and stunted growth [34]. Our study adds unique evidence from rural Pakistan, where pediatric SLT use remains underreported.

Despite the 2019 national ban on gutka and other SLT products [35]. Our findings highlight continued use among school-aged children, mostly via family and peer networks. This reflects weak enforcement of tobacco control laws and underscores the need for stricter retail compliance monitoring. Community-based and school-based awareness campaigns are essential to address early SLT initiation and its long-term health effects [36]. After raising awareness, the implementation of the law can help to curb the burden of tobacco in Pakistan. SLT use, particularly among girls, has been linked to reduced hemoglobin levels [37] through mechanisms such as appetite suppression and interference with iron absorption [29]. In our sample, SLT use was more prevalent among boys, consistent with evidence that social norms and peer influence drive higher tobacco consumption in males [8].

A highlighted concern is early initiation; several children reported first use before age six. Such early exposure increases the risk of long-term dependence and cumulative health harm. Higher SLT prevalence in government school students may reflect lower socioeconomic status, limited healthcare access, and inadequate nutrition [30], echoing other South Asian studies linking tobacco use to poverty, affordability, easy availability, and weak regulation [38]. Family influence remains a key driver; many children obtained SLT from relatives or friends, reflecting high community acceptance despite the ban [18].

Nutritional assessment showed that SLT users consumed less iron, zinc, and energy—nutrients critical for growth and development, and had poorer oral health than non-users, with higher prevalence of bleeding gums, tooth pain, and oral thrush. These oral conditions can exacerbate nutritional deficiencies by reducing food intake. Poor oral health among SLT users has been reported previously [39].

In rural Sindh, SLT use is deeply embedded in cultural and social practices—products like gutka, paan, and naswar are widely accepted, often normalized across generations, and perceived as benign or beneficial [40]. Health risk awareness remains low among the public, and despite regulatory bans, these products remain readily accessible [41]. Gutka and mawa contain slaked lime and areca nut, both known to impair iron absorption and disrupt iron metabolism [42], highlighting the need for further investigation into product-specific hemoglobin effects.

Despite strength, this study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The small number of SLT users, particularly females, reduced statistical power for stratified analysis. The sampling frame, restricted to four rural schools in Matiari, limits generalizability to other regions of Pakistan. SLT use was self-reported without biochemical validation (e.g., salivary cotinine), which may have led to exposure misclassification. Important confounders such as subclinical infections, inflammation, and worm infestation were not objectively assessed. Hemoglobin was measured using the HemoCue device, which may underestimate values, potentially overestimating anemia prevalence.

To mitigate both tobacco-related harm and nutritional deficiencies, school health programs should integrate SLT prevention into curricula alongside nutrition interventions such as iron-rich meals or fortified snacks. Enforcement and implementation of existing tobacco control laws, especially bans on sales to minors, must be strengthened. Future research should employ larger, representative samples, incorporate biochemical validation, and adopt mixed methods approaches to explore sociocultural drivers of early initiation. Interventional trials combining SLT cessation with nutritional supplementation in schools could help develop scalable models for child health promotion in high-risk settings.

Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence from rural Pakistan indicating that smokeless tobacco (SLT) use among school-aged children is associated with lower mean hemoglobin levels, with a notable gender interaction showing greater reductions among female users. Although the overall difference was not statistically significant, the observed patterns, coupled with early initiation, poor dietary intake, and high family influence, highlight an important yet under-recognized public health concern. Continued SLT use despite regulatory bans underscores the need for stricter enforcement of tobacco control laws, targeted school-based prevention programs, and integration of nutrition interventions to address anemia risk. Future large-scale, representative studies with biochemical exposure validation can help to strengthen these findings and guide comprehensive child health strategies.
