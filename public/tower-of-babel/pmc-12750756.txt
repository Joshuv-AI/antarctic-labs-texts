Health facilities readiness and associated factors to provide growth monitoring services in low-income and middle-income countries: evidence from national service provision assessment surveys of six countries
Authors: Darik Temesgen Assefa, Nigus Kabtu Belete, Mehert Kitaw Gebretsadik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesMalnutrition among under-5 children is a major challenge in low-income and middle-income countries, contributing to poor growth and development. Growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) is recommended by WHO for early detection, yet its implementation remains weak due to limited resources and health system gaps. While many studies focus on utilisation, little is known about facility readiness, especially from multicountry analyses. This study aimed to assess the readiness of health facilities to provide growth monitoring (GM) service and associated factors using nationally representative data.DesignSecondary analysis of existing cross-sectional survey data.SettingSecondary data from service provision assessment surveys of Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Haiti, Nepal and Tanzania was used.Participants and outcome measureWe included 6595 health facilities, which have complete information. Health facilities’ readiness score was constructed as a counting score with regard to the availability of essential indicators required to provide GM services by using WHO service availability and readiness assessment.ResultAmong 6595 health facilities, 28.5% (95% CI 27.4% to 29.6%) of the facilities reported at least 75% of the relevant items for GM service provision. The factors associated with health facilities’ readiness to provide GM service were quality assurance activities (incident rate ratio (IRR): 1.15; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.24), separate user fees (IRR: 1.10; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.20), fixed user fees (IRR: 1.20; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.31) and presence of trained health provider at facility 24 hours (IRR: 1.09; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.17).ConclusionsHealth facilities in six countries showed low readiness for GMP services. Strengthening facility capacity, staffing, equipment and quality assurance is essential to improve readiness.

Introduction

Malnutrition, among under-5 children, is a major public health problem that affects the growth and development of the children in many countries worldwide. Many children under 5 years of age are not receiving adequate nutrition and are exposed to poor growth and development, especially in low‐income and middle‐income countries. Globally, in 2024 an estimated 150.2 million children under the age of 5 were stunted, 42.8 million wasted and 35.5 million overweight and obese. Among these, 59% stunted, 73% wasted and 35% overweight were from low-income and middle-income countries.1 2 Impaired growth and development leads to delayed mental development, poor school performance, reduced intellectual capacity and impaired immune system, which increases the risk of diseases.3 4 Early detection of growth defects is crucial for initiating timely interventions to prevent them from becoming complicated. To tackle this issue, the WHO has recommended the growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) programme as part of essential primary healthcare to address childhood malnutrition.5

GMP is an intervention that involves anthropometric measurements by comparing them with an appropriate standard to assess the adequacy of the child’s growth. The measurements are viewed by plotting them on a growth chart and applied for promotion activities in the form of tailored, individual counselling for parents.5 6 In addition to its importance in identifying malnourished children early, it also helps to support primary healthcare delivery and serves as an entry point to broader maternal and child health services.7 Even though GMP programmes have been widely implemented for many decades, their appropriateness and effectiveness have been questioned since the 1980s. The main issues focused largely on low GM service provision, poor health professional performance and inadequacies in health system infrastructure that constrained effective GMP action.8 9 Despite its importance, due to the scarcity of health resources in many low‐income and middle‐income countries, the implementation has been suboptimal and there are ongoing debates over universal coverage and how to allocate available resources.10

A lot of studies have been conducted on the utilisation of GM service in low-income and middle-income countries and there is a scarcity of evidence on the readiness of health facilities, particularly from multicountry analyses. Multicountry studies are required to present facility capacities to perform GM services for nations with comparable sociocultural traits. To address this gap, this study aimed to assess the readiness of health facilities to provide GM service using nationally representative Service Provision Assessment (SPA) surveys from six low-income and middle-income countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Haiti, Nepal and Tanzania. Additionally, aimed to examine readiness differences by country and identify health system factors associated with readiness. Findings of this study provide cross-country evidence to guide targeted interventions and resource mobilisation to strengthen GM service provision in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Data sources

This study used secondary data from the SPA survey of six low-resource countries: Afghanistan (2018),11 Bangladesh (2022),12 Ethiopia (2021),13 Haiti (2016),14 Nepal (2021)15 and Tanzania (2015).16 The surveys were designed to provide information on the basic healthcare services availability and the health facilities readiness to provide quality services to clients, in collaboration with technical experts, representatives from WHO, UNICEF, Ministries of Health and others.

Country and health facility selection

The countries were selected based on the following criteria using SPA survey data: (1) the survey which was carried out in the year 2015 or later, (2) the presence of GM service-related questions and (3) the availability of specific tracer items for GM services. In addition, the functionality status of health facilities at the time of the survey was taken into account when we selected the health facilities in each selected country (figure 1).

Variables

Outcome variable

Health facilities readiness to provide GM services was the outcome variable in this analysis. It is a composite measure which was developed as a counting score with regard to the availability of essential indicators required to GM services provision within the facility. Three domains were employed to assess readiness for the delivery of GM services and a total of eight indicators were identified from the three domains. The WHO service availability and readiness assessment reference manual was used for the selection of indicators.17

The first domain comprised one indicator related to staff, measured by the presence of at least one trained personnel with any GM training. The second domain consisted of six indicators pertaining to essential supplies that are necessary for GM services provision, namely: infant scale, child scale, height/length board, tape to measure head circumference, tape to measure mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) and growth chart. The third domain includes one indicator related to the availability of GMP guideline. These all comprised a total of eight indicators.

To determine the availability of each of the eight indicators, binary variables were created, presence or absence. An equal weight was assigned to each indicator’s contribution to the total score. The readiness score was calculated by summing the presence of each indicator (seen and recorded by the interviewers), ranging from 0 to 8. A facility was ‘fully ready’ to provide services of GM, if all of these items were available.

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables were selected from the SPA survey data of the countries. The following variables were considered: facility location, managing authority, external sources of revenue, quality assurance activities, routine management meetings, routine staff meetings, external supervision, user fees, feedback on clients’ opinions, number of weeks GM service provided and presence of trained health professional at facility 24 hours with duty schedule or present on-call.

The location of the facility was classified as rural or urban, and managing authority was categorised as either public (government-owned) or private. External sources of funds were assessed in terms of whether the facilities were funded additionally or not. Quality assurance activities of facilities were assessed to determine if they regularly conduct quality assurance activities within the last 12 months or not. External supervision was indicated as whether the facilities were supervised by senior officials such as district or region health management teams in the past 12 months or not. Management meeting and staff meeting were examined as to whether the facilities reported having regular meetings in the past 12 months or not.

A categorical variable was created to account for user fees as none, an independent charge per each service and a fixed fee for every service, provided to patients. Two categories which are reviewed and non-reviewed were created to gather customer feedback. Finally, the number of weeks in which GM services offered per month was classified into three groups: 4 weeks, 2–3 weeks and 1 week.

Data management and analysis

The available SPA datasets for all six countries were pooled after applying pre-specified inclusion criteria. Harmonisation and cleaning of data were undertaken to ensure data consistency across surveys, as names of variables, coding schemes and tracer item availability were different. Only open facilities with successful interviews were included. The analysis was conducted using SPSS V.25.0 software. Multicollinearity between independent variables was checked for all candidate variables by using variance inflation factor. Descriptive statistics such as simple frequencies, mean and SD were used to summarise the facilities characteristics and availability of GM tracer items.

GM service readiness score was constructed from the availability of eight tracer items. Since MUAC was not applicable in Afghanistan and Tanzania, the readiness score was computed in two models. In the first model, the readiness score was analysed based on the seven common items across all six countries, ranging from 0 to 7. In model 2, all eight items were included to construct GM service readiness score, restricted to the four countries where MUAC was available (Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Haiti and Nepal), ranging from 0 to 8. Since the readiness score was a count variable with overdispersion (model 1: mean=3.03 and variance=4.56 while in model 2: mean=3.67 and variance=6.38), negative binomial regression was used to assess the association between readiness and explanatory variables. Initially, a bivariate analysis was conducted, and independent variables with a p<0.25 were selected for multivariable regression. The results were presented in incident rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CI, and statistical significance was at p<0.05. Finally, the results were presented in text, tables and figures.

Results

Background characteristics of facilities

This study included a weighted total of 6595 health facilities. The majority of facilities were from Nepal (23.9%) and Bangladesh (23.1%), while the least were from Afghanistan (2.2%). Almost all facilities in Afghanistan were urban-based (99.3%), while the majority in Bangladesh (74.9%), Haiti (62.5%) and Tanzania (63.6%) were located in rural areas. Ethiopia and Nepal showed a more balanced distribution, though urban facilities were slightly higher in Ethiopia (52.3%) and Nepal (65.2%). Public facilities dominated in Bangladesh (85%), Nepal (81%) and Ethiopia (71.6%).

All of the six countries reported receiving additional funds from government, non-governmental organisations and other sources. Regarding routine management and staff meetings, the data were not available for Bangladesh. However, out of 5071 facilities in the five countries, 79.1% and 59.5% of facilities held routine management and staff meetings, respectively. Among these, 47.7% and 32.1% of facilities had documented evidence of recent management and staff meeting, respectively. Routine quality assurance activities were not performed in most of the health facilities in all the six countries. Client opinions were reviewed in all of the health facilities in Nepal and in about three-fourths of facilities in Afghanistan (table 1).

Availability of growth monitoring service

The availability of items for growth monitoring (GM) service varied across the six countries. GM guidelines were available in 5.6%–43.7% of facilities, with the highest availability observed in Ethiopia and the lowest in Afghanistan. The proportion of facilities with trained staff in GM services ranged from 12% to 60.2%, with relatively low coverage in Afghanistan (12%) and Nepal (19.8%). Head circumference tape was most available in Tanzania (54.5%) followed by Ethiopia (44.4%), with the least availability in Afghanistan (7%). MUAC tapes were not relevant to Afghanistan and Tanzania, but among the remaining countries, the best availability was in Nepal and Ethiopia (64.4% each), followed by Bangladesh (55.9%) and Haiti (47.8%). The most readily available equipment was child scale (55.6%) with highest availability observed in Tanzania (78.3%) (table 2).

Facilities readiness score

By considering the seven items with exclusion of tape for measuring MUAC, 28.5% (95% CI 27.4% to 29.6%) of the facilities reported at least 75% (5/7) of the relevant items for GM service provision, with proportions across countries ranging from 4.9% (Afghanistan) to 38.9% (Ethiopia). Similarly, when all eight items with inclusion of MUAC tape were considered, 28.6% (95% CI 27.3% to 29.7%) of the facilities reported at least 75% (6/8) of the relevant items for GM service provision. Across the countries, this proportion ranged from 18.3% (Nepal) to 38.7% (Ethiopia). Full readiness was identified in 4.9% of facilities across the four countries where MUAC measurement was applicable and in 4.4% of facilities across six countries when MUAC was excluded (table 3).

Factors associated with readiness to provide GM service

In the pooled analysis of six countries using the seven-item readiness score, facilities that had routine quality assurance activities had 1.15 times higher expected readiness score than those without (IRR: 1.15; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.24). Facilities that had separate and fixed fees for all service increased the readiness score by 10% (IRR: 1.10; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.20) and 20% (IRR: 1.20; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.31), respectively, as compared with facilities that did not charge fees. Presence of a trained health provider at the facility 24 hours, either with a duty schedule or on-call, was associated with a 9% increase in readiness score (IRR: 1.09; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.17).

When the readiness score was constructed using eight items and restricted to the four countries where MUAC was applicable, the associations remained in the same direction, although with slightly different magnitudes. In addition, the number of weeks per month in which GM services were provided was positively associated with readiness. Facilities that had routine quality assurance activities had 1.13 times higher expected readiness score than those without (IRR: 1.13; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.23). Facilities that had separate and fixed fees for all service increased the readiness score by 19% (IRR: 1.19; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.33) and 18% (IRR: 1.18; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.30), respectively, as compared with facilities that did not charge fees. Facilities that had trained health providers at the facility 24 hours, either with a duty schedule or on-call, increased the readiness score by 10% (IRR: 1.10; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.19). Facilities that provide GM services 2–3 weeks per month had 1.12 times higher expected readiness score than those facilities that provide GM service once in a month (IRR: 1.12; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.25) (table 4).

Discussion

This is the first multicountry study to evaluate health facilities’ readiness to provide GM services in six low-income and middle-income countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Haiti, Ethiopia, Nepal and Tanzania, as well as the variables affecting the GM service. This study revealed remarkable cross-country differences in facility readiness for GM service provision. When readiness was assessed using seven tracer items (excluding MUAC), only 28.5% of the facilities from the six countries had at least 75% of the required items. This low rate indicates a large gap in meeting the minimum levels of effective GM service delivery. Ethiopia achieved the highest average readiness score. This might be due to long-standing government-led nutrition initiatives, including the integration of GM into the Health Extension Programme and broader commitments under the ‘Seqota Declaration’.18 In contrast, Afghanistan ranked the lowest, which may be attributed to the prolonged effect of war and political instability over the past decades. This trouble affected the economy and health system seriously, with Afghanistan’s healthcare system referred to as one of the world’s ‘inferior’ systems.19 20

Quality assurance means activities that are undertaken by facilities to set standards and improve performance with the aim of providing safe and effective services.21 22 The findings of this study showed that facilities that conducted routine quality assurance activities had a higher likelihood of achieving higher readiness scores. This might be due to quality assurance activities promoting regular monitoring of resources, adherence to service delivery standards and timely identification of gaps in service provision.23

User fees for health services by consumers have been adopted as a policy option that many developing nations used to generate money to meet the expanding demand for healthcare services.24 Health facilities that charged either separate or fixed expense for services were more likely to have higher readiness scores. This finding aligns with the evidence from a review that assessed the impact of user fees on health service utilisation in low-income and middle-income countries, which showed that removing user fees increased the utilisation of curative and preventive services, but also had a negative impact on service quality and readiness to provide quality service.25 This might be due to user fees’ direct association with adequate financing of health facilities, enabling them to improve quality of care by increasing skilled professionals, resources and maintaining staff training, all of which contribute to improved readiness.26

In this study, facilities that had trained health providers at the facility 24 hours, either with a duty schedule or on-call, increased the readiness score. The level of functionality of health systems, as well as its ability to provide services effectively and efficiently, depends on health workers. Adequate, fairly distributed, qualified, skilled, competent, motivated and empowered health workers are essential to ensure the achievement of universal health coverage, as well as to improve the health of a population.27 28

Facilities that provided GM services for 2–3 weeks per month had higher expected readiness scores. This may be attributed to the fact that frequent service provision creates more opportunities for facilitating stronger engagement in service. Interestingly, this association was not observed in facilities that offered GM services every week of the month. Facilities that provide services weekly may face other difficulties, which could compromise the readiness. However, the current data do not allow for an exploration of such underlying determinants in detail, suggesting the need for further studies to understand the interaction between service frequency and readiness.

This study has methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis depends on secondary data from SPA surveys, which limited variable selection to the items available in the datasets. Due to this, potentially relevant factors that could affect GM service readiness were not captured. At the same time, even though we controlled for available covariates, residual confounding may still exist because of unmeasured factors. Second, MUAC measurement was not collected in Afghanistan and Tanzania, which required the use of two separate models to construct the GM service readiness score. While this approach allowed the inclusion of all six countries, it may reduce the comparability between countries for which MUAC was available and countries for which it was not. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of the surveys limits causal inference.

Despite these limitations, these findings are very essential in the global effort to attain the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). Especially to meet SDG 3 (Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages) in which healthy and sustainable nutrition has a great role in reducing premature death including from non-communicable diseases and malnutrition.29 30 Likewise, WHO Essential Nutrition Actions also prioritise stepping up health system readiness for preventive nutrition interventions like GM.31 Low percentage of facilities achieving the minimum readiness requirement among nations indicates a critical barrier to realising these universal goals. Without guidelines, trained staff and needed equipment, facilities cannot effectively execute GM services, which discourages child malnutrition reduction and survival enhancement.

Conclusions and recommendations

In the six countries where malnutrition is a major problem, the finding of this study revealed that health facilities lack the capacity to offer GM services. In both models, considering seven or eight tracer items, only 28.5% of the facilities reported having at least 75% of the relevant items for GM service provision. Full readiness was also very limited, with 4.9% of facilities across the four countries where MUAC measurement was applicable and 4.4% of facilities across six countries when MUAC was excluded. Quality assurance activities, user fees, presence of trained health provider at facility 24 hours, and number of weeks in which GM service was offered were the factors associated with increasing health facilities’ readiness to provide GM service. In order to strengthen the readiness of health facilities to provide GM services in low-resource settings, policymakers and programme planners should integrate GM services into routine child health programmes and ensure the consistent availability of essential equipment. Further research is needed to capture additional determinants of readiness not identified in SPA surveys, including managerial practices, staff motivation and supply chain difficulties.
