Risk factors, predictive biomarkers, and microbial profile of ventilator-associated pneumonia in critically ill patients: a retrospective cohort Study
Authors: Meiqin Zhang, Yuehong Xu, Ping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a frequent ICU-acquired infection in critically ill patients undergoing prolonged mechanical ventilation. It is associated with significant morbidity, prolonged hospital stay, and challenges in antimicrobial therapy. This study aimed to identify clinical risk factors, inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein and white blood cell count), and microbial profiles associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) in critically ill patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including 267 adult ICU patients ventilated > 48 h. Demographics, clinical characteristics, ICU practices, laboratory markers, and microbiological profiles were collected. Statistical analysis involved Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, correlation analysis, and multivariable binary logistic regression to evaluate predictors of VAP and ICU mortality.ResultsOf the 267 patients, 222 (83.1%) developed VAP. VAP was significantly associated with prolonged ventilation duration (p = 0.016). Corticosteroid exposure was less frequent among VAP patients (30.4% vs. 50.0%; p = 0.019). In multivariable analysis, higher CRP (adjusted OR [aOR] = 1.29 per unit; 95% CI: 1.14–1.46) and WBC (aOR = 1.23 per unit; 95% CI: 1.09–1.40) independently predicted VAP, whereas ventilation duration was not independently associated (aOR = 0.93 per day; 95% CI: 0.85–1.01). In a mortality model, higher CRP was associated with lower odds of death (aOR = 0.968; 95% CI: 0.941–0.996).ConclusionVAP is highly prevalent in ICU patients requiring prolonged ventilation and is strongly linked with elevated inflammatory markers and multidrug-resistant pathogens. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective, single-center design.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Keywords: Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), Intensive care unit (ICU), Mechanical ventilation, Critically ill, Multidrug-resistant organisms, ICU mortality

Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a hospital-acquired lung infection that develops after at least 48 h of mechanical ventilation [1]. It is among the most common Intensive care unit (ICU) infections, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 20–36% of critically ill ventilated patients [2]. VAP is associated with increased morbidity and put vulnerable patients at high risk of adverse events and complications. In particular, critically ill adults with comorbidities such as sepsis, acute ischemic stroke, or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are at elevated risk for VAP, owing to high illness severity and impaired consciousness levels or decreased lung function [3, 4].

Numerous studies have identified key risk factors for VAP in these populations, notably prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation and extended ICU length of stay [5–7]. Since each additional day of intubation increases the opportunity for microbial colonization and biofilm formation on the endotracheal tube, thereby compounding VAP risk. Underlying comorbidities further predispose patients to VAP [1]. It has been reported that chronic lung disease like COPD, diabetes mellitus, renal failure, and immunosuppressive conditions significantly compromise host defenses and have been linked to higher VAP incidence. These comorbid conditions can lead to immune suppression and organ dysfunction, rendering patients more susceptible to infection [8].

Notably, the microbial etiology of VAP in such high-risk patients often includes opportunistic and multidrug-resistant organism infections. Late-onset VAP is frequently caused by gram-negative bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii, or gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus, pathogens notorious for antibiotic resistance and severe clinical outcomes. The prominence of these pathogens in VAP underscores the challenge of effective antimicrobial therapy and the importance of preventive measures in the ICU [9].

Despite current research on VAP, important gaps persist in the as reported incidence rates of VAP vary widely from 2 to 16 episodes per 1000 ventilator-days due in part to inconsistent diagnostic criteria and heterogeneity in surveillance methods across studies [1]. Additionally, geographic disparities have been observed since hospitals in low- and middle-income regions often report higher VAP rates and mortality [1, 7]. Additionally, data remain limited for certain patient subgroups such as, neurocritical care patients including stroke victims. However, studies in this population have yielded heterogeneous results and uncertainty regarding unique risk factors or outcomes and findings regarding risk factors associated with VAP are unclear and inconsistent [10, 11]. Given the variability in diagnostic criteria and limited data from resource-constrained settings, the present study aims to evaluate risk factors, inflammatory biomarkers, and microbial profiles associated with VAP in a real-world ICU cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was a retrospective cohort study involving data of adult critically ill patients admitted to the hospital between 1 June 2023 and 1 January 2025. Patients eligible for inclusion were adult patients aged 18 years or older who remained mechanically ventilated for more than 48 h due to severe injury or illness including traumatic injuries, sepsis or septic shock, stroke, myocardial infarction (MI) and ARDS. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, nasal intubation, intubation exceeding 12 h before ICU admission, and cases transferred from another hospital more than 2 days after trauma. The institutional review board (IRB) approved the study with number (2025-W-027), and all data were coded to maintain patient confidentiality.

VAP Prevention protocol

A comprehensive bundle of preventive measures was implemented to reduce the risk of ventilator-associated complications. Strict hand hygiene was applied among medical staff according infection control preventive measures. VAP was diagnosed using the Chest Echocardiography and Procalcitonin Pulmonary Infection Score (CEPPIS), integrating clinical, radiological, and laboratory findings as assessed by the attending intensivist.

Additionally, patients were positioned with the head of the bed elevated to 30–45 degrees with daily sedation to facilitate neurological assessment and reduce ventilation duration. Mechanical ventilation strategies followed a moderately lung-protective approach, with tidal volumes of 5–8 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP > 3 cm H₂O, and ventilator settings adjusted to maintain normocapnia and plateau pressures below 25 cm H₂O, accompanied by rapid weaning protocols [12]. Oral hygiene was performed every 12 h using 0.2% chlorhexidine digluconate solution. Standard prophylactic measures included prevention of peptic ulcer disease and deep vein thrombosis, both integral to comprehensive ICU care.

Data collection and outcomes evaluation

The data were collected from the medical records of patients, which contained demographic data including age and sex in addition to cause of ICU admission, type of ICU unit (surgical, medical or mixed ICU), length of hospital stay and duration of mechanical ventilation were collected. Additionally, Data about severity of illness evaluated by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score [13], steroid and medication use during ICU admission, feeding route and presence of comorbidities such as, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure or chronic kidney disease (CKD) were recorded for each patient. Pain-related characteristics were assessed using the German Pain Questionnaire (DSF; Deutscher Schmerzfragebogen), a standardized multidimensional instrument designed to evaluate pain characteristics, associated symptoms, psychosocial factors, and pain-related disability based on the biopsychosocial model of pain [14].

The outcome evaluation focused on the incidence of VAP in critically ill patients on ventilation then we stratified patients according to VAP status into VAP group and non-VAP AP was recognized according to the Chest Echocardiography and Procalcitonin Pulmonary Infection Score (CEPPIS) by the anesthesiologist on duty to evaluate the association between VAP and possible risk factors in ICU [15]. We reported the number of deaths, results of culture for determining causative organism for VAP and data of laboratory markers including C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood counts (WBC). Moreover, we evaluated the correlation of risk factors, clinical practices and laboratory markers with VAP. Disease severity was assessed at ICU admission using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) as they were not normally distributed, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was tested using Shapiro-Wilk test. Outliers were identified and excluded from the dataset. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test between the two groups and Pearson’s chi-square for categorical variables. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the predictors for VAP development and ICU mortality. We avoided imputation and managed missingness via pairwise deletion. A two-side p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (V.25.0, IBM Corp).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Among 267 mechanically ventilated ICU patients, 222 (83.1%) had ventilator‑associated pneumonia (VAP) and 45 (16.9%) did not. Median age did not differ between groups (No VAP: 32 [19–66] years; VAP: 39 [22–63] years; p = 0.383), and sex distribution was similar (female: 58.1% vs. 49.8%; p = 0.315). The distribution of ICU type differed, with VAP patients more often managed in surgical ICUs (37.5% vs. 17.1%) and non‑VAP patients more often in mixed ICUs (56.1% vs. 33.3%; p = 0.012). Comorbidities were comparable (diabetes 29.9% vs. 25.6%, p = 0.597; CKD 19.0% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.551; heart failure 11.7% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.887), with a nonsignificant trend toward lower COPD among VAP patients (17.4% vs. 29.3%; p = 0.08). Clinical course

VAP was associated with a longer duration of mechanical ventilation (28 [21–36] vs. 23 [17–30] days; p = 0.016), whereas ICU length of stay (33 [20–49] vs. 28 [18–44] days; p = 0.217) and admission APACHE II scores (26 [20–33] vs. 22 [18–33]; p = 0.228) were similar (Table 3). Inflammatory markers were higher in VAP: CRP (59.4 [35.7–81.5] vs. 7.0 [4.3–9.5]) and WBC (14.6 [12.1–17.3] vs. 6.6 [5.7–8.5]) (both p < 0.001) (Table 3). Positive microbiology was far more common with VAP (99.1% vs. 7.0%; p < 0.001). Among VAP cases, the most frequently isolated organisms were Pseudomonas spp. (24.7%), Acinetobacter spp. (22.5%), Klebsiella spp. (19.3%), Escherichia coli (17.0%), and MRSA (16.5%) (Table 3). ICU mortality did not differ significantly between patients with VAP and those without VAP (p = 0.793), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

By conducting Spearman correlation analysis (Table 4), VAP development correlated positively with duration of mechanical ventilation (r = 0.148, p = 0.016), CRP (r = 0.605, p < 0.0001), WBC (r = 0.549, p < 0.0001), and culture positivity (r = 0.930, p < 0.0001). An inverse association was observed for steroid exposure (r = − 0.015 with p = 0.019). COPD showed a nonsignificant inverse trend (r = − 0.112, p = 0.081).

Independent predictors of VAP

In multivariable logistic regression using stepwise approach (Table 5), higher CRP (aOR = 1.289 per unit increase; p < 0.001) and higher WBC (aOR = 1.234 per unit; p = 0.001) independently predicted VAP, whereas duration of mechanical ventilation was not independently associated after adjustment (aOR = 0.927 per day; p = 0.097). it is important to note that CRP and WBC values were measured at ICU admission rather than at the time of VAP diagnosis. Therefore, these biomarkers should be interpreted as markers of early systemic inflammatory burden rather than true predictors of subsequent VAP occurrence.

Discussion

It is very important to explain the risk factors of VAP in order to develop more effective prevention and control. This retrospective study explores incidence of VAP and associated risk factors in critically ill patients. The incidence of VAP observed in our cohort (83.1%) is substantially higher than rates reported in most epidemiological studies, which typically range from 20 to 40% [16, 17]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in diagnostic approaches, as VAP in the present study was identified using the CEPPIS score rather than standardized CDC or ATS/IDSA criteria. In addition, the prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation, high inflammatory burden, and potential overlap between airway colonization and true infection may have contributed to an overestimation of VAP incidence. Therefore, the incidence reported in this study should not be considered comparable to rates reported in prior cohorts. Moreover, patients who developed VAP required significantly longer duration of mechanical ventilation, with elevated levels of inflammatory markers including CRP and WBC compared to those without VAP (all p < 0.001). These findings align with current literature supporting the diagnostic and prognostic value of systemic inflammatory markers including CRP with or without interleukin 6 (IL-6), which showed promising value for early VAP risk stratification [18]. Additionally, recent observational studies reported higher WBC/CRP among non-survivors with VAP, reflecting the importance of inflammatory markers in tracking severity of condition [19, 20].

The regression analysis revealed that CRP and WBC counts were strongly correlated with VAP onset and acted as independent predictors for VAP. Elevated CRP has been increasingly recognized as a biomarker for distinguishing infectious from non-infectious causes of systemic inflammation in ventilated patients. Recent studies by Povoa et al. and Troisi et al., suggest that CRP kinetics, may aid in both diagnosing VAP and guiding antimicrobial stewardship as daily CRP monitoring was useful in VAP outcome prediction [21, 22]. Similarly, leukocytosis is a hallmark of VAP, though its specificity remains limited due to overlap with systemic inflammatory response from trauma or other ICU diagnoses [1].

On evaluation of the microbial spectrum associated with VAP in our study, Pseudomonas (24.7%), Acinetobacter species (22.5%) were the dominant causative organisms, followed by Klebsiella, E. coli, and MRSA, which closely mirrors global reports from medical and surgical ICUs and consistent with current literature, where non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli including Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter and multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae are repeatedly identified as leading VAP pathogens, while MRSA remains a prominent Gram-positive contributor [17, 23]. Importantly, these organisms represent core members of the “ESKAPE” pathogens, notorious for driving nosocomial infections and complicating treatment choices. The predominance of multidrug-resistant strains, particularly carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella and Acinetobacter, highlights the persistent challenge of selecting appropriate empiric antibiotics, as emphasized by surveillance data and global health agencies [24, 25].

Regarding the duration of mechanical ventilation, we observed a positive correlation between days of mechanical ventilation use and VAP (r = 0.15; p = 0.016) with no independent association after adjustment. This aligns with the idea that longer mechanical ventilation represents both a consequence and a risk marker rather than a stand-alone predictor once inflammation are considered [1, 26]. Furthermore, A meta-analysis by Li et al., similarly reported bidirectional links between VAP with prolonged mechanical ventilation and length of hospital stay since VAP is associated with longer hospital stay, however, it did not influence mortality outcomes [16].

Furthermore, corticosteroids exposure showed inverse correlation with VAP (r = − 0.15; p = 0.019) which contrast the study by Scaravilli et al., which linked dexamethasone to higher VAP risk in ARDS patients during COVID-19 [27]. However, a large multicenter analysis by Saura et al., found a time-dependent effect of steroid since there was no early risk with hazard ratio (HR of approximately 0.47) after 2 days, but increased risk after 2 weeks ( HR = 1.48, 95% CI: [1.01–2.16]) with not statistically significant overall association (p = 0.082), suggesting that dexamethasone’s impact on VAP risk may depend on exposure duration and course of disease, rather than being a uniform effect throughout the course of ICU stay [28]. Although VAP was associated with higher odds of ICU mortality, this association did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Outside COVID-specific contexts, a review and meta-analysis by Saleem et al., found insufficient to claim regarding protective steroid effect against pneumonia with no association between corticosteroid therapy and mortality or treatment failure [29]. Additionally, reintubation, use of paralytics, antibiotics, head-of-bed elevation, and feeding route distributions did not differ significantly between VAP and non-VAP in our unadjusted analyses. However, the current literature still cites reintubation and sedation depth as classic VAP risks, but effects can attenuate with prevention approaches according guidelines emphasizing multimodal prevention and shorter antibiotic courses for 7–8 days [30].

Clinical Implications and Limitations

The present study provides valuable insights for risk factors, causative organisms and inflammatory markers associated with critically ill patients on mechanical ventilation. Our findings underscore the importance of incorporating biomarkers such as CRP and WBC into early risk stratification for VAP, while recognizing their limitations in specificity. Furthermore, our analysis highlights the need for attention to modifiable care practices, including feeding tube strategy and use of corticosteroids, which may influence clinical outcomes. However, our study showed some limitations to acknowledge. The retrospective single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability to other ICU settings. This observational analysis is subject to residual confounding and potential confounding by indication, particularly for time‑varying exposures such as steroids, antibiotics, and reintubation. Although disease severity was assessed using APACHE II scores, it was not independently associated with VAP development and therefore was not retained in multivariable analyses. Residual confounding related to illness severity cannot be excluded. CRP and WBC measurements were obtained at ICU admission and not temporally aligned with the onset of VAP. As a result, these markers may reflect baseline inflammatory status rather than causally predicting VAP development, limiting conclusions regarding their predictive value; future studies should include biomarkers such as procalcitonin or IL-6. The absence of CDC or ATS/IDSA diagnostic criteria may have introduced diagnostic heterogeneity and contributed to the high reported incidence. The use of logistic regression without time‑to‑event methods may limit causal interpretation for outcomes that accrue with ventilation days. The marked imbalance between groups (221 VAP vs. 43 non‑VAP) may have affected model stability and precision of effect estimates. Consequently, results should be interpreted cautiously and validated in prospective, time‑aware analyses across diverse ICUs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, VAP is a common and serious complication in mechanically ventilated ICU patients, with CRP and WBC emerging as reliable independent predictors. The predominance of multidrug-resistant pathogens underscores the importance of early recognition and targeted antimicrobial strategies. Preventive measures, careful monitoring of inflammatory markers, and optimized clinical practices remain central to reducing VAP incidence and improving patient outcomes.
