Comparing the effectiveness and safety of different surgical procedures for haemorrhoids: a protocol for systematic evaluation and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Xue Li, Yicheng Cheng, Congcong Zhi, Ningyuan Liu, XiaoLong Wang, Lihua Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHaemorrhoidal disease affects 25–40% of adults worldwide and constitutes a primary reason for outpatient colorectal consultations. Surgical management is essential for grade III–IV or treatment-refractory cases. Numerous procedures have emerged, including Milligan-Morgan open haemorrhoidectomy, Ferguson closed haemorrhoidectomy, stapled haemorrhoidopexy, Doppler-guided haemorrhoidal artery ligation, transanal haemorrhoidal dearterialisation and laser haemorrhoidoplasty. However, randomised controlled trials and conventional meta-analyses report conflicting results on efficacy, postoperative pain, recurrence rates and complications such as bleeding, stenosis and incontinence. Although network meta-analyses exist, an updated synthesis is needed because outcomes and follow-up vary across trials. This protocol aims to determine the most effective and safest haemorrhoid interventions (office-based and operative) through systematic review and network meta-analysis, providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice and guideline development.Methods and analysisThe Cochrane Library, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP, SinoMed and Wanfang databases will be searched from inception to January 2025, limited to English or Chinese publications. Randomised controlled trials evaluating haemorrhoid interventions/procedures for haemorrhoidal disease will be included, with outcomes encompassing cure rate, recurrence, complications, postoperative pain, wound-healing time, anal function and operative duration. Subgroup analyses (haemorrhoid grade, follow-up duration), sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessments will be performed. Introduction

Haemorrhoidal disease (HD) is a common benign anorectal disorder, affecting an estimated 25–40% of adults worldwide and constituting a leading indication for outpatient colorectal consultations.1 It is defined by pathological enlargement and distal displacement of the anal cushions, leading to bleeding, prolapse, pain and pruritus.2 Conservative and office-based therapies—such as rubber band ligation (RBL) and infrared coagulation—are effective in early-stage disease, whereas surgery remains the standard of care for grade III–IV or refractory haemorrhoids.3

Over recent decades, surgical management of HD has evolved to improve efficacy and reduce postoperative morbidity.4 The Milligan-Morgan open haemorrhoidectomy (MMH), introduced in 1937, remains a benchmark but is associated with substantial postoperative pain and delayed wound healing.5 The Ferguson closed haemorrhoidectomy sought to expedite healing through primary closure, and stapled haemorrhoidopexy (Longo procedure) was developed to lessen postoperative pain and hasten recovery.6 More recently, minimally invasive, sphincter-preserving techniques—including Doppler-guided haemorrhoidal artery ligation (DGHAL), transanal haemorrhoidal dearterialisation (THD) and laser haemorrhoidoplasty—have gained popularity for potentially reducing tissue trauma and accelerating recovery.7

Despite these advances, consensus is lacking on the procedure that best balances efficacy, postoperative pain, recurrence and complications such as bleeding, stenosis and incontinence. Findings from high-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses remain inconsistent. A landmark multicentre RCT in The Lancet found that stapled haemorrhoidopexy caused less pain but higher recurrence than conventional excision.8 In contrast, meta-analyses in Ann Coloproctol and British Journal of Surgery reported substantial heterogeneity and conflicting conclusions regarding long-term outcomes.9 10

Yet the lack of a comprehensive quantitative comparison across the expanding array of haemorrhoidectomy techniques leaves key questions unresolved: which procedures best balance symptom control and safety; whether minimally invasive, energy-based approaches deliver durable efficacy comparable to excision; and which factors most influence recovery and recurrence.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) offers a rigorous framework that synthesises direct and indirect evidence from multiple RCTs, enabling simultaneous comparison and ranking of relevant interventions within a single model.11 NMA has been applied successfully in cardiovascular, oncologic and minimally invasive surgery to inform guidelines and establish evidence-based treatment hierarchies.12 13 Although several network meta-analyses have assessed haemorrhoid interventions,111416 clinically important uncertainties remain because outcome definitions and reporting vary substantially across trials (eg, ‘cure/success/overall effectiveness’), recurrence is time-dependent with heterogeneous follow-up durations, and pain is measured using different scales and time points. Therefore, an updated systematic review and NMA with prespecified outcome harmonisation, transparent handling of follow-up windows and pain time points, and certainty assessment (eg, GRADE/CINeMA) is warranted.

To address this gap, we will conduct a systematic review and NMA of RCTs comparing the effectiveness and safety of haemorrhoid interventions for HD. We hypothesise that contemporary minimally invasive, vessel-targeted techniques—such as DGHAL, THD and laser haemorrhoidoplasty—achieve long-term efficacy comparable to conventional excision while reducing postoperative pain and complications. By quantitatively ranking available procedures, this study aims to identify the most effective and safest options, inform clinical decision-making and advance understanding of optimal surgical management of HD.

Building on existing evidence syntheses, this study aims to provide an updated and methodologically transparent comparison of haemorrhoid interventions. We will update electronic searches to the most recent date prior to final data extraction, include studies from both international and Chinese databases, limited to English or Chinese publications, and incorporate relevant technique variants when supported by eligible randomised evidence. In addition, we will apply prespecified outcome harmonisation for heterogeneous endpoints and use structured approaches (eg, CINeMA/GRADE) to assess the certainty of network estimates.

Methods

This study protocol will be developed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P 2020) guidelines.17 The protocol has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration no. CRD420251053697). Any protocol amendments will be documented and updated in PROSPERO as appropriate.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this review.

As this review will use only previously published data, ethical approval is not required. The findings will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant scientific conferences.

Exclusion criteria were (i) studies without accessible full text; (ii) duplicate publications or overlapping reports (retaining the earliest publication); and (iii) studies with incomplete data or serious errors (retaining the version with the most complete data).

Outcome definitions and harmonisation: The terms ‘cure,’ ‘success’ and ‘overall/clinical effectiveness’ may be defined heterogeneously across trials. We will extract the original definitions as reported in each study and apply prespecified harmonisation rules. Where feasible, these outcomes will be mapped to a common dichotomous endpoint defined as clinical success (eg, cure or marked improvement) versus non-success (eg, no improvement or worsening), and the mapping rules will be transparently documented. If outcome definitions are not sufficiently comparable across studies to support valid mapping, those data will not be pooled and will be analysed separately and/or narratively, with sensitivity analyses where feasible.

Recurrence follow-up: Recurrence will be treated as a time-dependent outcome. We will extract recurrence at all reported follow-up times and analyse it within prespecified intervals (≤3 months, 3–12 months and >12 months). When multiple time points are reported within the same interval, we will preferentially use the longest follow-up within that interval.

Search process

Two reviewers (XL and YC) will independently perform the literature search and study selection, and any disagreements will be resolved by the corresponding author (LZ). Records will be managed using EndNote, and duplicates will be removed. Titles and abstracts will be screened to identify studies for full-text review, and reasons for exclusion will be recorded. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies will then be assessed against predefined criteria to determine the final set of included trials. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews will be manually searched to identify additional studies. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for RCTs will be assessed at the outcome level using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2).18 Two reviewers (XL and YC) will independently perform the assessments, resolving disagreements through discussion or, if necessary, adjudication by the corresponding author (LZ). The following domains will be evaluated using signalling questions and RoB 2 decision algorithms: (1) bias arising from the randomisation process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions (effect of assignment); (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result.

Overall risk-of-bias assessments will be categorised as ‘low risk,’ ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk.’ Where appropriate, the RoB 2 variants for cluster-randomised or cross-over trials will be applied. Authors of the primary studies will be contacted for clarification when required. Risk-of-bias summaries will be presented graphically and incorporated into sensitivity analyses. Small-study and publication biases will be examined using funnel plots and Egger’s test when at least 10 studies are available for a given outcome.

Statistical analysis and data synthesis

We will first perform pairwise meta-analyses for comparisons including at least two trials. We will use RevMan (V.5.4) software. Dichotomous outcomes will be expressed as risk ratios or ORs with 95% CIs, and continuous outcomes as mean differences or standardised mean differences with 95% CIs. For postoperative pain, we will extract the pain scale and assessment time points and analyse pain within prespecified postoperative time windows (eg, ≤24 hours, 2–7 days and >7 days), depending on data availability. When multiple pain assessments are reported within the same window, we will preferentially use the time point closest to the upper bound of that window (or the earliest assessment within 24 hours) to improve comparability; alternative selections will be explored in sensitivity analyses where feasible. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the χ² test and I² statistic. Fixed-effect models will be applied when heterogeneity is low, and random-effects models when substantial heterogeneity is present.19

For the network NMA, we will integrate direct and indirect evidence for all eligible haemorrhoid interventions (office-based and operative) within a single analytical framework, where nodes represent interventions and edges denote head-to-head trials. A Bayesian NMA based on Markov chain Monte Carlo methods will be implemented (eg, in GeMTC or R) with four chains, sufficient burn-in and convergence evaluated using Brooks-Gelman-Rubin diagnostics, considering a potential scale reduction factor close to 1 as acceptable. Treatments will be ranked using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve. As a sensitivity analysis, a frequentist NMA will also be fitted in Stata if model diagnostics indicate better fit within that framework.

Assumptions and model diagnostics

We will evaluate the key assumptions of NMA, including homogeneity, transitivity and consistency. Potential effect modifiers—such as haemorrhoid grade, baseline severity, study centre type and follow-up duration—will be examined across comparisons to assess the assumption of transitivity. Global and local inconsistency will be assessed by comparing consistency and inconsistency models, as well as by performing node-splitting analyses. Model fit will be assessed using deviance-based metrics. If inconsistency persists, potential causes will be explored, and inconsistency models will be considered.

Transitivity and clinical exchangeability

We will assess the plausibility of the transitivity assumption by comparing the distribution of key potential effect modifiers across treatment comparisons, with haemorrhoid grade considered a primary modifier. Interventions will be prespecified as office-based procedures (eg, injection sclerotherapy, rubber-band ligation) versus operative surgical techniques (eg, excisional haemorrhoidectomy, stapled haemorrhoidopexy, Doppler-guided ligation/dearterialisation). To minimise clinical intransitivity, the primary NMA will be conducted within clinically comparable populations, and we will implement grade-based stratification (eg, trials predominantly involving grade I–II vs grade III–IV disease) and/or separate networks for office-based versus operative procedures where appropriate. If the haemorrhoid grade is incompletely reported, this will be documented and explored through sensitivity analyses.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Planned subgroup analyses will be based on haemorrhoid grade, anaesthesia or analgesia regimen, single-centre versus multi-centre design and follow-up duration (≤3, 3–12, or >12 months). Recurrence will be analysed within these predefined follow-up intervals rather than pooling across substantially different follow-up durations. Sensitivity analyses will exclude trials with high risk of bias, reanalyse outcomes using alternative effect measures and omit studies with imputed data or unclear diagnostic criteria. Multi-arm trials will be accounted for using appropriate variance adjustments (eg, splitting shared comparators).

Assessment of publication bias and small-study effects

When at least 10 studies contribute to a given outcome, small-study and publication biases will be assessed using comparison-adjusted funnel plots and appropriate statistical tests (eg, Egger’s or Begg’s test, depending on the analytical framework).

Certainty/credibility of evidence

For guideline-oriented conclusions, the certainty of evidence will be assessed using the GRADE approach across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. For network NMA estimates, the CINeMA framework will also be applied to assess confidence in treatment effects, and a ‘Summary of Network Findings’ table will be presented with corresponding confidence ratings (high, moderate, low or very low).20

Discussion

This protocol describes a comprehensive, bilingual systematic review and network NMA designed to compare the effectiveness and safety of commonly used haemorrhoid interventions (office-based and operative procedures) for haemorrhoidal disease.21 By integrating direct and indirect evidence within a single analytical framework, this review aims to address a long-standing evidence gap: the lack of a unified hierarchy of surgical options that accounts for both efficacy and safety across diverse patient populations and settings. Consistent with prior protocols that highlight the role of NMA in informing clinical practice and guideline development, our study seeks to generate decision-ready estimates and treatment rankings to guide clinicians and policymakers.

The methodology adheres to established NMA guidance, including careful assessment of transitivity and consistency, as well as the use of ranking metrics to summarise comparative performance and enhance interpretability for end users.22 23 Where appropriate, node-splitting and related diagnostic approaches will be employed to examine local inconsistency, and comparison-adjusted outputs will facilitate transparent presentation of the evidence network’s structure and findings.

This review has several notable strengths. First, it includes an exhaustive search across major English and Chinese databases, trial registries and guideline repositories, maximising retrieval of eligible RCTs and minimising language bias. Second, the protocol follows contemporary reporting and appraisal standards (PRISMA-NMA extensions; GRADE and CINeMA), allowing structured and transparent assessment of the credibility of each network estimate.20 24 Third, planned subgroup, sensitivity and small-study bias analyses will test the robustness of results and explore the influence of study-level modifiers. Collectively, these design choices reflect best practices from comparable NMA protocols and aim to strengthen the credibility and clinical applicability of our conclusions.

Nevertheless, several important limitations are anticipated. Clinical and methodological heterogeneity—arising from variations in haemorrhoid grade, diagnostic criteria, perioperative management, surgeon experience, anaesthesia and analgesia protocols, and follow-up duration—may challenge the assumptions of homogeneity and transitivity underpinning NMA.25 As in many surgical trials, blinding is rarely feasible, and outcome assessment protocols often differ, introducing potential performance and detection biases.26 Definitions of key outcomes (eg, ‘cure’, ‘recurrence’ and specific complications) may not be standardised across studies, and short follow-up periods in some trials may underestimate late recurrences or complications. Finally, if few studies contribute to a given outcome, the power to detect small-study or publication bias will be limited. When at least 10 trials are available, we will assess funnel plot asymmetry and explore alternative explanations.25

The anticipated impact of this review is twofold. Clinically, transparent ranking of surgical options, combined with absolute and relative effect estimates for key outcomes—such as cure, recurrence, complications, pain, healing time, anal function and operative time—will support personalised, context-specific decision-making. From a policy and guideline perspective, certainty ratings derived from GRADE and CINeMA will support evidence-to-recommendation processes and identify areas where confidence in effect estimates is limited, thereby guiding priorities for future research.

In summary, by systematically synthesising and comparing the full range of contemporary haemorrhoid interventions (office-based and operative procedures) for haemorrhoidal disease within an NMA framework, and by appraising the credibility of the resulting estimates, this review aims to provide a robust evidence base to inform clinical practice, guideline development and future research agendas. Any protocol amendments will be documented, and the findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications to ensure transparency, accessibility and reproducibility.
