One-loop off-shell quark-gluon vertex in arbitrary gauge and dimensions: a streamlined approach through the second-order formalism of QCD
Victor Miguel Banda Guzmán, A. Bashir

Abstract
The standard Feynman rules used for perturbative calculations in quantum chromodynamics (QCD) are derived from a Lagrangian that is first-order in derivatives. It includes a three-point quark-gluon vertex which obscures the precise disentangled manner in which spin and momentum are interchanged during these interactions. An unambiguous understanding of this interchange is insightful for efficiently extracting physically relevant information from various Green’s functions. To separate the scalar and spin degrees of freedom and gain physical insight from the outset, we examine the quark-gluon vertex using the less commonly employed second-order formalism of QCD. We compute this off-shell vertex in arbitrary space-time dimensions and covariant gauges by using scalar integrals with shifted dimensions, which include higher powers of the propagators, within a combined first- and second-order formalism. This approach naturally identifies the transverse components of the quark-gluon vertex, even before evaluating the tensor Feynman integrals. Our results are in complete agreement with those obtained from the first-order formalism. We also compute the on-shell version of this vertex using exclusively the second-order formalism, facilitating a precise identification of spin and momentum interchange. Through analyzing the Pauli form factor at k 2 = 0 k^{2}=0 (where k k represents the momentum of the external gluon), we find that only a specific set of second-order Feynman diagrams are relevant for calculating the electromagnetic and chromomagnetic dipole moments. These diagrams represent quantum processes in which the spin of the incoming quark changes only once due to interactions with the virtual gluons that form the quark-gluon vertex. All other interactions involve only momentum interchange (scalar interactions). Furthermore, we confirm existing results in the literature, which suggest that defining the chromomagnetic dipole moment in the limit k 2 → 0 k^{2}\rightarrow 0 leads to infrared divergences.



I
Introduction

The conventional approach to perturbative calculations in quantum chromodynamics (QCD) relies on Feynman diagrams, with the rules illustrated in Fig.
1
.
These rules are derived from the following Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\dfrac{1}{4}\left(F^{a}_{\mu\nu}\right)^{2}+\overline{\psi}\left(i\not{D}-m\right)\psi-\frac{1}{2\xi}(\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu})^{2}\,.

(1)

This Lagrangian is first-order in the derivatives. The field strength tensor
F_{\mu\nu}^{a}
reads as,

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}^{a}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}^{a}-\partial_{\nu}A^{a}_{\mu}+gf^{abc}A_{\mu}^{b}A_{\nu}^{c}\,.

(2)

Here
f^{abc}
are the structure constants of the
SU(N)
group, and the covariant derivative
D_{\mu}
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-igA^{a}_{\mu}T^{a}\,,

(3)

where
T^{a}
are the generators of the
SU(N)
group.

Although the Feynman rules illustrated in Fig.
1
can be implemented straightforwardly, they often lead to cumbersome and lengthy calculations that blur the underlying physical information of a quantum field function.
For instance, in the context of quantum electrodynamics (QED), the anomalous magnetic dipole moment of the electron at the one-loop level is determined to be equal to
{\alpha}/{2\pi}
, where
\alpha={e^{2}}/{4\pi}
is the fine-structure constant. The higher order contributions to this physical observable emerge following extensive calculations of the electromagnetic vertex at the multi-loop level. It is desirable and perhaps conceivable that the final simple results could be derived through a more direct approach. It may be tempting to develop an alternative mathematical framework and computational strategy for perturbative calculations in both QCD and QED, one that enhances the extraction of physical insight from the outset and makes the computation of physical observables more manifest. The second-order formalism of QCD developed in Refs.
[
1
,
2
]
may serve as a valuable tool in developing such a worthwhile framework. By decoupling the scalar and spin degrees of freedom in the interactions between quarks and gluons, this formalism can provide a transparent picture of how quarks and gluons exchange spin and momentum. This methodology provides an alternative but equivalent approach to the first-order standard formalism. It has lately resurfaced in the literature as providing a connection between the worldline formalism of quantum field theory and the approach of Feynman diagrams, for details see Refs.
[
3
,
4
]
. It has also been applied recently to the study of the electromagnetic one-loop vertex in standard and reduced QED, Refs.
[
5
,
6
]
.

A simple example of how the second-order formalism decouples the scalar and spin degrees of freedom in the interaction of the quarks and gluons is given by the on-shell quark-gluon vertex
\left[\Gamma^{\text{OS}}_{\text{tree}}\right]^{a\mu}
at tree level. In the first-order conventional approach, the vertex is given by the Fig.
1(a)
, while in the second-order formalism it directly rewrites as (see Figs.
3

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{\text{OS}}_{\text{tree}}\right]^{a\mu}=\dfrac{igT^{a}}{2m}\left[(p+p^{\prime})^{\mu}-\sigma^{\mu\alpha}k_{\alpha}\right]\,,

(4)

where
k
corresponds to the gluon momentum, while
p
and
p^{\prime}
are the momenta of the incoming and outgoing quarks, respectively. Eq. (
4

k
. This separation gets entangled and obscure in the first order formalism.

The quark-gluon vertex is a fundamental component of QCD which has extensively been studied both perturbatively and non-perturbatively

The relevant Feynman diagrams for the one-loop calculations of the quark-gluon vertex using the first-order approach are presented in Fig.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the main features of the second-order formalism for QCD, and the techniques that we employ in the evaluation of the Feynman integrals that show up in the calculations.

II.1
Second-order formalism for QCD

The second-order formalism for fermions in gauge theories was first derived in Refs.
[
1
,
2
]
. It disentangles the gauge interaction of the fermionic particle into scalar and spin degrees of freedom with
appropriate usage of Gordon-like identities. It can be achieved, see
[
2
]
, by formally rewriting the product of the fermion propagator
S(p+k)
with the first-order vertex
igT^{a}\gamma^{\mu}
as,

\displaystyle S(p+k)(igT^{a}\gamma^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(i\dfrac{\not{p}+\not{k}+m}{(p+k)^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\left(igT^{a}\gamma^{\alpha}\right)\,,

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-gT^{a}\dfrac{A_{p,k}^{\alpha}}{D_{p+k}}\,,

where

\displaystyle A_{p,k}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=B^{\alpha}_{p,k}-C_{p}^{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle B_{p,k}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2p^{\alpha}+k^{\alpha}-\sigma^{\alpha\beta}k_{\beta}\,,

\displaystyle C_{p}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\alpha}(\not{p}-m)\,,

\displaystyle D_{q}

\displaystyle=q^{2}-m^{2}\,.

(6)

Here,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}]/2
,
iD^{-1}_{q}
represents the scalar propagator, and
B_{p,k}
corresponds to the total colorless three-point vertex of the second-order formalism (see Fig.
3
), which separates the scalar and spin gauge interactions. Thus a product of a propagator and a first-order vertex decomposes into a second-order contribution and a leftover first-order term given by the operator
C_{p}^{\alpha}
.

Figure 3
:
Three-point vertex of the second-order formalism for QCD, separated into its scalar (left) and spin (right) contributions.

Figure 4
:
Four-point vertex of the second-order formalism for QCD, separated into its scalar (left) and spin (right) contributions.

Additionally to the three-point vertex, in the second-order formalism there is also a four-point vertex whose scalar and spin contributions are displayed in Fig.
4
. It arises when there are two consecutive pairs of a fermion propagator and a first-order vertex in a Feynman diagram, see Fig.
5
. An amplitude
constructed from a first-order Feynman diagram that contains
a subdiagram as the one shown in Fig.
5
is proportional to the operator
M^{\mu\nu,ab}(p,k_{1},k_{2})
which is given by,

\displaystyle M^{\mu\nu,ab}(p,k_{1},k_{2})

(7)

\displaystyle\hskip-50.0pt=-g^{2}T^{a}T^{b}S(p+k_{1}+k_{2})\gamma^{\mu}S(p+k_{1})\gamma^{\nu}\,.

According to Eq. (
5
), this operator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle M^{\mu\nu,ab}(p,k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{2}T^{a}T^{b}\dfrac{A^{\mu}_{p+k_{1},k_{2}}A_{p+k_{1}}^{\nu}}{D_{p+k_{1}+k_{2}}D_{p+k_{1}}}\,.

(8)

Since

\displaystyle C_{p+k}^{\alpha}A_{p,k}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{p+k}\gamma^{\alpha}\gamma^{\beta}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{p+k}(\sigma^{\alpha\beta}+g^{\alpha\beta})\,,

Eq. (
8
) acquires the following form:

\displaystyle M^{\mu\nu,ab}(p,k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{2}T^{a}T^{b}\left[\dfrac{B^{\mu}_{p+k_{1},k_{2}}A^{\nu}_{p+k_{1}}}{D_{p+k_{1}+k_{2}}D_{p+k_{1}}}\right.

(10)

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 42.67912pt-\dfrac{g^{\mu\nu}+\sigma^{\mu\nu}}{D_{p+k_{1}+k_{2}}}\right]\,.

Thus, the multiplication of fermion propagators and first-order vertices generates a term with one less propagator. It readily gets identified as a four-point vertex.

The details of the transition between the first and second-order formalism can be found in Ref.
[
2
]
.

II.2
Summary of useful computational techniques

In the evaluation of the quark-gluon vertex at one-loop we need to perform tensor Feynman integrals with the following structure:

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{l^{\mu}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}D_{i}^{a_{i}}}\,,

\displaystyle J^{\mu\nu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{l^{\mu}l^{\nu}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}D_{i}^{a_{i}}}\,,

(11)

where the scalar propagators
D_{i}
are given by

\displaystyle D_{i}=(p_{i}+l)^{2}-m_{i}^{2}\,.

(12)

One way to achieve this goal is to employ the tensor reduction algorithm described in Refs.
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. This method uses the Schwinger parametrization of Feynman integrals to obtain identities between tensor and scalar Feynman integrals.

In our case, the one-loop scalar Feynman integral with three propagators
J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, defined as

\displaystyle J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}D_{i}^{a_{i}}}\,,

(13)

in the Schwinger parametrization reads as

\displaystyle J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\int\mathcal{D}x\dfrac{1}{c^{D/2}}\text{e}^{\frac{P}{c}-f}\,,

(14)

where

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{3}\dfrac{(-1)^{a_{i}}}{\Gamma(a_{i})}\int_{0}^{\infty}x_{i}^{a_{i}-1}\,dx_{i}\,,

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}\,,\qquad f=\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}m_{i}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}x_{i}x_{j}p_{i}\cdot p_{j}+c\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}p_{i}^{2}\,.

(15)

Here, the subscript in
J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
represents the number of massive propagators, just as in Ref.
[
7
]
, while the superscript represents the number of space-time dimensions.

The tensor integral
J^{\mu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
defined in Eq. (
11
) in the Schwinger representation is given by,

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=-\int\mathcal{D}x\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}p_{i}^{\mu}}{c^{(D+2)/2}}\text{e}^{\frac{P}{c}-f}\,.

(16)

Comparing Eq. (
16
) with Eq. (
14
), we obtain,

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_{i}p_{i}^{\mu}\,\mathbf{i}^{+}J_{3}^{D+2}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\,,

(17)

where the operator
\mathbf{i}^{+}
increases the power of the i-th propagator:

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}^{+}J_{3}^{D+2}(...,a_{i},...)=J_{3}^{D+2}(...,a_{i}+1,...)\,.

(18)

Similarly, one can readily obtain the following identity for
J^{\mu\nu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
,

\displaystyle J^{\mu\nu}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=-\dfrac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}J^{D+2}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}c_{ij}p_{i}^{\mu}p_{j}^{\nu}\mathbf{i}^{+}\mathbf{j}^{+}J^{D+4}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\,,

(19)

where the coefficients
c_{ij}
read as

\displaystyle c_{ij}=\begin{cases}a_{i}(a_{i}+1)&\text{for }i=j\\
a_{i}a_{j}&\text{for }i\neq j\,.\end{cases}

(20)

Eqs. (
17
) and (
19
) are particular examples of the general formulae obtained in Ref.
[
20
]
. Using these identities, we can transform the tensor integrals of the one-loop quark-gluon vertex into scalar integrals with shifted space-time dimensions and higher powers in the propagators.

To reduce the dimension of the scalar integrals, we can use the method introduced in Ref.
[
21
]
. According to this method, we again use the Schwinger representation of the scalar integral
J_{3}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
in Eq. (
14
) to obtain the following identity,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m_{i}^{2}}J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=-\int\mathcal{D}x\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}}{c^{D/2}}\text{e}^{\frac{P}{c}-f}\,.

(21)

Since
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}=c
, from the equation above, we obtain

\displaystyle J^{D-2}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_{i}\,\mathbf{i}^{+}J^{D}_{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\,.

(22)

Now, for the computation of the one-loop quark-gluon vertex, we need the following set of scalar integrals:

\displaystyle J_{1}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{(p^{\prime}+l)^{2a_{1}}(p+l)^{2a_{2}}(l^{2}-m^{2})^{a_{3}}},

\displaystyle J_{2}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}^{a_{1}}D_{p+l}^{a_{2}}(l^{2})^{a_{3}}}\,,

(23)

which correspond to
J_{3}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
defined in Eq. (
13
) with
m_{1}=m_{2}=0
,
m_{3}=m
for
J_{1}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, and
m_{1}=m_{2}=m
,
m_{3}=0
for
J_{2}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, while fixing the momentum
p_{1}=p^{\prime}
,
p_{2}=p
and
p_{3}=0
in both scalar integrals.

. There, the following identity is used:

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial_{\mu}}\left[\left(x_{3}^{(i)}l_{\mu}+x_{1}^{(i)}p^{\prime}_{\mu}+x_{2}^{(i)}p_{\mu}\right)A^{(i)}\right]=0\,,

where, in our case, we define the functions

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{(p^{\prime}+l)^{2a_{1}}(p+l)^{2a_{2}}(l^{2}-m^{2})^{a_{3}}}\,,

\displaystyle A^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}^{a_{1}}D_{p+l}^{a_{2}}(l^{2})^{a_{3}}}\,.

(25)

The identity in Eq. (
24
) is valid for any

. But, if we fixed the values of the

such that

\displaystyle x_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{Y^{(1)}}\left(p^{2}p^{\prime}\cdot k-m^{2}p\cdot k\right)\,,

\displaystyle x_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{2}{Y^{(1)}}\left(p^{\prime 2}p\cdot k-m^{2}p^{\prime}\cdot k\right)\,,

\displaystyle x_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{Y^{(2)}}\left(p^{2}p^{\prime}\cdot k+m^{2}p\cdot k\right)\,,

\displaystyle x_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{2}{Y^{(2)}}\left(p^{\prime 2}p\cdot k+m^{2}p^{\prime}\cdot k\right)\,,

\displaystyle x_{3}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{4G}{Y^{(i)}}\,,\quad i=1,2,

(26)

where

\displaystyle G=p^{2}p^{\prime 2}-(p\cdot p^{\prime})^{2},,

(27)

and

\displaystyle Y^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2k^{2}(m^{4}-2m^{2}p\cdot p^{\prime}+p^{2}p^{\prime 2})\,,

\displaystyle Y^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\left[k^{2}(m^{4}+p^{2}p^{\prime 2})+2m^{2}(p^{\prime 2}p\cdot p^{\prime}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt\left.+p^{2}(p\cdot p^{\prime}-2p^{\prime 2}))\right]\,.

\displaystyle J_{i}^{D+2}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{x_{3}^{(i)}(d+1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}a_{j})}\left[J_{i}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\right.

(29)

\displaystyle\hskip-70.0pt+x_{1}^{(i)}J_{i}^{D}(a_{1}-1,a_{2},a_{3})+x_{2}^{(i)}J_{i}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2}-1,a_{3})

\displaystyle\left.\hskip-70.0pt+(x_{3}^{(i)}-x_{1}^{(i)}-x_{2}^{(i)})J_{i}^{D}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}-1)\right]\,,

III
Off-shell quark-gluon vertex

In the case of an off-shell quark-gluon vertex, we can not completely introduce the second-order formalism since there are not enough pairs of propagators and the first-order vertex to rewrite them in terms of scalar propagators and second-order vertices. Thus, for the off-shell case, we combine the first and second-order formalisms by rewriting all possible pairs of a fermion propagators and first-order vertex using Eq. (
5
). This procedure leads to a natural decomposition between the transverse and longitudinal components with respect to the external gluon momentum, when this external momentum is attached to a fermion line as in the case of the abelian graph (Fig.
2(a)
).

We first present a summary of the combined first- and second-order formalism applied to the abelian graph (further details can be found in Ref.
[
5
]
). Then, in the preceding subsection we apply this method to the non-abelian graph (Fig.
2(b)
).

III.1
Off-shell abelian graph

Fig.
2(a)
depicts the abelian Feynman diagram for the vertex function
\Gamma^{\mu a}
at one-loop within the first-order formalism. It is basically the QED vertex which has also been extensively studied in the literature
[
26
,
27
,
28
,

,

,

]
.
Denoting this abelian contribution as
\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}
, its mathematical expression, using the standard Feynman rules, is:

\displaystyle i\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{3}T^{b}T^{a}T^{b}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{(2\pi)^{D}}\gamma^{\alpha}\dfrac{\not{p}^{\prime}+\not{l}+m}{(p^{\prime}+l)^{2}-m^{2}}\gamma^{\mu}\dfrac{\not{p}+\not{l}+m}{(p+l)^{2}-m^{2}}\gamma^{\beta}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(l)\,,

(31)

where the gluon propagator in an arbitrary covariant gauge reads as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(l)=\dfrac{1}{l^{2}}\left(g_{\alpha\beta}-\xi\dfrac{l_{\alpha}l_{\beta}}{l^{2}}\right)\,.

(32)

We can use the identities in Eqs. (
5
), (
6
)
and (

) to combine the first and second-order formalisms and rewrite the abelian part of the vertex function in Eq. (

) as

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{3}\overline{C}\,T^{a}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}\gamma^{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{B_{p+l,k}^{\mu}B_{p,l}^{\beta}}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}}-\dfrac{B_{p+l,k}^{\mu}C_{p}^{\beta}}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}}-\dfrac{\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\beta}}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}}\right)\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(l)\,,

(33)

with
\overline{C}=C_{F}-C_{A}/2
, and where
C_{F}
and
C_{A}
are, respectively, the eigenvalues of the quadratic Casimir of
SU(N)
in the fundamental and adjoint representations, respectively:

\displaystyle C_{F}=\dfrac{N^{2}-1}{2N}\,,\qquad C_{A}=N\,.

(34)

Now, the vector
B^{\mu}_{p+l,k}
in Eq. (
33
) has clear separation into transverse and longitudinal components with respect to the external gluon momentum
k^{\mu}
. The longitudinal component corresponds to
(p+p^{\prime}+2l)^{\mu}
, while the transverse component corresponds to
\sigma^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}
. This separation of longitudinal and transverse components remains after we commute
\gamma^{\alpha}
with
B^{\mu}_{p+l,k}
, since:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\alpha}B_{p+l,k}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{p+l,k}^{\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}+2(\gamma^{\mu}k^{\alpha}-g^{\mu\alpha}\not{k})\,,

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p+p^{\prime}+2l)^{\mu}\gamma^{\alpha}-\sigma^{\mu\beta}k_{\beta}\gamma^{\alpha}+2(\gamma^{\mu}k^{\alpha}-g^{\mu\alpha}\not{k})\,.

Here, the transverse components correspond to the second and third terms in the second line of Eq. (

), while the longitudinal component corresponds to the first term. Thus, after commuting
\gamma^{\alpha}
with
B^{\mu}_{p+l,k}
in Eq. (
33
), and contracting the Lorentz indices with the gluon propagator, we obtain the following natural decomposition of the abelian part of the quark-gluon vertex into its transverse and longitudinal components:

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{g^{3}\overline{C}\,T^{a}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{D}{2}}}\left(\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(A)}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(A)}\right]^{\mu}\right)\,,

(36)

where

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(A)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\left\{\dfrac{(p+p^{\prime}+2l)^{\mu}}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}l^{2}}\left[2\not{p}+\left(2-D-\xi-2\xi\dfrac{l\cdot p}{l^{2}}\right)\not{l}+(\xi-D)(\not{p}-m)\right]+\dfrac{(D-2-\xi)}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}l^{2}}\gamma^{\mu}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\dfrac{2\xi l^{\mu}}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}l^{4}}\not{l}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(A)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}l^{2}}\left\{\sigma^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}\left[\left(D-2+\xi+2\xi\dfrac{l\cdot p}{l^{2}}\right)\not{l}-2\not{p}+(D-4-\xi)(\not{p}-m)\right]\right.

(37)

\displaystyle\left.+\dfrac{2\xi}{l^{2}}(k\cdot l\gamma^{\mu}-l^{\mu}\not{k})\not{l}(\not{p}-m)+4\left[k\cdot(p+l)\gamma^{\mu}-(p+l)^{\mu}\not{k}+(k^{\mu}-\gamma^{\mu}\not{k})\not{l}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\xi\left(1+2\dfrac{l\cdot p}{l^{2}}\right)(k\cdot l\gamma^{\mu}-l^{\mu}\not{k})\right\}\,.

\displaystyle(p+p^{\prime})^{\mu}\left\{\left[(2-D)J_{2}^{D+2}(2,1,1)+\xi(p^{2}-m^{2})J_{2}^{D+2}(2,1,2)-\xi J_{2}^{D+2}(2,0,2)\right]\not{p}\right.

(38)

\displaystyle+2\left[m(D-\xi)+(2-D+\xi)\not{p}\right]\left[p^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+2}(1,2,1)+p^{\prime\mu}J_{2}^{D+2}(2,1,1)\right]+(D-2)\left[\gamma^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+2}(1,1,1)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\left((2p^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(1,3,1)+p^{\prime\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(2,2,1))\not{p}+(2p^{\prime\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(3,1,1)+p^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(2,2,1))\not{p}^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\left.\left.+(2p^{\prime\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(3,1,2)+p^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(2,2,2))\not{p}^{\prime}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(A)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}\left\{\left[(4-D+\xi)m+(D-6-\xi)\not{p}\right]J_{2}^{D}(1,1,1)+(D-2)\left[\not{p}J_{2}^{D+2}(1,2,1)+\not{p}^{\prime}J_{2}^{D+2}(2,1,1)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\xi(m^{2}-p^{2})\left[\not{p}J_{2}^{D+2}(1,2,2)+\not{p}^{\prime}J_{2}^{D+2}(2,1,2)\right]+\xi\not{p}^{\prime}J_{2}^{D+2}(2,0,2)\right\}+4J_{2}^{D}(1,1,1)(k\cdot p\gamma^{\mu}-p^{\mu}\not{k})

\displaystyle+2\xi\big\{\gamma^{\mu}\not{p}\left[2k\cdot pJ_{2}^{D+4}(1,3,2)+k\cdot p^{\prime}J_{2}^{D+4}(2,2,2)\right]-\not{k}\not{p}\left[2p^{\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(1,3,2)+p^{\prime\mu}J_{2}^{D+4}(2,2,2)\right]

III.2
Off-shell non-abelian graph

The non-abelian contribution
\left[\Gamma^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu a}
to the quark-gluon vertex within the first-order formalism, see Fig.
2(b)
, reads as,

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu a}=g^{3}f^{abc}T^{c}T^{b}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{(2\pi)^{D}}\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}\left(\dfrac{-\not{l}+m}{l^{2}-m^{2}}\right)\gamma^{\rho^{\prime}}\Delta_{\rho\rho^{\prime}}(p+l)\Delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}(p^{\prime}+l)V_{\rho\nu\mu}(p+l,-p^{\prime}-l,k)\,,

(40)

where the three-gluon vertex
V_{\alpha\beta\mu}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\alpha\beta\mu}(r,s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{\alpha\mu}(t-r)^{\beta}+g^{\alpha\beta}(r-s)^{\mu}+g^{\beta\mu}(s-t)^{\alpha}\,.

(41)

After performing the color algebra, and contracting the Lorentz indices of the quark and gluon propagators and the three-gluon vertex, the expression in Eq. (
40
) becomes

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}g^{3}C_{A}T^{a}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}\left(\dfrac{-\not{l}+m}{q^{\prime 2}q^{2}D_{l}}\right)\gamma^{\rho^{\prime}}\left\{g^{\mu\rho^{\prime}}(k-q)^{\nu^{\prime}}-g^{\mu\nu^{\prime}}(k+q^{\prime})^{\rho^{\prime}}+g^{\nu^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}}(q+q^{\prime})^{\mu}\right.

(42)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\xi q^{\rho^{\prime}}}{q^{2}}\left[g^{\mu\nu^{\prime}}q\cdot(k+q^{\prime})-k^{\nu^{\prime}}q^{\mu}-q^{\nu^{\prime}}q^{\prime\mu}\right]+\dfrac{\xi q^{\prime\nu^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime 2}}\left[g^{\mu\rho^{\prime}}q^{\prime}\cdot(q-k)+k^{\rho^{\prime}}q^{\prime\mu}-q^{\mu}q^{\prime\rho^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\left.+\dfrac{\xi^{2}q^{\rho^{\prime}}q^{\prime\nu^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime 2}q^{2}}\left(k\cdot q^{\prime}q^{\mu}-k\cdot qq^{\prime\mu}\right)\right\}\,,

where
q=p+l
and
q^{\prime}=p^{\prime}+l
.
Similarly to the abelian contribution, we combine the first and second-order formalism by rewriting

\displaystyle\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}(-\not{l}+m)\gamma^{\rho^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}A_{-l,0}^{\rho^{\prime}}=\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}B_{-l,0}^{\rho^{\prime}}-\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}C^{\rho^{\prime}}_{-l}

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt=-2\gamma^{\nu^{\prime}}l^{\rho^{\prime}}+(\sigma^{\nu^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}}+g^{\nu^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}})(\not{l}+m).

Using this identity, Eq. (
42
) can be cast in the following form:

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2(4\pi)^{\frac{D}{2}}}g^{3}C_{A}T^{a}\left(\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}\right)\,,

(44)

where the longitudinal
\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}
and transverse
\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}
components of the non-abelian graph that arise from the decomposition (
43

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{q^{\prime 2}q^{2}D_{l}}\Big\{(q+q^{\prime})^{\mu}\left[(D-3-\xi)\not{l}+m(D-1-\xi)\right]-2(\not{k}-\not{q})l^{\mu}+2\gamma^{\mu}l\cdot(k+q^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\xi}{q^{\prime 2}}\not{q}^{\prime}\left[2\left(q^{\prime}\cdot(k-q)l^{\mu}-l\cdot kq^{\prime\mu}+l\cdot q^{\prime}q^{\mu}\right)+\left((q^{2}-k^{2})\gamma^{\mu}+q^{\prime\mu}\not{k}\right)(\not{l}+m)\right]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\xi}{q^{2}}\left[2l\cdot q\left(q\cdot(k+q^{\prime})\gamma^{\mu}-\not{k}q^{\mu}-\not{q}q^{\prime\mu}\right)+\left((k^{2}-q^{\prime 2})\gamma^{\mu}+q^{\mu}\not{k}\right)\not{q}(\not{l}+m)\right]\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i\pi^{\frac{D}{2}}}\dfrac{1}{q^{\prime 2}q^{2}D_{l}}\Big\{3\sigma^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}(\not{l}+m)+\dfrac{\xi^{2}}{q^{\prime 2}q^{2}}\not{q}^{\prime}\left[2l\cdot q-\not{q}(\not{l}+m)\right]\left(q^{\mu}k\cdot q^{\prime}-q^{\prime\mu}k\cdot q\right)\Big\}\,.

(45)

Since the external gluon is not attached to a fermion line in the non-abelian case, the combination of the second-order formalism with the first-order formalism does not have a cleaner separation of longitudinal and transverse components as in the abelian diagram. However, it does provide a compact tensor structure for applying the tensor reduction formulas (Eqs. (
17
) and (

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{L}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{12}\alpha_{i}h^{\mu}_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\Gamma_{T}^{(NA)}\right]^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{8}\beta_{i}T^{\mu}_{i}\,,

(46)

where

\displaystyle h_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu},

\displaystyle\quad h_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\mu},

\displaystyle\quad h_{3}

\displaystyle=p^{\prime\mu},

\displaystyle h_{4}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu}\not{p},

\displaystyle\quad h_{5}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu}\not{p}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\quad h_{6}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\mu}\not{p},

\displaystyle h_{7}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\mu}\not{p}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\quad h_{8}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\prime\mu}\not{p},

\displaystyle\quad h_{9}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\prime\mu}\not{p}^{\prime},

\displaystyle h_{10}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\mu}\not{p}^{\prime}\not{p},

\displaystyle\quad h_{11}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\prime\mu}\not{p}^{\prime}\not{p},

\displaystyle\quad h_{12}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu}\not{p}^{\prime}\not{p},

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}\cdot kp^{\mu}-p\cdot kp^{\prime\mu}\,,

\displaystyle T_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=(p^{\prime}\cdot kp^{\mu}-p\cdot kp^{\prime\mu})(\not{p}+\not{p}^{\prime})\,,

\displaystyle T_{3}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\gamma^{\mu}-k^{\mu}\not{k}\,,

\displaystyle T_{4}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-(p^{\prime}\cdot kp^{\mu}-p\cdot kp^{\prime\mu})\sigma_{\nu\lambda}p^{\prime\nu}p^{\lambda}\,,

\displaystyle T_{5}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mu\nu}k_{\nu}\,,

\displaystyle T_{6}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu}(p^{\prime 2}-p^{2})-(p+p^{\prime})^{\mu}\not{k}\,,

\displaystyle T_{7}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p^{2}-p^{\prime 2})[(p+p^{\prime})^{\mu}-\gamma^{\mu}(\not{p}+\not{p}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle-(p+p^{\prime})^{\mu}\sigma^{\nu\lambda}p^{\prime\nu}p^{\lambda}\,,

\displaystyle T_{8}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\mu}\sigma_{\nu\lambda}p^{\prime\nu}p^{\lambda}-p^{\prime\mu}\not{p}+p^{\mu}\not{p}^{\prime}\,.

(48)

The coefficients

IV
On-shell quark-gluon vertex: A scalar-spin interaction analysis

For the case of on-shell quarks, we can perform a complete transition from first-order amplitudes to their
second-order version. Once we have a second-order version of the amplitude, we can analyze and classify its spin and scalar interactions. To illustrate this kind of analysis, we now compute the contributions to the quark magnetic and chromo-magnetic dipole moments that arise from different scalar and spin interactions. In the following, we fix
\xi=0
in order to simplify the calculations.

IV.1
On-shell abelian graph

According to the first-order rules, the product of the on-shell spinors and the one-loop
\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}
vertex reads as,

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})\left[\Gamma^{(A)}\right]^{\mu a}u_{s}(p)=\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})\left[g^{3}\overline{C}T^{a}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}\gamma^{\alpha}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\left.\times\dfrac{\not{p}^{\prime}+\not{l}+m}{(p^{\prime}+l)^{2}-m^{2}}\gamma^{\mu}\dfrac{\not{p}+\not{l}+m}{l^{2}[(p+l)^{2}-m^{2}]}\gamma_{\alpha}\right]u_{s}(p),

(53)

where the spinors
\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})
and
u_{s}(p)
satisfy the on-shell conditions,

\displaystyle(\not{p}-m)u_{s}(p)=0\,,\quad\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})(\not{p}^{\prime}-m)=0\,.

(54)

Now, for a complete description of the expression in Eq. (
53
) within the second-order formalism, we introduce a prefactor, as discussed in Ref.
[
2
]
, before the first
\gamma^{\alpha}
matrix using the following identity

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})=\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})\dfrac{(\not{p}^{\prime}+m)}{2m}\,.

(55)

Thus, the expression in (
53
) becomes

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\Gamma^{A}\right]^{\mu}u_{s}=\overline{C}\,\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\dfrac{g^{3}}{2m}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}(\not{p}^{\prime}+m)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\hskip 10.0pt\left.\times\gamma^{\alpha}\dfrac{\not{p}^{\prime}+\not{l}+m}{(p^{\prime}+l)^{2}-m^{2}}\gamma^{\mu}\dfrac{\not{p}+\not{l}+m}{l^{2}[(p+l)^{2}-m^{2}]}\gamma_{\alpha}\right]u_{s}\,,

(56)

where we have suppressed the color factor
T^{a}
, and the momentum dependence of the spinors
u_{s}(p)
and
\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})
for notational simplicity.
From Eq. (
5
), the expression above can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\Gamma^{A}\right]^{\mu}u_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{C}\,\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\dfrac{g^{3}}{2m}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}\dfrac{1}{l^{2}D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}}\right.

(57)

\displaystyle\times A^{\alpha}_{p^{\prime}+l,-l}A^{\mu}_{p^{\prime},l}A_{\alpha\,p,l}\left.\right]u_{s}\,,

After employing the definitions given in Eq. (
6
), the identity in Eq. (
9
), and the spinor on-shell conditions given in Eq. (
54
), the expression in Eq. (
57
) becomes

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\Gamma^{A}\right]^{\mu}u_{s}=\overline{C}\,\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left\{\dfrac{g^{3}}{2m}\int\dfrac{d^{D}l}{i(2\pi)^{D}}\dfrac{1}{l^{2}D_{p^{\prime}+l}D_{p+l}}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\times\left[B^{\alpha}_{p^{\prime}+l,-l}B^{\mu}_{p+l,k}B_{\alpha\,p,l}-D_{p+l}B_{\alpha\,p^{\prime}+l,-l}(\sigma^{\mu\alpha}+g^{\mu\alpha})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\left.-D_{p^{\prime}+l}(\sigma^{\alpha\mu}+g^{\mu\alpha})B_{\alpha\,p,l}\right]\bigg\}u_{s}\,,

(58)

which now contains only second-order scalar and spin (vertices) interactions that can be grouped as

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left[\Gamma^{A}\right]^{\mu}u_{s}=\dfrac{g\overline{C}}{2m}\overline{u}_{s^{\prime}}\left(\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sc-sc-sc}}\right]^{\mu}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 20.0pt+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sc-sc}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sp-sc}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sc-sc-sp}}\right]^{\mu}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 20.0pt+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sc-sp}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sp-sp}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sc-sc}}\right]^{\mu}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 20.0pt+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sc}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sc-sp}}\right]^{\mu}+\left[\Gamma^{A}_{\text{sp-sp}}\right]^{\mu}\right)u_{s}\,.

(59)

Here, the second-order vertex functions
\left[\Gamma^{A}_{a}\right]^{\mu}
are listed in Appendix
B

V
Conclusions

The second-order formalism of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is built upon a Gordon-type decomposition applied to the product of a fermion propagator and a first-order vertex, as illustrated in Eq. (
5
). This decomposition allows us to decouple the spin and scalar degrees of freedom in the chromodynamic interaction for a color-charged fermion. By employing this approach, we can enhance our understanding and intuition of quantum processes and functions that involve both fermions and gluons, such as the quark-gluon vertex
\Gamma^{\mu}(p,p^{\prime},k)^{a,\mu}
.

In this article, we apply the second-order formalism to examine the off-shell and on-shell one-loop quark-gluon vertex in detail. Notice that in the off-shell case, a full implementation of the second-order formalism is not possible due to a lack of sufficient first-order vertices with which to pair the fermion propagator. Especifically, we have one less fermion propagator, preventing a complete pairing. Thus, in this case, the Gordon-type decomposition of Eq. (
5
) produces a hybrid framework that combines both first- and second-order formalisms. This hybrid approach naturally separates the longitudinal and transverse components of the vertex when the external gluon is attached directly to the fermion line, as shown in the abelian first-order diagram in Fig.
2(a)
. The longitudinal and transverse components, as derived in Eq. (
37
), are determined prior to any attempt at performing the tensor Feynman integral. By applying the tensor reduction identities presented in Eqs. (
17
) and (
19
), we obtain relatively compact expressions for the longitudinal and transverse components, as summarized in Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
). In contrast, for the non-abelian graph shown in Fig.
2(b)
, the combination of first- and second-order formalisms does not lead to as straightforward a separation of the longitudinal and transverse components, since the external gluon is not attached to the fermion line. Nonetheless, even in this case, the longitudinal and transverse components, as expressed in Eq. (
45
), can still be extracted prior to the application of the tensor reduction formulae to the tensor integrals.

In the on-shell case, the second-order formalism can be fully implemented by inserting an additional fermion propagator into the vertex function, utilizing the identity in Eq. (
55
). Within this framework, a detailed analysis of various scalar and spin interactions between the fermion line and the gluons can be conducted. This analysis is presented in section
IV
, where Tables
1
and
2
summarize the contributions of different scalar and spin interactions to the form factors
F_{\text{spin}}^{NA}(0)
and
F_{\text{spin}}^{A}(0)
, which are associated with the electromagnetic and chromomagnetic dipole moments, respectively. The Tables indicate that, at the one-loop order, only a single second-order diagram is required to reproduce the scalar form factors
F_{1}^{A}(0)
and
F_{1}^{NA}(0)
. Specifically, for
F_{1}^{A}(0)
, the corresponding diagram is depicted in Fig.
6(a)
, while for
F_{1}^{NA}(0)
, the relevant diagram is depicted in Fig.
7(b)
.

Another conclusion that can be drawn from Tables
1
and
2
is that at
k^{2}=0
, only those diagrams containing a single spin interaction and at least one scalar interaction—excluding the diagram associated with the form factors
F_{1}(0)
- are relevant for the physical contributions to the dipole moments. In the non-abelian case, this corresponds to the diagrams where the virtual gluon interacts with the fermion line, exchanging spin only once, as shown in Fig.
7(c)
. In the abelian case, the second-order diagrams relevant to the magnetic dipole moment are represented in Figs.
6(e)
,
6(h)
and
6(i)
.

In the diagrams of Fig.
6(e)
, the external gluon exchanges momentum with the fermion line without altering the spin of the virtual fermions. The spin change of the fermion line arises from the loop of virtual gluons. These interactions reduce the value of the anomalous magnetic dipole moment by 1, as indicated in the fifth row of Table
1
. The other significant diagrams, shown in Figs.
6(h)
and
6(i)
, demonstrate that the spin of the fermion line is altered either through a spin interaction with the virtual gluons or via a spin interaction involving both the external and virtual gluons. In the former case, depicted in Fig.
6(i)
, the anomalous magnetic moment is reduced by half, while in the latter case, shown in Fig.
6(h)
, the value is increased by
\frac{7}{2}
, according to Table
1
, considering only the finite contributions. Summing these contributions yields a total value of 2, which, when multiplied by the global factor
\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}
, results in the Schwinger value for the anomalous magnetic dipole moment of the electron. Assuming that this result generalizes to higher-order loops, we conjecture that only a specific set of second-order diagrams contributes to the anomalous dipole moments, in which the spin interchange is minimal.
