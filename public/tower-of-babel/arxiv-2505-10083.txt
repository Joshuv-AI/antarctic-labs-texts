ChronoSteer: Bridging Large Language Model and Time Series Foundation Model via Synthetic Cross-Modal Alignment Dataset
Chengsen Wang, Qi Qi, Zhongwen Rao, Lujia Pan, Jingyu Wang

Abstract
. Conventional forecasting methods are trained end-to-end on unimodal time series, which limits their ability to exploit textual information and undermines their generalization in data-scarce scenarios. Recently, large language models (LLMs) and time series foundation models (TSFMs) have demonstrated powerful capabilities in complex textual reasoning and zero-shot temporal modeling, respectively. Integrating these strengths to construct a multimodal time series foundation model that jointly leverages temporal and textual information for zero-shot future inference has emerged as a promising research direction. However, the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality multimodal datasets remains a fundamental obstacle. To address this challenge, we propose ChronoSteer, a decoupled agentic framework that learns cross-modal alignment from synthetic paired supervision. Specifically, a pretrained LLM first converts textual events into revision instructions that steer the initial unimodal prediction produced by a frozen TSFM. These revision instructions form an intermediate instruction space that bridges the semantic gap between text and time series while fully leveraging pretrained knowledge. Technically, the instructions are discretized into a compact codebook of instruction anchors, effectively mitigating semantic divergence while reducing the cost of dataset construction. Finally, we adopt a two-stage training strategy to recover the fine-grained magnitude information lost during discretization. Furthermore, we release a leakage-controlled multimodal benchmark constructed with temporal separation and textual context available before the prediction window. When paired with an LLM and trained on synthetic cross-modal alignment data, ChronoSteer achieves a 25.8% improvement in zero-shot prediction accuracy over its unimodal backbone, and outperforms prior state-of-the-art unimodal and multimodal methods by 17.8% and 22.5%. These results suggest that synthetic paired supervision is a promising direction for multimodal time series analysis.

1.
Introduction

Time series forecasting holds considerable practical value across various domains such as finance
(
Liu et al., 2022
)
, transportation
(
Liu et al., 2023a
)
, healthcare
(
Huang et al., 2025
)
, and energy
(
Fan et al., 2024
)
. Human experts usually combine multimodal information to make predictions. For instance, transportation experts often infer flow trends by jointly analyzing historical series and public events. Recently, neural network-based methods
(
Zeng et al., 2023
;
Nie et al., 2023
;
Hu et al., 2025a
)
have demonstrated strong performance in unimodal forecasting tasks due to powerful modeling capabilities. However, their end-to-end training typically demands substantial computational resources and domain-specific data, which poses high barriers to deployment in low-resource or data-scarce scenarios. To alleviate these challenges, time series foundation models (TSFMs) have emerged as a promising alternative. Pretrained on massive collections of time series, these out-of-the-box models
(
Das et al., 2024
;
Woo et al., 2024
)
exhibit strong generalization and can perform zero-shot forecasting without additional fine-tuning. Although models like Chronos
(
Ansari et al., 2024
)
have shown impressive accuracy, they are pre-trained exclusively on unimodal time series and still lack the capacity to exploit textual information.

Meanwhile, large language models (LLMs)
(
Team, 2023b
;
Team, 2025a
)
have exhibited remarkable capability in textual reasoning, prompting growing interest in integrating them into time series analysis. Prior attempts to combine language and temporal modeling have followed two broad paradigms. Some approaches
(
Jin et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
)
transfer LLM weights into time series frameworks and perform cross-modal alignment fine-tuning to build end-to-end models for integrating temporal and textual information. However, they often require dataset-specific adaptation and lack zero-shot generalization. Alternatively, other studies
(
Gruver et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2024c
)
transform time series into string formats, leveraging prompts to enable LLMs for zero-shot forecasting in unimodal and multimodal scenarios. However, the inherent limitations of LLMs in precise numerical inference and fine-grained temporal reasoning hinder their accuracy in time series analysis
(
Fons et al., 2024
;
Merrill et al., 2024
)
. Another fundamental obstacle for this field is the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality multimodal datasets.
Consequently, how to effectively integrate the complementary strengths of TSFMs (zero-shot temporal modeling) and LLMs (rich textual reasoning) into a single multimodal framework, especially in the absence of high-quality aligned data, remains a promising yet underexplored direction.

To address this challenge, we propose ChronoSteer, a decoupled agentic framework that learns cross-modal alignment from synthetic paired supervision. Compared with fine-tuning LLMs to enhance domain-specific capabilities
(
Wang et al., 2025b
)
, equipping lightweight unimodal TSFMs with textual comprehension represents a substantially more computationally efficient path. By freezing the unimodal TSFM backbone and integrating a lightweight branch to process textual information, this paradigm reduces training overhead while strictly preserving the unimodal forecasting accuracy of TSFMs and the event reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Textual descriptions such as backgrounds and events often present semantic complexity and data sparsity, posing significant challenges for direct cross-modal alignment without high-quality paired data. We therefore leverage a pretrained LLM with domain knowledge to reinterpret textual events as concise revision instructions that specify how an initial unimodal prediction produced by a frozen TSFM should be adjusted. This intermediate instruction space both narrows the semantic gap between text and series and fully exploits the pretrained knowledge of LLMs. For example, although an event like
Christmas Holidays
may appear infrequently in raw training datasets, once reinterpreted by LLMs as an instruction such as
Elevate Peaks
, it can be easier for TSFMs to learn and apply. Notably, while some time series editing models
(
Jing et al., 2024
)
have explored attribute-based series tuning, they are restricted to simple attributes and tend to neglect incorporating historical information.

Considering the inherent limitations of LLMs in temporal reasoning
(
Fons et al., 2024
;
Merrill et al., 2024
)
, directly employing them to construct large-scale instruction-series paired datasets is both prohibitively expensive and unreliable. Besides, open-text instructions often exhibit semantic divergence and low signal-to-noise ratios
(
Xiong et al., 2025
)
, which impose stringent requirements on the quality and scale of aligned data. To address this issue, we adopt a hybrid data synthesis strategy. Specifically, we first employ LLMs to generate revision instructions for a small set of representative samples obtained via clustering. These instructions are subsequently refined and summarized based on expert knowledge and domain-specific priors. Following this summarization, the open-text instructions are discretized into a compact codebook of instruction anchors, which serve as discrete prototypes of temporal adjustment behaviors. For each anchor, we design a corresponding parametric synthesis function to expand the dataset, thereby substantially reducing the cost and complexity of constructing large-scale multimodal datasets. To further recover the fine-grained magnitude information lost during discretization and overcome the expressive limitations of the synthesis functions, we develop a two-stage training framework. The first stage coarsely aligns the model with the anchor-conditioned revision behaviors, while the second stage mines implicit cues from the historical context to refine magnitude-level details and improve temporal consistency.

Moreover, the development of benchmarks
(
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Williams et al., 2024
)
for multimodal time series forecasting (MTSF) remains nascent. A significant concern is the potential for data leakage arising from overlaps between benchmark datasets and the corpora used to pretrain LLMs and TSFMs. This can lead to inflated performance metrics during evaluation. To address this concern, we follow the established temporal-update paradigm of ChatTime
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
to construct MTSFBench-300, a leakage-controlled benchmark covering three representative domains: Energy, Traffic, and Weather. MTSFBench-300 itself is constructed after the evaluated open-source or open-weight checkpoints are fixed and uses only textual context available before the prediction window, so the released checkpoints are not trained on this benchmark and the evaluation does not expose future observations.

The contributions of our paper are summarised as follows:

•

Framework:
We introduce ChronoSteer, a decoupled multimodal framework that effectively bridges LLMs and TSFMs. By freezing the unimodal TSFM backbone and employing a lightweight adapter, ChronoSteer concurrently leverages temporal dynamics and textual instructions for efficient zero-shot future inference without compromising unimodal performance.

•

Strategy:
We design a two-stage training strategy to overcome the scarcity of high-quality multimodal alignment data. By discretizing open-text events into a codebook of instruction anchors and applying parametric synthesis functions, we learn cross-modal alignment without requiring manually annotated real-world cross-modal pairs.

•

Benchmark:
Addressing the risk of data leakage in existing evaluations, we construct MTSFBench-300, a leakage-controlled benchmark spanning the Energy, Traffic, and Weather domains. It provides a semantically aligned platform for evaluating multimodal forecasting under a clear temporal-separation protocol.

•

Performance:
We conduct extensive experiments comparing ChronoSteer with mainstream baselines. The results show that ChronoSteer, trained on synthetic cross-modal alignment pairs, improves zero-shot multimodal forecasting accuracy on MTSFBench-300 under the tested settings.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Unimodal Time Series Forecasting

As a critical real-world challenge, time series forecasting has attracted considerable attention. Early research mainly relies on statistical methods like ARIMA
(
Box and Jenkins, 1968
)
, which uses moving averages for prediction. However, these approaches often struggle with the complexity of real-world scenarios. With deep learning progress, neural network-based approaches have grown in popularity. RNNs
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997
;
Flunkert et al., 2017
)
capture temporal dependency through sequential architecture, yet encounter limitations such as vanishing/exploding gradients and information loss when handling long series. To overcome these challenges, researchers have introduced CNNs
(
Wang et al., 2023
;
Wu et al., 2023
)
and Transformers
(
Zhou et al., 2021
;
Liu et al., 2024d
)
to capture long-term temporal dependency. While these methods have achieved notable improvements, their training typically demands significant computational resources, domain expertise, and abundant data, posing high barriers to practical deployment. In response, TSFMs have emerged as a promising alternative. Pretrained on extensive datasets, these models
(
Goswami et al., 2024
;
Shi et al., 2025
;
Liu et al., 2025b
)
exhibit robust generalization to support zero-shot forecasting. Nonetheless, most existing TSFMs are trained solely on unimodal time series, lacking the capacity to process multimodal inputs such as text information, which constrains the applicability across diverse scenarios. Unlike these purely unimodal approaches, our proposed ChronoSteer equips frozen TSFMs with a lightweight adapter to interpret textual instructions. This design preserves the superior numerical forecasting capability of TSFMs while extending their applicability to multimodal contexts without expensive retraining.

2.2.
Multimodal Time Series Forecasting

The LLMs
(
Team, 2023a
;
Team, 2025c
)
with strong text reasoning capabilities have brought new opportunities to time series analysis. Some approaches
(
Pan et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024e
;
Hu et al., 2025b
)
transfer LLM weights into time series frameworks and apply cross-modal alignment fine-tuning to enable end-to-end methods for integrating temporal and textual information. However, they often require dataset-specific adaptation and lack zero-shot generalization. Moreover, the effectiveness of directly applying text-pretrained weights to model numerical time series still remains uncertain
(
Tan et al., 2024
)
. Alternatively, other studies
(
Gruver et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2024c
)
transform time series into string formats, leveraging prompts to enable LLMs for zero-shot forecasting in unimodal and multimodal scenarios. However, the inherent limitations of LLMs in temporal understanding, specifically the challenges in extracting complex features from numerical data
(
Cai et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024a
)
and generating precise values with intricate attributes
(
Gruver et al., 2023
)
, hinder their ability to perform accurate numerical inference. While instruction fine-tuning brings partial improvements
(
Guo et al., 2024
)
, the constrained training data undermines broader generalization. The development of multimodal time series foundation models capable of zero-shot future inference by jointly leveraging textual and temporal information remains largely unexplored. As the first attempt, ChatTime
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
still relies on LLMs to perform zero-shot forecasting, without incorporating TSFMs that are specifically designed for numerical modeling. In contrast to these approaches, ChronoSteer not only achieves zero-shot generalization but also strategically decouples the modeling process: it assigns temporal reasoning to a specialized TSFM while restricting the LLM to semantic instruction generation. Unlike prior methods that force language models to execute precise numerical predictions, our strategy effectively synergizes the numerical precision of TSFMs with the rich semantic understanding of LLMs.

The time series editing task in TEdit
(
Jing et al., 2024
)
shares similarities with our work. However, TEdit focuses on adjusting series based on specified attributes, while our goal is to generate future series conditioned on both instructions and historical data. Ignoring historical information limits their control over modification extent. Moreover, TEdit supports only relatively restricted attributes, allowing only complete changes in trends or cycles. It also lacks zero-shot generalization across various datasets. Different from TEdit, ChronoSteer possesses zero-shot generalization capabilities across diverse scenarios. Moreover, it generates future series conditioned on both textual instructions and historical data, ensuring that the predicted outcome is both semantically consistent with text and temporally coherent with historical context.

Several studies
(
Chen et al., 2024b
;
Liu et al., 2024b
)
have introduced benchmark datasets for MTSF. However, the sources of these datasets are largely outdated. Considering the ongoing expansion of corpus size required for LLMs and TSFMs, existing benchmarks may have already been included in training data. This overlap leads to information leakage during evaluation, undermining the reliability of experiment results. To address this issue, we construct MTSFBench-300 after the evaluated checkpoints are fixed and use only context available before the prediction window during evaluation. This temporal-separation protocol provides a more rigorous platform for evaluating zero-shot multimodal forecasting capabilities.

3.
Methodology

To enhance the distinction, a standardized notation for future series is introduced: ground truth is
\mathbf{y}
, unimodal prediction is
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
, multimodal prediction is
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
, and function-transformed series is
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
.

3.1.
Problem Definition

This paper explores MTSF, aiming to concurrently leverage both temporal and textual information for future inference. Consistent with most previous TSFMs
(
Ansari et al., 2024
;
Jin et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2025a
;
Garza and Canseco, 2023
)
, we focus on univariate time series. As for multivariate time series, they can be decomposed into multiple univariate series under the assumption of variable independence
(
Zeng et al., 2023
)
. Specifically, each input instance is represented by a multimodal pair
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\right)
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times 1}
denotes a univariate series of historical observations of length
H
, and
\mathbf{e}
indicates the corresponding textual information. The main goal is to build a generalizable multimodal forecasting framework
\mathcal{M}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
to predict future observations
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times 1}
over
P
time steps:

(1)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\right)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

Moreover, we expect the TSFMs to possess zero-shot forecasting capability, meaning that after training on large-scale datasets, the model can generate accurate predictions in downstream scenarios without any fine-tuning. To ensure fairer evaluation, benchmarks should be constructed after the evaluated checkpoints are fixed and should use only information available before the prediction window.

3.2.
Framework Overview

Figure 1.
The overview of ChronoSteer. Spark icons indicate trainable parameters, while snowflake icons denote frozen components. (a) illustrates the overall architecture, preserving the unimodal TSFM backbone while integrating a lightweight branch to process textual instruction. To mitigate the scarcity of cross-modal paired data, we introduce a two-stage training strategy: (b) synthetic data driven pre-training followed by (c) pseudo label guided fine-tuning.

Textual descriptions such as contextual backgrounds and event narratives often involve high semantic complexity and data sparsity, which create significant challenges for achieving effective cross-modal alignment without high-quality paired data. To address this issue, we propose a decoupled multimodal forecasting framework that bridges LLMs and TSFMs through textual revision instructions. Specifically, the TSFM
\mathcal{M}_{\text{series}}
first generates unimodal predictions
\bar{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times 1}
based on historical observations
\mathbf{x}
. Next, an LLM
\mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}
generates textual revision instructions
\mathbf{c}
by comprehensively analyzing the historical observations
\mathbf{x}
, the unimodal predictions
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
, and the associated textual context
\mathbf{e}
. Finally,
\mathcal{M}_{\text{series}}
steered by instructions
\mathbf{c}
produces the multimodal predictions
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
. This three-stage decomposition shifts the LLM task from value generation to event influence analysis, which aligns better with its textual reasoning strengths. The intermediate instruction space then offers a simpler interface between text semantics and TSFM prediction. This inference paradigm can be formalized as:

(2)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf{y}}&=\mathcal{M}_{\text{series}}\left(\mathbf{x},\varnothing\right)\\
\mathbf{c}&=\mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}\left(\mathbf{x},\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{e}\right)\\
\hat{\mathbf{y}}&=\mathcal{M}_{\text{series}}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}\right)\\
\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

This work aims to bridge LLMs and TSFMs by developing a multimodal TSFM
\mathcal{M}_{\text{series}}
that can be dynamically guided by textual revision instructions. The detailed process for generating these revision instructions, described in Appendix
C
, is beyond the primary scope of this paper. With the ongoing advancements in LLMs
(
Team, 2025a
)
, the accuracy of semantic transformation is expected to improve continuously, which further strengthens the practicality of our framework.

As illustrated in Figure
1
(a), we develop ChronoSteer, a multimodal TSFM capable of being steered by textual revision instructions. Technically, we introduce a lightweight branch to process textual instructions while preserving the original unimodal backbone of the TSFM. In the absence of textual input, ChronoSteer naturally reverts to the unimodal forecasting mode of the backbone, ensuring stability and adaptability in practical applications. Furthermore, to mitigate the scarcity of cross-modal instruction-series paired data, we design a two-stage training strategy (Figures
1
(b) and
1
(c)) built on synthetic alignment pairs. Our experiments show that this training strategy can improve zero-shot multimodal forecasting without relying on manually annotated cross-modal pairs.

3.3.
ChronoSteer Architecture

The architecture of ChronoSteer is illustrated in Figure
1
(a). Given an input pair
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}\right)
, the model first performs feature embedding. Specifically, the time series
\mathbf{x}
is transformed into a sequence of tokens
\mathbf{H}^{\text{x}}=\left\{\mathbf{h}_{1}^{\text{x}},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{L}^{\text{x}}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D_{\text{series}}}
through the embedding layer in pre-trained unimodal TSFM. Here,
L
represents the number of embedding tokens, which typically corresponds to the number of patches, and
D_{\text{series}}
is the dimension of time series embedding. Meanwhile, the textual instruction
\mathbf{c}
is mapped into a text embedding
\mathbf{h}^{\text{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D_{\text{text}}}
using a pre-trained text embedding model, where
D_{\text{text}}
is the dimension of the text embedding.

To alleviate semantic divergence in instructions
(
Xiong et al., 2025
)
and the scarcity of paired data, as discussed in Section
3.4.1
, we introduce an anchor matching mechanism. We construct a compact codebook
\mathbf{C}=\left\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{N}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D_{\text{text}}}
that contains
N
anchor revision instructions (with
N=9
in our experiments). These anchors act as discrete prototypes that represent different types of temporal adjustment behaviors. The cosine similarity retrieval is employed to map
\mathbf{h}^{\text{c}}
to its nearest anchor
\bar{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D_{\text{text}}}
:

(3)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}}=\mathbf{c}_{i},\ \ \operatorname{where}\ \ i=\arg\max_{j\in\left[1,N\right]}\cos\left(\mathbf{h}^{\text{c}},\mathbf{c}_{j}\right)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is used for alignment of dimension and semantics:

(4)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}}=\operatorname{ReLU}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}}\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\right)\mathbf{W}_{2}+\mathbf{b}_{2}\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

where
\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{text}}\times D_{\text{hidden}}}
and
\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{hidden}}\times D_{\text{series}}}
are weights,
D_{\text{hidden}}
is the hidden layer dimension.
\mathbf{b}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D_{\text{hidden}}}
,
\mathbf{b}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D_{\text{series}}}
are biases. Notably, only this module needs to be optimized.

Given that Transformers tend to allocate more attention to the initial token
(
Ji et al., 2025
)
,
\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D_{\text{series}}}
is concatenated to the start of
\mathbf{H}^{\text{x}}
, resulting in a combined representation
\mathbf{H}=\left[\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{\text{c}};\mathbf{H}^{\text{x}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(L+1\right)\times D_{\text{series}}}
. After processing by frozen TSFM backbone, the final prediction
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
is produced.

3.4.
Synthetic Data Driven Pre-training

3.4.1.
Multimodal Triplet Construction

We utilize Monash Repository
(
Godahewa et al., 2021
)
as our training data source. A sliding window (length 160, stride 32) slices the original series into about 15M slices. Each slice is independently normalized and split into historical series
\mathbf{x}
(length 128) and prediction series
\mathbf{y}
(length 32). K-means clustering
(
Pedregosa et al., 2011
)
is applied to optimize efficiency, like ChatTime
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
, grouping samples into 1K, 10K and 100K clusters, respectively. We randomly sample one slice from each cluster to balance diversity and efficiency.

A practical approach to constructing the instruction-series paired dataset is to input both the unimodal prediction
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
generated by the TSFMs and the corresponding ground-truth labels
\mathbf{y}
into LLMs, prompting them to produce revision instructions. We validated this approach on the representative dataset containing 1K samples. However, due to the inherent limitations of LLMs in temporal reasoning
(
Fons et al., 2024
;
Merrill et al., 2024
)
, the accuracy of the generated instructions in coarse-grained revision directions is only about 68%, as evaluated by five human experts. The performance in specifying fine-grained adjustment magnitudes is even less satisfactory. Prior research has shown that open-text instructions often suffer from semantic ambiguity and a low signal-to-noise ratio
(
Xiong et al., 2025
)
, which imposes stringent requirements on the quality and scale of aligned data. Considering that directly synthesizing large-scale data with LLMs is both costly and unreliable, we adopt a more conservative but controllable function-based strategy.

Specifically, these open-text instructions are refined and summarized based on expert knowledge. Considering both the coverage of the codebook over 1K representative samples and the feasibility of constructing synthetic functions, we consolidate the 1K open-text instructions into nine core anchors, which represent discrete prototypes of temporal adjustment behaviors. For each anchor (
Keep Unchanged
,
Increase Trend
,
Reduce Trend
,
Expand Amplitude
,
Compress Amplitude
,
Elevate Peaks
,
Lower Peaks
,
Raise Troughs
, and
Deepen Troughs
), we design a corresponding transformation function, as described in Algorithm
5
. These functions are then applied to 100K slices, generating the ChronoSteer-100K-PT dataset, which contains 900K precisely aligned synthetic triplets
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right)
. Since fine-grained adjustment magnitudes are difficult to obtain in evaluation or practice, we exclude them from the anchor instructions. This process is illustrated in the first row of Figure
1
(b) and Algorithm
1
. Specifically, for each historical series
\mathbf{x}
, a unimodal TSFM first predicts the initial output
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
. Subsequently, the nine transformation functions, each corresponding to an anchor revision instruction within the instruction codebook
\mathbf{C}=\left\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{N}\right\}
, are applied to
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
, producing enhanced series
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
.

In addition to the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}
, a contrastive loss
(
Radford et al., 2021
)
is introduced to further enhance cross-modal alignment. This loss minimizes the distance between the function output
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
and the predicted output
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
for the same text instruction
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, while maximizing the distance to outputs from other instructions.

(6)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Con}}=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigg[&\log\frac{\exp\left(\cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\right)\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right)\right)}+\log\frac{\exp\left(\cos\left(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\right)\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\cos\left(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right)\right)}\Bigg]\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

Finally, the complete loss function for a single instance is:

(7)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{Con}}\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

where
\alpha
is used to balance the relative contributions.

Considering that directly employing LLMs to construct large-scale instruction-series paired datasets is both prohibitively expensive and unreliable, we adopt a function-based synthesis strategy without specifying magnitude during the pretraining stage. These functions provide controllable directional supervision, but they do not fully reproduce the nonlinear, delayed, or domain-specific real-world event effects. Using only function-transformed future series
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
as targets discards fine-grained magnitude information and confines the model to the predefined transformation functions. To address this issue, we adopt observed future series
\mathbf{y}
as the optimization target during fine-tuning. This approach promotes the model to extract implicit cues from the historical context, refine magnitude-level details, and improve temporal consistency.

Technically, we propose an optimal matching-based pseudo-label generation strategy, shown in the left part of Figure
1
(c) and Algorithm
3
, to create revision instructions from unimodal predictions
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
to align with
\mathbf{y}
. For each
\mathbf{x}
, we freeze the pre-trained ChronoSteer and enumerate all anchors in the codebook to obtain a multimodal prediction set
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}=\left\{\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{N}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}
, where each candidate corresponds to one anchor instruction. The prediction
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
closest to
\mathbf{y}
is selected based on MSE, and its corresponding anchor instruction
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is assigned as the pseudo-label
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
:

(8)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{c}}=\mathbf{c}_{i},\ \ \text{where}\ \ i=\arg\min_{j\in\left[1,N\right]}\operatorname{MSE}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{j},\mathbf{y}\right)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

This pseudo-labeling process remains stable because the labels are generated offline and kept fixed during fine-tuning. For each sample, we enumerate all anchors in the finite codebook, generate the corresponding candidate forecasts, and select the anchor whose forecast is closest to the ground truth in terms of MSE. Because the labels are not updated iteratively, our method avoids the label drift commonly observed in self-training. For example, if the forecast under
Lower Peaks
is closer to the ground truth than those under
Increase Trend
and
Keep Unchanged
, then
Lower Peaks
is used as the pseudo-label. In the end, we obtain the ChronoSteer-100K-FT dataset containing 100K aligned synthetic triplets
\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{c}},\mathbf{y}\right)
, where
\mathbf{y}
is the observed future series and the association between
\hat{\mathbf{c}}
and
\mathbf{y}
is automatically established by anchor matching rather than manually annotated from real-world cross-modal pairs.

3.5.2.
Self-Supervised Continuous Fine-Tuning

The fine-tuning process is illustrated in the right side of Figure
1
(c) and Algorithm
4
. For each sample in ChronoSteer-100K-FT, ChronoSteer generates corresponding predictions:

(9)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{y}}=\operatorname{ChronoSteer}\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{c}}\right)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

During fine-tuning, parameter optimization exclusively uses MSE as the loss function.
Although the observed future series
\mathbf{y}
is used as the optimization target, it is not manually annotated but directly obtained from the future segment of the same unlabeled sequence. Following common practice in time series analysis
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, we therefore regard this forecasting objective as self-supervised.

. Detailed statistical characteristics are provided in Table

.

The Energy dataset is constructed using the UK PV generation system. The raw data consists of 317 GSPs, from which seven faulty units are excluded after a data integrity assessment. To ensure spatial representativeness, 100 GSPs are selected through clustering based on geographic coordinates. The PV generation series is recorded at a 30-minute resolution and includes contextual information such as background, city, forecast-only weather (to prevent future information leakage), and date. Time series slices are generated using a 14-day sliding window (7 days of historical data and 7 days for prediction) with a one-day stride. Finally, 100 representative samples are retained by clustering based on multidimensional characteristics, including geospatial information, weather conditions, and holidays. The dataset exhibits substantial diurnal variability, with non-zero values occurring exclusively during daylight hours. Appendix
C.2
provides corresponding text prompts and visual representations.

The Traffic dataset is derived from the highway sensor network in California. Following LargeST
(
Liu et al., 2023c
)
, 8,600 valid nodes are selected from an initial pool of 18,954 sensors. After integrating POIs, spatial clustering is performed based on geographic coordinates and POI information, yielding 100 representative nodes. Hourly traffic flow series is collected and supplemented with contextual information, including background, city, forecast-only weather, and date. Time series slices are generated using a 14-day sliding window (7 days of historical data and 7 days for prediction) with a one-day stride. Subsequently, 100 representative samples are retained through multidimensional clustering, considering factors such as geospatial information, POIs, weather conditions, and holidays. The dataset exhibits a relatively stable distribution. Appendix
C.3
provides corresponding text prompts and visual representations.

The Weather dataset focuses on monitoring PM10 concentrations in major global cities. From a total of 47,868 cities, 771 core cities with populations exceeding one million are selected. With clustering sampling based on geographic coordinates, climate types, and altitude, 100 representative cities are chosen for further analysis. Hourly PM10 concentration series is collected and enriched with contextual information, including background, forecast-only weather, and date. Time series slices are generated using a 14-day sliding window (7 days of historical data and 7 days for prediction) with a one-day stride. Finally, 100 representative samples are selected through clustering, considering multidimensional attributes such as geospatial information, climate, elevation, weather conditions, and holidays. The dataset demonstrates significant non-stationarity. Appendix
C.4
provides corresponding text prompts and visual representations.

5.
Experiment

5.1.
Experimental Setup

5.1.1.
Dataset

The synthetic cross-modal dataset from Section
3
is used to pre-train and fine-tune ChronoSteer, and is split into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio to reduce overfitting and support hyperparameter selection. Once fine-tuning is complete, the weights of ChronoSteer are frozen to enable zero-shot evaluation on benchmark. We evaluate on MTSFBench-300, which is constructed after the evaluated checkpoints are fixed and uses only context available before the prediction window during evaluation. This benchmark encompasses three key domains (Energy, Traffic, and Weather) with different frequencies and incorporates data updated to January 2025. We conduct evaluation experiments on MTSFBench-300, adhering to established zero-shot protocols
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
. Specifically, the prediction length is fixed at 1 day, while historical lengths vary across {2, 3, 4, 7} days. The historical window is maintained larger than the prediction window to ensure sufficient information for zero-shot forecasting. Further details about benchmark are provided in Section
4
.

5.1.2.
Baseline

Apart from ChatTime, the development of multimodal TSFMs capable of zero-shot inference by jointly leveraging textual and temporal information remains unexplored. To more comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of ChronoSteer, we additionally include several baselines capable of zero-shot forecasting. Specifically, we consider Chronos-Bolt
(
Ansari et al., 2024
)
, TimesFM-2.0
(
Das et al., 2024
)
, and Moirai-1.1
(
Woo et al., 2024
)
as baselines for unimodal forecasting, and DeepSeek-R1
(
Team, 2025a
)
, QwQ-32B
(
Team, 2025c
)
, and ChatTime-7B
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
as baselines for multimodal forecasting. Although these LLMs are not specifically designed for time series, other end-to-end methods
(
Nie et al., 2023
;
Jin et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025a
)
are excluded from the comparison due to their lack of zero-shot capabilities. Due to constraints on computing resources, evaluations of the LLMs are conducted via their official APIs, while the remaining baselines are tested on our identical hardware. For DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B, the APIs are used only as inference endpoints for their released open-source or open-weight checkpoints. Details are as follows:

•

DeepSeek-R1: The first large-scale reasoning model, with over 100 billion parameters, specializes in mathematics, programming, and logical reasoning tasks. Released in January 2025, it is post-trained via reinforcement learning based on DeepSeek-V3-Base (released in December 2024).
The reinforcement learning data of DeepSeek-R1 mainly come from mathematical competitions
(
Team, 2025a
)
, with a knowledge cutoff of July 2024.
We use the API provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values (
top_p=1.0, temperature=1.0
).

•

QwQ-32B: A reinforcement learning-based model performs comparable to DeepSeek-R1 with 671B parameters in mathematical and programming tasks, using only 32B parameters. Released in March 2025, it is post-trained via reinforcement learning based on Qwen2.5-32B (released in September 2024).
The reinforcement learning of QwQ-32B focuses on mathematical and coding capabilities
(
Team, 2025c
)
, with a knowledge cutoff of November 2024.
We use the API provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values (
top_k=40, top_p=0.8, temperature=0.7
).

•

ChatTime-7B: A multimodal foundation model tailored for time series analysis, supports dual-modal input and output of series and text. Released in December 2024, it is fine-tuned via LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
based on LLaMA-2-7B (released in July 2023). We use the weight provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values (
num_samples=8, top_k=100, top_p=1.0, temperature=1.0
).

•

Chronos-Bolt: A pretrained time series forecasting model based on the T5 encoder-decoder architecture, trained on nearly 100 billion time series observations. Released in November 2024. We use the weight provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values.

•

TimesFM-2.0: A pretrained time series foundation model developed by Google Research for time series forecasting, achieving up to 25% improvement over v1.0 on leading benchmarks. Released in December 2024. We use the weight provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values.

•

Moirai-1.1: A masked encoder-based universal time series forecasting transformer pretrained on the LOTSA dataset, which demonstrates significant improvements over v1.0 in low-frequency scenarios. Released in June 2024. We use the weight provided on its official website, with all parameters set to default values (
num_samples=100
).

MTSFBench-300 contains target observations from January 2025, all of which were already available within that month. The benchmark was assembled from these data in March 2025. ChatTime-7B and the three TSFM baselines were released before this period, while the reported knowledge cutoffs for DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B both fall in 2024. These temporal boundaries rule out any overlap between the benchmark targets and the training data of the evaluated models. Moreover, each evaluation sample includes only textual context available before the prediction window and does not reveal any future observations.

5.1.3.
Implementation

Given its widespread adoption and standardized architecture, we choose Chronos-Bolt-base
(
Ansari et al., 2024
)
as the unimodal TSFM backbone in our experiments, with an embedding dimension of 768. The text embedding model is set to BGE-M3
(
Chen et al., 2024a
)
, whose output embeddings have a dimensionality of 1024. Textual events are transformed into revision instructions using the LLM QwQ-32B
(
Team, 2025c
)
, with all parameters set to default values (
top_k=40, top_p=0.8, temperature=0.7
). The prompts for generating these textual revision instructions and their corresponding answers are detailed in Appendix
C
. Additionally, ablation studies on the choice of LLM, text embedding model, and unimodal TSFM backbone are provided in Appendix
B
, offering further insights into the impact of each module.

During both the pre-training and fine-tuning stages of ChronoSteer, the Adam optimizer is consistently employed. The model is trained for up to 100 epochs, with early stopping triggered if the validation loss does not decrease over 10 consecutive epochs, mitigating overfitting. In the pre-training stage, which involves contrastive learning tasks with multiple anchor revision instructions, a batch size of 32 is used. During fine-tuning, the batch size is increased to 256. Through grid search, the optimal hyperparameters for ChronoSteer are determined: an initial learning rate of 0.001 (selected from {0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005}), a hidden layer dimension of 1024 in the alignment module (selected from {512, 1024, 2048, 4096}), and a contrastive loss weight of 0.001 during pre-training (selected from {0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1}). Section
5.6
provides a detailed analysis of each hyperparameter.

All experiments are conducted using Python 3.10.13 and PyTorch 2.1.2. The prediction accuracy is evaluated using mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), where lower values indicate better model performance. To ensure the statistical representativeness of our results, all experiments are repeated across ten different random seeds, and we report both the mean and standard deviation. Notably, TimesFM and Chronos exhibit deterministic behavior during inference, yielding a standard deviation of zero. While ChronoSteer inherits this determinism from Chronos, we seek to comprehensively evaluate its robustness. Therefore, unlike the baselines which are evaluated solely in inference mode, we conduct the ChronoSteer experiments by re-initiating the process from the pre-trained stage for each of the ten repetitions. To facilitate open science and community collaboration, we will make all data, source code, and pre-trained weight checkpoints publicly accessible following the release. These resources will offer substantial support for subsequent research.

, both temporal and textual information contribute to enhanced prediction accuracy. In the absence of textual data, the unimodal TSFM, TimesFM, yields the best performance. Even when other baselines incorporate textual context, TimesFM and Chronos remain competitive due to their superior temporal modeling capabilities. Further analysis reveals that for short historical series, multimodal LLMs can partially compensate for limited temporal modeling by leveraging their textual reasoning capabilities, thereby outperforming unimodal TSFMs. However, as the historical series length increases, temporal information becomes sufficient; consequently, multimodal LLMs, which are limited in their ability to perform precise numerical inference, begin to underperform.

To complement the fixed prediction window experiments, we invert the experimental setup to evaluate model performance across varying prediction horizons while fixing the historical window. Specifically, we fix the historical window at 7 days and vary the prediction window across {6 hours, 12 hours, 2 days, 4 days}. The historical window is maintained larger than the prediction window to ensure sufficient information for zero-shot forecasting. Table
3
shows that ChronoSteer maintains its advantage across the tested prediction windows. Moreover, consistent with observations in Table
2
, existing multimodal approaches lose their competitive edge when sufficient historical context is available. This insight motivates our research: by synergizing the textual reasoning strengths of LLMs with the temporal modeling capabilities of TSFMs, we aim to develop a multimodal TSFM that concurrently leverages both modalities for effective zero-shot future inference.

Beyond reporting the mean and standard deviation, we further conduct paired statistical significance tests to examine whether the improvements are consistent at the sample level. For each domain, window setting, and metric, ChronoSteer Multimodal is compared with the strongest non-ChronoSteer baseline in the same row using a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-sample errors, followed by Holm-Bonferroni correction over all 48 primary comparisons. The significance markers in Tables
2
and
3
show that the improvement is significant in 44 of 48 primary comparisons, which provides evidence beyond mean and standard deviation reporting. The few non-significant cases correspond to rows with very small error gaps, while the overall ranking remains consistent with the main conclusion.

To further examine the role of the proposed training strategy, we conduct three additional comparative experiments, summarized in Table
4
. Two LLM-based variants directly modify the initial predictions of the unimodal TSFM by incorporating the historical series and textual information (
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathcal{M}_{\text{text}}\left(\mathbf{x},\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{e}\right)
). The Function variant instead adjusts
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
using the transformation function tied to the anchor most aligned with the textual instruction (
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{c}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{y}}\right)
). Both LLM-based variants benefit from the initial predictions and improve over their direct multimodal counterparts in Table
2
, but they remain less accurate than ChronoSteer. The Function variant performs the worst, indicating that fixed transformation alone are insufficient for temporally consistent revisions.

Furthermore, we evaluate the coverage of the compact codebook and the accuracy of LLM-generated revision instructions to investigate factors limiting system performance. A sample is considered covered if there exists an anchor instruction that reduces the prediction error compared to the unimodal backbone. As shown in the first two columns of Table
6
, the discretized codebook covers more than 95.2% of samples from real-world scenarios (col 1). If the optimal instruction is selected for each sample (col 2), the prediction error can be further reduced by approximately 34.6% relative to the actual results in Table
2
, indicating that the codebook possesses sufficient representational capacity. Section
5.5
provides further analysis regarding these anchors.

Therefore, the primary performance bottleneck lies in the accuracy of revision instructions generated by the LLMs. The last two columns of Table
6
report this accuracy, showing that only 40.9% (col 3) of generated instructions match the optimal anchors. However, matching the exact optimal anchor is not a strict prerequisite for reducing prediction error, as anchor coverage is not exhaustive. Conversely, missing the optimal anchor does not preclude error reduction. Multiple anchors can benefit the same sample rather than following a strict one-to-one correspondence (e.g., both
Increase Trend
and
Raise Troughs
may improve the forecast in some cases). Table
6
(col 4) shows that the LLM identifies a beneficial anchor in 68.1% of scenarios. This rate measures whether the generated instruction reduces prediction error, whereas the 40.9% exact-match rate measures whether it selects the single best anchor. While this success rate is not high, it demonstrates practical value. Section
5.3
details further analysis on instruction generation. These observations once again highlight the inherent limitations of LLMs in temporal reasoning
(
Fons et al., 2024
;
Merrill et al., 2024
)
, which, although beyond the scope of this study, remain an important direction for future research. Specifically, prompt refinement, candidate instruction reranking, and the fine-tuning of specialized small models are potential directions worth exploring. Such approaches could augment domain knowledge for event analysis and deepen the comprehension of time-series features. Accurately understanding the current state appears to be a challenging yet essential prerequisite for generating precise incremental corrections.

To assess the practicality of ChronoSteer in real-world scenarios, we compare it with its unimodal backbone, Chronos, focusing on inference speed and memory consumption. A lower value denotes superior efficiency. The results in Table
6
demonstrate that by integrating a lightweight textual instruction processing branch and employing a training strategy that freezes the backbone network, ChronoSteer inference speed remains nearly identical to that of Chronos, even showing a slight enhancement, while memory usage increases by only 0.9%. In fact, the true efficiency bottleneck for the entire system, regarding both latency and memory, stems from the LLM component responsible for instruction generation. Our existing hardware resources fail to support even local deployment, which necessitates reliance on official API calls as a substitute. Fortunately, the task assigned to the LLM involves generating high-level text instructions rather than directly predicting values for every timestamp. Consequently, under comparable conditions, our method requires significantly fewer tokens than other multimodal foundation models, especially when the prediction window is long. Fine-tuning smaller language models such as SmolLM2-135M
(
Team, 2025d
)
or Gemma-3-270M
(
Team, 2025b
)
presents a viable solution for the future. In conclusion, we fully preserve the unimodal prediction accuracy of Chronos while extending a multimodal interface at a minimal cost. This approach effectively addresses scenarios where users are willing to invest more resources to pursue higher prediction precision.

In the Energy dataset, foggy weather causes a gradual decline in peaks within the historical series. Without textual context, a unimodal TSFM extrapolates this trend and forecasts even lower peaks. Here the instruction is appropriate. The weather forecast indicates
"transitioning to clear skies tomorrow"
, so the LLM generates an instruction to raise the peaks, and ChronoSteer adjusts its prediction upward. After fine-tuning, the adjustment magnitude more closely matches the observed future series.

In the Traffic dataset, persistent heavy fog causes a further reduction in traffic flow peaks. Without textual information, a unimodal TSFM predicts a typical non-working day pattern similar to the previous day. This example shows an imprecise instruction case. The LLM provides the revision instruction
"reduce series uniformly"
, although the event should mainly affect the peaks rather than the entire series. After pre-training, ChronoSteer follows the instruction strictly, resulting in unrealistic troughs. After fine-tuning, it integrates the instruction with historical series and partly mitigates this issue.

In the Weather dataset, the transition from weekdays to non-working days reduces air pollution due to decreased human activity. Without textual information, a unimodal TSFM struggles to capture this pattern and overestimates future values. This example shows that a directionally correct instruction may still need historical context to determine the appropriate magnitude. The revision instruction guides ChronoSteer to lower the prediction. After fine-tuning, ChronoSteer predicts troughs and peaks more accurately by balancing this instruction with historical context.

5.4.
Ablation Study

To validate the effectiveness of each component, we evaluate ChronoSteer and its variants in Table
8
. The results show that modifying any component will reduce the performance, confirming its architectural soundness.

In w/o Contrastive, the contrastive learning loss is omitted during pretraining. This absence hinders the ability of ChronoSteer to effectively distinguish between different anchor instructions, thereby reducing the cross-modal alignment performance. Specifically, removing operation leads to a prediction accuracy drop of 7.7%, 4.7%, and 4.5% on the Energy, Traffic, and Weather datasets, respectively.

In w/o Finetune, the fine-tuning stage is omitted. Since the model has already converged after pretraining, its parameters are directly evaluated. During fine-tuning, observed future series combined with pseudo-labeling techniques serve as supervised targets, allowing the model to recover the fine-grained magnitude information lost during discretization and to mitigate the expressive limitations of the synthetic transformation functions. Without fine-tuning, prediction accuracy drops by 10.4%, 8.2%, and 16.1% on the Energy, Traffic, and Weather datasets, respectively.

In w/o MLP, the MLP in the alignment module is replaced with a linear layer, similar to the design in LLaVA
(
Liu et al., 2023b
)
. This substitution causes a substantial performance drop. Unlike LLaVA, which represents an image with multiple tokens, our model encodes each textual instruction as a single token. This design requires a more expressive nonlinear mapper to achieve effective cross-modal alignment. The decision to use a single-token embedding arises from the scarcity of high-quality cross-modal alignment datasets. Using multiple tokens not only increases computational cost but also tends to produce collapsing solutions without large-scale alignment data, whereas a well-pretrained text embedding model is better suited for distinguishing instruction semantics.

We further investigate the necessity and rationality of the discrete codebook mechanism with the experimental results summarized in Table
9
. Considering the costs associated with LLM API calls, this series utilizes the 10K samples obtained from the clustering in Section
3.4.1
for training rather than the full 100K samples. All other experimental settings remain consistent with Section
5.1.3
.

In the first set of experiments, we examine the effect of the anchor mechanism. We feed the unimodal predictions
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
generated by the TSFM and the corresponding ground truth labels
\mathbf{y}
into the LLM to generate revision instructions. We utilize these natural text instructions for training without applying discretization. The results indicate that neither embedding natural text into multiple control tokens via GPT-2
(
Radford et al., 2019
)
nor into a single control token via BGE-M3
(
Chen et al., 2024a
)
yields significant benefits. Previous studies indicate that open-ended text instructions often suffer from semantic ambiguity and a low signal-to-noise ratio
(
Xiong et al., 2025
)
, and this characteristic imposes strict requirements on both the quality and scale of alignment data. Direct embedding into multiple tokens hinders model convergence and even leads to negative performance gains. Although embedding models reduce noise by summarizing instructions into a single token, directly synthesizing large-scale cross-modal alignment datasets with LLMs remains expensive, inefficient, and unreliable. In contrast, our discrete codebook mechanism enables the training of multimodal models within the current context of scarce high-quality data.

In the second set of experiments, we conduct ablation studies on the four types of anchors within the codebook. Experimental results demonstrate that the codebook coverage rate improves as anchors are progressively added and eventually reaches 94.0%. The nine anchors reflect a practical balance among coverage, interpretability, and the feasibility of designing reliable transformation functions. The nine anchors are intended to cover common directional revision behaviors under limited paired supervision, rather than to exhaust all possible event effects in open-world scenarios. The ablation results in Table
9
show that adding anchor groups increases coverage to 94.0%, which supports the current design within the tested benchmark setting. Specifically, anchors controlling trend and amplitude yield greater performance improvements compared to those controlling peaks and troughs. Peak and trough anchors still improve coverage by handling local changes that cannot be represented by global trend or amplitude controls. At the same time, compound, delayed, nonlinear, continuous, or domain-specific effects may require combinations of anchors, richer instructions, or new transformation functions. Simply enlarging the codebook is not sufficient, because each new anchor must be paired with a reliable transformation function to provide controllable training targets. Future research directions include designing more refined anchors with corresponding transformation functions, exploring anchor composition or continuous instruction embeddings, or fine-tuning smaller LLMs
(
Team, 2025d
;
Team, 2025b
)
to generate accurate revision instructions more efficiently.

Overall, although the discrete codebook mechanism and synthetic cross-modal associations limit the generalization of ChronoSteer to some extent, they form a necessary compromise given the current scarcity of high-quality alignment data. Furthermore, the experimental results indicate that these core anchors cover most benchmark cases and yield useful predictive gains under the tested setting.

5.6.
Hyperparameter Analysis

To assess the sensitivity of ChronoSteer to hyperparameter selections, we evaluate its prediction performance under various configurations. The historical length is set to 4 days and the prediction length to 1 day. The key hyperparameters examined include the learning rate, the contrastive loss weight during pre-training, and the hidden layer dimension of MLP in alignment module. To further support the design of the synthetic cross-modal supervision pipeline, we also provide a sensitivity analysis on the parameter ranges of the synthetic transformation functions in Appendix
A
.

The contrastive loss weight during the pre-training stage directly affects the ability of ChronoSteer to distinguish anchor revision instructions. A lower weight shifts optimization focus toward MSE loss, reducing discriminative power. Conversely, a higher weight leads to over-reliance on contrastive loss, potentially hindering the capture of fine-grained temporal characteristics. As shown in Figure
3
(a), increasing the contrastive loss weight initially boosts prediction accuracy on the Energy and Traffic datasets before declining, consistent with theoretical expectations. Notably, the unstable Weather dataset shows robustness to this parameter, achieving peak performance at a weight of 0.1.

The learning rate is a key parameter affecting model training. A small learning rate may cause slow convergence and risk trapping the model in local optima, while a large learning rate, though speeding up convergence, can lead to instability or divergence. As shown in Figure
3
(b), ChronoSteer consistently improves prediction accuracy on the Traffic and Weather datasets as the learning rate increases, revealing a clear pattern. However, due to the high proportion of zero-value samples in the Energy dataset, it shows greater sensitivity to the learning rate and achieves optimal performance at a learning rate of 0.001.

The dimension of the hidden vector influences the learning capacity and representational power. A smaller dimension reduces computational overhead but may hinder the capture of complex characteristics. Conversely, a larger hidden vector improves expressiveness and generalization. However, this often comes at the cost of higher computation and an elevated risk of overfitting, particularly when training data is limited or of poor quality. As shown in Figure
3
(c), prediction accuracy across all datasets initially increases and then decreases as the hidden dimension grows, consistent with theory. Optimal performance is typically achieved when it falls between 1024 and 2048.

6.
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we propose ChronoSteer, a agentic multimodal forecasting framework that integrates the complementary strengths of TSFMs and LLMs for zero-shot future inference using both temporal signals and textual event descriptions. ChronoSteer adopts a decoupled design, in which a pretrained LLM translates textual events into revision instructions that steer the predictions of a frozen TSFM, thereby bridging the semantic gap between text and time series while effectively leveraging pretrained knowledge. To address the scarcity of paired alignment data, we develop a two-stage training pipeline built on a synthetic cross-modal dataset. It first performs coarse anchor-based alignment and then refines fine-grained magnitude details. We also introduce MTSFBench-300, a leakage-controlled benchmark to support more transparent evaluation of multimodal forecasting models.

Despite these encouraging results, ChronoSteer still has limitations on both sides of the pipeline, namely event-to-instruction translation and instruction-to-series forecasting. On the LLM side, when textual events are ambiguous, underspecified, or only weakly correlated with the target series, the resulting instructions may be insufficiently informative or even misleading, thereby limiting the gains over the unimodal TSFM. Improving the validity and reliability of generated instructions therefore remains an important direction. Prompt refinement, candidate instruction reranking, specialized small models, and recent advances in agent-based reasoning
(
Yan et al., 2026
)
may serve as useful complements for event understanding and instruction generation.

On the forecasting side, the current codebook provides a practical low-resource interface between textual events and temporal revisions, but its discrete design cannot fully represent complex real-world event dynamics. Discretization still constrains open-domain generalization, and the current synthetic functions cannot fully describe nonlinear, delayed, continuous, compound, or domain-specific event effects. The two-stage training strategy recovers fine-grained magnitude information from historical context, but more flexible instruction-based forecasting will likely require higher-quality paired training data, whether synthetic or real.

For evaluation coverage, MTSFBench-300 provides carefully curated samples across three representative domains, but its scale remains limited. Experiments with varying historical and prediction windows, repeated runs, and paired significance tests improve the statistical reliability of the evaluation, while broader leakage-controlled benchmarks are still needed to support stronger external validity.

ChronoSteer can also be extended beyond the current univariate setting. Most existing TSFMs are univariate, and methods such as AdaPTS
(
Benechehab et al., 2025
)
adapt them to multivariate forecasting through lightweight adapters, suggesting a similar route for ChronoSteer in future work. For richer multimodal inputs, external signals such as structured metadata, calendar information, and visual observations can first be converted into revision instructions. A more flexible instruction space may further support such inputs once higher-quality paired resources become available.

Overall, ChronoSteer offers a useful foundation for MTSF at the current stage of the field. Further progress will likely depend on higher-quality paired training data and more rigorous evaluation benchmarks.

By applying cluster sampling and anchor extraction to the Monash Repository, we obtain 100K high-quality time series slices and identify nine core anchors that represent discrete prototypes of temporal adjustment behaviors. For each anchor, we design a corresponding transformation function, as detailed in Algorithm
5
, where the specific perturbation distributions are defined within the default parameters. These functions are subsequently applied to the 100K slices to yield the ChronoSteer-100K-PT dataset, comprising 900K precisely aligned synthetic triplets
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right)
.

. Starting from the default perturbation ranges in Algorithm

, we introduce a global scaling factor

,

, and

The results in Table

\beta

\beta

Overall, the default parameter ranges provide the stable trade-off between controllability and realism.

B.1.
Ablation Study on Large Language Model

In the primary experiment, the LLM QwQ-32B is employed to transform textual events into revision instructions. To assess how different LLMs affect the prediction accuracy of the system, this section replaces QwQ-32B with alternative models for comparison. Specifically, four models are tested: standard LLM Qwen2.5-32B, reasoning-enhanced LLM QwQ-32B, standard vLLM Qwen2.5-32B-VL, and reasoning-enhanced vLLM QVQ-72B-Preview.

As shown in Table
12
, the reasoning-enhanced LLM QwQ-32B demonstrates significantly higher instruction transformation accuracy than the standard LLM Qwen2.5-32B, particularly on unstable datasets. Improvements in prediction accuracy reach 2.1%, 3.5%, and 18.5% on the Energy, Traffic, and Weather datasets, respectively.

Previous research indicates that LLMs struggle to comprehend temporal characteristics effectively. Representing time series as images and leveraging vLLMs can better capture complex temporal patterns. Based on this, we introduce the standard vLLM Qwen2.5-32B-VL and the reasoning-enhanced vLLM QVQ-72B-Preview for comparative experiments. Results show that the standard vLLM Qwen2.5-32B-VL achieves competitive performance, closely matching the reasoning-enhanced LLM QwQ-32B. For instance, on the Energy dataset, its prediction accuracy outperforms QwQ-32B by 2.7%, while on the Traffic dataset, it trails by only 3.5%. However, the accuracy of Qwen2.5-32B-VL on the unstable Weather dataset is 17.8% lower than QwQ-32B. This may be due to cross-modal fine-tuning reducing textual reasoning capability. Despite this, it still outperforms the standard LLM Qwen2.5-32B by 3.5%. Surprisingly, the reasoning-enhanced vLLM QVQ-72B-Preview performs the worst across all datasets. This underperformance may stem from the preview version being in an optimization phase, where insufficient stability leads to suboptimal results.

B.2.
Ablation Study on Embedding Model

In the primary experiment, the text embedding model BGE-M3 is employed to transform textual revision instructions into high-dimension embedding vectors. To examine the impact of different text embedding models on system prediction accuracy, BGE-M3 is replaced with several widely used alternatives. Specifically, four models are selected: all-MiniLM-L6-v2, Voyage-3-lite, BGE-M3, and NV-Embed-v2. Based on the MTEB Leaderboard, these models show a progressive increase in representational capability.

As shown in Table
13
, prediction accuracy improves incrementally with enhanced embedding capability. However, despite NV-Embed-v2 having nearly ten times the parameters of BGE-M3, its performance does not significantly surpass that of BGE-M3. This may be due to the high dimension of NV-Embed-v2, which tends to semantic dispersion. Given its larger embedding size, the current alignment dataset may be insufficient to effectively train an alignment module of this scale, limiting the potential of the model.

In the primary experiment, the unimodal TSFM Chronos-Bolt-base serves as the backbone for ChronoSteer. To explore how varying scales of unimodal TSFM affect prediction accuracy, Chronos-Bolt-base is replaced with several other models differing in parameter size. Specifically, four models are tested: Chronos-Bolt-tiny, Chronos-Bolt-mini, Chronos-Bolt-small, and Chronos-Bolt-base, with parameter counts increasing progressively.

The experiment results in Table
14
show that prediction accuracy does not clearly improve with an increase in the parameter of backbone, for either unimodal or multimodal tasks. However, our method demonstrates strong adaptability across backbones with different scales, consistently boosting multimodal prediction accuracy relative to unimodal results in all cases. Specifically, applying the proposed method improves prediction accuracy for Chronos-Bolt-tiny, Chronos-Bolt-mini, Chronos-Bolt-small, and Chronos-Bolt-base by 19.8%, 23.1%, 22.4%, and 25.8%, respectively.

Furthermore, to validate the generalization of ChronoSteer across different architectures, we evaluate its performance on various families of unimodal TSFMs. The results are summarized in Table
15
. Consistent with the findings on model scales, our method yields uniform improvements, boosting the performance of timesfm-1.0-200m, timesfm-2.0-500m, moirai-1.0-large, and moirai-1.1-large by 18.2%, 19.1%, 26.6%, and 28.9%, respectively.
