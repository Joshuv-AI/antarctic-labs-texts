ABHD11-Mediated mtDNA Transcription Restored Mitochondrial Function to Attenuate Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis After Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Yu Liu, Yijin Chen, Hao Zheng, Yingxuan Li, Chuling Li, Wenlong Xu, Wei Chen, Zhiwen Xiao, Guojun Chen, Senlin Huang, Xinzhong Li, Jia-Guo Zhou, Ying Wu, Wangjun Liao, Yulin Liao, Jianping Bin, Yanmei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Cardiomyocytes exhibit marked susceptibility to ferroptosis after myocardial infarction (MI), rendering ferroptosis inhibition a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate ischemic myocardial injury. Although mitochondrial dysfunction is recognized as a core driver of ferroptosis, the potential role of mitochondrial DNA transcription in regulating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis remains unexplored.METHODS:To clarify the temporal role of the various modes of cell death in MI progression, we performed time-course echocardiography in MI models treated with various cell death inhibitors. To characterize the crucial process and molecular regulator in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, we integrated RNA sequencing and single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from murine post-MI hearts and performed functional rescue experiments using mitochondrial protective agents. To determine the role of ABHD11 (αβ-hydrolase domain-containing protein 11) in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and cardiac repair after MI, we used loss- and gain-of-function approaches. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we conducted transcriptomics, nontargeted lipidomics, site-specific mutagenesis, molecular docking, coimmunoprecipitation, native gel electrophoresis, proximity ligation assay, methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction, and chromatin immunoprecipitation assay.RESULTS:We found that cardiac ferroptosis peaked at day 7 after MI and was enriched in peri-infarct cardiomyocytes. Mitochondrial dysfunction was a key driver of cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI, and the lipid enzyme ABHD11 was identified as a potential regulator of both processes. ABHD11 expression was consistently reduced in mouse and human MI hearts, and its transcription was repressed by DNMT1 (DNA methyltransferase 1)–mediated promoter hypermethylation. Functionally, cardiac-specific overexpression of ABHD11 markedly alleviated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and improved cardiac function after MI. Conversely, loss of ABHD11 in adult mice exacerbated pathological cardiac remodeling and heart failure. Mechanistically, independent of its canonical enzymatic activities, ABHD11 acted as a mitochondrial DNA transcription coactivator by enhancing the TEFM (mitochondrial transcription elongation factor)–POLRMT (mitochondrial RNA polymerase) interaction. This promoted mitochondrial DNA transcription, restored mitochondrial function, and reduced reactive oxygen species/PUFA-PLs (polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing glycerophospholipids)–driven lipid peroxidation and 4-hydroxynonenal generation. The reduction in 4-hydroxynonenal stabilized YY1 (Yin Yang 1), which subsequently regulated key ferroptosis-driving genes governing iron deposition, reactive oxygen species production, and polyunsaturated fatty acid lipids accumulation, further inhibiting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, and ultimately promoting cardiac recovery after MI.CONCLUSIONS:This study revealed that ABHD11-mediated mitochondrial DNA transcription attenuated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI by orchestrating a mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for ischemic myocardial injury.

Acute myocardial infarction (MI) remains one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide because of sudden coronary artery occlusion.1 Although timely revascularization can salvage ischemic myocardium, a substantial number of patients experience persistent cardiomyocyte ischemia because of delayed intervention or incomplete microvascular reperfusion, resulting in cardiomyocyte death and eventual heart failure.2 Suppressing cardiomyocyte death is therefore essential for preventing adverse cardiac remodeling and heart failure progression.3 Ferroptosis, a recently identified iron-dependent cell death mechanism driven by uncontrolled lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a critical contributor to ischemic cardiomyocyte death.4,5 The cardiomyocytes are highly susceptible to ferroptosis because of their high abundance of PUFA-PLs (polyunsaturated fatty acid–containing glycerophospholipids), which serve as preferential substrates for peroxidation and ferroptosis execution.6,7 Moreover, ferroptosis exhibits a unique “wave-like” propagation among neighboring cells, amplifying cardiomyocyte loss and expanding infarct areas.8,9 A better understanding of the mechanisms underlying cardiomyocyte ferroptosis is needed to develop new intervention strategies for reducing ischemic myocardial damage.

Ferroptosis is a complex process orchestrated by multiple organelles, including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, lysosomes, and the nucleus.10 Mitochondria, central to cardiomyocyte lipid metabolism and redox homeostasis, have emerged as pivotal regulators of ferroptosis.11 Mitochondrial dysfunction promotes ferroptosis by perturbing iron/lipid homeostasis, oxidative phosphorylation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) dynamics, whereas preserving mitochondrial integrity effectively resists ferroptosis across diverse cell types.12 As semiautonomous organelles, mitochondria are dually regulated by nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).13,14 Although the roles of nuclear-encoded factors in regulating mitochondrial function and ferroptosis have been well established,5,15 emerging evidence indicates that mtDNA processes also participate in ferroptosis. For instance, impaired mtDNA synthesis enhances ferroptotic susceptibility, and mtDNA damage directly triggers ferroptosis.16 Crucially, the accurate expression of mtDNA largely depends on the regulation of mtDNA transcription governed by POLRMT (mitochondrial RNA polymerase), transcription factors (TFAM [mitochondrial transcription factor A ] and TFB2M [mitochondrial transcription factor B2]), elongation factor (TEFM [mitochondrial transcription elongation factor]), and the mitochondrial transcription termination factor (MTERF) family (MTERF1-4).17 Dysregulation of these transcriptional components disrupts mtDNA-encoded gene expression, thereby inducing metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction.18 This intriguing connection between mtDNA transcription, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid metabolism led us to hypothesize that mtDNA transcription processes influence cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

In this study, we unveiled the previously unrecognized role of mtDNA transcription in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and identified mitochondrial lipid enzyme ABHD11 (αβ-hydrolase domain-containing 11) as a crucial mtDNA transcription regulator. ABHD11, a mitochondria-localized αβ-hydrolase domain–containing enzyme, has been reported to regulate lipid metabolism with its dual catalytic activities.19 Intriguingly, this study revealed that ABHD11 acted as a new mtDNA transcription coactivator to reduce ROS/PUFA-PLs–driven lipid peroxidation by enhancing the interaction of TEFM and POLRMT in mitochondria, which was independent of its reported catalytic activities. Furthermore, we identified a novel mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk mediated by the ABHD11/4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)/YY1 (Yin Yang 1) axis. By reducing 4-HNE accumulation, ABHD11 stabilized the transcription factor YY1, which subsequently regulated ferroptosis-related genes and reinforced mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby inhibiting cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. Overall, our findings established ABHD11 as a key regulator of mtDNA transcription and a promising therapeutic target for mitigating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and improving cardiac function after MI.

Methods

All supporting data from this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We deposited the RNA sequencing data to National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus with the accession number GSE324137. The lipidomics data were deposited to Metabolomics Workbench with the accession numbers ST004694 and ST004695. Detailed descriptions of all experimental procedures and statistical analyses are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Animals

All experimental procedures complied with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University.

Myocardial CRISPR-Associated Protein 9 Knock-In Transgenic Mouse Model

A myocardial CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) knock-in transgenic mouse model was established as described in our previous studies.20,21 The Cre-dependent Cas9 knock-in mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Cardiac-specific Cas9 mice were generated by crossing homozygous Rosa26-LSL-Cas9-tdTomato mice with α-MHC-Cre transgenic mice. To achieve cardiomyocyte-specific gene targeting, adeno-associated virus 9–single guide RNA targeting (sg) ABHD11, adeno-associated virus 9–sgTEFM, adeno-associated virus 9–sgPOLRMT, and adeno-associated virus 9–sgYY1 were synthesized and injected into the myocardium.

Human Cardiac Tissue Collection

Human cardiac tissue specimens were collected from patients undergoing heart transplantation or surgical ventricular septal repair. Samples were obtained from discarded or leftover tissue blocks. Ischemic cardiomyopathy tissues were collected from patients with end-stage ischemic heart failure, whereas control tissues were obtained from donor hearts without ischemic cardiac conditions. All tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C. Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, and all patients provided informed consent.

Recombinant Adenovirus Construction

Recombinant adenoviruses carrying mouse wild-type ABHD11 (Adv-ABHD11 WT), mutated ABHD11(ABHD11 S133A, ABHD11 H288A, ABHD11 MUT), PDK2, MRPS34, PPA2, MRPL44, ABHD11+MAPK9, ABHD11+NFIL3, wild-type YY1 (YY1 WT), mutated YY1 (K178R, K179R, K182R, K288R), and a control adenovirus (Adv-NC) were constructed and transduced into cardiomyocytes at a multiplicity of infection of 20.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS 19.0 (IBM), and all data are expressed as mean±SEM. Normality tests were conducted for all continuous variables. Unpaired Student t test was used to determine the significance of differences between 2 groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni multiple comparisons test, 2-way ANOVA followed by Šidák test, or Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn test was used for analyzing multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Results

Cardiac Ferroptosis Peaks at Day 7 After MI and Is Enriched in Peri-infarct Cardiomyocytes

To determine the contributions of different types of cell death to MI, we established MI models in adult mice by permanent left anterior descending coronary artery ligation, ensuring minimal technical variation across groups, and subsequently treated with various cell death inhibitors (Figure S1A and S1B). Time-course echocardiography showed that inhibition of necroptosis (Nec-1 [Necrostatin-1]) or apoptosis (Emricasan) attenuated cardiac dysfunction as early as 3 days after MI, whereas inhibition of ferroptosis (Fer-1 [ferrostatin-1]) improved cardiac function from day 7 onward (Figure S1C and S1D; Figure 1A). In addition, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from post-MI hearts (GSE114695) at days 1, 7, and 56 after MI revealed that apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis were all activated by day 1 but showed distinct dynamics, with apoptosis/necroptosis peaking at day 1 and ferroptosis peaking at day 7 (Figure S2A and S2B; Figure 1B). These findings were confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction and immunohistochemistry, both showing that apoptosis and necroptosis peaked at days 1 to 3 and ferroptosis at day 7 (Figure S2C through S2I; Figure 1C and 1D). Temporal profiling of cardiac ferroptosis using Prussian blue staining demonstrated that iron deposition peaked at 7 days after MI (Figure 1E). This peak in ferroptotic activity was further confirmed by the concomitant suppression of the GPX4 (glutathione peroxidase 4) pathway, evidenced by reduced GPX4 protein levels (Figure 1F) and enzymatic activity (Figure 1G), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and a decreased GSH/oxidized glutathione ratio (Figure 1H through 1J). Consistently, the level of malondialdehyde (MDA) was also significantly elevated at this 7-day time point (Figure 1K). Hence, these findings suggested that cardiac ferroptosis peaked at 7 days after MI.

To determine the spatial and cellular localization of ferroptosis, we performed coimmunostaining for 4-HNE and cell-specific markers on heart tissues at 7 days after MI. Our results revealed a distinct spatial and cellular distribution of ferroptosis (Figure 1L and 1M), which was most elevated in peri-infarct cardiomyocytes and significantly increased in macrophages within the infarct core (Figure 1N and 1O). These findings demonstrated that cardiomyocyte ferroptosis occurs primarily in the peri-infarct zone, highlighting the therapeutic importance of targeting this process at 7 days after MI to mitigate cardiac injury and promote functional recovery.

Mitochondrial Dysfunction Is a Key Driver of Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis After MI With ABHD11 as a Potential Regulator

To identify critical biological processes involved in MI-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, we performed time-series cluster analysis and classified genes into 7 clusters according to their expression patterns during MI progression (Figure S3A and S3B). Among the 7 clusters, genes in cluster 3 or 6 exhibited expression trends either similar to or opposite the cardiac ferroptosis pattern after MI (Figure S3A and S3B). To identify potential ferroptosis inhibitors, we focused on cluster 6, which had the highest ratio of ferroptosis suppressors among negatively correlated clusters (Figure 2A; Figure S3C). Differential gene expression analysis of single-nucleus RNA-seq data from sham and 7 days post-MI heart tissues (GSE176092) revealed that most genes in cluster 6 were highly expressed in cardiomyocytes (Figure S2D through S2G). Biological analysis showed that cluster 6 contained the highest proportion of mitochondria-localized genes (Figure 2B; Figure S3H), which were enriched in fatty acid catabolic processes, mitochondrial gene expression, mitochondrial organization, oxidation-reduction processes, and related pathways (Figure 2C; Figure S3I). We further investigated whether mitochondrial dysfunction causally contributes to cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. Inducing mitochondrial dysfunction in neonatal cardiomyocytes, either through treatment with the mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone or through genetic knockdown of the mitochondrial complex I subunit Ndufs1 (NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S1), led to increased peroxidized lipids and MDA levels (Figure S4A through S4H). Moreover, administration of mitochondrial antioxidant Mitoquinone (MitoQ) or protective peptide Elamipretide (SS-31) after MI markedly improved mitochondrial structure, enhanced energy output (ATP), and subsequently suppressed lipid peroxidation and improved cardiac function (Figure 2D through 2H; Figure S4I). These findings indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key driver of cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI.

Next, we performed an integrated analysis of RNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq data to identify mitochondrial-associated genes potentially regulating mitochondrial function and cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI (Figure S5A). Venn diagram analysis showed that 104 mitochondrial-localized genes in cluster 6 were significantly downregulated in cardiomyocytes at 7 days after MI (Figure 2I). The Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were mainly involved in mitochondrial protein–containing complex, mitochondrial gene expression, respiratory chain complex, fatty acid catabolic process, and so on (Figure 2J). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis indicated that these genes were involved in metabolic pathways, oxidative phosphorylation, cardiac muscle contraction, and so on (Figure S5B). We identified mitochondrial-localized hub genes exhibiting high correlation with ferroptosis suppressors, which were then selected for experimental verification (Figure 2K and 2L). Among these, ABHD11 was identified as the top candidate, characterized by high predicted mitochondrial specificity through using the ProteinSubcellular Localization Prediction Tool (Figure 2L), a heart-enriched expression pattern that was markedly downregulated after MI (Figure S5C through S5E; Figure 2M and 2N), and superior efficacy in suppressing ferroptosis in functional validation (Figure S5G through S5L). Further validation using human samples confirmed that ABHD11 expression was significantly reduced in left ventricular tissues of patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy (Figure 2O). Moreover, ABHD11 was highly expressed in isolated mouse cardiomyocytes (Figure S5F) and predominantly localized to cardiomyocyte mitochondria (Figure 2P and 2Q). Together, these results indicate that ABHD11 may act as a critical regulator of mitochondrial function and MI-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

ABHD11 Overexpression Attenuates Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis and Promotes Functional Recovery After MI

To evaluate the role of ABHD11 in cardiac ferroptosis after MI, cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11-overexpressing mice were subjected to left anterior descending coronary artery ligation to establish MI models (Figure S6A through S6E). Ischemia-induced myocardial damage led to a substantial increase in iron deposition in adult mouse MI models, which was significantly counteracted by ABHD11 overexpression (Figure 3A and 3B). Consistently, cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 overexpression after MI upregulated the protein levels and activity of GPX4, along with the GSH concentration and GSH/oxidized glutathione ratio (Figure 3C through 3E; Figure S7A and S7B). Moreover, cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 overexpression markedly attenuated lipid peroxidation in MI mice, as evidenced by reduced 4-HNE and MDA levels (Figure 3F through 3H). This antiferroptotic effect was accompanied by reduced cardiomyocyte death (Figure S7C), preserved systolic function (Figure 3I and 3J), and limited infarct size and fibrosis (Figure 3K and 3L), indicating its cardioprotective capacity against post-MI ferroptosis. The cardioprotection conferred by ABHD11 overexpression in MI mice was partially reversed by cotreatment with the ferroptosis Inducer RAS-selective lethal small molecule 3 (RSL3) (Figure 3F through 3L), in which we validated its cardiac distribution (Figure S7D through S7F) and efficacy in inducing ferroptosis in vivo (Figure 3F through 3L). These results establish that ABHD11 confers cardioprotection primarily by suppressing ferroptosis.

We next overexpressed ABHD11 in cardiomyocytes under hypoxia conditions to further explore whether ABHD11 participates in hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis (Figure S8A and S8B). ABHD11 overexpression enhanced cardiomyocyte viability (Figure S8C), reduced iron levels (Figure S8D and S8E), increased the concentration of GSH and ratio of GSH/oxidized glutathione (Figure S8F through S8H), and decreased lipid peroxidation (Figure S8I and S8J) in hypoxia-treated cardiomyocytes, all of which were partially blocked by RSL3. Taken together, the above results indicated that ABHD11 improved cardiac function after MI through attenuating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

ABHD11 Depletion Aggravates Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis and Impairs Cardiac Repair After MI

To investigate whether ABHD11 depletion could affect cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and cardiac repair after MI, we used loss-of-function approaches both in vitro and in vivo. ABHD11 deficiency aggravated hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte death in vitro (Figure 4A; Figure S9A and S9B). Furthermore, ABHD11 deficiency increased ferroptosis susceptibility in cardiomyocytes under hypoxic conditions, as evidenced by elevated hypoxia-induced iron accumulation (Figure 4B; Figure S9C and S9D), reduced GSH concentration and GSH/oxidized glutathione ratio (Figure S9E through S9G), and increased lipid peroxidation and MDA content (Figure 4C and 4D). It is important to note that all of these effects were reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Figure 4A through 4D; Figure S9D through S9G). To investigate the loss-of-function role of ABHD11 in vivo, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 knockout mice using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9 (Figure 4E and 4F) and confirmed the specificity of ABHD11 deletion in adult cardiomyocytes (Figure S10A through S10H). These mice exhibited normal baseline cardiac morphology and function (Figure S10I and S10J). After MI, cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 depletion aggravated lipid peroxidation, increased the percentage of cardiomyocyte death, reduced viable myocardium proportion, and impaired cardiac functional recovery in MI models, all of which could be rescued by Fer-1 (Figure 4G through 4K). Together, ABHD11 depletion could aggravate cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and impair cardiac repair after MI.

Given that ferroptosis is a proinflammatory cell death process characterized by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns and 4-HNE,22 we further explored the role of ABHD11 in regulating this inflammatory response. Cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 knockout exacerbated post-MI injury, manifested as increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL [interleukin]–6, IL-1β, TNF-α [tumor necrosis factor-α]), aggravated inflammatory cell infiltration, elevated 4-HNE levels, and consequently, impaired cardiac function (Figure S11A through S11F). These adverse effects were partially attenuated by Alda-1, which scavenges 4-HNE by specifically activating ALDH2 (acetaldehyde dehydrogenase 2) (Figure S11A through S11F). Thus, ABHD11 promotes functional recovery after MI by partially suppressing the 4-HNE–triggered inflammatory cascade.

ABHD11 Attenuates Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis Through a Noncanonical Role in Enhancing mtDNA Transcription

ABHD11 contains a typical α/β hydrolase fold with 2 catalytic motifs: the nucleophilic GHSMG motif responsible for hydrolase activity and the histidine HWVHSD motif mediating acyltransferase activity (Figure 5A through 5C). To investigate whether ABHD11 regulates cardiomyocyte ferroptosis through its canonical catalytic function, we generated S133A and H288A catalytic mutants and confirmed their loss of enzymatic activity (Figure S12A through S12D). Both catalytic mutants barely affected the protective effects of ABHD11 on cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, as reflected by cardiomyocyte viability, iron levels, oxidized lipids, and MDA levels (Figure S12E through S12H; Figure 5D). In cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 knockout mice, overexpression of the S133A or H288A catalytic mutants of ABHD11 impaired enzymatic function (Figure S13A and S13B). Nevertheless, these mutants retained the ability to suppress lipid peroxidation and improve cardiac function (Figure 5E through 5G). These findings suggested that ABHD11 attenuated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis independent of its canonical catalytic function.

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying ABHD11-mediated cardioprotection against ferroptosis, we conducted RNA-seq on cardiomyocytes subjected to hypoxia conditions with or without ABHD11 overexpression. RNA-seq analysis identified 2573 upregulated genes and 2092 downregulated genes, which were associated with processes such as the negative regulation of programmed cell death, cellular lipid metabolism, mitochondrial organization, and ROS metabolism (Figure S14A and S14B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis revealed that ABHD11 enhanced the expression of genes related to the respiratory chain complex, fatty acid β-oxidation, and mitochondrial gene expression while inhibiting oxidative stress, superoxide anion generation, and ferroptosis (Figure 5H). Heatmap analysis revealed that ABHD11 overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of genes involved in the mitochondrial respiratory chain (electron transport chain) complex (Figure 5I). It is interesting that we found that ABHD11 upregulated most of mtDNA-encoded mRNAs (Figure S14C and S14D; Figure 5I), establishing ABHD11 as a potential mtDNA transcription regulator. Overexpression of ABHD11 increased the mRNA and protein levels of mtDNA-encoded genes in hypoxic cardiomyocytes (Figure 5J and 5K). In cardiomyocyte-specific ABHD11 knockout mice, overexpression of ABHD11 increased mtDNA-encoded genes, and this upregulation was not impaired by the S133A or H288A catalytic mutants (Figure S14E). The increased expression of mtDNA-encoded genes by ABHD11 was partially reversed by mtDNA transcription inhibitor IMT1B23 (Figure S15A and S15B). We then explored whether ABHD11 restored hypoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that ABHD11 mitigated mitochondrial ultrastructural damage in cardiomyocytes both in vitro and in vivo (Figure 5L and 5M). JC-1 immunostaining and ATP detection further showed that ABHD11 improved mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and energy production in cardiomyocytes (Figure 5N and 5O). We further investigated whether ABHD11 inhibits ferroptosis by enhancing mtDNA transcription. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that mtDNA transcription inhibitor IMT1B significantly aggravated lipid peroxidation and cell death in hypoxic cardiomyocytes and adult MI models, effects that were partially rescued by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (Figure S15C through S15F; Figure 5P). Crucially, the protective effects of ABHD11 were partially reversed by IMT1B and further rescued by Fer-1 (Figure S15C through S15F; Figure 5P). A consistent trend was observed in a classical RSL3-induced ferroptosis model (Figure S16A through S16E), collectively indicating that ABHD11 attenuated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis primarily through enhancing mtDNA transcription.

ABHD11 Promotes mtDNA Transcription to Restore Mitochondrial Function by Enhancing the TEFM-POLRMT Interaction

The mtDNA transcription is orchestrated by the RNA polymerase POLRMT in coordination with accessory transcription factors (Figure 6A). Molecular docking analysis predicted interactions between ABHD11 and several mitochondrial transcription factors, including POLRMT, TEFM, TFAM, and MTERF3 (Figure 6B). Coimmunoprecipitation assays in cardiomyocytes confirmed a specific interaction between ABHD11 and TEFM, TFAM, and POLRMT, but not with MTERF3 (Figure 6C and 6D; Figure S17A and S17B). In addition, molecular docking analysis suggested that ABHD11, TEFM, and POLRMT have the potential to form a ternary complex (Figure 6E). Sub-mitochondrial localization assays revealed the co-localization of ABHD11, TEFM, and POLRMT within the mitochondrial matrix (Figure 6F), providing evidence that they form a tripartite complex. Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) of heart tissues from sham and MI mouse models demonstrated that ABHD11, TEFM, and POLRMT interacted as a complex, and this endogenous binding of the ABHD11-TEFM-POLRMT complex in heart was weakened after MI (Figure 6G; Figure S17C through S17E). It is important to note that immunofluorescence co-localization and proximity ligation assay directly demonstrated the interaction between ABHD11 and its partners TEFM and POLRMT in human left ventricular tissues (Figure 6H and 6I). Consistent with this, human ischemic cardiomyopathy tissues exhibited reduced ABHD11 levels, a weakened TEFM-POLRMT interaction, and increased lipid peroxidation (Figure 6J; Figure S17F through S17H). Collectively, these results indicated that ABHD11 acts as a mtDNA transcription coactivator by enhancing the TEFM-POLRMT interaction.

Coimmunoprecipitation analysis showed that ABHD11 overexpression robustly strengthened the TEFM-POLRMT interaction (Figure 6K). In contrast, ABHD11 binding-sites mutation severely impaired this interaction (Figure 6K; Figure S17I and S17J). Rescue experiments showed that TEFM or POLRMT deficiency and ABHD11 binding-sites mutation abolished the upregulation of most mtDNA-encoded genes, the alleviation of mitochondrial damage, and the reduction of lipid peroxidation induced by ABHD11 overexpression (Figure 6L through 6N). Furthermore, the mitochondrial structure and attenuated lipid peroxidation in MI models with ABHD11-MUT overexpression were improved after Fer-1 treatment (Figure 6M and 6N). Taken together, these results indicated that ABHD11 strengthened the TEFM-POLRMT interaction to promote mtDNA transcription, thus restoring mitochondrial function and ultimately suppressing ferroptosis.

ABHD11-Mediated mtDNA Transcription Suppresses Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis Through Inhibiting ROS Production and Reprogramming PUFA-PLs Metabolism

Our RNA-seq and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis implicated that ABHD11 potentially regulates ROS and the generation of superoxide anions (Figure S18A and S18B). The “response to ROS” pathway was negatively correlated with mtDNA transcription (Figure S18C), while positively correlated with cardiomyocyte ferroptosis (Figure S18D). Functionally, ABHD11 overexpression significantly attenuated both mitochondrial and cellular ROS levels induced by hypoxia, and these protective effects were partially abolished by TEFM/POLRMT knockdown or ABHD11 binding-site mutation (Figure S18E and S18F).

To determine whether ABHD11 suppresses cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by modulating ROS levels, we conducted rescue experiments. ABHD11 overexpression reduced both mitochondrial and cellular ROS in hypoxic cardiomyocytes, an effect that was reversed by the ROS inducer MitoParaquat (MitoPQ) and augmented by the scavenger Mitoquinone (MitoQ) (Figure 7A and 7B). Consistently, the ABHD11-mediated suppression of oxidized lipids was also negated by MitoPQ and enhanced by MitoQ (Figure 7C). Collectively, these findings established that ABHD11 attenuates cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by partially suppressing mitochondrial ROS generation.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis also suggested a potential role for ABHD11 in regulating fatty acid catabolism (Figure S19A and S19B). The “fatty acid beta-oxidation” pathway was positively correlated with mtDNA transcription (Figure S19C), while negatively correlated with cardiomyocyte ferroptosis (Figure S19D). We performed nontargeted lipidomics in both hypoxic cardiomyocytes and MI models (Figure S19E and S19F). Partial least-squares discriminant analysis revealed that ABHD11 induced consistent and significant lipidomic remodeling in both in vitro and in vivo models (Figure 7D). Lipidomic profiling revealed that glycerophospholipids constituted the predominant class of altered lipids, with PUFA-PLs being the major subtype (Figure 7D). Both in vitro and in vivo analysis confirmed that ABHD11 preferentially reduced the levels of these PUFA-PLs, which are key substrates for lipid peroxidation (Figure 7E and 7F). The most significantly affected PUFA species were arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, and linoleic acid, primarily within phosphatidylethanolamines and phosphatidylcholines (Figure 7G through 7J; Figure S19G through S19K).

We further performed rescue experiments to explore whether ABHD11 suppressed cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by reprogramming PUFA-PLs metabolism. The ABHD11-mediated reduction in oxidized lipids was partially reversed by supplementation with exogenous PUFA-PLs precursors (arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, linoleic acid) (Figure 7K). Conversely, the depletion of PUFA/arachidonic acid/docosahexaenoic acid/linoleic acid by ABHD11 was abolished upon TEFM/POLRMT knockdown or ABHD11 binding-sites mutation (Figure 7L and 7M). Furthermore, ABHD11-enhanced oxygen consumption rate in hypoxic cardiomyocytes was also abolished by TEFM/POLRMT knockdown or ABHD11 binding-sites mutation (Figure 7N and 7O).

Collectively, these findings establish that ABHD11-mediated mtDNA transcription attenuates lipid peroxidation and suppresses cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by both inhibiting ROS production and reprogramming PUFA-PLs metabolism.

ABHD11 Orchestrates Mitochondrial-Nuclear Crosstalk Through the 4-HNE/YY1 Axis

We further investigated whether ABHD11-mediated mitochondrial transcription regulates nuclear gene expression. Using the KnockTF 2.0 tool, we identified 6 transcription factors potentially regulating ABHD11 downstream targets involved in ferroptosis, as well as nuclear-encoded electron transport chain and fatty acid beta-oxidation genes (Figure 8A and 8B). Among these, YY1 showed the most pronounced upregulation at the protein level after ABHD11 overexpression in hypoxic cardiomyocytes (Figure 8C). Functional assays confirmed that YY1 knockdown most effectively abolished the protective effects of ABHD11 on oxygen consumption rate, PUFA levels, and MDA levels (Figure 8D through 8F; Figure S20A through S20C). ABHD11 did not alter YY1 mRNA levels but stabilized YY1 protein through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, as evidenced by the finding that MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor), but not chloroquine, increased YY1 expression (Figure 8G through 8J). Consistently, ABHD11 overexpression led to a reduction in YY1 ubiquitination (Figure 8K).

Given the interplay between 4-HNE–induced carbonylation and ubiquitination in ferroptosis,24 we examined whether ABHD11 suppresses 4-HNE–mediated carbonylation to modulate YY1 ubiquitination. We found that carbonylated YY1 levels increased with 4-HNE concentration, and 4-HNE supplementation reversed ABHD11-induced YY1 stabilization (Figure 8L and 8M). Site prediction and mutation experiment identified the evolutionarily conserved residue K179 as the critical carbonylation site on YY1 (Figure S20D; Figure 8N and 8O). The K179R mutation attenuated 4-HNE–induced carbonylation, reduced YY1 ubiquitination, and extended its protein half-life (Figure 8P through 8R), indicating that ABHD11 stabilizes YY1 by inhibiting K179-specific carbonylation. Protein–protein interaction network analysis of the 64 overlapping genes (associated with ABHD11‑mediated ferroptosis and predicted as YY1 targets) identified 20 core nodes as key ferroptosis drivers and suppressors (Figure 8B; Figure S20E and S20F). Chromatin immunoprecipitation–quantitative polymerase chain reaction confirmed that YY1 directly binds to the promoters of the mitochondrial genes COX8B (cytochrome c oxidase subunit 8B) and SLC1A3 (solute carrier family 1 member 3), as well as the ferroptosis‑related genes ACSL4 (acyl-coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 4), TFRC (transferrin receptor), and ATF3 (activating transcription factor 3) (Figure S20G through S20K). ABHD11 overexpression downregulated the key ferroptosis drivers while concurrently upregulating nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes, a transcriptional profile that was partially reversed by YY1 knockdown (Figure 8S and 8T; Figure S20L through S20Q), suggesting that ABHD11 stabilizes YY1 to promote its transcriptional program.

We next determined whether YY1 is a critical downstream mediator of ABHD11 in suppressing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI. In hypoxic cardiomyocytes, depletion of YY1 partially abolished the ABHD11-mediated mitochondrial improvements, including the enhancement of membrane potential (Figure S21A) and ATP production (Figure S21B), as well as the reduction in mitochondrial ROS (Figure S21C). In normal adult hearts, cardiomyocyte-specific YY1 depletion alone did not affect cardiac function under basal conditions (Figure S21D). After MI, cardiomyocyte-specific YY1 depletion significantly elevated the levels of key ferroptotic mediators, including cellular iron, ROS, and PUFA (Figure S21E through S21G), leading to excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides such as 4-HNE and MDA (Figure S21H and S21I). YY1 depletion also partially abrogated the suppression of these ferroptosis markers and the improvement of cardiac function in MI mice conferred by ABHD11 (Figure S21E through S21J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ABHD11 stabilizes YY1 through the 4‑HNE/YY1 axis to orchestrate mitochondrial‑nuclear crosstalk, which suppresses iron deposition, ROS production, and PUFA lipids accumulation, thereby limiting ferroptosis and promoting cardiac recovery after MI.

DNA Methyltransferase 1 Represses ABHD11 Transcription Through Promoter Hypermethylation

We next investigated the upstream signals governing ABHD11 loss after MI. Using the KnockTF 2.0 database, we identified 4 potential transcriptional activators and 3 repressors of ABHD11 (Figure S22A). To determine which of these candidates might potentially regulate ABHD11 after MI, we performed correlation analysis using RNA-seq data from heart samples collected at 1, 7, and 56 days after MI (GSE114695) (Figure S22B). Among all candidates examined, DNMT1 (DNA methyltransferase 1) exhibited the strongest negative correlation with ABHD11, a finding further validated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction using the temporal post-MI heart samples (Figure S22B through S22D). Given that DNMT1 is a DNA methyltransferase responsible for transcriptional silencing, we hypothesized that it represses ABHD11 expression through promoter methylation. To test this, we first analyzed the ABHD11 promoter region using the MethPrimer 2.0 platform and identified a dense cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) island cluster (Figure S22E). Subsequent chromatin immunoprecipitation assays confirmed DNMT1 binding to the ABHD11 promoter in hypoxic cardiomyocytes (Figure S22F). Consistently, methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction revealed that DNMT1 knockdown significantly reduced ABHD11 promoter methylation in hypoxic cardiomyocytes (Figure S22G). Knockdown of DNMT1 in hypoxic cardiomyocytes enhanced ABHD11 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure S22H and S22I), indicating transcriptional repression. This repression was also confirmed by luciferase reporter assays showing that DNMT1 overexpression significantly suppressed ABHD11 promoter activity (Figure S22J). Collectively, these findings establish that DNMT1 binds to the ABHD11 promoter and mediates its hypermethylation, thereby repressing its transcription. Based on previous reports of DNMT1 upregulation after MI,25 we establish that this elevation represses ABHD11 transcription through promoter hypermethylation.

Discussion

The present study uncovers a unique role of ABHD11-mediated mtDNA transcription in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI, a novel mechanism distinct from ABHD11’s canonical catalytic functions (Figure 8U). We found that cardiac ferroptosis peaked at day 7 after MI, primarily in peri-infarct cardiomyocytes, when mitochondrial dysfunction emerged as a crucial pathophysiological feature. By integrating multi-omics analysis with ferroptosis-related functional assays, we newly identified the mitochondrial enzyme ABHD11 as a key suppressor of cardiomyocyte ferroptosis to promote cardiac functional recovery after MI. Mechanistically, ABHD11 acts as an mtDNA transcription coactivator by enhancing the TEFM-POLRMT interaction, thereby augmenting the expression of mtDNA-encoded respiratory chain genes to restore mitochondrial function. This reduces mitochondria-derived ROS, which directly lowers lipid peroxidation and consequently inhibits ferroptosis. In addition, ABHD11 stabilizes YY1 through the 4‑HNE/YY1 axis to orchestrate mitochondrial‑nuclear crosstalk, which suppresses the core ferroptotic cascade, including iron deposition, ROS production, and PUFA accumulation, ultimately limiting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis and promoting cardiac recovery after MI. Furthermore, we identified that DNMT1 represses ABHD11 transcription through promoter hypermethylation. Our findings establish ABHD11 as a transcriptional regulator of mtDNA that restores mitochondrial function and define the ABHD11/4-HNE/YY1 axis as a novel mediator of mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk that suppresses cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and improves cardiac function after MI.

Ferroptosis Emerges as a Critical Contributor of Subacute Phase After MI

Multiple forms of cell death have been implicated in MI progression, yet their stage-specific contributions remain poorly defined.3 Here, we identified ferroptosis as a major component of injury during the subacute phase after myocardial infarction, with distinct spatiotemporal advantages for therapeutic intervention. Our results revealed that apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis were all activated by day 1 but showed distinct dynamics, with apoptosis/necroptosis peaking at days 1 to 3 and ferroptosis peaking at day 7, indicating a dynamic shift in the dominant modes of cardiomyocyte death after MI, rather than a strict sequential activation. In addition, time-course echocardiography showed that apoptosis/necroptosis inhibitors conferred protection as early as day 3 after MI, whereas ferroptosis inhibitors showed protective effects from day 7 onward, with sustained efficacy through the subacute phase. Critically, this subacute phase not only coincided with ongoing infarct expansion and heart failure progression but also provided a broader therapeutic window, underscoring the unique therapeutic benefit of targeting ferroptosis during this period. Spatially, we further showed that cardiomyocyte ferroptosis was confined to the peri-infarct zone, where viable but stressed cells remained amenable to rescue, in contrast with the irreversibly damaged infarct zone. Salvaging these border zone cardiomyocytes is essential for preserving contractile function and limiting adverse remodeling. Together, our findings suggest that targeting ferroptosis represents a promising spatiotemporal strategy to limit infarct expansion and improve long-term outcomes after MI.

Role of mtDNA Transcription on Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis After MI

In contrast with previous research, which primarily focused on the effects of mtDNA damage in regulating cell ferroptosis,16,26 our study uncovered a novel role for mitochondrial mtDNA transcription in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. The mtDNA transcription requires multiple transcription factors and RNA polymerase POLRMT to ensure the accurate expression of mtDNA-encoded genes.17 Previous studies have shown that dysregulation of TEFM can lead to mtDNA transcription deficiencies, resulting in impaired cardiomyocyte mitochondrial homeostasis and cardiac dilation.18 Patients with mutations in TEFM exhibit lower expression of mtDNA-encoded proteins, making them susceptible to mitochondrial diseases, including mitochondrial myopathy, developmental delay, and early fatality.27 In this study, we demonstrated that ABHD11 directly interacted with TEFM in mitochondria, and TEFM deficiency significantly abrogated the enhanced mtDNA transcription and alleviated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis induced by ABHD11. These findings provided further evidence that TEFM was crucial for maintaining cardiomyocyte mitochondrial homeostasis and ferroptosis resistance. In addition, TEFM-mediated mtDNA transcription relies on its interaction with POLRMT.28 Specifically, TEFM forms a critical interaction with POLRMT to create a “sliding clamp” structure that encircles the mtDNA, facilitating the high processivity and efficiency of mtDNA transcription.29 It is interesting that we identified ABHD11 as a mtDNA transcription coactivator that enhances the TEFM-POLRMT interaction, a mechanism conserved in both mouse and human hearts. This interaction was specifically impaired after MI but could be rescued by ABHD11 restoration. Our findings established ABHD11 as a key upstream enhancer of mtDNA transcription and a promising therapeutic target for mitochondrial dysfunction.

ABHD11-Alleviated Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis Is Not Dependent on Its Canonical Catalytic Function

An intriguing finding of this study was that ABHD11-alleviated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis occurs independently of its canonical catalytic function. ABHD11 is a bifunctional mitochondrial enzyme characterized by its canonical α/β-hydrolase fold, which enables it to orchestrate 2 distinct metabolic processes through its dual catalytic domains.19 Its hydrolase activity mediates the hydrolysis of triacylglycerols to maintain lipid homeostasis,30 whereas its acyltransferase activity facilitates the lipoylation of the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, thus supporting 2-oxoglutarate metabolism.19 Previous studies have shown that the canonical catalytic functions of ABHD11 are important for various processes, such as embryonic stem cell differentiation, breast cancer cell resistance to paclitaxel, and T cell–mediated autoimmunity.31 In the current study, both in vitro and in vivo data demonstrated that mutating the catalytic sites of ABHD11 had a limited effect on cardiomyocyte ferroptosis resistance, suggesting that ABHD11 inhibited cardiomyocyte ferroptosis through alternative mechanisms. Accumulating evidence indicated that certain metabolic enzymes exhibited surprising activities beyond their canonical metabolic functions in response to both extracellular and intracellular signaling. These activities include the regulation of mRNA transcription,32 DNA damage repair,33 and protein phosphorylation,34 among others. Recently, several metabolic enzymes have been found to inhibit cell ferroptosis through their noncanonical functions. For example, the glycolytic enzyme ENO1 (enolase 1) acted as an RNA-binding protein to promote the degradation of IRP1 (iron regulatory protein 1) mRNA, thereby reducing mitochondrial iron accumulation and inhibiting ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells.35 Similarly, creatine kinase CKB (creatine kinase B) functioned as a protein kinase to phosphorylate GPX4, preventing its autophagic degradation and thereby attenuating ferroptosis in tumor cells.36 The noncanonical functions of metabolic enzymes in cardiac pathologies, especially in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, remain largely unexplored. In this study, we demonstrated that ABHD11 overexpression sustained mitochondrial DNA transcription to attenuate cardiomyocyte ferroptosis after MI through its noncanonical function of bridging the TEFM-POLRMT interaction. Based on our findings, we proposed that metabolic enzyme-mediated mitochondrial gene expression could be a rapid adaptive response to environmental changes after MI, opening new therapeutic avenues for targeting cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

Mitochondrial ABHD11 Coregulates ROS and PUFA-PLs to Suppress Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis

We also demonstrated that mitochondrial ABHD11 acted as a key coregulator of ROS and PUFA-PLs metabolism, both causally linked to lipid peroxidation and cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. Lipid peroxidation, a central mediator of ferroptosis, is determined by the initiation phase driven by oxidative stress and the propagation phase sustained by the abundance of membrane PUFA-PLs.37,38 We found that ABHD11 overexpression concurrently reprograms both ROS generation and PUFA-PLs composition, thereby inhibiting both the initiation and propagation of lipid peroxidation. This dual regulatory function establishes ABHD11 as a promising therapeutic target for attenuating lipid peroxidation and cardiomyocyte ferroptosis in permanent MI models. Given the critical role of excessive ROS generation and phospholipid peroxidation in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis during I/R (ischemia/reperfusion) injury,5,6 ABHD11 likely exerts a similar cardioprotection in both permanent MI and I/R models through its dual regulation of lipid metabolism and oxidative stress. Although ABHD11 has not been directly studied in I/R models, the varying therapeutic window of ferroptosis inhibition across models suggests that its protective effects likely occur earlier in the I/R model. In the permanent MI model, we found the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 led to sustained functional improvement beginning 7 days after MI. In contrast, in the I/R model, Fer-1–mediated protection occurred within 24 hours and lasted 7 days.5 Given that ABHD11 may function through a common mechanism in both models, its protective effects would be expected to manifest earlier in the I/R setting. ABHD11 is enriched in cardiomyocytes, suggesting targeted therapy could minimize off-target effects.

ABHD11/4-HNE/YY1 Axis Orchestrates a Mitochondrial-Nuclear Crosstalk to Suppress Cardiomyocyte Ferroptosis

A bidirectional mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk is essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. Although nuclear-to-mitochondrial anterograde regulation is well characterized, mitochondria-to-nucleus retrograde signaling remains elusive. In this study, we uncovered a novel mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk mechanism regulating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, mediated by the ABHD11/4-HNE/YY1 axis. ABHD11-driven mtDNA transcription restored mitochondrial respiration, thereby suppressing ROS/PUFA-PLs–driven lipid peroxidation and 4-HNE generation. Emerging evidence shows that 4-HNE functions not only as a toxic byproduct of lipid peroxidation but also as a signaling mediator by inducing protein carbonylation, a process known to interact with ubiquitination during cell ferroptosis.24 Here, we found that reduced 4-HNE levels attenuated the carbonylation and ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the transcription factor YY1, leading to its stabilization. Consistent with its known role as a context-dependent transcriptional regulator,39 YY1 stabilized by ABHD11 coordinated a multifaceted transcriptional program to suppress ferroptosis. First, stabilized YY1 limited the substrate for lipid peroxidation by downregulating PUFA lipids accumulation, aligned with its known function as a negative regulator through the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs)-ACSL1/4 axis.40 Second, YY1 reduced the primary trigger of peroxidation by reducing ROS production through upregulating nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes to restore mitochondrial homeostasis. Third, YY1 restricted the catalytic metal required for peroxidation by transcriptionally suppressing genes involved in iron metabolism, such as TFRC (iron uptake) and HMOX1 (heme oxygenase 1, releasing iron). Together, these YY1‑mediated pathways, which include reducing peroxidation substrates, decreasing oxidative triggers, and limiting catalytic iron, converged to robustly inhibit the ferroptosis cascade. Our study elucidates a novel mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk mediated by the ABHD11/4-HNE/YY1 axis, providing new mechanistic insights into cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and identifying potential therapeutic targets for improving post-MI cardiac function.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of pharmacological inhibitors cannot fundamentally quantify the contribution of each cell death forms to cardiomyocyte death after MI, because of their potential off-target effects on other forms of death. Second, although ABHD11 may function through a common mechanism in both MI and I/R models, its protective effects likely manifest earlier in I/R injury compared to permanent MI. Further studies will be necessary to validate this hypothesis and clarify the temporal role of ABHD11 across different models. In addition, only male animals were used in this study. Similar mechanisms are likely to operate in female mice, which requires further investigation.

Conclusions

By enhancing the TEFM-POLRMT interaction, ABHD11-mediated mtDNA transcription restores mitochondrial function, thereby initiating a protective mitochondrial-nuclear crosstalk that ultimately suppresses cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and improves cardiac function after MI. This study uncovers a novel mechanism linking mtDNA transcription to cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, establishing ABHD11 as a promising therapeutic target for restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.
