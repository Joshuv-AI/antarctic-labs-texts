Provenance, environments, and tectonic signatures of Tanjong-Sandakan Formations, Northeast Sabah, Malaysia: Implications from sedimentology, palynology, and geochemistry
Authors: Muhammad Firdaus Abd Halim, Nor Syazwani Zainal Abidin, Ery Arifullah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates marginal marine deposits found in the Tanjong and Sandakan formations from the Early to Late Miocene in Northeast Sabah. It examines sedimentology, trace elements (TE), rare earth elements (REE), hydrocarbon generation potential, and palynology to understand provenance, depositional environments, and paleoenvironmental conditions. Facies analysis identified four associations in the Tanjong and three in the Sandakan Formation, ranging from fluvial-deltaic to shallow marine environments. Mangrove-associated taxa, herbaceous and tree ferns typical of freshwater swamps, and coastal plants indicate a transition zone between terrestrial and marine environments. Siliciclastic deposits in the Northeast Sabah basin show varying redox conditions, ranging from dysoxic to oxic, as indicated by the Ni/Co, V/Cr, and U/Th ratios. These fluctuations may be linked to the tectonic evolution of the Sulu Sea and related sea-level lowstands. The low abundance of marine dinoflagellate cysts, along with high levels of rainforest and peat swamp pollen, suggests periods of subaerial exposure that contributed to the redox conditions. C-values and Sr/Cu ratios in the mudstone samples indicate semihumid to humid and warm conditions during the Miocene. Additionally, the distribution patterns of rare earth elements (REE) and the (La/Yb)N and Th/U ratios reveal consistent sedimentation rates in marginal marine environments. Palynofacies analysis indicates water column fluctuations, leading to oxidation during transport, resulting in low TOC values and kerogen types III and IV.Ternary diagrams of immobile TEs indicate that mudstone samples originate from active continental margins, linked to the rifting of the Sulu Sea during the Early to Middle Miocene. In contrast, sandstone samples exhibit characteristics of passive margins, likely due to recycled sediments from the early "Rajang Group Accretionary Complex." The plots for immobile TEs in the mudstones suggest intermediate and mafic sources, supporting the idea of uplift and erosion from deepwater sediments, as well as volcanic activity from the Cagayan and Sulu Arc systems. Sandstone samples may be sourced from acidic rocks associated with felsic intrusions in the Segama Valley, indicating a significant continental basement beneath the ophiolite. Keywords: Marginal marine, trace element, provenance, palynology, tectonic setting, Northeast Sabah.

Introduction

The Tanjong and Sandakan Formations are a continuous succession of marginal marine deposits that span from the Early to Late Miocene. These sediments form circular to subcircular basins onshore and extend northeastward offshore, depositing in the depocentres of sub-basins of the Northeast Sabah basin, known as the Central Sabah and Sandakan Sub-basins (Fig. 1B). Throughout these sedimentation cycles, the siliciclastic rocks of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations undergo various sedimentary processes due to the opening and closure of the Sulu Sea [[1], [2], [3]]. This variability is linked to the subduction of the Proto-South China Sea [4] and the Celebes Sea [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. [10] observed that the once contiguous Tanjong and Sandakan Formations underwent segmentation during subsidence into discrete circular to sub-circular basins. This subsidence was triggered by far-field tectonic forces activated during major tectonic events, such as the collision and subsequent uplift of exotic microcontinents associated with the Australian and Philippines blocks [11,12].

The long-held notion of Southeast Sabah Basin was formed during the Early Miocene through extensional tectonism in eastern Sabah, recent studies have solidified this understanding based on general geology [13], lithostratigraphy [14,15], and analysis of geochemical and petrographical features of coal seams [16,17]. [18] recently conducted a thorough study involving a systematic investigation of sedimentology, bulk geochemistry, and palynology, focusing on the sedimentary successions and their stratigraphic correlation to the depositional environment of the onshore Tanjong Formation in southeast Sabah.

The Tanjong and Sandakan Formations in the Northeast Sabah Basin reveal a thick marginal marine succession depicting intricate interactions between fully marine and continental processes [[19], [20], [21], [22]] throughout Miocene, providing an opportunity for in-depth environmental studies. These Miocene sequences exhibit composite interbedding of sedimentary facies, reflecting complex processes. While researchers have extensively investigated the sedimentological and paleontological aspects of these deposits, with a focus on the well-established Sandakan Formation [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]], the Tanjong Formation has received comparatively less attention, with the most recent significant study conducted by Ref. [29] primarily covering lithostratigraphy and palynology. Despite the influence of intense Miocene tectonic events altering these deposits, whose chemical compositions remain relatively unchanged but underwent significant physical transformations through weathering, erosion, transportation, and deposition, no geochemical approaches have been employed for either formation until now. Recognizing that the chemical composition of the constituent detritus reveals crucial information about the origins of these rocks [30], understanding a basin's source, depositional environments, and morphological and tectonic settings becomes imperative. This can be accomplished via organic and inorganic geochemical analysis [[31], [32], [33]].

The extensive geographical distribution and substantial thickness of siliciclastic strata, coupled with their Miocene depositional age, render these deposits a crucial source of knowledge regarding geological events during the opening and closure of the Sulu Sea. Several researchers have reported the deposition of allochthonous palynomorphs within this marginal marine succession [26,29,34], providing a mechanism for detailed differentiation among terrestrial, transitional, and fully marine deposits in these complex settings [35,36]. When integrated with sedimentary characteristics and biogenic structures, detecting in situ pollen and spores in the sediment record may provide a more complete picture of the depositional environment [37,38]. As a result, integrated physical, geochemical, and biological investigations may contribute to a deeper comprehension of the provenance and paleotectonic settings, allowing for a comprehensive paleodepositional model detailing the evolution of the Tanjong and Sandakan marginal marine settings during the Miocene.

This study broadens the investigation of the in-depth paleoenvironment of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations in the Northeast Sabah Basin, employing a multi-disciplinary approach to establish connections between the sedimentary environment and the geochemical and organic composition of the sediments. The primary objectives of this study are (a) developing a paleodepositional model for the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations in the Northeast Sabah Basin through in-depth facies analysis supported by palynology, (b) providing a comprehensive dataset of systematic trace elements (TEs), rare earth elements (REEs), and organic geochemistry (from Rock-Eval pyrolysis) to determine geochemical variations throughout Miocene time, consequently complement the interpretation of paleoenvironmental conditions, (c) assessing the composition of source areas and the potential influence of provenance and tectonic settings on the rocks at the source region based on well-established geochemical proxies (from TEs) supported by palynomorph analysis, (d) evaluating the type, origin, and maturation of organic matter, and (e) predicting different tectonic settings of both formations in the Northeast Sabah Basin through geochemical signatures of marginal marine deposits during the Miocene.

Geological setting

The Northeast Sabah region features disrupted rock units, primarily mudstone-matrix melanges, and fractured formations, namely Garinono, Kuamut, and Ayer Melanges. Geographically, these melanges best represent the early Sulu Sea rifting during the Middle Miocene, resulting in the olistostrome flows into the active rift [2]. The ENE-trending Sulu and Cagayan ridges bounding the Sulu Sea are widely acknowledged to be remnant arcs from intra-arc rifting systems facing a SE subduction [6,41,42], forming a trench at the NW margin of the Celebes Sea [9,43]. This rifting occurred within an active volcanic arc, evident in the widespread distribution of pyroclastic and volcanic rocks (Tungku Formation). The active rifting caused the formation of steep, unstable submarine slopes, resulting in the deposition of extensive olistostrome deposits.

A significant erosional event occurred to the west, simultaneous with the olistostrome formation and the Sulu Sea rifting due to the uplifted "Rajang Group Accretionary Complex," comprising pre-Sulu Sea rift ophiolite and deepwater sediments (Fig. 2). The obducted ophiolite-deepwater sediments comprise complex oceanic crust (Chert-Spilite, Sapulut, Trusmadi, Crocker, and Temburong Formations) caused by the underthrust of southward subduction involving the Miri Zone of Borneo and the adjacent Dangerous Grounds terrain of the Proto-South China Sea crust [1,43]. Furthermore, the early development of mud volcanoes in eastern Sabah during the Middle Miocene could be linked to the obduction and underthrusting events driven by the ongoing mud/shale diapirism [44,45].

Following erosion, Middle to Late Miocene quartz-rich sandstones were sourced from the Tanjong, Sandakan, and other basin-fill formations (Segama Group including Sebahat and Ganduman Formations). The sediments were transported by the ancestral Kinabatangan River and deltas, eventually prograding into the sub-basins of the Northeast Sabah and Celebes Sea basins [11]. The extension of the Sulu Sea was halted in the late Middle Miocene and onwards due to the collision and uplift of exotic microcontinents associated with active Australian and Philippine blocks during the Miocene, which may still be occurring today [11,46]. This compressional phase is evidenced by wrenching and inversion features observed in the seismic of the offshore Sandakan Sub-basin and the associated volcanic activity as suggested by the offshore Segama Group, which contains Late Miocene basalt [47]. As a result of this geological activity, the Late Pliocene to Pleistocene Togopi Formation was deposited unconformably overlying the Ganduman Formation, both onshore and offshore [48]. This event signaled the closure of the previously opened Sulu Sea, forming NE-SW trending en echelon anticlines and normal faults offshore [49].

Materials and methods

Eight well-preserved outcrops of marginal marine deposits were investigated in this study (Fig. 1C), comprising the Early to Middle Miocene Tanjong Formation (four outcrops; Fig. 1D) along the Kota Kinabatangan-Lahad Datu roads within the Kinabatangan District, specifically known as the Bukit Garam Basin Tanjong Formation [13,29], and the Middle to Late Miocene Sandakan Formation (four outcrops; Fig. 1E) from the Sandakan Peninsula areas.

The outcrops were thoroughly examined utilizing high-resolution sedimentological methods at various scales. Sedimentary logs at a scale of 1:50 provided information on lithology, geometry, sedimentary, and biogenic structures. Sedimentary logs, created at a detailed scale of 1 cm representing 50 cm of the actual sedimentary sequence, provided information on lithology, geometry, and sedimentary and biogenic structures. Relative abundances of sedimentary facies, sedimentary structures, and ichnological features were interpreted to qualitatively assess facies succession and succession sets [19,50].

Thirty samples were collected from mudstone and sandstone units to investigate the geochemical properties of various facies (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). These include seven sandstone and seven mudstone samples from the Tanjong Formation, as well as seven sandstone and nine mudstone samples from the Sandakan Formation. Total Organic Carbon (TOC), trace elements (TE), and rare earth elements (REE) concentrations were analyzed using a systematic approach. The powdered samples were rinsed, baked, and crushed to achieve a particle size of less than 200 mesh with an agate mortar. TOC analysis, performed at CORELAB Malaysia, involved dissolving inorganic carbon with 10 % HCl at 60 °C for 24 h, followed by high-temperature combustion in an oxygen-rich atmosphere. The generated carbon dioxide was measured, and the TOC content was determined using a LECO carbon analyzer.

The powdered samples were rinsed, dried, and crushed to a particle size of less than 200 mesh using an agate mortar. For TE and REE analysis, the whole-rock samples were digested with a mixture of acids (HF/HNO3/HClO4 = 1:1:3) for 12–24 h at 200 °C in a pressure-sealed Teflon bomb. The resulting solution was analyzed using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) at Bureau Veritas Commodities Canada Ltd., following the method described by Ref. [51].

The TOC analysis, conducted at CORELAB Malaysia, involved fifteen mudstone-shale samples. Inorganic carbon was dissolved with 10 % HCl at 60 °C for 24 h, followed by high-temperature combustion in an oxygen-rich atmosphere. The carbon dioxide produced was measured, and TOC content was determined using a LECO carbon analyzer.

The same set of mudstone samples underwent standard palynofacies analysis using the non-oxidative technique proposed by Refs. [52,53] in the Orogenic Resources laboratory in Malaysia. Before destroying mineral constituents with hydrochloric acid (32 %) and cold hydrofluoric acid (HF) (40 %), the samples were crushed, and the residues neutralized with mineral-free water. Organic matter separation involved a heavy liquid of zinc bromide (2.2 specific gravity). Residues were smeared on a glass slide with an alcohol solution and Norland 61 optical adhesive glue and examined under transmitted light and fluorescence microscopy.

At least 500 particles were counted (raw counts) and classified into three main kerogen types: amorphous, phytoclasts, and palynomorphs. The classification used herein is according to Ref. [54], where the palynomorphs identification followed the methods of [55] with some modification and was based on published works by Refs. [[56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61]] which focus more on Southeast Asia taxa. Detailed paleoenvironmental interpretation took into account sedimentary organic matter descriptions (e.g., Refs. [52,53,[62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67], [68]]). Environmental analyses included kerogen distribution patterns and parameters (cf., [53,64,65,69,70]).

Results

Sedimentary facies and facies associations

Exposed outcrops representing the Tanjong Formation in the Kinabatangan areas reveal eight (8) distinct sedimentary facies (Fig. 3 and Table S1). Similarly, the Sandakan Formation from the Sandakan Peninsula exhibits and interprets seven (7) sedimentary facies (Fig. 4 and Table S2). These sedimentary facies were then grouped into facies associations. Four (4) facies associations were identified from the Tanjong Formation (Table 1), while three (3) were distinguished from the Sandakan Formation (Table 2). This observation emphasizes the dynamic interplay of processes within the marginal marine environment.

Tanjong Formation

Facies association 1 (FA1): interbedded current ripple cross-laminated and low-angle planar cross-laminated sandstone and mudstone units

Facies association 1 (FA1) comprises 50.0–120.0 cm thick, erosive base sandstone and 20.0–40.0 cm thick mudstone layers (Fig. 7). The very fine-to fine-grained sandstone layers exhibit centimetre-scale current ripple cross-lamination and low-angle planar cross-lamination. Sharp-based, very thin centimetre-scale (10.0–20.0 cm) and very fine-grained sandstone sheets with vertical and lateral transitions from cross-bedded to horizontal-laminated facies indicate a fining-upward trend. Mudstone layers on a centimetre-to decimetre-scale are structureless and parallel-laminated. The facies can measure up to 3.0 m thick. Finer FA2 deposits typically overlie them, exhibiting a fining upward succession. Rootlets and amber fragments are also abundant within the mudstone facies.

Crevasse splay facies association (FA1) was observed at the TJG-1 outcrop of Tanjong Formation in the Kinabatangan areas.

Five (5) ichnotaxa were identified in this facies association: Ophiomorpha annulata, Rhizocorallium isp., Palaeophycus isp., Planolites isp., and Skolithos isp.) in this facies association. Bioturbation in the sandstone facies is generally sporadically distributed with low bioturbation (BI 1–2), typically concentrated on the bed surfaces. Rhizocorallium isp. and Palaeophycus isp. are more abundant (80 %) than Ophiomorpha annulata. Bioturbation can also occur at sandstone-mudstone interfaces, where Skolithos isp., Planolites isp., and Ophiomorpha annulata. The bioturbation in the sandstone-mudstone interfaces is lower (BI 0–1) than in sandstone layers.

Interpretation: The erosive base of FA1 indicates turbulent deposition and constricted flows caused by a breach in the parent channel flood [71,72]. Low-angle planar cross-lamination suggests splay frontal accretion, while ripple cross-lamination indicates low-energy unidirectional currents. The vertical successions of these bed types indicate a transition from lower flow regime bedforms to higher flow regime bedforms, with rapid high-energy conveyance and decreased water depth during the late stages of individual flood events [73]. Such rapid burial ceased bioturbation and preserved it in the shallow tier of the mixed-layer deposits, indicated by the reduced diversities and low bioturbation index, with occurrences of opportunistic communities (e.g., Ophiomorpha annulata and Palaeophycus isp.) within the sandstone-mudstone interfaces. The structureless mudstone was most likely formed through fluid mud deposition, implying a high concentration of suspended sediment or periodic mixing of saltwater and freshwater [74,75]. The alternating sandstone and mudstone layers illustrate the periodic calm of slack-water and unidirectional currents, allowing enough turbulence to keep food particles in suspension, which is an ideal condition for Ophiomorpha annulata, Palaeophycus isp. and Skolithos isp. tracemakers. The parallel-laminated mudstone suggests that sediment deposition resulted from minor traction currents and low-energy suspension. The vertical transition and thinning of sand sheets suggest crevasse splay deposits in a lower delta plain environment, presumably driven by tides or progressive overbank spilling from the channel [[76], [77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82]].

Facies association 2 (FA2): sharp-based, laminated, and structureless mudstone with sandy laminae units

Facies Association 2 (FA2) is distinguished by mudstone layers ranging in thickness from 1.0 to 3.0 m and exhibits well-defined sharp bases. Parallel lamination can be observed within these mudstone layers, occasionally interlayered with undulating ripples formed from very fine-grained sandstone laminae. The mudstones typically lack identifiable internal structures and have a grey to dark grey coloration, with some reddish hues indicating pedogenic processes (soil aggregates in crumbly, blocky, sub-prismatic peds and granular structures). Rhizoliths and rootlets are also frequently discovered in the mudstone facies (Fig. 8). The boundaries at the base and top of these mudstone layers vary from sharp to transitional, with several upper boundaries associated with crevasse splay breaches (FA1).

The distribution of flood plain facies and their associations (FA2) at the Tanjong Formation succession.

Interpretation: The parallel laminated, structureless mudstones are thought to result from fluid-mud deposition [83,84]. The massive thickness of structureless mudstones may also record sediment deposition by turbulent-plug flows [85,86], which shield the underlying sandy substrates from faunal colonization [50]. The grey to dark grey, organic-rich mudstone with abundant carbonaceous material, sporadically interlayered with very fine-grained sandstone lamina and plane-parallel lamination, suggests deposition in subaqueous settings such as floodplain ponds or shallow-water lakes. The presence of rootlets and rhizolith in light grey, massive to poorly laminated mudstone with low organic matter content implies a subaerial overbank deposition. Consequently, FA2 is interpreted as sedimentation in interdistributary zones or flood plains, most likely overbank discharge from active channels.

Facies association 3 (FA3): amalgamated swalley cross-stratified, very fine-to fine-grained sandstone units

Facies association 3 (FA3) comprises 3.0–6.0 m thick amalgamated sandstone-dominated units with very fine-to fine-grained sandstone beds exhibiting faint swalley cross-stratification (Fig. 9). Centimetre-to decimetre-scale lenticular beds consist of very fine-grained current ripple cross-lamination and planar cross-stratification. The centimetre-to decimetre-scale sandstone beds form bedsets up to 5.0 m thick, displaying single-story bed geometries up to 4.0 m thick and 10.0 m lateral continuity with erosional bases. The stratal dips in the bed forms range between 30° and 60°. They are separated from one another within a comparable sand body as the accretionary sets of strata with uniform dips change in dip angle and direction. Rootlets and carbonaceous material are less common in the decimetre-scale thick mudstone facies. Additionally, FA3 is commonly overlain but sometimes underlain by FA4 deposits.

The distribution of facies and facies association of distributary channel and interdistributary bay deposits (FA3 and FA4).

Three (3) ichnotaxa are identified from the sandstone facies and the sandstone-mudstone interfaces: Ophiomorpha nodosa, Thalassinoides isp., and Palaeophycus isp. The sandstone shows lower bioturbation (BI 1–2) than sandstone-mudstone interfaces (BI 2–3) as the sandstone-mudstone interfaces have the highest abundance of Ophiomorpha nodosa (70 %). In contrast, the sandstone facies have fewer occurrences of Palaeophycus isp. and Thalassinoides isp.

Interpretation: Faint swaley cross-stratification, erosional surfaces, varying grain sizes, and a lack of ichnotaxon variability suggest rapid sedimentation under fluctuating conditions of tidal and river hydrodynamics and sediment sources [50,87]. The moderate bioturbation intensity (BI 2–3) may indicate brackish-water conditions, possibly related to mixed tidal and fluvial processes, and partial environmentally stressed conditions, most likely due to the low variable salinity [50,88]. The amalgamation of sandstone beds with faint swaley cross-stratification and decreasing thickness may suggest a decrease in storm-wave reworking or sand availability due to increasing water depths or reduced wave energy [[89], [90], [91]]. This has resulted in low bioturbation intensity (BI 1–2) in the sandstone facies covered by Thalassinoides isp. and Palaeophycus isp., suggesting opportunistic colonization of the sand-rich deposits. The colonization may be observed during upstream saltwater incursions, particularly during periods of low river discharge [[92], [93], [94]]. The sandstone facies are characterized by aggradational sediment deposition, resulting in horizontal layers at the channel base with bulk sedimentation in accretionary bars. Inclined beds along the accretion direction represent lateral-accretion units within point bars [95]. Less frequent lenticular beds with planar cross-stratification, current ripple cross-lamination, erosive bases, and fining-upward trends indicate deposition in high-energy environments and unidirectional currents, which are typical of distributary channels [[96], [97], [98], [99]]. The lower occurrence of carbonaceous material and rootlets implies a fluvial influence. Therefore, the FA3 units are interpreted as tide-influenced distributary channels within the lower delta plain, influenced by changing tides and fluvial processes.

Facies association 4 (FA4): laminated and structureless mudstone units

Facies association 4 (FA4) consists of 1.0–4.0 m thick units with erosional to sharp bases often overlapping FA3 (Fig. 10). The dominant facies are interbedded structureless and parallel laminated mudstones. The parallel laminated mudstone contains very fine-to fine-grained sandstone laminae, and the structureless mudstone layers range in thickness from centimetres to decimetres and are commonly composed of carbonaceous material. Locally, there are also abundant centimetre-scaled amber fragments.

Interdistributary bay facies association (FA4) overlain by distributary channel deposits (FA3).

Only one (1) trace fossil was discovered within the mudstone facies in this deposit: Planolites isp. Here, the bioturbation level, indicated by FA5, is predominantly low (BI 1), with Planolites isp. sparsely abundant.

Interpretation: The mudstone formation strongly implies low energy conditions during sediment deposition. The composition and geometry of FA4 indicate sediment deposition from suspension in channel-like depressions. Another interpretation is that the channel was abandoned and later filled during the avulsion stage [100,101]. The dark grey mudstones show that soil formation occurred in a water-saturated environment, susceptible to varying oxygen levels [102]. The presence of Planolites isp. in low bioturbation (BI 0–1) further supports a low-energy water-logged environment. The distributary channel deposits of FA3 commonly overlain by this vertical succession may suggest a genetic relationship between them. Therefore, FA4 may be interpreted as an interdistributary bay or abandoned tidal-influenced distributary channel.

Sandakan Formation

Facies association 1 (FA1): interbedded heterolithic with laminated and structureless mudstone units

Facies association 1 (FA1) consists of 1–4 m thick units with erosional, flat, concave-upward bases that abruptly overlie FA2 and FA3 (Fig. 12). The dominant facies within FA1 are interbedded laminated and structureless mudstones, with sandstones comprising less than 20 % of the composition. The mudstone facies (F1 and F2) exhibit thin silty beds ranging in thickness from millimeters to centimeters, accompanied by carbonaceous material. These beds feature slightly erosive bases and display low-angle cross-lamination without a well-defined vertical arrangement. Additionally, there are occurrences of starved current ripple cross-lamination within the facies. The structureless mudstone layers in FA1 are typically centimeters to decimeters in scale and frequently overlain by carbonaceous laminae. Centimeter-thick thin sandstone layers of very fine-to fine-grained sand are occasionally observed. These sandstone layers possess sharp bases, planar to trough cross-stratification, and are often interbedded with mudstone and carbonaceous laminae.

Offshore facies association (FA3) overlain by shoreface facies associations (FA2 and FA3).

Bioturbation varies (BI 0–4) at sandstone-mudstone interfaces, increasing towards the tops of beds, and contains traces like Ophiomorpha isp., Planolites isp., Palaeophycus isp., and Thalassinoides isp. Some locations exhibit minimal bioturbation (BI 0–1), particularly in mudstone regions, while others, notably sandstone or heterolithic facies, showcase a diverse assemblage of trace fossils (BI 3–4). This diverse assemblage includes Palaeophycus isp., Thalassinoides isp., Rhizocorralium isp., and even centimetre-scale Ophiomorpha isp., which extend downward from the overlying FA2 or FA3.

Interpretation: The observed characteristics of the sedimentary features and their relationships to underlying and overlying facies (FA2 and FA3) give evidence for offshore sediment deposition. This environment experienced a significant decrease in the supply of sand and hydrodynamic energy [91] as it moved away from the shoreline. Laminated mudstones are most likely formed by sediment settling in a low-energy environment, whereas structureless mudstones imply the deposition of fluid mud [74,75]. The presence of structureless mudstones suggests freshwater-saltwater mixing and large quantities of suspended sediment have persisted and may occur periodically. Additionally, the low bioturbation intensity observed in trace fossils, particularly those associated with detritus feeding behaviors (e.g., Planolites isp.) in organic-rich mudstone layers, might reflect changes in oxygen levels [103,104]. The high concentration of carbonaceous material in the sediment suggests that fluvial-tidal currents transported and reworked vegetation from the lower delta plain. Occasional sandstone layers likely represent sediment deposition during infrequent floods [50]. These findings support the interpretation that the examined facies association (FA1) represents sediment deposition in an offshore environment with limited sand supply.

Facies association 2 (FA2): interbedded hummocky cross-stratified, very fine-to fine-grained sandstone with mudstone units

Facies Association 2 (FA2) is typically found underlying and overlying FA1 within the Taman Mutiara outcrops while occasionally in thickness sharply overlying FA3 (Fig. 13). This facies association is characterized by units ranging in thickness from 0.5 to 10 m, with sandstone dominating approximately 60 % of the facies. These sandstone units consist of very fine-to fine-grained sandstone beds with HCS (F7), which are interbedded with millimeter-to centimeter-scale mudstone layers and occasionally associated with millimeter-scale carbonaceous material. Sandstone bedsets can reach thicknesses of up to 8 m and are composed of individual beds ranging in thickness from 0.2 to 1.0 m. These beds show erosional bases and localized soft-sediment deformation.

Facies association of the lower shoreface (FA2) observed at the Taman Harbour outcrop.

Mudstone sections are often either unburrowed or lightly burrowed (BI 0–2). Sandstone layers, characterized by hummocky cross-stratification (HCS), are also frequently unburrowed (BI 0–2). Heterolithic deposits have higher BI values (BI 3–4). Six trace fossils are identified in FA2, including Arenicolites isp., Ophiomorpha isp., Palaeophycus isp., Planolites isp., Rhizocorallium isp., and Skolithos isp.

Interpretation: The thinner heterolithic cross-stratification (HCS) facies (F7) and bedsets in FA2 suggest that storm-induced sediment reworking was more intense and rapid [91]. Traditionally, this has been explained as a result of shallower water depths. However, it could also signify increased storm strength or reduced sediment supply, resulting in accelerated reworking and progressive sorting [90,91,[105], [106], [107]]. The presence of HCS with a wavelength in the decametre scale and low angles implies the presence of combined flows with higher energy capable of causing bedform degradation [108,109]. The decreased amalgamation of HCS-bearing sandstone beds indicates increased rates and magnitude of storm-wave reworking, as well as potential changes in sediment availability. Meanwhile, the occurrence of interbedded rippled sandstones and sandy heterolithics (F3 and F5) implies deposition during periods of reduced wave force, whereas moderate bioturbation intensity (BI 3–4) shows significant organism burrowing activity during fair weather. Biogenic structures at sandstone-mudstone interfaces (BI 0–4) document how organisms adapt to colonizing substrates in high-energy environments and increased sedimentation rates (e.g., Rhizocorallium isp. and Ophiomorpha isp.). Mud drapes or layers may indicate fluid mud deposition under high suspended sediment concentrations near the bed, resulting from sediment resuspension during coastal storms (e.g., Refs. [83,85]). When coastal and hinterland storms occur simultaneously, fluid mud formation is optimized [110,111]. Additionally, strong monsoonal winds dramatically increase wave height and energy along the shoreline. As a result, FA2 is interpreted as reflecting deposition in a lower shoreface region impacted by storms.

Facies association 3 (FA3): amalgamated hummocky cross-stratified very fine-to fine-grained sandstone unit

Facies association 3 (FA3) comprises units varying in thickness from 2 to 6 m, with the majority (>80 %) of sediment being amalgamated very fine to fine-grained sandstone exhibiting hummocky cross-stratification (F7) (Fig. 14). Many sandstone characteristics observed in this facies association are comparable to those seen in FA2, such as sharp scoured bases, gutter casts, normal grading, carbonaceous debris, bioclastic and mud clast lags, and bioturbation. However, there are some notable differences. One key difference is the thickness of amalgamated sandstone and bedsets observed in FA3, ranging from 1.0 to 8.0 m. Mud and carbonaceous clasts ranging in size from millimeters to centimeters are commonly observed at basal boundaries and discontinuity surfaces. Within the facies, a unit known as rippled sandy heterolithic (F3) comprises bedsets ranging in thickness from 0.1 to 0.5 m. These bedsets are made of siltstone to very fine-grained sediment and exhibit oscillation and combined-flow ripple cross-lamination. The sandstone beds in these facies are frequently draped by millimeter-thick mudstone layers that appear structureless or feature minor silty laminae similar to those observed in F1 and F2.

The shoreface sedimentary facies and their facies association at Taman Mutiara outcrop within the Sandakan succession.

Sandbodies in FA3 usually lack burrows, with traces sparsely scattered (BI 0–2). FA3 contains five (5) distinct trace fossils: Thalassinoides isp., Palaeophycus isp., Arenicolites isp., Macaronichnus isp., and Ophiomorpha isp. Macaronichnus isp. is not commonly detected in FA3. Ophiomorpha isp. is also present near the upper sections of sandstone bodies, characterized by vertical burrows with pellets lining their walls.

Interpretation: The prevalence of HCS demonstrates the impact of energetic hydrodynamic conditions during deposition. The occurrence of sharp-based beds further suggests episodic sedimentation events, possibly related to storm activity. The stratification style, notably the occurrence of hummocky cross-stratification (HCS), implies sediment reworking by storm waves. The increased thickness may be attributed to a combination of factors, including increased storm-wave energy, decreased water depth, and increased proximity to the sediment source [[89], [90], [91]]. These factors could increase the transport and deposition of sand, resulting in thicker and more amalgamated sandstone beds. This is substantiated by the low bioturbation intensity (BI 0–2) and the presence of structures adapted to colonizing substrates in energetic environments with high sedimentation rates, alongside the absence of biogenic structures [103,112,113]. Furthermore, the frequency of mud and carbonaceous material within the facies suggests the presence of a mixed sediment load, which fluvial processes may have partially supplied. This indicates a dynamic sedimentary environment influenced by both marine and terrestrial inputs. The occurrence of carbonaceous material suggests preserving organic matter, possibly derived from terrestrial vegetation or marine organisms. Based on these observations, FA3 is interpreted as representing a relatively upper shoreface environment.

Trace elements

Clastic samples obtained from Tanjong and Sandakan deposits (Table 3) show variations in TE composition, notably from the Bukit Garam TJG-1 and TJG-2 outcrops (Fig. 3), as well as the Taman Mutiara outcrop (Fig. 4). Many of these elements, including Cu, Zn, Mn, V, Ba, W, and Rb, have average concentrations that exceed 100 parts per million (ppm). Conversely, certain TEs have an average range of 50–100 ppm, with some elements exceeding 10 ppm and others remaining below 10 ppm. To facilitate interpretation, this study focuses primarily on TEs detected above the detection limit, categorising them into three groups: i) transition trace elements (TTE), which include Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn; ii) large ion lithophile elements (LILE), encompassing U, Th, Sr, Ba, and Rb; and iii) high field strength elements (HFSE), such as Zr, Hf, Nb, and Y.

When compared to the typical composition of the upper continental crust (UCC) [114], the sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations exhibit elevated levels of TTE, LILE, and HFSE.

Sedimentary rock samples from both the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations possess elevated concentrations of transition trace elements (TTE) compared to the average upper continental crust (UCC) composition [114]. Notably, the increase in TTE content is more pronounced in mudstone samples than in sandstone samples (Table 4; in mudstones, V ranges from 0.44 to 1.19 with an average of 0.91; Co ranges from 0.65 to 1.61 with an average of 1.06; Ni ranges from 0.40 to 1.31 with an average of 0.80; Cu ranges from 0.31 to 2.56 with an average of 0.75; and Zn ranges from 0.64 to 2.25 with an average of 1.22). In the Sandakan Formation (Fig. 16), sandstone samples exhibited slightly lower TTE levels than the UCC, while mudstone samples had slightly higher TTE concentrations. Meanwhile, the Tanjong Formation's sandstone samples also had lower TTE concentrations than the UCC, indicating element depletion. However, the mudstones from the Tanjong Formation displayed TTE enrichment, with concentrations exceeding those of the UCC.

The concentrations of large-ion lithophile elements (LILE) in sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations are more enriched compared to the typical upper continental crust (UCC) composition [114]. However, the degree of enrichment varies within and between formations. However, distinctions surface when examining specific formations. In the Tanjong Formation, both sandstone and mudstone samples demonstrate LILE depletion, with sandstone displaying a more pronounced depletion. Conversely, sandstone samples display more pronounced LILE depletion within the Sandakan Formation.

The concentrations of high field-strength elements (HFSE) in both sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations are significantly higher than those observed in the upper continental crust (UCC) [114]. The concentrations of high field-strength elements (HFSE) in both sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations overall showed depletion than those observed in the upper continental crust (UCC) [114]. Sandstone samples consistently exhibit the lowest HFSE depletion across all formations, while mudstone samples show a slightly lower depletion than sandstone when assessed against the UCC (Table 4; in mudstone, Sr ranges from 0.30 to 0.57 with an average of 0.45, while in sandstone, Sr ranges from 0.12 to 0.31 with an average of 0.22). When examining individual formations (Fig. 16), both sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong Formation show overall HFSE depletion, with mudstones exhibiting a more pronounced depletion. In contrast, sandstone samples from the Sandakan Formation display more significant HFSE depletion compared to the UCC.

Rare earth elements

Tables S3 and 5 and Fig. 17 reveal that the concentrations of REEs in both sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations are slightly depleted compared to the typical composition of the upper continental crust (UCC) [114]. This depletion is particularly noticeable when comparing the formations. A detailed breakdown by lithology reveals clear patterns: sandstone samples consistently display REE depletion across all formations, with ΣREE ranging from 25.32 to 112.28 ppm and an average of 63.42 ppm, while mudstone samples consistently exhibit slight depletion, with ΣREE ranging from 76.41 to 170.10 ppm and an average of 114.59 ppm, indicating lower concentrations compared to the UCC (UCC, 146.40 ppm; [114]). Mudstone within the Tanjong Formation is more enriched than the sandstone (ΣREE ranging from 101.99 to 140.69 ppm and an average of 116.49 ppm for mudstone, and ΣREE ranging from 25.32 to 81.33 ppm and an average of 54.56 ppm for sandstone). Conversely, in the Sandakan Formation, sandstone samples exhibit slight REE enrichment compared to Tanjong Formation sandstone, while mudstone samples demonstrate an even higher degree of enrichment compared to sandstone samples (ΣREE ranging from 76.41 to 170.10 ppm and an average of 112.69 ppm for mudstone, and ΣREE ranging from 49.20 to 112.28 ppm and an average of 70.32 ppm for sandstone).

Discussions

Depositional settings

The Tanjong Formation deposits offer clear evidence of a marginal-marine environment influenced by varying tidal and fluvial processes within a delta plain setting (Fig. 19), as observed through facies analysis of the Bukit Garam TJG-1 and TJG-2 outcrops. TJG-1 outcrops depict a low-energy environment characterized by overbank deposits (FA1 and FA2) in interdistributary areas, resulting from distributary channel avulsion (FA3). These overbank deposits likely formed due to the immediate breach of fluvial processes by the active channels. The crevasse splay (FA1) serves as the primary sediment source for these overbank deposits, situated near the mouth bar of the crevasse splay, evident from the vertical change and thinning-out of thin sand sheets with wave accretion modifications. The presence of rhizolith and organic-rich mudstones in FA2 suggests waterlogged environments comprising interdistributary and distal standing water bodies, indicative of flood plain conditions resulting from overbanking FA3 distributary channels. The high proportion of sandstones in the distributary channel (FA3) serves as the primary sediment source in this depositional setting. The occurrences of reworked material like amber and rootlets in interdistributary bays or abandoned distributary channels (FA4) suggest fluvial influence.

A paleodepositional model for the investigated Tanjong Formation in the Northeast Sabah Basin regions illustrates different subenvironments within the delta plain setting, constructed using identified facies associations ranging from FA1 to FA4.

The Sandakan Formation was deposited in an area where river and marine processes interacted. Previous sedimentological studies have identified various environments, including fluvial, estuarine, marine, and shallow marine environments [24,25,117]. This study further emphasizes the presence of shoreface environments (Fig. 20), where deposition likely occurred within a supply-dominated regime. This resulted in the rapid spread of large volumes of mainly fine-grained sediment, as indicated by the shoreface facies associations (FA2 and FA3), possibly sourced from rivers across the shelf, forming thick, uniform accretionary units. Such characteristics are commonly observed in Highstand System tracts [118]. These deposits also exhibit evidence of mixed tide- and wave-influenced processes, indicated by low levels of bioturbation, the presence of tidal indicators, and hummocky cross-stratification, supporting a mixed energy coastal depositional model. The coarsening upward, wave-dominated shoreface facies successions are interpreted as representing barrier shoreface environments, consistent with those observed in the Nyalau Formation, Sarawak [119]. A shoreface system serves as an effective mechanism for the segregation of offshore processes, resulting in mud-dominated facies (FA1) associated with fluvial-tidal currents.

A paleodepositional model for the studied Sandakan Formation in the Northeast Sabah Basin areas delineates various subenvironments within the shoreface setting, constructed based on identified facies associations ranging from FA1 to FA3.

In terms of palynomorphs, the analyzed palynomorphs from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations are depicted in the vertical frequency distribution of key taxa (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 21, and Table S4), predominantly derived from the structureless and laminated mudstone facies, while the identified pollen grains mainly belong to taxa associated with rainforests and peat swamps. Families such as Arecaceae, Calamus, Euphorbiaceae, Loranthaceae, Meliaceae, Proteaceae, and Rubiaceae represent pollen grains typical of rainforests. Additionally, pollen grains from families like Alangium, Anacolosa, Blumeodendron, Calophyllum, Cephalomappa, Durio, Garcinia, Melanorrhoea, Palaquium, Stemonurus, and Timonius are indicative of peat swamp environments. The examined palynomorph assemblages consist of terrestrial palynofossils, suggesting a marginal marine depositional setting [120]. The interpreted delta plain environment reported in the Tanjong Formation at Bukit Garam TJG-1 and TJG-2 outcrops is characterized by fine-grained clastic sediments rich in plant-derived organic matter. Two types of vegetation are identified in this environment: 1) a combination of mangrove-associated taxa such as Rhizophora and Barringtonia, and 2) herbaceous and tree ferns commonly found in freshwater swamps, including Ceratopteris and Cyatheaceous trees. This observation is consistent with similar environments found locally in areas like the West Baram Delta Provinces, Sarawak [121,122], and regionally in the Makum Coalfield Delta floodplain in Northeast India [123], and Bohai Bay Basin in eastern China [124]. In the interpreted shoreface environment at the Taman Mutiara outcrop of the Sandakan Formation, the occurrence of coastal plants like Barringtonia, Dactylocladus type, Randia, and Terminalia suggests a transition zone between true terrestrial and marine environments. These findings are consistent with storm deposits observed in the Baram Delta Province, Sarawak [121,125].

Paleoenvironmental conditions

To construct the paleoenvironmental conditions, various geochemical proxies derived from TEs extracted from sandstone and mudstone facies of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations were analyzed. These proxies include assessments of (1) paleoredox conditions, (2) paleoclimate, and (3) sedimentation rate. Examining these established geochemical proxies and their correlations with the Total Organic Carbon (TOC) level could offer insights into the mechanisms governing the accumulation and preservation of organic matter within marginal marine environments (e.g., Refs. [32,126,127]).

Paleoredox condition

Various ratios of TEs, including Ni/Co, V/Cr, and U/Th, are reliable indicators for interpreting paleoredox conditions (e.g., Refs. [32,128,129]). Previous studies have established reference standards for these ratios, such as U/Th (oxic <0.75, dyoxic 0.75–1.25, anoxic >1.25), Ni/Co (oxic <5.0, dyoxic 5.0–7.0, anoxic >7.0), and V/Cr (oxic <2.0, dyoxic 2.0–4.25, anoxic >4.25), to distinguish between oxic, dysoxic (suboxic), and anoxic (reducing) conditions [[130], [131], [132], [133], [134]]. A binary plot by Ref. [131], depicting U/Th versus V/Cr, suggests a prevailing oxic environment during the deposition of these siliciclastic samples, although some fall within the dysoxic field (Fig. 22A and B). The U/Th ratios of sandstone and mudstone samples from the Tanjong Formation range from 0.20 to 0.26 and 0.20 to 0.27, respectively. For Sandakan's sandstone and mudstone samples, the ratios range from 0.19 to 0.28 and 0.20 to 0.31, respectively. Meanwhile, the V/Cr ratios of mudstone and sandstone samples range from 0.95 to 2.08 and 1.59 to 2.21, respectively, for the Tanjong Formation and from 1.16 to 3.00 and 1.54 to 2.10, respectively, for Sandakan Formation's sandstone and mudstone samples. According to the binary plot of [135], the projected Ni/Co against V/Cr ratios of both formations' sandstone and mudstone samples fluctuate from an oxic to a dysoxic environment (Fig. 22C and D), with Ni/Co ratios of sandstone and mudstone samples ranging from 0.47 to 1.82 and 1.85 to 3.77, respectively, for the Tanjong Formation, and from 0.33 to 1.46 and 1.88 to 3.22, respectively, for Sandakan's sandstone and mudstone samples. Utilizing similar ratio values, the U/Th versus Ni/Co binary plot by Ref. [131] indicates a predominant oxic climate during the deposition of these sandstone and mudstone samples from both formations (Fig. 22E and F). On the whole, the Tanjong and Sandakan siliciclastic facies exhibit fluctuating conditions ranging from dysoxic (suboxic) to oxic during deposition in marginal marine settings (Fig. 23, Fig. 24).

The depositional environments of both marginal marine deposits were significantly influenced by tectonic activity linked to the opening and closing of the Sulu Sea. Sedimentation occurred under dysoxic (suboxic) to oxic conditions, corresponding with periods of low sea levels during basin development. Tectonic movements, such as uplift and subsidence driven by the Cagayan and Sulu Arc volcanic systems, likely influenced these sea-level fluctuations during the Miocene. This volcanic activity, along with tectonic shifts, affected relative sea levels in the Northeast Sabah Basin, causing alternating episodes of shallowing and deepening, which resulted in mostly dysoxic to oxic conditions. The high abundance of rainforest and peat swamp pollen, along with the low presence of marine dinoflagellate cysts (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, and Table 8), suggests a more terrestrial environment with increased subaerial exposure during sea-level lowstands, indicating a setting near an active fluvio-deltaic source.

Paleoclimate

Two elemental groups are enriched during wet and arid climates, serving as climate climatic conditions. These climatic elemental groups can be expressed as Σ(V + Co + Fe + Mn + Cr + Ni)/Σ(Ba + Mg + Na + Ca + K + Sr) (referred to as the C–value), widely used for paleoclimate interpretation [136,137]. C–values are commonly employed to classify climates, with suggestions that C–values within the range of 0–0.2 indicate arid conditions, 0.2–0.4 suggest semiarid climates, 0.4–0.6 represent semiarid to semihumid conditions, 0.6–0.8 imply semihumid conditions and 0.8–1.0 indicate humid climates [136,137].

In this study, the focus is placed on mudstone data, as it is more representative of source rock and is a reliable proxy for paleoclimate and paleoenvironmental reconstructions. This approach is consistent with the original geochemical indicator studies, which were developed using fine-grained sediments like mudstones. Including sandstone data may introduce inconsistencies, as sandstone reflects depositional processes more than paleoclimatic signals.

The calculated C–values for the Tanjong Formation mudstones range from 0.49 to 1.04, with a mean value of 0.72, while those for the Sandakan Formation range from 0.39 to 1.04, with an average of 0.74. These values suggest a predominantly semihumid paleoclimate during deposition, with some minor evidence of semiarid conditions.Another proxy used to infer past climatic conditions is the Sr/Cu ratio (e.g., Refs. [32,138,139]). Sedimentary rocks with fine-grained clastic composition and Sr/Cu ratios falling between 1.3 and 5.0 are typically associated with warm and humid climates. Conversely, Sr/Cu ratios exceeding 5.0 suggest deposition in hot and arid climates [32,138,139]. By focusing on mudstone samples, potential inconsistencies from including sandstone data are minimized. In the Tanjong Formation, Sr/Cu ratios in mudstones range from 0.58 to 3.27, with an average of 2.38, while in the Sandakan Formation, values range from 1.46 to 3.26, averaging 2.22. These ratios indicate predominantly warm and humid paleoclimatic conditions during deposition.Overall, the C-values and Sr/Cu ratios from mudstone samples suggest that the paleoclimate during the deposition of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations was predominantly semihumid, with limited intervals of warm conditions in the Northeast Sabah Basin during the Miocene. (Fig. 23, Fig. 24).

Sedimentation rate

The qualitative analysis of REE distribution patterns and (La/Yb)N ratios is commonly employed to determine sedimentation rates [136]. The sedimentation rate is crucial for the accumulation of organic matter (OM) [136,[140], [141], [142]]. In considering post-depositional processes, diagenesis typically leads to Ce enrichment and Eu depletion, resulting in negative correlations between Ce/Ce∗ and Eu/Eu∗ [143]. However, such correlations are not observed in this study. As shown in Table 5, the Ce/Ce∗ values for Tanjong Formation mudstones range from 0.45 to 0.63, with an average of 0.52, while Sandakan Formation mudstones show values ranging from 0.31 to 0.71, with an average of 0.47. Similarly, the Eu/Eu∗ values for Tanjong Formation mudstones range from 0.50 to 0.68 (average: 0.57), and for Sandakan Formation mudstones, from 0.37 to 0.88 (average: 0.54). These results suggest only minor diagenetic alteration of Ce/Ce∗ and Eu/Eu∗ ratios.

Higher sedimentation rates frequently result in limited REE fractionation due to the transient interaction between clay minerals and REEs [144,145], resulting in (La/Yb)N ratios close to 1 (where the subscript “N” denotes normalization to UCC in this study) [146]. As illustrated in Fig. 17, the REE distribution patterns, particularly for mudstone samples from both the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations, are relatively flat, indicating that sedimentation rates were relatively stable during the deposition of mudstones in the Northeast Sabah region. Moreover, the calculated (La/Yb)N ratios for mudstone samples from the Tanjong Formation exhibit a range from 0.29 to 0.44, with a mean value of 0.36. In contrast, mudstone samples from the Sandakan Formation display (La/Yb)N ratios ranging from 0.20 to 0.44, averaging 0.31. These values suggest a relatively low sedimentation rate in both formations. Consequently, the accumulation of organic matter (OM) in the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations likely underwent extended decomposition periods, which may have facilitated OM preservation. This preservation contributes to the fair source rock potential observed (Table 7), potentially linked to dysoxic (suboxic) to oxic conditions prevailing during deposition.The Th/U ratio is also indicative of sedimentation rate dynamics [126,147], with higher ratios suggesting faster sedimentation rates. As shown in Table 6, the Th/U ratios for mudstone samples from the Tanjong Formation range from 0.08 to 0.44, with an average of 0.34. For the Sandakan Formation, mudstone samples exhibit Th/U ratios ranging from 0.19 to 0.44, averaging 0.31. These findings indicate that sediment deposition during the formation of both the Tanjong and Sandakan formations was relatively consistent and reflected a low sedimentation rate.

Overall, the REE distribution patterns and calculated (La/Yb)N and Th/U ratios in siliciclastic samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations exhibit a relatively consistent profile (Fig. 23, Fig. 24).

Palynofacies evidence

The palynofacies study of the mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations revealed two distinct fields were identified based on the classification by Refs. [53,54]: Field II (marginal dysoxic-anoxic) and Field IV (shelf to basin). The majority of samples fell within the marginal dysoxic-anoxic condition rather than the shelf-to-basin condition (Fig. 23, Fig. 24, and Table 8).

The prevalence of phytoclasts in mudstone samples from both formations influenced the classification of palynofacies, placing them in Field II and indicating deposition in marginal dysoxic-anoxic basin conditions [69]. These phytoclasts are primarily opaque equidimensional and lath (rectangular-shaped), accounting for an average of 60 % in the Tanjong Formation and 61 % in the Sandakan Formation, respectively, compared to non-opaque phytoclasts (averaging 12 % for the Tanjong Formation and 9 % for the Sandakan Formation) (Fig. 25, Fig. 26, and Table 8). This suggests the influence of nearby active fluvio-deltaic source areas [53]. The increased occurrence of opaque phytoclasts in such settings implies that terrestrial phytoclast particles have undergone significant oxidation, most likely under dynamic littoral conditions where tidal activity and water table fluctuations are the primary controlling processes [69]. This finding is consistent with the reported sedimentary facies in both formations, distinguished by intercalations of fine siltstones and sandstones, as well as heterolithic facies (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Additionally, the minimal presence of marine dinoflagellate cysts compared to plant spores suggests a high terrestrial influx (pollen grains with 10 % average in Tanjong Formation, while 11 % average in Sandakan Formation), likely during a phase of sea level lowstand, further indicating a proximal setting near an active fluvio-deltaic source due to the short transport distance of plant spores [53,148,149].

On the other hand, mudstone samples with moderate to high average values of terrestrial phytoclasts, as well as low to moderate proportions of amorphous organic matter (AOM) and palynomorphs are categorized into palynofacies fields IVa (Fig. 25, Fig. 26, and Table 8). These palynofacies fields indicate deposition in a transition zone from shelf to basin, reflecting increased basin subsidence and water column depth [53,69]. Field IVa signifies deposition under intensified dysoxic to suboxic redox conditions [53]. Meanwhile, mudstone samples falling within Field IVa from both formations exhibit average AOM proportions of 17 % in the Tanjong Formation and 16 % in the Sandakan Formation. This suggests deposition in a relatively proximal, moderate-energy, oxygenated environment [53,148,149], consistent with the preceding discussion of dysoxic to oxic conditions based on calculated TE proxies. This interpretation is further supported by the low abundance of dinoflagellate cysts and the presence of low amounts of pores and pollen grains (Fig. 25, Fig. 26, and Table 8), implying a slightly proximal inner neritic shelf environment [[150], [151], [152]].

The occurrence of opaque phytoclasts in sedimentary samples frequently implies exposure to sub-aerial oxidation during seasonal water column fluctuations and oxidation during transportation [53,69]. The low to moderate fluorescent intensity and percentage of AOM suggest preservation under oxygenated conditions, with plant pollen and spores indicating moderate proximity to fluvio-deltaic sources [53,69]. Many organic matter components have undergone selective degradation in the oxidizing environments represented by Fields II and IVa (Fig. 25), leading to high proportions of small opaque or semi-opaque and brown phytoclasts [53,69,153]. Consequently, this contributes to both formations' relatively low TOC values (Fig. 18), indicating poor to fair source rock potential. Overall, the high abundance of opaque phytoclasts and the low to moderate presence of AOM and palynomorphs shape the organic matter composition in the source rocks of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations, with the analyzed type III and IV kerogen suggested to have originated from terrestrial sources with high gas and low oil potential (Fig. 18).

Tectonic signature

To acquire a better understanding of the tectonic context of the studied marginal marine deposits, this study employs TE discrimination diagrams proposed by Ref. [154], utilizing elements such as La, Th, Sc, Th, Co, Zr/10, Th, Sc, Zr/10, and Sc/Cr vs. La/Y (Fig. 27). These elements are highly resistant to weathering and remain relatively immobile during erosion and transportation, making them valuable tools for discerning tectonic environments [30,155].

Ternary and binary discrimination diagrams developed by Ref. [154] are employed to visualize the tectonic settings for marginal marine sequences found in the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations. These diagrams include (a) La/Th/Sc, (b) Th/Co/Zr/10, (c) Th/Sc/Zr/10, and (d) Sc/Cr vs La/Y.

The ternary diagrams in Fig. 27 illustrate that the mudstone samples from the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations predominantly fall within the field associated with active continental margins. In contrast, most sandstone samples from both formations are positioned within the field of passive margin settings.

The tectonic interpretation based on ternary discrimination diagrams for the mudstone samples indicates an active continental margin setting (Fig. 27) consistent with the findings by Ref. [154], characterised by higher concentrations of Th and Zr. The suggested tectonic setting aligns with previous geological models describing the marginal basin setting of the Tanjong and Sandakan Formations. This setting is linked to the intra-arc rifting of the Sulu Sea during the Early to Middle Miocene [6,41,42], which resulted in the formation of extensive mélange deposits from olistostrome flows into the active rift (Fig. 28; [2]). The geochemical data for the sandstone samples, however, appear inconsistent, as they suggest a passive margin tectonic setting (Fig. 27), characterized by lower Sc concentrations, consistent with the findings of [154]. This inconsistency between the previously inferred tectonic settings and the geochemical interpretations, particularly for the sandstone samples, may be due to the recycled nature of passive margin sediments, which are associated with the early development of the "Rajang Group Accretionary Complex" [1,43].

Schematic cross-sections depict the active rifting phase of the Sulu Sea during the Early to Middle Miocene, extending inland into Sabah (modified after [2]). Following this period, during the Middle to late Miocene, the quartz-rich Tanjong and Sandakan formations developed marginal marine systems over the olistostrome deposits, contributing sediment to the Northeast Sabah Sub-Basins.

Most TEs tend to become immobile during weathering and erosion, with some falling into the category of compatible elements, such as Sc, Ni, Cr, and Co, which preferentially associate with mafic rocks. Conversely, incompatible elements like La, Th, Hf, Zr, and REE are typically concentrated in acidic rocks [156].

The Tanjong Formation has a La/Sc ratio of 0.99–5.26, with an average of 3.06, while the Sandakan Formation has a ratio of 1.51–4.03, with an average of 2.42. Similarly, Th/Co ratios range from 0.25 to 0.98, with an average of 0.55 for the Tanjong Formation, and 0.71 to 6.29, with an average of 3.03 wt% for the Sandakan Formation. These ratios position both formations within areas associated with felsic volcanic rocks; however, a few sandstone samples from the Sandakan Formation display an affinity for basaltic rocks (Fig. 29A and B). Additionally, a binary plot of La/Th versus Th/Yb, as suggested by Ref. [114], aids in discerning the felsic and mafic characteristics of parent rocks. The studied samples exhibit relatively low La/Th ratios (<5.0) and average Th/Yb values of 5.96 for the Tanjong Formation and 15.28 for the Sandakan Formation. This interpretation implies that both formations originated predominantly from mafic sources, most likely volcanic rocks (Fig. 29C and D). Using the discrimination diagram proposed by Ref. [157], La/Th ratios versus Hf are used to discern between different sources, mainly intermediate (andesite) and mafic (basalt) sources. The Hf content ranged from 0.77 to 2.61 ppm, with an average of 1.52 ppm, indicating that the rocks most likely originated from felsic and intermediate source rocks (Fig. 29E and F).

Identifying the felsic and mafic source rocks or the origin of Tanjong and Sandakan Formations siliciclastic samples. (A, B) La/Sc versus Th/Co plot (adapted from Ref. [159]). (C, D) Th/Yb versus La/Th plot (adapted from Ref. [114]). (E, F) Hf (ppm) versus La/Th plot (adapted from Ref. [157]).

The varied composition of the source area implies that the hinterland consists of rocks with diverse origins and compositions. Siliciclastic sediments were likely transported into the basin by the fluvial system, possibly from the ancestral Kinabatangan River and deltas, originating from the hinterland, notably the "Rajang Group Accretionary Complex." This complex corresponds to rocks from the pre-Sulu Sea rifting ophiolite-deepwater sediments, which were obducted due to southward subduction involving the Miri Zone of Borneo and the adjacent Dangerous Grounds terrain of the Proto-South China Sea crust [1,43]. These siliciclastic sediments may have been eroded directly from the source region into the fluvial system and subsequently deposited into the Northeast Sabah sub-basins (Central Sabah and Sandakan Sub-Basins) before being distributed offshore through various submarine gravity-flow processes. The mafic source material (Fig. 29A, B, C, D) likely originated from volcanic eruptions of the onshore Cagayan and Sulu Arcs (Fig. 28), eroding extensive mélange deposits such as the Garinono, Kuamut, and Ayer Mélanges. The presence of acidic source rocks (Fig. 29E and F) is possibly linked to felsic intrusions in the Segama Valley region, suggesting an extensive continental basement beneath the ophiolite. This basement likely represents mantle-derived arc magmatism, part of a continuation of the Mesozoic Paleo-Pacific continental arc that extended through SW Borneo, Sundaland, Vietnam, and SE China [13,158].

Conclusion

Sedimentological, TE, REE, and palynology analyses of siliciclastic sedimentary facies of the Early to Late Miocene Tanjong and Sandakan marginal marine deposits in Northeast Sabah were combined to refine the paleodepositional model, investigate palaeodepositional conditions, tectonic settings, and type, origin, and maturation of organic matter.

Ternary diagrams of immobile trace elements (La/Th/Sc, Th/Co/Zr/10, Th/Sc/Zr/10, and Sc/Cr vs. La/Y) indicate active continental margins from mudstone samples, attributed to the Sulu Sea intra-arc rifting during the Early to Middle Miocene. In contrast, the sandstone samples show some influence of passive margin settings, possibly related to the recycled nature of passive margin sediments associated with the early development of the "Rajang Group Accretionary Complex." The La/Sc vs. Th/Co, Th/Yb vs. La/Th, and Hf (ppm) vs. La/Th plots for mudstone samples reveal intermediate and mafic sources, supporting the idea of ophiolite-deepwater sediment uplift and erosional events, as well as the onshore eruptions of the Cagayan and Sulu Arc volcanic systems. The sandstone samples, sourced from acidic rocks, may be linked to felsic intrusions in the Segama Valley region, suggesting an extensive continental basement beneath the ophiolite. This basement likely represents mantle-derived arc magmatism and is part of the Mesozoic Paleo-Pacific continental arc.
