Value‐based prices of emerging disease‐modifying therapies for Alzheimer's disease in 174 countries: a cost‐effectiveness and threshold analysis
Authors: Men Thi Hoang, James G. Kahn, Sanjib Saha, Dominic Trépel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCountries have varying, limited healthcare budgets for emerging disease‐modifying therapies. Cost‐effectiveness analysis, combined with country‐level cost‐effectiveness thresholds, can be used to estimate value‐based prices (VBPs) for lecanemab and donanemab across 174 countries.METHODSThe cost‐effectiveness of lecanemab and donanemab was estimated using incremental cost and quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) compared to usual care. Published cost‐effectiveness thresholds were used to estimate VBPs of these drugs across 174 countries.RESULTSCompared to usual care, lecanemab and donanemab respectively increased average QALYs by 0.38 and 0.51. By country income status, VBPs for lecanemab and donanemab respectively ranged between $254 to $9434 and $387 to $13,964 (high income), $90 to $1025 and $137 to $1507 (upper middle income), $11 to $623 and $21 to $956 (lower middle income) and $4 to $18 and $9 to $32 (low income).DISCUSSIONVBPs indicate how much 174 countries should be willing to pay. This framework can also be adapted and refined in the negotiation of country pricing.

BACKGROUND

For decades, available drugs for Alzheimer's disease (AD) offered only limited symptomatic relief, without affecting disease progression. 1 , 2 , 3 Recently, innovative disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) targeting amyloid beta (Aβ) have been developed to alter AD's trajectory. 4 , 5 , 6 However, considering constrained health budgets, access to emerging DMTs for AD is increasingly likely to depend on their pricing.

Recapping recent global events for DMTs, in June 2021, the first DMT, aducanumab (at $56,000 per annum), gained accelerated approval by the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA); however, after EMERGE and ENGAGE trials yielded insignificant results, in January 2024, it was withdrawn from the US market. 7 , 8 Lecanemab was then approved in July 2023 in the US ($26,500). In what appears to be a rush‐to‐market scenario, this was followed by Japan ($20,500), China ($8,970), and, where prices remain unclear, South Korea, Hong Kong, Israel, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the United Kingdom (UK), Mexico, Macau, Oman, Taiwan, and the European Union (EU). 9 , 10 , 11 In July 2024, donanemab, another Aβ antibody, was granted FDA approval ($32,000), followed by Japan, China, the UK, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Singapore, Taiwan, Brazil, Mexico, and Australia. 5 , 12 , 13

Having secured footholds in certain markets, DMTs appear now to be seeking market access in countries where approval processes require evidence of both clinical and cost‐effectiveness. 14 However, their high prices and severe adverse effects are emerging as sources of concern for market approval. Notably, in July 2024, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) rejected lecanemab's licensing application due to serious amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which are associated with an increased risk of brain bleeds and can lead to treatment discontinuation. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) also recommended against the approval of lecanemab and donanemab, citing costs that do not justify the health benefits. 19 , 20 This is because each country has a cost‐effective threshold (CET) that can justify the incremental health benefits, and guidelines vary across countries. 21 Given observed trends in DMT pricing, it seems unlikely that they may be considered cost‐effective and may ultimately not be reimbursed in many markets. Alternatively, where market access is achieved, current DMT prices may limit use to those with greater coverage.

Pharmaceutical companies set prices to recover research and development costs and secure market returns, while healthcare systems negotiate for affordability, cost‐effectiveness, and budget sustainability. Global dementia spending reached $263 billion in 2019 and is expected to rise sharply with aging populations and new DMTs. 22 Policies that assess value and set appropriate pricing structures are, therefore, vital to ensure access to innovative treatments.

Cost‐effectiveness analysis (CEA) is a method used to assess the “value for money” of interventions by comparing net costs to net health benefits, commonly expressed in quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). 23 Value‐based prices (VBPs) align the market price of an intervention with its estimated health benefits to the healthcare system and society, aiming to set a price commensurate with the local ability to invest in health, expressed through the country‐level CETs. 21 CEAs and VBPs are crucial tools for evaluating the cost‐effectiveness of drugs and setting their prices. VBPs, therefore, could help accelerate access to innovative DMTs for AD, where the value of effective DMTs is high; yet the lack of financial resources in many countries poses accessibility challenges.

This paper aims to demonstrate how DMTs could offer an order of magnitude more health benefits than usual care. To inform markets where lecanemab and donanemab are seeking regulatory approval, threshold analysis based on country‐level CETs is conducted to provide VBP estimates for these two drugs across 174 countries. Highly customized CEAs in specific countries are ideal when possible. However, developing countries with limited health economic capacity face challenges in conducting CEAs and implementing value‐based pricing. Our study, therefore, provides initial estimates and an adaptable framework that can be refined with country‐specific inputs as they become available to further refine the estimates.

METHODS

Overview

A cohort Markov model was developed to simulate the costs and health outcomes of three treatment pathways over a lifetime horizon: (1) lecanemab plus usual care (UC), (2) donanemab plus UC, and (3) UC alone. 24

The effectiveness outcomes were estimated in terms of QALYs and the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) for the two drugs by comparing their incremental QALYs and costs relative to UC.

The base case analysis, which considered a healthcare perspective (including long‐term care) and a societal perspective (incorporating costs and consequences related to informal caregivers), was examined in the scenario analysis. To account for future values, an annual discount rate of 3% was applied to both costs and consequences.

The base‐case CEA first used data from the United States to estimate outcomes in the United States. Then, for the remaining 173 countries, cost parameters were weighted and adjusted by using health expenditure per capita. 25 Age‐ and sex‐dependent mortality tables from 174 countries were used to estimate the number of deaths annually for each country. 26 , 27

For the threshold analysis to estimate VBPs, country‐level CETs were adopted from a recent analysis of healthcare spending growth and QALY gained. 28 The Excel Goal Seek function was used to calculate VBPs, ensuring that the estimated ICER equaled the country‐level CETs.

Visual Basic for Applications (VBA) in Microsoft Excel was employed to perform a random‐sample analysis in a Monte Carlo simulation for probabilistic CEA.

Model description

A cohort Markov model depicting AD progression with five health states (mild cognitive impairment due to AD [MCI‐AD], mild AD, moderate AD, severe AD, and dead) was the basis for CEA. The model simulated progression in 1‐year cycles until participants either died or reached 100 years old. The model structure is illustrated in Figure 1.

Schematic diagram of model structure and transition pathways.

Within each 1‐year cycle, state‐specific transition probabilities determined whether individuals remained in their current state, transitioned to a different alive state, or moved to the dead state. With reference to the best available evidence, each state was then specified to incur specific costs, health‐state utilities, relative risk (RR) of death, and transition probabilities to long‐term care according to disease severity.

Population

A hypothetical cohort of 70‐year‐olds with MCI‐AD (55%) and mild AD (45%) was entered into the model and assigned to three arms: lecanemab, donanemab, or UC. Age, sex, allocation of the population between MCI‐AD and mild AD states and between different care settings were aligned with amyloid‐positive individuals who received long‐term care in the literature and clinical trials. 4 , 5 , 29 , 30

Treatment efficacy

In line with existing clinical evidence that DMTs do not benefit patients in moderate or severe stages, we assumed that two drugs were effective in patients with MCI‐AD and mild AD states.

To reflect the drugs’ efficacy, the hazard ratio (HR) of reducing disease progression of the two DMTs was incorporated into the model. The HR for DMTs was similar to the relative changes in the measurement of the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) in the trials. For lecanemab and donanemab, the HRs were 0.69 (0.57 to 0.83) 4 and 0.6 (0.473 to 0.748), respectively. 5

Transition probabilities

Transition across alive states and mortality probabilities

The annual transition probabilities between the four alive states were initially derived from a study of AD progression in amyloid‐positive individuals. 29 However, the Potashman et al. study 29 included the asymptomatic state, which was not included in our model; and because of discrepancies in age and sex (factors known to influence mortality), these transition probabilities required recalibration. Specifically, the transition to asymptomatic and death states were excluded, and the remaining probabilities were adjusted to be conditional on survival within the MCI‐AD, mild AD, moderate AD, and severe AD states, as follows: TransitionprobabilityfromAtoBconditionalonfourstates=TransitionprobabilityfromstateAtostateBSurvivalprobabilitywithinfourstatesfromstateA

These transition probabilities were applied to the UC arm. The transition probabilities for the lecanemab and donanemab arms were adjusted based on treatment efficacy. Specifically, the transition from MCI‐AD and mild AD to more severe states were updated by applying the HR of each DMT. The estimates are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

The probability of moving from an alive state to death in each cycle depended on age, sex, and the alive health state of the preceding cycle. Mortality probabilities, adjusted for age and sex, obtained from life tables of 174 countries, were multiplied by the AD‐state‐specific relative risk (RR) of death, 26 , 27 , 31 using the formula: 1−e−RR×age−andsex−specificannualdeathprobability

Transition probabilities to long‐term care

The annual probabilities of transitioning to long‐term care, varying by health state, were obtained from the literature. 32 , 33 Transition probabilities for moving to long‐term care by stage and progression thereafter were assumed to remain constant. Additionally, individuals could move from community to long‐term care; however, once in long‐term care, they were unable to revert to the community and remained there until their death.

Adverse events (AEs)

The occurrence of ARIA was a significant AE associated with receiving DMTs. The probabilities of any ARIA and symptomatic ARIA occurrences are currently a key differentiator between DMTs and were derived from specific clinical trials. 4 , 5 All AEs were modeled solely in the initial cycle, aligning with clinical evidence indicating that the most severe AEs likely occurred within 6 months of receiving DMTs. 33

Treatment discontinuation

Treatment discontinuation due to serious AEs from two trials of lecanemab and donanemab was used to calculate the probabilities of drug discontinuation. 4 , 5 Treatment discontinuation was modeled based on severe AEs within the first cycle. Individuals who discontinued treatment received UC and neither acquired benefits nor incurred costs related to DMTs.

Additionally, treatments were discontinued once patients progressed to the moderate or severe stages, in alignment with the efficacy of DMTs.

Costs

The primary cost categories comprised costs for treatment acquisition, administration and monitoring, managing AEs, and community and long‐term care costs. We relied on US costs and then adjusted for other countries using health expenditures per capita as described in the “Threshold analysis” section below. All costs were adjusted to 2023 prices using the US Consumer Price Index (CPI) for medical care. 34 Cost inputs were reported in US dollars (USD) and described in the following details.

US market prices of DMTs: In the base‐case analysis, the annual price of lecanemab was $26,500 per year, while the price of donanemab was $32,000. 12 , 35

Administration costs: Both DMTs were administered intravenously for lecanemab and donanemab, respectively, every 2 and 4 weeks. The unit cost of $64.72 per administration (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System [HCPCS] Code: 96365) was applied to calculate administration costs. 36

Monitoring costs: Patients prescribed lecanemab and donanemab underwent four brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, respectively, within the first year to monitor for ARIA. The cost for each MRI scan was $337.52 (HCPCS code: 70553). 36

Adverse event costs: All patients who experienced AEs underwent two additional MRI scans to manage ARIA. If patients suffered from symptomatic ARIA, they received one physician visit and three methylprednisolone IV infusions. Patients with severe symptomatic ARIA were prescribed an additional seven prednisolone oral tablets. The unit costs for a physician visit, infusion, methylprednisolone IV, and prednisolone were $112.84 (HCPCS code: 99203), $64.72 (HCPCS Code: 96365), 36 $63.13, and $43.38, respectively. 37 , 38

Community care cost: Annual average medical costs, encompassing hospital inpatient and outpatient services, emergency department visits, and ambulatory care, were derived from a population‐based study of AD patients aged 70 to 90 in the United States. 39 This study calculated the annual costs for cognitively normal individuals, those with MCI, newly diagnosed dementia patients, and those with prevalent dementia. We assumed that the newly diagnosed dementia corresponded to mild AD and that prevalent dementia corresponded to moderate and severe AD. This study's estimates were used to calculate AD state‐specific multipliers based on cognitively normal, which were then applied to health expenditures for the US general population.

Besides lecanemab and donanemab, 33.3% of individuals with MCI and mild AD received donepezil 10 mg once daily ($14.29 per month), and 33.3% of patients with moderate and severe AD received memantine 10 mg twice daily ($50.3 per month). 40 , 41 , 42

Long‐term care costs: Additional costs associated with long‐term care settings were incurred once individuals transitioned to long‐term care. The 2023 annual median cost of a semi‐private room in a US nursing home facility ($104,025) was retrieved from Genworth's Cost of Care survey tool. 43 Other long‐term care costs for patients were assumed to be the same as those for community care.

Effectiveness (QALYs)

To estimate QALYs, health state utility values were derived from the EuroQol five‐dimension questionnaire (EQ‐5D), identified in a previous systematic review as the most reliable utility‐based instrument for individuals with dementia. 44 Utility values specific to AD severity were sourced from a meta‐regression of EQ‐5D data reported in a systematic review. 45 In the absence of setting‐specific estimates, the same utility values were applied across both community and long‐term care settings. The assigned utilities for individuals with MCI‐AD, mild AD, moderate AD, and severe AD were 0.80, 0.74, 0.59, and 0.36, respectively. 45 Additionally, a disutility of −0.14 was applied in the first model cycle to account for the temporary impact of symptomatic ARIA. 33 These utility values were used to adjust survival time in each health state, enabling the calculation of QALYs in the model.

Threshold analysis

Threshold analysis was performed to determine the prices at which lecanemab and donanemab would achieve cost‐effectiveness, matching the cost‐effectiveness thresholds observed in each of 174 countries. Country‐level thresholds were derived from recent figures published in The Lancet Global Health. 28

To account for healthcare cost variations between the United States and other countries, costs were adjusted using health expenditures per capita data across 174 countries (USD, 2020): 25 Costweight=HealthexpenditurepercapitaofcountryHealthexpenditurepercapitaofUS

Following this, country‐specific cost weights were multiplied by all cost parameters (excluding drug acquisition costs) to estimate the total cost of DMTs across 174 countries.

Also, QALY gains were adjusted using country‐specific annual mortality probabilities, accounting for sex and age differences to reflect demographic variations across jurisdictions. 27 These adjusted cost parameters and life‐year gains were used to calculate country‐specific ICERs.

Finally, the standard Excel “Goal Seek” function was used to determine the drug acquisition costs for lecanemab and donanemab, ensuring that the country‐level ICERs of each drug were equal to the specified national CE thresholds.

Uncertainty analyses

Scenario and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess how variations in base‐case assumptions and parameter values would affect the results.

Scenario 1: This scenario considered a societal perspective that accounted for the spillover costs and effects associated with informal caregivers. We assumed a 1:1 ratio between patients and their corresponding informal caregivers.

Caregivers’ productivity costs: The time caregivers spent providing care to individuals with MCI and mild, moderate, and severe AD was obtained from the literature. Monthly caregiving time for MCI due to AD and mild, moderate, and severe AD were 69, 113, 169, and 298 h, respectively. 46 , 47 To account for differences in caregiver time by national income level, we applied the ratios of mean hours providing activities of daily living support across high‐income countries (HICs), upper‐middle‐income countries (UMICs), lower‐middle‐income countries (LRMICs), and low‐income countries (LICs) from Table 8 in the World Health Organization (WHO) dementia report 202148 to adjust for caregiver time across 174 countries. Specifically, caregiving time estimates were adjusted using multipliers of 1.00 for HICs, 1.178 for UMICs, 1.333 for LRMICs, and 1.778 for LICs.

Subsequently, the 2023 average hourly earnings of $33.44 were used to estimate caregiver productivity costs in the United States. 49 For the remaining 173 countries, we assigned a weighted wage to reflect the differences in earnings between these countries and the United States, using the most up‐to‐date average monthly earnings available. 50 Country−levelwageweight=AveragemonthlyearningsineachcountryAveragemonthlyearningsintheUS

Informal caregiving time in long‐term care was assumed to be 44% of the time in community care. 24 , 33 Additionally, we assumed that caregivers in the community spent 2 h per infusion session. This time included travel to the clinic, waiting during the patient's infusion, and possibly staying afterwards to monitor for any delayed side effects from the injection.

Caregivers’ utilities: The utility of the general population aged 65 to 70 (0.824) was used as a proxy for caregivers, 51 from which disutility values of −0.03, −0.05, −0.08, and −0.10 were applied for MCI‐AD, mild AD, moderate AD, and severe AD, respectively, to estimate caregivers’ utility values. 33

RESULTS

This section briefly presents the results of the base‐case CEA, followed by VBPs and uncertainty analyses.

Base‐case CEA

This CEA used market prices of $26,500 for lecanemab and $32,000 for donanemab. Considering simulated total incremental costs (i.e., prices of DMTs plus other healthcare costs, adjusted for variations in healthcare costs between the United States and other countries), Japan emerged with the highest incremental costs ($111,298 and $134,882, respectively) and incremental QALYs (0.47 and 0.64, respectively) for both drugs. By contrast, the Central African Republic (CAR) had the lowest incremental costs ($78,457 and $94,016, respectively) and QALYs (0.26 and 0.34, respectively). The highest ICERs were in CAR ($300,541/QALY for lecanemab and $272,944/QALY for donanemab). The lowest ICERs were in Peru for lecanemab ($235,100/QALY) and Canada for donanemab ($209,876/QALY). For further results of CEA, see Tables S2 and S3.

Value‐based prices

Overall, VBPs required to achieve CE thresholds were substantially below the current prices of DMTs. The prices of DMTs varied significantly across income levels, depending on health expenditures per capita and country‐specific CETs. The price of lecanemab and donanemab, respectively, ranged between $254 to $9434 and $387 to $13,964 in HICs, $90 to $1025 and $137 to $1507 in UMICs, $11 to $623 and $21 to $956 in LRMICs and $4 to $18 and $9 to $32 in LICs (see Table S5 for specific country values). Switzerland had the highest price for lecanemab ($9434), while the United States had the highest price for donanemab, $13,964.

Notably, at the current market US prices and considering the CET of $95,958, lecanemab and donanemab exceed the VBPs by 182% and 129%, respectively. Similarly, the market price of lecanemab in Japan is approximately five times higher than its VBP, while it is 19 times higher in China.

Sensitivity analysis

Scenario analysis

Adopting a societal perspective increased VBPs by up to 112% for donanemab, whereas lecanemab VBPs increased by approximately 85%. The highest VBPs for both drugs were in Switzerland (lecanemab: $17,778; donanemab: $27,988), the United States (lecanemab: $17,257; donanemab: $26,829), and Norway (lecanemab: $14,457; donanemab: $23,394) (Table S5). Several LRMICs had negative prices when the societal perspective was applied. Caregivers’ productivity loss associated with infusion time was the primary driver of cross‐country variation in VBPs.

In the most optimistic scenario, assuming a 2‐year maximum treatment duration with sustained efficacy, the prices of lecanemab and donanemab rose by an average of 139% and 137%, respectively, driven primarily by the halving of treatment costs. By contrast, assuming 2‐year treatment with efficacy ceasing after 2 years, the average VPBs for lecanemab and donanemab decreased by approximately 13% and 8%, respectively.

Conversely, in the scenario assuming mandatory use of PET scans for monitoring patients on DMTs, the prices of lecanemab and donanemab were 53% and 68% of the base‐case VBPs, respectively. This reduction was driven by the substantial increase in total costs resulting from the mandatory use of PET scans.

Notably, using CETs equal to GDP per capita substantially increased the VBPs of the two drugs, particularly in LRMICs and LICs, due to the higher threshold values. In HICs, compared to the base case, the mean VBPs for lecanemab and donanemab increased by 65% and 58%, respectively. In LICs, the VBPs rose more than seven‐fold for lecanemab and nearly six‐fold for donanemab.

VBPs in four other scenarios were similar to the base case. Further details of these scenarios are provided in Supplemental S3 and Tables S6 and S7.

Impact of person and public involvement

A societal perspective was undertaken to examine the VBPs of DMTs, based on public input from members of the Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative. Scenario 7 was performed separately for LRMICs and LICs, reflecting feedback on the unavailability of long‐term care facilities in these regions and the dynamic care settings worldwide. The paper was revised for clarity in response to comments from health economists at the EuHEA seminar.

DISCUSSION

This study presents the first global analysis of value‐based pricing of DMTs for AD. At current prices, neither drug would be cost‐effective in 174 countries. There is a substantial discrepancy in VBPs between the top HICs and the rest. Adopting a societal perspective resulted in increases of up to 112% and 85% in the VBPs of donanemab and lecanemab, respectively. As current prices may prohibit global market access and regulatory approval, our results suggest that adopting value‐based pricing will increase access to these emerging therapies worldwide.

Our findings are consistent with prior analyses indicating that neither lecanemab nor donanemab is cost‐effective at current prices in the United States and Japan. 33 , 37 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 For lecanemab in the United States, our estimated VBP of $9388 closely aligns with the $8900 estimate reported by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review. 33 The lowest published VBP for lecanemab was $5100, reported by Nguyen et al., 52 while the highest estimate, $18,709, was from an updated study sponsored by the manufacturer. 53 In Japan, this study estimated the VBP of lecanemab at $4308, whereas a manufacturer‐sponsored study reported a price of ¥1,636,827 ($10,843). 55 For donanemab, our estimated VBP was $12,432 compared to $17,000 in a study by Ross et al. 54 and substantially lower than the $44,691 estimated from a manufacturer‐sponsored CEA. 56

Several factors explain those price discrepancies. First, our study used a newly published threshold of $95,958 per QALY gained in the United States and $39,190 in Japan; whereas others adopted an unofficial threshold of $100,000/QALY gained in the United States and ¥5,000,000 ($32,500) in Japan for lecanemab and a threshold of $150,000/QALY gained for donanemab in Ross et al. 54 Second, variations in model structure and input data contributed to differing results. Third, the study by Nguyen et al. integrated testing to identify eligible patients, incurring additional costs that would need to be offset by the lower price of lecanemab, whereas ours and other studies did not include testing costs.

Our study highlights significant disparities in drug prices across high‐, middle‐, and low‐income countries, providing decision makers with value‐based estimates to facilitate price negotiations. Assuming an annual price of $1000, approximately 24% of countries can afford lecanemab and 30% can afford donanemab, all of which are HICs. Most low and‐middle‐income countries (LMICs) cannot afford prices above $100 annually for either drug. These findings suggest that the manufacturer may focus on recouping its upfront investments and earning a profit in HICs, such as the United States and Europe. It makes economic sense for wealthier countries to contribute more to drug innovation costs. 57 , 58

Notably, applying the WHO‐recommended CETs, set at GDP per capita, resulted in substantial increases in estimated VBPs. Despite this, the average VBPs in LMICs (including low‐, lower‐middle‐income, and upper‐middle‐income countries) remained markedly lower than in HICs, at about 9% for both drugs. In particular, the VBPs in LICs were approximately 1.6% of those in HICs. When adopting a societal perspective, which accounts for higher caregiving times in LMICs, VBPs increased but were still only about 6% and 7% of those for lecanemab and donanemab, respectively, in HICs. This reflects the fact that although caregiving time is greater in LMICs, its cost is constrained by lower wages compared with HICs, which is consistent with the global dementia cost estimate by Wimo et al. 59 To promote health equity and universal access in LMICs while ensuring economic optimization, prices should be set at or slightly above the marginal cost of production. The global pricing of human immunodeficiency virus antiretroviral and hepatitis C drugs demonstrates the feasibility of substantially lowering prices in LICs compared to HICs, with prices in LICs reduced to approximately 1% of those in HICs. 60 , 61

This study suggests that the current prices of lecanemab and donanemab are too high, potentially placing them out of reach for patients, even in HICs. In the United States, even when adopting a societal perspective to account for the benefit of drugs for informal caregivers, the prices for both drugs would need to be substantially reduced to become cost‐effective. The history of rejection by the EMA and NICE has reinforced doubts about whether these DMTs are worth the money. 18 , 19 , 35 A prior study also demonstrated that, given the large European population eligible for DMTs, the existing lecanemab pricing would be unsustainable. 35 Therefore, considering an alternative pricing approach to mitigate the economic uncertainty associated with the evolving evidence regarding new DMTs is crucial. 62 Using value‐based pricing in negotiations ensures that the net health benefits of drugs counterbalance the costs incurred by the payer. In the long term, when the patent expires and cheaper generics become available, the payer may ultimately enjoy greater benefits.

The strength of this analysis lies in providing a breadth of country‐level estimates and a framework that can be refined through further efforts within countries. For countries seeking to use this model in price negotiations to approve emerging DMTs, it is recommended that a systematic search be conducted to identify the most relevant parameters for their country‐specific model.

This study has several limitations. First, this study is based primarily on data derived from the US, which represents a limitation given the considerable differences in healthcare systems, demographic profiles, and economic contexts across countries. Nevertheless, this limitation underscores one of the significant contributions of the present analysis: By applying systematic adjustments to reflect relevant epidemiological and socioeconomic factors, the study offers a pragmatic, scientifically grounded framework for projecting potential outcomes and drug prices in low‐ and middle‐income settings where local data are scarce or unavailable. While these projections are not substitutes for context‐specific studies, they provide valuable preliminary estimates to guide policy discussions, resource planning, and future research in countries at different income levels. Second, assumptions about long‐term treatment duration and efficacy in the model (i.e., patients receive treatment and its benefit for as long as they are in the MCI or mild AD stage) might not reflect the real‐world patterns, where benefit may be limited to an 18‐month trial or may persist after treatment cessation. Given the current lack of empirical evidence on the optimal duration of DMTs and the persistence of their effects, scenarios 2 and 3 were conducted to assess the sensitivity of VBPs to alternative assumptions about treatment duration and waning efficacy. To inform decision‐making in the immediate term, our scenario analyses revealed that applying 2‐year duration treatment along with ongoing long‐term efficacy raises VBPs approximately 2.5 times compared to the base case. In contrast, applying a 2‐year treatment duration with treatment efficacy ending after 2 years reduced the VBPs of lecanemab and donanemab by 13% and 8%, respectively. Finally, in line with concerns raised in the literature, treatment efficacy data are predominantly based on clinical trials involving predominantly White populations in the United States, which may not accurately reflect treatment efficacy in other populations and settings. 63 Future research should consider conducting subgroup analyses, potentially advocating for different prices tailored to specific patient groups. 64

Acknowledging that in the real world the long‐term value of drugs and pricing decisions is shaped by multiple factors beyond relying solely on CEA, this study is only one part of the larger decision‐making process. 65 Additionally, there are other challenges to DMT implementation, such as the capacity for patient identification, monitoring patients on DMTs, and other logistical barriers that must be addressed to understand and fully optimize the impact of these treatments.

CONCLUSION

With the current trend in prices, neither lecanemab nor donanemab will be cost‐effective in 174 countries. This study provides provisional VBP estimates and highlights significant price disparities across income levels. Substantial reflection on price is necessary to improve accessibility worldwide.
