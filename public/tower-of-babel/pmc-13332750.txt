Assessing the risk of drug-induced corrected QT interval prolongation in primary care
Authors: Divya Garg, Vishal S Bhella, Trudy Huyghebaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTisdale risk score has been validated for predicting drug-induced QTc prolongation (QTP) in cardiac care. This study aims to determine whether it can guide prescribing and monitoring of QTc-prolonging medications in primary care.MethodsA retrospective chart audit using Tisdale Risk Score was conducted. Data from 486 primary care patients from two Family Medicine Clinics, prescribed medications with known or possible risk of Torsades de Pointes (TdP) were reviewed. Eighty-five patients with pre- and post-pharmacotherapy ECGs comprised the cases. A control group of 184 patients not on QTc-prolonging medications was assessed. Patients were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk for QTP.ResultsPsychotropics were the most frequently prescribed QTc-prolonging medications. Baseline QTc did not differ significantly between cases (428 ms) and controls (426 ms). Among cases, most were stratified as low risk (69.4%), with fewer in moderate (25.9%) and high-risk (4.7%) categories. Comparatively, 99.5% of control patients were at low risk. QTc prolongation occurred in 5/85 (5.9%) post-pharmacotherapy, with none in the high-risk category. Odds ratio for QTP in moderate-risk patients versus low-risk was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.07–6.98).ConclusionsMost primary care patients were at low risk for drug-induced QTc prolongation. QTP events were low with none in the high-risk category, which limited evaluation of association between risk categories and QTc prolongation. The study highlights commonly prescribed QTc-prolonging medications in family medicine and their variable QTc effects, emphasizing the need for an outpatient-tailored risk assessment tool.

Introduction

A prolonged corrected QT (QTc) interval on electrocardiogram (ECG) is a marker of risk for development of torsades de pointes (TdP), a potentially fatal arrythmia [1, 2]. Drug induced QTc interval prolongation (QTP) is a potential cause of TdP [1]. A QTc interval above 500 ms increases the risk for drug associated TdP. The precise incidence of TdP is unknown, however an epidemiological study in Germany using active surveillance reported the incidence of drug-induced TdP to be around 3.2 per million person-years [3]. While rare, the consequences of TdP are significant and accordingly there is a focus on QTc-prolonging drugs as a modifiable risk factor.

Despite several clinical guidelines on preventing drug-induced QTP, evidence-based and practical recommendations tailored to primary care remain limited for guiding safe prescribing and monitoring. Primary care clinicians lack feasible guidance on when ECG monitoring is warranted and how to stratify risk using tools validated outside their practice environment.

The American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) 2017 “Guideline for Management of Patients with Ventricular Arrhythmias and the Prevention of Sudden Cardiac Death’ emphasized that medications known to prolong the QT interval pose a significant risk for individuals with congenital or acquired long QT syndrome [4]. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2022 guidelines suggest risk stratification before prescribing QTc-prolonging medications, along with ECG monitoring at baseline and short follow-up intervals after initiating or increasing the dose [5]. Recommendations on frequent monitoring remain challenging to translate to the outpatient context, where QTc-prolonging medications are frequently initiated and patients often require additional appointments or referrals to external facilities for ECG completion.

In 2013, Tisdale et al. [6] identified key risk factors for QTP and developed a scoring system to estimate the likelihood of its occurrence, defined as QTc>500 ms or an increase of >60 ms from baseline. This scoring system includes many known risk factors for TdP including advanced age, female sex, electrolyte abnormalities as well as cardiac disease including acute myocardial infarction and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [6, 7]. Tisdale score was validated in the cardiac critical care setting to identify low, medium and high-risk groups for QTP [6]. However, the score has not been studied or validated in ambulatory patients. The score considers many acute clinical presentations including sepsis and myocardial infarction that are relevant in hospitalized patients but may not be applicable to outpatient treatment decisions. Therefore, utilizing this risk score in an outpatient setting has its limitations.

Despite the widespread use of QTc-prolonging medications in primary care, to our knowledge, no QTc risk prediction tool has been validated in an outpatient primary care population. Existing evidence is limited to models developed and validated in inpatient settings where the risk of TdP is higher due to increased prevalence of predisposing acute conditions [6, 8]. As a result, clinicians are currently limited to general risk-factor assessment rather than primary care specific evidence to guide prescribing decisions.

We hypothesized that incidence of QTP in primary care would be low and that risk stratification may reduce the need for frequent ECG monitoring.

Methods

A retrospective chart audit with secondary data analysis was conducted to evaluate the applicability of the Tisdale Risk Score in primary care. The study was performed at two Academic Family Medicine Clinics using a shared electronic health record (EHR). Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board.

A modified version of the Tisdale Risk Score, shown in Table 1 and excluding risk factors relevant to acute clinical presentations such as sepsis or myocardial infarction, was applied for outpatient risk assessment. A maximum score of 16 was used, maintaining the original risk stratification thresholds (low risk <7, moderate risk 7–10, and high risk ≥11) [6]. The medications of interest are those assigned an AZCERT (Arizona Center for Education and Research on Therapeutics) classification of Known or Possible risk of TdP, as listed in the CredibleMeds® database [9].

Patients aged 18 years or older who were prescribed at least one medication with a known or possible risk of TdP and who had an ECG available for analysis were eligible for inclusion. An EHR query identified 486 patients who had been prescribed one or more QTc-prolonging medications. Patients without a pre-medication ECG, a post-medication ECG, or a clear temporal association between ECG timing and medication use were excluded. Cases were defined as patients with a baseline ECG prior to starting QTc-prolonging medication(s) and a subsequent ECG obtained afterward. Eighty-five patients met these criteria and were designated as cases. Independently, a control group of 184 patients was randomly selected from an EMR query identifying individuals who had ECGs available but were not prescribed QTc-prolonging medications, for baseline QTc interval and risk comparison. QTP was defined as a post-pharmacotherapy QTc duration ≥500 ms or an increase of ≥60 ms from baseline.

Primary outcome: To assess the applicability of the Tisdale Risk Score in primary care by examining its association with QTc values in the context of prescribing QTc-prolonging medications. The odds of QTc prolongation among moderate and high-risk patients were compared with those in the low-risk group, with the aim of reducing unnecessary ECGs in low-risk patients without compromising safety.

Secondary outcome: To identify commonly prescribed QTc-prolonging medications in primary care.

Results

As summarized in Table 2, cases had a higher proportion of females (66%) compared with control group (50%). Additionally, the mean age was greater in cases (61.7 years, SD 16.2) than in the control group (54.8 years, SD 19.3). Demographic differences in sex and age between cases and controls may be attributable to the selection criteria, which were based on the prescription of QTc-prolonging medications. Despite these variations, baseline QTc values did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) between cases (428 ms, SD 39) and controls (426 ms, SD 31).

In cases, there was higher frequency of low-risk Tisdale classification (69.4%) compared with moderate (25.9%) and high (4.7%) risk. About 99.5% of patients in the control population were at low risk of QTP.

Across the entire study population, the most prescribed long-term QTc-prolonging medications were psychotropic medications including antidepressants and antipsychotics. Highest prescribed medications as outlined in Table 3 included—Escitalopram (24%), Venlafaxine (19%), Citalopram (14%), Mirtazapine (11%), Nortriptyline (8%), and Aripiprazole (5%). Comparatively antiarrhythmic medications such as amiodarone only accounted for about 6% of prescriptions. There was a lower proportion of gastrointestinal motility medications such as domperidone; antiemetics such as ondansetron; medications used for overactive bladder such as mirabegron and opioids including buprenorphine and tramadol in this cohort.

QTP occurred in 5 of 85 patients (5.9%) on post-pharmacotherapy ECGs. Four were classified as low risk and one as moderate risk according to the Tisdale score. No high-risk patients experienced QTP. Among these five patients, three were male and two were female, and three were younger than 65 years. Two patients had a post-pharmacotherapy QTc greater than 500 ms, and all five demonstrated an increase of more than 60 ms from baseline. The odds ratio for QTP in the moderate-risk group compared with the low-risk group was 0.71 [95% CI: 0.07–6.98]. No cases of TdP were identified.

All patients with QTP were receiving one or more medications with a known risk of QTc prolongation alongside additional risk factors. Three of the five were taking the antiarrhythmic medication amiodarone, which was otherwise infrequently prescribed in the study cases and two were on psychotropic medications. Two were concurrently receiving a loop diuretic in addition to a QTc-prolonging agent and one had heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

The Tisdale Risk Score does not adjust for the individual medication-specific variability in QTc-prolonging effects. Medications assigned to the same AZCERT category vary considerably in their effect on the QTc interval, as do those in the same pharmacologic class [9–11]. The psychotropic medications that represented the most prescribed long-term QTc-prolonging agents in this primary care cohort are known as having low or moderate effect on QTc interval with an average increase ranging from <10 ms to 20 ms [10, 11].

Prior studies have shown that antiarrhythmics have higher potential risk and degree of QTc prolongation compared to psychotropics [11]. Three of the five QTP cases were receiving amiodarone; however, the small number of patients taking this medication in the study cohort and low event count limits inference about its contribution to QTP risk. While amiodarone is associated with higher rates of QTP, the occurrence of TdP with its use remains rare [12]. All QTP cases in our study also had additional concomitant risk factors.

Discussion

This study evaluated the applicability of the Tisdale Risk Score in a primary care population and found both a low baseline risk profile and a low incidence of QTc prolongation. Administration of QTc-prolonging drugs may pose greater TdP risk in hospitalized patients, with acute illness, polypharmacy, and electrolyte disturbances contributing to the higher incidence of QTP seen in inpatient studies [1, 6, 8].

Low incidence of QTP including none in the high-risk category limited conclusions about Tisdale risk categories and their ability to predict QTP. Additionally, individual variability in QTc intervals and physiological or metabolic factors at the time of ECG acquisition, which cannot be fully accounted for in retrospective analyses, may further limit associations between pharmacotherapy and QTc changes [13].

Not all medications classified as having known or possible risk of QTP have similar effect on the QTc interval. Our study suggests that patients at low baseline risk for QTc prolongation, who are initiated on psychotropic medications with low risk of QTc-prolongation, and who are not concurrently prescribed other QTc-prolonging agents or loop diuretics, do not require routine ECG monitoring. This is consistent with recent clinical practice guidance and prescribing algorithms that recommend ECG monitoring primarily for patients with additional risk factors or higher-risk medications [10, 14].

As QTc prolongation events were rare, the study could not determine how Tisdale risk categories relate to QTc changes in this primary care cohort, but structured risk assessment remains clinically important. Risk is dynamic and may increase substantially during hospitalization due to acute conditions. Outpatient baseline risk assessment can inform transitions of care including safer prescribing and monitoring.

Strengths of the study include the use of real-world primary care data and inclusion of a control group. Study limitations include the small number of patients with both pre- and post-pharmacotherapy ECGs available in the EHR and the inability to evaluate the impact of short-term QTc-prolonging medications such as antibiotics due to lack of comparative ECG data. Additionally, low incidence of QTc prolongation in the study population further limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions about risk prediction.

Conclusion

The study noted a low baseline risk of QTc prolongation. Low incidence of QTP precluded conclusions about QTP risk prediction using Tisdale risk categories. Findings suggest that patients at low baseline risk, without concurrent QTc-prolonging medications or loop diuretics, who initiate psychotropics with low to moderate effect on QTc do not routinely require ECG monitoring. The study highlights the need for primary care specific risk assessment tools and targeted ECG monitoring recommendations that account for chronic comorbidities, long-term medication use, and the QTc-prolonging effects of commonly prescribed drugs at the individual medication level.
