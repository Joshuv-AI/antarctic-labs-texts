Association of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism with dietary patterns, glycemic factors, and anthropometric indices in women with prediabetes
Authors: Roghayeh Molani-Gol, Sana Aftabi-Yousefabad, Golnoosh Azarsina, Dariush Shanehbandi, Mahsa Malekian, Mohammad Asghari Jafarabadi, Maryam Rafraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrediabetes is an early stage of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) that is prevalent around the world. A combination of genetic and lifestyle factors is contributed in conversion of prediabetes to T2DM. The present study aimed to investigate the association of the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism with dietary patterns, glycemic factors, and anthropometric indices in women with prediabetes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was carried out on 202 women with prediabetes aged 18–65 years who were selected from the healthcare center using the convenience sampling method. Fasting blood samples, anthropometric measurements, and information about the dietary intake of the participants were collected. The ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism genotypes were recognized by the high-resolution melting-polymerase chain reaction (HRM-PCR) method. Dietary patterns were obtained by exploratory factor analysis and JASP software. Data analyses were conducted by analysis of covariance and ordinal regression using IBM SPSS Statistics software.ResultsAA, AT, and TT genotypes of the ITLN1 polymorphism were found in 15.8%, 52.0%, and 32.2% of participants, and three dietary patterns, including vegetarian, high-fat, and mixed dietary patterns, were extracted. According to the ordinal regression, participants in the AT genotype had a lower chance of having a high-fat dietary pattern compared to the AA genotype (Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) (CI 95%): 0.466 (0.219, 0.989), p = 0.047). The ANCOVA test also indicated that participants with the AT (Mean difference (MD) (CI 95%): -3.073 (-5.245, -0.901), p = 0.007) and TT (MD (CI 95%): -3.188 (-5.513, -0.862), p = 0.006) genotypes had a decreased body weight compared to the AA genotype. Moreover, women in the TT genotype group had higher waist circumference compared to the AA genotype (MD (CI 95%): 2.557 (0.060, 5.055), p = 0.045). Individuals with genotypes AT (MD (CI 95%): 0.028 (0.011, 0.044), p = 0.001) and TT (MD (CI 95%): 0.022 (0.005, 0.040), p = 0.013) also have higher WHthR compared to the AA genotype. There was no significant association between the ITLN1 polymorphism genotypes and glycemic parameters in the adjusted model.ConclusionThe findings revealed a link between the ITLN1 Val109Asp polymorphism and dietary pattern 2 and general and central obesity in women with prediabetes. Further well-designed studies are required to confirm these findings and clarify the association of the ITLN1 polymorphism with glycemic factors.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), as a chronic condition, is prevalent around the world [1]. The stage before that is called prediabetes, which is a high-risk state characterized by elevated glucose levels but not reaching diabetes criteria [2]. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) defined prediabetes by fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels of 100–125 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 5.7–6.4%, or glucose tolerance test two hours after ingesting a standardized 75 g glucose solution resulting in 140–199 mg/dL [2]. The prevalence of prediabetes is increasing around the world, and it is expected that 6.5% (414 million) and 10.0% (638 million) of the population will have impaired fasting glucose (IFG) and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) by 2045, respectively [3]. In 2021, the global prevalence of IFG and IGT was 5.8% (298 million) and 9.1% (464 million), respectively [3]. It was reported that 18.2% and 15.4% of males and females in Iran had prediabetes, respectively [4]. Given the high complications of T2DM and the financial burden of managing it, prioritizing the prevention and control of this disease has become a global concern [1].

Risk factors for T2DM are both unmodifiable, including age, race, and genetics, and modifiable, such as lack of physical activity and unhealthy lifestyle [5]. Lifestyle changes, including unhealthy dietary patterns, lead to the rapid increase in overweight and obesity, which is the leading cause of the high prevalence of T2DM [6]. Prediabetes, diabetes, and adiposity are closely connected. Omentin-1 is an adipose tissue secreted adipocytokines, which play a critical role in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and diabetes [7, 8]. It was reported that the levels of serum omentin-1 were lower in subjects with obesity, impaired glucose regulation, and T2DM than in the normal glucose tolerance group [9, 10]. The gene encoding omentin-1 is located on the first chromosome (1q23.3 (GRCh38 assembly)) and involves a single-nucleotide variant (SNV), variant rs2274907, at the fourth exon, where adenine is replaced with thymine (c.326T > A; p. Val109Asp). This missense variant results in the replacement of aspartic acid (ASP) with valine (Val) at codon 109 of the omentin-1 protein [8, 9]. SNVs or single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) can influence the onset and progression of various diseases [11]. Recent studies have evaluated the association of the Val109Asp polymorphism of the ITLN1 gene with T2DM [12, 13]. For instance, a study by Khushi et al. on newly diagnosed T2DM adults revealed a significant association between the ITLN1 Val109Asp polymorphism and increased insulin resistance [12]. Furthermore, Splichal et al. demonstrated that the interaction between the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism and body mass index (BMI) can decrease insulin sensitivity [13]. They also reported a connection between this polymorphism and daily intake of energy, fat, and protein [13]. It was reported that some genetic polymorphisms could modulate the effects of dietary patterns on T2DM [14–16]. The controversial association between dietary patterns and health results could be attributed to the influence of genetic background [14, 17, 18].

Dietary modifications are recommended for the prevention and reversal of prediabetes. Research has shown notable associations between specific foods and the risk of developing T2DM [6]. However, these connections are intricate because individuals typically consume a combination of foods rather than single items. A dietary pattern consists of a collection of food items that reflect a particular eating habit [19]. Analyzing and identifying dietary patterns is often employed to determine nutritional risk factors related to diabetes and to improve blood glucose control. We hypothesized that carriers of the ITLN1 polymorphism would have association with central adiposity and altered glycemic parameters, potentially influenced by dietary patterns. To the best knowledge, there is no study assessed the association of the ITLN1 polymorphism with dietary patterns. Moreover, the findings of a few studies have evaluated the relationship of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism with glycemic and anthropometric factors, which were controversial [12, 13, 20]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the association of the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism with dietary patterns, glycemic factors, and anthropometric indices in women with prediabetes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on 202 women with prediabetes in Tabriz, Iran. The participants were recruited from the healthcare center using convenience sampling and met the eligibility criteria. The inclusion criteria were women aged 18–65 years diagnosed with prediabetes who had signed an informed consent to participate. Prediabetes was diagnosed according to ADA criteria (FBS 100–125 mg/dL) [2]. The exclusion criteria were sever obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2) or wasting (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2); pregnancy or lactation; smoking or alcohol addiction; renal failure (in dialysis); cardiovascular, thyroid, liver, or kidney diseases; cancer; chronic neurological diseases; Cushing’s syndrome; rheumatoid arthritis; surgery in the last three months; use of vitamin/nutritional supplements current or last three months; following weight loss diets; use of various drugs such as glycemic controller or lipid-lowering drugs; concomitant participation in another experimental study; and other conditions in which the researchers believes the subject were the exclusion criteria for this study. All procedures were conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol of the current project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (Ethical code: IR.TBZMED.REC.1402.618).

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated using Stata 18 software (StataCorp, College Station, Texas 77845 USA). Based on the allele frequency of the rs2274907 G > A SNP in an Iranian population [12], and considering the power of 80%, alpha 0.05, and about a 15% loss to follow-up accounted for potential recruitment non-response, a sample of 204 participants was calculated for the study. Furthermore, this sample size meets the minimum threshold recommended for robust pattern extraction in exploratory factor analysis (i.e., n ≥ 200) [21].

Data collection

A structured and content and face validated (by the research team) questionnaire checklist was administered to collect information regarding the general characteristics, medical, and treatment history. The participants’ dietary intake was recorded using a 168-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) that had been validated previously for the Iranian population [22]. Then, the dietary patterns were recognized based on the FFQs information. In addition, the FFQs were analyzed using Nutritionist-4 software, and the mean daily caloric intakes of participants were estimated.

Dietary patterns definition

The JASP software [23] was used for determining dietary patterns. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to identify the dominant food patterns. One hundred sixty-eight food items were divided into 30 food groups based on their composition and content, and previous study [24]. The number of factors was based on the parallel analysis and the minimum residual method. In the next step, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett test were used to determine the model’s suitability. To identify the dominant food patterns based on the EFA method, after calculating the correlation matrix between the variables, groups were extracted by the minimum residual extraction method, in which variables with a high correlation are placed in one group. Thus, a Maximum Likelihood Analysis with Promax rotation was performed on 30 food groups to identify the dietary patterns. To detect the number of factors (dietary patterns), the change point in the behavior of the scree plot curve was used. According to previous nutritional epidemiology studies [25–27], values with a factor loading greater than or equal to 0.2 were considered to determine the items of each food pattern. The factors were named based on the interpretation of the food items in each factor. Each individual was given a score based on the amount of food consumed in different groups and their factor loading for each dietary pattern. Anthropometric measurements

To measure the weight of participants, a digital platform scale was used, with women standing barefoot in minimal clothing at the center. The same scale was utilized for height measurement, with subjects standing barefoot, arms hanging freely at their sides, and palms facing their thighs. BMI was determined by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared. To measure waist circumference (WC), a flexible plastic tape was placed at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and iliac crest, perpendicular to the body. During this measurement, the participant was standing with feet approximately 20 cm apart and arms hanging freely. Hip circumference (HC) was assessed at the widest point over the buttocks, with the researcher kneeling beside the participant to accurately observe the maximum extension level. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated by dividing WC (cm) by HC (cm) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) by dividing WC by height (m).

Genotyping of rs2274907 (ITLN1)

Blood samples were collected after 8–12 h of fasting by a professional individual at the baseline and after 12 weeks. About 2 cc of the obtained blood sample was used to determine the Val109Asp polymorphism of the Omentin-1 gene, and the remaining was centrifuged at 22 °C, 5000 rpm, for 6 min, and the serum was stored in microtubes at − 70 °C for later analysis.

Omentin-1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism genotypes were identified using the high-resolution melting-polymerase chain reaction (HRM-PCR) method. HRM is a new method for identifying mutations (polymorphisms), in which genotypes are distinguished by differences in the melting curve. First, the participants’ genomic DNA was extracted using a standard phenol/chloroform method from the whole blood. Second, the PCR amplification of the rs2274907 was conducted using the forward: CCCTCACCCGAGTGGGTA and reverse: GAATGACATGCGTGGGAAG primers. Then, the melting curve was produced using the real-time device and the fluorescent color Eva green, which is more sensitive than other colors such as CybrGreen. The following items were used for the HRM procedure: 4 µl of EvvaGreen PCR Master mix 5x, 0.5 µl (each) of Primer (4pmol/µl), 50 ng of DNA, and up to 20 µl of H2O. A 10% random sample was re-genotyped for quality control. Genotype distribution was tested for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using the χ² test.

Biochemical assessments

A professional individual collected blood samples after 8–12 h of fasting. The obtained blood sample was centrifuged at 22 °C, 5000 rpm, for 6 min, and the serum was stored in microtubes at − 70 °C for later analysis. FBS was evaluated by the enzymatic colorimetric method (kit: Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran), and serum insulin was assessed by the ELISA technique (Monobind Inc., Lake Forest, CA, USA). The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), Homeostatic Model Assessment of β cell function (HOMA-β), quantitative insulin-sensitivity check index (QUICKI) were calculated using the formulas (HOMA-IR = [Fasting Insulin (µIU/ml) * Fasting Glucose (mg/dl)]/405; HOMA-β = (Fasting Insulin (µIU/ml) * 360)/(Fasting Glucose (mg/dl) − 63); QUICKI = 1/[log(Fasting Insulin (µIU/ml)) + log(Fasting Glucose (mg/dl))] [28, 29].

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20, IBM Corp, Armonk, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and measures of distribution (skewness and kurtosis) were used to assess the normality of the data distribution. Normally distributed numerical variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was assessed using χ² test.

The association of ITLN1 gene polymorphism genotypes with continuous variables was assessed by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) in a crude model and adjusted model for age, BMI, and daily energy intake. Associations were evaluated under additive and dominant genetic models. Model selection was based on allele frequency distribution and biological plausibility. ANCOVA for non-normally distributed variables was conducted after log transformation. To investigate the relationship of ITLN1 gene polymorphism genotypes with tertiles of dietary patterns and the categories of BMI, the ordinal regression was applied in both crude and adjusted models that adjusted for the confounders. All statistical tests were considered significant at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Table 2 presents the general characteristics of the participants. Two hundred and two women with prediabetes participated in this study. The median age of participants was 49.50 (IQR (Q3-Q1) = 15.0) years. Moreover, 98.5% of patients were married, and 97.0% were housewives. A first-degree family history of diabetes was reported by 51% of participants. The median BMI was 31.09 (IQR (Q3-Q1) = 4.95) kg/m2, and 4%, 32.7%, and 63.4% of women were of normal weight, overweight, and obesity, respectively. About the ITLN1 gene polymorphism, 15.8%, 52.0%, and 32.2% of women had genotypes AA, AT, and TT, respectively. The minor allele frequency (MAF) was 41.68%. Genotype distribution was consistent with the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P > 0.05).

As shown in Table 3, three dominant dietary patterns, including vegetarian, high-fat, and mixed dietary patterns, were diagnosed. The three highest factor loadings in vegetarian, high-fat, and mixed dietary patterns were respectively related to the vegetables group (0.65), tomatoes (0.50), and nuts (0.47); the mayonnaise group (0.69), snacks (0.63), and unsaturated oils (0.37); and the refined grain group (-0.48), legumes (0.45), and salt (-0.44). In addition, the lowest factor loadings in vegetarian, high-fat, and mixed dietary patterns were related to the dried fruit (0.23), the full-fat dairy products (0.24), and the tea and coffee (-0.21), respectively. The KMO index is 0.57, indicating that the data is sufficiently adequate, and the significance of the Bartlett’s test also shows that there is a sufficient correlation between the food groups (p < 0.001). Food groups with a loading factor higher than 0.2 were important contributors to the dietary pattern. The cumulative predictive value of the obtained dietary pattern was 15%. The scree plot for the identification of the dietary patterns by principal component analysis was presented in Fig. 1.

Table 4 indicates the association of ITLN1 gene polymorphism genotypes with dietary patterns, biochemical parameters, and anthropometric indices. According to the ordinal regression adjusted for age, BMI, and dietary energy intake, participants in the ITLN1 gene polymorphism AT genotype had a lower chance of having a high-fat dietary pattern compared to the AA genotype (OR (95%CI): 0.466 (0.219, 0.989), p = 0.047). Moreover, the results of the ANCOVA test indicated participants with the AT (MD (CI 95%): -3.073 (-5.245, -0.901), p = 0.007) and TT (MD (CI 95%): -3.188 (-5.513, -0.862), p = 0.006) genotypes of ITLN1 gene polymorphism had a decreased body weight compared to AA genotype following adjusting for age, BMI, and daily energy intake. Moreover, women in the TT genotype group had higher WC compared to the AA genotype (MD (CI 95%): 2.557 (0.060, 5.055), p = 0.045) after adjusting for the confounders. Individuals with genotypes AT (MD (CI 95%): 0.028 (0.011, 0.044), p = 0.001) and TT (MD (CI 95%): 0.022 (0.005, 0.040), p = 0.013) also have higher WHthR compared to the AA genotype in the adjusted model.

Regarding the glycemic factors, participants in the TT genotype of ITLN1 gene polymorphism had increased serum FBS levels compared to the AA genotype (MD (95%CI): 3.305 (0.098, 6.512), p = 0.043), but this result was non-significant after adjusting for age, BMI, and dietary energy intake.

Discussion

The present study investigated the association between the Val109Asp polymorphism of the ITLN1 gene and dietary patterns, glycemic factors, and anthropometric indices in women with prediabetes. The findings demonstrated that individuals with the AA genotype were significantly more likely to follow a high-fat dietary pattern compared to those with the AT genotype. High-fat dietary pattern is characterized by a high intake of mayonnaise, snacks, unsaturated oils, and full-fat dairy products, foods typically rich in fats and calories. The preference for this energy-dense dietary pattern may indicate a genotype-influenced tendency toward high-fat food consumption, aligning with previous studies suggesting that genetic variants can influence taste preference, appetite regulation, and dietary behavior [14, 30]. Limited studies have explored the association between the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism and dietary patterns. Splichal et al. in the study on the Central-European adult population with and without obesity demonstrated that the AA genotype of the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism was associated with the highest energy intake, and this polymorphism was also significantly associated with the daily fat and proteins consumption [13]. This suggests a potential genetic influence on dietary behaviors, which may have implications for personalized nutrition interventions. On the other words, genetic polymorphisms have been shown to influence individual differences in dietary preferences and susceptibility to obesity-related traits and metabolic disorders [14, 18, 31]. Specific dietary patterns have been linked to protective or deleterious health effects depending on an individual’s genetic background [14, 32]. The rs2274907 variant is a missense substitution (Val109Asp). Although direct functional studies are limited, missense changes may influence protein stability or secretion. However, current evidence does not conclusively demonstrate functional alteration; therefore, the variant may act as a marker of adipokine-related metabolic pathways. Further research with larger sample sizes may help clarify this relationship.

Based on the findings, participants with the AA genotype had lower abdominal obesity compared to the AT genotype carriers. The observed pattern of lower body weight but greater central adiposity among certain genotypes may reflect differential fat distribution rather than total adiposity. Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically distinct and more strongly associated with insulin resistance. Similar results were reported by Isakova et al. on adults with abdominal obesity, that the TT genotype of the ITLN1 gene increased the risk of abdominal obesity, whereas the AA homozygous genotype was not associated with this condition [33]. Genetic predisposition is a well-defined risk factor for obesity [34], especially its abdominal form, which is a major risk factor for T2DM and metabolic syndrome [35, 36]. Adipocytokine family genes, such as the ITLN1 gene, because of their products’ connection influencing various metabolic and cellular activities, can have a direct or indirect impact on the metabolism of carbohydrates and lipids [8, 9, 34]. This association may reflect a protective effect of the AA genotype of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism on visceral fat accumulation, even in the context of a high-fat diet. Being rich in unsaturated fats of the high-fat dietary pattern may compensate the adverse effects of the high amount of fat in this diet, as the high-fat diet plays a significant role in the onset of insulin resistance and the related metabolic issues, including metabolic syndrome and T2DM [36]. Genetic research has effectively confirmed that genetic elements, along with their interaction with environments conducive to obesity, are likely to contribute to weight gain and insulin resistance [37, 38]. Additionally, the findings of the current study indicated that individuals with the AA genotype had higher rates of overweight and obesity compared to the AT and TT genotypes. Despite those favorable indicators of the AA genotype, its direct association with overweight and obesity indicates that the AA genotype may confer a sex-specific risk for overall adiposity. In the study on the patients with knee osteoarthritis, BMI and WHR were not significantly different between the genotype groups of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism [39]. In another study, individuals with the TT genotype were found to have nearly a fivefold higher likelihood of having obesity compared to those with the AA genotype (OR = 4.5) [20]. Meanwhile, Splichal et al. in the study on individuals with and without obesity revealed a notable variation in genotype distribution between participants with obesity and sever obesity. Specifically, the AA genotype was significantly less common among the individuals with sever obesity, whereas the AT genotype appeared more frequently in the participants with sever obesity compared to the group of subjects with obesity [13]. Hormonal differences, particularly estrogen-related fat deposition, may contribute to this divergent pattern in our women [40]. However, the high chance of having a diet rich in fats and calories explains the high likelihood of having overweight and obesity in AA genotype carriers. Furthermore, abdominal adiposity appears to have a pivotal role in the development of insulin resistance and is more closely related to insulin resistance and elevated blood glucose than overall obesity [41, 42].

According to this study, the serum FBS levels were slightly lower in participants with the AA genotype compared to the TT genotype group. Although this association did not reach statistical significance following adjustment for confounders, the trend toward reduced FBS suggests a potentially beneficial effect of the AA genotype on glycemic regulation. Nevertheless, in the patients with knee osteoarthritis, there was no significant difference in serum FBS levels between the genotype groups of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism [39]. In another study by Khoshi et al. in Iranian individuals with newly diagnosed T2DM, genetic polymorphisms of ITLN1 Val109Asp are significantly related to a higher HOMA-IR index only in subjects with overweight or obesity [12]. They reported that the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism was also significantly positively correlated with a familial history of diabetes [12]. Evidence has reported associations between genetic variants and insulin sensitivity or glucose metabolism, which may underlie this observed relationship [43, 44].

Overall, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of genetic variation in influencing both dietary behaviors and metabolic health outcomes. Interestingly, the AA genotype was associated with some favorable metabolic indicators. Participants in the AA genotype group had a dietary pattern rich in fats and calories and unsaturated oils, together with lower central obesity and FBS and higher general obesity compared to the AT and TT genotype groups. It may be because of the importance of abdominal obesity in predicting insulin resistance and diabetes. In addition, this suggests a potential genetic influence on dietary behaviors, which may have implications for personalized nutrition interventions.

It should be acknowledged that glycemic regulation and adiposity are complex, polygenic traits influenced by multiple genetic and environmental factors. The present study focused on a single missense variant (rs2274907) within ITLN1, which limits the ability to capture the broader genetic architecture underlying prediabetes. Future studies incorporating polygenic risk scores or multi-locus pathway-based approaches may provide a more comprehensive understanding of gene–diet interactions in metabolic regulation.

Strengths and limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study was unable to infer causality. Although the sample size was calculated to achieve 80% power for the primary association, subgroup analyses by genotype may have been underpowered due to allele distribution. Moreover, the FFQ that was used for data collection relies on respondents’ memory, and overestimating or underestimating food intake may occur. Furthermore, it is common for adults with obesity to underreport their dietary intake. Nevertheless, the FFQ is both convenient and cost-effective, and it was adapted to ensure that the resulting dietary patterns closely reflect daily eating habits. Identifying these gene–diet interactions could offer the most effective personalized dietary recommendations for individuals at high risk, based on their genetic profiles, to alleviate the significant burden of prediabetes and, subsequently, T2DM.

Implications and further recommendations

This study’s results add to the increasing evidence that genetic variation plays an important role in shaping dietary habits and metabolic outcomes. From a nutritional genomics perspective, this highlights the potential for personalized nutrition strategies based on genotype to better target metabolic health risks. Future research should seek to confirm these associations in larger, more diverse populations and explore mechanistic explanations, including gene expression, hormonal profiles, and longitudinal metabolic changes.

Conclusion

The findings suggest an association between the ITLN1 gene Val109Asp polymorphism and a high-fat dietary pattern (diet including unsaturated oils) and general and abdominal obesity in women with prediabetes. However, further well-designed studies are required to confirm these findings and clarify the association of the ITLN1 gene polymorphism with glycemic indices in these patients among different populations.
