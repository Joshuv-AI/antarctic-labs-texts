Paediatric suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries diagnosed after an emergency call: a national population-based cohort study from Denmark
Authors: Kristine Sørensen, Morten Breinholt Søvsø, Charlotte Nørkjær Eggertsen, Giles Haythornthwaite, Søren Mikkelsen, Erika Frischknecht Christensen, Vibe Maria Laden Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecognition of a non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury by emergency clinicians is essential to help prevent further physical and mental morbidity.The study objectives were to calculate the probability of paediatric suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries after an emergency call and to investigate the mechanisms of injury and any involvement of legal or social authorities noted by emergency medical services (EMS).MethodsNationwide population-based cohort study in Denmark from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2023, including children ≤15 years for whom an emergency call had been made and who received a diagnosis of a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury by a hospital physician. Data were obtained from national hospital registers and from prehospital patient medical records. Injury probability after an emergency call was calculated stratified by sex.ResultsIn hospital registers, the incidence of suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries was 34 per 100 000 children per year and among 138 599 paediatric EMS calls, 0.52% (95% CI 0.48% to 0.56%) resulted in such a diagnosis. Subgroups were ‘Asphyxiation’ (2.5%), ‘Maltreatment’ (15.1%), ‘Assault’ (31.2%), ‘Sexual abuse’ (5.9%), ‘Intentional self-harm’ (28.7%) and ‘>1 of the above categories’ (16.6%). Children aged 12–15 years had a higher probability of these injuries, both in males and females. In a sensitivity analysis excluding ‘Assault’ and ‘Intentional self-harm’, the probability increased markedly for males <1 year. All-cause mortality was 1.4% (n=10, 95% CI 0.7% to 2.6%). EMS had involved police in 17.6% and social authorities in 2.0%.ConclusionOf all the children diagnosed with a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury in a hospital in Denmark, only a small proportion had been brought to the hospital by the EMS. Two-thirds of the injuries had been inflicted by another person, while a third was due to intentional self-harm. Involvement of legal or social authorities by EMS was uncommon.Keywords: Child Abuse, Child Health, Epidemiology, Health services research

Introduction

Non-accidental injuries and intentional self-harm in children contribute to extensive morbidity and even mortality worldwide.1,5 Whether the injury is sustained by another person or by deliberate self-harm, any child with such an injury is not a healthy child. The recognition of a non-accidental or intentional self-harm mechanism of injury by healthcare personnel is essential to offer the child interdisciplinary support, which is not always initiated in the emergency care system. While intentional self-harm and injuries inflicted by other persons may be studied separately, the same interdisciplinary approach to understanding the child’s social circumstances is necessary.

In Denmark, all healthcare personnel, dental care personnel, police, public institutions and the educational system are obliged by law to inform social services by a written report if they are made aware that a child or adolescent has a need for social support or has been subjected to any form of abuse, physical or mental harm by a peer, by a caregiver, by themselves or by any other person.6

A non-accidental mechanism of injury is often identified late, even though bruises and other skin lesions are frequently visible on a child’s head, neck or upper limbs.3 7 62% of Danish emergency departments have policies and procedures to guide clinicians in cases of suspected child abuse.8 Despite the available policies, emergency department personnel do not always react adequately when they encounter non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries.9

The emergency medical services (EMS) may have information on the mechanism of injury and the circumstances leading up to the injury, as they are the only emergency care professionals who are present at the scene of injury. They may also have made objective observations on the home environment surrounding the child. This information may support emergency department personnel in their consideration and decision to report the case as a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm mechanism of injury to the social services.

Knowledge about these EMS observations made at the scene of injury is needed to illustrate whether this information may be useful for the emergency department personnel, with the overall aim of identifying more cases and initiating timely action.

The aims of this study are (1) to investigate the incidence of any form of suspected paediatric non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury among patients for whom a call to the EMS had been made, and any association between these types of injuries and age and (2) to explore the extent of clinical information noted by the EMS personnel at the scene of injury in these cases.

Setting

Denmark has a ground area of 42 962 km2 with 5 707 251 inhabitants in mixed rural, semi-rural and urban areas. At the beginning of the study period (1 January 2016), children aged ≤15 years (1 029 401) comprised 18% of the total population.10

The Danish tax-funded EMS have been described in extensive detail in previous works.11 All emergency calls to 1-1-2 are answered by the police, and if they suspect that the call regards any type of medical emergency, they forward the call to an emergency medical coordination centre. The police decides on dispatch of a police unit along with the EMS dispatch. 24-hour operative ambulances and rapid response vehicles are staffed by emergency medical technicians and paramedics. For life-threatening conditions or potentially life-threatening conditions, a physician-staffed mobile emergency care unit and/or a physician-staffed helicopter can be dispatched simultaneously. Emergency medical technicians and paramedics can also request the physician-staffed units. Generally, a physician-staffed unit is dispatched alongside an ambulance in about 20%–25% of dispatches for children.12 In this study, all types of prehospital vehicles were included. The Danish social authorities have a social worker on duty 24 hours a day for the initiation of any emergency child protective measures. They are, however, not part of the emergency medical system, and they are not dispatched to the scene.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Data sources and linkage

The data collection process and data set linkage are outlined in figure 1.

Patient flow diagram. A unique patient could have had more than one ePMR, that is, more than one emergency call during the study period. ePMR, electronic patient medical record; ICD-10, International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision; PIN, personal identification number.

All persons residing in Denmark have a unique personal identification number (PIN).13 14 For identification of the study population, patients with the abovementioned hospital ICD-10 diagnoses were identified in the Danish National Patient Register. The register contains data from inpatient and outpatient hospital visits and visits to private practitioners in all medical specialities, except general practice and thus information about comorbidity prior to the emergency call.13 Then, the identified 11 395 PINs were merged with prehospital electronic patient medical records (ePMR) to identify which patients had arrived at the hospital after a call to the national EMS.11 Other pathways into the hospital could have been via a primary care physician, via an out-of-hours primary care physician, via a private paediatrician or via self-referral. Patients without a valid PIN could not be identified and therefore not included in the study population.

We excluded cases with patients where the time interval from ambulance arrival at the hospital until an entry in the Danish National Patient Register was >24 hours (n=9713), and cases with ambulance dispatches at another point in time during the study period than the particular hospital stay, where the diagnosis of a suspected non-accidental injury or intentional self-harm had been given (n=1039).

For patients who met all of the inclusion criteria, data from free-text fields in the ePMR were entered into REDCap.15 Outcome data were collected from the Danish National Patient Register and the Danish Civil Registration System, which includes the migration status and vital status of all residents in Denmark.14 Registers were linked using PINs and timestamps of hospital visits and ambulance dispatches. Data were pseudonymised before analyses via remote desktop access through a secure virtual private network tunnel at the Danish Health Data Authority.

Outcomes

The study used outcomes from both fixed entry fields and free-text fields from the ePMR. Vital signs and prehospital triage level were reported according to the Danish Emergency Process Triage system. Information regarding the mechanism of injury and surrounding factors was collected from free-text entry fields on the prehospital ePMR. Categories of information were derived from the literature before data extraction.16 17 A random sample of 52 records was screened to confirm, dismiss or add to the categories of information collected from the prehospital ePMR and inter-observer agreement between VMLN and KS was investigated. All 715 records were then read by authors VMLN, KS, MBS, CNE and EFC.

Statistical methods and analysis

Descriptive variables include age, sex, reason for calling the EMS noted by the call handler (dispatch criterion), previous comorbidity, physician-staffed prehospital unit dispatched along with ambulance and time interval from emergency call to arrival at hospital. Age was categorised into newborns (0–2 months), infants (3–11 months), toddlers (1–2 years), preschool children (3–7 years), school children (8–11 years) and adolescents (12–15 years) according to the nationally implemented paediatric triage model.18

Following extraction from the prehospital ePMRs, interobserver agreement calculated from a sample of 52 records was acceptable, with Cohen’s kappa=0.72–1.00 (substantial agreement) for 7/10 of the variables and 0.37–0.48 (fair to moderate agreement) for 2/10 variables and a negative Cohen’s kappa (−0.07) (poor agreement) for one variable. The latter three variables were discussed in detail by KS and VMLN, after which the wording was clarified, and an extra variable was added.

Follow-up outcome was all-cause mortality. Patients were considered ‘lost to follow-up’ if they had no current address in Denmark, PIN changed due to a change of sex, or the patient had migrated, as indicated in the Civil Registration System.14

For the association between age and suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries after an EMS call (dichotomous outcome, no missing data), logistic regressions were performed with age as a grouped exposure (age group 12–15 years set as reference). Assumptions for a logistic regression were checked, and the ‘vce’ option was used to adhere to the assumption of independent observations. Crude ORs for suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries according to age groups were calculated in two strata, male and female (missing data on age n=74, missing data on sex n=951 of the total number of EMS calls (N=138 599)). A sensitivity analysis excluding the children who had been diagnosed with ‘Assault’ or ‘Intentional self-harm’ was performed (N=1 38 097). No data were imputed. Analyses were performed with Stata/MP V.18.5 (StataCorp, Texas, USA) with two-sided p values <0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Incidence of suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries

During the study period, 11 395 children were diagnosed in a hospital with a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury. Using the total population of children ≤15 years in Denmark as the population at risk, the average annual incidence was 34 per 100 000 children per year.10 In only 715 (6.27%, 95% CI 5.84% to 6.74%) of these cases, the child had arrived at the hospital after an emergency call.

For the EMS personnel, the probability of encountering a child who was subsequently suspected of a non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury in the hospital was 715 of 138 599 calls to the EMS regarding children ≤15 years in the entire study period, which equals 0.52% (95% CI 0.48% to 0.56%) (figure 1).

There were marked variations in the probability across age groups. Both males and females in the age categories 0–11 years had significantly lower probability (OR<1) of suspected non-accidental injury or intentional self-harm compared with adolescents (12–15-year reference category) (figure 2A).

Follow-up on all-cause mortality was complete for the majority of children with suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries (634/643 unique patients, 98.6%). 10 deaths (1.4%, 95% CI 0.7% to 2.6%) occurred during the study period. Due to insufficient information on the prehospital ePMR, two of these ten deaths remained undetermined as to whether the injury was definitively accidental or non-accidental.

Mechanism of injury

Subgroups of suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries were ‘Asphyxiation’ (n=18), ‘Maltreatment’ (n=108), ‘Assault’ (n=223), ‘Sexual abuse’ (n=42), ‘Intentional self-harm’ (n=205) and ‘>1 of the above categories’ (n=119). We refer to the Methods section for the specific diagnostic codes included in each subgroup.

For the majority of the children, the injury had been inflicted by another person, and most frequently by different kinds of blunt force trauma (table 3). Among the children with intentional self-harm, the most frequent mechanisms of injury were overdose of pharmaceuticals, foreign body ingestion or cutting (table 3). The EMS personnel had described an objective examination of the child in almost all cases (87.0%), and in addition, commented on the plausibility of the stated mechanism of injury in some cases (4.3%). In 17.6% of cases, the EMS had involved the police, most often in cases where the injury had been inflicted by another person, and contacted the social authorities in 2.0% of cases.

Discussion

Among all calls to the EMS regarding children ≤15 years in Denmark, 0.52% resulted in a hospital diagnosis for a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury, corresponding to 1 in 200 paediatric emergency calls.

The EMS personnel had noted a mechanism of injury and described objective signs of the injuries on the prehospital ePMR in almost all cases, while involvement of authorities was 17.6% for police and 2% for social services.

Adolescents had the highest probability of suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries, both in males and females. In a sensitivity analysis excluding ‘Assault’ and ‘Intentional self-harm’, the probability of a suspected non-accidental injury increased markedly for the younger males.

Comparison with other studies

Other studies on this multifaceted issue from an EMS perspective could not be identified. Consequently, comparison of our findings is made with accessible research on the issue conducted in in-hospital or outpatient settings.

Infants can have significant occult injuries, and they have a higher risk of non-accidental injuries than older children.19,21 In a previous study conducted in the EMS setting in Denmark, the age group of <1 year had a higher short-term mortality risk than older children.22 Other risk factors include prematurity or low birth weight, limited socioeconomic resources, maternal age <22 years, parenting behaviours and parental alcohol or drug abuse, among others.3 16 17 20 21 23 24

In the national Trauma Audit Research Network (TARN) database of England and Wales for patients with major trauma, 76.3% of the cases with suspected child abuse occurred in children <1 year old.25 26 Although the TARN data and our study include different populations (major trauma patients with an ISS >15 vs all EMS calls, including intentional self-harm), smaller children are underrepresented in our study. This may be explained by the fact that caregivers themselves are responsible for initiating the call to the EMS, and the symptoms of abuse in infants and toddlers may be vague or unnoticeable.27 28

Previous studies indicate that males are more frequently victims of maltreatment than females.16 25 29 Our study design with inclusion of ‘Intentional self-harm’ as a mechanism of injury might affect the reported lower male-to-female ratio, as intentional self-harm is more frequent among females.4 30 31 The sensitivity analysis excluding ‘Intentional self-harm’ and ‘Assault’ showed a trend of higher probability of suspected non-accidental injuries for young males.

Limitations and generalisability

It is a general presumption that there is a large number of unrecognised cases of child abuse. This study was designed to investigate this complex medical issue from an EMS perspective, and as depicted in figure 1, only a small proportion of the children with suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries had been brought to the hospital after an emergency call to the EMS. If the EMS personnel had suspected a non-accidental or intentional self-harm mechanism of injury, but no hospital physician had assigned a corresponding ICD-10 code anywhere in the hospital medical record, the patient was not included in the study population.

Nevertheless, this study included nationwide data from all hospitals and all ambulance stations in all Danish health regions across the country, which increases the internal validity. Results may differ in countries with other population compositions, prehospital systems, variability in ICD-10 coding practices and reporting structures to social authorities. The specific ICD-10 diagnostic codes were chosen from previous research and expert consensus to define the study cohort, and the cohort may differ if alternative ICD-10 codes are applied.

Clinical implications

The EMS are used to activate special teams in special circumstances before reaching the hospital: trauma teams, cardiopulmonary support/cardiac arrest teams, medical emergency teams. In the UK, the Joint Royal Colleges Ambulance Liaison Committee clinical guidelines encourage paramedics to pre-alert the hospital in cases with infants who have suffered injuries, which allows a team of senior clinicians to be ready to assess the patient on arrival.

Our study population had a high prevalence of neuropsychiatric comorbidity or psychosocial challenges, which has also been identified as a risk factor for childhood maltreatment.32 33 Suspicion of a non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury could lead to activation of or conference call to a senior paediatrician, who may help initiate a safeguarding plan or other investigative measures via social authorities.

At least, EMS providers should carefully pass on observations to the hospital personnel from the scene of injury or home environment, if any observation has triggered an odd sense about the mechanism of injury or about the well-being of the child in general.

Prehospital providers’ qualifications to identify and to report child maltreatment may be inadequate.34 35 All Danish healthcare professionals are obligated by law to file a mandatory written report to the social services if they are made aware that a child or adolescent has a need for social support or has been subjected to abuse, and to document the report in the patient’s medical record. Consequently, if this information was absent on the ePMR, we could reasonably assume that a mandatory written report to the social services had not been made. In only 2% of these suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries, any contact with the social services had been documented on the prehospital ePMR, while involvement of police was more frequent (17%). Still, absence of documentation does not necessarily reflect absence of concern.

The reporting process differs across municipalities, and each EMS provider may cover several municipalities during one shift. For each of the EMS providers, cases with suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injuries will be rare, as only 1 in 200 paediatric emergency calls regard these types of injuries.

During the study period, clinical guidelines for the EMS personnel describing reports to the social services were only established in one of the five Danish health regions. The absence of a clear clinical guideline may partly explain the low reporting rate. Since the completion of the study period, two more regions have either implemented a clinical guideline or have a guideline currently in progress.

Clear guidelines and intuitive referral processes are vital to support EMS personnel in this highly important societal task. Appointing a key person at the administrative level might be beneficial for practical guidance on relevant websites, electronic forms and the wording of a mandatory written report to the social services. Future research could include identification of the total number of cases with concern to EMS that were subsequently not diagnosed by a hospital physician, and qualitative studies to identify potential practical and psychological barriers for EMS providers and prehospital physicians to contact social services whenever a non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury is suspected.

In conclusion, out of all calls to the EMS in Denmark regarding children, only a small proportion resulted in a hospital diagnosis for a suspected non-accidental or intentional self-harm injury. Two-thirds of the injuries had been inflicted by another person, while a third had been intentional self-harm. The EMS personnel had noted a mechanism of injury and described objective signs of the injuries on the prehospital ePMR in almost all cases, while involvement of legal or social authorities was uncommon. While intentional self-harm and injuries inflicted by other persons may be studied separately, the same interdisciplinary approach to understanding the child’s social circumstances is necessary. The presence of a senior paediatrician in the emergency department could also be valuable to help identify these patients and act as a coordinator of care between social services, orthopaedics, paediatricians and paediatric psychiatrists.
