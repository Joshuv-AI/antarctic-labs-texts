NIOSH sound level meter application as an adjunct in noise measurement in manufacturing industry
Authors: Zhi Qing Ooi, Sheng Qian Yew, Azizah Zafira, Nur Alya Asyqin Irwan Syarzizi, Chan Hui Ying, Mohammad Fuad Mohammad Affader
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mobile sound level applications have been proposed as potential adjuncts for conventional sound level meters (SLM) in noise measurement. However, their performance remains uncertain in the manufacturing industry setting. This study was aimed to determine the validity, reliability, and agreement of the NIOSH SLM app in measuring noise in the manufacturing industry with reference to conventional SLM. A total of 93 samples were collected from five machineries. An iPhone 12 Pro and an iPhone 12 mini, each installed with the latest version of NIOSH SLM app, were tested against the conventional SLM (as gold standard). Three noise parameters, namely the average noise level (i.e., LAeq), maximum sound level (i.e., LMax) and 8-hour time-weighted average (i.e., TWA-8 h) were measured by all devices. Concurrent validity was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient while two-way mixed effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC 3,1) was used to assess the intra-rater reliability. The agreement in the noise parameters measured was assessed using the Bland-Altman plot. The NIOSH SLM app demonstrated high concurrent validity, as measured by Pearson’s correlation (p < 0.001) and moderate-to-good reliability, as measured by ICC 3,1 (p < 0.001) in all three noise parameters measured. The LAeq and TWA-8 h measured by the NIOSH SLM app were in agreement with the conventional SLM, with the exception of LMax. The NIOSH SLM app is valid and reliable in measuring noise when compared to conventional SLM. Two out of three noise parameters measured by NIOSH SLM app were agreeable to that measured by the conventional SLM.

Introduction

Unwanted sound, commonly referred to as noise, poses a significant risk to hearing loss, characterised by the partial or complete inability to perceive sounds in one or both ears according to the World Health Organization (WHO)1. This condition represents a significant global public health concern, impacting a substantial segment of the population. A systematic review by Haile et al. (2021)2 indicated that in 2019, around 20.3% of the world’s population, equivalent to 1.57 billion individuals, encountered hearing loss. Notably, out of this total, 403.3 million individuals experienced moderate or more severe forms of hearing loss. In Malaysia, the prevalence of hearing loss was estimated to be 21.5%2. Of note, noise can be generated from various sources, both natural and human-made3. However, a substantial proportion of noise is generated from industrial activities, specifically the manufacturing industry4.

Despite numerous efforts to mitigate the exposure to excessive noise during the conduct of manufacturing activities, the incidence of occupational noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL), defined as partial or complete hearing impairment resulting from one’s employment, remains elevated, ranging from 11.2% to 58%5. Worst still, the burden of occupational NIHL is more pronounced in developing nations6 and it has been reported to be the second most self-reported occupational illness in this region7. Therefore, it comes as no surprise that a recent study conducted locally among palm oil mill workers revealed a substantial overall prevalence of occupational NIHL in this particular workforce, amounting to 50.8%8. Consistent with this research, another local study involving 173 workers in manufacturing factories reported a significant occupational NIHL prevalence of 49.7%9. This evidence indicates that the noise surveillance and/or control measures at manufacturing industry is inadequate. Consequently, the elevated prevalence of occupational NIHL has markedly intensified the adverse impact on the health and overall well-being of workers.

Conventionally, a sound level meter (SLM) is used to measure noise level in the workplace10. The conventional SLM is designed for real-time measurement of noise levels, typically used as a handheld or fixed device to gauge instantaneous noise levels at a specific location10. Despite its significance in measuring noise levels, this device has certain limitations. For instance, regular calibrations are required for accurate measurements using the conventional SLMs, and a lack of calibration or improper calibration can introduce errors11. Moreover, the correct operation and placement of the conventional SLMs require knowledge and skill, and inaccurate measurements may result from the misapplication of the conventional SLM by inexperienced users12. Not to mention, the device itself and the regular calibration require high cost of operation. In view of the abovementioned limitations, various types of mobile sound level applications have been developed and reported in literature, with many of them offering free access in Google Play Store and/or Apple Store. Such applications include the SPLnFFT app13, SoundMeter app13, SLA Lite run14, and NIOSH SLM app15. While these mobile sound level applications offer numerous advantages, they do come with inherent limitations, with the key concern being that a majority of mobile sound level applications are lacking of proper validation in real-world scenarios, in terms of validity and reliability16–18. While some applications have been tested in settings like coffee shops, restaurants, commuter trains, classrooms, offices, hospitals, mining areas, markets, and streets, their effectiveness and appropriateness in the manufacturing sector remain unknown.

Given the above limitations and the lack of evidence-based noise measuring mobile applications for noise measurement in manufacturing setting, the current study was aimed to determine the validity, reliability and agreement of the NIOSH SLM app for noise measurement in the manufacturing industry, in comparison to the conventional SLM.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted in May 2024, involving noise measurement in a manufacturing factory located within the Klang Valley. Such study location is chosen due to its status as the economic focal point of Malaysia, housing a significant concentration of manufacturing industries. Furthermore, the selection of this location is informed by a notable incidence of reported cases of occupational NIHL in this area19.

Sampling method

A convenience sampling approach was employed to select an eligible manufacturing factory for the noise measurement. A metal processing manufacturing factory was selected as it: (i) involves at least one manufacturing process using machinery in their production lines; (ii) operates machineries that expose workers to noise; (iii) is easily accessible by the investigators; (iv) is willing to cooperate with the investigators; and (v) consists of at least three departments in the workplace. Upon the factory provided their written consent, a walk-through survey was conducted at that manufacturing factory to identify the location and number of machineries available in each of the department.

Sample size Estimation

The sample size (noise measurement) required for each machinery was calculated using the GitHub online software20. In term of validity, given the Pearson’s correlation coefficient, r = 0.921, power = 80%, confidence interval = 95%, and type I error (α) = 0.05, the sample size required was 7. Meanwhile, as for reliability, given the intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC = 0.90 (ρ1)22, minimum acceptable ICC (ρ0) = 0.7023, power = 80%, and type I error (α) = 0.05, the sample size required was 23. The sample size of 23 was inflated by at least 10% to cater for missing data. Hence, the final sample size estimated was 26. To encompass a broad spectrum of noise levels (Table 1), ranging from low to high noise level, efforts were made to measure noise emitted by five different machineries (i.e., milling machine, grinder, table saw machine, lathe machine and boring machine) in that manufacturing factory.

Data collection tools

A conventional SLM, iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini were utilised to measure the noise levels. The Quest™ SoundPro™ SE-DL Series SLM (model number SP-SE-2-1/3) was used. The conventional SLM was configured to the A-weighting and the data-logged noise levels were set at 5-min intervals. The conventional SLM was calibrated before data collection using a sound calibrator. The selection of the iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini were influenced by the exclusive availability of the NIOSH SLM app on Apple Store. The latest version (i.e., version 1.2.6) of NIOSH SLM app was used.

Data collection procedures

The data collection procedures were carried out by the four researchers, with the assistance and monitoring by an industrial hygienist. During data collection, the conventional SLM (i.e., the gold standard) as well as the iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini (i.e., test device) were mounted on separate tripods, at one meter away from the machineries in the manufacturing factories. All devices were placed at one meter above the floor and at least 0.6 m away from any walls whenever possible to minimise the influence of reflected sound. Also, all devices were placed as close together as possible to capture the same noise. The noise level was measured by all devices simultaneously, with the differences in start and stop times between all devices within two seconds. The length of each measurement lasted for 10 min for each machinery. The average noise level (LAeq), maximum sound level (LMax), and the 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA-8 h) measured by the conventional SLM, iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini were recorded respectively.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed to describe all three noise parameters (i.e., LAeq, LMax, and TWA-8 h) measured by both conventional SLM and the NIOSH SLM app (operated on iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini). The effectiveness of the NIOSH SLM app was assessed using the concurrent validity, intra-rater reliability, and agreement test.

Concurrent validity was assessed by calculating the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the noise parameters measured by the NIOSH SLM app and those measured by the conventional SLM. As the assumption of normality was not met, Spearman’s correlation was used to analyse the concurrent validity of the NIOSH SLM app. If the Spearman’s correlation coefficient, r, is between 0.70 and 1.00, there is a strong correlation between the noise parameters measured by both devices. If r is between 0.40 and 0.69, there is a moderate correlation between the noise parameters measured by both devices. If r is less than 0.40, the correlation between the noise parameters measured by both devices is weak24. Concurrent validity of the mobile sound level application was considered acceptable if the correlation with the conventional SLM is at least 0.7023.

A two-way mixed effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC 3,1) was used to assess the intra-rater reliability of the NIOSH SLM app25. ICC was described in the form of range, defined by the 95% confidence interval. ICC of 0.90 or greater indicate excellent reliability, ICC between 0.75 and 0.89 indicate good reliability, ICC between 0.50 and 0.74 indicate moderate reliability, and ICC less than 0.5 are indicative of poor reliability25. ICC is a non-parametric statistic and is robust against violations of normality assumptions.

The agreement in the noise parameters measured by both devices (i.e., conventional SLM versus iPhone 12 Pro, conventional SLM versus iPhone 12 Mini, and iPhone 12 Pro versus iPhone 12 Mini) was graphically presented using the Bland-Altman plot26. If the points are evenly distributed around the line of equality (i.e., zero line), it indicates good agreement between the two devices26. If there is a consistent pattern above or below the line of equality, it suggests a systematic bias that needs to be considered. Limits of agreement will also be calculated in the Bland-Altman plot, which represent the range within which 95% of the differences between the devices are expected to fall. These limits are typically calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 times the standard deviation of the differences. A cut-off of 10dBA was set a priori as the acceptable discrepancy in order to determine agreement.

Ethics consideration

Ethical approval for the present study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the National University of Malaysia (JEP-2024-071). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. While the participating manufacturing factory did not receive monetary benefits, their workers were educated on workplace hearing protection. The top management was notified for noise level measured exceeding the permissible limits (i.e., 85 dBA) and further actions were discussed. All data collection and analysis procedures were adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

In total, 93 samples were collected from the five machines (i.e., milling machine, grinder, table saw machine, lathe machine and boring machine) in the factory. There were significant correlations in LAeq between iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini (r = 0.89, p < 0.001), between iPhone 12 Pro and conventional SLM (r = 0.96, p < 0.001), and between iPhone 12 Mini and conventional SLM (r = 0.86, p < 0.001). The correlations in LMax between iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini (r = 0.57, p < 0.001), between iPhone 12 Pro and conventional SLM (r = 0.80, p < 0.001), as well as between iPhone 12 Mini and conventional SLM (r = 0.72, p < 0.001) were also significant. Notably, there were significant correlations in TWA-8 h between iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini (r = 0.71, p < 0.001), between iPhone 12 Pro and conventional SLM (r = 0.92, p < 0.001), and between iPhone 12 Mini and conventional SLM (r = 0.73, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the validity, reliability, and agreement of the NIOSH SLM app in measuring noise as compared to a conventional SLM. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the NIOSH SLM app operating on an iOS smartphone without calibration or an external microphone, can be valid in noise measurement in real world setting. Such finding is in agreement with Jacob et al. 202028. Whereby the Spearman’s correlation of all noise parameters between different pair of measuring devices remains statistically strong (r ≥ 0.700).

To our knowledge, this is the first study that investigated the reliability of the NIOSH SLM app. It was found that the ICC for LAeq and TWA-8 h exhibits excellent reliability while the ICC for LMax shows moderate reliability, with all three remain statistically significant at 95% confidence interval. Thus, this suggests that the NIOSH SLM app is reliable to be employed for noise measurement in the industries in comparison with the conventional SLM.

Although the NIOSH SLM app demonstrated high validity and reliability in measuring noise as compared against the conventional SLM, its performance in terms of agreement was inconsistent. The different performance in terms of validity and agreement could be explained by the nature of these two analytic methods. This is because validity (as measured via correlation) studies the strength of relationship between two quantitative measurements, whereas Bland-Altman plot focuses on analysing the mean differences as well as constructing the limits of agreement29. Of note, the latter is the better method to assess the comparability between the measurements made by different devices. The high correlation in this study suggests that different devices track changes in the noise level in a similar way, which implies that these three devices are related and tend to respond to changes in a similar manner. Despite the high correlation, the Bland-Altman plot reveals that the different devices do not consistently agree on the exact values.

From the Bland-Altman plot, it was observed that the LAeq and TWA-8 h measured by both iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini were agreeable to those measured by the conventional SLM (i.e., less than 10 dBA for LAeq and TWA 8-Hour, respectively). However, it was observed that LMax measured by both iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini showed disagreement to those measured by the conventional SLM (i.e., more than 10 dBA). Such disagreement can be explained by the fact that the iPhones’ built-in microphone may have high sensitivity to higher impulsive noise and responds more aggressively compared to the conventional SLM at high sound levels, resulting in higher LMax readings30. This is supported by the high agreement between the iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini in all noise parameters (LAeq, LMax, and TWA-8 h). Another plausible explanation is that unlike the conventional SLM, no microphone shield was used to filter out the background noise in iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini31.

Strengths and limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, there are certain unavoidable limitations. First and foremost, the position of gold standard and test devices from the machine in the field was more than the proposed distance of one meter due to hazard consideration (e.g., sharp and high-speed metal dusts produced from the machines may damage the devices). In addition, the lack of compartmentalisation in the real-world setting resulted in the lack of isolation of noise produced by each machine, limiting effective collection of broadest spectrums of noise levels possible. Additionally, the NIOSH SLM app operated on iPhone 12 Pro and iPhone 12 Mini were not calibrated prior to the data collection. However, a previous study conducted by Jacobs et al. has shown the potential of NIOSH SLM app as a screening tool without needing calibration or attaching to any external microphone28. Furthermore, the intra-group variability of the devices (i.e., how consistent a device is when measuring the same noise repeatedly) were not investigated, hence, it was unsure of how consistent or stable the devices are in various environment.

Nonetheless, to our best knowledge, this is the first study that investigated the validity and reliability of a mobile noise measuring app in the real-world setting, which better examined the accuracy of its performance in terms of consistency and accuracy of noise data collected. One of the major strengths of this study is the availability of technical assistance from an assistant industrial hygienist who is knowledgeable and skillful in conducting noise measurement on site, ensuring the standard of procedures in noise measurements was fully adhered. On top of that, the use of Bland-Altman plot allowed further analysis of the performance of the app which focused on agreement of various noise measurements between devices, providing unique insight that previous studies have not extensively explored.

Recommendations

Extension of device testing is highly encouraged to include a wider variety of smartphone models which could help with the generalisation of findings. Efforts in developing standardised protocols and calibration routines for smartphone-based noise measurements are also strongly anticipated to minimise measurement biases as well as to improve the validity and reliability of measurements taken. It is also hoped that the NIOSH SLM app will be available in the Android platform.

Study implications

This study highlights the urgent need for robust noise surveillance in industries, particularly in developing nations facing a substantial burden of occupational NIHL. Although the NIOSH SLM app has yet to be approved for its usage in replacement of the gold standard (i.e., conventional SLM), this study lays the groundwork for future exploration into the use of mobile app for noise monitoring, especially in a manufacturing industry where violation of noise limits is common. It also highlights the potential for integrating smartphone-based noise measurement systems into broader environmental and occupational health monitoring, especially for screening purpose. These findings pave the way for proactive interventions, promising healthier and safer working environments in Malaysia’s manufacturing sector and offering a blueprint for similar industries globally.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the NIOSH SLM app is valid and reliable in measuring noise when compared to the conventional SLM. The LAeq and TWA-8 h measured by NIOSH SLM app were agreeable to that measured by SLM, with the exception of LMax. As such, the NIOSH SLM app is proven to be an ideal adjunct of noise measuring tools in manufacturing industry settings. Steps should be taken to further optimise the performance of NIOSH SLM app and widen its availability to Android mobile devices.
