Ventricular Tachycardia With a Narrow QRS Complex
Authors: Alejandro Manuel López-Pena, Dahyr Olivas-Medina, Alejandro Used-Gavín, Oleksandr Shangutov, Juliana Elices-Teja, Carlos González-Juanatey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular tachycardia (VT) is typically associated with wide QRS complexes; therefore, narrow QRS tachycardia in pacemaker-dependent patients represents a diagnostic challenge.Case SummaryWe report a 79-year-old man with permanent atrial fibrillation and complete atrioventricular block who presented with a regular narrow QRS tachycardia. Device interrogation confirmed ventricular pacing dependence. Electrophysiological studies demonstrated scar-related VT arising from the basal interventricular septum adjacent to the left bundle branch. The tachycardia was characterized by fragmented potentials and slow conduction channels and was entrainable with a short postpacing return cycle, supporting a reentrant mechanism. Targeted ablation rendered VT noninducible. No recurrent arrhythmic events occurred during follow-up.DiscussionVT may present with a narrow QRS morphology in pacemaker-dependent patients when reentrant circuits are located near the His-Purkinje system.Take-Home MessageRecognition of this uncommon mechanism and advanced electrophysiological mapping are essential for accurate diagnosis and targeted ablation.

On arrival at the emergency department, he was tachycardic with borderline hemodynamic tolerance (systolic blood pressure: 90 mm Hg) but preserved peripheral perfusion.

Past Medical History

Four years earlier, he had received a single-chamber pacemaker for AF with complete atrioventricular (AV) block, with near-complete ventricular pacing. The device was functioning normally.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included ventricular tachycardia (VT), supraventricular tachycardia with intrinsic AV conduction, idiopathic conduction system–dependent tachycardias including fascicular VT, and nodoventricular accessory pathway–mediated tachycardias.

Investigations

The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed a regular narrow QRS tachycardia at 180 beats/min (Figure 1A). Pacemaker interrogation revealed VVIR programming (lower rate: 60 beats/min, maximum tracking rate: 130 beats/min), absence of an intrinsic escape rhythm, and nearly 100% ventricular pacing. An episode of sustained tachycardia with a cycle length of 320 milliseconds lasting over 1 hour was documented. Transthoracic echocardiography showed mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction without other structural abnormalities.

Management

An electrophysiological study with right ventricular electroanatomical mapping identified a scar extending from the free wall to the basal septum, consistent with the suspected tachycardia origin, with fragmented and early diastolic potentials. As clinical tachycardia was noninducible, substrate modification of the basal septum was performed, abolishing these potentials, and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator with left bundle branch pacing was implanted for secondary prevention. The patient was later readmitted with an arrhythmic storm due to recurrent monomorphic VT effectively treated by the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. ECG documentation revealed recurrence of the same regular narrow QRS tachycardia, prompting a second electrophysiological study via a retroaortic left ventricular approach (Figures 2A and 2B). VT was induced, and a slow conduction channel with late diastolic potentials was identified in the basal posteroseptal region near the proximal left bundle branch. During the arrhythmia, more than 90% of the tachycardia cycle length was recorded in this region, and entrainment demonstrated a short postpacing return cycle, supporting a reentrant mechanism. Outcome and Follow-Up

At more than 6 months of clinical and device follow-up, no further arrhythmic events have been recorded.

Discussion

VT is typically associated with wide QRS complexes; therefore, the occurrence of a regular tachycardia with a narrow or borderline QRS morphology in a patient with complete ventricular pacing dependence represents a major diagnostic challenge. In this setting, supraventricular tachycardia with intrinsic AV conduction—such as organization of AF into atypical atrial flutter—is often initially suspected.

In the present case, however, the patient was completely pacemaker-dependent, as confirmed by device interrogation and prior follow-up, with no evidence of intrinsic AV conduction. Furthermore, VT was documented during the electrophysiological study while the patient remained in AF, effectively excluding a supraventricular mechanism.

Idiopathic conduction system–dependent tachycardias, including fascicular VT, were also considered. Nevertheless, electroanatomical mapping revealed a heterogeneous scar in the basal posteroseptal region of the left ventricle, adjacent to the proximal left bundle branch. A slow conduction channel with late diastolic potentials was identified, and the tachycardia was entrainable, supporting a scar-mediated reentrant ventricular mechanism rather than enhanced automatism or a conduction system–dependent circuit occurring in a structurally normal myocardium.

The close proximity of the reentrant circuit to the His-Purkinje system explains the narrow QRS morphology during VT, allowing rapid ventricular activation through the conduction system. Although rarely reported, this mechanism may lead to misdiagnosis, particularly in patients with ventricular pacing devices.

Much less frequently, true nodoventricular accessory pathways have been described, which can generate regular tachycardias with narrow QRS complexes even in the context of complete AV block. However, these entities are exceptional and, in our case, would not explain the electroanatomical findings or the response to ablation targeting the ventricular substrate.1

Conclusions

This case highlights a rare form of reentrant VT arising from the left basal septum and closely related to the conduction system, capable of presenting with a narrow QRS complex.
