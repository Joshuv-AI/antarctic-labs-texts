PHYB and PIF3 repress OLE4 expression to regulate hypocotyl length and ABA signaling in Arabidopsis
Authors: Manchun Ji, Yixiang Gao, Huiying Liu, Xiaoya Lin, Hao Zhao, Zhongbao Wang, Quanjun Liu, Lanjie Zheng, Yong Shi, Jie Gao, Wenjing Hu, Xu Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOLEOSIN proteins (OLEs) are the predominant class of proteins localized on the surface of oil bodies. Previous studies have established that light facilitates the degradation of oil bodies via the action of PHOTOCHROME A/B (PHYA/B). However, the specific roles of OLEs in light signaling pathways and their underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we provide evidence that OLE4 inhibits hypocotyl elongation under red light conditions but has no significant effect in the dark. We demonstrated that red light suppressed both the expression and protein accumulation of OLE4. PHYB reduces OLE4 levels, moreover PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 3 (PIF3) directly represses OLE4 by binding to its G-box motifs; this repression is further strengthened by HDA5 and HDA6. Transcriptomic analysis of the ole4 mutant indicated substantial changes in the expression of genes involved in abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, stress responses, and light stimulus pathways. Correspondingly, ABA levels were markedly elevated in OLE4 overexpression lines (OLE4#1), as well as in phyB-9 and pif3-3 mutants under red light exposure. Applying external ABA restored the hypocotyl growth in ole4 mutants and increased the expression of both OLE4 and PIF3. Additionally, the ole4 mutant exhibited heightened sensitivity to ABA under red light and diminished tolerance to salinity and drought stress conditions. Collectively, these results reveal that OLE4 connects the PHYB-PIF3-HDA5/6 signaling pathway with ABA signaling, thereby controlling hypocotyl elongation regulated by red light.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07787-x.Keywords: PHYB, PIF3, HDA5/6, OLE4, Red light, ABA

Introduction

Oilseed plants store significant amounts of lipids, mainly triacylglycerols (TAGs), within the oil bodies of their seeds, such as those found in Arabidopsis thaliana and soybean. Most seed plants use oil bodies to store energy in their seeds [1]. In addition to energy storage, oil bodies contribute to plant growth and development and help plants withstand both biotic and abiotic stresses. For instance, in the apical dominance pathway, abscisic acid (ABA) enhances apical dominance by promoting the accumulation of oil body proteins, which increases oil body content [2]. Oil bodies are enclosed by a phospholipid monolayer covered with specific membrane proteins [3]. These proteins include oleosins (OLEs), caleosins (Cals), putative adipose-regulatory protein (SEIPIN), oil body-associated protein 1 (OBAP1), and lipid droplet-associated proteins 1 and 2 (LDAP1/LDAP2). Oleosins are the main membrane proteins in oil bodies. In Arabidopsis, there are five seed-specific oleosins: OLE1 ~ 5.

Research has demonstrated that oil bodies are converted into sugars during de-etiolation [4]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, growth under high light conditions led to an increase in oil content compared to that under lower light levels [5]. Additionally, oil bodies disappear under red and far-red light (R/FR) through the action of phytochromes in Arabidopsis seedlings [4]. Most studies have concentrated on regulating oil body accumulation, as well as managing their size and stability [3, 6–9]. Nevertheless, the role of OLEs in light signal transduction is still unclear.

Light is a crucial environmental factor that influences plant growth and development. Various hormonal pathways are connected to light signaling to facilitate developmental and environmental responses. For instance, PIF3 and PIF4 integrate signals from light and gibberellin [10]. Moreover, PIFs and HY5 link light signals with several hormones [11–14]. PHYB is the main photoreceptor responsible for red/far-red (R/FR) light responses [15]. PIFs are basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors that negatively regulate photomorphogenesis [16] and act as central signaling components in this process [17]. PIFs specifically bind to the G-box DNA motif (CACGTG) [17–21], controlling the transcription of their target genes. Both PHYs and PIFs have been shown to link light signaling with abscisic acid (ABA) signaling. Light-activated photoreceptors suppress ABA signaling by interacting with key genes in the ABA pathway [22]. PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 directly regulate ABI5 transcription by binding to its promoter, thereby modulating ABA signaling in dark conditions [23]. Moreover, unlike the wild type, ABA does not inhibit hypocotyl elongation in pif4pif5 mutants [24]

The transcription of OLE4 is inhibited by red light, as we found in this study. We showed that both PHYB and PIF3 inhibit OLE4 transcription, with PIF3 directly interacting with the G-box motif located within the promoter region of OLE4. Furthermore, PIF3 interacts with HDA5 and HDA6 to repress OLE4 expression. RNA-seq analysis revealed that OLE4 responds to ABA, and the ole4 mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to ABA and osmotic stress under red light. In summary, OLE4 acts as a positive element in the light signaling regulatory network, which is independently regulated by PHYB and PIF3.

Result

OLE4 represses hypocotyl elongation under red light

Light can trigger the breakdown of oil bodies [4]. We explored whether OLE proteins participate in light signaling and discovered that light suppresses the transcription of OLE family genes (Supplemental Fig. 1). We obtained an OLE4 loss-of-function mutant line, SALK-206544 (Supplemental Fig. 2 A and 2B), and created transgenic plants overexpressing OLE4-Flag-mCherry (Supplemental Fig. 2C). Notably, ole4 mutant seedlings showed longer hypocotyls under white (WL), red (R), far-red (FR), and blue (BL) light conditions, whereas OLE4-overexpressing plants had shorter hypocotyls, with no significant difference observed in darkness (Fig. 1A). This suggests that OLE4 acts as a negative regulator of hypocotyl elongation, with the strongest effect observed under red light (Fig. 1A). To verify OLE4 involved in red light signaling pathway, this study checked the expression of light signal-related marker genes, CHALCONE SYNTHASE (CHS) and RUBISCO SMALL SUBUNITs (RBCSs), which expression level is highly correlated with light-dependent growth inhibition [25–27]. We then grew Col-0, ole4, OLE4#1, and OLE4#3 seedlings for four days and measured the transcript levels of RUBISCO SMALL SUBUNITs (RBCSs) and CHALCONE SYNTHASE (CHS) using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). As shown in Fig. 1B and Supplemental Fig. 4, all RBCS genes were upregulated in OLE4#1 and OLE4#3 compared to Col-0, while their expression was significantly lower in ole4 mutants relative to Col-0. These results highlight the important role of OLE4 in light-exposed seedlings. In conclusion, our findings indicate that OLE4 suppresses hypocotyl elongation in red light.

To further investigate whether OLE4 regulates hypocotyl elongation under red light through cell number or cell size, we examined the hypocotyl epidermal cells of 4-d wild-type and ole4 mutant seedlings under red light. There was no significant difference in the number of hypocotyl cells between the ole4 mutant and the wild type (Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, the length of hypocotyl cells in the ole4 mutant was significantly greater than that in the wild type (Supplementary Fig. 3A and 3 C). These findings imply that OLE4-mediated inhibition of hypocotyl elongation under red light may be attributed to cell elongation.

PHYB represses the expression of OLE4

To elucidate the specific light wavelengths influencing OLE4 expression, we assessed OLE4 transcript levels under DK, WL, R, FR, BL, as well as during transitions from dark to light conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, compared with the dark condition, all light treatments could inhibit the transcriptional level of OLE4; notably, red light exerted a more significant inhibitory effect on OLE4 expression than far-red light. Meanwhile, during the transition from dark to red light, the transcript abundance of OLE4 was also significantly lower than that in seedlings transferred from dark to far-red light (Fig. 1D). We further detected the transcriptional levels of other members of the OLE family under red light, and the results all showed that red light could inhibit the expression of this family of genes (Supplementary Fig. 6). To examine the impact of light on OLE4 protein accumulation, OLE4#1 seedlings were cultivated under DK, WL, R, FR, and BL for four days, or grown in darkness for three days followed by exposure to various light conditions for one day. Protein quantification of OLE4-Flag-mCherry revealed decreased levels under red light compared to far-red and blue light treatments (Figs. 1E and 1 F). Collectively, these findings indicate that light modulates both the transcriptional expression and protein accumulation of OLE4. To investigate the involvement of photoreceptors in regulating OLE4 expression, we compared OLE4 transcript levels between wild-type and photoreceptor mutant lines. No significant difference was detected in cry1-304 mutants; however, OLE4 transcripts were upregulated in phyA-211 and phyB-9 mutants, with a more pronounced increase in phyB-9 (Fig. 1G). Subsequently, we detected the expression level of OLE4 in the phyB-9 mutant and phyA-211 mutant under red light (PHYB-activated condition) and far-red light (PHYA-activated condition), respectively. The results indicated that both PHYB and PHYA can repress the transcription of OLE4 (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results suggest that the photoreceptors PHYA and PHYB serve as principal regulators of OLE4 expression.

PIF3 binds to the promoter of OLE4 and represses its expression

Given the significant regulation of OLE4 expression by light, we explored the potential involvement of transcription factors associated with the light signaling pathway in modulating OLE4 expression. PIFs and HY5 are pivotal transcription factors within these pathways. We assessed OLE4 gene expression in loss-of-function mutants and observed that expression levels remained unaltered in the hy5-215 mutant, whereas they were elevated in the pif3-3 and pif4-1 mutants, with a more pronounced increase in pif3-3 relative to the wild-type (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have conclusively demonstrated that PIF3 directly binds to promoters of genes containing the G-box (CACGTG) cis-regulatory element to modulate their transcription [17, 18, 20, 21]. Subsequent analysis of the OLE4 promoter sequence revealed the presence of three G-box motifs (Fig. 2B). To investigate whether PIF3 directly interacts with the OLE4 promoter and regulates its expression, we conducted in vitro electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs). The results indicated that both the G1 motif and the combined G2G3 motifs induced a mobility shift (Fig. 2C), signifying PIF3 binding to these sites within the OLE4 promoter in vitro. Furthermore, a transient transcriptional assay was performed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves to evaluate PIF3’s regulatory effect on OLE4 expression in vivo. Recombinant plasmids containing the OLE4 promoter fused to the luciferase reporter gene (OLE4pro:LUC), encompassing the G-box motifs, were constructed. Notably, co-expression of PIF3-GFP, but not GFP alone, resulted in suppression of OLE4 promoter activity (Figs. 2D and 2E). Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence that PIF3 directly binds to the OLE4 promoter and represses its transcriptional activity.

PIF3 interacts with HDA5/HDA6 to repress OLE4 expression

PIF3 has been conventionally characterized as a transcriptional activator; however, in the present context, it functions to repress the expression of OLE4. This finding implies the participation of a transcriptional coregulator that cooperates with PIF3 to inhibit OLE4 expression. Prior research has established that HDA19 and PIF3 act synergistically to repress downstream gene transcripts [28]. Based on this, we hypothesized that histone deacetylases (HDAs) may interact with PIF3 to regulate the transcriptional activity of genes targeted by PIF3. Consistent with this hypothesis, yeast two-hybrid assays demonstrated a physical interaction between PIF3 and both HDA5 and HDA6 (Fig. 3A). To corroborate these interactions in planta, we conducted split-luciferase (split-LUC) assays, which confirmed a direct association between PIF3 and HDA5/6 (Fig. 3B, 3 C). Our further investigation revealed that in tobacco leaves under red light, PHYB can promote the interaction between PIF3 and HDA5 or HDA6 (Supplementary Fig. 8A, 8B). These results indicate that PIF3 is capable of interacting with HDA5/6 proteins.

To further elucidate the regulatory role of PIF3 in collaboration with HDA5 and HDA6 on the expression of OLE4, we constructed luciferase (LUC) reporter constructs driven by the OLE4 promoter (OLE4pro:LUC) and co-expressed them with PIF3 alone or in combination with HDA5 or HDA6 in N. benthamiana leaves. Consistent with the necessity of cofactors for transcriptional repression, the expression levels of OLE4pro:LUC were reduced when HDA5 or HDA6 were co-expressed with PIF3, relative to the expression observed with PIF3 alone (Fig. 3D, 3E). On this basis, we further verified whether PHYB could enhance the inhibitory effect of PIF3 and HDA5/6 on the OLE4 promoter. Tobacco transient expression assays showed that the addition of PHYB indeed enhanced the inhibitory function of this complex (Supplementary Fig. 8C and 8D). These results suggest that PIF3, HDA5/6, and PHYB may form a regulatory complex to collectively participate in the transcriptional repression of OLE4.

OLE4 modulates ABA signaling pathway

The regulation of hypocotyl elongation by OLE4 under red light conditions led us to examine the impact of OLE4 on downstream gene expression. The wild type and the ole4 mutant were cultivated under red light for four days. Subsequently, RNA was extracted from these seedlings and subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. Our analysis revealed that 1,045 genes exhibited significant upregulation, while 578 genes showed significant downregulation in the ole4 mutant, using a threshold of ≥ 2.0-fold change and P ≤ 0.05 (Fig. 4A). Using gene ontology analysis, it was found that the genes that were differentially expressed were primarily linked to abscisic acid (ABA), lipid metabolism, and light stimulation (Fig. 4B). Notably, OLE4 influenced the expression of several genes involved in the ABA signaling pathway (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Table 1). To validate these transcriptomic changes, qRT-PCR was performed on seedlings grown in red light for four days. The expression of PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE 1-LIKE 5 (PYL5), PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE 1-LIKE 6 (PYL6), and ABA INSENSITIVE 4 (ABI4) were found to be reduced in the ole4 mutant compared to Col-0 (Fig. 4D). Conversely, the expression of ABSCISIC ACID RESPONSIVE ELEMENTS-BINDING FACTOR 3 (ABF3) and ARABIDOPSIS THALIANA PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2CA (PP2CA) was elevated in ole4 relative to Col-0. These findings suggest that OLE4 modulates the ABA signaling pathway by regulating key ABA-responsive genes.

OLE4 affects ABA content and sensitivity in red light

Previous research has established that abscisic acid (ABA) acts as an inhibitor of plant growth [24]. PHYB modulates ABA biosynthesis and signal transduction in plants through various mechanisms, including the direct regulation of genes involved in ABA biosynthesis and interactions with components of ABA signaling pathways [22, 29]. Given our observation that OLE4 influences ABA signaling, we subsequently quantified ABA levels in OLE4#1 transgenic plants, as well as in phyB-9, pif3-3 mutants, and wild-type plants under both DK and R conditions. No significant differences in ABA content were detected under dark conditions. In contrast, under red light exposure, ABA concentrations were significantly elevated in OLE4#1, phyB-9, and pif3-3 plants (Fig. 5F).

Subsequently, we examined the responsiveness of OLE4, PIF3, and PHYB genes expression to abscisic acid (ABA) treatment. Wild-type seedlings were cultivated in red light conditions for four days, followed by treatment with 20 μM exogenous ABA. Expression of PHYB was suppressed by ABA within 15 min (Fig. 5A), and PHYB protein levels exhibited a significant decline after 3 h of exposure to red light (Supplemental Fig. 9A). Conversely, PIF3 transcript abundance increased after one hour of ABA treatment under red light (Fig. 5B); however, PIF3 protein levels did not display notable differences relative to untreated controls (Supplemental Fig. 9B). Regarding OLE4, transcription was rapidly upregulated following one hour of ABA exposure under red light (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis revealed that OLE4 protein levels remained largely unchanged in response to ABA under these conditions (Supplemental Fig. 9C). Additionally, exogenous ABA treatment resulted in the repression of HDA5 and HDA6 transcript levels under red light (Fig. 5D, 5E). Furthermore, expression of PIF3 and HDA6 was elevated in the phyA-211, phyB-9, and cry1-304 mutant lines (Supplemental Fig. 7B–D). Collectively, these results suggest that PHYB and PIF3 modulate OLE4 expression via distinct regulatory pathways. In conclusion, we investigated the potential modulation of OLE4-mediated regulation of hypocotyl elongation by abscisic acid. Our results demonstrated that ABA at concentrations of 0.1 and 0.25 μM inhibited hypocotyl elongation in both Col-0 and ole4 mutant seedlings. Notably, hypocotyls of ole4 mutant seedlings exhibited increased sensitivity to abscisic acid relative to wild-type seedlings when exposed to red light conditions (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, increasing ABA concentrations corresponded with a more pronounced reduction in hypocotyl length in ole4 mutants relative to wild-type seedlings.

RNA-seq data indicated that OLE4 regulates the expression of genes associated with key stress-related pathways, including the ABA signaling pathway, salt stress, and osmotic stress (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Consistent with this, the ole4 mutant showed significantly increased sensitivity to ABA under red light conditions linking the stress-related function of OLE4 to light-dependent contexts (Supplementary Fig. 10A-C). Furthermore, the ole4 mutant exhibited significantly reduced growth vigor and decreased tolerance to salt (NaCl)- and mannitol-induced osmotic stress (Supplementary Fig. 10D-E). These results suggest that OLE4 plays a role in enhancing plant tolerance to osmotic stress.

ABA negatively regulates OLE4 expression in a PIF3-dependent manner

To clarify whether the regulation of OLE4 by ABA is PIF3-dependent, we examined the transcriptional level of OLE4 in wild-type and pif3-3 mutant plants treated with ABA under red light. The results showed that compared with the inductive effect of ABA on OLE4 in the wild type, this induction was significantly enhanced in the pif3-3 mutant (Fig. 6A). To clarify the function of PIF3 in the ABA-mediated OLE4 expression regulatory pathway, we transiently expressed the OLE4p:Luc reporter construct in N. benthamiana leaves. After ABA treatment, luciferase signals were detected in the presence or absence of PIF3, respectively. The results demonstrated that PIF3 could significantly repress the activity of the OLE4 promoter (Fig. 6C, 6D). To investigate the effect of ABA on the binding of PIF3 to the G-box element in the OLE4 promoter, we further performed an EMSA assay, and the results showed that ABA treatment enhanced the binding ability of PIF3 to the probe containing the G-box element (Fig. 6B). Collectively, under red light, ABA negatively regulates OLE4 expression in a PIF3-dependent manner by enhancing the binding of PIF3 to the G-box element in the OLE4 promoter.

Discussion

Plants sense changes in light to regulate their growth and developmental processes [30]. Previous research has indicated that light facilitates the degradation of oil bodies via phytochrome-mediated signaling pathways [4]. This study is the first to reveal that the oleosin protein OLE4 acts as a light-responsive factor, participating in photomorphogenesis through a unique expression regulatory pattern, thereby providing new insights into the non-storage functions of oleosin proteins. Our findings show that light significantly suppresses OLE4 transcription and protein accumulation, with red light exerting the most prominent inhibitory effect (Fig. 1C, 1E). This expression characteristic forms an interesting contrast to its function as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis: the ole4 mutant exhibits elongated hypocotyls under light, while OLE4-overexpressing seedlings display a hypocotyl elongation-inhibited phenotype (Fig. 1A). Additionally, the transcriptional levels of photomorphogenesis marker genes such as RBCS and CHS are significantly upregulated in OLE4-overexpressing lines (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 4). This "light-suppressed expression yet positive regulatory function" pattern is similar to that of PHYA [31], suggesting that OLE4 may participate in light signal transduction through the efficient function of low-abundance proteins or by relying on specific protein interaction networks. The dynamic balance of its expression level may be crucial for the fine-tuning of light responses.

Further mechanistic analysis revealed that the light-responsive expression of OLE4 is dependent on PHYB and PHYA (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Fig. 5), which echoes the previous finding that PHYA/PHYB mediate oil body degradation during seedling de-etiolation [4]. This suggests that a conserved core pathway may underlie the regulation of oil body-related genes by light signals. The weak phenotype of the ole4 mutant is presumably associated with functional redundancy among other members of the OLE gene family—our experimental results showed that OLE4 mutation induces the expression of OLE2 (Supplementary Fig. 12), and its expression compensation effect may attenuate the phenotypic differences of the single mutant. Through promoter analysis and validation, we identified three G-box motifs in the OLE4 promoter region (Fig. 2B) and confirmed that PIF3 can directly bind to these sites to repress OLE4 transcription (Fig. 2C, 2D). Consistent with the enrichment pattern of PIF1/PIF3 at the G-box elements in the OLE5 promoter [32, 33], this indicates that PIF family transcription factors may regulate oleosin genes through a sequence-specific conserved mechanism. Combined with the experimental observation that OLE1 ~ OLE5 genes are all repressed by red light (Fig. 1C-D, Supplementary Fig. 6), we speculate that PIF3 may coordinately regulate multiple OLE family members by directly binding to the G-box motifs in their promoters. Additionally, histone deacetylases HDA5 and HDA6 can further inhibit OLE4 promoter activity through interaction with PIF3 (Fig. 3). This result implies that PIF3 may form a transcriptional repressor complex by recruiting epigenetic modifiers, thereby regulating OLE4 expression at both transcriptional and epigenetic levels, which expands the network dimension of "transcription factor-epigenetic modifier" cooperative regulation in the light signaling pathway. Previous studies have confirmed that PHYB and PIF3 exert complex and complementary regulatory effects on gene expression, and they collectively regulate plant growth, development, metabolism, and immune responses through light signaling pathways [34]. Under specific environmental conditions, PHYB and PIFs can also cooperatively repress the expression of certain target genes; for example, under long-day photoperiods, PHYB can work together with PIF4 and PIF7 to downregulate the transcription of CBFs and COR15a [35]. Our study further found that under red light, PHYB can enhance the inhibitory effect of the PIF3-HDA5/6 complex on the OLE4 promoter (Supplementary Figs. 8C-D). This indicates that under specific signal or environmental contexts, PHYB and PIF3 still regulate the expression of certain genes in a cooperative manner, enriching the regulatory modes of their interaction.

Abscisic acid is a pivotal phytohormone mediating plant responses to abiotic stresses. Environmental stressors such as drought, salinity, and low temperature elevate endogenous ABA concentrations, thereby enhancing stress tolerance [24, 36]. This study is the first to confirm that OLE4 acts as a critical integrator linking light signaling and ABA-mediated regulation, providing a novel molecular target for dissecting the "light-hormone" crosstalk network. Under red light conditions, OLE4 overexpression significantly promotes ABA accumulation (Fig. 5F), while the ole4 loss-of-function mutant exhibits markedly increased sensitivity to drought and salt stresses (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating that OLE4 enhances plant stress resistance by elevating endogenous ABA levels. Meanwhile, OLE4 is deeply involved in the regulation of ABA signaling pathways: in the ole4 mutant, the transcriptional levels of ABA receptors and positive regulatory genes such as PYL5, PYL6, and ABI4 are downregulated, whereas the expression of negative regulators including PP2CA, ABF3, and AP2C1 is upregulated (Fig. 2C, 2D). This suggests that OLE4 may optimize ABA-mediated stress responses by bidirectionally regulating the expression of ABA signaling components. Consistent with previous reports that ABA induces OsOLEO5 transcription [37], this study further expands the function of oleosin proteins in hormone signaling pathways, revealing that they not only respond to ABA signals but also act as key regulators to feedback-regulate ABA biosynthesis and signal transduction.

The synergistic regulatory mechanism of red light and ABA provides a new perspective for understanding the balance between plant growth and stress resistance. Our findings show that under red light, ABA rapidly suppresses the expression of PHYB and HDA5/6, followed by an increase in OLE4 transcriptional level at 1 h and an upregulation of PIF3 expression at 3 h (Fig. 5A-5E). Notably, although ABA induces the initial expression of OLE4 under red light, it simultaneously enhances the binding ability of PIF3 to the G-BOX elements in the OLE4 promoter (Fig. 6B), thereby further strengthening the inhibitory effect on OLE4 (Fig. 6A, 6C-D). Additionally, our luciferase complementation assay indicates that ABA has no significant impact on the interaction between PIF3 and HDA5/HDA6 (Supplementary Figs. 11), suggesting that ABA participates in the inhibitory process mainly by regulating the binding capacity of PIF3 rather than the interaction efficiency between PIF3 and HDA5/HDA6. Based on the aforementioned molecular evidence, we propose a dynamic regulatory model of OLE4 under red light (Fig. 7): ABA first relieves the transcriptional repression of OLE4 by inhibiting PHYB expression, promoting OLE4 accumulation, which in turn suppresses hypocotyl elongation (inhibiting vegetative growth) and enhances stress resistance. Subsequently, the elevated ABA level induces PIF3 transcription, and PIF3 to inhibit OLE4 expression, avoiding excessive OLE4 accumulation that would restrain growth. Thus, this model maintains the dynamic balance between plant growth and stress resistance. This model clearly illustrates the dual regulatory role of ABA in the light signaling pathway: it activates stress response through short-term regulation and maintains growth homeostasis through long-term feedback regulation, reflecting the sophisticated regulatory strategy of plants in adapting to the environment.

A Working Model Depicting that PIF3 interacts with HDA5/6 to inhibit OLE4 expression in PHYB-mediating ABA signaling. Red, red light; Arrows, positive regulation; bar, negative regulation

Although this study has initially revealed the OLE4-mediated crosstalk mechanism between light signaling and the ABA pathway, several key scientific questions remain to be addressed. First, the molecular mechanism by which PHYB coordinates with the PIF3-HDA5/6 complex to regulate OLE4 in Arabidopsis thaliana remains unclear, particularly the in vivo regulatory effect of PHYB and ABA on the interaction between PIF3 and HDA5/6, which requires further verification through in vivo experiments. Unfortunately, due to the lack of relevant multiple overexpression materials and the extremely low abundance of PIF3-YFP protein under red light conditions, direct in vivo verification of this interaction in Arabidopsis is currently not feasible. In future studies, this technical bottleneck needs to be overcome by optimizing material construction (e.g., obtaining stably expressed multi-transgenic lines) or improving detection techniques (e.g., adopting high-sensitivity protein–protein interaction detection methods) to clarify the transcriptional regulatory mode. Second, light simultaneously inhibits OLE4 transcription and protein accumulation (Fig. 1C, 1E). As a core regulator of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation in the light signaling pathway [38, 39], COP1 has been confirmed to be involved in oleosin degradation. We speculate that light may trigger COP1-mediated ubiquitination of OLE4 and its subsequent degradation via the 26S proteasome. Future studies should verify this protein degradation pathway through in vitro ubiquitination assays, cop1 mutant analysis, and other approaches to improve the regulatory network governing OLE4 expression and protein stability. Furthermore, the ole4 single mutant only exhibits a weak phenotype, which suggests that its function may be compensated by other proteins or buffered by regulatory networks. To address the aforementioned issues, in subsequent studies, we will generate double mutant or multi-mutant materials of OLE4 and other oleosin proteins, conduct phenotype identification of light/ABA responses, and further clarify the functional characteristics of OLE4 and its role in the oleosin protein family. Additionally, the specific regulatory network among OLE family members is a direction requiring in-depth exploration in the future, which will help improve the molecular pathway of hypocotyl growth regulation by light signals.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and plant growth conditions

All experiments utilized Columbia-0 (Col-0) ecotype plants. The mutants phyA-211 [40], phyB-9 [41], cry1-304 [42], pif3-3 [43], pif4-1 [44], and hy5-215 [45] were all derived from the Col-0 background. The ole4 mutant (SALK_206544) was procured from the AraShare Science and Technology Service Center of China (www.arashare.cn) and verified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Primer sequences used for ole4 mutant detection are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Seeds were surface-sterilized with 30% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by five to six rinses with sterile water. Subsequently, we place the seeds at 4 °C for 2 to 3 days to simulate spring flowering. Seedlings were cultivated on MS medium under standard growth conditions consisting of a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod.

Creation of genetically modified plants

The full-length CDS of OLE4 was amplified from cDNA via PCR. The amplified fragments were subsequently cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector, resulting in the construction of the Psuper:OLE4-flag-mCherry plasmid. Agrobacterium GV3101 strain was inoculated with the plasmid structure and subsequently transferred into Col-0 using the floral dip technique [46]. The selection of transgenic materials took place in medium containing 25 mg/L hygromycin and they were confirmed through PCR and primer sequencing, as explained in Supplementary Table 2. Confirmed transgenic lines were subjected to western blot analysis for protein detection, and T4 generation plants were utilized for subsequent experimental procedures.

Treatment and phenotypic analysis

For light treatment assays, seeds were sown on agar plates and initially exposed to white light for 12 to 16 h. Thereafter, seedlings were incubated under various light conditions-WL, R, FR, BL, and DK-at intensities of 60, 30, 2.5, 10, and 0 μmol/s/m2, respectively, for a duration of four days unless otherwise specified. Hypocotyl length measurements were performed on 20 to 30 seedlings per genotype. Digital images of four-day-old seedlings were captured, and hypocotyl lengths were quantified using ImageJ software (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

To evaluate the effect of abscisic acid on hypocotyl elongation in ole4 mutants under red light, Col-0 and ole4 mutant were grown for four days with either a mock buffer or ABA at concentrations of 0.1 or 0.25 μM under red light conditions. Hypocotyl lengths were subsequently measured. Additionally, Col-0, OLE4#1, PHYB-GFP, and YFP-PIF3 seedlings were cultivated under red light for four days on ½ MS medium containing either mock buffer or 20 μM ABA for varying durations (0, 15 min, 30 min, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h).

For the assessment of salt and osmotic stress sensitivity, wild-type and ole4 mutant seedlings were grown on media supplemented with varying concentrations of NaCl or mannitol (0, 150, 200, and 300 mM) for five days, with distilled water serving as the control. Digital images of five-day-old seedlings were recorded. Phenotypic classification was based on seedling size and germination status, categorized as large seedlings, small seedlings, seed germination, and non-germinated seeds. The frequency of each category was quantified, and percentages were calculated. All data presented represent the mean values obtained from at least three independent biological replicates.

Method for statistical analysis of epidermal cell length and number in hypocotyls

First, Arabidopsis seedlings cultured under red light for 4 days were selected, and microscopic images of a 2-mm segment in the middle region of the hypocotyl were captured using a 10 × objective lens. Subsequently, the image scale was calibrated using ImageJ software, and the longitudinal length of each intact epidermal cell within the field of view was quantitatively measured. Meanwhile, the total number of longitudinally arranged epidermal cells in this region was counted. Three biological replicates were set for each genotype, with at least 50 cells counted per replicate to ensure data reliability.

RNA Extraction and qRT-PCR Analysis

Seedlings were cultivated for four days under either standard or experimental conditions. Total RNA was isolated from plants utilizing the AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biotechnology) to facilitate gene expression analysis. cDNA was synthesized using All-in-One-Script RT premixes (Kermey Biotechnology). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on the LightCycler® 480 System (Roche) utilizing the 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Kermey Biotechnology). Primer sequences for all genes analyzed are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

RNA-seq analysis

The RNA-seq experiment included four samples, comprising two biological replicates per genotype. Total RNA was isolated from wild-type and ole4 seedlings grown on ½ MS medium for four days in R. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using the Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina PE150 platform by Wuhan Frasergen Bioinformatics Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The resulting reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via DESeq2, with statistical significance adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure; genes exhibiting an adjusted p-value (padj) less than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Subsequently, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was conducted to determine significantly enriched biological processes using appropriate bioinformatics tools.

Protein extraction and western-blot

Total protein extraction was performed using an IP lysis buffer comprised 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM sodium chloride, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, and 1 mM PMSF, and 1 × PIC (Beyotime)[47]. Primary antibodies included anti-Flag (diluted 1:15,000 to 1:30,000 [v/v], Sigma), anti-GFP (1:10,000 [v/v], Abways), anti-Histones (1:10,000 [v/v], Abways), and anti-Actin (1:15,000 [v/v], Abways). Secondary antibodies comprised horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG and anti-rabbit IgG, both diluted 1:10,000 (v/v) (Abways). Protein signals were detected using the Li-Cor/Odyssey imaging system. Histones or Actin served as loading controls to verify equal protein loading. Consistent results were obtained by independently repeating all immunoblotting trials at least three times. Representative data are provided.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

The pGBKT7 (BD) vector was used to clone the full-length CDS of PIF3 and HDA5/HDA6, with the GAL4 DNA binding domain in place, and then digested with EcoR I and BamH I enzymes, as described in Supplementary Table 2. Similarly, the pGADT7 (AD) vector was used to clone the full-length CDS of HDAs, also digested with EcoR I and BamH I, fused to the GAL4 activation domain. These constructs were co-transformed into yeast cells and cultured on SD-Trp-Leu (DDO) at 30 °C for 3 to 5 days. Transformed yeast cells were then suspended in liquid DDO medium to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1. Subsequently, 4 μL aliquots of the suspension were spotted onto DDO or QDO (SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade) media supplemented with 50 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) for growth assessment. These dropout plates were used to detect direct interactions.

Split-luciferase Assays

The expression assays for OLE4 and HDA5/HDA6 in N. benthamiana leaves were performed. The full-length CDS of PIF3 was cloned into the N-terminal LUC (N-LUC) vector, while HDA5 and HDA6 CDS were cloned into the C-terminal LUC (C-LUC) vector. These plasmids were transformed into GV3101. Plasmids within GV3101 were cultured overnight. Cells were collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min) and resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 of 1.0. After mixing at a 1:1 ratio, the suspensions containing the expression constructs permeated into N. benthamiana leaves[48]. Luciferase images were captured using the NightShade LB 985 cooled CCD imaging system (Berthold, Germany) with exposure times ranging from 90 to 120 s.

Cis-acting element analysis

In order to enhance our understanding of the potential cis-regulatory elements located within the promoter regions of the OLE4 gene, a 2 kb sequence upstream of the OLE4 gene was extracted utilizing the Tbtools software. This 5’ upstream sequence serves as a critical component for mapping the regulatory elements that may influence gene expression. Subsequently, a thorough analysis of the various putative cis-regulatory elements present within this sequence was conducted using the PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [49]. This comprehensive analysis allows ours to systematically identify and characterize the regulatory motifs that could play significant roles in the transcriptional regulation of the OLE4 gene.

Effector-reporter Assays

The OLE4 promoter fragment, located upstream of the start codon, was PCR-amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA and cloned into the modified pCAMBIA1305-LUC vector to create the reporter construct OLE4pro:LUC. The CDS of PIF3, PHYB, HDA5, and HDA6 were inserted into the pCAMBIA2300-GFP vector to generate effector expression constructs. All primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 2. Transformed Agrobacterium cultures were grown in LB medium at 28 °C with shaking until reaching an optical density at OD600 of 0.5–0.6. The cells were then harvested, resuspended to an OD600 of 1.0, and combined in ratios of 1:1 or 1:1:1 as needed. This mixture was incubated at room temperature for over 3 h before being infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves in various combinations. Post-infiltration, plants were kept in darkness at 22 °C for 24 h, followed by 24 h under normal growth conditions. Luciferase activity was detected using a cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) imaging system (NightShade LB 985, Berthold, Germany) with a 2-min exposure. Each experiment included at least three biological replicates.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays

To purify the protein, the complete CDS of PIF3 was inserted into the pET28a vector and transformed into BL21. The recombinant PIF3-His protein was produced in E. coli. Induction of protein expression occurred at 37 °C for 3 h with 1 mM IPTG once the culture reached an optical density (OD600) of 0.8. The samples were spun at 8000 rpm for 20 min at a temperature of 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was harvested. After thawing, the pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer that was composed of 50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole. Cell lysis was achieved through sonication on ice, followed by clarification of the lysate via centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then placed in a new 50 mL tube and incubated with 1 mL of Ni–NTA resin (Qiagen) while shaking at 30 rpm at 4 °C for 2 h. The Ni–NTA resin underwent washing with both lysis buffer and wash buffer. Ultimately, the recombinant PIF3-His protein was eluted using the elution buffer.

In the EMSA experiments, either PIF3-His or His was mixed with CY5-labeled probes in PBS and incubated for 0.5 h at 25 °C. In 0.5 × TBE buffer, the mixture was separated by 7.5% natural polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the detection of the labeled probes was accomplished using the Li-Cor/Odyssey system.

Quantification of plant ABA contents

The seedlings of Col-0, phyb-9, pif3-3 and OLE4#1 grown in red light or darkness for 4 days were detected ABA contents. For each genotype, three separate replicates were gathered. Endogenous ABA extraction was performed on 50 mg of plant material per biological replicate. Endogenous ABA content was determined by Wuhan Maiwei Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. ABA content was detected by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/). To extract ABA from seedlings, a methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, V/V/V) approach was employed. An internal standard (IS) for quantification was introduced into the extract by adding 10 μL of a 100 ng/mL internal standard mixed solution. Subsequently, the mixture was vortex—agitated for 10 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. After that, the supernatant was decanted into new plastic microtubes. It was then evaporated until completely dry, redissolved in 100 μL of 80% methanol (V/V), and passed through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The processed sample was then ready for further LC—MS/MS analysis [50].

Statistical analysis

All of the bar charts were presented using GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25 software: Levene’s test was first applied to assess the homogeneity of variances among groups; when homogeneity was confirmed, Tukey’s HSD multiple comparison test was employed to identify intergroup differences (p < 0.05). According to the t-test of the students, an asterisk indicates a significant difference (* P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01).
