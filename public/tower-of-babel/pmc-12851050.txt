Esophageal varices detection and bleeding risk assessment with artificial intelligence: a systematic review
Authors: Rahul Ramakrishnan, Kevin Kuang, Vijay Rajput, Mark Benson, Sachin Mohan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsEsophageal varices (EVs) result from portal hypertension in decompensated liver disease secondary to liver cirrhosis. Diagnosis and grading is done using EGD and mucosal examination. However, interobserver differences may occur when analyzing EGD results. Recent application of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms yielded mixed results for varices detection and bleeding risk estimation. The capabilities of AI in the detection and grading of EVs need to be evaluated.MethodsA systematic review was conducted with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. PubMed, EMBASE, and MEDLINE were searched from January 1956 to July 2023. Data were manually identified and extracted by reviewers, assessing outcomes of AI tools used, EV detection accuracies, and bleeding risk prediction accuracies. Average accuracies were derived from result sections or manual calculations.ResultsSixteen studies with 26,170 patients and 19 AI systems and algorithms were included after a review of 1670 studies. The most common AI systems were artificial neural network and random forest. The categorical boosting machine learning (ML) algorithm was most accurate for prediction of bleeding (100%), whereas the radiomic model ML tool was the least accurate for EV detection (49%). Overall, AI had an average EV detection accuracy of 78.67% and variceal bleed accuracy of 83.2%.ConclusionsAI could provide an accessible interface for EV prediction and estimation of bleeding risk. Limitations include the dependence on a single dataset for efficacy, assessment with specific AI tools, and potential overinterpretation of broad geographic variances. Newer algorithms should have larger datasets with reproducible validity to strengthen the predictive value in clinical practice.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a field dedicated to the development and programming of machines to achieve functions that traditionally demanded human intelligence and decision-making.1 The role of AI in medicine emerged in the 1950s as a tool for medical discovery, diagnosis, rehabilitation, clinical procedures, and preventative medicine.2 As time progressed, the capacity of AI to process medical information and data has rapidly improved, solidifying its pivotal role in health care. This evolution has led to the growing application of AI to improve patient care.3 In the field of gastroenterology and hepatology, the integration of AI has especially progressed the detection and analysis of various medical conditions.

The first mention of AI in gastroenterology can be traced back to the mid-2000s with the development of computer-aided detection systems designed to accurately identify polyps in patient populations.4 Since then, AI has undergone significant evolution, impacting the analysis, diagnosis, and treatment of various conditions including upper GI tract malignant and premalignant lesions, inflammatory and nonmalignant lesions, GI bleeding, and hepatic and pancreatobiliary pathologies.5 Studies conducted from 2010 onward have evaluated the role of AI in diagnostic prediction for colonic polyps.6,7 These investigations highlight the accuracy of AI in identifying polyps, reducing progression risk for cancer, and predicting patient prognosis after AI-assisted diagnosis. In the context of GI bleeding arising from peptic ulcers, research has explored the ability of AI to predict recurrent ulcer bleeding at different time intervals.8 These studies used retrospective analyses considering diverse patient diagnostic criteria such as age and hemoglobin levels. Hepatic pathologies, including cirrhosis, have become a focal point in AI for predicting mortality rates and treatment responses.5 Nonetheless, it is crucial to note that in the analysis of generative pretrained transformer (ie, GPT) models such as ChatGPT, researchers emphasize the technology’s role in identifying research questions and interpreting datasets.9

AI advancements demonstrated significant progress in the realm of esophageal conditions. By 2019, AI algorithms, particularly convolutional neural networks, highlighted not only the capability to detect esophageal cancers like squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, but also achieved 98% accuracy in characterizing both superficial and advanced cancers.10 Building on this, a convolutional neural network in 2022 identified dysplastic lesions crucial for diagnosing early neoplasia in Barrett’s esophagus. This algorithm outperformed multiple endoscopists, revealing an accuracy that reached up to 97%.11

AI can also play a crucial role in addressing esophageal varices (EVs). EVs are abnormal dilated veins typically because of portal hypertension in patients experiencing decompensated liver disease secondary to cirrhosis.12 Mortality is 20% for those with ruptured EVs, with 50% of EV patients experiencing bleeding.13 The diagnosis and grading of EVs is currently through EGD and examination of esophageal mucosa. Although EGDs are considered the criterion standard for EV detection, their repetitive use in high-risk patients poses disadvantages of increased bleeding risk because of trauma related to procedure, cost-ineffectiveness, and associated sedation risks.14 Interobserver variability may also occur when analyzing EGD results, and several AI algorithms have been evaluated with mixed results for real-time and retrospective detection of varices and estimation of bleeding risk.

Therefore, here our objective was to conduct a systematic review to assess the ability of AI to detect EVs and evaluate bleeding risk. Given the increasing prevalence and significance of AI not only in gastroenterology and hepatology but also in the broader field of medicine, we believe that conducting a comprehensive review is highly topical and clinically relevant.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.15 A protocol was not registered for this systematic review.

Information sources, search strategy, and eligibility criteria

The systematic review used PubMed, EMBASE, and MEDLINE databases from January 1956 to July 2023. The database search was defined for English-language studies. The key words used in the search were ((artificial intelligence) OR (machine learning)) AND ((esophageal bleed) OR (esophageal varices) OR (esophagus) OR (oesophagus)). Additionally, the reviewers thoroughly reviewed reference lists of identified articles for additional studies.

Inclusion criteria consisted of case series, previous systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and other English-language peer-reviewed studies relevant to the research question regarding AI and EVs. Exclusion criteria included studies not published in English, narrative reviews, studies that were not relevant to the research question, short communications such as letters to the editors, high risk of bias studies, and gray literature such as government documents and newsletters.

Data collection and data items

Data were collected from the included studies using an extraction sheet, Microsoft Word, and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, Wash, USA). Information was gathered on authors, affiliations, limitations, study methodology, patient populations, and conclusions. The outcome defined was the calculated accuracy of AI tools in detecting EVs.

Risk of bias assessment

The authors independently used the ROBINS-I tool (Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions) to assess risk of bias.16 High risk of bias studies were excluded. Results

Study selection and study characteristics

A search of the 3 databases found 1670 studies (PubMed, 472; EMBASE, 998; MEDLINE OVID, 200). After removing 398 duplicates, the remaining 1272 articles were screened. After screening, 1233 studies were excluded. The remaining 39 studies were assessed for eligibility, and 23 full-text articles were excluded because of a lack of applicability to the research question. There were 23 articles that our team initially assumed would meet the inclusion criteria but were ultimately excluded. For example, Pallio et al33 briefly discussed how AI can enhance the accuracy of EGDs in detecting and predicting EVs. However, the research was a review on various diagnostic and management techniques of EVs, so the authors never performed AI studies on actual patient datasets. Thus, this study was excluded.

Detecting EVs

Six studies discussed the ability of AI to detect EVs. Wang et al29 developed an AI system called ENDOANGEL-GEV and found the system surpassed endoscopists in diagnosing and grading EVs. The research team determined that this AI system can have a diagnostic accuracy of around 90%, diminish healthcare costs, and improve patient health outcomes. Similar to Wang et al’s study, Chen et al22 used an artificial neural network (ANN) named ENDOANGEL to diagnose EVs and found that ENDOANGEL had a 97% EV detection accuracy when compared with the 92% detection accuracy of endoscopists. The researchers studied a patient population that consisted of 3021 patients with varices and 3168 patients without varices to obtain 14,718 images from both groups. The study concluded that ANN integration into clinical practice would assist gastroenterologists in detecting and evaluating risk factors of bleeding.

Dong et al23 analyzed a cohort of 238 patients in the United States using the random forest algorithm and concluded that machine learning (ML) algorithms can indirectly detect EVs through the development of an intermediary score such as EVendo. This study is unique because although the other studies directly used ML algorithms to detect and predict EVs through medical imaging, this study outlined how ML algorithms can develop a score (EVendo) that can assist physicians in detecting EVs. In the conclusion, the authors emphasized that the use of ML will allow certain patients to avoid unnecessary and additional follow-up EGDs.

Huang et al25 developed an ML model called ML EGD to detect high-risk EVs. To create this model, researchers added various variables (age, sex, prothrombin time, Child-Pugh class, etc) from 2794 patients into the light gradient-boosting machine algorithm. The ML EGD was effective in detecting high-risk EVs in patients with compensated cirrhosis. This study explained that the light gradient-boosting machine algorithm seemed to have a high accuracy and quicker training time when compared with other ML algorithms such as extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and gradient-boosted decision trees. Yan et al31 created an ML-based radiomic model (RM) to identify high bleeding risk EVs in 796 Chinese patients with cirrhosis. Although the study demonstrated that RM could be used to detect high-risk EVs, the accuracy was low at 49%. The RM tool was used with a smaller sample size and therefore needs further validation by larger and multicenter studies. Abd Elsalam et al18 used 2 AI algorithms to develop a tool called boosted-naive Bayes tree and studied 5013 hepatitis C patients with portal hypertension in Egypt. The researchers discovered that the boosted-naive Bayes tree tool demonstrated a 79% accuracy in predicting EVs. This indicates the potential for the tool to reduce the need for repeated endoscopy screening in patients who are not predicted by the algorithm to have EVs. Further validation is required to confirm its clinical applicability.

Four studies18,22,23,25 were ultimately included in the statistical analyses because of appropriate outcomes that enabled extraction of data from the studies. The pooled sensitivity on detection of EVs was 96.87% (95% confidence interval [CI], 96.33-97.42), specificity was 94.24% (95% CI, 93.74-94.73), and the area under the curve was .863 (95% CI, .856-871). Predicting EV bleeding

Ten studies reported the role of AI in predicting EV bleeding. Abd El-Salam et al17 used 6 ML algorithms to predict EVs in 4962 chronic hepatitis C patients (2218 patients had EVs). The Bayesian network algorithm was the most optimal tool based on its performance and 68.9% accuracy out of the 6 algorithms. The ML techniques as a whole could be safe and cost-effective for GI screening. Wang et al30 outlined how ML can use endoscopic images and patient data to successfully predict 12-month EV bleeding when compared with current clinical indices that assess cirrhosis such as the Child-Pugh score and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score. The stacking model, an AI algorithm where multiple ML tools are combined, had the highest accuracy of 93.2% when analyzing 341 Chinese patients.

Agarwal et al19 used the XGBoost ML algorithm to study 828 patients with EVs to predict their risk of future bleeds. This research found that ML algorithms improved a clinician’s ability to predict future EV bleeds in patients with compensated advanced chronic liver disease with an accuracy of 98.7%. Bayani et al20 evaluated 4 different ML algorithms on 490 Iranian patients with cirrhosis to predict EVs and found that the random forest algorithm had the highest accuracy average of 99%. In a separate study, Bayani et al21 used 2 ML algorithms, categorical boosting (CatBoost) and XGBoost classifier, and an ML technique, called ensemble learning, to predict EV risk in 490 Iranian patients and found that the CatBoost model had a 100% accuracy in predicting EVs and their grading. The authors concluded that this ML model could not only predict EV but also reduce mortality, follow-up, financial costs, and morbidity when compared with unneeded EGDs. Liu et al26 evaluated the performance and accuracy of 3 predictive models: radionomics signature, nomogram, and a combined CT and clinical model. The study revealed that although the 2 models incorporating ML algorithms (radionomics and nomogram) demonstrated high overall performance, their accuracy rates were 61% and 68%, respectively. In contrast, the combined CT and clinical model, which did not use ML, achieved a higher accuracy of 82%.

Hou et al24 retrospectively investigated 1100 Chinese patients with liver cirrhosis and discovered that the ANN model has a high prediction accuracy for EV bleeding. The ANN model was developed using 12 different risk factors for varices that included hematocrit, red blood cell count, ascites, drinking history, and smoking history. Similar to Hou et al, Zhong et al32 used an ANN model in conjunction with established grading systems (MELD score, albumin-bilirubin grade, platelet-albumin-bilirubin grade, and Child-Pugh score) to attempt to enhance the prediction of EV bleeding in patients who were receiving a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt procedure. However, the research study showed that the integration of ANN with these grading systems and other factors was not effective in predicting EV bleeding. It should be noted that this conclusion explained that the 4 grading systems (MELD score, albumin-bilirubin grade, platelet-albumin-bilirubin grade, and Child-Pugh score) were not effective in predicting EV bleeding, but it was never explicitly mentioned whether AI technology was effective or not. However, because AI technology was still used in the methodology to attempt to determine EV bleeding, the study was included in this review.

Jin et al27 studied 7 Chinese patients by using AI to develop a noninvasive measurement model called Virtual Ruler. This Virtual Ruler allowed researchers to measure the EV diameter that in turn enabled the prediction of EV bleeding in at-risk patients. The study explained that a larger EV diameter is correlated with an increased risk of rupture because of high pressure. Thus, this AI-based model provided an affordable, safe, and easy to operate method for physicians. Simsek et al28 evaluated 201 Turkish patients and found that the light gradient-boosting machine ML algorithm had a 63% accuracy in predicting EV in cirrhotic patients. The authors concluded that although there were the limitations of retrospective study design and smaller patient population, the ML algorithm was not inferior to other existing tests such as the platelet count-to-spleen diameter, aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet index (APRI), aspartate aminotransferase-to-alanine ratio, and Fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) scores.

Five studies17,21,24,26,28 were ultimately included in the statistical analyses because of appropriate outcomes that enabled extraction of data from the studies. The pooled sensitivity on the prediction of EV bleeding was 83.55% (95% CI, 81.36-85.74), specificity was 89.91% (95% CI, 88.70-91.12), and the area under the curve was .894 (95% CI, .883-.904). Discussion

Main findings

This is the first systematic review focused on the role of AI in detecting EVs and predicting bleeding risk in patients with portal hypertension and liver cirrhosis. Most studies included in this review reached a consensus that ML algorithms and AI models have the potential to spare patients from unnecessary EGDs and its associated risks of bleeding, sedation adverse effects, and financial burdens. The CatBoost ML algorithm was the most accurate for prediction at 100%, whereas the RM ML tool was the least accurate for detection at 49%. On average, AI achieved a detection accuracy of 78.67% for EVs and 83.2% accuracy in predicting bleeding. Numerous studies emphasized the suitability of AI for long-term integration into medical practices. The capacity to adapt and refine its performance with each additional dataset holds the promise of enhancing the precision and accuracy of this technology, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and decreased healthcare costs. Furthermore, many authors suggested that AI is poised to enhance healthcare professionals’ prognostication and decision-making in clinical settings. When comparing older AI models with newer AI models in regard to performance for the detection and prediction of EVs and bleeding, our review did not identify any significant differences in performance.

Most studies included in the review were conducted in China with a publication ratio of 56.25%, whereas countries such as India, Egypt, and Iran had publication ratios of 12.5%. The significant interest from researchers in China and India, regarding how AI can detect EVs, correlates with epidemiologic data in that both India and China are the most affected countries for liver cirrhosis deaths and incident cases, respectively, related to liver cirrhosis.34 However, this geographic finding presents a potential issue of selection bias that can limit the generalizability of the findings. The performance of AI can differ based on various factors such as healthcare system, patient demographics, genetics, and clinical guidelines. Because of these factors, multiple studies from geographic regions such as China may not show the true differences in the performance of AI across clinical settings, causing a potential issue with this systematic review’s conclusions. This concern affirms the need for more studies from a broad range of geographic areas to confirm the reliability of AI tools in various settings.

One study in our systematic review compared noninvasive tests such as the APRI, aspartate aminotransferase-to-alanine ratio, and FIB-4 with an AI model.28 The result of this study showed that the AI model received a higher performance score for predicting EVs compared with these noninvasive methods. The APRI, FIB-4, Forns index, and transient elastography are noninvasive tests that can assist medical professionals in their assessment of liver fibrosis in various medical conditions. Previous studies have compared the performance of AI with these pre-existing noninvasive tests in the detection of liver fibrosis. For example, researchers in a prospective cohort study of 3460 individuals in Denmark from 2013 to 2020 discovered that the diagnostic performance of various AI ensemble learning models for detecting liver stiffness was higher than any models using conventional tests such as the APRI, FIB-4, and Forns index.35 The authors concluded that AI-based models are both an accurate and cost-effective method for predicting significant liver fibrosis and a useful tool for identifying patients with asymptomatic chronic liver disease. A separate systematic review and meta-analysis discovered that although the conventional tests of the APRI and FIB-4 had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 60% and 77%, respectively, for confirming significant fibrosis, the integration of AI tools into the datasets increased the pooled sensitivity and specificity to .99 to 1.00 and .76 to 1.00, respectively.36

Strength and limitations

Our review underscores the importance of further research into the role of AI, not only in the context of EVs but also among other gastroenterologic and hepatology conditions. One included study was able to explain how AI models can be necessary even when criteria, such as the Baveno VI criteria, already have a high sensitivity for ruling out clinical significant portal hypertension.19 ML models can appropriately capture patient risk profiles and allow for detailed stratification, particularly in the identification of higher-risk patients with either viral or nonviral etiologies. Improvement in stratification and AI-based tools could serve to complement the Baveno VII criteria and provide more personalized management strategies. This is particularly important in clinical settings where endoscopies are restricted based on noninvasive screening criteria.

Although our study strictly complied with PRISMA guidelines and included studies with diverse AI algorithms, it is crucial to acknowledge certain limitations. First, the efficacy of certain AI systems was exclusively studied and documented by 1 paper or dataset, raising concerns regarding the generalizability of the evidence. Larger datasets with the same algorithms would assist in strengthening the predictive value in a clinical practice. Second, the broad geographic variances observed in the studies could be prone to overinterpretation, because these differences may arise from variations in the classification system used for estimation and the subjective interpretation of EVs in terms of size and location. A third limitation is that the exclusion of non–English language studies introduces the possibility of missing pertinent data. We attempted to address these limitations through a methodological approach to key word selection and a comprehensive search strategy and selection process aligned with the PRISMA guidelines. Fourth, none of the studies evaluated patients who were using standard medical therapy such as nonselective beta-blockers or endoscopic variceal ligation. Access to this information in the cited articles would not only assist in the prediction of EV bleeding but would have also provided appropriate assessment of which patients are most likely to be unsuccessful on therapy. Despite these limitations, our novel systematic review effectively consolidated and summarized the existing research on the role of AI in the detection and prediction of EVs.

The high accuracy rates of certain AI tools, such as the CatBoost algorithm in the prediction of bleeding, should be examined carefully to avoid any overinterpretation. Although the CatBoost algorithm did show a 100% accuracy for prediction, the study was completed with a small sample size and limited external validation. These 2 aspects reduce the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, particular patient populations and AI models in single-center studies could overestimate the accuracy rates. For tools such as CatBoost, further validation with more research studies is needed across more diverse populations and multicenter locations. These changes could confirm the robustness and true accuracy rates of specific AI tools.

One study in the systematic review discussed cirrhosis patients with various etiologies such as viral hepatitis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, or alcohol use.23 Although the authors mentioned that the breakdown of patients in their study was representative of cirrhosis patients in various clinical practices, the applicability of AI models could vary depending on the underlying etiology of cirrhosis. Cirrhosis secondary to viral hepatitis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, or autoimmune conditions may present with varying clinical profiles and adverse events that could impact the applicability of AI models. Traditional noninvasive tests might underestimate the severity of fibrosis or portal hypertension in patients with presinusoidal portal hypertension (primary sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis). In contrast, AI models could potentially offer more evaluation by incorporating a broader range of clinical and imaging data. Further studies are needed to specifically evaluate the performance of AI models across different patient subgroups to determine whether the model’s applicability differs significantly.

Implications for practice, policy, and future research

With a surge in AI research and funding in recent years, there has been an increase in our understanding of AI and its integration across various medical specialties. Future investigations into the ability of AI to determine effective treatment regimens for patients with cirrhosis and EVs may drastically reshape clinician practices and enhance patient outcomes. We envision an increase of AI studies focused on EV detection, prediction, prognosis, and treatment. Because most existing datasets for AI algorithms originate from higher-population countries and thus contain over-represented patient populations, datasets and studies with increased diversity and inclusivity are needed. Future research can aim to develop AI models using multicenter datasets that encompass a wide range of healthcare settings, socioeconomic backgrounds, and ethnicities. This diversity is critical to ensure that AI is more applicable across various clinical settings.

Because AI systems are generally trained on standardized datasets from academic centers or higher-population geographic areas, an issue arises with data variability. These AI models may struggle to perform accurately in community settings with diverse patient demographics and clinical protocols. Future research could address these gaps by incorporating diverse data sources and creating flexible AI systems that can learn and train themselves from various clinical settings. The development of prospective studies to evaluate AI systems in different clinical settings can provide strong evidence regarding their applicability in decision-making. Research could also attempt to integrate AI with existing scoring systems such as MELD or Child-Pugh scores to offer better severity assessments and management planning.

Algorithmic transparency and interpretability are other barriers to the adoption of AI tools in clinical practices. Many AI tools may not only be difficult to explain but also to understand. This aspect can directly hinder clinician trust and acceptance. Future research can look into the development of interpretable models that can provide clear insights into how different clinical predictions are made. This would not only allow more clinician trust but also enable a seamless integration in existing clinical workflows. Building off this idea, the development of AI tools that can interact effectively with electronic health records and clinical support systems is another area for research exploration. This integration would require addressing challenges such as technical development, data standardization, and need for user-friendly systems. Additionally, ethical, policy-related, and regulatory challenges are associated with the implementation of AI in healthcare settings. Data privacy, patient consent, and algorithmic biases need to be thoroughly addressed. Any further research should encourage creating standardized guidelines to ensure the safe and equitable use of AI technologies in medical practices.

Conclusions

AI tools offer a cost-effective, accurate, and user-friendly interface for EV prediction and the bleeding risk estimation. As technology and algorithms continue to undergo refinement, further research is essential to ensure comprehensive efficacy and usability. The potential of AI in the fields of gastroenterology and hepatology is promising, encouraging clinicians to remain open-minded about its integration in clinical practice. These tools support early intervention and may reduce mortality and morbidity associated with variceal bleeding.
