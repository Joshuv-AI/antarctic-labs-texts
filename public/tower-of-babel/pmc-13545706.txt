Stakeholders' perspectives on an autonomous brain MRI: An interview study
Authors: Christien N Mensinga, Leonie NC Visser, Jonathan Ebbers, Sjenny ACM Winters, Dennis WJ Klomp, Marielle H Emmelot-Vonk, Huiberdina L Koek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study explored how patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals perceive an autonomous Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scanning system in which individuals perform their own brain scans and images are interpreted using Artificial Intelligence (AI) for early detection and monitoring of brain-related conditions.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with patients (n = 11), care partners (n = 5), and healthcare professionals (n = 18). Data were coded using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF).ResultsTwelve of fourteen TDF domains were found relevant. Knowledge and communication were key facilitators for confidence and trust. Participants emphasised the need for clear, step-by-step information before and during the scan. Concerns focused on patients' ability to manage scanning independently, the absence of on-site staff in emergencies, and the transparency and accountability of AI. Patients valued convenience, while professionals highlighted early detection and efficiency as primary benefits.ConclusionTrust in autonomous MRI depends on clear communication and education, and clarity about human oversight and responsibility.InnovationOur findings show the relevance of combining technological innovation with human-centred communication. Integrating effective communication strategies and educational materials, such as visual and interactive instructions, can empower patients and professionals and support the adoption of autonomous MRI in healthcare practice, making neuroimaging more accessible while maintaining safety and quality.Keywords: Autonomous MRI, Artificial intelligence, Communication, Patient education, Trust, Implementation, Healthcare innovation

Introduction

Life expectancy worldwide is increasing, with one in six people expected to be 60 or older by 2030, and the population aged 80 or older predicted to triple by 2050 [1]. Longer lifespans increase the risk of age-related diseases, particularly brain-related disorders such as dementia, a syndrome that affects cognitive and daily functioning [1]. Globally, over 55 million people live with dementia, and nearly 10 million new cases occur yearly. Dementia is the seventh leading cause of mortality and imposes a substantial burden on patients and their loved ones [2], making it a major global health challenge [1]. Reflecting this global trend, the number of people living with dementia in the Netherlands is projected to rise from about 320,000 in 2026 to more than 600,000 by 2050, placing increasing pressure on healthcare services and diagnostic pathways [3].

Early detection and recognition of dementia, as well as its underlying cause(s), are essential for several reasons. Timely and adequate coping with the disease can benefit the individual's physical and psycho-social health [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. These benefits include improved symptomatic disease management and an earlier start to formal and informal care that aligns with patient needs. By facilitating proactive planning, early diagnosis helps reduce hospital admissions and institutionalisation [4], [8], [9]. Beyond health benefits, early diagnosis may significantly reduce the costs of dementia care. Delaying institutionalisation, even slightly, yields significant savings when timely alternatives, such as home care or a dementia case manager, are arranged [2], [4], [7], [8], [9]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that multi-model prevention interventions and disease-modifying therapy could slow down cognitive decline for individuals with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) or early-stage dementia, highlighting the early disease stages as a window of opportunity [10], [11], [12]. Thus, an early dementia or MCI diagnosis has potential benefits on various levels. In addition, the absence of signs of neurodegenerative disease could also be reassuring and inform life decisions and care planning, especially in the case of people of older age or with co-morbidities.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) enables early detection of brain pathology associated with dementia [7]. MRI is also a powerful tool for prognosticating dementia and assessing or monitoring the effects of interventions [7], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. However, MRI technology has limitations. It is relatively expensive, as experts must operate the scanner, analyse the images, and generate reports. Moreover, there is a shortage of these experts on the labour market. Furthermore, from a patient's perspective, the traditional MRI scanning process can be unpleasant due to the loud noise and long duration, which ranges from 15 to 90 min, depending on the scanned area and the number of images [21], [22], [23]. To optimally implement MRI for the early detection and monitoring of dementia, we therefore need to develop appropriate solutions.

To overcome these problems, researchers have been and continue to work towards a more autonomous, silent, and fast MRI system. For example, new technologies can simplify operations and minimise discomfort and anxiety [24], [25], [26]. Additionally, due to advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI), image interpretation and reporting can be automated [27], [28]. In the envisioned MRI system, individuals (possibly supported by a relative) will be adequately comforted, instructed, and empowered to self-scan. Afterwards, an AI-based report is sent to their referring healthcare professional without requiring a radiologist's input. Such an autonomous MRI scanner could be used for various purposes, including but not limited to detecting or excluding neurodegeneration and cerebrovascular injury consistent with dementia. Currently, an autonomous MRI is being developed in the Netherlands for use in primary care within the Scan2Go project, focusing on the detection of neurodegeneration and cerebrovascular injury consistent with MCI and dementia.

Although the technical advancements made in Scan2Go are promising, implementing an autonomous MRI process in practice will likely face barriers. For example, related to user skills, attitudes, knowledge, and external factors such as reimbursement or the cultural context. Understanding these barriers and facilitators from the perspectives of patients and their relatives, as well as referring healthcare professionals, is crucial [29], [30], [31], [32]. We need more insight into the cognitive, social, and environmental factors that influence adoption [32] to develop successful implementation strategies, such as patient education materials. Therefore, this interview study aimed to explore stakeholders' perspectives on the use of autonomous MRI to assess brain integrity.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative interview study was conducted within the context of Scan2Go, a project of a Dutch public-private consortium funded by Health Holland [33]. We aimed to collect the perspectives of patients, their care partners (i.e., relatives accompanying patients to healthcare appointments and providing support and informal care if needed), and healthcare professionals regarding autonomous brain MRI and identify barriers and facilitators to its implementation using the refined Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) described by Cane et al. [32], [34]. This qualitative study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure transparency and completeness in the reporting of methods and findings [35]. See Supplemental file A.

Participants and procedures

Patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals were primarily recruited from an academic hospital and an elderly care and rehabilitation facility. We set the minimum number of participants to 10 patients and care partners (combined), and 10 healthcare professionals. After that number of interviews, data saturation was used as a stopping criterion, i.e., data collection ceased when two consecutive interviews yielded no new insights beyond the previously gathered data. This approach aligns with previous qualitative studies that recommended a minimum of 10 interviews while allowing flexibility to achieve and confirm data saturation, as excessive interviews may raise ethical concerns [32], [36], [37]. Due to the considerable variation in data within both groups of participants, we determined that data saturation was achieved after 16 interviews with patients and care partners (11 patients and 5 care partners) and 18 interviews with healthcare professionals.

Eligible patients and care partners were selected by their treating healthcare professionals at various clinics, including memory clinics, geriatric outpatient clinics, and inpatient wards. Patients and care partners were eligible if they were at least 18 years old and fluent in Dutch. Patients with severe cognitive impairment, as assessed by the treating healthcare professional, were excluded. Healthcare professionals provided verbal information about the study and asked whether they wished to participate. Upon agreement, a researcher offered additional verbal and written details and answered questions. Interviews took place privately and included the patient, the care partner, or both, at the patient's preference. Interviews ranged from 11 min 04 s to 32 min 12 s, with a mean of 15 min 57 s. Informed consent was obtained in writing before the interview and, for patients, was also documented in their electronic patient file.

A diverse group of healthcare professionals was selected based on their work experience and expertise. All had experience referring patients for MRI or supporting them in the MRI process. This included, among others, neurologists, radiologists, geriatricians, general practitioners, and radiology technicians. Eligible healthcare professionals were contacted either in person or by email and provided with study information. Interviews with professionals ranged from 10 min 30 s to 26 min 26 s, with a mean of 17 min 53 s. Written informed consent was obtained before the interview.

The interviews and collecting background data

Participants completed brief questionnaires reporting age and gender. Patients and care partners also completed the functional health literacy subscale one of the Dutch translations of the Functional, Communicative, and Critical Health Literacy Scales and the Dutch Verhage scale [38], [39], [40]. The International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) was used for international comparison [40], [41]. Healthcare professionals reported their profession and the years they had been in this position.

An interview guide was developed based on the refined 14-domain TDF described by Cane et al., with input from the research team (CM, DK, JE, LV, ME). The TDF was used to ensure comprehensive exploration of potential barriers and facilitators related to the implementation and adoption of autonomous MRI. Interview questions were mapped to relevant TDF domains. Rather than including one question for each domain, open-ended questions and follow-up prompts were used to explore multiple domains. Interview questions were designed to explore one or more TDF domains. During analysis, participant responses could be coded to different domains depending on their content. For example, the domain Emotion was explored through questions such as “What is your initial reaction to this?” and “What concerns would you have?” The domain Skills was explored by asking, “What would you need to be able to do this?”

Separate interview guides were developed for patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals, with questions adapted to each stakeholder group's perspective while maintaining alignment with the TDF domains.

The questionnaire and interview guide were pilot-tested during initial interviews; no changes were made. Additionally, a process description (introduction and explanation of the Scan2Go concept) with images of the envisioned autonomous MRI process was created and tested for clarity with 5 patients at a rehabilitation and care facility for older adults. The description was read aloud during each interview to ensure understanding. Interview duration was based on the recorded question-and-answer portion of the interview and therefore excluded the standardised interview introduction and explanation of the Scan2Go concept.

Two researchers, CM and JE, conducted interviews between April and July 2024. The first eight interviews, involving patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals, were conducted jointly to ensure alignment and consistency. Interviews with patients and care partners were conducted face-to-face, except for one care partner interview conducted via Microsoft Teams. Interviews with healthcare professionals were primarily conducted face-to-face, with a few conducted via Microsoft Teams. Interviews were recorded using Microsoft Teams, with a Philips device as a backup. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim using Microsoft Teams' automatic transcription function, then reviewed and corrected by the interviewer. After verification, audio files were deleted.

Data analysis

CM and JE coded all transcripts. Data were analysed using a deductive framework guided by the refined Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). They first read all transcripts and identified segments related to barriers and facilitators, then deductively categorised them into relevant TDF domains [32]. Interview questions were designed to explore one or more TDF domains. During analysis, participant responses could be coded to different domains depending on their content.

The first two interviews were jointly coded to align strategy; subsequent transcripts were coded independently, with regular meetings to discuss and resolve discrepancies. Coding updates were applied retrospectively as the coding tree evolved. NVivo 20 and NVivo 15 supported the coding process [32], [42], [43].

Although coding was primarily deductive, the researchers remained open to themes outside the TDF. Inductive coding was therefore applied to identify potential new domains or subdomains. While no additional domains outside the TDF were identified, inductively derived subthemes emerged within the existing TDF domains. During analysis, the researchers actively considered all 14 TDF domains. Some participant statements were initially explored in relation to the domains of Social Influences and Memory, Attention and Decision Processes. However, these statements ultimately aligned more closely with other TDF domains. Consequently, no distinct evidence was identified for these two domains, and they were therefore not represented in the final analysis.

Following coding, content within each domain was analysed to identify recurring themes, barriers, and facilitators. For each domain and subdomain, summaries were written and illustrated with representative quotes. Differences among patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals were explored. The results were discussed and refined with the research team. Potential codes for two TDF domains were considered during analysis, but ultimately aligned more closely with other TDF domains and were therefore not retained as separate domains.

The interviewers were involved in the Scan2Go project and may therefore have held assumptions regarding autonomous MRI. To enhance reflexivity, transparency, and credibility, a TDF-based interview guide, joint interviewing and coding sessions, and regular discussions within the whole research team were applied.

Facilitators and barriers mapped onto the Theoretical Domains Framework

From the interviews with three stakeholder groups, we identified various barriers and facilitators for using and implementing autonomous brain MRI. The responses were mapped onto 12 of the 14 TDF domains: Knowledge, Skills, Professional/Social Role and Identity, Beliefs about Capabilities, Optimism, Beliefs about Consequences, Reinforcement, Intentions, Goals, Environmental Context and Resources, Emotion, and Behavioural Regulation. Knowledge

Patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals identified knowledge as a key facilitator in building confidence and trust in the autonomous MRI scanning process. Patients and care partners emphasised the need for clear information before arriving at the scanning location and straightforward instructions during the scan. Understanding what to expect and what is expected of them would help patients feel more confident about undergoing the procedure independently. Healthcare professionals suggested developing instructional videos and creating user-friendly materials to make the process more accessible. Some participants suggested that instructions could be translated into different languages.

Knowledge was also mentioned as crucial for referring physicians. Healthcare professionals noted that referrers' lack of understanding of the entire process is a barrier, as it can undermine their confidence in recommending patients for autonomous MRI scanning. Some indicated that written materials may not be sufficient to address this barrier. Instead, trust can be more effectively fostered through practical experiences, such as observing the autonomous MRI in action.

Skills

Care partners and healthcare professionals, in particular, highlighted challenges related to patients' impairments in specific skills, such as physical skills or cognitive abilities. Patients did not mention this barrier and reported they could navigate the MRI process independently. Care partners and healthcare professionals expressed concern about patients with cognitive deficits' lack of self-awareness.

Professional/Social Role and Identity

The role of AI in relation to their own role was another barrier mentioned among healthcare professionals, particularly regarding who should be consulted and held accountable for potential errors. Although participants generally agreed that AI has the potential to outperform humans in interpreting medical imaging, whether AI can do this autonomously (for all patients) is not just a question of capabilities. Participants had concerns about responsibility and liability. Unlike humans, who can be held accountable for their errors, it is unclear who is responsible for AI errors. AI operates solely within the parameters of its training, which complicates trust in its independent use without human oversight. Some healthcare professionals expressed concerns about relying entirely on AI for these reasons.

Beliefs about Capabilities MRI scanner, and Environmental Context and Resources

Most participants commented on Beliefs about Capabilities (of the MRI), and Environmental Context and Resources. The identified barriers and facilitators in these two domains were often interconnected. For example, many participants highlighted the need for signalling and immediate action in emergencies (as an essential capability of the autonomous MRI scanner). To respond effectively in such cases, personnel should be available to act as a safety net, ready to address urgent situations, for instance, contacting a neurosurgeon in the event of a haemorrhage or comforting patients during panic attacks (Environmental Context and Resources). The autonomous MRI process might lack the capability to signal and respond, unlike on-site personnel, who can respond quickly, which healthcare professionals mentioned as a significant barrier. Additionally, concerns were raised about the reliability of checks for contraindications (falling within the Capabilities domain of the autonomous MRI scanner), which are currently checked by on-site personnel (Environmental Context and Resources). Without these checks, safety could be compromised.

Other identified barriers relating to scanner capabilities included whether the metal detector's accuracy is sufficient, whether the consequences of pressing the emergency button are secured, and whether the ID check is foolproof so that people cannot pretend to be someone else. Another barrier in the Environmental Context and Resources domain was the absence of another person during the scanning process. While most patients did not view this as an issue, care partners and healthcare professionals emphasised the importance of accompaniment, particularly for older patients.

Beliefs about Capabilities AI

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has two components: its role, elaborated under Professional Role and Identity, and its capabilities. Regarding the latter, most patients expressed positive views when asked about AI-generated reports. Patients depend on experts as facilitators to create trustworthy and effective AI systems.

Healthcare professionals generally had a positive view of AI's capabilities, but also expressed some barriers. Many noted that, at its current stage, AI is not advanced enough to assess scans autonomously. Additionally, some raised concerns about AI's capability to report incidental findings if its model is trained for a specific task. Assuming AI can surpass human capabilities in assessing scans when designed well and trained on a sufficiently large dataset for accurate diagnosis, participants looked to the future with optimism about AI's potential.

Optimism and Intentions

Most patients agreed to undergo an autonomous MRI if their physician recommended it (Optimism). This openness was primarily based on a general willingness to “try it out” and “if necessary”, rather than on detailed logical reasoning (Intentions).

Beliefs about Consequences

Medicalisation and overdiagnosis were identified as potential unintended barriers to the implementation of an autonomous MRI system. While the technology offers greater accessibility, it may also increase healthcare demand/access, resulting in more incidental findings and higher costs. In contrast, other healthcare professionals mentioned concerns about missing incidental findings.

Reinforcement and Intentions

Patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals acknowledged several facilitators of an autonomous MRI system, including its convenience and patient-friendly features, such as reduced noise levels, shorter scan times, and decreased waiting times. Healthcare professionals cited the shortened waiting times in their outpatient clinics, due to shorter waiting times for MRI scans, as the main reason for using an autonomous MRI. The preferred location for an autonomous MRI system varied. While some healthcare professionals favoured nursing homes for their accessibility, others argued that diagnostic centres would be better suited due to the availability of trained personnel.

Goals

The choice of a brain MRI as the initial case for testing autonomous scanning prompted mixed reactions. Healthcare professionals expressed barriers, noting that patients requiring brain scans are often less capable of self-administering the scan.

Moreover, interpreting brain scans is more complex than interpreting scans of the knee or spine. Several healthcare professionals suggested starting with musculoskeletal scans as a more practical alternative. The need for contrast agents in brain MRI's is typically decided shortly beforehand and requires on-site healthcare professionals, further complicating the feasibility of autonomous operation.

Additionally, healthcare professionals identified potential goal-related facilitators for autonomous MRI systems, including their use for screening and monitoring. For example, the technology could facilitate affordable screening for vascular abnormalities in high-risk populations or provide quick follow-ups to detect changes in known conditions. Breast and prostate cancer screening programs were also suggested as promising applications for autonomous MRI.

Emotion

Patients and their care partners expressed discomfort with reading results on a phone or computer. They preferred to hear the results from a doctor, as this provided better explanations, particularly regarding incidental findings.

Among the stakeholder groups, healthcare professionals expressed that they consider an autonomous MRI to be impersonal. They noted that human interaction is essential for many patients, and the absence of this personal touch was viewed as a significant barrier. While detailed instructions and checklists might mitigate some issues, unpredictable anxiety or panic attacks can still occur.

Behavioural Regulation

Healthcare professionals highlighted the need for cultural shifts, fundamental changes in mindset, values, and attitudes. This means moving away from deeply ingrained habits and fostering a more adaptive, open, and forward-thinking perspective to changes in medical imaging. In addition to these cultural shifts, behavioural changes will be essential. For instance, encouraging healthcare professionals to integrate the autonomous MRI into their routines by providing education and insight into its procedures could help embed this new technology into the workflow.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study examined the perspectives of patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals regarding the use of an autonomous MRI scan for diagnosing and monitoring brain-related conditions, such as dementia. Understanding the barriers and facilitators from these stakeholders' perspectives is essential for successfully implementing autonomous scanning in practice [29], [30], [31], [32].

Adequate knowledge was identified as a key facilitator across all stakeholder groups, whereas impaired or limited skills were a significant barrier, especially for patients. This highlights the need for education and clear instructions before and during the on-site process. These should be available in relevant languages and supported by simple illustrations, instructional videos, and clear step-by-step guidance throughout the scanning procedure. Patients reported feeling capable, but their care partners and healthcare professionals reported barriers related to physical skills and cognition. They suggested that support from a care partner could help overcome these barriers and reduce anxiety when needed. Designing the process to require minimal skills and prior knowledge may further facilitate successful implementation. These findings emphasise that implementation strategies should focus not only on providing information but also on ensuring it is accessible and easy to understand. Our findings are in line with a previous TDF-based study suggesting that Knowledge, Skills, and Beliefs about Capabilities are important determinants of healthcare implementation and technology adoption [44].

Care partners and healthcare professionals thus identified several barriers to implementation and expressed more concerns than the interviewed patients. Another barrier mentioned by them was the lack of on-site medical personnel, particularly in emergencies, which was identified as a critical challenge. Most patients did not express these concerns and indicated they would rely on their physician's advice to undergo such a scan if necessary. This aligns with a study of Van Gils et al., which demonstrated that when physicians trust a tool, patients tend to adopt that trust [45]. Therefore, fostering healthcare professionals' confidence in autonomous scanning is crucial for the successful implementation of this technology. One way to achieve this is to allow healthcare professionals to co-create and observe the autonomous scanning process in practice, thereby providing and obtaining valuable insights into its operation and integration with existing workflows. A website or other tool offering accessible, trustworthy information and/or training for referring physicians could also be helpful. Other solutions to overcome this barrier include the availability of a reliable metal detector gate and systematic screening for contraindications by the referring physician.

Patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals also identified factors that could facilitate autonomous MRI scanning. A key factor was patient-friendliness and convenience, which many patients considered essential. Patients valued shorter waiting times, reduced scan durations, lower noise levels, and scanning outside the hospital, consistent with existing literature [21], [22]. Healthcare professionals highlighted early screening and broader population reach as facilitators, noting that monitoring and follow-up of the disease course could help reduce pressure on hospitals. All stakeholder groups thus underscored the potential of autonomous MRI scanning.

Still, participants, particularly healthcare professionals, identified barriers to AI analysing scans and generating reports. Concerns included AI's lack of transparency in diagnostics and decision-making. Some doubted AI's ability to handle exceptions, such as incidental findings, and preferred discussing results with a human expert. However, participants acknowledged AI's potential to surpass human capabilities in the future. Beyond capability, professionals also raised ethical and legal concerns, particularly regarding accountability in case of errors. An editorial on AI in radiology stated that radiologists should remain responsible, and a review indicated that most radiologists support this perspective [46], [47].

Furthermore, our study participants highlighted the importance of transparency and explainability, which aligns with the joint statement from European and North American radiology societies. This statement emphasises the need for clear methods in AI applications [48]. Our findings on accountability, autonomy, and potential benefits are consistent with insights from other studies examining patients', medical students', and radiologists' perspectives on the role and impact of AI in radiology. Across these groups, there was a recognition of the efficiency and improved quality of care that AI can provide. However, concerns were raised about AI operating entirely autonomously, as errors could occur without additional checks. Across studies, participants strongly preferred a collaborative approach in which human experts would verify AI systems' conclusions [47], [49], [50], [51].

These findings not only identify barriers and facilitators to implementation but may also inform the development of theory-based implementation strategies. The identified TDF domains may be linked to intervention functions within the Behaviour Change Wheel framework. For example, barriers related to Knowledge and Skills may be addressed through education and training, whereas concerns related to Emotion and Beliefs about Consequences may be targeted through persuasive communication and reassurance [52].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has provided valuable insights into the facilitators and barriers to implementing an autonomously operated brain MRI, i.e., a self-scan. A predefined theoretical model, the refined Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), ensured that we did not overlook essential themes. The structure of the TDF enabled us to systematically identify barriers and facilitators, while the flexibility of the semi-structured interviews allowed us to explore unexpected, context-specific themes.

Our study also has some limitations. As in most qualitative studies with more than one interviewer, differences in interviewing style and probing may have influenced how questions were asked and responses were explored. To enhance consistency, two members of the research team (CM, JE) developed an interview guide based on the refined TDF and had it reviewed by two senior researchers (DK, LV) of the research team with expertise in qualitative research and geriatric care. Additionally, the two interviewers (CM, JE) jointly conducted the first eight interviews, which included patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals, to align interviewing techniques and ensure a consistent approach throughout the study. Another limitation is the risk of reduced external validity, as the participants may not have been representative of the broader source population, including those with or without cognitive complaints who were referred for an MRI scan. Participants were recruited from two hospitals and an elderly care facility. Only patients who could communicate effectively in Dutch were included, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other cultural and linguistic contexts. Given the high prevalence of dementia in the Netherlands, the findings are particularly relevant to the Dutch healthcare system and its growing demand for accessible diagnostic services. Future studies conducted in other languages and cultural settings may help to confirm and extend the present findings.

Most healthcare professionals were selected from an academic hospital. Healthcare professionals at academic hospitals are often involved in scientific research and may be more receptive to new or experimental diagnostic methods than those in non-academic settings. We did recruit a heterogeneous group of professionals, including both experienced and less experienced healthcare professionals from diverse backgrounds and specialities, as well as those working outside the academic setting.

Innovation

The development of an autonomous MRI system represents a technological and procedural innovation that could fundamentally change diagnostic workflows in neuroimaging. Building on advances in Artificial Intelligence, user-centred design, and automated image acquisition, this system enables individuals, potentially with a care partner's support, to perform their own brain scans. By reducing the need for radiology technicians, autonomous MRI offers a potential solution to workforce shortages, cost inefficiencies, and accessibility barriers that currently limit timely brain imaging for dementia detection and monitoring. This work highlights various barriers and facilitators to implementing the innovative ‘Scan2Go’ system. It therefore informs communication strategies and other implementation efforts required for the successful integration of this innovation into healthcare practice. It also has the potential to make neuroimaging more accessible while maintaining safety and image-quality standards.

Conclusion

Our study identified barriers and facilitators to implementing an autonomous brain MRI as perceived by patients, care partners, and healthcare professionals. The results indicate that these stakeholders see the potential of this innovation. Providing adequate information and education, communicating effectively, and clarifying responsibilities are key facilitators of confidence and trust during implementation. Our findings suggest that healthcare professionals, particularly referring physicians and MRI staff, may play a central role in providing information, addressing concerns, and supporting shared decision-making. Patients frequently reported that a physician's recommendation increased their willingness to undergo an autonomous MRI, underscoring the importance of trusted healthcare professionals in conveying information and fostering acceptance. Information could be delivered through multiple modalities, including verbal explanations during consultations, written materials, instructional videos, and step-by-step guidance before and during the MRI procedure. Such tailored communication may be especially important for older adults and their care partners when introducing novel technologies such as autonomous MRI. Clear communication about healthcare professionals' responsibilities and the role of artificial intelligence in image acquisition and interpretation may further enhance trust and acceptance.

A significant advantage of an autonomous MRI, as identified by professionals, is its potential use for screening and monitoring. This is particularly relevant given recent advancements in multimodal dementia prevention interventions and disease-modifying treatment for brain diseases such as Alzheimer's disease, requiring frequent monitoring [53], [54].

A key barrier mentioned by various stakeholder groups was the risk of detecting clinically irrelevant incidental findings and missing those that could be relevant. Future research should examine the likelihood of these findings and explore the broader role of AI, including the potential risks and ethical dilemmas associated with misinterpretations, incidental findings, and the emotional impact of information from automatic AI reports.

Ethical statement

This research involved minimal risk and did not fall within the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. Therefore, the study did not require formal approval from an accredited Medical Research Ethics Committee in the Netherlands.

An independent quality check was conducted at UMC Utrecht to ensure compliance with legislation and regulations regarding the Informed Consent procedure, data management, privacy, and legal aspects. Therefore, the study was approved by the local Medical Research Ethics Committee of the UMC Utrecht, Vidatum Ethics nummer: 24 U-0181 on 2024-04-02.

All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and their confidentiality was ensured by pseudonymizing all identifying information. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines for qualitative research.
