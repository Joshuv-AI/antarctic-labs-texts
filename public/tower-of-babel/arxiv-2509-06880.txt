The Parameter Report: An Orientation Guide for Data-Driven Parameterization
Christian Komusiewicz, Nils Morawietz, Frank Sommer, Luca Pascal Staus

Abstract
A strength of parameterized algorithmics is that each problem can be parameterized by an essentially inexhaustible set of parameters. Usually, the choice of the considered parameter is informed by the theoretical relations between parameters with the general goal of achieving FPT-algorithms for smaller and smaller parameters. However, the FPT-algorithms for smaller parameters usually have higher running times and it is unclear whether the decrease in the parameter value or the increase in the running time bound dominates in real-world data. This question cannot be answered from purely theoretical considerations and any answer requires knowledge on typical parameter values. To provide a data-driven guideline for parameterized complexity studies of graph problems, we present the first comprehensive comparison of parameter values for a set of benchmark graphs originating from real-world applications. Our study covers degree-related parameters, such as maximum degree or degeneracy, neighborhood-based parameters such as neighborhood diversity and modular-width, modulator-based parameters such as vertex cover number and feedback vertex set number, and the treewidth of the graphs. Our results may help assess the significance of FPT-running time bounds on the solvability of real-world instances. For example, the vertex cover number 𝗏𝖼 \mathsf{vc} of n n -vertex graphs is often only slightly below n / 2 n/2 . Thus, a running time bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 𝗏𝖼 ) \mathcal{O}(2^{\mathsf{vc}}) is only slightly better than a running time bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1.4 n ) \mathcal{O}(1.4^{n}) . In contrast, the treewidth 𝗍𝗐 \mathsf{tw} is almost always below n / 3 n/3 and often close to n / 9 n/9 , making a running time of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 𝗍𝗐 ) \mathcal{O}(2^{\mathsf{tw}}) much more practical on real-world instances. We make our implementation and full experimental data openly available 1 1 1 The source code of all solvers, all graph data and our experimental results are all publicly available at https://www.fmi.uni-jena.de/en/19723/parameter-report . . In particular, this provides the first implementations for several graph parameters such as 4-path vertex cover number and vertex integrity.

Introduction

In the quest to cope with computational intractability, parameterized algorithmics offers an approach for designing exact algorithms with practically useful running time guarantees. The aim is to design FPT-algorithms, that is, algorithms with running time
f(k)\cdot\operatorname{poly}(n)
where
k
is a problem-specific parameter and
n
denotes the overall input size.
Now, if
k
is small on real-world instances and if
f
does not grow too quickly then these algorithms have a practically feasible running time. The classic example for this approach is the NP-hard
Vertex Cover
problem where the input is a graph
G
and a number
k
and the task is to decide whether
G
contains a set of at most
k
vertices that cover all edges.
Vertex Cover
can be easily seen to be solvable in
2^{k}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(n)
time by a simple branching algorithm. After achieving such a first FPT-algorithm for a problem at hand, the daily trade of researchers in parameterized algorithms is now to chip away from the rock of intractability by providing better and better FPT-algorithms
[
15
]
.

There are two pathways to achieve this: The first is to improve FPT-algorithms for the parameter
k
at hand which means first and foremost to improve the superpolynomial running time part
f(k)
. For
Vertex Cover
this pathway has been thoroughly explored, with the current best FPT-algorithm having a running time of
\mathcal{O}(1.26^{k}+n)

[
31
]
. The second pathway is to consider smaller and smaller parameters: After developing an FPT-algorithm for some parameter
k
, one may try to find an FPT-algorithm for some parameter
k^{\prime}
which is never larger than
k
. For
Vertex Cover
, such a parameter is for example the treewidth
\mathsf{tw}
of
G
for which an algorithm with running time
\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot n)
is known.

\mathsf{tw}
.
From a purely theoretical standpoint, the latter algorithm is usually deemed preferable: For
Vertex Cover
, the parameterization by the solution size bound
k
is essentially a parameterization by the vertex cover number of
G
, denoted
\mathsf{vc}
. This number is the size of a smallest vertex cover of
G
. Due to the known parameter relation
\mathsf{vc}\geq\mathsf{tw}
, any FPT-algorithm for the treewidth trivially implies an FPT-algorithm for the vertex cover number
\mathsf{vc}
.
This line of research in parameterized algorithms is commonly referred to as multivariate algorithmics
[
7
,
18
,
19
,
39
,
43
]
or structural parameterization
[
6
,
24
,
32
,
38
]
. There are at least two potential pitfalls with this approach: First, at least for graph problems, the space of known parameterizations is huge and ever-growing. Hence, it is infeasible to thoroughly study all parameterizations and essentially every choice of a parameter subset in an algorithmic study is in some sense arbitrary. Second, the improvement in the parameter dimension often comes at the cost of an increased running time. In the
Vertex Cover
example, the running time bound for treewidth is larger than for the vertex cover number. Hence, it is not clear whether the theoretical improvement in the parameter dimension carries over to better algorithms in practice or whether the running times actually get worse.

To avoid both pitfalls, we propose a data-driven approach, where the actual values of parameters in real-world instances guide the navigation through parameter space: When going from a parametrization
k
to a parameterization
k^{\prime}
, we may then choose the parameterization which has smaller values in practice so any obtained FPT-algorithms will be more relevant in practice. Moreover, when knowing the typical relation between the values of two parameters in real-world instances, we may better compare known running time bounds.

Klam-Values.

A classic idea for computing the range of feasible parameter values for an FPT-algorithm are Klam-values, proposed by Downey and Fellows
[
15
]
. For a given FPT-algorithm with running time
f(k)\cdot\operatorname{poly}(n)
, this is the largest value of
k
such that
f(k)\leq 10^{20}
. Surely, this is a simplification of the actual border of tractability: the choice of
10^{20}
may seem arbitrary, the polynomial running time factor is ignored, and we assume that the actual running times are close to the proven running time bound. Nevertheless, this value serves as a rough estimate of the range of tractable parameters.

For a range of typical running times
f(k)
, we count how many of the 87 benchmark instances would be solvable for the observed parameter values. The results are shown in
Table

2^{k/\log k}
would give feasible algorithms for almost all instance-parameter combinations. For a running time of
2^{k}
, still at least half of the instances are solvable for the majority of the parameters. For a running time of
k^{k}
, one needs to consider either the smaller degree-based parameters or
\mathsf{tw}
to solve half of the instances. Maybe somewhat surprisingly, more than a third of the instances can be solved when the parameter is the treewidth and the running time is doubly exponential.

The table also reveals a danger when comparing two parameters based on the relation to
n
as done above: for splitwidth, the average value of

\nabla

3.6
Parameterizations Based on Quotient Graphs

As observed in the analysis of the single parameters, the neighborhood-based parameterizations neighborhood diversity and modular-width are often not small enough to lead to practical FPT running times. Still, for many problems two vertices with similar neighborhood are, informally speaking, redundant and we would like to have parameters that are small whenever we have many such redundancies. We propose a systematic way to define such parameterizations by basing them on the quotient graphs of the modular decomposition or on the
twin-quotient graph
.

Twin-Quotient Graph-Based Parameters.

Recall that in the definition of neighborhood diversity, two vertices
u
and
v
are twins if
N(u)\setminus\{v\}=N(v)\setminus\{u\}
. The twin relation is an equivalence relation. For a vertex
u
, let
[u]
denote the equivalence class (also called
twin class
) of
u
.

Definition 1
.

The
twin-quotient graph
of a graph
G
is the graph
G_{\mathsf{nd}}
with vertex set
V(G_{\mathsf{nd}})\coloneqq\{[u]:u\in V(G)\}
and
E(G_{\mathsf{nd}})\coloneqq\{\{[u],[v]\}:[u]\neq[v]\land\{u,v\}\in E(G)\}
.

Informally, the twin-quotient graph has one vertex of every twin class and two such vertices
t
and
t^{\prime}
are adjacent if the members of
t
are adjacent to the members of
t^{\prime}
. Note that the the twin-quotient graph of
G
is an induced subgraph of
G
. We use the notation
G_{\mathsf{nd}}
to highlight that the neighborhood diversity is essentially the number of vertices of that graph. Now for any parameter
k
we define a corresponding parameter
k_{\mathsf{nd}}
which is defined just as
k
but on the twin-quotient graph. This is formalized as follows.

Definition 2
.

Let
k:\mathcal{G}\to\mathds{N}
be a parameter. Then, the parameter
k_{\mathsf{nd}}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathds{N}
is defined via
k_{\mathsf{nd}}(G)\coloneqq k(G_{\mathsf{nd}})
.

In general, it is not guaranteed that
k_{\mathsf{nd}}(G)\leq k(G)
holds for every graph. Most common parameters and all parameters considered in this work, however, behave monotonely with respect to taking induced subgraphs. More precisely, they do not increase whenever we delete a vertex from
G
. For such parameters,
k_{\mathsf{nd}}(G)\leq k(G)
holds since
G_{\mathsf{nd}}
is an induced subgraph of
G
. Let us mention in passing that we are so far only aware of one application of a similar type of parameterization: Golovach et al.
[
27
]
used a parameter very similar to
\mathsf{vc}_{\mathsf{nd}}
in a study on enumeration kernels, the difference is that they do not consider
G_{\mathsf{nd}}
but instead a graph obtained by merging only the
true
twins, that is, vertices
u
and
v
with
N[u]=N[v]
, of
G
.

Modular-Width-Based Parameters.

For the modular-width, the parameter definition is more involved. Recall that the modular-width is defined over modular decompositions. These are pairs
(\mathcal{T},\beta)
consisting of a rooted tree
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A})
and a function
\beta
that maps each node
x\in\mathcal{V}
to its quotient graph
\beta(x)
. The modular-width is the maximum number of vertices of any
\beta(x)
. The idea now is simply to consider any parameter
k
instead of the number of vertices. Formally, this leads to the following.

Definition 3
.

Let
k:\mathcal{G}\to\mathds{N}
be
a parameter.
Moreover, for a given graph
G
and a modular decomposition
(\mathcal{T},\beta)
of
G
, we define
k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T},\beta))\coloneqq\max_{x\in V(\mathcal{T})}k(\beta(x))
.
Then, the parameter
k_{\mathsf{mw}}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathds{N}
is defined as
k_{\mathsf{mw}}(G)\coloneqq k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G}))
, where
(\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G})
denotes a modular
decomposition of
G
that minimizes
k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G}))
.

Such a parameterization of the modular decomposition was used in the context of parameterized local search where an FPT-algorithm for
\Delta
was improved to one for
\Delta_{\mathsf{mw}}

[
41
]
.

Note that with these definitions, we have
n_{\mathsf{nd}}(G)=\mathsf{nd}(G)
and
n_{\mathsf{mw}}(G)=\mathsf{mw}(G)
. Since we consider parameters
k
that are never larger than
n
, each parameter
k_{\mathsf{nd}}(G)
is simultaneously upper-bounded by
k

and
by
\mathsf{nd}(G)
.
Moreover, as the twin-quotient graph of
G
is a supergraph of every prime graph in every modular decomposition of
G
, we further obtain that
k_{\mathsf{mw}}\leq k_{\mathsf{nd}}
.

We now describe how we find the right modular decomposition of a graph, that is, the modular decomposition that defines the parameter
k_{\mathsf{mw}}
, efficiently.

Lemma 1
.

Let
k:\mathcal{G}\to\mathds{N}
be a parameter for which the value does not increase by taking induced subgraphs.
Then, for each graph
G
, we can compute a modular decomposition
(\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G})
of
G
in linear time, such that
k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G}))=k_{\mathsf{mw}}(G)
.

Proof.

We can compute in linear time
[
57
]
a modular decomposition
(\mathcal{T},\beta)
of
G
for which each quotient graph
\beta(x)
on at least two vertices is
prime
, that is, the modular width of each quotient graph
\beta(x)
equals the number of vertices of
\beta(x)
.
Let
(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
be any other modular decomposition of
G
, then for each
x\in V(\mathcal{T})
, there is a
y\in V(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
, such that
\beta(x)
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
\beta^{\prime}(y)
.
In particular for
x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in V(\mathcal{T})}k(\beta(x))
, there is some
y\in V(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
where
\beta(x^{*})
is isomorphic to an induces subgraph of
\beta^{\prime}(y)
.
As parameter
k
does not increase by taking induced subgraphs, this implies that
k(\beta(x^{*}))\leq k(\beta^{\prime}(y))
, which implies that
k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T},\beta))\leq k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}))
.
Hence,
k_{\mathsf{mw}}((\mathcal{T}_{G},\beta_{G}))=k_{\mathsf{mw}}(G)
.
∎

4
Algorithms

Next, we will briefly explain what algorithms we use to compute the various parameters and how they are implemented.
The algorithms for most of the simple parameters that can be computed in polynomial time are directly implemented in Python with the help of the NetworkX package
[
29
]
.
These algorithms are described in
Section

4.1
.
For some of the parameters that are NP-hard to compute, we used existing solvers written in Java, C, or C++.
We will list these solvers in
Section

4.2
. Finally, to compute the remaining parameters, we use ILP-formulations that are then solved by the ILP-solver Gurobi
[
28
]
.
These will be explained in
Section

4.3
.

4.1
Polynomial-time Computable Parameters

To compute the parameters
n
,
m
, the maximum degree
\Delta
, the degeneracy
d
, and the
k
-core size we use existing implementations that are part of the NetworkX package
[
29
]
.
For the parameters
h
-index, closure number, and neighborhood diversity we implemented simple and straightforward algorithms that directly follow from the definition of these parameters.
To compute the dilworth number, we use the known reduction of the problem to a bipartite maximum matching instance
[
22
,
20
]
. This instance solved by a method provided by NetworkX.

To compute the weak closure number, we first fix some number
\gamma
.
We then continuously remove any vertex
v
from
G
that does not share at least
\gamma
neighbors with any non-neighbor of
v
.
A graph
G
is weakly
\gamma
-closed if and only if the graph is empty after this process
[
21
]
.
Hence, we can compute the weak closure number of
G
by performing a binary search for
\gamma
.
We use the minimum of
d+1
and the closure number
c
of
G
as an initial upper bound for
\gamma
.

4.1.1
Split-Width

Our implementation for computing the split-width is based on an algorithm developed by Cunningham
[
10
]
for directed graphs that runs in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time and which is trivially adaptable to undirected graphs.
Even though there are linear time algorithms to compute the split-width of an undirected graph
[
9
]
, we decided to implement the comparably slower algorithm by Cunningham
[
10
]
based on the simplicity of the algorithm.

Intuitively, Cunningham’s algorithm
[
10
]
works as follows:
For a given graph
G
, the algorithm tries to find a simple decomposition of
G
, if one exists.
If no such simple decomposition exists, that is, if
G
is prime, the algorithm terminates.
Otherwise, if there is a simple decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2})
of
G
, the algorithm performs the above procedure for both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
recursively.
To determine, whether there is a simple decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2})
of
G
, the algorithm iterates over all edges
e
of
G
and checks whether there is a split
(V_{1},V_{2})
of
G
that is “crossed” by
e
.
Here, we say that an edge
e

crosses
a split
(V_{1},V_{2})
of
G
if each of the parts of the split contains exactly one endpoint of
e
.
Checking whether there is a split
(V_{1},V_{2})
of
G
that is crossed by
e
(and if so, finding one) can be done efficiently
[
10
]
.

To speed up our implementation, we prevent the algorithm from checking whether an edge
e
crosses a split in any subgraph, if we already checked that
e
does not cross any split of the supergraph.
This is based on the fact that, if an edge
e
of
G
crosses no split of
G
, then for each simple decomposition
(G_{1},G_{2})
of
G
,
e
does not cross any split of
G_{1}
and
e
does not cross any split of
G_{2}
. Below, we give a proof of this fact.

Lemma 2
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
(G_{1},G_{2})
be a simple decomposition of
G
, and let
\{u,v\}\in E(G)\cap E(G_{1})
.
If there is a split
(W_{1},W_{2})
of
G_{1}
with
u\in W_{1}
and
v\in W_{2}
, then there is a split of
G
that is crossed by
\{u,v\}
.

Proof.

Let
x
be the unique vertex of
V(G_{1})\setminus V(G)
.
Without loss of generality assume that
x\in W_{1}
.
We define a partition
(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
of
V(G)
with
u\in V^{\prime}_{1}
and
v\in V^{\prime}_{2}
as follows:
We set
V_{1}^{\prime}\coloneqq(W_{1}\cup V(G_{2}))\setminus\{x\}
and
V_{2}^{\prime}\coloneqq V(G)\setminus V_{1}^{\prime}=W_{2}
.
In the following, we show that
(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
is a split of
G
.
Since
(W_{1},W_{2})
is a split of
G_{1}
,
|W_{1}|\geq 2
and
|W_{2}|\geq 2
.
Hence,
V_{2}^{\prime}
has size at least two.
Moreover, since
(G_{1},G_{2})
is a simple decomposition of
G
,
V(G_{2})
contains at least three vertices.
Consequently,
V_{1}^{\prime}
has size at least two.
Hence, to show that
(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
is a split of
G
, it remains to show that each vertex of
Y\coloneqq\{w\in V_{1}^{\prime}:\exists w^{\prime}\in V_{2}^{\prime}.\,\{w,w^{\prime}\}\in E(G)\}
has the same neighborhood in
V_{2}^{\prime}=W_{2}
.
Let
y
and
z
be distinct vertices of
Y
.
We show that
N(y)\cap V_{2}^{\prime}=N(z)\cap V_{2}^{\prime}
.
To this end, we distinguish three cases about the location of
y
and
z
.

Case 1:
Both
y
and
z
are contained in
V(G_{1})
.

Then, the statement holds by the fact that
(W_{1},W_{2})
is a split of
G_{1}
with
\{y,z\}\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}
and
V_{2}^{\prime}=W_{2}
.

Case 2:
Both
y
and
z
are contained in
V(G_{2})
.

The, the statement holds by the fact that
W_{2}
contains no vertex of
V(G_{2})
and each vertex of
V(G_{2})
with at least one neighbor in
V(G_{1})\setminus\{x\}
has the same neighborhood in
V(G_{1})\setminus\{x\}
.
The latter holds, since
(G_{1},G_{2})
is a simple decomposition of
G
.

Case 3:
Without loss of generality
y
is contained in
V(G_{1})
and
z
is contained in
V(G_{2})
.

Since
(G_{1},G_{2})
is a simple decomposition of
G
,
z
has the same neighborhood in
V(G_{1})
as
x
.
Moreover, since
z
is contained in
Y
,
z
has at least one neighbor in
W_{2}
.
Hence,
x
and
z
have the same neighborhood in
W_{2}=V^{\prime}_{2}
as
y
since
(W_{1},W_{2})
is a split of
G_{1}
.

Hence, each vertex of
Y
has the same neighborhood in
W_{2}
.
This implies that,
(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
is a split of
G
.
∎

4.2
Existing Solvers

To compute the parameters modular-width, feedback vertex set number, treedepth and treewidth we use existing solvers.
For modular-width we use a solver by Mizutani who implemented a linear-time algorithm by Tedder et al.
[
57
]
. The original code can be found at
https://github.com/mogproject/modular-decomposition
.
For feedback vertex set we use the winning solver from the 2016 PACE challenge
[
11
]
by Iwata and Imanishi. Their solver first computes a linear-time kernel
[
34
]
and then uses an FPT branch and bound algorithm
[
36
]
. The original code can be found at
https://github.com/wata-orz/fvs
.
For treedepth we use an updated version of the winning solver from the 2020 PACE challenge
[
44
]
by Trimble
[
59
]
. The original code can be found at
https://github.com/jamestrimble/bute
.
Finally, for treewidth we use a solver by Tamaki
[
56
]
. The original code can be found at
https://github.com/twalgor/RTW
.

4.3
ILP-Formulations for the Remaining Parameters

We use standard ILP-formulations of the respective optimization problems to compute the vertex subset parameters from
Section

2.2.3
with the exception of feedback vertex set for which we use the above-mentioned solver by Iwata and Imanishi. To solve these formulations we then use Gurobi
[
28
]
. In this section we will explain these ILP-formulations.

Generally, the ILP-formulations for all these problems work similarly, since each of these problems involves finding a minimum-size modulator
S
to some graph property
\Pi
:
For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, we introduce a binary variable
x_{v}
.
The variable
x_{v}
should be set to
1
by the ILP if and only if
v
is contained in the sought minimum-size modulator
S
.

We now just need to add constraints to ensure that
G-S
fulfills property
\Pi
.
In the following, we describe how these constraints look for the individual parameters.

•

For the vertex cover number, we add for each
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
the constraint
x_{u}+x_{v}\geq 1
.

•

For the
r
-bounded-degree deletion number, we need to ensure that for each vertex
v\in V(G)
we include
v
in
S
or we include at least
\deg_{G}(v)-r
neighbors of
v
in
S
.
To this end, we add for each vertex
v\in V(G)
the constraint

\deg_{G}(v)-r\leq\deg_{G}(v)\cdot x_{v}+\sum_{n\in N_{G}(v)}x_{n}.

•

For the cluster vertex deletion number, we add the constraint
1\leq x_{u}+x_{v}+x_{w}
for each induced path
(u,v,w)
in
G
.

•

For distance to cographs, we add the constraint
1\leq\sum_{v\in V(P)}x_{v}
for every induced path
P
of length
3
.

•

For
4
-path vertex cover we add the constraint
1\leq\sum_{v\in V(P)}x_{v}
for every (not necessarily induced) path
P
of length
3
.

Additionally, for cluster vertex deletion and
4
-path vertex cover we used some straightforward reduction rules and added some additional constraints to the ILP for small-degree vertices to slightly speed up the computation of these parameters.

Note that for
4
-path vertex cover, we might add
\Theta(n^{4})
constraints to the ILP.
To avoid this, we add most of the constraints in a lazy fashion.
That is, initially we only add a small subset of the constraints.
Afterwards, we let Gurobi find an optimal solution
S
to the currently added constraints.
If
S
is not a modulator to the respective graph property
\Pi
, then at least one (currently not added) constraint is violated.
In this case, we add a small amount of new constraints to the ILP that are currently violated.
This procedure is repeated until, eventually, a minimum-size modulator is found.

4.3.1
Vertex Integrity

We compute the vertex integrity via ILPs for a related parameter, the
r
-component order connectivity number
of a graph
G
. This parameter, denoted by
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
, is the size of a smallest modulator of
G
to the graph property that consists of all graphs
G^{\prime}
with
\mathsf{cc}(G^{\prime})\leq r
where
\mathsf{cc}(G^{\prime})
is the number of vertices in the largest connected component of
G^{\prime}

[
16
]
.
Recall that
\mathsf{vi}(G)\coloneqq\min\{|X|+\mathsf{cc}(G-X):X\subseteq V(G)\}

[
2
,
16
,
25
]
. An equivalent way to define the vertex integrity via
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
is
\mathsf{vi}(G)\coloneqq\min\{r+{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G):r\in\mathds{N}\}
.
Hence, to compute
\mathsf{vi}(G)
, we simply compute
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
for all values of
r\in[ub]
in increasing order where
ub
is an upper bound for
\mathsf{vi}(G)
. As initial upper bound we set
ub\coloneqq\mathsf{cc}(G)

[
26
]
.

To compute
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
, we use the above-desribed general ILP approach and add the constraint
1\leq\sum_{v\in C}x_{v}
for every connected subgraph

of

with exactly

After computing

for some

. Once we have

must be equal to
\mathsf{vi}(G)
. We can also use

as an upper bound for the objective value of the ILPs. Since we only care about improving

until it is equal to
\mathsf{vi}(G)
it is fine if this leads to some of the ILP formulations being infeasible. We also use

as a lower bound for the objective value of the ILPs
[
26
]
.
We denote this algorithm as
vi

basic
.

Our experiments show that
vi

basic
is not fast enough to compete with the solvers for the other parameters.
Because of this, we developed several improvements for our implementation in the form of an initial graph reduction or additional constraints for the ILP formulations. We will use this subsection to explain and show the correctness of these improvements.

The first two improvements let us identify some vertices that are either always part of a minimum size modulator for
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
or never.
We can use these to reduce the number of variables we add to each ILP instance.

Lemma 3

(Folklore)
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
r
be some integer. Any vertex
v\in V(G)
that is contained in a connected component

with
|C|\leq r
is never part of a minimum-size modulator for
{r}\textnormal{-}\mathsf{coc}(G)
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
G
be a graph and let

a connected subset of
V(G)
with
|N[C]|>\mathsf{vi}(G)
. Then each vi-set of
G
contains at least one vertex of

.

Proof.

Assume towards a contradiction that there is a vi-set
X
of
G
with
X\cap C=\emptyset
.
Note that since

is connected and since

, all vertices in

must be in the same connected component
C^{\prime}
of

.
Now, any vertex in

is either in

or in

which implies
|X|+|C^{\prime}|\geq|N[C]|>\mathsf{vi}(G)
.
But since

is a vi-set of

,
\mathsf{vi}(G)=|X|+\mathsf{cc}(G-X)>|X|+|C^{\prime}|
; a contradiction.
∎

The special case of
Lemma

4
for

was originally shown by Drange et al.
[
16
, Theorem 6]
.
We can use this special case to identify vertices that are part of any vi-set.
Since we do not know the exact value of
\mathsf{vi}(G)
while the algorithm is running, we instead use the current best upper bound.
When we would add constraints for a connected vertex set
D
of size at most
r+1
to the ILP, we can keep track of the size of smaller connected subsets

.
If the size of the closed neighborhood of such a vertex set

exceeds the current upper bound for
\mathsf{vi}(G)
, we add the constraint only for the vertex set

to the ILP.
Due to

, this is correct.

Next, we use a result by Gima et al.
[
25
, Lemma 2.2]
.
For any vertex set

, a vertex

is

Gima et al.

The correctness of this statement follows from the observation that if

are in at most one connected component of

In fact, since we know that

is a vi-set, the size of

must increase by exactly one.

Lemma

5
states some properties that directly follow from this result.

Lemma 5
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
S
a vi-set that contains no redundant vertex.
Then,

1.

there is no vertex

with

,

2.

the vertex set

contains no simplicial vertex, and

Proof.

We prove the statement by contradiction.
To this end, we show that

contains a redundant vertex, if at least one of the three properties is not fulfilled.

Let

be a vertex with

. In this case the neighborhood of

is empty which implies that by definition,

is a redundant vertex.

Item

:
Let

be a simplicial vertex in

Since the neighborhood of

in

Hence,

is a redundant vertex.

Item

:
Let

Then each neighbor of

It would be difficult to add constraints to the ILP enforcing the property that the solution set is not allowed to contain redundant vertices. Instead, we add constraints that enforce the three properties of
Lemma

.
For
Item

1
, for every vertex

we add the constraint

\deg(v)\geq\sum_{w\in N[v]}x_{w}.

We can further use
Item

2
to reduce the number of variables in the ILP by not including simplicial vertices.
Finally, for

3

For each two distinct vertices

Item

contains at least all vertices of

should also contain vertex

.
This can be expressed by the constraint

x_{v_{1}}\leq x_{v_{2}}+|R|-\sum_{v\in R}x_{v}.

We could theoretically add this constraint for every pair of vertices.
However, we decided to only add the constraint for pairs for which

has size at most three.

Our final improvement is based on the following observation. Let
r
be some number and let

be a cut vertex in

increases the number of connected components by at least one.
If removing

from

in a modulator for

instead of including any vertex in

To generalize this idea, we use the following definition.
To this end, recall that for a vertex set

denotes the smallest integer

of size

Definition 4
.

For a graph
G
and an integer

, a family

of pairs of vertex sets is called an

-cut decomposition
of

fulfills the following properties:

a)

b)

\mathsf{cut}(C)\subseteq N(C)

c)

\mathsf{conn}(C\cup\mathsf{cut}(C))\geq|\mathsf{cut}(C)|

for each two pairs

One possible

-cut decomposition consists of the pairs

However, in most graphs this will only cover a small part of the graph.
Ideally we would like to find an

For each pair

The reason for this is the following lemma, which describes how an

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}

There is a minimum-size modulator

(N(C)\setminus S\subseteq\mathsf{cut}(C)\wedge|C|=r)\Rightarrow\mathsf{cut}(C)\subseteq S

Let

Hence, we only have to show that

Without loss of generality, we assume that

Also, notice if we have

Otherwise, if

By definition of

.
Next, we show that for each pair

with respect to

To compute an

-cut decomposition we do the following:

\mathcal{D}
,

Lemma 7

An

Proof.

The Properties a), b) and c) of

5
Conclusion

We evaluated 19 popular graph parameters on 87 real-world graphs.
On one hand, some parameters such as neighborhood diversity
\mathsf{nd}
or modular-width
\mathsf{mw}
are surprisingly large for most graphs; for more than half of the graphs, they are larger than
n/2
.
Thus, in order to improve for example upon a trivial brute-force algorithm with running time
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
, one needs to design single-exponential algorithms for
\mathsf{nd}
or
\mathsf{mw}
whose base is not much larger than 2.
Of course studying these parameters is still well-motivated if, for example, the resulting algorithms use interesting techniques.

On the other hand, treewidth
\mathsf{tw}
and degree-based parameters that are smaller than the maximum degree
\Delta
such as
h
-index or degeneracy
d
are quite small on all graphs.
For example, the largest two values of
\mathsf{tw}
in all graphs are 58 and 57; in all other graphs the treewidth is at most 41. In addition, the best considered modulator-based parameters such as distance to cographs and feedback vertex set number are also reasonably small in many instances.
Hence, from a data-driven perspective the parameterized algorithmics community should focus on developing efficient FPT-algorithms for these parameters.
In addition, we showed considered combination of treewidth with other parameters and showed that the combination of
h
-index and treewidth is, from the data perspective, a good parameter combination that could be considered for problems that are hard when parameterized by treewidth alone. We also showed that an abundance of similar neighborhoods in the graphs can be captured by parameters based on the modular decomposition and the twin-quotient graph of the input graph. This could be a way to cope with the rather large values of neighborhood diversity and modular-width in some real-world data.

There are many possibilities to improve and extend our results. First, it would be clearly interesting to measure the parameter values on a much larger set of graphs. We would thus like to invite the community to point to interesting and relevant instances that could be added to the data repository. One of the factors that limits the number of evaluated graphs is the running time of the solvers for computing the parameter values. Hence, any improvement for the slowest solvers, which are currently the solvers for treedepth, treewidth, and vertex integrity will help broaden the data on which the analysis is based. Another way to overcome this limitation is to work instead with heuristic solvers for computing these parameters which one would do in practice anyway. To keep the study simple and to give knowledge on the actual parameter values and not on some upper bounds, we decided against this approach here but for the future it might be worth investigating.

Ideally, once the number and diversity of graphs has increased sufficiently, one could and should also investigate how parameter values depend on the application background. For example we may ask whether the treewidth of social networks is smaller or larger than the treewidth of infrastructure networks.

One may also consider more sophisticated descriptive statistics for comparing parameters. For example, the currently considered median and percentile values of the relation
k/n
ignore whether an instance with
k/n=0.1
was large or small. In a similar direction, one could think of more sophisticated models for estimating the tractable parameter value range other than the Klam-values.

Adding more parameters is also a clear goal for future studies. In particular, it would be interesting to extend the neighborhood-based category by some smaller parameters. In this context, we note that we excluded twinwidth and cliquewidth since current solvers for these parameters are not cabaple of solving a sufficient number of instances.

Finally, it would be interesting to perform similar studies for other classes of inputs such as directed graphs, strings, or Boolean formulas.
