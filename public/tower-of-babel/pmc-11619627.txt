Geographical disparities in cancer and occupational exposure to pesticides in a French-West Indies territory (2006–2019)
Authors: Rémi Houpert, Jacqueline Véronique-Baudin, Thierry Almont, Murielle Beaubrun-Renard, Manon Boullard, Aimée Pierre-Louis, Mylène Vestris, Stephen Ulric-Gervaise, Christelle Montabord, Jonathan Macni, Emmanuelle Sylvestre, Clarisse Joachim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the French West Indies, previous studies have reported findings on pesticide exposure and cancer incidence. A new exploratory geographical study, including a more sensitive soil contamination indicator, will provide more detailed information on the link between cancer incidence and organochlorine pesticide exposure. This study aims to investigate the association between incident cases of cancer and chlordecone soil contamination at an IRIS scale over the period 2006–2019 in Martinique.MethodsData on the 18 most frequent cancer sites were collected from the Martinique Population Based Cancer Registry. First, smoothed standardized incidence ratios at the level of the 141 IRIS were estimated, and then regression models including the smoothed standardized ratios were performed between cancer incidence and soil contamination index by sex and cancer site, using the Besag, York and Mollié models. Models were adjusted using an index of social deprivation. Results23,218 cancer cases were eligible for this study, with 21,920 cases included in the final analysis. Soil contamination was associated with a higher relative risk of cancer in women than in men, but no significant correlation was found for breast cancer in women or for prostate cancer. Lung, thyroid, stomach and rectal cancers in women, and melanoma in men, all increase significantly with the level of contamination. There was a significant increasing gradient in the incidence of multiple myeloma in men, from reference to highly contaminated areas.ConclusionIntegrating the latest available data on soil contamination and cancer incidence will extend on-going knowledge of chlordecone exposure and cancer incidence, which remains a major environmental concern in Martinique. Unexpectedly, our results show that the female population of Martinique is the most affected by chlordecone soil contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13221-6.Keywords: Caribbean, Pesticides, Epidemiology, Environment, Neoplasms

Background

Martinique, with an area of 1,128 km², is a French overseas region located in the West Indies. In terms of health, Martinique reports a high prevalence of chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and chronic renal insufficiency. The distribution of cancers differs significantly from that in mainland France, with major disparities for certain cancer sites (higher standardized incidences of prostate and stomach cancers while lower rates for cancers of the corpus uteri, head and neck, liver, lung, and brain). According to GLOBOCAN, the age-standardized incidence rate per 100,000, in both sexes for all cancers in 2022, is 224.2 per 100,000 in Martinique, lower than mainland France (339 per 100,000) but higher than in the Caribbean region (193.5 per 100,000) [1, 2].

This situation could evolve unfavourably due to the ageing of the population and the development of certain avoidable risk factors linked to lifestyle (sedentary lifestyle, obesity, alcohol, and tobacco consumption in particular). Prostate cancer, cervical cancer, stomach cancer, and multiple myeloma have a higher incidence than in mainland France. Infectious agents and genetic and environmental factors are implicated in the occurrence or aggravation of these cancers. However, the role of environmental factors such as pesticide pollution needs to be studied.

Martinique has experienced significant environmental exposure to chlordecone, a persistent organochlorine pesticide used extensively between 1972 and 1993 in banana plantations to eradicate the banana root borer [3, 4]. Chlordecone, a chemical endocrine disruptor, remains in the environment, contaminating soil, water and ecosystems for decades after its use [5, 6]. The intensive use and persistence of this pesticide have led to widespread contamination of farmland, agricultural products, water sources and wildlife [7, 8]. Consequently, this pesticide has had a significant impact on public health, the environment and the local economy [9]. Although the population’s exposure to chlordecone has decreased overall, the most exposed population continues to have high levels of chlordecone contamination [10].

Environmental studies on the impact of chlordecone exposure on the population have focused on cancer and have suspected chlordecone of being linked to some cancers, notably prostate cancer [11, 12]. In Martinique, a geographical analysis on the incidence of cancers linked to soil pollution by organochlorine pesticides identified a particular spatial distribution of multiple myeloma cases in contaminated areas [13, 14]. However, the studies reported the difficulty to establish a direct causal link between exposure and incidence of cancer, considering the indirect effects on human health through the impact of pesticides on ecosystems.

Soil pollution and cancer are significant environmental issues that could seriously affect the environment and human health. The geographical disparities in cancer incidence observed at different scales in the French West Indies regions could highlight high-risk areas that require special attention to improve health policies in order to contribute to the development of strategies to reduce the expected increasing incidence of these neoplasms.

A new geographical study of cancer incidence based on data on contamination by pesticides at a finer geographical level, incorporating spatialized indicators of exposure risk, was needed to study possible associations [15]. The aim will be to analyse the correlation between the mapping of chlordecone contaminated soils and the geolocation of cancer cases in Martinique from 2006 to 2019.

Methods

Population and design

This retrospective observational epidemiological study covers the period from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2019. The study involved all patients aged 18 and above diagnosed with invasive tumours, utilizing data from the Population Based Cancer Registry (PBCR) of Martinique database.

Data collection

Sociodemographic data (age at diagnosis, sex, area of social deprivation) and clinical variables (incidence date, histology and topography) were exhaustively collected from the 18 most common cancer sites in Martinique: prostate (International Classification of Diseases 10: C61), breast (C50), colon (C18-C19), rectum (C20, C21), stomach (C16), esophagus (C25), pancreas (C15), liver (C22), lung (C33-C34), multiple myeloma (C42. 1 and morphology code 9731 to 9734), cervix (C53), body of uterus (C54), ovary (C56), kidney (C64), brain (C71), thyroid (C73), upper aerodigestive tract (VADS) (C00 to C14 and C30 to C32), melanoma (C44 and morphology code 8720 to 8770) and the location of any cancer (C00-C80).

Geographical data (social deprivation and chlordecone soil contamination) were collected for each cases according to the 141 Statistical Information Clusters (Ilots Regroupés pour l’Information Statistique, IRIS) that define Martinique [16]. An IRIS is the smallest spatial statistical unit, lower than the communal level. It represents the foundation for sub-municipal data distribution. Additional data from the French National Institute for Statistics and Economic Studies (Institut National de la Statistique et des Etudes Economiques, INSEE) were used census data by age group at IRIS level.

Chlordecone soil contamination index

The chlordecone soil contamination index is the result of a multi-criteria analysis performed by the the French Geological and Mining Research Institute (Bureau de Recherches Géologiques et Minières, BRGM) and Martinique research teams [17] (Supplementary file 1). It is a geographical cross-referencing based on: (i) the distribution of the 141 IRIS in Martinique, (ii) the mapping of soil contamination by chlordecone (2022) and (iii) the mapping of banana-growing history (computed on the 1970, 1980 and 1992 land-use maps). For each IRIS, areas are classified as highly contaminated, moderately contaminated, low contaminated and non-contaminated, where areas with and without a banana history are taken into account.

A 4-class zoning was developed based on the level of contamination (chlordecone found (< 0.1 mg/kg soil), intermediate (0.1 to 1 mg/kg soil) and high (> 1 mg/kg soil)):

Class 1: less than 25% of the analysed area contaminated,

Social deprivation index

We used an indicator described by D. Luce [18] (Supplementary file 1). It was developed from 2008 census data, at the IRIS level. From an initial a priori selection of 137 social, demographic and economic indicators, principal component analyses were used to select 8 variables: proportion of unemployed, blue-collar workers, executives, permanent jobs, people without qualifications, households without cars, main residences with hot water and air conditioning. The deprivation index is defined as the first component of a principal component analysis of these 8 variables. The index was calculated for each IRIS. A hierarchical classification was used to group IRIS into 3 classes: IRIS with low deprivation, IRIS with moderate deprivation and IRIS with high deprivation.

Missing data

Missing census population data for 2006 and 2019 missing IRIS were estimated using diagonal interpolation [19]. Diagonal interpolation is useful when working on population data distributed over a geographical area.

We assessed the convergence of the Markov chains generated by WinBUGS using several criteria, which enabled us to reliably interpret the posterior distributions. Firstly, we visually inspected the traces plot for the key model parameters, ensuring that the chains showed stable fluctuations around a constant mean after a sufficient burn-in period (trace plot and history plot). Next, we checked the plot density, ensuring that only one modality was observed, and the quantile analysis, verifying that the chains were stacked. Then we examined the autocorrelation of the samples to assess the independence of successive iterations, looking to see if the autocorrelations of the chains were decreasing rapidly. Finally, we checked for the R-Hat (Gelman-Rubin criterion) [21], whether the values were close to 1.

SAS2WinBUGS was used to program Bayesian models in WinBUGS (v1.4.3) from SAS (v.9.4). R software (v3.6.2) with the R2WinBUGS package was used to calculate the 80% credibility intervals when the models were convergent. Maps were generated using QGIS. Results of the model are presented with their 80% credibility intervals, given the exploratory context of this study.

In women, we observed an excess RR in lung and rectal cancers (27% and 29% respectively) in moderate contamination area. Whereas, for liver, colon and ovarian cancers, a lower RR was significantly associated with low contamination exposure areas. There were significant increasing gradients in incidence of thyroid cancer and multiple myeloma in women, from reference to highly contamination areas. In the high contamination area two main results was found among women, (i) a 28% excess RR for stomach cancers and (ii) a 19% lower RR for cervical cancers (Table 3; Fig. 1).

In men, an 40% excess RR was found for oesophagus cancers in areas of high contamination, and 93% for malignant melanomas in areas of moderate contamination. Lower RRs were found for cancers of the rectum (19%) and kidney (29%) in areas of moderate contamination, and for pancreatic cancers (17%) in areas of high exposure. There was a significant increasing gradient in incidence of multiple myeloma in men, from reference to highly contaminated areas and a lower RR was associated with lung cancers and all soil contamination zones (Table 3; Fig. 1).

No significant RRs were found for breast cancer in women or prostate cancer in men. In both sexes, lower RR were found for brain cancers and chlordecone soil contamination areas (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This article describes the results of an epidemiological analysis of the distribution of cancers and their correlation with a soil contamination index at IRIS level, by location, over the period 2006–2019, using data from the PBCR of Martinique.

For the first time, results from this study revealed that women in Martinique were most affected by soil contamination. To our knowledge, this is the first study to establish that the risk of developing thyroid, stomach and rectal cancer in women, and melanoma in men, increases significantly with the level of contamination. This study was also the first to highlight the link between moderate contamination with chlordecone and lung cancer incidence among women in Martinique, compared with men.

Martinique, as the others French overseas departments, has more marked social inequalities than mainland France, with consequences in terms of mortality and morbidity from chronic diseases [22]. Although smoking is a known major risk factor for lung, stomach and rectum cancers [23–25], pesticide exposure can also contribute to the development of these cancers [26]. An increase in lung cancer mortality among pesticide applicators, almost exclusively men, has been reported, suggesting the possibility that pesticide exposure may increase the risk of lung cancer in farmers [27–30]. In vivo studies have shown that pesticides induce biochemical changes that lead to the development of tumor cells in the lungs [31]. However, our results showed an association mainly in women. In Martinique, over the period 2007–2014, an average of 89 men (4.1%) and 51 women (3.6%) are diagnosed with lung cancer per year. Standardized incidence and tobacco-related mortality rates are lower than in France. Lung cancer diagnosis remains mostly at an advanced stage, and survival rate also remains low, whatever the histology [32]. The prognosis of small-cell lung carcinoma is improving, however, with the development of targeted therapies and better identification of molecular markers for theranostic purposes in patients with metastatic cancer [33, 34].

Association between pesticide exposure and colorectal cancer has been found in the literature in epidemiological studies more than in studies measuring the level or concentration of pesticides in biological samples [35]. Martin FL et al. reported in the Brazilian population that pesticides played a role in the etiology of colorectal cancers, despite other risk factors [36]. Divergent gradient trends were observed (increasing in women and decreasing in men), although no significant risk was observed. In Martinique, colorectal cancer incidence is rising steadily, unlike in France, and conversely with the United States and United Kingdom, where incidence is decreasing [37]. Since 2007, an organized screening program has been proposed every 2 years to people aged 50 to 74, to deal with the increase in incidence. However, participation rates are still too poor compared with national and European expectations. Collaboration with existing organizations in the Caribbean is required to initiate larger-scale studies [38]. Anatomical-clinical characteristics of colorectal cancer and specific risk factors have to be studied in comparison with other regions of the world.

Regarding thyroid cancer, our results showed, for the first time in French West Indies, an excess risk coupled with an ascending gradient from reference to high contamination zones, especially among women. In Martinique, thyroid cancer mainly affects women (81%). These results are in line with those observed in a study analyzing the survival of thyroid cancer patients in Martinique over the period 2008–2018, including clinical characteristics. Lin et al. showed that women had a better overall 5-year survival rate (94% compared with 78% for men) [39].

The gradients found with multiple myeloma, for both sexes, confirm the results of previous Chlordecone plans [40]. Excess incidence of Multiple Myeloma was observed in men living in areas with the most contaminated soil. In Martinique, the incidence is double that in France [41]. Houpert et al. have documented that the temporal distribution of the incidence of hematological malignancies in Martinique, over the period 2009–2018, increases with age among men and women [42]. Well-established risk factors support the idea of etiological heterogeneity, including lifestyle and environmental factors. In addition, work on the banana worker cohort has shown a non-significant excess of deaths from non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma and a significant excess of deaths from multiple myeloma [43].

Results observed on melanoma shows excess risks only for the moderate contamination area in men. People exposed to pesticides, particularly those with professional exposure to the sun, have a higher risk of melanoma [44]. However, the effect of sun exposure remains difficult to demonstrate, given the data available in Martinique. The lack of significance may indicate that other factors, such as genetic predisposition (such as clear phenotypes) and extrinsic factors (such as immunosuppression), are more influential in melanoma development than pesticide exposure in this context. In fact, the possible link between contaminants such as chlordecone and the incidence of melanoma, evidence is not conclusive and no experimental study to date has established a definitive link. Findings from a meta-analysis showed no significant association between exposure to insecticides, pesticides or chlordecone and the risk of melanoma. Only an increased risk of cutaneous melanoma was found in people exposed to herbicides [45]. In addition, a population-based cohort study in British Columbia revealed an increased risk of cutaneous malignant melanoma in women associated with exposure to certain organochlorine pesticides, after adjustment for other risk factors such as exposure to sunlight. Environmental contaminants could be involved in the risk of cancer, but their impact varies according to the specific substance and the level of exposure [46].

Moreover, our results confirm once again that there is no link between the incidence of prostate cancer and chlordecone soil contamination. These results are also confirmed by the exploratory study carried out by the PBCR of Guadeloupe [47].

Nevertheless, the results obtained need to be interpreted at a geographical level rather than an individual level. Indeed, the methodology used has an ecological bias, due to the aggregation of data at a small geographical level. The estimated dose-response relationship at the level of the geographical unit may not necessarily reflect a dose-response relationship at the individual level. The time of residence of each individual in an IRIS, which would vary the time of exposure to the various risk factors, could not be taken into account.

Using the address at the time of diagnosis to geolocate a patient and assign him to an IRIS generated some classification errors due to the absence of residence history. A patient who had moved just prior to diagnosis was assigned the risk factors of his new residence, which did not correspond to his actual exposure. However, the intra-departmental moving rate in Martinique is low (around 3.31% in 2014) [48], which allows us to assume that this bias is minor. Moreover, the geographical analysis at municipality level was not possible, as the aggregation of data would have been very large and would have added to the heterogeneity bias within geographical units. In Martinique, IRIS units vary in size; for example, in less populated areas, IRIS units can be very large, or even cover the entire municipality. Consequently, over-dispersion due to intra-IRIS heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of results.

The soil contamination index is another indirect indicator. Most contamination at individual level comes from the diet. People living in contaminated areas are also more exposed through the food chain, as they often consume local products. However, dietary habits have evolved over time, and the soil pollution index probably does not perfectly reflect the level of exposure to chlordecone.

One of the main strengths of this study was the use of data from the PBCR of Martinique. This registry is a member of the European and French network of cancer registries and has contributed to European estimates of the burden of cancer. It has developed expert internal tools enabling the exhaustiveness of the IRIS geolocation.

Nevertheless, the data from soil contamination sampling carried out by BRGM is high quality, with a total of 9,965 ha analyzed [17]. The inclusion of historical banana cultivation (1970, 1980, 1992) and 93 “roadside” sources allowed the mapping of contamination. The levels of contamination used are robust, as they take account of IRIS aggregation and contamination potential.

Conclusion

Understanding environmental exposure to pesticides is a major public health issue. Data generation and research, through exploratory biomonitoring studies, will contribute to a better understanding of contamination patterns and processes.

Similar to most studies on the relationship between pesticide exposure and cancer risk, our study includes all cancer sites in order to confirm previous findings, or to identify new positive associations between pesticide exposure and cancer risk.

Findings from this study provide leads and trends for future research exploring the link between soil contamination and multiple myeloma and melanoma incidence. In addition, additional studies including a comprehensive analysis of water contamination would be necessary to confirm the trends in the results.

The unexpected finding revealed that the female population of Martinique is the most affected by soil contamination. Prevention and health promotion initiatives need to be targeted primarily at the female population identified as most at risk.

Finally, the results of these analyses are consistent with the epidemiological trends observed by our teams over the last few years in Martinique. The burden of cancer observed in our region, combined with an increasing risk gradient linked to soil contamination by Chlordecone, highlights new challenges in terms of prevention, diagnosis and management strategies, particularly through the integration of promising new therapies.
