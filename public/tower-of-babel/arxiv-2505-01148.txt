The class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} and mixture distributions with dominated continuous singular parts
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We consider a new class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} of distribution functions F F that have the property of rational-infinite divisibility: there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} such that F 1 = F ∗ F 2 F_{1}=F*F_{2} . A distribution function of the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} is quasi-infinitely divisible in the sense that its characteristic function admits the Lévy-type representation with a “signed spectral measure”. This class is a wide natural extension of the fundamental class of infinitely divisible distribution functions and it is actively studied now. We are interested in conditions for a distribution function F F to belong to the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} for the unexplored case, where F F may have a continuous singular part. We propose a criterion under the assumption that the continuous singular part of F F is dominated by the discrete part in a certain sense. The criterion generalizes the previous results by Alexeev and Khartov for discrete probability laws and the results by Berger and Kutlu for the mixtures of discrete and absolutely continuous laws. In addition, we describe the characteristic triplet of the corresponding Lévy-type representation, which may contain some continuous singular part. We also show that the assumption of the dominated continuous singular part cannot be simply omitted or even slightly extended (without some special assumptions). We apply the general criterion to some interesting particular examples. We also positively solve the decomposition problem stated by Lindner, Pan and Sato within the considered case.

Introduction

This paper is devoted to the study of a new class of probability laws that naturally extends the fundamental class of infinitely divisible distributions.

Let
F
be a distribution function on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that
F
(and the corresponding probability law) is called
infinitely divisible
if for every positive integer
n
there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” denotes the convolution operation, i.e.
F
is the
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. Let
f
be the characteristic function of
F
, i.e.

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It is well-known (see
[
12
]
,
[
31
]
, and
[
37
]
) that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if
f
admits
the Lévy representation
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)dL(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
the Gaussian variance

\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
, and
the Lévy spectral function

L:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is non-decreasing on every interval
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,+\infty)
, and it satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}L(x)=\lim_{x\to+\infty}L(x)=0,

(2)

and also

\displaystyle\int_{O_{\delta}}x^{2}dL(x)<\infty\quad\text{for any}\quad\delta>0,

where
O_{\delta}:=(-\delta,0)\cup(0,\delta)
. The function
L
is assumed to be right-continuous at every point of the real line. It is important to note that
the characteristic triplet

(\gamma,\sigma^{2},L)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
. Due to this representation, the class of infinitely divisible probability laws found a lot of applications through the Lévy processes (see
[
31
]
) in the stochastic calculus (see
[
4
]
), the teletraffic models (see
[
25
]
), and the insurance mathematics (see
[
32
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{I}
denote the class of all infinitely divisible distribution functions on the real line. This class is naturally extended by the following way. We call a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding probability law)
rational-infinitely divisible
if there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
. In terms of characteristic functions, this definition is equivalent to the formula
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, for the characteristic function
f
of
F
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are the characteristic functions of some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. We denote by
\boldsymbol{Q}
the class of all rational-infinitely divisible distribution functions. Since
F_{2}
may be chosen as degenerate at some point
a
here (i.e.
f_{2}(t)=e^{ita}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
), it is clear that, indeed,
\boldsymbol{I}\subset\boldsymbol{Q}
. Moreover, from the difinition, it is seen that the characteristic function
f
of any
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
admits
the Lévy-type representation
. Namely, if
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have the characteristic triplets
(\gamma_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2},L_{1})
and
(\gamma_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},L_{2})
, respectively, then formula (
1
) holds with
the shift parameter

\gamma=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
the Gaussian variance

\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}
, and
the spectral function

L=L_{1}-L_{2}
. In that case,
L
has a bounded total variation on
\mathbb{R}\setminus O_{\delta}
for every
\delta>0
, and, in general, it is non-monototic on the intervals
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,+\infty)
. The function
L
also inherits from
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
the right-continuity on
\mathbb{R}
and property (
2
). Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{O_{\delta}}x^{2}d|L|(x)<\infty\quad\text{for any}\quad\delta>0,

where we integrate over the variation of
L
. We now suppose that, conversely,
f
admits representation (
1
) with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
, and
L
satisfying the above conditions. Following Lindner and Sato
[
26
]
, the corresponding distribution function
F
(and the corresponding probability law) is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
. Let us fix any real
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
\gamma=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
. Let us fix any non-negative
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
such that
\sigma_{1}^{2}\geqslant\sigma_{2}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{2}
. Due to the Hahn–Jordan decomposition, it is not difficult to show that there exist some canonical Lévy spectral functions
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
satisfying the usual conditions with monotonicity such that
L=L_{1}-L_{2}
. Then
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are represented by canonical Lévy’s formula (
1
) with the characteristic triplets
(\gamma_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2},L_{1})
and
(\gamma_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},L_{2})
, respectively. So
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are the characteristic functions for some infinitely divisible distribution functions and hence the distribution function
F
corresponded to such an
f
is rational-infinitely divisible. Thus
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f
admits representation (
1
) with some
\gamma
,
\sigma^{2}
, and
L
satisfying the above conditions. Additionally,
the characteristic triplet

(\gamma,\sigma^{2},L)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
as for infinitely divisible laws (it can be concluded from the assertion from
[
12
]
, p. 80). It is also clear that for any rational-infinitely divisible
F
its characteristic function
f
has no zeroes on the real line, i.e.
f(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
and its multivariate analog are actively studied now (see
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
, and
[
26
]
) and they find some interesting applications in probability limit and compactness theorems (see Sections 4 and 8 in
[
26
]
, Section 3 in
[
1
]
, the paper
[
19
]
, and also
[
3
]
,
[
17
]
), and in other areas (see, for instance,
[
10
]
,
[
29
]
, and
[
30
]
). But, actually, non-degenerate representatives of
\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
appeared even earlier in the theory of decompositions of probability laws as components of some infinitely divisible distribution functions (see
[
12
]
p. 81–83 and
[
27
]
p. 165).

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is seen to be rather wide. For instance, it contains the distribution function of every probability law, which has a mass
>1/2
at some point. Hence the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
contains non-degenerate distribution functions of some probability laws with bounded supports (see examples in
[
26
]
), which are “far” from the infinite divisibility property in a known sense (see
[
5
]
). So it is rather interesting and important to obtain criteria for belonging to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. The existing results in this direction usually have quite simple and nice formulations in terms of characteristic functions. The first quite general result of such type was obtained by Lindner, Pan, and Sato in
[
26
]
(see Theorem 8.1, p. 30). It states that a lattice distribution function
F
belongs to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if its characteristic function
f
does not have zeroes on the real line, i.e.
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In the articles
[
1
]
and
[
18
]
, this result was generalized to the class of arbitarary discrete probability laws. Namely, a discrete distribution function
F
belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if its characteristic function
f
is separeted from zero, i.e.
|f(t)|\geqslant\mu
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for some constant
\mu>0
. Moreover, in that case, the components of the characteristic triplet are fully described. This result is a generalization of the previous one for discrete lattice distributions, because the absolute value
|f(\,\cdot\,)|
of the characteristic function
f
of a discrete lattice distribution is a periodic continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore such
f
is zero-free on the period segment (and hence on
\mathbb{R}
) if any only if it is separated from zero.

In the paper
[
6
]
, Berger considered mixtures of a degenerate law (with a non-zero coefficient) and absolutely continuous distributions. According to his result, a distribution function
F
of such type belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the result describes the structure of the components of the characteristic triplet in that case. The author also formulated more general criterion for the case, when the degenerate law from the previous one is replaced by a discrete lattice distribution with characteristic function, which has no zeroes on the real line. At present, however, the most general criterion (among those that use assumptions about the type of distribution) is the following result for the mixtures of discrete and absolutely continuous probability laws, which was obtained by Berger and Kutlu in the paper
[
7
]
. Let us formulate it with more details here. Namely, assume that
F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+c_{a}F_{a}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
F_{d}
is a discrete distribution function,
F_{a}
is an absolutely continuous distribution function,
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
, and
c_{d}+c_{a}=1
. We write the characteristic function
f
in the corresponding form:
f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f(t)\neq 0
and
|f_{d}(t)|\geqslant\mu
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
with some constant
\mu>0
. It is equivalent to the condition
|f(t)|\geqslant\mu^{\prime}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for some constant
\mu^{\prime}>0
. Moreover, Berger and Kutlu showed the existence of some discrete part in the spectral function and they fully described its absolutely continuous part for this case. It should be noted that we are not aware any similar results for pure absolutely continuous distribution functions
F
. However, for some cases the problem of membership to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
for a given distribution function of such type is not difficult to solve by the general criteria proposed in
[
20
]
with some additional analysis.

This article is devoted to generalization and complementation all the mentioned results (except
[
20
]
) for the case, when
F
may have a continuous singular part. Namely, we propose a criterion of belonging a distribution function
F
to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
under the assumption that its continuous singular part is dominated by its discrete part in a certain sense. In fact, we show that the conditions on
f
from the results
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
are carried over to this case. In addition, we describe the characteristic triplet of the corresponding Lévy-type representation, which may contain some continuous singular part.
We next show that the assumption of the dominated continuous singular part cannot be simply omitted or even slightly extended without some additional assumptions. In addition, for any
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
we solve the decomposition problem, which was stated by Lindner, Pan, Sato in
[
26
]
(see Open Question 8.4), within the considered case. Here we obtain a positive solution generalizing similar rusults from
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
.

The article has the following structure. Section 2 contains necessary preliminaries, more detailed statements of some existing results mentioned above and the formulations of the new results of the paper. In Section 3, we formulate some important known theorems and useful lemmata, which will be auxiliary tools needed for the proofs of our results. In Section 4, we first prove a key auxiliary lemma and we next propose the proofs of the main results of the article.

Let

\displaystyle F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+c_{a}F_{a}(x)+c_{s}F_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

,

We will consider only the case, when

\displaystyle F_{d}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\\

\displaystyle f_{d}(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{k}e^{itx_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}:=\{x_{k}:p_{k}>0,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}.

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
,
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
v
is a real-valued function from
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

It should be noted that, in addition to this theorem, the paper
[
7
]
contains a number of interesting assertions concerning decomposition of
F
and characterization of the existence of the
H
–moment of
F
for certain positive functions
H
. Additionally, there is a rather general theorem similar to Theorem
2
for the function
f
, but without the assumption that it is a characteristic function of some probability law (Theorem 2.1 in
[
7
]
).

Theorem
2
generalizes above mentioned (in introduction) results by Berger from
[
6
]
(Theorems 4.5 and 4.12), where
F_{d}
was assumed to be discrete lattice. It is also seen that, in fact, Theorem
2
is a strengthening of Theorem
1
. However, we note that
\gamma_{0}
and the discrete part in (
7
) are described in a greater detail than in formula (
8
).

We now propose a generalization of the previous criteria for the case, when
F
may have a continuous singular part, i.e.
c_{s}\geqslant 0
in (
3
). The following theorem is the main result of this article.

For convenience, we preliminarily select the following property of distributions. Let
\mu_{d}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|
. We say that a distribution function
F
has
the dominated continuous singular part
if

c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}
for the case
\mu_{d}>0
and if
c_{s}=0
for the case
\mu_{d}=0
.

Theorem 3

Suppose that
F
has decomposition (
3
) with some
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
,
c_{s}\geqslant 0
, and
F
has the dominated continuous singular part. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
,

(ii)

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
,

(iii)

f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
.

If one of the conditions is satisfied, and hence all, then
f
admits the following representation

f(t)=\exp\Biggl\{it\gamma_{0}+\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\bigr(e^{itu}-1\bigr)+\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}(e^{itx}-1)\biggl(v_{a}(x)+\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,\dfrac{\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cdot e^{-|x|}}{|x|}\biggr)dx\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}(e^{itx}-1)\,dW(x)\Biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

Here
\gamma_{0}\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
,
\lambda_{u}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
. Next, the function
v_{a}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v_{a}(x)|dx<\infty
, and, in the case
c_{a}=0
,
v_{a}
is identically
0
; the constant
\mathfrak{m}_{a}\in\mathbb{Z}
is defined by the formula

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{a}:=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl(\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{Arg}\,R_{a}(t)-\lim_{t\to-\infty}\mathrm{Arg}\,R_{a}(t)\Bigr),

(10)

with

\displaystyle R_{a}(t):=1+\dfrac{c_{a}f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where, in particular,
\mathfrak{m}_{a}=0
for the case
c_{a}=0
. Next, the function
W:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
belongs to
\boldsymbol{V}
and it is always continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. If
c_{s}=0
then
W
is identically
0
. If
c_{s}\neq 0
then
W
is not absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e. it always contains some continuous singular part. In addition, if all the functions
F_{s}^{*k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, are continuous singular, then the function
W
is
(
pure
)
continuous singular.

It is important to note that the discrete part in the exponent in (
9
) depends only on discrete part of
f
, i.e. on
f_{d}
. More precisely, we have the following remark, which will be seen from the proof of Theorem
3
.

Remark 1

In representation (
9
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{d}(t)=it\gamma_{0}+\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\bigr(e^{itu}-1\bigr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It is easily seen that if we set
c_{a}:=0
and
c_{s}:=0
in the assumptions of this theorem, then we come to Theorem
1
. If we set only
c_{s}:=0
, then we conclude the statement of Theorem
2
, but with a full discription of the discrete part in (
8
). In the unexplored case
c_{s}>0
, the new function
W
appears and it always has some continuous singular part.

Using Theorem
3
, it is easy to construct a lot of particular examples of
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
with non-zero continuous singular parts.

Example 1.
Suppose that

\displaystyle F(x):=c_{d}\mathds{1}_{0}(x)+c_{a}F_{a}(x)+c_{s}F_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

with
c_{d}>c_{s}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
, and
c_{d}+c_{a}+c_{s}=1
. Let
F_{s}
be an arbitrary continuous singular function, but
F_{a}
be an absolutely continuous distribution function, whose characteristic function
f_{a}
is real and non-negative (for instance,
f_{a}
is a Pólya-type characteristic function or
f_{a}
is corresponded to a symmetric continuous stable distribution). Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Indeed,
F
has the dominated continuous singular part, because
f_{d}
is identically
1
here, i.e.
\mu_{d}=1
, and hence
c_{d}\mu_{d}=c_{d}>c_{s}
. Let us check
(ii)
from Theorem
3
. For this, we consider the characteristic function of
F

\displaystyle f(t)=c_{d}+c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Under the assumptions, we observe that

\displaystyle|f(t)|\geqslant|c_{d}+c_{a}f_{a}(t)|-c_{s}|f_{s}(t)|=c_{d}+c_{a}f_{a}(t)-c_{s}|f_{s}(t)|,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
f_{a}(t)\geqslant 0
and
|f_{s}(t)|\leqslant 1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
|f(t)|\geqslant c_{d}-c_{s}>0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
(ii)
holds. Thus
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
by Theorem
3
.
\Box

In general case, of course, checking
(ii)
or
(iii)
of Theorem
3
may require more subtle analisys. We illustrate it by the following rather special example, which is also interesting, because there is a (pure) continuous singular function
W
from (
9
).

Example 2.
Let us consider the mixture of the degenerate law, the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and the classical Cantor distribution. Namely, we set

\displaystyle F(x):=c_{d}\mathds{1}_{0}(x)+c_{a}U(x)+c_{s}S(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
U
is an absolutely continuous distribution function with the density
\mathbb{I}_{[0,1]}
, and
S
is the cumulative function of the classical Cantor distribution supported on the Cantor set
\mathcal{C}\subset[0,1]
;
c_{d}>c_{s}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
, and
c_{d}+c_{a}+c_{s}=1
.

We first observe that
f_{d}
is identically
1
here and, similarly as in Example 1,
F
has the dominated continuous singular part. We next check condition
(iii)
of Theorem
3
. Since
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=1
, it remains to show that
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
f
is expressed by the formula

\displaystyle f(t)=c_{d}+c_{a}\,\dfrac{e^{it}-1}{it}+c_{s}e^{it/2}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos(t/3^{k}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We set

\displaystyle\hat{f}(t):=f(t)e^{-it/2}=c_{d}e^{-it/2}+c_{a}\,\dfrac{\sin(t/2)}{t/2}+c_{s}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos(t/3^{k}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The functions
f
and
\hat{f}
have the same set of zeroes on
\mathbb{R}
. Observe that it contains in the set of zeroes of the function
\mathrm{Im}\{\hat{f}(t)\}=-c_{d}\sin(t/2)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The last set is exactly
\{2\pi m:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Therefore it is sufficient to show that
f(2\pi m)\neq 0
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Obviously, we can exclude
m=0
. For any
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
we write

\displaystyle|f(2\pi m)|=|\hat{f}(2\pi m)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl|c_{d}\cos(\pi m)+c_{a}\,\dfrac{\sin(\pi m)}{\pi m}+c_{s}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi m/3^{k})\biggr|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl|c_{d}(-1)^{m}+c_{s}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi m/3^{k})\biggr|.

It follows that

\displaystyle|f(2\pi m)|\geqslant c_{d}-c_{s}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl|\cos(2\pi m/3^{k})\bigr|\geqslant c_{d}-c_{s}>0.

Thus
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and condition
(iii)
of Theorem
3
is satisfied. So, by this theorem,
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Let us consider representation (
9
) for
f
. Since
f_{d}(t)=1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the sum
it\gamma_{0}+\sum_{u}\lambda_{u}\bigr(e^{itu}-1\bigr)
is identically
0
according to Remark
1
. There are the function
v_{a}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and the constant
\mathfrak{m}_{a}\in\mathbb{Z}
, but we will not determine these here. Next, we turn to the function
W
. It is known that all convolution powers
S^{*n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of the Cantor distribution function
S
are continuous singular. We recall that this fact follows from the famous Jessen–Wintner theorem (see
[
16
]
, Theorem 35) and the representation of
S(x+\tfrac{1}{2})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, as the infinite symmetric Bernoulli convolution, but it was explicitly shown in
[
36
]
(see p. 520). According to Theorem
3
, it implies that
W
is pure continuous singular. Moreover, it will be seen from the proof of Theorem
3
that
W
is the limit of the sums

\displaystyle W_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}S^{*k}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

as
n\to\infty
with respect to convergence in total variation on
\mathbb{R}
.
\Box

So Theorem
3
yields the sufficient condition for
W
to be (pure) continuous singular. Is it true that
W
is actually always (pure) continuous singular function in the case
c_{s}\neq 0
?

Proposition 1

Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3
with
c_{s}>0
, and the characteristic function
f
is represented by (
9
) with some
W
. Suppose that there exists an integer
n_{a}\geqslant 2
such that the function
F_{s}^{*(n_{a}-1)}
is
(
pure
)
continuous singular, but the function
F_{s}^{*n_{a}}
is not, i.e.
F_{s}^{*n_{a}}(x)=\alpha H_{a}(x)+(1-\alpha)H_{s}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\alpha
is a number from
(0,1]
,
H_{a}
is an absolutely continuous distribution function,
H_{s}
is a continuous singular distribution function. If
\alpha\geqslant\tfrac{n_{a}}{n_{a}+1}\cdot\tfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}
then the function
W
is not
(
pure
)
continuous singular. In particular, it is always true for the case
\alpha=1
.

It should be recalled here that there exist examples of continuous singular distribution functions (say of
F_{s}
), whose convolution squares (say
F_{s}^{*2}
) are absolutely continuous (see
[
36
]
and also
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
). Therefore we answer the question asked before Proposition
1
in the negative.

The following remark yields the characteristic triplet for
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
in the explicit form under the assumptions of Theorem
3
.

Remark 2

Representation (
9
) can be written in the following forms:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma_{0}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}(e^{itx}-1)\,dL(x)\biggr\}=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\,dL(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle\gamma:=\gamma_{0}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\sin(x)\,dL(x)

and
L(x):=L_{d}(x)+L_{a}(x)+L_{s}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
with

\displaystyle L_{d}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}:\\
u\leqslant x\end{subarray}}\lambda_{u},&x<0,\\
-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}:\\
u>x\end{subarray}}\lambda_{u},&x>0,\end{cases}

(11)

\displaystyle L_{a}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\int\limits_{u\leqslant x}\Bigl(v_{a}(u)+\mathrm{sgn}(u)\,\tfrac{\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cdot e^{-|u|}}{|u|}\Bigr)du,&x<0,\\
-\int\limits_{u>x}\Bigl(v_{a}(u)+\mathrm{sgn}(u)\,\tfrac{\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cdot e^{-|u|}}{|u|}\Bigr)du,&x>0,\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle L_{s}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}W(x),&x<0,\\
W(x)-W(+\infty),&x>0.\end{cases}

(13)

It is seen that
L
satisfies all admissible conditions for a spectral function in the Lévy type representation. Thus, under the assumptions of Theorem
3
,
(\gamma,0,L)
is the characteristic triplet for
F
satisfying one of the conditions
(i)
–
(iii)
. On the other hand, if we know that (
9
) represents the characteristic function of some probability law, then its distribution function
F
is quasi-infinitely divisible by the definition and hence
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

The following remark shows that the property of the dominated singular part is not necessary condition for belonging to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Remark 3

Let
F
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
3
with
c_{s}>0
. Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Then
F^{*n}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but
F^{*n}
don’t have the dominated singular parts for all sufficiently large
n
.

Indeed, it is seen from the definition of the rational-infinite divisibility that the convolution of two any distribution functions from
\boldsymbol{Q}
belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
. Therefore
F^{*n}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Next, we consider the characteristic function
f
of
F
. It admits decomposition (
4
) with
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
,
c_{s}>0
, and
c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}
, where
\mu_{d}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|
as before. Then every
F^{*n}
has the following characteristic function:

\displaystyle f(t)^{n}=\bigl(c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)\bigr)^{n},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

From this it is easily seen that
t\mapsto c_{d}^{n}f_{d}(t)^{n}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is the Fourier–Stieltjes transform of the discrete part of
F^{*n}
. Hence
c_{d}^{n}
is the weight of this part and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)^{n}|=\mu_{d}^{n}
. Next, there are
n
terms
c_{s}c_{d}^{n-1}f_{s}(t)f_{d}(t)^{n-1}
, which are included to the Fourier–Stieltjes transform of the continuous singular part of
F^{*n}
. This is due to the well known fact that a convolution of continuous singular function with a discrete function is continuous singular (see
[
28
]
p. 190 or
[
34
]
p. 319). So the weight of the continuous singular part of
F^{*n}
is not less than
nc_{s}c_{d}^{n-1}
. For any integer
n\geqslant c_{d}/c_{s}
we have that
nc_{s}c_{d}^{n-1}\geqslant c_{d}^{n}\geqslant c_{d}^{n}\mu_{d}^{n}
. Therefore the exact weight of the continuous singular part of
F^{*n}
is not less than
c_{d}^{n}\mu_{d}^{n}
too, i.e. the condition of the dominated singular part doesn’t hold for
F^{*n}
for
n\geqslant c_{d}/c_{s}
.

The following interesting example shows that the condition of the dominated singular part can not be simply omitted and, moreover, it cannot be extended to the case
c_{s}=c_{d}\mu_{d}
with
\mu_{d}>0
without certain additional assumptions.

Example 3.
Let
B
denote the distribution function of the Bernoulli law on the points
\pm 1
with equal probabilities, i.e.
B(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathds{1}_{-1}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathds{1}_{1}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We set

\displaystyle F_{*}(x):=\dfrac{1}{2}\,\mathds{1}_{0}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}\,F_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(14)

where
F_{s}
is the following infinite symmetric Bernoulli convolution:

\displaystyle F_{s}=B_{1}*B_{2}*\ldots*B_{n}*\ldots

with
B_{k}(x):=B(k!\,x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f_{*}
and
f_{s}
denote the characteristic functions of
F_{*}
and
F_{s}
, respectively. Then
f_{*}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,f_{s}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle f_{s}(t)=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos(t/k!),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(15)

It is known (see
[
28
]
pp. 20 and 67) that the function
F_{s}
is continuous singular. Let us consider Lebesgue’s decomposition (
4
) for
f=f_{*}
. We have
c_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
c_{a}=0
, and
c_{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Observe that the component
f_{d}
is identically
1
for
f_{*}
and hence
\mu_{d}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=1
. So
c_{d}\mu_{d}=c_{s}
, i.e. the condition of the dominated continuous singular part doesn’t hold. Next,
f_{*}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, since otherwise
f_{s}(t_{0})=-1
for some
t_{0}\neq 0
and hence
|f_{s}(t_{0})|=1
, which would mean that
F_{s}
is a discrete lattice distribution function (see
[
28
]
Theorem 2.1.4), a contradiction. Thus
F_{*}
satisfies condition
(iii)
of Theorem
3
and the assumption
c_{d}>0
, but
F_{*}
doesn’t have the dominated continuous singular part.

Proposition 2

The function
F_{*}
doesn’t belong to
\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Thus, in general, conditions
(i)
and
(iii)
from Theorem
3
are not equivalent even if
c_{s}=c_{d}\mu_{d}>0
.
\Box

By the way, we recall that distribution functions of discrete laws with a point mass
\tfrac{1}{2}
, which have characteristic functions without zeroes on the real line, don’t always belong to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
too (see
[
22
]
for more details). However, if the distribution is an atom of mass
\tfrac{1}{2}
plus a continuous part, which is not purely singular, then the answer is definite.

Example 4.
Suppose that

\displaystyle F(x):=\dfrac{1}{2}\,\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{0}}(x)+c_{a}F_{a}(x)+c_{s}F_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
F_{a}
is an absolutely continuous distribution function,
F_{s}
is a continuous singular distribution function,
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
c_{a}>0
,
c_{s}\geqslant 0
, and
c_{a}+c_{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Such a function
F
always belongs to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. Let us check it. We consider the characteristic function of
F
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\dfrac{1}{2}\,e^{it\gamma_{0}}+c_{a}f_{a}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Here
c_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
f_{d}(t)=e^{it\gamma_{0}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence
\mu_{d}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=1
and
c_{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}-c_{a}<\tfrac{1}{2}=c_{d}\mu_{d}
, i.e.
F
has the dominated continuous singular part. Next,
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, otherwise
2c_{a}f_{a}(t_{0})+2c_{s}f_{s}(t_{0})=-e^{it_{0}\gamma_{0}}
for some
t_{0}\neq 0
and, in particular,
|2c_{a}f_{a}(t_{0})+2c_{s}f_{s}(t_{0})|=1
. Then the latter implies that the distribution function with the characteristic function
2c_{a}f_{a}(\cdot)+2c_{s}f_{s}(\cdot)
is discrete lattice, but this is actually continuous by the assumptions, a contradiction.

So
F
satisfies condition
(iii)
and other assumptions of Theorems
3
. It follows that
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.
\Box

The next assertion solves the decomposition problem for any distribution function
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem

.

Proposition 3

Let
F
be a distribution function with decomposition (
3
) with some
c_{d}>0
,
c_{a}\geqslant 0
,
c_{s}\geqslant 0
, and the dominated continuous singular part. Suppose that

and

with some distribution functions

and
F_{2}
. Then
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
belong to
\boldsymbol{Q}
.

This proposition generalizes the similar result from
[

]
(see Corollary 2.3 from Theorem 2.2), which solves the problem for

satisfying the assumptions of Theorem

. We note that there is a general decomposition problem for arbitrary

, which was stated by Lindner, Pan, Sato in

]
(see Open Question 8.4):
is it true that if
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and

(

and

are distribution functions on

)
, then

The proof of the main result (Theorem

,

Theorem 4

Let
H

\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\mathbb{C}}
with the following Lebesgue’s decomposition

\displaystyle H(x)=H_{d}(x)+H_{a}(x)+H_{s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
H_{d}
,
H_{a}
,
H_{s}
are correspondingly discrete, absolutely continuous and singular parts of

, which belong to
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\mathbb{C}}
. Let

\displaystyle h(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dH(x),\quad\text{and}\quad h_{d}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dH_{d}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Suppose that
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|h(t)|>0
and
\|H_{s}\|<\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|h_{d}(t)|
. Then there exists a function
K
from
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle|c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)|\geqslant c_{d}|f_{d}(t)|-c_{s}|f_{s}(t)|\geqslant c_{d}\mu_{d}-c_{s}>0,

i.e.

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)|>0.

(25)

Then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
we can write

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{d}(t)\cdot\dfrac{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)+c_{a}f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}\cdot\dfrac{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{f_{d}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{d}(t)\cdot\biggl(1+\dfrac{c_{a}f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}\biggr)\cdot\dfrac{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{f_{d}(t)}.

So it is convenient to represent

\displaystyle f(t)=f_{d}(t)f_{a,ds}(t)f_{s,d}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(26)

where

\displaystyle f_{a,ds}(t):=c_{d}+c_{s}+\dfrac{c_{a}(c_{d}+c_{s})f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)},\qquad f_{s,d}(t):=\dfrac{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{(c_{d}+c_{s})f_{d}(t)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us consider
f_{d}
represented by (
6
). Due to (
24
), by Theorem
1
, it admits the representation

\displaystyle f_{d}(t)=\exp\biggr\{it\gamma_{0}+\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\bigr(e^{itu}-1\bigr)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(27)

with some
\gamma_{0}\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
and
\lambda_{u}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
.

We now consider the function
f_{a,ds}
. If
c_{a}=0
then
f_{a,ds}(t)=c_{d}+c_{s}=1-c_{a}=1
. We next suppose that
c_{a}>0
. The function
t\mapsto c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is separated from
0
according to (
25
) and for its continuous
singular part we have

\displaystyle\|c_{s}f_{s}\|=c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}=c_{d}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|.

By Theorem
4
, there exists a function
I_{ds}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
from
\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\mathbb{C}}
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dI_{ds}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We next observe that

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dF_{a}(x)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dI_{ds}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}d(F_{a}*I_{ds})(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

and we write

\displaystyle\dfrac{c_{a}(c_{d}+c_{s})f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}d\widetilde{F}_{a}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\widetilde{F}_{a}(x):=c_{a}(c_{d}+c_{s})(F_{a}*I_{ds})(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
\widetilde{F}_{a}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\!\mathbb{C}}
inherits the absolute continuity from
F_{a}
, i.e. there exists a function
\widetilde{p}_{a}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widetilde{p}_{a}(x)|dx<\infty
and
\widetilde{F}_{a}(x)=\int_{y\leqslant x}\widetilde{p}_{a}(y)dy
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}d\widetilde{F}_{a}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\widetilde{p}_{a}(x)dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

and we have

\displaystyle f_{a,ds}(t)=c_{d}+c_{s}+\dfrac{c_{a}(c_{d}+c_{s})f_{a}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}=c_{d}+c_{s}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\widetilde{p}_{a}(x)dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
c_{d}+c_{s}\geqslant c_{d}>0
. Since
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and the function
t\mapsto c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is bounded, we conclude that
f_{a,ds}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. By Theorem
5
,
f_{a,ds}
admits the representation

\displaystyle f_{a,ds}(t)=\exp\Biggl\{\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}(e^{itx}-1)\biggl(v_{a}(x)+\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,\dfrac{\mathfrak{m}_{a}\cdot e^{-|x|}}{|x|}\biggr)dx\Biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(28)

where
v_{a}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is a function satisfying the condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v_{a}(x)|dx<\infty
,
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
is a constant defined by the formula

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{a}:=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl(\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{Arg}\,f_{a,ds}(t)-\lim_{t\to-\infty}\mathrm{Arg}\,f_{a,ds}(t)\Bigr).

Since
c_{d}+c_{s}
is positive, formula (
10
) gives an equivalent definition of
\mathfrak{m}_{a}
. By the way, it is easily seen that
\mathfrak{m}_{a}=0
if
c_{a}=0
. In general,
\mathfrak{m}_{a}\in\mathbb{Z}
(see Remark
4
). Therefore, in the case
c_{a}=0
, we set
v_{a}(x):=0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and the formula (
28
) remains valid.

We now turn to the function

\displaystyle f_{s,d}(t)=\dfrac{c_{d}f_{d}(t)+c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{(c_{d}+c_{s})f_{d}(t)}=\biggl(1+\dfrac{c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)}\biggr)\cdot\dfrac{c_{d}}{c_{d}+c_{s}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

From (
25
) we know that
f_{s,d}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and we see that
f_{s,d}(0)=1
. Hence the distinguished logarithm
\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{s,d}
is uniquely defined on
\mathbb{R}
with condition
\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{s,d}(0)=0
. Due to the assumptions,

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)}\biggr|\leqslant\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}\mu_{d}}<1\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(29)

and we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{s,d}(t)=\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)}\biggr)-\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(30)

where
\ln(\cdot)
returns the principal value of the logarithm. Let us consider the first logarithm in the right-hand side. Due to (
29
), the following expansion holds:

\displaystyle\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)}\biggr)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}f_{s}(t)}{c_{d}f_{d}(t)}\biggr)^{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}f_{s}(t)^{k}g_{d}(t)^{k},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(31)

where we set
g_{d}(t):=1/f_{d}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We note that the series uniformly converges on
\mathbb{R}
by the Weierstrass M-test, because, due to (
29
), for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
the absolute values of its terms are majorized by the terms of the following convergent numerical series

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}\mu_{d}}\biggr)^{k}.

Let us consider the function
g_{d}
and show that it is actually a Fourier–Stieltjes transform of some function from class
\boldsymbol{V}
. Indeed, according to (
27
),

\displaystyle g_{d}(t)=e^{-it\gamma_{0}}\cdot e^{-\lambda_{0}}\cdot\exp\Bigl\{-\!\!\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}e^{itu}\Bigr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where we set

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}:=-\!\!\sum_{u\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}.

There is an absolutely convergent Fourier series with real coefficients in the last exponential function. Hence this function itself is expanded into absolutely convergent Fourier series with the coefficients from
\mathbb{R}
and the exponents from
\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
. It remains true after multiplying this series by
e^{-\lambda_{0}}
and
e^{-it\gamma_{0}}
(that leads to
g_{d}
), because
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma_{0}\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
. Thus we have

\displaystyle g_{d}(t)=\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle}q_{y}e^{ity},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(32)

where
q_{y}\in\mathbb{R}
for every
y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
and
\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle}|q_{y}|<\infty
. Obviously, the series (
32
) can be written as a Fourier–Stieltjes transform:

\displaystyle g_{d}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dI_{d}(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with the discrete function
I_{d}\in\boldsymbol{V}
:

\displaystyle I_{d}(x):=\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle:\,y\leqslant x}q_{y},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(33)

Let us return to the formula (
31
). We observe that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
the functions
f_{s}^{k}
,
g_{d}^{k}
,
f_{s}^{k}\cdot g^{k}
are the Fourier–Stieltjes transforms of functions from
\boldsymbol{V}
:

\displaystyle f_{s}(t)^{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dF_{s}^{*k}(x),\quad g_{d}(t)^{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dI_{d}^{*k}(x),\quad f_{s}(t)^{k}g_{d}(t)^{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}d(F_{s}^{*k}*I_{d}^{*k})(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(34)

Consequently, the partial sums of the series (
31
) are the Fourier–Stieltjes transforms for the following functions:

\displaystyle W_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}\bigl(F_{s}^{*k}*I_{d}^{*k}\bigr)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(35)

Let us show that the whole sum of the series (
31
) admits the representation:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}f_{s}(t)^{k}g_{d}(t)^{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dW(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(36)

with some function
W\in\boldsymbol{V}
. According to Lemma
1
, for this it is sufficient that
1)
the partial sums of (
31
) uniformly converge to the infinite sum on every bounded interval and
2)

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|W_{n}\|<\infty
. The uniform convergence of the partial sums (even on whole real line) were showed above, i.e.
1)
holds. Let us prove
2)
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|W_{n}\|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}\bigl\|F_{s}^{*k}*I_{d}^{*k}\bigr\|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}\bigl\|F_{s}^{*k}\bigr\|\cdot\bigl\|I_{d}^{*k}\bigr\|\biggr\}.

Here
\bigl\|F_{s}^{*k}\bigr\|=1
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, because all
F_{s}^{*k}
are distribution functions. Also, it is convenient for us to represent
\|I_{d}^{*k}\|=\|g_{d}^{k}\|
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus we come to the inequalities

\displaystyle\|W_{n}\|\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k}\biggl(\dfrac{c_{s}}{c_{d}}\biggr)^{k}\bigl\|g_{d}^{k}\bigr\|,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(37)

We now estimate
\|g_{d}^{k}\|
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us return to decomposition (
32
) for the function
g_{d}
. The set
\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
is countable, and
\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle}|q_{y}|<\infty
. So we fix arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and choose
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
and the distinct points
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N_{\varepsilon}}\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N_{\varepsilon}}\}}|q_{y}|<\varepsilon.

We introduce the polynomial

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t):=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}e^{ity_{m}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(38)

So we have
\|g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}\|<\varepsilon
. We also observe that

\displaystyle\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}{g_{d}}\biggr\|\leqslant\|g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}\|\cdot\biggl\|\dfrac{1}{g_{d}}\biggr\|=\|g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}\|\cdot\|f_{d}\|=\|g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}\|<\varepsilon.

(39)

Let us fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and write

\displaystyle\|g_{d}^{k}\|=\biggl\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\cdot\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr)^{k}\biggr\|\leqslant\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|\cdot\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr\|^{k}.

In order to get a convenient representation for
g_{d}/\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}
, we observe that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle 1-\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\biggr)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl(1-\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\biggr)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\biggr)^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\biggr)^{j},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

So we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{g_{d}(t)}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g(t)}\biggr)^{j}+\biggl(\dfrac{g_{d}(t)-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{g_{d}(t)}\biggr)^{n}\cdot\dfrac{g_{d}(t)}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, due to (
39
), we get

\displaystyle\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr\|\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}{g_{d}}\biggr\|^{j}+\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}-\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}{g_{d}}\biggr\|^{n}\cdot\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr\|<\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon^{j}+\varepsilon^{n}\cdot\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr\|,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, letting
n\to\infty
yields

\displaystyle\biggl\|\dfrac{g_{d}}{\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}}\biggr\|\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{j}=\dfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\|g_{d}^{k}\|\leqslant\dfrac{\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|}{(1-\varepsilon)^{k}}.

(40)

So the estimation of
\|I_{d}^{*k}\|=\|g_{d}^{k}\|
is reduced to finding an upper bound for
\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|
.

Let us consider
\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}
defined by formula (
38
). Let
Y_{\varepsilon}:=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{N_{\varepsilon}}\}
. Suppose that
Y_{\varepsilon}=\{0\}
, i.e.
\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|q_{y}|<\varepsilon
and
\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)=q_{0}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|=|q_{0}|^{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that

\displaystyle|q_{0}|=|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(0)|\leqslant|g_{d}(0)|+|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(0)-g_{d}(0)|,

where
|g_{d}(0)|=|1/f_{d}(0)|=1
and

\displaystyle|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(0)-g_{d}(0)|=\biggl|\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}q_{y}\biggr|\leqslant\sum_{y\in\langle\mathcal{X}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|q_{y}|<\varepsilon.

Thus
|q_{0}|<1+\varepsilon
and hence
\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|<(1+\varepsilon)^{k}
.

We next assume that
Y_{\varepsilon}\neq\{0\}
, i.e.
Y_{\varepsilon}
contains some non-zero elements. So we select a basis over
\mathbb{Q}
in the set
Y_{\varepsilon}
(see
[
24
]
p. 67–68), i.e. we choose non-zero elements
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}\in Y_{\varepsilon}
with some
d\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}\}
, which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and for any
m\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}\}
there exist some
r_{m,1},\ldots,r_{m,d}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
y_{m}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}r_{m,l}\beta_{l}
. The linear independence over
\mathbb{Q}
means that the equality
r_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+r_{d}\beta_{d}=0
holds with
r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}\in\mathbb{Q}
only in the case
r_{1}=r_{2}=\ldots=r_{d}=0
. It is clear that the coefficients
r_{m,l}
of the decomposition of
y_{m}
are uniquely determined. Let
\varkappa
be the minimal positive integer such that
\bar{r}_{m,l}:=\varkappa\cdot r_{m,l}\in\mathbb{Z}
for any admissible
m
and
l
. We set
\bar{\beta}_{l}:=\beta_{l}/\varkappa
for every
l\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Then we have
y_{m}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\bar{r}_{m,l}\bar{\beta}_{l}
for any
m\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}\}
. Here the coefficients
\bar{r}_{m,l}
are uniquely determined by
y_{m}
too. We define the vectors
\bar{r}_{m}:=(\bar{r}_{m,1},\ldots,\bar{r}_{m,d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
m=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}
. Since
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N_{\varepsilon}}
are assumed to be distinct, all
\bar{r}_{m}
are distinct too. Let us introduce the function

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}):=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}\exp\biggl\{i\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d}\bar{r}_{m,l}t_{l}\biggr\},\quad t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}.

It is easily seen that this function is continuous and
2\pi
-periodic over every variable. We will also use the short expression for it:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}e^{i\langle t,\bar{r}_{m}\rangle},\quad t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The functions
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}
are related:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\bar{\beta}_{1}t,\ldots,\bar{\beta}_{d}t\bigr)=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}\exp\biggl\{it\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d}\bar{r}_{m,l}\bar{\beta}_{l}\biggr\}=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}e^{ity_{m}}=\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

i.e.
\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}
is diagonal for the function
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\mapsto\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\bar{\beta}_{1}t_{1},\ldots,\bar{\beta}_{d}t_{d})
. Hence the image of the first function is dense in the image of the second one (see
[
24
]
, Theorem 2.4.1., p. 116) and, consequently, in the image of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, this yields

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}(t)|=\sup_{t\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t)|.

(41)

Let us show that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|=\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|\quad\text{for any}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(42)

We fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and write

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}e^{ity_{m}}\biggr)^{k}=\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\ldots\sum_{m_{k}=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\Bigl(q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}e^{it(y_{m_{1}}+\ldots+y_{m_{k}})}\Bigr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}q_{y_{m}}e^{i\langle t,\bar{r}_{m}\rangle}\biggr)^{k}=\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\ldots\sum_{m_{k}=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\Bigl(q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}e^{i\langle t,\bar{r}_{m_{1}}+\ldots+\bar{r}_{m_{k}}\rangle}\Bigr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We next represent

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}}\Biggl(\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon},\\
\ldots\\
m_{k}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}:\\
y_{m_{1}}+\ldots+y_{m_{k}}=z\end{subarray}}q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}\Biggr)e^{itz},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}}\Biggl(\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon},\\
\ldots\\
m_{k}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}:\\
\bar{r}_{m_{1}}+\ldots+\bar{r}_{m_{k}}=v\end{subarray}}q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}\Biggr)e^{i\langle t,s\rangle},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Bigl\{y_{m_{1}}+\ldots+y_{m_{k}}:m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}\}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\bar{r}_{m_{1}}+\ldots+\bar{r}_{m_{k}}:m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}\}\Bigr\}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{g}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}}\Biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon},\\
\ldots\\
m_{k}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}:\\
y_{m_{1}}+\ldots+y_{m_{k}}=z\end{subarray}}q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}\Biggr|,

(43)

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}}\Biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon},\\
\ldots\\
m_{k}=1,\ldots,N_{\varepsilon}:\\
\bar{r}_{m_{1}}+\ldots+\bar{r}_{m_{k}}=s\end{subarray}}q_{y_{m_{1}}}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{y_{m_{k}}}\Biggr|.

(44)

There is a natural map between two finite sets
\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{R}^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}
: every number
z=\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{m_{j}}
from the first set is paired with a vector
s=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{r}_{m_{j}}
from the second set. Here
s
is the vector of the coefficients of the decomposition for
z
with the basis
\bar{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\beta}_{d}
. This map is actually bijection. Indeed, it is injective, because distinct numbers from
\mathcal{Y}_{\varepsilon}^{(k)}
have the distinct decompositions, i.e. they are paired with the distinct vectors from the set
\mathcal{R}^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}
. The map is surjective, because any vector

, where

\displaystyle M_{n}:=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{2n}\cos(t_{n}/k!)\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n}:=\prod\limits_{k=2n+1}^{\infty}\cos(t_{n}/k!),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\biggl(\dfrac{\pi(2n)!}{(2n)!}\biggr)\cdot\cos\biggl(\dfrac{\pi(2n)!}{(2n-1)!}\biggr)\cdot\cos\biggl(\dfrac{\pi(2n)!}{(2n-2)!}\biggr)\cdot\ldots\cdot\cos\biggl(\dfrac{\pi(2n)!}{1!}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\pi)\cdot\cos(\pi\cdot 2n)\cdot\cos\bigl(\pi\cdot 2n(2n-1)\bigr)\cdot\ldots\cdot\cos\bigl(\pi\cdot 2n(2n-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot 1\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot\ldots\cdot 1=-1.

Next, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every integer
k\geqslant 2n+1
we have

\displaystyle 0<\dfrac{t_{n}}{k!}=\dfrac{\pi(2n)!}{k\cdot(k-1)!}\leqslant\dfrac{\pi}{k}\leqslant\dfrac{\pi}{3},

and, by the well-known inequality
\cos(x)\geqslant 1-x^{2}/2
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we get

\displaystyle\cos(t_{n}/k!)\geqslant 1-\dfrac{(t_{n}/k!)^{2}}{2}\geqslant 1-\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{2k^{2}}\geqslant 1-\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{18}>0.

Therefore, on the one hand, it is clear that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\cos(t_{n}/k!)<1
for any
k\geqslant 2n+1
and hence
R_{n}<1
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle R_{n}\geqslant\prod\limits_{k=2n+1}^{\infty}\biggl(1-\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{2k^{2}}\biggr)=\exp\biggl\{\sum_{k=2n+1}^{\infty}\ln\biggl(1-\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{2k^{2}}\biggr)\biggr\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where the sum in the exponent tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. So we conclude that
R_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Thus

\displaystyle f_{s}(t_{n})=M_{n}\cdot R_{n}=-R_{n}\to-1\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

and we know that
f_{s}(t_{n})>-1
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We next observe that

\displaystyle f_{s}(t_{n}\pm\pi)=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\cos\biggl(\dfrac{t_{n}\pm\pi}{k!}\biggr)=0.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\cos\biggl(\dfrac{t_{n}\pm\pi}{k!}\biggr)\bigg|_{k=2}=\cos\biggl(\dfrac{\pi(2n)!\pm\pi}{2!}\biggr)=\cos\biggl(\pi\cdot 3\cdot\ldots\cdot(2n)\pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}\biggr)=0.

We now return to the characteristic function
f_{*}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,f_{s}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and we consider the quantities

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{*}(t_{n}-\pi)f_{*}(t_{n}+\pi)}{f_{*}(t_{n})^{2}}=\dfrac{\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,f_{s}(t_{n}-\pi)\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,f_{s}(t_{n}+\pi)\bigr)}{\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,f_{s}(t_{n})\bigr)^{2}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

By the above,

\displaystyle\dfrac{f_{*}(t_{n}-\pi)f_{*}(t_{n}+\pi)}{f_{*}(t_{n})^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\bigl(1+f_{s}(t_{n})\bigr)^{2}}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Therefore, by Theorem
7
,
f_{*}
can not be characteristic function for a distribution function from the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
. Thus
F_{*}\notin\boldsymbol{Q}
.
\Box

Proof of Proposition
3
.
We have decompositions (
3
) and (
4
) for the distribution function
F
and its characteristic function
f
, respectively. Since
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
, we know that
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu_{d}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
according to Theorem
9
. Due to the assumption of the dominated singular part and
c_{d}>0
, we have
c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}
.

Let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
denote correspondingly the characteristic functions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Then the assumed decomposition
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
means that
f(t)=f_{1}(t)f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence we immediately note that
f_{1}(t)\neq 0
and
f_{2}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us write the Lebesgue decompositions for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
:

\displaystyle F_{j}(x)=c_{j,d}F_{j,d}(x)+c_{j,a}F_{j,a}(x)+c_{j,s}F_{j,s}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

We also write the corresponding decompositions for
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
:

\displaystyle f_{j}(t)=c_{j,d}f_{j,d}(t)+c_{j,a}f_{j,a}(t)+c_{j,s}f_{j,s}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

Here
c_{j,d}
,
c_{j,a}
,
c_{j,s}
are non-negative and
c_{j,d}+c_{j,a}+c_{j,s}=1
for
j=1
and
j=2
.
For clarity, we write the equality
F=F_{1}*F_{2}
in the expanded form:

\displaystyle c_{d}F_{d}+c_{a}F_{a}+c_{s}F_{s}=\bigl(c_{1,d}F_{1,d}+c_{1,a}F_{1,a}+c_{1,s}F_{1,s}\bigr)*\bigl(c_{2,d}F_{2,d}+c_{2,a}F_{2,a}+c_{2,s}F_{2,s}\bigr).

(53)

Since
F
has non-zero discrete part (
c_{d}>0
), the functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have non-zero discrete parts too, i.e.
c_{1,d}>0
and
c_{2,d}>0
. Since a convolution of any two distribution functions is discrete if and only if these functions are discrete, we conclude that
c_{d}F_{d}(x)=c_{1,d}c_{2,d}(F_{1,d}*F_{2,d})(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
c_{d}=c_{1,d}c_{2,d}
and
F_{d}=F_{1,d}*F_{2,d}
. Thus we have
f_{d}(t)=f_{1,d}(t)f_{2,d}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
|f_{1,d}(t)|\leqslant 1
and
|f_{2,d}(t)|\leqslant 1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mu_{1,d}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{1,d}(t)|\geqslant\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=\mu_{d}>0,

and, analogously,

\displaystyle\mu_{2,d}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{2,d}(t)|\geqslant\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|=\mu_{d}>0.

We next observe that
F_{1,s}*F_{2,d}
and
F_{2,s}*F_{1,d}
are continuous singular. Therefore the corresponding summands from (
53
) are included in the continuous part of
F
, i.e.
c_{s}F_{s}(x)\geqslant c_{1,s}c_{2,d}(F_{1,s}*F_{2,d})(x)+c_{2,s}c_{1,d}(F_{2,s}*F_{1,d})(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle c_{s}=c_{s}F_{s}(\infty)\geqslant c_{1,s}c_{2,d}(F_{1,s}*F_{2,d})(\infty)+c_{2,s}c_{1,d}(F_{2,s}*F_{1,d})(\infty)=c_{1,s}c_{2,d}+c_{2,s}c_{1,d}.

Then we get

\displaystyle c_{1,s}c_{2,d}\leqslant c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}=c_{1,d}c_{2,d}\mu_{d}\leqslant c_{1,d}c_{2,d}\mu_{1,d},

i.e.
c_{1,s}<c_{1,d}\mu_{1,d}
. Analogously,

\displaystyle c_{2,s}c_{1,d}\leqslant c_{s}<c_{d}\mu_{d}=c_{1,d}c_{2,d}\mu_{d}\leqslant c_{1,d}c_{2,d}\mu_{2,d},

i.e.
c_{2,s}<c_{2,d}\mu_{2,d}
. Thus
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have the dominated continuous singular parts.

So we have showed that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
3
and the condition
(iii)
from it. Thus, according to this theorem,
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
belong to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
.
\Box
