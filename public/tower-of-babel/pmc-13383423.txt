Drug-induced psoriasis following sacubitril/valsartan: a case report and literature review
Authors: Tang Tang, Mu Guo, Huimin Yu, Yun Zhang, Shufang Lei, Siqi Li, Wei Chen, Yuefen Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug exposure is one of the potential mechanisms implicated in the onset or exacerbation of psoriasis. To date, several cardiovascular agents have been implicated. Sacubitril/valsartan (SV) has been widely used in the treatment of hypertension and heart failure (HF). Its common adverse reactions include angioedema, hypotension, renal impairment, and hyperkalemia. However, SV may cause psoriasis, which needs clinical attention.Case presentationThis report describes a case of a middle-aged male patient who developed psoriasis following SV therapy. The rashes presented as well-demarcated red or violaceous plaques with noticeable scaling. After being diagnosed with psoriasis, he was started on topical clobetasol propionate ointment, and SV was discontinued and replaced with ramipril for blood pressure control. During follow-up, the skin lesions showed marked improvement. However, the patient developed similar cutaneous manifestations again when SV was re-administered. He had previously developed psoriasis while using nifedipine, but transitioned to olmesartan and subsequently improved significantly with topical medication. Sacubitril enhances the effect of natriuretic peptides, which may be accompanied by a compensatory increase in angiotensin Ⅱ (Ang Ⅱ) levels, possibly contributing to the psoriasis.ConclusionTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case worldwide of psoriasis potentially associated with SV, and the underlying mechanism appears likely to be related to sacubitril. It serves as a caution for clinicians prescribing SV, particularly in patients with a history of psoriasis or positivity for the HLA-Cw*0602 allele. Further studies are warranted to confirm the mechanism by which sacubitril induces psoriasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06004-3.

Background

SV is a novel therapeutic agent for HF management. As an angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor, its primary active components consist of sacubitril and valsartan. Sacubitril functions by inhibiting neprilysin activity to induce vasodilation. Valsartan as the angiotensin Ⅱ receptor blockers (ARBs) effectively modulates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [1]. Thereby SV exhibit dual neurohormonal antagonism and has become the first-line medication for HF therapy [2].

Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing, inflammatory and systemic disease mediated by the immune system, triggered by the interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Its pathogenesis involves a cycle of skin barrier dysfunction and inflammatory cytokines, genetic susceptibility, the cutaneous microenvironment, and immune drift following biologic therapy [3–6]. Additionally, factors such as infections, physical trauma, psychological stress, and exposure to certain medications may also contribute to the onset or exacerbation of psoriasis [4, 7]. This article reports a case in which psoriasiform drug eruption was triggered by SV, and explores the potential mechanisms.

Case presentation

A man in his 40s presented with a one-month history of exertional dyspnea, a two-week history of cough and fever, and a two-day history of hemoptysis. Additionally, the patient also presented with bilateral lower extremity edema and required a sitting position at night to maintain adequate respiration. He is 172 cm tall with a baseline weight of approximately 80 kg and a body mass index (BMI) of 27.0 kg/m2. Due to severe edema, his weight on admission was 95 kg. He was admitted to a hospital with diagnoses of acute pulmonary thromboembolism and HF. Comprehensive investigations were performed after admission. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated multiple emboli in the bilateral lower pulmonary arteries and their branches, with partial occlusion of a left lower lobar pulmonary artery branch. The patient initially underwent enoxaparin sodium for anticoagulation, clopidogrel bisulfate for antiplatelet therapy, spironolactone and furosemide for diuresis, metoprolol for heart rate control, and sodium ilaprazole for gastric protection. Laboratory tests indicated elevated infection markers. Chest CT revealed multiple bilateral pulmonary exudates and consolidation. Based on the diagnosis of severe pneumonia, meropenem and moxifloxacin were added for anti-infective treatment. Subsequently, transthoracic echocardiography showed a persistently decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to 36% and the presence of an intraventricular mass. SV was added for the treatment of HF. Given the possible of endocarditis, the anti-infective treatment was adjusted to meropenem and vancomycin. Following treatment, laboratory tests indicated a gradual decline in infection markers. However, he continued to experience low-grade fever and dyspnea, and the edema progressively worsened. Consequently, he was transferred to a specialty department for further diagnostic evaluation.

After the transfer, the medical therapy was adjusted as follows: enoxaparin sodium injection (8,000 IU q12h) for anticoagulation; clopidogrel bisulfate (75 mg qd) for antiplatelet therapy; spironolactone (20 mg qd) combined with torasemide injection (20 mg qd) for diuresis; metoprolol succinate extended-release (23.75 mg qd) to control heart rate; sodium ilaprazole for injection (10 mg qd) for gastric protection; meropenem for injection (1 g q8h) plus vancomycin hydrochloride for injection (1 g q12h) for antimicrobial coverage; SV (100 mg bid) for HF management; atorvastatin calcium (20 mg qn) for lipid-lowering; and correction of electrolyte imbalances. Dosages were titrated according to the patient’s clinical status. Two days later, thoracentesis and drainage were performed. Genetic testing of the drained fluid indicated the infections with Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus. Based on this, meropenem and vancomycin were discontinued. Considering the possibility of latent bacterial infection, moxifloxacin injection 0.4 g qd was used. Both blood and sputum cultures have yielded negative results since admission. Repeat cultures also remained negative and moxifloxacin was discontinued. Methylprednisolone tablets were added for step-down therapy. Then, the patient experienced a sudden acute cerebral infarction. Edaravone and Xueshuantong injection were subsequently added to the treatment. Edaravone is an antioxidant and free radical scavenger, which can protect neuronal cells and enhance their tolerance to ischemia and hypoxia [8]. Xueshuantong injection is a traditional Chinese medicine that can lead to better improvement in neurological function deficit [9, 10]. An endomyocardial biopsy was performed to establish a definitive diagnosis, and the results suggested probable viral myocarditis. Furthermore, a repeat echocardiography was performed. The results revealed hypokinesis of left ventricular wall, a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF 37%), and a left ventricular apical thrombus. Therefore, digoxin, dapagliflozin, ivabradine, and vericiguat were sequentially added to the treatment for HF management. The treatment regimen was adjusted based on the laboratory and imaging findings of the patient several times. During hospitalization, laboratory tests showed a glycated hemoglobin level of 6.0%, with a maximum fasting blood glucose of 5.5 mmol/L. The patient was placed on a regular diet without any special restrictions. When he was discharged, a follow-up examination suggested that the ventricular thrombus had resolved and cardiac function showed significant improvement compared to prior assessments. The discharge medication regimen consisted of warfarin 2.5 mg qd, spironolactone 20 mg qd, metoprolol succinate extended-release 47.5 mg qd, rabeprazole 20 mg qd, SV 100 mg bid, atorvastatin sodium 20 mg qn, methylprednisolone 20 mg qd (already being tapered gradually), digoxin 0.125 mg qd, dapagliflozin 10 mg qd, ivabradine 5 mg bid, and vericiguat 2.5 mg qd. Following discharge, the patient attended regular clinical follow-ups. His weight remained stable at around 75 kg, corresponding to a BMI of approximately 25.4 kg/m². Dietitian of the multidisciplinary team recommended a low-salt and low-fat diet to him. Cardiac rehabilitation therapist suggested walking approximately 1200 m in 30 min, once daily, at least three times a week. He discontinued SV on his own two weeks after discharge due to low blood pressure (107–118/63–74 mmHg), and methylprednisolone was also discontinued. One month later, digoxin and rabeprazole were discontinued, and the dosage of vericiguat was adjusted to 5 mg qd based on the patient’s condition. At that time, the patient switched to cycling for 30 min daily on his own. He reported high blood pressure (137/74 mmHg) three months after discharge. SV was resumed at a dose of 150 mg bid due to the requirements of HF and blood pressure management. The treatment regimen was adjusted to the following: warfarin 2.5 mg qd, spironolactone 20 mg qd, metoprolol succinate extended-release 47.5 mg qd, SV 150 mg bid, atorvastatin calcium 20 mg qn, dapagliflozin 10 mg qd, ivabradine 5 mg bid, and vericiguat 5 mg qd. Two months later, the patient gradually developed scattered erythematous rashes over the body, predominantly on the trunk and limbs, which progressively worsened. The rashes presented as well-demarcated red or violaceous plaques with noticeable scaling (Fig. 1a). The patient sought medical attention at an external hospital. Although he refused the imaging and skin pathological examination, the physical examination revealed the thin film phenomenon with a positive Auspitz’s sign. Then, a diagnosis of psoriasis was made, and topical clobetasol propionate ointment was prescribed. However, there was no significant clinical improvement. At that time, the patient attended a HF management follow-up visit. Multidisciplinary team considered that the psoriasis might be associated with SV and discontinued this drug. Meanwhile, transthoracic echocardiography indicated that the left ventricular thrombus had resolved, so warfarin was discontinued. Blood pressure control therapy was switched to ramipril 5 mg qd, while continuing topical ointment therapy for psoriasis. During a follow-up approximately three months later, spironolactone and ivabradine were discontinued based on the patient’s heart rate and examination results. All other treatment remained unchanged. The psoriasis had markedly improved after half a year of ointment therapy (Fig. 1b). However, during a subsequent visit to another hospital, the physician likely considered the established therapeutic benefits of SV in HF, discontinued ramipril, and switched the patient to SV 100 mg bid. Approximately one months after initiating this regimen, the patient developed recurrent skin changes (Fig. 1c). During a telephone follow-up, we learned of this, reinstated the previous treatment, and advised him to avoid SV lifelong. The therapeutic drugs administered to this patient during the clinical follow-ups were showed in Additional file 2.

The patient had a history of hypertension only, and denied a history of diabetes mellitus or other diseases. Two years ago, he started taking nifedipine sustained-release tablets for hypertension. Circular red patches accompanied by white scales gradually appeared all over his body after about 3 months. Then, he sought medical attention at a hospital, where psoriasis was diagnosed and considered a potential adverse reaction related to nifedipine. Therefore, nifedipine was stopped and replaced with olmesartan, alongside topical ointment treatment (specific details unknown). Gradual improvement was observed after two months therapy. Later, the patient self-discontinued both olmesartan and the ointment. Afterwards, the patient had not taken any medication prior to this admission.

Discussion and Conclusions

Psoriasis is characterized by scaly erythematous plaques, which may be localized or widespread. Genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, and environmental factors play a critical role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis [4, 11]. The HLA-Cw*0602 allele is strongly associated with psoriasis, and the presence of this allele appears to be a risk factor for psoriasis [4]. There have been reports that medications including lithium salts, β-blockers, antimalarial drugs, interferons, calcium channel antagonists (CCBs), and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as programmed cell death protein 1 inhibitors can induce psoriasiform rashes [12, 13]. However, the mechanisms by which drugs induce psoriasis are mostly not yet fully clear.

Patient reported in this article had previously taken nifedipine sustained-release tablets, which induced psoriasis. The patient was not concurrently taking any other medications. Naranjo’s algorithm is one of the most widely used methods for assessing the causality of adverse drug reactions. By assigning scores to individual items, it minimizes intra- and inter-observer variability. Furthermore, Naranjo’s algorithm generally has good reproducibility [14, 15]. Therefore, the Naranjo scale was used to evaluate the causality of the adverse drug reaction in this patient. The causality between the suspected drug nifedipine and the adverse drug reaction was evaluated (Table 1) [16]. A score of 5 was obtained, indicating a probable association. Nifedipine belongs to CCBs of antihypertensive drugs, and the mechanism by which it induces psoriasis has not been fully elucidated. A calcium ion concentration gradient exists in the epidermal tissue, which plays a crucial regulatory role in the differentiation and proliferation of keratinocytes and inflammatory processes. CCB drugs can penetrate into the epidermis to interfere with calcium ion concentrations, thereby upregulating the expression of epidermal growth factor receptors and promoting the proliferation and differentiation of keratinocytes. Therefore, CCBs-induced psoriasis may be directly triggered by the pharmacological effects of these drugs [17, 18].

After discontinuing nifedipine, the patient was switched to olmesartan for antihypertensive treatment and received a topical ointment for psoriasis. Olmesartan belongs to the ARBs, and previous literature has reported that this class of drugs may induce or aggravate psoriasis [19]. The rash gradually improved and this may suggest that olmesartan did not induce psoriasiform skin lesions in this patient. ARBs exert the effects by blocking angiotensin Ⅱ receptor (ATR), which leads to a feedback increase in plasma renin levels and consequently elevates Ang Ⅱ concentrations. Currently, it is widely believed that Ang Ⅱ promotes keratinocyte proliferation through non-AT1R and non-AT2R pathways [20]. Similarly, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), which also belong to the class of renin-angiotensin inhibitors, may induce psoriasis. It is related to the blockage of bradykinin inactivation and the gene polymorphism of angiotensin converting enzyme [21, 22]. In the subsequent management of this patient, ramipril was introduced, but no recurrence of psoriasis was observed.

After being transferred to the specialty department, the patient received SV for HF during hospitalization. Following discharge, methylprednisolone was discontinued. And he discontinued SV on his own due to hypotension, with a total treatment duration of approximately one month. Subsequently, digoxin and rabeprazole were discontinued. About three months later, SV 150 mg bid was resumed for HF management. All other medications remained unchanged. Methylprednisolone had been discontinued for 5 months when psoriasis occurred, while SV had been reintroduced and used for 2 months. At that time, the patient’s medications included warfarin, spironolactone, metoprolol, SV, atorvastatin calcium, dapagliflozin, ivabradine, and vericiguat. Metoprolol is a beta-blocker, one of the important medications for HF treatment. Dapagliflozin can lower blood glucose by inhibiting sodium and glucose reabsorption in the renal proximal tubules. It can reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization for HF in HF patients, and as a result, the indications of dapagliflozin have expanded to include the therapy of HF [23]. Beta-blockers, dapagliflozin, the valsartan component of SV, and atorvastatin calcium have been reported to cause or worsen psoriasis [19, 24–26]. However, all of these drugs, except for SV, had been used continuously since the time of discharge. Following the discontinuation of SV, the patient’s psoriasis gradually improved, and by 6 months, the skin had returned to the state shown in Fig. 1b. Although warfarin was also discontinued at that time due to the resolution of the ventricular thrombus, the reintroduction of SV during subsequent treatment led to a recurrence of psoriasis. The Naranjo scale scores of associated drugs were also evaluated. Among them, SV scored 6 points, which indicate a probable association (Table 1). In contrast, metoprolol, dapagliflozin, and atorvastatin calcium scored 3 points, while warfarin, spironolactone, ivabradine, and vericiguat scored 2 points. All these scores were lower than that of SV, and they were assessed as having a possible association. All of these increase the suspicion that there is a correlation between SV and psoriasis.

After admission, the patient was diagnosed with viral myocarditis, and the tests during hospitalization indicated an infection with Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus. Viral myocarditis can extend cardiac injury mechanisms to the brain via the heart-brain axis, leading to neurological complications. This process involves pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-13, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-33. The resulting neuroinflammation and alterations in humoral factors and cytokines further increase the risk of viral myocarditis progressing to chronic HF and systemic inflammatory complications [27]. In this patient, cerebral infarction and HF occurred following viral myocarditis, and the psoriasis may also be associated with systemic inflammation triggered by the viral myocarditis. However, during follow-up, when the patient first developed psoriasis, it had been five and a half months since discharge and five months since methylprednisolone was discontinued. The average elimination half-life of methylprednisolone is 1.8–5.2 h. It is unlikely that the corticosteroid delayed the onset of viral myocarditis-related psoriasis. Additionally, the patient’s re-exposure to SV still induced psoriasis, making SV a more likely suspected trigger of psoriasis. The patient’s baseline BMI was 27.0 kg/m², classified as overweight. Multiple studies have shown that as BMI increases, the prevalence and severity of psoriasis also increase accordingly [28]. Obesity and psoriasis share several key pathophysiological mechanisms, including a chronic inflammatory state, abnormal expression of adipokines and cytokines, and alterations in the skin microbiota. In addition, reduced gut microbiota diversity and obesity-related dietary habits in obesity patients also play important roles in the pathogenesis of psoriasis [28]. During follow-up, the patient’s BMI remained around 25.4 kg/m², which is still in the overweight range. However, after discontinuation of SV, the psoriasis improved markedly, and rechallenge was positive. Therefore, the patient’s psoriasis is less likely to be correlated with her overweight status.

Neprilysin can degrade endogenous vasoactive peptides, including natriuretic peptides, Ang Ⅱ, and endothelin [29]. The neprilysin inhibitor sacubitril increases the concentrations of natriuretic peptides, thereby promoting natriuresis and vasodilation. It can further suppress the activity of RAAS, reduce sympathetic excitability, ameliorate myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, and has the anti-proliferative and anti-hypertrophic effects on the myocardium [30, 31]. Valsartan can selectively block the AT1R, thereby inhibiting RAAS and reducing water and sodium retention caused by aldosterone release. Studies have also revealed that ARBs can prevent the adhesion and migration of leukocytes to vascular endothelium, consequently diminishing cardiac inflammation and fibrosis, and improving ventricular remodeling [32, 33]. Guidelines explicitly recommend its use in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction. It is one of the important drugs in combination therapy for HF and has been widely used in the standardized treatment [2, 34]. Its skin‑related adverse effects mainly include angioedema, rash, pruritus, and allergic reactions. Among these, the incidence of angioedema is approximately 0.5%, and it is higher in Black patients (2.4%) than in non‑Black patients. Rash, pruritus, and allergic reactions are all post‑marketing reports and case reports. Because these reactions are reported voluntarily from a population of uncertain size, it is not always possible to reliably estimate the frequency [35]. There have been no reports of it causing psoriasis to date. Similar to olmesartan, valsartan can increase plasma renin levels feedback by blocking AT1R, thereby leading to an elevation in Ang Ⅱ concentration. However, considering that olmesartan and ramipril did not cause psoriasis in this patient, we suspect that sacubitril in SV may be responsible for this adverse reaction. Sacubitril enhances natriuretic peptide effects by inhibiting neprilysin, a process that may be accompanied by compensatory elevation of Ang Ⅱ levels, potentially contributing to the development of psoriasis observed during follow-up [36]. Additionally, the patient did not develop psoriasis upon first exposure to SV during hospitalization but experienced this adverse reaction twice after resuming SV therapy. This difference may be attributed to concurrent methylprednisolone use for viral myocarditis treatment during the initial treatment, which could have masked or suppressed the cutaneous manifestation.

This report describes a rare case of drug-induced psoriasis involving multiple agents, including nifedipine and SV, which is uncommon in clinical practice. Meanwhile, this is also the first report of psoriasis induced by SV, and its induction mechanism may be independent of ARBs, but further studies are needed to confirm this. For patients with a prior history of psoriasis or those with high-risk factors for psoriasis, such as positivity for the HLA-Cw*0602 allele [4], SV should be used with caution. In conclusion, clinicians should remain vigilant and attentive to this potential adverse effect when managing patients with cardiovascular disease.
