Incident Type 2 Diabetes in Individuals With CKD: The REGARDS Study
Authors: Xingsheng Li, Javier A Jurado Vélez, Nita Kanney, Archie Landrum, Lloyd J Edwards, Orlando M Gutierrez, D Leann Long, Bertha Hidalgo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveChronic kidney disease (CKD) and diabetes are comorbid metabolic diseases that share common factors such as insulin resistance. Few studies have examined the association between CKD and incident diabetes, with limited consensus and associated risk factors. We conducted an analysis of participants in the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in stroke study to investigate incident diabetes among participants with CKD compared with non-CKD participants.Study DesignA prospective cohort studySetting & ParticipantsTotal of 7,511 participants who completed 10 years of follow-up were included in the analysis, of whom 994 had CKD (an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73m2 or albumin-to-creatinine ratio >30mg/g) at baseline.ExposureCKDOutcomeIncident diabetesAnalytical ApproachLogistic regression analysis was performed to estimate the odds of incident diabetes among CKD participants, with odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI. Risk factors for incident diabetes among CKD participants were also examined.ResultsAfter 10 years of follow-up, 11.0% (717) of non-CKD participants and 15.8% (157) of CKD participants developed diabetes. Incident diabetes was higher among CKD participants than in non-CKD participants using creatinine-based equations, which was not seen when using a creatinine-based and cystatin C-based equation for CKD. Several risk factors such as race, prediabetes, age, body mass index, hypertension, current smoking, and exercise ≥ 4 times/week increased the odds of incident diabetes, whereas current alcohol use had a lower risk of incident diabetes. Prediabetes had the highest risk of incident diabetes (OR = 4.94, 95% Cl: 4.22-5.78; P < 0.01).LimitationsAttrition bias, limited generalizability, and limited diagnostic classification.ConclusionsThe association between CKD and incident diabetes depends on how CKD is defined. These findings highlight the need for future studies to explore how renal function is assessed when determining adverse outcomes related to CKD.Index Words: Type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, eGFR, hypertension, prediabetes, dyslipidemia, lifestyle, cystatin C

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a public health epidemic with a rising prevalence in both developed and developing countries over the past 50 years. The International Diabetes Foundation estimated that 536.6 million people between the ages of 20-79 years were living with diabetes in 2021, with the global burden of disease expected to rise by 246.6 million people before 2045.1 The cost related to diabetes prevention and treatment has generated a heavy burden on individuals and their families, the government, and the health care system.2

Patients with diabetes have a higher risk of developing hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD).3, 4, 5 CKD is a well-established complication of diabetes, with a higher prevalence and risk of CKD among individuals with diabetes.6, 7, 8 Individuals with diabetes account for 27%-50% of all CKD cases.5,9 The development of CKD among those with diabetes may be related to multiple risk factors such as age, sex, race, hypertension, obesity, insulin resistance, and body mass index (BMI).5,10,11 Lifestyle factors–including smoking, physical inactivity, and alcohol consumption have also contributed to this association.6,12

Although CKD is a well-established complication of diabetes, it may also serve as a predisposing factor for the development of DM. Both diseases share similar risk factors, including insulin resistance, hypertension, pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, and dyslipidemia.13, 14, 15, 16 However, the mechanisms by which kidney disease influences the onset and progression of diabetes remains unexplored.17 Insulin resistance is present in the early stage of CKD before the decline of glomerular filtration rate (GFR),15,18 which can lead to a glucose imbalance in patients with CKD and potentially an increased risk of diabetes. Additionally, CKD is accompanied by the development of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which may disrupt insulin secretion signaling pathways and contribute to increased insulin resistance.17 In a study of CKD mice models, Koppe et al19 demonstrated defects in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, both in vivo and in isolated islets. Furthermore, insulin secretion was impaired in normal mouse and human islets when cultured with elevated concentrations of urea found in patietns with CKD.19

Nevertheless, there are few studies concerning the incidence of diabetes among individuals with CKD and they have conflicting results.7,14,15 The variation in these studies suggests that identifying risk factors associated with incident diabetes among individuals with CKD is challenging, given the multiple potential risk factors for the development of diabetes. In this study, we conducted an analysis of participants enrolled in the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study to investigate the odds of incident diabetes among participants with CKD compared with those without CKD.

Methods

Study Participants

The REGARDS study is a US national cohort study of community-dwelling adults designed to examine risk factors contributing to the excess stroke mortality among Black adults and adults living in the southeastern part of the US known as the Stroke Belt. The study design has been previously described in detail.20 To briefly summarize, 30,239 non-Hispanic Black and White participants ≥45 years old from the 48 contiguous states in the US were recruited between 2003-2007. Baseline demographic data were collected by a computer-assisted telephone interview, and clinical and physical measurements were collected via an in-person home visit ∼3-4 weeks later. This included blood and urine samples, as well as a physical examination, blood pressure measurements, and a medication inventory. After initial participant recruitment, a complete blood count was included as part of the blood samples for the REGARDS renal ancillary study.21 Approximately 10 years after enrollment (2013-2016), a second computer-assisted telephone interview and in-home examination was conducted, similar to the first. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided written informed consent.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25

Primary Outcome and Exposure

Incident diabetes was our primary outcome, defined as a diagnosis of diabetes at the 2nd assessment among participants without diabetes at enrollment. Participants with diabetes at enrollment were excluded from our analysis. Diabetes was determined by the participant’s blood glucose measurements meeting any of the following criterion: (1) fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL; (2) nonfasting random blood glucose ≥200 mg/dL; and (3) self-reported use of insulin or glucose-controlling drugs. Chronic kidney disease, defined in the REGARDS study as an estimated (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73m2 calculated using the modification of diet in renal disease study equation or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) >30 mg/g, was our primary exposure.23

Covariates

Age, sex, race, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and exercise were self-reported by participants. Smoking and alcohol consumption were coded as current, never, or past. Exercise was defined by response to the question “How many times per week do you engage in intense physical activity, enough to work up a sweat?” and categorized as none, 1-3, or >4 times. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated based on standard World Health Organization guidelines.26 Fasting blood samples were collected in the morning of an in-home visit and centrifuged and refrigerated so they could be sent to a central laboratory (University of Vermont Laboratory for Clinical Biochemistry Research).25 Serum markers, including total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), triglyceride, glucose, cystatin C, creatinine, and later hemoglobin, were measured with colorimetric reflectance spectrophotometry on the Ortho Vitros 950 IRC clinical analyzer. Participants were considered to have prediabetes if they met either of the following criteria: (1) fasting blood glucose of 100-125 mg/dL; (2) nonfasting random blood glucose of 140-199 mg/dL. The ACR (mg/g) was calculated by calculating urinary albumin/urinary creatinine. Blood pressure was measured twice and averaged for analysis. Hypertension status was categorized and defined as hypertension if SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg or self-reported current medication use to control blood pressure. Dyslipidemia was defined if triglyceride ≥240 or LDL ≥160 or HDL ≤40, or being on medication.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for all variables were calculated and compared by CKD status, including the Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables reported as frequencies and proportions. For continuous variables, computed values are expressed as mean ± SD. Two-sample t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between CKD and non-CKD groups. The odds of incident diabetes was modeled by logistic regression. An unadjusted regression model was run with the primary outcome of incident diabetes and the primary exposure of baseline CKD status. A fully adjusted model with all variables of interest at baseline was performed, including age, age2, sex, race, BMI, total cholesterol, HDL, hemoglobin, LDL cholesterol, triglyceride, prediabetes, hypertension status, diastolic and systolic blood pressure, history of alcohol use, smoking history, and physical activity.

To assess whether the association between incident diabetes and CKD differed by age, race, or sex, we evaluated for effect measure modification. We conducted additional analyses that included a separate logistic regression model with interaction terms between CKD and race, sex, and age. In addition, we provided stratification of the association between diabetes and CKD status by age, race, and sex.

To evaluate potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between participants included in the final sample (n = 7,511) and those within the baseline at-risk cohort (n = 11,320). We additionally performed inverse probability-of-inclusion weighting (IPW) to account for differential probability of being included in the complete-case analytic sample. We modeled inclusion (R = 1) as a function of baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory covariates used in the primary model and estimated stabilized weights as P(R = 1)/P(R = 1|X). We truncated stabilized weights at 10 to reduce the influence of extreme weights. Weighted logistic regression model (Model 9) was then fit among included participants to estimate the association of CKD with incident diabetes under this selection model.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed with all covariates in Model 2 to test the robustness of the model. In Model 3, CKD was defined by eGFR using the 2021 CKD-EPI equation, and in Model 4 by eGFR calculated using creatinine and cystatin C.27 A logistic regression with all covariates included in Model 2 was run for Model 3 and Model 4. We also ran 3 linear regression models (Models 5-7) where a continuous variable for eGFR—based on the different equations—was used as the exposure. Finally, in Model 8, we removed all participants without prediabetes and conducted a fully adjusted logistic regression with the covariates included in Model 2. All analyses were done using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Participants' recruitment criteria are displayed in Fig 1. In total, 16,150 participants completed follow-up, 9,087 (30.1%) participants either withdrew or chose not to participate in the second follow-up visit, and 4,946 (16.4%) participants died. Three groups were then excluded: (1) 2,729 participants who had a diabetes diagnosis at their initial visit; (2) 521 participants with missing diabetes data measured at baseline; (3) 1,580 participants who did not have diabetes diagnostic measurements performed at follow-up. Further, 3,809 participants with missing covariates were removed from the final analysis (Table S1). A total of 7,511 participants were included in the final analysis.

Participant’s inclusion criteria from the REGARDS study 2003-2016.

The baseline characteristics of participants, grouped by CKD status, are shown in Table 1. Among a total of 7,511 participants (Fig 1), there are 994 CKD and 6,517 non-CKD participants, with a similar distribution by race and sex. Participants with CKD were more likely to have prediabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, elevated BMI score and ACR, lower levels of eGFR, and older age. Plasma biomarkers, including cholesterol, LDL, triglycerides, mean cell hemoglobin, and cystatin C, were significantly associated with CKD (P < 0.05). Participants with CKD had a lower rate of alcohol consumption and exercised less. However, smoking status, HDL, and urinary creatinine were found not to be associated with CKD status.

Prevalence of Incident Diabetes Among CKD and Non-CKD Participants

After a median follow-up of 9.5 years (interquartile range 8.6-9.9 years), approximately 157 CKD and 717 non-CKD participants developed diabetes. Among those with CKD, 80 (10.7%) participants had a fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 7 (0.9%) had a random glucose ≥200 mg/dL, and 660 (88.4%) had a self-reported use of glucose-lowering medications. Among those without CKD, 334 (18.7%) participants had a fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 19 (1.1%) had a random glucose ≥200 mg/dL, and 1,436 (80.3%) had a self-reported use of glucose-lowering medications. Of note, there was no reported use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors among participants. Incident diabetes among participants with CKD was higher than among participants without CKD (15.8% vs 11.0%, P < 0.01, Table 2).

Incident Diabetes Among CKD in the Logistic Regression Model

The odds of incident diabetes among individuals with CKD compared with those without CKD are shown in Table 2. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), CKD was significantly associated with incident diabetes (OR = 1.52, 95% Cl: 1.26-1.83; P < 0.01). In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), CKD was still significantly associated with the incidence of diabetes (OR = 1.45, 95% Cl: 1.16-1.81; P < 0.01).

Risk Factors of Incident Diabetes

In addition, we evaluated potential risk factors associated with incident diabetes in individuals with CKD compared with those without CKD (Table 3). Among these factors, CKD participants who identified as Black, having prediabetes, hypertension, being current smokers, having higher exercise level (≥4 times/week), age2, and a higher BMI had a higher risk of developing incident diabetes relative to the non-CKD group in both models (Table 3). Interestingly, current alcohol use was associated with a lower risk of incident diabetes (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.60-0.86; P < 0.01). Among those risk factors, prediabetes was associated with the greatest risk of incident diabetes (OR = 4.94, 95% Cl: 4.22-5.78), which was ∼4 times higher than other risk factors (P < 0. 01). However, several interaction terms of risk factors with CKD status were examined and were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess model robustness, we conducted 2 sensitivity analyses included in Table 5. Model 3 showed a similar increased odds for incident diabetes among participants with CKD (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.03-1.65; P = 0.03); however, Model 4 showed no statistically significant association between CKD and incident diabetes (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.66-1.09; P = 0.20). When switching into a continuous eGFR variable (Table S2), linear regression models showed that a one unit increase in eGFR was associated with a minimally increase odds of incident diabetes (Model 5: OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00-1.01, P = 0.06; Model 6: OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.02, P = 0.20; Model 7: OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.01, P = 0.04). Furthermore, Model 8 (Table S3) only included 1,486 participants with prediabetes, and had no significant association between CKD and incident diabetes (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.64-1.29; P = 0.60).

Discussion

Using a US national cohort study of Black and White adults, we observed a significant association between creatinine-based CKD and incident diabetes that was not observed when using a creatinine-based and cystatin C-based definition for CKD. Furthermore, there was not a clinically notable association between eGFR and incident diabetes when using either a creatinine-based or creatinine-based and cystatin C-based definition for eGFR. Race, prediabetes status, age, hypertension, BMI, and lifestyle factors, including current smoking and exercise, are important risk factors that affect incident diabetes. Further subgroup analysis showed that the incidence of diabetes associated with CKD did not differ by race, sex, or age.

Contributors to the development of diabetes have been studied thoroughly in epidemiology; however, few studies have explored the risk of CKD-associated incident diabetes.7,14,15,28,29 Similar to our study, Lin et al7 examined a cohort of 15,403 individuals in Taiwan and reported a higher incidence of diabetes among patients with CKD compared with non-CKD patients, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.20 (95% CI: 1.11-1.31). Interestingly, a study of 2,228 patients with kidney failure on dialysis reported that patients on hemodialysis had an adjusted HR of 0.46 for incident DM (95% CI: 0.37-0.58, P < 0.0001).30 The authors suggest that hemodialysis attenuates the development of insulin resistance attributed to kidney disease, which lowers the risk of incident DM.30 Both of these studies used study populations recruited in healthcare systems as compared with our study population, which was recruited externally to the health care system.

Studies examining cardiovascular outcomes and mortality have demonstrated that cystatin C-based eGFR equations provide more accurate risk stratification over creatinine-based equations,31, 32, 33, 34, 35 although limited literature exists observing incident diabetes. This continues to be observed within studies that included participants with diabetes since cystatin C-based equations identify more individuals with reduced kidney function and stronger associations with adverse outcomes.31,36, 37, 38 On the contrary, most studies examining the relationship between CKD and incident diabetes used a creatinine-based definition for eGFR.14,15,28,30,39 Findings from our study highlight the importance of biomarker choice in observational studies and suggest that the CKD–diabetes relationship may be sensitive to how renal function is assessed.

When running linear regression models using all three eGFR equations, we did not observe a significant association between eGFR and incident diabetes. Tu et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 25,541 nonselective participants in China with impaired fasting glucose, and identified a nonlinear, positive correlation between eGFR and the probability of returning to normoglycemia by using a Cox proportional hazards regression model.39 Additional cohort studies have also identified a higher risk of incident diabetes among individuals with reduced levels of eGFR.14,28 Lorenzo et al30 further indicated that the relationship between GFR and diabetes is not linear: individuals with lower and upper limits of GFR had a higher risk of incident diabetes. However, one study with 437 older adults (aged 65-98) showed that older adults with lower cystatin C-based GFR do not have an increased risk of diabetes, although they were associated with greater insulin resistance.15 The results of this study are limited by their smaller study population and age-related renal function decrease. The results from our models likely suggest a non-linear relationship between CKD and incident diabetes, indicating an indirect pathophysiology rather than a direct correlation between kidney function and diabetes.

Animal models have provided further evidence that toxic metabolites such as urea can also influence the development of insulin resistance.19 Xie et al40 evaluated 1,337,452 US veterans without DM over a median follow-up time of 4.93 years. Among US veterans, higher levels of BUN were associated with an increased risk of incident DM, and additional spline analyses identified that the increased risk of DM due to lower levels of eGFR actually occurs due to elevated BUN levels. Despite various methods of identifying kidney disease, our findings are consistent with previous studies, which suggest that there is a pathological pathway in which kidney disease can lead to DM.

In this study, we found that race, prediabetes, age, hypertension, BMI, and lifestyle factors, including current smoking and exercise, are important risk factors that affect incident diabetes in the full and adjusted models. Prediabetes was the strongest risk factor of incident diabetes, as reinforced by Model 8, which is expected since insulin resistance and impaired insulin signaling has already begun prior to reaching diagnostic criteria for DM.41 BMI, as a greater contributor to metabolic syndrome and diabetes, was also identified as a significant risk factor since it is correlated with insulin resistance in chronic kidney disease.40,42 In general, high BMI, hypertension, increased blood glucose levels, and aging are interrelated as comorbid factors increasing the risk of incident diabetes.

Despite glucose and blood pressure management, lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol use, and exercise remain closely linked to both CKD and diabetes.43 In this study, current smokers and individuals who exercised ≥4 times per week had a higher odds of diabetes, whereas current alcohol users had a lower odds of incident diabetes than those who never consumed alcohol. Similarly, Yang et al13 showed that current smokers and current alcohol users had a lower risk of diabetes in a large-scale, community, cross-sectional study. In comparison, a cohort study in the Chinese population found that current smoking and alcohol use have a higher risk of diabetes.28 Although our findings on alcohol use and exercise might seem unexpected, several possible explanations exist. Notably, a greater proportion of CKD participants were never alcohol users, while a greater proportion of non-CKD participants were current alcohol users (P < 0.001). Most of these lifestyle factors were self-reported, and detailed information regarding the duration or intensity of these behaviors was not collected, which may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, several biases should also be considered in this association, including selection bias and recall bias.

Subgroup analysis performed by stratifying age (> or ≤60 years), sex, and race did not find any statistically significant difference in the association between CKD and incident diabetes. Lin et al7 found that participants who were male and younger had a higher risk of incident diabetes. However, contradicting studies showed that the risk of CKD-associated diabetes is higher in women and older people, which may partly be due to age-related renal function changes and the prevalence of hypertension.13 Regarding age, additional studies have found no to minimal association related to age and incident diabetes among participants with CKD.28, 29, 30 Among patients with kidney failure, Wu et al29 found that among their patients on dialysis, age was the only risk factor for diabetes with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.02 (1.01-1.04).

Our study included a large-scale nationwide population recruited with an average follow-up of around 10 years, which allows us to track the development of diabetes among CKD versus non-CKD participants. This study includes critical blood markers related to diabetes and kidney function, which allows us to fit our model with adjusted comorbid factors. However, there are certain limitations to this study. Attrition bias may be prevalent since 46.5% of recruited participants were lost to follow-up or withdrew from the study during the second visit. Long et al24 focused on this concern and found little evidence of selection bias when estimating race disparity in stroke. Because excluded participants had modestly higher baseline cardiometabolic risk, complete-case analysis could plausibly attenuate the association between CKD and incident diabetes. Consistent with this, the IPW estimate was slightly larger than the complete-case estimate, suggesting any bias due to missing covariates was modest in magnitude. Second, this study population is comprised of individuals older than 45 years, which limits the generalizability of the findings of this study to younger populations. Also, CKD was categorized based on eGFR, and there was no way to confirm CKD by measuring the kidney histological structure. Finally, diabetes status was defined based on a single fasting glucose or random glucose measurement or self-reported use of glucose-lowering medications.23 Most participants were classified as having diabetes due to self-reported use of medications; however, certain medications like metformin may be prescribed for other conditions (eg, PCOS), increasing the risk of false positives. Furthermore, single glucose measurements with fixed cutoffs may not account for biological variability or measurement error, especially in population-based studies, increasing the risk of misclassification between diabetes and prediabetes.

Conclusion

Among REGARDS participants, those with creatinine-defined CKD had higher odds of developing incident diabetes as compared with non-CKD participants, which was not observed when using creatinine-defined and cystatin C-defined CKD. Prediabetes, higher BMI, age, hypertension, smoking, and exercise ≥4 times per week increased the risk of developing incident diabetes among CKD participants as compared with non-CKD participants. In contrast, current use of alcohol decreased the risk of incidence of diabetes. Future studies should further explore how renal function is defined to determine how to best identify adverse outcomes related to CKD.
