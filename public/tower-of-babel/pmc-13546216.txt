Modulation of Total Corneal Astigmatism, Corneal Thickness, and Corneal Higher-Order Aberrations Following Tear-Enhancing Drop Instillation: A Retrospective Comparative Study
Authors: Monika Sarnat-Kucharczyk, Aleksandra Ziemba, Anna Hitnarowicz, Ewa Mrukwa-Kominek, Sudi Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to quantify changes in corneal astigmatism, thickness, and corneal higher-order aberrations after instilling a tear-enhancing drop (Systane Hydration Ultra, SHU) or 0.9% saline.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study included 56 eyes (SHU, n = 38; 0.9% saline, n = 18). Corneal pachymetry and optical parameters were assessed using CASIA2 swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography. Measurements were acquired immediately after a natural blink (baseline, time 0). Subsequently, one drop of SHU or 0.9% saline was instilled, and measurements were repeated at 5 and 10 min postinstillation.ResultsNo clinically relevant changes in mean total corneal astigmatism (TCA), apparent corneal thickness (CT), or corneal higher-order aberrations were observed following instillation of either SHU or 0.9% saline. On a case-by-case basis, changes observed in the 0.9% saline group correlated with preinstillation values. CT tended to reduce with saline and generally increase with SHU.The induced change in TCA (y) 10 min after instillation significantly correlated with preinstillation.(i)CT (t) in SHU cases, where y = 0.317−0.003t +0.006t2 (r2 = 0.361, n = 38, p < 0.001); and(ii)TCA (x) in 0.9% saline cases, where y = 0.68x−0.30 (r2 = 0.562, n = 18, p < 0.001).Overall, the induced change in TCA exceeded 0.50 D in 18% of SHU and 17% of 0.9% saline 10 min after instillation.ConclusionsTear supplementation induces short-term case-specific changes in astigmatism and optical parameters, particularly within the first 5 min after Systane Hydration Ultra instillation. These findings may be relevant when interpreting corneal measurements in patients with mild ocular surface dysfunction undergoing refractive or cataract assessment.Keywords: Tear film, Artificial tears, Tear-enhancing drop, Ocular surface, Corneal astigmatism, Total corneal astigmatism, Corneal thickness

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Ocular surface instability and dry eye disease may affect corneal measurements relevant to refractive and cataract surgery planning.Tear-enhancing drops have been proposed to improve tear film stability; however, their short-term effects on total corneal astigmatism, measurement of corneal thickness, and corneal higher-order aberrations remain insufficiently characterized.This retrospective comparative study evaluated the short-term effects of Systane Hydration Ultra and 0.9% saline on corneal and optical parameters assessed using swept-source anterior segment OCT. What was learned from the study? No clinically relevant mean group-level changes in total corneal astigmatism, corneal thickness, or corneal higher-order aberrations were observed following instillation of the drops.Tear supplementation induced short-term case-specific changes in astigmatism and optical parameters, particularly within the first 5 min after Systane Hydration Ultra instillation. These changes were correlated with some preinstillation properties of the cornea.These findings suggest that ocular surface modulation may influence interpretation of corneal measurements in patients undergoing refractive or cataract assessment.

Introduction

Several review papers indicate that a substantial proportion of patients undergoing cataract surgery are either receiving treatment for, or exhibit signs of, dry eye disease (DED) such as reduced tear film stability. This may adversely affect presurgical biometry and, consequently, postoperative refractive outcomes [1–10].

Accurate biometry is essential for precise intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation. However, DED, reduced tear film stability, and their treatment may influence corneal measurements, including measurements of anterior corneal curvature [1, 4, 10–14], measured corneal thickness [11, 15–17], and the repeatability of keratometric readings and anterior corneal astigmatism measurements [11, 18].

The thickness distribution of the precorneal tear film is influenced by evaporation during the interblink interval [19]. A previous study in patients with ocular surface disease (OSD) demonstrated that tear film instability during the interblink interval may increase the variability of measured corneal astigmatism [20]. This variability most likely reflects the influence of tear film optical quality on the measurement process rather than true anatomical changes in corneal astigmatism. Tear film evaporation may also alter intracorneal fluid dynamics, potentially affecting corneal thickness distribution and, consequently, astigmatism estimates at both anterior and posterior corneal surfaces [21, 22].

Ocular surface treatments designed to enhance tear film stability and reduce evaporation may influence measured corneal thickness and limit fluctuations in total corneal astigmatism (TCA) during measurement by altering the optical properties of the tear film [15, 16, 23, 24]. However, the extent to which tear-enhancing drops (also referred to as wetting agents or ocular surface modulators) affect the measurements of corneal thickness and TCA in the early phase following instillation under real world conditions in a clinical setting remains unclear. Furthermore, the time course over which such changes may reach clinical relevance has not been well established.

Previous studies have primarily focused on anterior keratometry or tear film metrics [12, 18, 25], whereas the short-term influence of tear-enhancing drops on total corneal astigmatism, posterior corneal astigmatism, and corneal higher-order aberrations assessed using swept-source anterior segment OCT remains insufficiently characterized.

We hypothesized that short-term instillation of tear-enhancing drops might induce measurable changes in CASIA2-derived corneal measurements compared with 0.9% saline.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of tear-enhancing drops (Systane Hydration Ultra® [SHU]) and 0.9% saline on corneal and optical parameters. Specifically, we investigated whether drop instillation:

(i)affects the estimate of total corneal astigmatism (TCA), and whether any induced astigmatism depends on drop type;

affects the estimate of total corneal astigmatism (TCA), and whether any induced astigmatism depends on drop type;

(ii)alters apparent corneal thickness (CT), and whether changes in CT (ΔCT) are associated with induced astigmatism at the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces or with TCA;

alters apparent corneal thickness (CT), and whether changes in CT (ΔCT) are associated with induced astigmatism at the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces or with TCA;

(iii)influences corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs), and whether changes in HOAs are associated with induced astigmatism or TCA; and

influences corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs), and whether changes in HOAs are associated with induced astigmatism or TCA; and

(iv)produces changes at 5 and 10 min that are related to baseline tear film stability.

produces changes at 5 and 10 min that are related to baseline tear film stability.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective comparative study was based on standardized clinical examinations performed at the outpatient clinic of the Department of Ophthalmology at a tertiary referral center in Poland. The study analyzed anonymized clinical data obtained from patients who underwent routine ocular surface assessment and anterior segment imaging according to a predefined clinical protocol. The study conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethical Approval

The Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia in Katowice (Katowice, Poland) confirmed that the retrospective analysis of medical records does not constitute a medical experiment and therefore does not require formal approval by the Committee (reference No. BNW/NWN/0052/KB/334/25, dated 23 November 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted in accordance with applicable regulations regarding personal data protection and patients’ rights, and the data were used exclusively for research purposes. Patient consent was not required because the study was based exclusively on anonymized retrospective clinical data collected during routine clinical care.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible participants were adults (> 18 years) able to provide informed consent. Key exclusion criteria included contact lens wear within the specified wash out period, prior ocular surgery, active ocular surface disease or infection, use of topical medications likely to affect the tear film, known corneal ectatic disorders or significant corneal irregularity, and hypersensitivity to study drop components.

Participants

Novak et al. [26] found significant changes in corneal surface power following instillation of tear-enhancing drops in 18 normal eyes. Karadayi et al. [27] reported significant changes in pachymetry values following instillation of tear-enhancing drops in 11 dry and 20 control eyes. Therefore, in keeping with the design of these studies, it was decided that no fewer than 18 eyes should be enrolled in each of the treatment groups.

As part of routine clinical assessment, patients had undergone best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessment, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, completion of the OSDI questionnaire, and noninvasive break up time (NIBUT) evaluation using the IDRA ocular surface analyzer (SBM Sistemi, Italy). CASIA2 imaging was performed at three time points: before eye drop administration (baseline, 0 min), and at 5 and 10 min after instillation. These time intervals are typically encountered in a real world clinical setting. Biological sex was recorded as part of the routine clinical data collection and was included in the baseline characteristics of the study groups. Data on gender identity, race, and ethnicity were not collected.

Study Groups

Patients were categorized according to the type of eye drop administered during routine clinical assessment: SHU (group I) or 0.9% saline (group II). Details of the drops used are noted in Table 1. Patients were assigned to treatment groups according to the eye drop administered during routine clinical assessment. Eye drop selection was based on routine clinical practice and physician preference and was not randomized. Patients were consecutively enrolled into the SHU group and then the 0.9% saline group. Baseline ocular surface characteristics were not used as predefined criteria for treatment allocation.

Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI)

Subjective symptoms of ocular surface discomfort were assessed using the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire [28]. The OSDI is a validated 12-item instrument designed to evaluate the frequency of dry eye symptoms, visual function, and environmental triggers over the previous week. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

Noninvasive Tear Break Up Time (NIBUT) Assessment

Noninvasive tear break up time (s) was measured using the IDRA ocular surface analyzer prior to the CASIA2 examination.

CASIA2 Swept-Source Anterior Segment OCT Measurement Protocol

Pachymetry and anterior and posterior corneal astigmatism were measured using the swept-source anterior segment OCT (CASIA2). Corneal higher-order aberrations values were obtained for standardized pupil diameters. The corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs) were over 4 mm and spherical aberration (corneal SA) over 6 mm. Maximum keratometry (Kmax) was derived from the full corneal map (up to approximately 10 mm) to capture the steepest corneal curvature, in accordance with manufacturer algorithms. Measurements were performed under ambient lighting conditions. Measurements were performed immediately after a natural blink (baseline at time 0) then at 5 and 10 min after eye drop instillation. At each time point, participants were instructed to blink naturally immediately before image acquisition.

A single CASIA2 scan was obtained and analyzed at each time point.

Only scans successfully acquired by the CASIA2 software were included in the analysis.

The 5-min and 10-min time points were selected to evaluate the short-term effects of eye drop instillation on corneal measurements, in accordance with previous studies investigating the temporal effects of artificial tears on corneal parameters [12, 26, 29].

“Pachymetry”

CASIA2 software calculates and generates a map of corneal thickness distribution. The smallest pachymetric value, and the value at the corneal apex, was recorded and stored for subsequent analysis.

“Corneal astigmatism”

CASIA2 software generates a series of corneal topography maps calculated from elevation data. The CASIA2 software automatically calculates total corneal power using measurements of the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces according to the manufacturer’s validated algorithm. Corneal astigmatism is then derived from the calculated principal meridians [30].

The power and axis values for both corneal surfaces were used to calculate the TCA in each case. Astigmatism measurements were consistently documented in the positive cylinder format. A single factory calibrated, recently serviced CASIA2 model was used during the study.

Eye Drop Instillation and Measurement Protocol

For clinical assessment purposes, baseline corneal measurements were obtained using CASIA2 prior to eye drop instillation. Subsequently, a single drop of either SHU or 0.9% saline was administered, followed by repeat measurements at 5 and 10 min using the same imaging protocol.

All examinations were performed in the same draft-free clinical examination room. Ambient temperature and humidity were not formally monitored or recorded.

Data Collection and Analysis

Anonymized clinical data were retrospectively extracted from medical records and transferred to a dedicated Excel spreadsheet for statistical analysis (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

The astigmatism at the corneal surfaces were subjected to vector analysis using Alpins’ procedure [31]. This determines the exact difference in power and axis of any two astigmatic components in a polar format that is amenable to statistical analyses. The total corneal astigmatism (TCA) is represented by the vectorial difference between the power and axis values describing the astigmatism at the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces. Therefore, the vectorial difference between the TCA at predrop and postdrop instillation is the induced change (IA) in TCA.

The results were subjected to analysis as follows.

Firstly, to determine the significance of any apparent change in pachymetry, corneal astigmatic powers (at both surfaces and for TCA), and corneal aberrations within each group (1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for repeat measures).

Secondly, to determine the significance of any intergroup differences in pachymetry, corneal astigmatic powers (at both surfaces and for TCA), and corneal aberrations at each stage of the study (unpaired t-test).

Thirdly, to determine if any changes in pachymetry, induced changes in corneal astigmatic powers (at both surfaces and for TCA), and corneal aberrations were associated with preinstillation values within each group (Pearson correlation).

Fourthly, to determine if any changes in pachymetry, induced changes in corneal astigmatic powers (at both surfaces and for TCA), and corneal HOAs were associated with preinstillation values of NIBUT within each group (Pearson correlation).

Fifthly, to determine if any induced changes in corneal astigmatic powers (at both surfaces and for TCA) were associated with changes in pachymetry or corneal HOA values within each group (Pearson correlation).

Finally, to determine if there were any significant differences between the groups for any induced changes in corneal astigmatism (at both surfaces and for TCA) that occurred between 0–5, 0–10, and between 5 and 10 min after drop instillation.

Nonparametric equivalent tests were planned for application where datasets were not normally distributed (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The statistical tests in Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) were intended for parametric tests and the online facility (https://www.socscistatistics.com/) for more complex tests and nonparametric tests.

Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

The retrospective analysis included 56 eyes (1 right eye per patient). Of these, 38 eyes had received SHU (group I) and 18 eyes had received 0.9% saline (group II) during routine clinical assessment.

Mean (± SD) age and median (range) OSDI scores of the patients were 61.8 (± 17.1) and 18.8 (0–67) in group I, and 59.2 (± 18.0) and 12.5 (0–67) in group II. Differences in age, OSDI scores (Mann–Whitney U test) and gender ratios between the two groups were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

There were no significant differences in the mean values of ACT, CA powers, and corneal higher-order aberrations (1-way ANOVA for repeat measurements p > 0.05 for all comparisons in each group). In group I, the differences in CA powers between 5 and 10 min after drop instillation were significant (paired t-test, front surface CA p = 0.023, TCA p = 0.005). Intergroup comparisons revealed no significant differences other than the differences in axis of astigmatism at corneal back surface before drop instillation (unpaired t-test, p = 0.016)

The mean changes in pachymetry between pre and 5 min and between pre and 10 min after drop instillation were negative in group I and positive in group II. The differences were significant (unpaired t-test, for changes between pre and 5 min p < 0.001 for the minimal corneal thickness and apical corneal thickness; between pre and 10 min p = 0.004 for the minimal corneal thickness and 0.002 for apical corneal thickness).

There were significant differences in the mean values between groups I and II for corneal HOA (4 mm) values 5 min after drop instillation (unpaired t-test, p = 0.045) and axis of posterior corneal surface astigmatism prior to drop instillation (unpaired t-test, p = 0.016).

The apparent differences in the mean axes were not significant. The mean values were ~90° and this was not unexpected when axes values fall within the range of 0 to 180°. An axis value of 5° is very different from 170°, but the sines of the two axis values are very similar. Transforming axis values from degrees to the respective sine values reduces the variability in the datasets. This transformation did not reveal any significant differences within each group, or between groups, for the axes of astigmatism.

In group I, the differences in astigmatism at the anterior corneal surface and for the TCA, between 5 and 10 min after drop instillation, were significant (paired t-test, p = 0.023 for anterior surface astigmatism, p = 0.005 for TCA). No other significant differences were found within each group (p > 0.05).

Discussion

An important consideration when interpreting the present findings is that CASIA2 measurements are obtained through the precorneal tear film, which represents the first refractive surface of the eye. Therefore, the observed short-term changes most likely reflect alterations in the optical properties of the tear film and their influence on measured corneal parameters rather than true structural changes of the cornea.

Table 2 shows the mean values for measured apparent corneal thickness; astigmatism and corneal aberrations remained stable following application of either SHU or 0.9% saline onto the ocular surface. These findings suggest that instillation of these drops onto the eye has no effect on these measurements. The results must be viewed with caution because the relatively high intersubject variability may have masked subtle treatment-related effects. For example, Eq. 1 and the corresponding expression in Table 3 indicate that corneal SA is expected to decrease 10 min after droplet instillation when the preinstillation value is 0.20 µm or more. Equation 2 shows a statistically significant association between SHU instillation and the induced change in astigmatism at the posterior corneal surface. However, the magnitude of this association appears to be of limited clinical relevance. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2, on a case-by-case basis, SHU tended to increase apparent corneal thickness (CT) while the opposite occurred with 0.9% saline. The expected fall in magnitude of SA (according to Eq. 2) and increase in apparent CT following instillation of a drop such as SHU aligns with the outcomes in previous reports [24, 27, 32–35]. These findings most likely reflect changes in the optical properties of the tear film rather than true structural changes within the cornea.

Bar graph showing the mean change in corneal thickness at the apex (ΔCT), defined as corneal thickness before minus corneal thickness after eye drop instillation, at 5 and 10 min following instillation of Systane Hydration Ultra (group I) or 0.9% saline (group II). ΔCT was calculated as baseline minus postinstillation corneal thickness. Therefore, negative ΔCT values indicate an increase in measured corneal thickness after instillation, whereas positive values indicate a decrease

The present study focused on measurements obtained at 5 and 10 min after eye drop instillation to evaluate the short-term effects on corneal parameters. Previous studies have shown that the greatest alterations in corneal measurements may occur within the first few minutes after instillation, with stabilization occurring after approximately 5 min [12, 26, 29]. Therefore, very early transient changes immediately following drop instillation were beyond the scope of the present study.

Figure 3 shows that 10 min after instilling 0.9% saline, the apparent CT tended to increase in cases where preinstillation CT values were > 530 µm and reduce in cases < 530 µm. These observations most likely reflect tear film-related effects on the OCT-derived measurements rather than true changes in corneal thickness. The former aligns with the results according to other studies [36], but not the latter. Naturally occurring changes in the osmolarity of the tears indirectly correspond with changes in CT [37]. CT tends to reduce as the tear osmolarity increases. The cases where the apparent CT reduced after instilling 0.9% saline could reflect tear film-related effects on OCT-derived measurements rather than a specific physiological mechanism.

A statistically significant association was not found in group I (the Systane Hydration Ultra, p > 0.05)

The analyses presented in Table 4 show that changes in measured astigmatism were linked to changes in apparent apical CT in group I. However, the r2 and p values suggest that the induced astigmatism appeared to be more closely associated with changes in corneal SA and corneal higher-order aberrations in group II. Turning to Table 2, there was a tendency for the corneal HOA values to rise after instilling SHU and fall with 0.9% saline, although the changes in mean values for the corneal HOAs were not significant. Furthermore, the mean values for corneal SA did not change after SHU but tended to become more positive after saline. Thus, it is reasonable to suggest the two drops may have dissimilar effects on measured corneal optical properties by modulating of the tear film which may also influence the estimates of corneal astigmatism obtained with CASIA2. Thus, the two drops may have different effects and influence on the estimation of corneal astigmatism.

Turning to Table 5, the induced changes in the astigmatism at the posterior surface of the cornea were negligible in both groups. However, the same cannot be said for the anterior surface or TCA.

Figure 4 consists of polar graphs showing the induced change in TCA power between 0–5 and 0–10 min after drop instillation. At 5 min after instillation, the bulk of changes were < 0.50 D with SHU, but > 0.50 D with 0.9% saline. At 10 min after instillation, the induced change in TCA power had exceeded 0.50 D in 7 of the SHU (18%) and 3 of the 0.9% saline (17%) cases. There was a gradual convergence towards 0 between 5 and 10 min after instillation in both groups. On closer examination, the axes of the induced changes in the saline group were nested in the range 60° to 120° sector. Equations 3 and 4 describe the association between the preinstillation NIBUT and the likely induced change in TCA and posterior corneal surface (PCS) axis. For a preinstillation NIBUT of 20 s, the estimated induced changes in axes are 62° for TCA by 5 min and 48° for the PCS by 10 min after instilling 0.9% saline. These estimates are broadly consistent with the results shown in Fig. 4, although the latter is for the PCS not TCA.

Table 5 shows there were significant intergroup differences in the axes of the induced changes in TCA over the period 0–5 min, but not between 0–10 min. The mean values for the sines of the axes can be interpreted to show the mean axes values for the induced change in TCA are 39.2° and 46.5° for the periods 0–5 and 0–10 min for group I, while the mean changes in group II are 52.8° and 38.8°. These findings suggest that the two drops may differentially influence the induced change in the estimation of TCA.

Previous studies have demonstrated excellent repeatability of CASIA2-derived corneal power measurements [30, 38]. Although repeatability data for TCA are limited, approximately 17–18% of eyes in the present study demonstrated induced TCA changes exceeding 0.50 D. Such changes may be clinically relevant, as they may influence toric IOL selection and preoperative planning for refractive surgery. Therefore, optimization of the tear film before corneal measurements may improve the reliability of surgical planning in selected patients with mild ocular surface dysfunction. However, the induced change in TCA depends on both the type of drop used and the interval between instillation and corneal assessment. As a result, it may affect refractive error assessment in both preoperative and postoperative cases.

The median OSDI scores of the subjects, 18.8 in group I and 12.5 in group II, were on par with the scores for the mild-to-moderate dry eyes according to the study by Schiffman et al. [28]. So, the cases enrolled can be regarded as having mild ocular surface dysfunction and the results cannot be interpreted as being typical of what one can expect in normal eyes.

Although several statistically significant associations were identified, the relatively modest coefficients of determination indicate that only a limited proportion of the observed variability was explained. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered as hypothesis-generating.

The present findings should be interpreted within the context of the studied population and measurement protocol. They should not be directly extrapolated to patients with severe dry eye disease, meibomian gland dysfunction, chronic artificial tear use, or those undergoing ocular surface optimization before cataract surgery, as these clinical settings were not evaluated in the present study. Further prospective studies are required to determine whether similar effects occur under these conditions.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, nonrandomized investigation of a relatively small sample, and the lack of a formal a priori sample size or power calculation may limit the generalizability of the findings. Instead, the sample size was based on previously published studies evaluating the effects of tear-enhancing drops on corneal measurements. Consequently, the study may have been underpowered to detect small differences in some outcome measures. Second, the treatment groups were unequal in size, and treatment allocation was not randomized, with patients enrolled sequentially into the SHU and 0.9% saline groups Therefore, the possibility of selection bias and confounding by indication cannot be excluded, as the clinical factors influencing treatment selection may also have affected the measured outcomes. Third, multiple exploratory statistical comparisons were performed, without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons, which might increase the risk of type I error. Therefore, the observed associations, including the regression analyses, should be considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted with caution until larger prospective fully randomized studies. Fourth, this was a single-center study, which may limit external generalizability. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these observations. Finally, although all examinations were performed under standardized clinical conditions, ambient temperature and humidity were not formally monitored or recorded. These factors may influence tear film stability, and their potential confounding effect cannot be excluded. Moreover, the measurements were limited to the first 10 min after eye drop instillation; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to the long-term effects of repeated or chronic artificial tear use. Finally, although all examinations were performed in the same draft-free clinical examination room, ambient temperature and humidity were not formally monitored or recorded. As these factors may influence tear film stability, their potential confounding effect cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In this retrospective comparative study, short-term instillation of a tear-enhancing eye drop did not result in significant changes in mean corneal astigmatism, corneal thickness, or higher-order corneal aberrations when compared with saline.

However, temporal effects were observed following Systane Hydration Ultra® instillation, with greater changes in induced astigmatism occurring within the first 5 min after application. In addition, individual changes in corneal thickness and higher-order corneal aberrations were associated with induced astigmatism in both groups, whereas no consistent association was found with tear film stability.

These findings highlight the dynamic and subject-specific interaction between the ocular surface and measured corneal optical properties, suggesting that the observed changes most likely reflect alterations in the optical properties of the tear film, thereby affecting measured corneal parameters, rather than true structural alterations of the cornea. Therefore, tear supplementation may warrant consideration when interpreting corneal measurements in patients with mild ocular surface dysfunction, particularly in refractive and cataract assessment.
