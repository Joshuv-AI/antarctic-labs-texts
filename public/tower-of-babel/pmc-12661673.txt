Association between stress hyperglycemia ratio and delirium risk in elderly surgical patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fengwei Yao, Hongyan Zhao, Lei Liu, Yue Ming, Xiaolan Chen, Donghao Fang, Zhijun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is an emerging glycemic marker in critical care, but its relationship with delirium in elderly surgical and trauma ICU patients remains unclear. This study explored the association between SHR and delirium to inform early identification strategies.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database, including 1,111 ICU patients aged ≥ 65 years with surgical or trauma admissions. SHR was calculated as the ratio of admission glucose to estimated average glucose (based on HbA1c) and categorized into quartiles. The primary outcome was ICU delirium, identified using the CAM-ICU tool; in-hospital mortality served as a secondary outcome. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and propensity score matching (PSM) were used to assess associations and robustness. performance was evaluated via ROC curves.ResultsDelirium occurred in 31.1% of patients. Higher SHR was independently associated with greater odds of delirium (Q4 vs. Q1 adjusted OR = 2.79; 95% CI: 1.79–4.35), and this association remained after PSM. RCS analysis indicated a nonlinear relationship between SHR and delirium (P for nonlinearity = 0.0019), and a linear association with in-hospital mortality. SHR demonstrated limited discriminatory ability (AUCs: 0.625 for delirium, 0.654 for mortality).ConclusionSHR was independently associated with delirium in elderly surgical ICU patients. These findings support its potential role in delirium risk stratification, though prospective validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06726-7.Keywords: Stress hyperglycemia ratio, Delirium, ICU, MIMIC-IV, Elderly patients, Mortality

Background

Stress hyperglycemia (SH) refers to a brief rise in blood glucose levels occurring in reaction to acute physiological stress, typically found in circumstances such as trauma, surgery, or illness.This phenomenon is closely associated with neuroendocrine activation and inflammatory responses triggered by acute stressors [1, 2]. Excessive or prolonged stress hyperglycemia has been extensively associated with negative outcomes in critically ill patients, such as increased risks of infection, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and higher mortality rates, even though an increase in glucose may be thought of as an adaptive mechanism in times of crisis [3–5]. However, random or fasting blood glucose measurements fail to distinguish between acute stress-induced hyperglycemia and chronic dysglycemia related to conditions such as diabetes mellitus. This limitation hinders the accurate assessment of the severity of “true” stress hyperglycemia.

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), which incorporates both acute blood glucose and chronic glycemic markers such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), provides a quantitative measure of the independent impact of acute stress on glucose metabolism [6]. Delirium is an acute and reversible neurocognitive disorder characterized by impaired attention, fluctuating consciousness, disorientation, and disturbances in cognitive function. It is particularly common among elderly individuals, critically ill patients, and those in the postoperative period, especially following trauma or surgery. Hyperglycemia has been consistently associated with detrimental effects on the central nervous system, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired cognitive function. In the intensive care unit (ICU), where metabolic dysregulation is common, the incidence of delirium is remarkably high [7, 8]. Its occurrence is associated with numerous factors, including acute stress, metabolic disturbances, and adverse drug effects. Delirium not only prolongs hospital stays and increases healthcare costs but is also associated with higher mortality and long-term cognitive decline [9, 10]. In trauma and surgical patients, the development of delirium is often related to surgical stress, anesthesia, and postoperative pain. Therefore, early identification and management of high-risk patients are essential to improving outcomes. In recent years, SHR has emerged as a valuable prognostic marker in several critical care contexts, including acute myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, atrial fibrillation, and sepsis [11–14].

One prior study demonstrated that elevated SHR was independently associated with the risk of delirium in elderly hospitalized patients [15]; however, most existing studies have focused on general or non-critically ill populations, with a lack of dedicated research on elderly trauma/surgical ICU patients. This study specifically focuses on this high-risk subgroup, who often face greater perioperative risks and complications. The mechanisms underlying delirium in these patients may differ significantly from those in general hospitalized populations, necessitating more precise predictive tools. Based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV) database [16], this study aims to investigate the association between SHR and the risk of delirium in elderly critically ill trauma/surgical patients.

Methods

Research data

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database, a publicly available and internationally recognized resource maintained by the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The database contains detailed clinical data from 53,150 adult patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, medications, procedures, and diagnostic codes.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Approval No. 65083321). As the MIMIC-IV database is de-identified and publicly accessible, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All data extraction and analysis procedures complied with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) regulations.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first admission to the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) or Trauma Surgical Intensive Care Unit (TSICU); (2) age ≥ 65 years; and (3) availability of baseline glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) measurements; (4) Patients who developed delirium 24 h after being admitted to the ICU. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was considered. A total of 1,111 elderly surgical critically ill patients met these criteria and were included in the final analysis. Patients were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on their calculated stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR). The detailed cohort selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Data extraction and analysis

Structured Query Language (SQL) was used in Navicat Premium (version 17) to extract data from the MIMIC-IV database. Candidate variables included demographic characteristics, vital signs, comorbidities, laboratory measurements, and illness severity scores upon ICU admission. All laboratory data and severity scores were calculated based on the first values recorded within first day after ICU admission. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was calculated using the following formula: SHR = [Admission glucose (mg/dL)]/[28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7] [17]. Delirium was identified using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU), which evaluates four features: (1) acute onset or fluctuating course of mental status (2), inattention (3), disorganized thinking, and (4) altered level of consciousness. A positive CAM-ICU assessment requires the presence of features 1 and 2, plus either feature 3 or 4 [18]. In the MIMIC-IV database, CAM-ICU assessments were routinely conducted multiple times per day by trained bedside nursing staff and recorded as structured chart events. All documented assessments during the ICU stay were extracted, and a patient was considered delirium-positive if any CAM-ICU evaluation was recorded as positive.

To reduce bias from missing data, variables with >10% missingness were excluded. For variables with < 10% missingness, multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R, employing the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) method [19]. This approach recursively partitions the data to model complex nonlinear associations and accommodates both continuous and categorical variables, enhancing the robustness and accuracy of imputation.

Extreme values in blood glucose and HbA1c were handled using winsorization via the “DescTools” package in R [20, 21]. Values below the 1 st percentile and above the 99th percentile were replaced with the respective boundary values. This approach effectively mitigated the influence of outliers while preserving overall data distribution. Boxplots of variable distributions before and after winsorization are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, demonstrating a substantial reduction in skewness and extreme influence.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution, while categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using t-tests or ANOVA for normally distributed variables, and Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed variables. The primary outcome was ICU delirium assessed by CAM-ICU. The secondary outcome was in-hospital mortality.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the association between SHR and the primary outcome (ICU delirium), with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). SHR was modeled both as a continuous and a categorical variable (quartiles, with Q1 as the reference group), and trend tests were performed across quartiles. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Four hierarchical models were constructed as follows: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for sex, age, temperature at ICU admission, SOFA score, GCS score, and SAPS II score; Model 3: further adjusted for laboratory variables including partial thromboplastin time, bicarbonate, creatinine, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, red cell distribution width, and platelet count; Model 4: additionally adjusted for comorbidities and treatments: myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, calcium channel blocker (CCB) use, midazolam use, aspirin use, ACEI/ARB use, vasoactive drug use, mechanical ventilation, acute kidney injury (AKI), and sepsis.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots was applied to explore potential nonlinear associations between SHR and delirium risk. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the discriminatory ability of SHR and identify the optimal cutoff value. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex, diabetes, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, sepsis, and coronary artery disease. Interaction effects were tested using likelihood ratio tests. Subgroups were defined based on clinically relevant characteristics commonly associated with ICU outcomes (e.g., sex, diabetes, cardiovascular comorbidities). While these strata were not formally prespecified in a study protocol, their selection was guided by prior literature and biological plausibility.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by stratifying patients into high and low SHR groups based on the optimal cutoff from the ROC curve and conducting propensity score matching (PSM) in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), with a two-tailed P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics by SHR quartiles

A total of 1,111 elderly surgical or trauma patients were included in this study. Based on their admission SHR levels, participants were divided into four groups: Q1 (0.76 ± 0.11), Q2 (0.95 ± 0.05), Q3 (1.14 ± 0.07), and Q4 (1.59 ± 0.33). Significant differences were observed across the quartiles in various clinical and laboratory parameters. Patients in the Q4 group were slightly younger and exhibited progressively higher SOFA and SAPS II scores from Q1 to Q4, indicating greater illness severity among those in the highest SHR quartile. In terms of laboratory findings, patients in the Q4 group had higher partial thromboplastin time, anion gap, and white blood cell count, along with lower bicarbonate levels, reflecting a more pronounced physiological disturbance. In contrast, no significant differences were noted across groups in body temperature, international normalized ratio, red blood cell count, calcium, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, blood urea nitrogen, potassium, sodium, red cell distribution width, or prothrombin time. Analysis of differences among the four groups based on SHR quartile values

Among the 1,111 patients included, 382 (34.38%) developed delirium, while 729 (65.62%) did not. Compared with patients without delirium, those with delirium had significantly higher SOFA and SAPS II scores at ICU admission, indicating more severe illness. Additionally, delirious patients presented with elevated body temperature and heart rate, and lower systolic blood pressure, suggesting enhanced stress response and hemodynamic instability. Laboratory results revealed a higher anion gap and lower bicarbonate levels among delirious patients, indicative of possible metabolic acidosis. Serum calcium levels were also lower in this group. Regarding hematologic indices, patients with delirium had lower platelet counts, higher mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and reduced MCHC, which may reflect underlying inflammation or anemia. Detailed clinical and laboratory features by delirium status are presented in Table 2. These findings suggest that the development of delirium is closely associated with disease severity, hemodynamic instability, and metabolic derangements.

Variable selection and multicollinearity diagnostics

To examine every potential variable in our investigation, we first used LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation. The ideal penalty parameter was determined by applying the “minimum lambda plus one standard error” criterion. All significant predictors were selected with a lambda value of 0.02505297 (λ = 0.02505297) [22]. Fig. 2 illustrates the coefficient profiles of all variables in the LASSO regression model, where each curve represents the trajectory of a predictor’s coefficient across varying levels of penalization. The right panel of the figure shows the process of selecting the optimal value of the regularization parameter using cross-validation. Fig. 3 illustrates the order of variables selected using LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression based on their contribution to the model.

LASSO coefficient paths and cross-validation for variable selection (delirium model)

Variables selected by LASSO and their relative contribution (delirium model)

In the final model, we incorporated all variables selected by LASSO, excluding SHR itself. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to assess multicollinearity among variables in the multivariate logistic regression model. All included variables had a VIF less than 10, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity. Supplementary Table S1 shows the variance inflation factors (VIFs) of the confounding variables included in the model. As glucose and the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) showed substantial collinearity, glucose was excluded from the final multivariable logistic regression model to avoid instability in the estimation and interpretation of SHR. The adjusted covariates in the final multivariate model included myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, use of calcium channel blockers, use of midazolam, aspirin use, use of ACEI/ARB, use of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, acute kidney injury, sepsis, age on admission, body temperature, SOFA score, GCS score, SAPS II score, activated partial thromboplastin time, bicarbonate, creatinine, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), and platelet count.

Association between SHR and delirium (primary outcome)

Multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 3) demonstrated that patients in the highest quartile of SHR (Q4) had significantly higher odds of developing delirium across all models. The odds ratios (ORs) with 95%CI were as follows: Model 1 (unadjusted): OR = 3.43 (2.36–4.97); Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and severity scores): OR = 3.27 (2.22–4.81); Model 3 (further adjusted for laboratory parameters): OR = 3.39 (2.26–5.09); Model 4 (fully adjusted model): OR = 2.79 (1.79–4.35). A significant relationship across SHR quartiles was observed in each model (P for trend < 0.001). When SHR was treated as a continuous variable, the association with delirium remained robust in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.29–2.98; P = 0.002). Fig. 4 presents the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for SHR in predicting ICU delirium. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.625 (95% CI: 0.591–0.659), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.9 determined by the Youden index. At this threshold, the sensitivity and specificity were 69.1% and 53.2%, respectively.

In comparison with established ICU severity scores (Fig. 4), the AUC for SHR (0.625) was numerically higher than SOFA (0.567) and GCS (0.446), and similar to SAPS II (0.623); however, the absolute differences were small, and overall discrimination remained limited. Visual inspection of the RCS curve suggested a modest downturn at very high SHR; however, confidence intervals widened in this range, consistent with small patient counts, so this feature should be interpreted cautiously.

The association between SHR and in-hospital mortality among elderly surgical ICU patients was further examined. Covariates for adjustment were selected via LASSO regression, with coefficient profiles displayed in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3. Four multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using the selected variables.

As shown in Table 4, patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) had significantly elevated odds of in-hospital mortality across all adjustment model: Model 1 (crude): OR = 5.09 (3.19–8.12); Model 2: OR = 5.26 (3.19–8.68); Model 3: OR = 5.23 (3.07–8.93); Model 4: OR = 4.06 (2.31–7.12). A significant dose–response trend across SHR quartiles was observed in each model (P for trend < 0.001). When SHR was modeled as a continuous variable, the association with mortality remained robust (OR = 3.52, 95% CI: 2.17–5.73; P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the association between SHR and delirium, a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was performed. Patients were stratified into high-SHR (≥ 0.9) and low-SHR (< 0.9) groups based on the Youden index-derived threshold. Using the MatchIt package in R, 1:1 nearest neighbor matching with replacement was applied, employing a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit-transformed propensity score to ensure adequate matching quality.

After matching, standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all covariates were below 0.1 (range: 0.01–0.09), indicating excellent balance. Covariate balance improvement was visually confirmed using a Love plot (Fig. 6). From the initial cohort of 1,111 patients, 298 matched pairs (n = 596) were retained for analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Love plot showing covariate balance before and after propensity score matching

The association between SHR and delirium was further evaluated in the matched cohort using multivariable logistic regression models. As shown in Table 5, SHR ≥ 0.9 remained significantly associated with higher odds of delirium both in the full cohort (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.12–2.65) and in the matched sample (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.48–3.06), demonstrating consistency and robustness against potential selection bias.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the association between SHR and clinical outcomes differed across key patient characteristics, including sex, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial infarction, hypertension, and coronary heart disease (CHD) (Fig. 7). In all subgroups, the direction of association between elevated SHR and the occurrence of delirium remained consistent. No statistically significant interactions were detected (all P for interaction > 0.05), suggesting that the observed association was stable across different patient strata, including those with or without diabetes.

Subgroup analysis of the association between SHR and ICU delirium

Further subgroup analyses focusing on in-hospital mortality yielded similar findings (Fig. 8). The association between higher SHR and mortality remained consistent across all examined subgroups, with no evidence of significant effect modification. These results support the robustness of the relationship between SHR and adverse clinical outcomes in elderly surgical ICU patients, irrespective of underlying comorbidities.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study identified a robust and independent association between elevated stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and the occurrence of delirium in elderly surgical and trauma ICU patients. The associations remained consistent across progressively adjusted logistic regression models and were supported by restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses. Notably, we observed a nonlinear relationship between SHR and delirium, while the association with in-hospital mortality was linear. The hint of an inverted-U shape likely reflects data sparsity at extreme SHR values with widening CIs, and should not be over-interpreted.

Given that glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels are less impacted by critical illness and that current research on glucose levels has found it to be a biomarker for delirium, the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) in conjunction with fasting glucose levels may more accurately represent the connection between glucose and delirium [23, 24]. Hyperglycemia not only reflects the inflammatory response due to trauma or perioperative stress but is also a potential trigger for inflammation [25–27]. The association between stress hyperglycemia and adverse neurologic outcomes such as delirium is biologically plausible. Acute hyperglycemia during critical illness may trigger systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation, which collectively disrupt the integrity of the blood–brain barrier and promote neuroinflammation [8, 28]. Hyperglycemia has been shown to enhance the release of proinflammatory cytokines [29, 30]. The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the accumulation of inflammatory factors further affect the integrity of the blood-brain barrier, which in turn damages neural network function and increases the incidence of delirium [29, 30]. Studies have shown that inflammatory markers such as IL-6 surge under stress conditions, and elevated IL-6 levels are directly associated with the incidence of delirium [31].

Previous studies on surgical patients have reported a positive correlation between perioperative stress-induced hyperglycemia and postoperative delirium [32–34]. However, these studies only considered transient glucose factors and did not account for the impact of background glucose levels or the presence of diabetes. Other studies have also shown that the SHR is significantly associated with delirium in patients after coronary artery bypass surgery, elderly patients with community-acquired pneumonia, and elderly hospitalized patients [15, 35, 36]. In line with results from a research on delirium risk in older patients, our analysis showed a significant association between raised SHR and delirium risk (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.36–2.90). This shows that SHR is linked to an increased risk of delirium. However, their study was limited to patients with HbA1c < 6.5% [15].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, This study was based on data from a single tertiary academic medical center in the United States, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other settings with different patient populations, care protocols, or health systems. External validation using multicenter and international cohorts is warranted. Second, although we adjusted for a wide range of confounders, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, and Subgroup analyses were exploratory and not prespecified; results are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously given multiplicity and limited power. Third, the timing and frequency of delirium assessments (via CAM-ICU) were not strictly standardized, and hypoactive delirium may have been underrecognized, leading to underestimation of incidence and non-differential outcome misclassification. Fourth, although SHR accounts for both acute and chronic glycemia, it does not fully capture glycemic variability. Fifth, this study does not address interventions, and prospective trials are needed to assess whether modulating SHR improves neurologic outcomes. Lastly, serial HbA1c is unavailable; because SHR hinges on HbA1c to approximate background glycemia, a time-varying SHR cannot be estimated with fidelity. Using serial glucose as a surrogate would conflate acute stress with chronic dysglycemia and is vulnerable to confounding-by-indication; therefore, we did not add longitudinal SHR/treatment-effect analyses. We also did not compute delirium- and coma-free days (DAFC) because robust DAFC requires standardized, frequent CAM-ICU/RASS assessments, whereas assessment timing and RASS availability vary in MIMIC-IV, creating informative missingness and potential non-random bias in a non-prespecified analysis.

Taken together, SHR should be regarded as a risk-association marker with minimal to modest stand-alone discrimination; its principal utility may lie in risk enrichment as part of multimarker models, which requires prospective validation. Future studies should validate these findings in multicenter prospective cohorts, and investigate whether combining SHR with other metabolic, inflammatory, and neurologic biomarkers can enhance risk stratification or inform targeted prevention strategies for delirium. Prospective, protocolized studies with serial HbA1c (or alternative markers of chronic glycemia) and standardized CAM-ICU/RASS schedules are needed to evaluate temporal dynamics of SHR and to test whether effective management of stress hyperglycemia can reduce the incidence and/or duration of delirium.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a higher stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was independently associated with an increased occurrence of delirium and in-hospital mortality among elderly surgical and trauma ICU patients. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these associations and explore the integration of SHR into delirium assessment frameworks and individualized care strategies.
