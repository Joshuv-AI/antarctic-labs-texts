Automated method for determining the zeta potential of red blood cells via motion tracking in microelectrophoresis
Authors: Oleksii Borikov, Volodymyr Berest, Daniil Liadov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe surface charge of red blood cells (RBCs), commonly expressed as zeta potential, is a key biophysical parameter influencing RBC aggregation, deformability, and microcirculatory behavior. Despite its physiological and clinical relevance, routine assessment of RBC zeta potential is limited by methodological complexity and poor reproducibility of conventional microelectrophoretic techniques. In this study, we present an automated method for determining the zeta potential of individual RBCs based on computer-assisted tracking of cell motion during microelectrophoresis. Video recordings of RBC movement in an electric field were analyzed using automated spot detection and trajectory reconstruction, allowing electrophoretic mobility and zeta potential to be calculated at the single-cell level. Application of the method to RBCs from healthy donors revealed reproducible zeta potential distributions, while additionally revealing distinct subpopulations of cells with markedly reduced surface charge. The effect of electric field direction on zeta potential was systematically evaluated, demonstrating the contribution of diffuse layer relaxation effects and supporting the use of bidirectional measurements to improve accuracy. Furthermore, incubation of RBCs with gramicidin S, trypsin, and neuraminidase induced pronounced and statistically significant reductions in zeta potential, confirming the sensitivity of the method to alterations in membrane composition and sialic acid content. Overall, the proposed automated microelectrophoretic approach enables objective, high-resolution analysis of RBC electrokinetic properties and overcomes key limitations of manual techniques. This method holds promise for studying red blood cell aging, membrane damage, and drug–cell interactions, and may contribute to the development of novel biophysical biomarkers relevant to hematological and systemic disorders.Keywords: Red blood cells, Zeta potential, Microelectrophoresis, Gramicidin S, Trypsin, Neuraminidase

Introduction

Determining the biochemical and morphological characteristics of blood cells is essential not only for fundamental research but also for clinical diagnostics and the safety assessment of new pharmaceuticals. One of the principal physical characteristics influencing cell function is the electric charge of the plasma membrane. However, the direct measurement of cellular surface charge remains technically challenging. In practice, the potential at the slipping plane is more commonly assessed. Zeta potential, an electrochemical property of the cellular surface, is determined by the cumulative electric charge of the molecules present at the surface. The RBC membrane is negatively charged and is surrounded by a fixed layer of cations. This stationary layer is further enclosed by a diffuse layer composed of both cations and anions. Within the diffuse layer, Brownian motion of RBCs and fluid flow establish a “shear” plane separating loosely associated ions from those bound to the fixed layer. The potential at this shear plane is defined as the zeta potential.

The predominant source of the negative surface charge in RBC is attributed to sialic acids, which are derivatives of neuraminic acid found in glycolipids and glycoproteins located on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. Under physiological conditions, the carboxyl groups of these sialic acids contribute to the overall charge. Notably, human RBCs exhibit a significantly higher concentration of sialic acids in comparison to those of other species. Importantly, not only the magnitude of the charge but also its spatial localization plays a critical role in cellular interactions [1]. A high negative charge facilitates mutual repulsion among cells, thereby preventing their aggregation within the bloodstream. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that sialic acids influence various properties of RBCs, including membrane morphology, deformability, oxygen-carrying capacity, and even the structural organization and distribution of intracellular hemoglobin molecules [2,3].

The majority of sialic acids are primarily associated with glycoproteins such as glycophorin A and band 3, which are abundantly expressed on the RBC surface. Glycophorin A, the most abundant sialoglycoprotein, accounts for a significant portion of the total sialic acid content and is vital for maintaining the cell's negative surface charge, which is crucial for preventing aggregation and regulating various cellular interactions [4]. The band 3 protein is crucial for determining the mechanical properties of the membrane. The dynamic interplay between the cytoskeleton's reduction and relaxation is mediated by the conformational flexibility of spectrin, which is linked to band 3 via an adhesion protein. This intricate organization of integral membrane proteins and the cytoskeleton, primarily composed of spectrin and actin, is crucial for the phospholipid bilayer to maintain its characteristic biconcave shape and mechanical integrity [5]. Consequently, alterations in the RBC's surface charge induce structural changes in both band 3 and glycophorin A, thereby influencing RBC morphology.

RBCs have an average lifespan of approximately 120 days, during which they cover a distance of around 240 km. Throughout their circulatory journey, RBCs are continually required to traverse narrow capillaries, resulting in mechanical stress and a progressive loss of surface sialic acids. This cumulative damage alters both structural integrity and cellular function, contributing to erythrocyte senescence. In freshly collected blood samples, around 16% of total RBCs are identified as older cells. Importantly, the concentration of sialic acids in RBCs aged 90-120 days is approximately 21-30% lower than that in younger RBCs, particularly those aged 1-30 days. As a result, the zeta potential of aged RBCs demonstrates a reduction of approximately 30% when compared to that of younger RBCs [6]. Thus, the zeta potential is directly correlated with the amount of sialic acids on the cell membrane surface, establishing it as a valuable parameter for estimating the proportion of age-related RBC populations in blood samples.

Changes in membrane composition, protein adsorption, or oxidative damage can modify the zeta potential, thereby influencing blood rheology. Over the past decades, several experimental and clinical studies have examined RBC zeta potential as a biophysical marker in disease. In malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum, infected RBCs exhibit a reduction in negative zeta potential compared with non-infected cells. This change is attributed to parasite-induced remodeling of the RBC membrane, including loss or masking of sialic acid residues and insertion of parasite proteins [7]. Similar trends toward decreased zeta potential have been described in systemic infections and sepsis, where inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress alter membrane charge and promote RBC aggregation [8]. In diabetes mellitus, chronic hyperglycemia leads to non-enzymatic glycation of membrane proteins and enhanced oxidative stress. These processes modify membrane structure and surface charge, resulting in altered zeta potential and reduced RBC deformability. Such changes are thought to contribute to microvascular complications characteristic of long-standing diabetes [9]. Studies in preeclampsia report a decrease in RBC zeta potential compared with normotensive pregnancy. This reduction correlates with abnormal RBC morphology and increased aggregation, suggesting impaired microcirculatory flow. Some authors have proposed RBC zeta potential as a potential biophysical biomarker for early detection or monitoring of preeclampsia, although evidence remains limited [10]. Pronounced alterations in RBC zeta potential have been documented in rare hematological conditions involving abnormal glycosylation of membrane proteins, such as Tn syndrome. These findings further support the central role of membrane carbohydrates in maintaining RBC surface charge [11]. The main mechanisms proposed to explain disease-related changes in RBC zeta potential include: (1) loss or modification of sialic acid residues; (2) adsorption of plasma proteins, immunoglobulins, or complement components onto the RBC surface; (3) oxidative damage to membrane lipids and proteins; and (4) structural remodeling of the membrane cytoskeleton. These processes often coexist and collectively influence the electrokinetic properties of RBCs. A reduction in the negative zeta potential of RBCs favors aggregation and increased blood viscosity, potentially impairing microcirculatory perfusion. While current evidence supports an association between altered zeta potential and several diseases, its clinical utility as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker is not yet established.

There are various methods for determining zeta potential based on measuring the velocity of cell movement under the influence of an electric field, including microelectrophoresis, capillary electrophoresis and dynamic light scattering. Instruments utilizing dynamic light scattering are user-friendly and yield reproducible results. However, they are limited to providing only the average zeta potential of colloidal particles or cells in suspension [12]. In contrast, microelectrophoresis allows for direct observation of cells through an optical microscope, enabling the determination of not only average values but also the distribution of cells based on their zeta potential. On the other hand, this method is quite labor-intensive and gives poorly reproducible results when studying cells, which is primarily associated with subjective errors depending on the experience and skills of the researcher. A potential solution is the use of computer-assisted analysis of microelectrophoresis video recordings, enabling automated detection, tracking of cell movement, and subsequent data processing. Therefore, this study aims to develop a methodology for the automatic analysis of RBC distribution by zeta potential through the tracking of cell movement during microelectrophoresis.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

RBCs were isolated from the blood of healthy male and female donors aged 40 to 60 years. A blood sample of 2 mL was collected from the antecubital vein of each donor into a vacuum tube (Vacurate®) containing K3EDTA as an anticoagulant. RBCs were extracted from whole blood through centrifugation at 200g for 15 min at 20 °C. The resulting RBC pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) composed of 1.7 mM KH2PO4, 5.2 mM Na2HPO4, and 150 mM NaCl, adjusted to a pH of 7.5. This resuspension was followed by centrifugation at 2000g for 5 min at 20 °C. The washing procedure was repeated three times. The final RBC pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS and stored at 4 °C for no more than 3 h prior to use. The RBC count in the samples was assessed using a Goryaev chamber, and the cells were diluted to a final concentration of 1010 cells/mL. Immediately before analysis, an aliquot of the RBC suspension was further diluted with the electrophoresis buffer consisting of 10 mM HEPES and 240 mM sucrose (pH 7.0) to achieve a final cell concentration of 106 cells/mL.

Incubation of RBCs with gramicidin S

A suspension of RBCs (1010 cells/ml) was incubated in a phosphate-salt buffer containing gramicidin S at a concentration of 10 μg/ml for 15 min at 37°С. Immediately before the study, an aliquot of the RBC suspension was diluted with a buffered sucrose solution (10 mM HEPES, 240 mM sucrose, pH 7.0) to a final cell concentration of 106 cells/mL.

Treatment of RBCs with trypsin

A suspension of washed RBCs in a phosphate-salt buffer was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the presence of 16 pU/ml trypsin (Sigma St.Louis, MO). RBCs were centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min. The sediment was resuspended in a phosphate-saline buffer. The washing procedure was repeated three times.

Treatment of RBCs with neuraminidase

A suspension of washed RBCs in a phosphate-salt buffer was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the presence of 135 mU/ml neuraminidase (type V, Sigma St.Louis, MO). RBCs were centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min. The sediment was resuspended in a phosphate-saline buffer. The washing procedure was repeated three times.

Microelectrophoresis

The electrophoresis chamber was assembled and positioned on the stage of a MicroMed XS-3330 light microscope, which was outfitted with a DCM-900 digital camera featuring a resolution of 9.0 megapixels. The microelectrophoresis chamber was constructed based on a Goryaev hemocytometer, which is housed within a specially designed hermetic plastic enclosure. The dimensions of the working cell are 10 × 8 × 3 mm. The electrodes are made from platinum wire with a diameter of 0.8 mm, and the distance between the electrodes is 10 mm. The distance from the electrode to the graduated grid of the hemocytometer is 5 mm (Fig. 1). The microscope was focused on the chamber grid at a magnification of ×200. The output voltage of the power supply was calibrated to 10 V using a multimeter. Subsequently, 0.8 mL of the red blood cell suspension was introduced into the microelectrophoretic chamber, and the electrical circuit was closed while video recording was initiated simultaneously with the digital camera. To improve the accuracy of velocity measurements, electrophoresis was performed in both directions by alternating electrode polarity, with each direction applied for 30 s. The video footage was recorded in *.avi format for subsequent analysis.

Automated analysis of video images and determination of electrokinetic characteristics of RBCs

Automated determination of individual RBC velocities was achieved using an image processing algorithm developed in the open-source bioimaging platform Icy (https://icy.bioimageanalysis.org/). The analysis workflow consisted of several sequential stages: video fragment preparation, object detection, trajectory construction, and the calculation of cell velocity.

Preparation and object detection

Representative 4-s fragments of cell movement were extracted from the original microelectrophoresis video recordings (which lasted 60 s and included a polarity reversal). RBCs were identified within the frames using the Spot Detector plugin (Fig. 2) The algorithm was configured to detect “dark spots on a bright background”, which corresponds to the visualization of cells in a Goryaev chamber under trans-illumination microscopy. The primary detection parameters were set to Scale №5, matching the characteristic pixel size of an RBC, and a Sensitivity level of 70, which ensured stable object recognition while maintaining a low false-positive rate.

Identification of individual red blood cells using the Spot Tracking plugin.

Trajectory tracking and filtering

The construction of spatio-temporal trajectories was performed using the Spot Tracking plugin (Fig. 3). The average length of displacement between two consecutive frames was set to 30 pixels. To enhance data reliability, a rigorous filtering procedure was applied within the Track Manager module: only cells that remained within the frame for the entire duration of the video fragment were included in the final analysis. This step eliminated errors associated with cells entering or leaving the focal plane. Trajectories corresponding to cell aggregates or debris were manually removed during the visual inspection stage.

Trajectories of red blood cells obtained using the Spot Tracking plugin.

Statistical analysis

To characterize the zeta potential distribution and identify markers associated with surface properties, individual cells were treated as observations to construct population histograms. Peak detection in RBC zeta potential distributions as a function of zeta potential was performed using the Multi-peak Fit tool in OriginPro 2026. The experimental data were fitted by nonlinear regression with minimization of the sum of squared residuals between the experimental and model curves. The distribution was modeled as a sum of peak functions, representing subpopulations of RBCs differing in zeta potential, with peak parameters optimized using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. Initial parameter estimates were obtained automatically based on local maxima detection, followed by iterative refinement during the fitting procedure.

The parameters for the normal distribution fit of the main peaks were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) method. The goodness-of-fit of the distribution was evaluated with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, utilizing Statistica 12 software. The data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Welch's t-test was used to compare zeta potential values obtained in the forward and reverse electric field directions, as well as the combined zeta potential values determined by the manual and automated methods. For comparisons between the two analytical approaches, donor-level combined zeta potential values were treated as the primary statistical units.

A comparison of the zeta potential of intact cells and cells treated with membrane surface modifiers was performed by one-way ANOVA. The Newman-Keuls test was used for post-hoc multiple comparisons of the groups. Statistical analysis was performed using six independent experimental replicates for each group (n = 6). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Values were considered significant at p < 0.05.

The analysis was performed separately for cell movement in forward and reverse directions of the applied electric field. Although the distributions differed between samples, their overall shape remained largely preserved upon reversal of the electric field direction (Fig. 4, Table 1).

In all samples, the majority of RBCs formed a dominant peak that could be approximated by a normal distribution, typically comprising 70–80% of the total population. In most cases, this peak exhibited slight asymmetry toward lower zeta potential values, as reflected by negative skewness coefficients. The goodness-of-fit assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated acceptable agreement with normal distribution for the main peak in most conditions.

In Sample 1, the main peak followed an approximately normal distribution with slight negative skewness toward lower potential values (Fig. 4A–Table 1). A statistically significant difference was observed between zeta potential values measured in forward and reverse electric field directions (|ΔMode| = 5.3 mV), although the overall distribution shape and dispersion remained unchanged. Notably, approximately 20% of the population consisted of cells with low zeta potential (Mode = −3.3 mV).

Sample 2 demonstrated a more complex distribution profile, characterized by the presence of multiple subpopulations (Fig. 4B). In addition to the main peak, a distinct low-potential subpopulation (13%, Mode = −4.4 mV) was observed. Furthermore, a secondary peak corresponding to cells with higher absolute zeta potential values (Mode ≈ −40 mV) accounted for approximately 14% of the population. The main peak exhibited a pronounced shift upon reversal of the electric field direction (|ΔMode| = 3.6 mV), whereas the additional peaks were comparatively stable.

In Sample 3, the distribution was similar to that of Sample 1 (Fig. 4C–Table 1), with a clearly defined main peak and an additional subpopulation of low-potential cells (approximately 18%, Mode = −3.3 mV).

Sample 4 exhibited the lowest zeta potential among all samples (Mode = −8.9 and −11.2 mV for forward and reverse direction, respectively), along with minimal displacement of the main peak upon field reverse (|ΔMode| = 2.3 mV) (Fig. 4D–Table 1). This sample also contained a subpopulation of low-potential cells (approximately 20%, Mode = −3.2 mV).

In Sample 5, no distinct low-potential subpopulation was observed. Instead, the distribution was best described as a single asymmetric peak with a pronounced tail toward lower zeta potential values. The difference between modes in forward and reverse directions was 2.7 mV (Fig. 4E–Table 1).

Finally, Sample 6 exhibited a main peak that shifted toward lower values upon reversal of the electric field direction (|ΔMode| = 3.5 mV), along with a low-potential subpopulation (Mode = −4.0 mV). Notably, in this sample the goodness-of-fit to a normal distribution was reduced in the reverse direction electric field (p = 0.003), indicating increased heterogeneity (Fig. 4F–Table 1).

Across all samples, reversal of the electric field direction resulted in a systematic shift of the main peak toward lower zeta potential values, with magnitudes ranging from 2.3 to 5.5 mV. Importantly, this shift did not substantially affect the overall shape of the distributions or the relative contribution of subpopulations.

Statistical comparison of automated and manual measurements revealed key differences between the approaches (Table 2). The automated method consistently detected statistically significant differences between forward and reverse directions in all samples (p1 ≤ 0.012, in most cases p1 < 10−6), confirming the robustness of the directional effect. In contrast, the manual method detected significant differences only in selected samples (Samples 1, 2 and 5, while in others no statistically significant differences were detected (p1 > 0.05).

Despite this, the combined zeta potential values obtained by averaging measurements from both directions were comparable between the two methods for all samples (p2 > 0.05). However, a major distinction between the methods was observed in the precision of the estimates. The automated approach, based on analysis of several hundred cells, yielded consistently narrower confidence intervals (Table 2), whereas the manual method, based on a limited number of observations, produced substantially broader intervals. This difference indicates higher variability and reduced reliability of the manual measurements.

Overall, the results demonstrate that RBC populations exhibit structured heterogeneity in zeta potential distributions and that the automated method provides improved sensitivity and precision, particularly in detecting directional effects and resolving subpopulations.

In investigating the impact of cell surface-modifying agents on the zeta potential of RBCs, it was observed that pre-incubation of the cells with the cyclic decapeptide gramicidin S resulted in a 21% reduction in the average zeta potential (Fig. 5). The surface charge of RBCs is predominantly influenced by the negatively charged carboxyl groups of sialic acids, which constitute a component of the carbohydrate structures associated with glycoproteins and glycolipids. Consequently, adsorption of gramicidin S, containing positively charged amino groups derived from ornithine residues, likely results in partial neutralization of the membrane's negative surface charge.

The impact of cell surface-modifying agents on the zeta potential of RBCs (Mean ± SD, n = 6).

It has been demonstrated that treatment with trypsin results in a nearly 40% reduction in the zeta potential of RBCs compared to intact cells, with a measured value of −12.33 ± 0.61 mV (see Fig. 5). Treatment with trypsin leads to the cleavage of peptide bonds formed by the carboxyl group of lysine and arginine. Since trypsin is unable to penetrate the plasma membrane, only the extracellular domains of some proteins are susceptible to proteolysis. Thus, after treatment with trypsin, the surface membrane of RBCs loses part of the sialic acids, which are cleaved together with the extracellular polypeptide chains of some integral glycoproteins, including band 3 and glycophorin A. At the same time, the sialic acids that are part of the glycolipids remain intact.

Treatment of RBCs with neuraminidase has been shown to result in a substantial reduction in zeta potential, exceeding 90% compared to intact cells (see Fig. 4). Neuraminidase is a glycosyl hydrolase that hydrolyzes α2→3- and α2→6-glycosidic bonds of the terminal residues of N-acetylneuraminic acid in oligosaccharides, glycoproteins, and glycolipids. Treatment of RBCs with neuraminidase leads to the loss of most of the negatively charged sialic acids in the glycoproteins and glycolipids of the plasma membrane, which leads to a dramatic decrease in the zeta potential.

Discussion

Analysis of RBC zeta potential distributions obtained using automated trajectory tracking allows several conclusions to be drawn. The main peak, comprising 70–80% of all cells, is well approximated by a normal distribution with a tendency toward asymmetry in the direction of lower zeta potential values. The average zeta potential values of all studied samples, with the exception of sample No. 4, were not statistically different and were predominantly within the range of −18 to −21 mV. The absolute value of zeta potential depends strongly on experimental conditions, including ionic strength, pH, and cell concentration, and therefore may vary between studies. The range of average values of the zeta potential of intact human RBCs obtained by different researchers is from −15 to −37 mV [7,[13], [14], [15]].

In five of the six samples, in addition to the main peak, an additional subpopulation of RBCs with low zeta potential values (Mode −3.3 to −4.4 mV) was observed, accounting for 13–22% of the total population. This subpopulation is most likely associated with aged or damaged RBCs that have partially lost sialic acids during circulation [16]. The presence of such subpopulations highlights the importance of analyzing complete distributions rather than relying solely on averaged values.

Particular attention should be given to the appearance of an additional peak in the high zeta potential region (Mode ≈ −40 mV) in sample No. 2. While most studies report a decrease in RBC zeta potential under pathological conditions, data on factors leading to increased surface charge remain limited. One possible explanation involves redistribution of negatively charged phosphatidylserine to the outer membrane leaflet, which has been reported under specific stress conditions [14]. However, in our study this explanation remains speculative and requires further experimental confirmation.

In all examined samples, a notable shift of the main peak toward lower zeta potential values was observed following a change in the direction of the electric field. This shift varied across different samples, showing an absolute range from 2.3 to 5.5 mV. A plausible explanation for this phenomenon may involve the disruption of the symmetry within the diffuse layer surrounding the cell during electrophoretic movement within the diffuse ionic layer, commonly referred to as the relaxation effect. Consequently, the heterogeneous distribution of low-molecular ions within the diffuse layer generates an internal electric field (Ein) that opposes the external field (Eex). This interaction results in a reduction of the effective external field strength (Eeff), thereby leading to an underestimation of both the electrophoretic mobility and the zeta potential (Fig. 6).

The influence of changing the direction of the electric field on the state of the diffuse layer of ions. A - before closing the electrical circuit; B - after closing the electrical circuit; C - immediately after changing the direction of the electric field.

When the direction of the external electric field is altered abruptly the asymmetry of the diffuse layer reorients with a certain time delay. Initially, the directions of the internal and external electric fields may align, which could result in an apparent overestimation of the zeta potential values measured immediately after the alteration of the electric field direction.

To mitigate the relaxation effect and achieve a more precise determination of the zeta potential of cells, it is recommended to conduct multiple measurements while systematically varying the direction of the electric field. Additionally, averaging measurements obtained in both electric field directions may improve the accuracy of zeta potential determination.

The statistical comparison of automated and manual measurements provides additional insight into the reliability of these observations. The automated method consistently detected statistically significant differences between forward and reverse field directions, confirming the robustness of the directional effect. In contrast, the manual method did not consistently reveal these differences, indicating limited sensitivity. This discrepancy is most likely attributable to the substantially smaller number of analyzed cells and the influence of operator-dependent variability in manual measurements.

Importantly, while the combined zeta potential values obtained by the two methods were in good agreement, the precision of the estimates differed markedly. The automated method yielded consistently narrower confidence intervals, reflecting reduced variability and higher statistical reliability. In contrast, the broader confidence intervals obtained using the manual approach indicate increased uncertainty and reduced ability to detect subtle but systematic effects, such as those associated with diffuse layer relaxation.

Thus, the automated analysis of RBC motion during microelectrophoresis not only improves measurement reproducibility but also enables detection of distributional features and directional effects that are not reliably captured by conventional manual methods. This is particularly important for the identification of RBC subpopulations and for the investigation of subtle changes in membrane surface properties under physiological and pathological conditions.

The effects of cell surface–modifying agents, including trypsin, neuraminidase, and gramicidin S, highlight the potential of this method for using RBC zeta potential as a diagnostic marker of pathological processes. For example, increased trypsin activity in blood plasma was observed in acute and chronic pancreatitis, as well as in chronic renal failure [17,18]. Elevated neuraminidase activity has been reported in mice with type 2 diabetes, in patients with diabetic nephropathy, and in experimental arthritis in rats [[19], [20], [21]]. It can therefore be assumed that all these conditions are accompanied by a decrease in RBC zeta potential.

Changes in erythrocyte zeta potential may also influence blood rheology and microcirculatory perfusion due to altered electrostatic repulsion between cells. Reduction of surface charge promotes erythrocyte aggregation and may contribute to increased blood viscosity and impaired capillary flow. Similar alterations have been described in inflammatory and cardiovascular disorders associated with endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Because the glycocalyx and membrane-associated sialic acids are major determinants of erythrocyte surface charge, zeta potential measurements may indirectly reflect structural and biochemical changes occurring at the cell membrane surface. Altered RBC electrophoretic properties have additionally been associated with cell aging, membrane damage, and eryptosis. Monitoring zeta potential may therefore provide useful information regarding erythrocyte functional state and membrane integrity under pathological conditions. The proposed automated approach may be especially useful for large-scale screening studies requiring analysis of substantial numbers of cells with reduced operator-dependent variability. In addition, automated determination of zeta potential could potentially be applied for monitoring the effects of therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring membrane stability and normal hemorheological properties. Because the method is based on conventional microelectrophoresis recordings, it may also facilitate retrospective analysis of archived experimental datasets. Taken together, these observations suggest that automated assessment of erythrocyte zeta potential may have potential applications not only in basic membrane biology but also in translational and clinical research.

Many pharmacological agents can bind to RBC membranes, leading to disruption of their functions, premature elimination, and hemolysis. Such drugs include neurotropic phenothiazines, quinolones, penicillin and other beta-lactams, as well as certain antihypertensive agents [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. By analogy with the effects observed for gramicidin S, it can be assumed that interactions of these compounds with RBC membranes are accompanied by changes in zeta potential. Consequently, zeta potential measurements may be used to assess the potential toxicity and safety of both existing and newly developed pharmacological agents.

Conclusion

In this study, an automated method for determining the zeta potential of red blood cells based on motion tracking during microelectrophoresis was developed and validated. The proposed approach enables objective, high-resolution analysis of electrophoretic mobility at the single-cell level and allows reconstruction of full zeta potential distributions rather than relying solely on averaged values.

Application of this method revealed pronounced heterogeneity in RBC populations, including the presence of distinct subpopulations with low zeta potential, likely corresponding to aged or damaged cells characterized by reduced membrane negative charge.

A key finding of this work is the systematic dependence of measured zeta potential on the direction of the applied electric field, reflecting the contribution of diffuse layer relaxation effects. The automated approach consistently detected these directional effects, whereas the manual approach demonstrated limited sensitivity. At the same time, although both methods yielded comparable mean zeta potential values, the automated analysis provided substantially narrower confidence intervals and improved statistical reliability. The method also demonstrated high sensitivity to modifications of the RBC surface. Treatment with gramicidin S, trypsin, and neuraminidase resulted in pronounced reductions in zeta potential, supporting the applicability of this approach for studying membrane alterations.

Overall, the developed automated microelectrophoretic analysis represents a robust and reproducible tool for investigating RBC electrokinetic properties. This approach offers clear advantages over conventional manual techniques and holds significant potential for applications in the study of red blood cell aging, membrane damage, drug–cell interactions, and the development of novel biophysical biomarkers relevant to hematological and systemic disorders.
