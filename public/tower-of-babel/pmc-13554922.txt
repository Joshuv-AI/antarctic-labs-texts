Dual-Task Exercises for Dynamic Balance, Motor Function, Functional Mobility, and Dual-Tasking in Individuals With Parkinson’s Disease: A Meta-Analysis With Meta-Regression
Authors: Stanley J Winser, Mohammad Jobair Khan, Abu Jor, Priya Kannan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the (1) effectiveness of dual-task (DT) exercise in improving dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility, dual-tasking, and falls, and (2) influence of DT intervention parameters on treatment effects in individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD). A comprehensive literature search was conducted across 12 electronic databases to identify relevant published articles from inception to September 2025. Risk of bias, methodological rigour, and evidence quality were evaluated using the RoB-2, PEDro, and GRADE tools, respectively. Dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility, dual-tasking, and falls were assessed. Meta-analysis was performed to estimate intervention effects, and meta-regression evaluated how parameters of DT intervention affected treatment effects. The meta-analysis included 25 studies involving 1,162 individuals with PD. The mean PEDro score was 6.5, RoB-2 indicated a low risk of bias in 11 studies (45%), and GRADE indicated low to high evidence quality. Meta-analysis revealed significant improvements in dynamic balance (mean difference [MD]=4.45, 95% confidence interval [CI]=2.06 to 6.84; p<0.001), motor function (MD=-2.00, 95% CI=-3.32 to -0.67; p<0.001), functional mobility (MD=-1.64, 95% CI=-2.01 to -1.26; p<0.001), and dual-tasking (MD=-3.39, 95% CI=-4.67 to -2.11; p<0.001). Meta-regression revealed that dynamic balance improved with moderately challenging and moderate-intensity DT exercises, practised for 2 hours weekly with ≥90% session adherence. Subgroup meta-analyses revealed that practising for more than 4 weeks significantly improved dynamic balance and functional mobility. Compared with traditional single-task exercises, motor–cognitive DT exercises improved dynamic balance, functional mobility, motor function, and dual-tasking. Further studies examining the effects of DT exercises on falls among individuals with PD are warranted.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative condition that often leads to impaired movement, especially gait and balance disorders [1]. Balance deficits and cognitive dysfunction are widely recognised as key risk factors for falls in individuals with PD [2,3]. As PD progresses, balance dysfunction worsens, and the risk of falls increases [4]. However, incorporating repeated simultaneous lower-limb exercise with cognitive training can enhance motor–cognitive integration and reduce the risk of falls [5].

Dual-tasking refers to the simultaneous performance of two tasks, often resulting in compromised performance of one or both due to motor–cognitive interference [6,7]. Dual-task (DT) exercises involve practising two tasks concurrently to improve the ability to manage simultaneous motor and/or cognitive demands. They are broadly classified into motor–cognitive, motor–motor, and cognitive–cognitive exercises [6]. Efficient performance of activities involving dual-tasking requires dividing attention and resources between the two tasks [6]. Unlike the traditional single-task (ST) exercise, DT exercises can train individuals’ ability to divide attention and resources between the two tasks, resulting in improved multitasking and movement coordination alongside cognitive processes [6].

Although recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated the efficacy of DT exercise interventions for improving gait parameters and certain aspects of balance and motor impairment [8-10], a critical research gap remains regarding the comprehensive evaluation of the effects of DT exercises on functional balance, motor function, functional mobility, dual-tasking ability and falls in individuals with PD. Previous systematic reviews have primarily focused on isolated outcomes without thoroughly exploring the influence of key intervention characteristics, such as DT exercise intensity, task challenge, duration, and adherence, on the effectiveness of DT exercises. Recent systematic reviews have reported that DT exercises are safe and effective for improving gait parameters (e.g., walking speed, stride length, and cadence), dynamic balance, functional balance, and motor impairment [8,10]. In addition, one meta-analysis with meta-regression reported factors influencing gait outcomes following DT exercises [10].

Notably, no prior systematic reviews have examined the relationship between the effects of DT exercises and study-level characteristics in the context of balance improvement and fall prevention. Therefore, this meta-analysis with meta-regression aimed to (1) evaluate the effectiveness of DT exercises in improving dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility, dual-tasking ability, and falls in individuals with PD, and (2) explore whether specific treatment parameters and characteristics, such as adherence, task challenge, exercise intensity, and duration, contribute to intervention outcomes in this population.

METHODOLOGY

This meta-analysis with meta-regression was pre-registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024497723) and conducted in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The search syntax and eligibility criteria were guided by the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework.

Search strategy

Systematic searches were conducted across 12 electronic databases, 10 English-language (MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, the Physiotherapy Evidence Database [PEDro], Cochrane Library, SafetyLit, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature [CINAHL], Embase, PubMed, and PsycINFO) and 2 Chinese-language (WanFang Data and China National Knowledge Infrastructure [CNKI]) databases, to identify relevant articles published from inception to September 2025. Two clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) and the reference lists of identified studies and relevant systematic reviews were also manually searched to identify other potentially relevant studies. The search syntax was developed by integrating keywords related to individuals with PD, DT exercises, and balance outcomes using Boolean operators. The search syntax employed for the PubMed database is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Two authors (SJW and MJK) independently screened and selected studies, and disagreements were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. A third reviewer (PK) was consulted for unresolved conflicts.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) randomized controlled trials (RCTs), pilot RCTs, cross-over RCTs, or cluster RCTs; (2) involved individuals with PD who received DT exercises as the primary intervention; (3) the control conditions were ST exercise (active control), usual care, or no intervention; and (4) dynamic balance was evaluated using the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), motor function using Part III of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS-III), functional mobility using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, dual-tasking ability using the DT TUG (dTUG) test, and falls as incidence rate ratios, numbers, or rates using a fall diary. These outcome measures were selected based on previously published recommendations for assessing balance and falls in individuals with PD [12]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) published in languages other than English or Chinese due to the limitation in the language proficiency among the review team members; (2) DT exercises were combined with other interventions such as dance, music, Tai Chi, or brain stimulation; (3) the full text was unavailable; and (4) conference proceedings, systematic reviews, or registered protocols.

Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the PEDro scale. PEDro scores were directly retrieved from the PEDro database (https://pedro.org.au/english/resources/pedro-scale/). When a study was not present in the PEDro database, two authors (SJW and MJK) independently calculated the score using the PEDro scale. Inconsistencies in manual scoring were resolved by discussion. PEDro scores were classified as follows: ≥7, high quality; 5–6, moderate quality; 0–4, low quality [13].

Two authors (SJW and MJK) independently evaluated the risk of bias using the revised Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool (RoB-2), which assesses five domains relevant to methodological quality. The risk of bias in each domain is classified as ‘high,’ ‘some concerns,’ or ‘low.’ Risk of bias was categorised as ‘high’ if one or more domains were ranked as high, as ‘some concerns’ if one or more domains were ranked as some concerns, and as ‘low’ only if all domains were ranked as low [14].

Evidence quality was assessed by two authors (SJW and MJK) using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tool with the GRADE Profiler software. Evidence quality was categorised as high, moderate, low, or very low [15]. As recommended by the GRADE system, indirectness, inconsistency, publication bias, imprecision, and risk of bias were considered to rate the quality of evidence for each outcome measure [15].

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standard tabulation form in Microsoft Excel. One author (MJK) entered the data into Stata software without transformation. For the meta-regression, studies were categorised by sample size, intervention period, PEDro score, PD severity and duration, exercise intensity per week, task challenge, and adherence to the DT intervention.

Data processing and synthesis

Meta-analysis and meta-regression were performed using Stata software (version 17; StataCorp LLC). The mean, standard deviation, and sample sizes of pre–post investigations were extracted for both control and experimental groups. If studies reported medians with minimums and maximums, confidence intervals (CIs), or interquartile ranges, the data were converted to standard deviations. Differences in mean and standard deviation between interventions and from pre- to post-intervention were examined by meta-analysis [16]. Because of high methodological heterogeneity and small sample sizes (<30), a random effects model was used for the meta-analysis [17]. An I2 of >50% was considered high heterogeneity [15].

Multiple subgroup analyses were conducted to increase the robustness of the results. The planned sub-group analyses stratified studies by: (1) sample size (n<30 vs. n≥30), as smaller samples may lack sufficient statistical power to detect significant effects [18]; (2) methodological quality (PEDro score <7 vs. ≥7); (3) DT exercise intensity (same for all participants versus tailored to participants’ ability); (4) intervention provider (physiotherapist, instructor, or studies that did not mention who provided the intervention), given the potential for greater efficacy with physiotherapist-led interventions [19]; (5) DT exercise duration (≤4 vs. >4 weeks); and (6) DT exercise method (technology-mediated vs. conventional without using technology), given the potential for greater benefits with technology-mediated interventions [20,21].

The effects of study-level factors on meta-analysis outcomes were evaluated using univariate meta-regression, including population characteristics (average age), methodological quality (sample size and PEDro score), exercise characteristics (duration: <2, 2–3, and >3 hours per week; intensity: mild, moderate, and high; task challenge: mild, moderate, and high; and adherence rate: excellent, good, and poor), and disease-related variables (PD severity on the Hoehn and Yahr scale, L-dopa equivalent dose [daily mean dose], and PD duration). Exercise intensity was classified according to the metabolic equivalent of task (MET), estimated based on the type of exercise: mild, 1.6–2.9 METs; moderate, 3.0–5.9 METs; and high, ≥6.0 METs [22]. Task challenge was classified according to the number of difficulty factors incorporated into the secondary motor task based on our previous review [23]: The potential factors considered for modifying the task challenge include reducing base of support, disrupting surface, reducing upper limb support, altering centre of gravity and reducing visual feedback among the physical tasks and recall/memory, information processing speed, visual search, verbal fluency, and arithmetic for the cognitive exercise. Exercises were classified as mildly challenging if they included one of the above factors (physical or cognitive), moderately challenging if they included two, and highly challenging if they included three or more. Adherence was classified based on the number of sessions participants attended: ≥90%, excellent; 75%–89%, good; <75%, poor.

Potential publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. Funnel plots were generated by plotting effect sizes against their standard errors at three significance levels (1%, 5%, and 10%) using a two-sided test, and the plots were visually inspected for asymmetry. Egger’s regression test was used to statistically assess small-study effects, with a p-value<0.05 considered indicative of significant publication bias.

RESULTS

Study selection

The database searches identified 5,910 studies, of which 418 were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 25 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis and meta-regression. A PRISMA flow chart of study selection is shown in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Participants’ demographics and studies’ characteristics are reported in Table 1. Data from 25 studies involving 1,162 individuals with PD aged 65.9±3.9 years were included in the meta-analysis and meta-regression. In total, 48 DT intervention trials with varying DT exercise modalities were evaluated across different outcomes. Among the included studies, 11 (11 DT intervention trials: all motor–cognitive) [24-34] investigated dynamic balance using the BBS, 12 (13 DT intervention trials: all motor–cognitive) [33,35-45] investigated motor function using the UPDRS-III, 10 (12 DT intervention trials: 10 motor–cognitive and 2 motor–motor) [24,25,30,31,37,43,46-49] investigated functional mobility using the TUG test, 4 (6 DT intervention trials: all motor–cognitive) [33,44,46,47] investigated dual-tasking ability using the dTUG test, and 1 (1 DT intervention trial: motor–cognitive) [24] investigated falls. The DT intervention trials, including their study-level characteristics across different outcomes, are summarised in Table 2.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

Regarding methodological quality (Table 1), the mean PEDro score was 6.5, indicating moderate overall quality. Regarding risk of bias (Fig. 2), 12 studies (48.0%) had a low risk, 6 (24.0%) had some concerns, and 7 (28.0%) had a high risk. Regarding evidence quality, the GRADE assessments ranged from low to high (Supplementary Table S2).

Effects of DT exercise on dynamic balance

Meta-analysis of 11 DT intervention trials (methodological quality: moderate to high; risk of bias: low to high; evidence quality: high) [24-34] assessing dynamic balance using the BBS revealed significant improvement following motor–cognitive DT exercises compared with ST exercise/active control (mean difference [MD]=4.45, 95% CI=2.06–6.84; p<0.001; I2=40%; Fig. 3A). Each DT exercise lasted 30–90 minutes, with participants receiving 2–5 sessions per week over 4–12 weeks. The meta-regression indicated that DT interventions involving (1) moderately challenging tasks (coefficient [Coeff.]=0.99, 95% CI=0.76 to 1.23; p<0.001; I2=40%), (2) moderate intensity (Coeff.=0.59, 95% CI=0.32 to 0.85; p<0.001; I2=8.6%); (3) lasting for >2 hours per week (Coeff.=0.52, 95% CI=0.00 to 1.04; p=0.040; I2=50%); and (4) ≥90% adherence (Coeff.=0.99, 95% CI=0.74 to 1.24; p<0.001; I2=36%) yielded a significant treatment effect (Table 3).

Effect of DT exercise on motor function

Meta-analysis of 13 DT intervention trials (methodological quality: moderate to high; risk of bias: low to high; evidence quality: low) [33,35-44] assessing motor function using the UPDRS-III revealed significant improvement following motor–cognitive DT exercises compared with control (MD=-2.00, 95% CI=-3.32 to -0.67; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; Fig. 3B). Each DT exercise session lasted 15–90 minutes, and participants received 2–5 sessions per week over 3–10 weeks. The meta-regression indicated that DT exercises involving (1) moderate intensity (Coeff.=0.52, 95% CI=0.09 to 0.94; p=0.010; I2=0.00%) and (2) <75% adherence (Coeff.=-0.44, 95% CI=-0.74 to -0.14; p=0.003; I2=0.00%) yielded a significant treatment effect (Table 3).

Effect of DT exercise on functional mobility

Meta-analysis of 12 DT intervention trials (methodological quality: moderate to high; risk of bias: low to high; evidence quality: high) [24,25,30,31,37,43,46-49] assessing functional mobility using the TUG test revealed significant improvement following DT exercises compared with ST exercise/active control (MD=-1.64, 95% CI=-2.01 to -1.26; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; Fig. 3C). The DT intervention involved motor–cognitive DT exercises in 10 studies [24,25,30,31,37,43,46-49] and motor–motor DT exercises in 2 studies [48,49]. Each DT exercise session lasted 20–90 minutes, and participants received 2–5 sessions per week over 2–12 weeks. The meta-regression indicated that DT exercises involving (1) moderate task challenge (Coeff.=1.36, 95% CI=0.41–2.31; p=0.005; I2=0.07%) had a significant treatment effect (Table 3).

Effect of DT exercise on dual-tasking

Meta-analysis of 6 DT intervention trials (methodological quality: moderate to high; risk of bias: low to high; evidence quality: high) assessing dual-tasking ability using the dTUG test revealed significant improvement following DT exercises compared with ST exercise/active control (MD=-3.39, 95% CI=-4.67 to -2.11; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; Fig. 3D). Due to the low number of studies identified, meta-regression was not conducted. The sub-group analysis revealed DT exercises lasting for ≤4 weeks of intervention delivered by an instructor was associated with significant treatment effects.

Effect of DT exercise on falls

One study involving one DT intervention trial (methodological quality: high; risk of bias: some concerns; evidence quality: high) [24] reported a significantly lower fall incidence with DT exercises than with the control (risk ratio=0.33, 95% CI=0.03–0.62; p=0.030). Participants underwent 30-minute motor–cognitive DT exercise sessions three times per week for 12 weeks, followed by a 24-week follow-up period. Analysis of data from the 192 participants revealed a significant reduction (-67%) in fall incidence following the DT intervention.

Subgroup analysis of DT exercise

In the subgroup analyses (Table 4), high methodological quality (PEDro scores ≥7) was associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=3.10, 95% CI=1.85 to 4.36; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=4), and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-1.97, 95% CI=-2.41 to -1.53; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=8). Tailoring DT exercise intensity to participants’ abilities was associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=6.33, 95% CI=4.47 to 8.20; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=2) and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-1.60, 95% CI=-2.03 to -1.18; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=11). A DT intervention duration of >4 weeks was associated with significant treatment effect on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=3.60, 95% CI=2.66 to 4.55; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=8), and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-1.77, 95% CI=-2.16 to -1.38; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=9). Physiotherapist-led DT exercises were associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=2.18, 95% CI=0.45 to 3.91; p=0.010; I2=39%; n=6), motor function (UPDRS-III: MD=-1.84, 95% CI=-3.48 to -0.21; p=0.030; I2=0.00%; n=9), and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-0.91, 95% CI=-1.50 to -0.32; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=8). Exercise instructor-led DT exercises were associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=4.29, 95% CI=3.30 to 5.28; p=0.001; I2=0.00%; n=8), motor function (UPDRS-III: MD=-1.60, 95% CI=-3.14 to -0.06; p=0.040; I2=0.00%; n=10) and dual-tasking (dTUG: MD=-4.12, 95% CI=-5.67 to -2.58; p<0.001; I2=63.14%; n=3).

Compared with ST exercise/active control, DT exercise interventions were associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=5.07, 95% CI=4.41 to 5.74; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=11), motor function (UPDRS-III: MD=-1.95, 95% CI=-3.35 to -0.55; p=0.010; I2=0.00%; n=10), and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-1.64, 95% CI=-2.01 to -1.26; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=11). Compared with usual care, DT exercise interventions were associated with significant treatment effects on motor function (UPDRS-III; MD=-3.90, 95% CI=-6.93 to -0.87; p=0.010; I2=0.00%; n=2).

Technology-mediated DT exercises were associated with significant treatment effects on dynamic balance (BBS: MD=3.96, 95% CI=2.94 to 4.99; p<0.001; I2=44.78%; n=9), motor function (UPDRS-III: MD=-2.72, 95% CI=-4.35 to -1.09; p<0.001; I2=0.00%; n=5), and functional mobility (TUG: MD=-1.88; 95% CI=-3.59 to -0.17; p=0.030; I2=33.49%; n=4).

Exploration of small-study effects

Small-study effects were examined by sample size (<30 vs. ≥30), language (English vs. Chinese), and geography (by continent). Visual inspection of the funnel plots identified no significant asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. S3), and Egger’s regression test was not significant (BBS: p=0.370; UPDRS-III: p=0.270; TUG: p=0.530), indicating no small-study effect.

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis with meta-regression aimed to explore the benefits and optimal treatment parameters of DT exercises for improving dynamic balance, functional mobility, motor function, and dual-tasking ability, and reducing falls among individuals with PD. To our knowledge, this meta-analysis is the first to include meta-regression to examine the effectiveness of DT exercises and to investigate optimal parameters and characteristics that contribute to its effectiveness on dynamic balance, functional mobility, motor function, and dual-tasking in individuals with PD.

Our meta-analysis revealed significant effects of DT exercise interventions compared with control interventions on dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility, and dual-tasking ability. One high-quality study reported a reduction in falls following DT exercises [24]. Among treatment parameters, DT training for >2 hours per week at either mild or moderate intensity, with moderate task challenge and ≥90% adherence, was associated with a significant improvement in dynamic balance. In addition, DT exercises that were tailored to participants’ abilities or delivered by either physiotherapists or exercise instructor were associated with significant improvements in dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility and dual-tasking compared with DT exercises that were not tailored or delivered by practitioners other than physiotherapists or exercise instructors. Both conventional and technology-mediated DT exercises were effective in improving dynamic balance, motor function, and functional mobility.

Cardinal features in PD, including bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability, directly influence balance in affected individuals [50]. Among individuals with PD, dual-tasking compromises balance due to limited frontal-executive resources [51] and the inability to process two tasks simultaneously, consistent with the bottleneck theory [52]. Activities involving two tasks can reduce gait speed and step width, increase variability and symmetry during walking, and increase gait freezing [53] in individuals with PD [51,54], all of which can adversely affect balance. Moreover, individuals with PD experience difficulties with automatic movement [55] and reduced attentional control [56], which hinder multitasking during DT activities. The improvements in dynamic balance and functional mobility following DT training found in this meta-analysis with meta-regression are consistent with previous studies [8,57,58]. They are attributed to enhanced motor automaticity, as evidenced by reduced slowness in lower-limb movement [55], and improved switching between motor and cognitive tasks following repeated DT practice [57]. Interestingly and importantly, the positive effects of DT practice are achieved without increasing the risk of falls in individuals with PD [54].

The pooled MDs for several outcomes reached thresholds considered clinically meaningful. According to previous studies, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is 5 for the BBS [59], -5 for the UPDRS-III [60], -1.14 for the TUG test [61], and -1.98 for the dTUG test [61] in individuals with PD. Among these four outcomes, the MD in functional mobility, assessed using the TUG test (-1.64), and dual-tasking ability, assessed using the dTUG test (-3.39), met the MCID threshold, providing evidence of clinical meaningfulness for DT exercise interventions. In addition, the MD in dynamic balance, assessed using the BBS (4.45), was close to the MCID threshold, providing marginally clinically meaningful evidence.

The use of the dTUG test is significant for understanding motor–cognitive interference during dual-tasking [62-64]. The estimated DT cost using the dTUG test is of clinical value in early detection of cognitive or motor decline among older adults [65] and in distinguishing between individuals with PD with and without cognitive impairment [66]. Among the included studies, only four assessed the effects of DT exercise interventions using the dTUG test. Therefore, future studies should include DT-specific outcome measures, such as the dTUG test, to assess changes following DT interventions.

An optimum prescription for DT exercise is crucial in clinical and research practice [23]. Our meta-regression highlighted the treatment parameters that led to significant improvements in dynamic balance, motor function, functional mobility, and dual-tasking. Therefore, our recommended treatment parameters are not prescriptive; instead, it is based on associations between treatment parameters and the best possible treatment outcome. Among the included studies, only 9 (36%) delivered the desired treatment regimen for improving dynamic balance, 15 (58%) delivered the desired treatment parameters for improving motor function, 5 (18%) delivered the desired treatment parameters for improving functional mobility, and 13 (50%) delivered the desired treatment parameters for dual-tasking ability. However, the limited number of studies on falls precluded a meta-regression to examine the optimal treatment parameters for reducing falls through DT training.

One included study of high methodological and evidence quality, but with some concerns regarding risk of bias, supported the use of technology-mediated motor–cognitive DT exercise intervention for reducing falls in individuals with PD using virtual sporting activities [24]. Our meta-analysis found technology-mediated DT exercises to lead to significant improvements in dynamic balance, motor function, and functional mobility. Technology-mediated DT exercises offer accessibility and personalised interventions for individuals with PD. However, the cost of technology-mediated DT exercise interventions limits their feasibility [67]. Thus, further studies of high methodological quality with larger sample sizes are warranted to (1) assess the benefits of technology-mediated DT exercises on the risk of falls and (2) develop cost-effective technologies to deliver DT interventions for improving dynamic balance and functional mobility among individuals with PD.

Integrating DT exercise interventions into clinical practice for treating individuals with PD faces multiple challenges, including (1) a lack of standardised treatment parameters guidelines, making it difficult to determine the best approach; (2) the need for one-to-one therapy sessions, posing challenges to their routine delivery in resource limited healthcare settings; and (3) the need to purchase expensive devices to deliver technology-mediated DT exercises, limiting their adoption across different healthcare settings. Despite these challenges, DT exercises offer substantial opportunities to improve patient outcomes and treatment quality. DT exercises enhance functional outcomes by addressing both motor and cognitive impairments. In addition, a personalised DT intervention can be delivered by adjusting task challenge and intensity, enabling its use among a wide range of individuals with PD with varying functional capabilities. Lastly, and more importantly, DT exercise mimics real-life circumstances, such as multitasking. Addressing the deficiency during motor–cognitive interaction enables the easy translation of learned skills into day-to-day tasks, such as safe walking in a crowded walkway, thereby helping to reduce accidental outdoor falls, which are common among individuals with PD.

Our meta-analysis with meta-regression had several strengths. Firstly, it is the first to employ meta-regression to identify treatment parameters and characteristics of effective DT exercise interventions on treatment outcomes (dynamic balance, motor function and dual-tasking) in individuals with PD. Secondly, it employed a robust methodology, including a comprehensive search strategy to identify RCTs and psychometrically sound evaluation tools. Finally, the statistical heterogeneity observed across several outcomes was either negligible (I2=0.00% for motor function and functional mobility) or low (I2=40% for dynamic balance), thereby increasing the reliability of the overall effect and the generalisability of the results [68].

However, our meta-analysis with meta-regression also had several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, it did not conduct subgroup analyses by DT exercise type (motor–cognitive, motor–motor, or cognitive–cognitive) because there were insufficient studies. Thus, the recommended treatment parameters are limited to motor–cognitive DT exercises. Secondly, in studies comparing multiple interventions with a single control, double-counting the control group may introduce bias in the meta-analysis by increasing the control group’s weight, thereby underestimating variability and distorting intervention effects. Thirdly, the statistical heterogeneity in the meta-regression of all study-level variables was ≤50%, which raises concerns about the generalisability of the effect sizes; therefore, the observed effects must be interpreted with caution. However, the statistical heterogeneity in the subgroup analyses was negligible (I2=0.00%), indicating that the subgroup effects are consistent and free of sampling error [69]. Fourthly, the consideration of only studies published in English or Chinese may have introduced language bias. Fifthly, as most included studies recruited community-dwelling ambulant individuals with PD, our findings may not generalise to all individuals with PD. Finally, the exclusion of conference proceedings, unpublished studies, and grey literature may have introduced publication bias.

CONCLUSION

Motor–cognitive DT exercises are effective in improving dynamic balance, functional mobility, motor function, and dual-tasking ability in individuals with PD. Benefits gained in dynamic balance, functional mobility and dual-tasking are clinically relevant. However, further studies evaluating the effects of DT exercises on falls among individuals with PD are warranted. Practising moderately challenging moderate-intensity DT exercises for ≥2 hours per week for >4 weeks with ≥90% adherence was associated with greater improvement in dynamic balance. The benefits of motor–motor and cognitive–cognitive DT exercises remain incompletely understood.
