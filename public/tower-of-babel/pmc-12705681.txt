Simulation-based reservoir analysis assisted by chemical tracers transport for the development of enhanced oil recovery strategies
Authors: Amir Javad Porbar, Hossein Afarideh, Fariborz Rashidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding subsurface fluid flow behavior is essential for optimizing enhanced oil recovery (EOR) strategies in petroleum reservoirs. This study presents a comprehensive tracer-based diagnostic approach combining both aqueous- and gas-phase tracers to characterize pre-EOR water and gas movement in a synthetic reservoir model. Methanol, a conservative aqueous-phase tracer, and perfluoromethylcyclopentane (PMCP), an inert gas tracer, were employed in two separate simulations using the commercial software reservoir simulator. In the water-phase tracer study, methanol was injected to assess water flow dynamics. Tracer recovery ranged from 20% to 29% across four production wells, with water velocities between 0.35 and 0.41 m/d. Breakthrough times varied from 3092 to 4247 days, and mean residence times were estimated between 3900 and 4810 days. The calculated swept pore volume ranged from to . Production Well-2 showed the highest recovery and swept volume, indicating a stronger hydraulic connection with the injection well and making it a suitable candidate for targeted EOR operations. In the gas-phase tracer simulation, PMCP was injected into a gas injection well to evaluate gas flow behavior. Tracer concentration-time curves obtained from four production wells revealed that Production Well-1 exhibited the highest tracer recovery of 38% and the shortest breakthrough time of 796 days, signifying a direct flow path and strong connectivity. Conversely, other wells showed longer residence times up to 1736 days and lower recoveries between 19% and 21%, indicating more complex flow paths and lower sweep efficiency. Differences in swept pore volume among wells underscored the impact of reservoir heterogeneity on gas movement and distribution. The combined results demonstrate the utility of integrating conservative aqueous and inert gas tracers for detailed reservoir characterization. Introduction

Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) utilizes cutting-edge techniques to increase the profitably recoverable oil from reservoirs. Before implementing a field-scale EOR project, a reservoir’s structure and properties have to be understood in detail. Achieving such goals is made possible by numerous tools such as reservoir simulation, seismic study, production log data analysis, and tracer testing. Generally, oil recovery is divided into three stages. Primary recovery alone is capable of recovering approximately 10 to 15% of the original oil in place. Once primary production has run its course, secondary and tertiary recovery methods take over in association with a decline in reservoir pressure. When secondary recovery becomes economically unfeasible, tertiary recovery techniques are applied to optimize oil production1–4. Since EOR methods are often costly, a detailed reservoir analysis must be performed beforehand to maximize production from the producing wells. The objective of this study is to interpret tracer breakthrough curves to analyze fluid movement and estimate key reservoir parameters in an oil field. The declining rate of oil production, together with the economic limitations of conventional recovery methods, has increased the demand for Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) techniques. The heightened global demand for oil has intensified the need for Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) techniques due to the natural decline in production levels over time. These techniques are essential for maximizing oil recovery from mature and depleting fields and for mobilizing residual oil that would otherwise remain trapped. By significantly improving recovery factors, EOR methods ensure that no valuable hydrocarbon resource is left underutilized5. EOR processes incorporate a wide range of chemical and physical methods, each specifically designed to optimize both injection and production efficiency. These techniques are carefully tailored to suit the unique conditions of each reservoir, taking into account factors such as fluid properties, reservoir characteristics, and the underlying geological formations. Traditional EOR methods, including the injection of alkaline solutions, polymers, surfactants, and combinations of these substances, have proven to be particularly effective. They not only enhance sweep efficiency, but also improve reservoir performance by mobilizing trapped oil, thus maximizing hydrocarbon recovery. These methods have been successfully implemented in various oil fields, demonstrating their potential to significantly extend reservoir life and increase overall production rates6. Tracers employed in EOR technologies possess specific characteristics that make them non-reactive, inert, and phase-compatible. Consequently, they do not alter any reservoir fluid properties, allowing precise monitoring of fluid movement without disturbing the natural state of the reservoir. When the optimal pre-injection dilution is achieved, tracers are expected to appear at detectable concentrations in the production wells. This enables the analysis of reservoir dynamics over defined time periods. In EOR applications, tracer technology enhances diagnostic capabilities by providing estimates of swept pore volumes, which help determine fluid distribution within the reservoir. It also facilitates the identification of high-permeability channels with freely flowing fluids, as well as geological boundaries that confine fluids and reduce recovery efficiency. Such data are essential for evaluating the effectiveness of EOR techniques and for designing efficient recovery strategies. Tracers are essential in improving the hydrocarbon recovery rate by providing vital information on reservoir behavior especially in difficult areas where traditional methods do not work7. Chemical tracers are compounds injected in a well for evaluating the flow characteristics of subsurface geology. They are utilized for Single-Well Chemical Tracer Test (SWCTT) or Inter-Well Chemical Tracer Test (IWCTT). In an SWCTT, a tracer is injected into the formation through a well and is later produced from that well. In contrast, in IWCTT, the tracer is introduced together with a carrier fluid (water in waterflooding or gas in gas flooding) through a well into a system peer well. After a certain period, tracer recovery is expected from the production well. The time span for this can be from days to years. Analysis of the tracer injected and tracer produced as well as the breakthrough times gives insightful information about the subsurface conditions such as fluid flow dynamics within the system and estimation and distribution of residual oil saturation Sor. These insights support improved reservoir management and enhanced oil recovery8. Historically, radioactive tracers were the standard in the petroleum industry9, but advancements in chemical tracer technology over recent decades have led to the widespread adoption of chemical tracers. Commonly used chemical tracers include fluorinated benzoic acids for water and perfluorocarbons for gas10–12.

Materials and methods

Theoretical background

Tracer technology is a versatile technique employed to analyze subsurface reservoir conditions. It has wide-ranging applications in evaluating inter-well connectivity, fluid flow pathways, swept reservoir volumes, and other key reservoir parameters13. Moreover, tracer studies play an essential role in identifying and characterizing fluid communication within oil fields. The technology is defined as small identifiable particles which are above the defined threshold level and contains the ability to pass through the production wells. Type of tracers include: (a) radioactive tracers, (b) chemical tracers, and (c) solid particulate tracers (Fig. 1)14. Until recent decades, radioactive tracers were widely regarded as the most effective tracers available. They have played a vital role across the oil industry in detecting subsurface reservoirs and supporting various diagnostic applications. Reservoir evaluation, identification of factors influencing recovery efficiency, and assessment of enhanced oil recovery techniques represent some of their key industrial applications. However, over time, chemical tracers have proven to offer superior advantages. The composition and design protocols of these tracers solely determine their properties and intended uses14.

Classification of tracer types.

In reservoir engineering, chemical tracers such as PMCP (Perfluoromethylcyclopentane) and methanol are increasingly utilized to enhance subsurface fluid monitoring and improve the understanding of reservoir dynamics. These tracers represent a significant advancement in reservoir characterization, particularly through the use of sophisticated monitoring and diagnostic tools that yield highly precise data15. The effectiveness of PMCP as a gas-phase tracer lies in its chemical stability, compatibility with reservoir fluids, and detectability across different phases, allowing it to provide critical insights into porosity, permeability, fluid saturation, and inter-well connectivity—especially in complex, heterogeneous reservoirs. Conversely, methanol functions as a conservative aqueous-phase tracer, enabling detailed assessment of water movement and sweep behavior prior to EOR implementation. Their combined application in simulation studies offers a robust framework for the optimization of EOR strategies based on high-resolution flow diagnostics and spatial analysis of fluid distribution. This study focuses on the application of chemical tracers in both gas and aqueous phases to enhance monitoring of subsurface fluid dynamics. Specifically, PMCP (Perfluoromethylcyclopentane) is employed as a gas-phase tracer due to its well-established stability, inertness, and high detection sensitivity in complex reservoir environments. Tracers like PMCP offer significant advantages, including compatibility with reservoir fluids, minimal chemical reactivity, and the ability to monitor gas flow behavior with high precision across heterogeneous formations. In parallel, methanol is utilized as a conservative aqueous-phase tracer to assess water flow patterns and assist in the evaluation of pre-EOR fluid distribution. To accurately determine the required tracer mass for field-scale injection, a systematic calculation framework is applied for both tracers. These equations consider injection parameters, reservoir geometry, and fluid properties to ensure sufficient tracer detectability at production wells, while maintaining the reliability and resolution of flow diagnostics16,17. The combined use of gas- and water-phase tracers provides a comprehensive assessment of both gas and water flow mechanisms, ultimately enhancing the accuracy of reservoir characterization and the design of effective EOR strategies.

The tracer PMCP is an advanced chemical gas tracer, characterized by its unique molecular structure, high sensitivity, stability under reservoir conditions, and precise detectability. This tracer plays a crucial role in reservoir engineering and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) research. In the reservoir, PMCP is injected alongside a gaseous reference phase through a dedicated gas injection well, ensuring uniform distribution across the formation. This enables high-resolution monitoring and detailed analysis of gas-phase fluid dynamics and reservoir behavior under subsurface conditions. In parallel, methanol, a conservative and non-reactive aqueous-phase tracer, is utilized to characterize water flow pathways. Injected through a water-phase injection well, methanol effectively tracks water movement and supports the identification of preferential flow channels and unswept zones. Following tracer injection, samples are collected at multiple production wells and analyzed using advanced laboratory techniques. The combination of gas-phase (PMCP) and aqueous-phase (methanol) tracers allows for a more comprehensive understanding of multiphase flow behavior in heterogeneous reservoirs. This integrated approach enhances the accuracy of reservoir diagnostics and provides essential insights for optimizing production strategies and improving EOR implementation.

Method of moments

The Method of Moments (MOM) involves deriving the temporal moments from the tracer response curve. Initially developed for closed reactor vessels in the chemical process industry, this technique has since been adapted to more general conditions, including open boundary systems, fractured media under continuous tracer reinjection, and flow geometry estimation18. MOM is grounded in a rigorous mathematical framework and provides valuable additional insights into subsurface dynamics. While it can be used independently, it is also an effective tool for constraining numerical models by defining inter-well volume and flow geometry. The Method of Moments and its applications are primarily based on the analysis of tracer residence time distribution. Tracer particles follow distinct paths through the system, requiring varying amounts of time to travel from the inlet to the outlet. The distribution of these time periods is referred to as the exit age distribution, or RTD of the tracer in the system18. Notably, some modern simulators are capable of modeling tracer adsorption and partitioning. However, the current formulation of the Method of Moments does not account for these factors. Incorporating these properties into numerical simulations can significantly enhance the accuracy of history matching and tracer curve interpolation. The residence time distribution (RTD) method analyses tracer response curves by extracting key time points, helping to characterize porous media during tracer injection and to estimate flow patterns17. This approach relies on studying how long tracer particles stay within the system, using the method of moments. Because tracer particles move along different flow paths, they reach the production well at different times, resulting in a distribution known as the residence time distribution. By applying the method of moments along with proper boundary conditions, it’s possible to accurately estimate the swept pore volume19,20. Simulation methodology

In this study, two separate reservoir models were constructed using advanced simulation software to investigate the injection and transport behavior of both gas-phase and aqueous-phase chemical tracers. These models enabled the independent analysis of fluid movement in gas and water phases under realistic reservoir conditions. For the gas-phase simulation, perfluoromethylcyclopentane (PMCP) was selected as the tracer and injected into the gas reservoir through Injection Well I (Fig. 2). The model was designed to monitor the migration of PMCP toward four production wells (Production Well-1 to Production Well-4) and to evaluate its spatial distribution. This setup allowed for detailed analysis of gas-phase flow dynamics, breakthrough times, and inter-well tracer dispersion patterns. The simulation results highlighted dominant flow paths and the effects of reservoir heterogeneity on tracer transport.

Additionally, the production rates of the wells and the inter-well distances applied in the simulation are provided in Tables 3 and 4, respectively, to ensure the robustness and reliability of the modeling outcomes. In parallel, a separate aqueous-phase reservoir model was developed for the injection and tracking of methanol, a conservative and non-reactive tracer fully miscible with water (Fig. 3). Methanol was injected through a dedicated water injection well, and its movement was monitored across the same four production wells. This simulation focused on water-phase flow behavior, enabling precise calculation of tracer breakthrough times, mean residence times, and estimation of swept pore volumes. The transport patterns observed provided critical insights into water movement and connectivity between wells. The integration of these two models—one for gas-phase PMCP and the other for aqueous-phase methanol—provides a comprehensive diagnostic framework. The findings support enhanced interpretation of subsurface multiphase flow behavior, leading to improved reservoir management, inter-well connectivity assessment, and EOR strategy optimization.

Results and discussion

The numerical simulations conducted on the designed reservoir model (as shown in Fig. 2) provided important insights into tracer transport behavior and fluid flow characteristics. In this study, PMCP was employed as a non-reactive gas tracer and was injected as a single slug into the gas injection well. PMCP tracer was injected as a one-time slug into the gas injection well. The resulting tracer concentration–time profiles were obtained for four production wells, enabling a detailed analysis of inter-well connectivity and flow dynamics. The breakthrough of PMCP at the production wells occurred at different times, indicating varying degrees of connectivity and heterogeneity within the reservoir. Production Well-1 exhibited the earliest breakthrough and the highest tracer concentration, suggesting the presence of a more direct and higher-permeability pathway between the injection and production locations. In contrast, Production Wells 2 through 4 showed delayed tracer responses and lower peak concentrations, reflecting slower transport and potentially more tortuous flow paths. These observations are consistent with the designed spatial heterogeneity of the permeability field, which influences the overall sweep efficiency and residence time of the injected gas. The concentration–time curves (Fig. 4) further highlight differences in tracer recovery and flow regimes among the production wells, confirming the impact of geological features and structural variations on tracer dispersion. These findings highlight the value of tracer-based simulation as a practical approach for assessing inter-well connectivity and detecting unswept or poorly contacted zones. Moreover, the application of PMCP as a gas-phase tracer proved to be a reliable method for characterizing flow patterns and heterogeneities within the reservoir.

The analysis of both water- and gas-phase tracer tests demonstrates that tracer transport is primarily governed by: (i) effective permeability along preferential flow paths, (ii) local porosity providing storage capacity, and (iii) phase-dependent mobility, with gas exhibiting higher mobility than water under the modeled reservoir conditions.

These phase-dependent tracer responses reveal distinct flow domains and connectivity patterns that cannot be inferred solely from production data. In a reservoir with similar heterogeneity and well configuration, such tracer-based diagnostics would provide a quantitative basis for designing efficient EOR strategies. Specifically, gas-injection processes would preferentially communicate with zones linked to Production Well-1, while water-based displacement would be more effective in regions draining toward Production Well-2. Therefore, the dual-tracer methodology not only characterizes flow connectivity for each phase but also directly informs the optimization of EOR operations through selective conformance control, zonal injection planning, and tailored injectant allocation.

Conclusion

This integrated reservoir simulation study, employing both PMCP as a gaseous-phase tracer and methanol as an aqueous-phase tracer, provided a comprehensive understanding of multiphase fluid flow dynamics and inter-well connectivity under realistic reservoir conditions. The PMCP tracer enabled detailed tracking of gas movement, revealing heterogeneous flow paths and significant variability in tracer recovery, breakthrough times, and swept pore volumes across the production wells. Notably, Production Well-1 demonstrated the highest tracer recovery (38%) and earliest breakthrough, indicating a more direct and high-permeability gas pathway. In contrast, Production Well-4 exhibited the lowest recovery and smallest swept volume, reflecting limited gas connectivity and possible geological barriers. Similarly, methanol injection prior to enhanced oil recovery (EOR) operations offered valuable insights into water flow behavior. Among the monitored wells, Production Well-2 achieved the highest methanol recovery (29%) and swept pore volume, suggesting a strong hydraulic connection and favorable water injectivity. Meanwhile, Production Well-3 displayed the weakest response, indicative of lower permeability or more complex water flow paths. The combined application of gas-phase and aqueous-phase tracers effectively captured the spatial variability in reservoir properties, including porosity, permeability, and flow continuity. These findings highlight the critical role of tracer diagnostics in evaluating sweep efficiency, identifying bypassed zones, and guiding optimized well placement and EOR strategy development. Overall, the dual-tracer approach enhances reservoir characterization and supports more informed, data-driven decisions for improved reservoir management and hydrocarbon recovery.
