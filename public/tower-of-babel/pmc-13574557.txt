Evaluation of the Correlation of Calprotectin and SES-CD Score at the Time of Diagnosis and Follow-up After Diagnosis in Pediatric Patients With Crohn’s Disease in Korea
Authors: Yiyoung Kwon, Seo Hee Kim, Yoo Min Lee, Eun Sil Kim, Eun Hye Lee, Sujin Choi, Byung-Ho Choe, Hyo-Jeong Jang, In Sook Jeong, Juyoung Kim, Dae Yong Yi, Soon Chul Kim, Yeoun Joo Lee, So Yoon Choi, Yoon Lee, Yon Ho Choe, Ben Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrohn’s disease (CD) requires reliable tools for monitoring intestinal inflammation. Fecal calprotectin (FC) has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker. This study aimed to evaluate the correlation between FC and endoscopic activity in pediatric CD confined to the colon and to establish FC cutoff values corresponding to disease severity and remission.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, retrospective, cross-sectional study including 154 pediatric patients (< 19 years) with Paris classification L2, B1 CD from 15 centers in Korea (2017–2022). Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease (SES-CD) and FC were assessed simultaneously at diagnosis and follow-up.ResultsAt diagnosis, the median FC was 1,355.0 mg/kg and SES-CD 14.0, with 39.6% classified as severe. At follow-up, the median FC was 126.0 mg/kg and SES-CD 2.0, with 50.7% achieving remission. FC correlated most strongly with SES-CD (r = 0.56, P < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed acceptable diagnostic accuracy (area under the ROC curves of 0.785 at diagnosis and 0.738 at follow-up). Optimal FC cutoffs were 1,585.0 mg/kg for predicting severe disease (sensitivity 73.8%, specificity 74.2%) and 195.0 mg/kg for predicting remission (sensitivity 78.2%, specificity 61.8%).ConclusionFC strongly correlates with endoscopic activity in pediatric colonic CD, surpassing clinical indices and serum markers. The identification of clinically meaningful cutoff values may reduce the need for repeated endoscopy and support the use of FC as a surrogate marker in real-world pediatric practice.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), represented primarily by Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis, is characterized by chronic, relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract. Among these, CD is more common in children and can involve any part of the gastrointestinal tract from the mouth to the anus, leading to impaired nutrient absorption and diarrhea.1 IBD requires lifelong management and has a profound impact on patients’ quality of life. Early detection of intestinal inflammation and appropriate monitoring are therefore critical in the management of CD.

While gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and dyspepsia can reflect intestinal inflammation, they are subjective and have limitations. Serum inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate are widely used, but their sensitivity and specificity for assessing CD activity remain suboptimal. At present, endoscopy is considered the gold standard for assessing intestinal inflammation2; however, it is costly, invasive, and not suitable for frequent monitoring.3 Consequently, there is a need for simple, rapid, minimally invasive, and effective biomarkers. In recent years, fecal calprotectin (FC) has emerged as a promising tool.456

Several studies have demonstrated that FC correlates well with endoscopic and histologic disease activity in IBD.78 In adult patients, FC has been shown to correlate strongly with the Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease (SES-CD). Using a cutoff of 70 mg/kg, FC distinguished endoscopically active IBD with a sensitivity of 89%, specificity of 72%, and overall accuracy of 87%.9 A recent study in pediatric CD also demonstrated a significant correlation between FC and SES-CD (r = 0.428, P = 0.001).10 However, many previous studies have raised concerns about whether FC reliably reflects inflammation in the small bowel.1112 A systematic review evaluating the sensitivity of FC according to different disease locations in CD reported that the sensitivity of FC ranged from 42.9% to 100% in small bowel involvement and from 66.7% to 100% in large bowel involvement, suggesting that FC may more reliably reflect colonic disease activity.13

The incidence of CD is increasing not only in Western countries but also in Asia, including Korea.141516 In Korean children and adolescents with CD, ileocolonic involvement (L3) is the most common phenotype (approximately 60–70%), whereas isolated small bowel (L1, 15–20%) or colonic (L2, 10–15%) disease is less frequent.16171819 Although the proportion of colonic CD was relatively low in children, considering that FC is less reliable in reflecting small-bowel disease activity, we aimed to evaluate the role of FC as a potential surrogate for endoscopy in clinical practice; therefore, we included only pediatric patients confined to the large bowel.

METHODS

Patients and study design

This study was a multicenter retrospective cohort study with paired assessments, and it conformed to the institutional review board guidelines. Children and adolescents under 19 years of age who were diagnosed with CD and had documented SES-CD scores and FC levels obtained simultaneously at both diagnosis and follow-up were included from fifteen participating centers. A total of 154 patients were enrolled in this study between June 2017 and May 2022. Only patients whose disease status at diagnosis corresponded to L2 (colonic disease) and B1 (non-stricturing, non-penetrating behavior) according to the Paris classification were included. FC measurement and endoscopic evaluation were performed on the same day or within 3 days of each other and were therefore considered simultaneous assessments.

At diagnosis, data collection included patient demographics, Pediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (PCDAI) scores, and FC levels. Endoscopic disease activity was evaluated using SES-CD. During follow-up, information on PCDAI, FC levels (including unit consistency and maximum values), and endoscopic outcomes—assessed by SES-CD—was also obtained. Each patient underwent a single follow-up endoscopy, which was performed mostly around 12 months after diagnosis. Some patients, however, had their procedures delayed by approximately 3–4 months to accommodate scheduling during school vacations. Endoscopic examinations or FC measurements performed in the presence of fever or suspected infection, which could act as potential confounding factors, were excluded from the analysis. For the determination of FC cutoff values, the severity of disease was categorized according to the conventional SES-CD classification: remission (< 3), mild (3–6), moderate (7–15), and severe (> 15).20 FC was analyzed using an automated immunoassay at all participating centers, and the upper limit of measurement was uniformly set at 6,000 mg/kg.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between FC levels and endoscopic disease activity, as assessed by the SES-CD, at diagnosis and during follow-up in pediatric patients with CD diagnosed before age 19 in Korea. The secondary objective was to determine FC cutoff values corresponding to disease severity categories (mild, moderate, and severe) defined by the SES-CD.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges, whereas categorical variables were summarized as absolute numbers with corresponding percentages. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the associations between FC and SES-CD. The diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) reported. The optimal cut-off values were determined using Youden’s J statistic. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

Ethics statement

The Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of Inha University Hospital approved the review of patient medical records and the procedures for the laboratory tests and imaging studies (File No.: 2023-12-041). Appropriate written consent to participate in the study was obtained from each patient.

RESULTS

Patient’s clinical characteristics

The clinical characteristics of the patients at diagnosis of CD and at follow-up endoscopy are summarized in Table 1. The patients had a median age of 14.6 years at diagnosis. Among the 154 patients included in the study, 105 (68.2%) were male. The median FC level at diagnosis was 1,355.0 mg/kg, whereas the median level at follow-up was 126.0 mg/kg. At diagnosis, the median SES-CD was 14.0. A total of 61 patients (39.6%) had severe disease, defined as an SES-CD greater than 15. At follow-up endoscopy, the median SES-CD was 2.0. A total of 78 patients (50.7%) achieved endoscopic remission. Because the inclusion criteria for this study were limited to patients with Paris classification L2 and B1, the variables with distinct distributions among enrolled patients were age and growth parameters (Table 1).

Calprotectin values corresponding to endoscopy

In patients with the L2, B1 disease phenotype, a strong correlation was identified between SES-CD and FC. Based on this finding, we evaluated the role of FC in predicting severe disease at diagnosis and endoscopic remission at follow-up (Figs. 1 and 2). In the ROC analysis performed on all patients at diagnosis, the AUC was 0.785 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.712–0.858; P < 0.001), indicating an acceptable diagnostic accuracy. The optimal cut-off of FC for predicting severe disease status was identified as 1,585.0 mg/kg with a sensitivity of 73.8% and a specificity of 74.2%. In the ROC analysis performed on all patients at follow-up, the AUC was 0.738 (95% CI, 0.660–0.817; P < 0.001), also indicating an acceptable diagnostic accuracy. The optimal cut-off for FC in predicting endoscopic remission was 195.0 mg/kg, with a sensitivity of 78.2% and a specificity of 61.8%.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the correlation between FC and endoscopic findings in patients with CD confined to the colon, without small bowel involvement. The purpose was to determine whether FC alone could serve as a reliable marker for monitoring disease activity in situations where frequent endoscopic examinations are impractical.

An important finding of our study is that, in CD patients with colonic involvement only, FC demonstrated the strongest correlation with SES-CD, surpassing subjective symptom scores and conventional blood markers. Although numerous studies have evaluated the association between disease activity, as assessed by the SES-CD, and FC, most of them have been conducted in adult patients with CD.9122122 Among pediatric patients, only the study conducted by Akutko et al.10 has addressed this topic. However, their cohort included only 54 pediatric patients with CD, and the sample size was smaller than in our study. Such a limited number of patients may have contributed to the slight differences in results, which may explain why the correlation coefficient between SES-CD and FC reported in their study (r = 0.428) was lower than that observed in ours (r = 0.560).

Another important finding of our study is that we identified cutoff values of FC for predicting severe disease at diagnosis and endoscopic remission at follow-up. We found that patients with an FC level of 1,585.0 mg/kg or higher at diagnosis were in a state of severe disease, defined as an SES-CD score above 15. At follow-up, an FC level of 195.0 mg/kg or lower was indicative of endoscopic remission. In adult cohorts with colonic CD, FC has been reported as a useful predictor of endoscopic activity, suggesting a diagnostic cutoff value of FC. Reported cutoff values of 100–200 mg/kg were able to predict endoscopic activity with high sensitivity but modest specificity.11 The fact that no prior studies have established these cutoff values in pediatric cohorts highlights the unique contribution and clinical relevance of our study. The identification of these cutoff values may help reduce the need for repeated endoscopic examinations during follow-up in pediatric patients.

One limitation of this study is the possible inter-laboratory variation in FC measurement due to the use of different assay kits and analytical platforms among centers. However, because all centers used validated automated quantitative immunoassays and the upper measurement limit was uniformly set at 6,000 mg/kg, the impact of this variability on the overall results is likely to be minimal. Another limitation of our study arises from the very design that we adopted to highlight its strengths. Specifically, we restricted our analysis to patients with colonic CD. While this design enhanced the validity of our findings within this subgroup, it also limits the generalizability of our results to the broader population of patients, particularly those with small bowel involvement, who represent a substantial proportion of CD cases. Nevertheless, this restriction can also be considered a strength. Because stricturing or penetrating complications are relatively uncommon in pediatric CD, our findings in patients with colonic CD may have direct clinical implications. The proposed approach could help reduce the frequency of endoscopic examinations required for follow-up in real-world clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that FC is strongly correlated with endoscopic disease activity in pediatric patients with CD confined to the colon, surpassing conventional clinical indices and blood markers. Importantly, we established FC cutoff values that may predict severe disease at diagnosis (1,585.0 mg/kg) and endoscopic remission at follow-up (195.0 mg/kg), providing clinically meaningful thresholds that have not previously been reported in pediatric cohorts. These findings suggest that FC may serve as a useful surrogate for endoscopy in pediatric colonic CD.
