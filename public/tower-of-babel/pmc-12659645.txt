CLIC3 emerges as a novel prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer through integrated multi-omics analysis
Authors: Shuai Yuan, Jiangxia Li, Weimei Liu, Meitian Ren, Lianyu Lin, Shuangying Hao, Lina Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains clinically challenging due to its aggressiveness, limited targeted therapies, and absence of reliable prognostic biomarkers. Identifying TNBC-specific molecular drivers is critical for improving patient outcomes.MethodsWe employed an integrated multi-omics approach to analyze tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 4 TNBC and 4 non-TNBC patients via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and whole-exome sequencing (WES), complemented by an extensive analysis of 115 TNBC and 976 non-TNBC samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Prognostic screening was performed to identify potential biomarkers correlated with patient survival. In vitro experimental validation was carried out in MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells, and immunohistochemistry was used to confirm protein expression levels.ResultsWeighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and comparative WES analysis revealed that TNBC exhibited unique transcriptional modules enriched in DNA replication and heterochromatin organization, distinct somatic mutation landscapes, and alternative splicing patterns. Prognostic screening identified chloride intracellular channel 3 (CLIC3) as the top risk factor for poor survival. In vitro experimental validation showed that CLIC3 knockdown significantly inhibited proliferation (CCK-8, colony formation , invasion and migration in MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells. Immunohistochemistry assay confirmed significantly higher CLIC3 protein expression in TNBC versus non-TNBC tissues.ConclusionsCLIC3 is a TNBC-specific prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Its inhibition impedes TNBC aggressiveness, providing a foundation for targeted therapy development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15205-6.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, RNA-seq, Whole-exome sequencing, Prognostic biomarkers, CLIC3

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) is a type of cancer that originates in the breast tissue and is characterized by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in the breast [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosed in women, accounting for the majority of all new cancer cases in females [2]. It is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women as well. The risk of developing BRCA is influenced by various factors, including age, gender, genetic predisposition (such as mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes), hormonal factors, reproductive history, and lifestyle factors like diet and alcohol consumption [1, 3–6]. According to the expression of specific receptors and proteins in tumor cells, BRCA can be classified into several subtypes, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), hormone receptor (HR) positive/HER2-negative (Luminal A and Luminal B), HER2-positive, hormone receptor positive/HER2-positive (ER+/PR+/HER2+), inflammatory breast cancer (IBC), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS), Paget’s disease of the nipple (PDN) [7, 8]. The classification of breast cancer into these subtypes is crucial for determining the most effective treatment strategies and for providing information about the prognosis.

Among these subtypes, TNBC is generally more aggressive and has a higher risk of recurrence compared to other subtypes mainly due to the lack of targetable receptors (ER, PR, HER2), high tumor mutational burden, treatment resistance [9]. Understanding the unique characteristics of TNBC is essential for the development of new therapeutic strategies. Recent advances in targeted strategies for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) are centered around the development of therapies that exploit the molecular vulnerabilities of TNBC, such as PARP inhibitors targeting DNA repair mechanisms, immunotherapies to enhance anti-tumor immune responses, and combination treatments using chemotherapy, monoclonal antibodies, and antibody-drug conjugates [10–13].

Identification of potential biomarkers is crucial for understanding the molecular underpinnings of TNBC’s aggressiveness and exploring novel treatments [14, 15]. The previous study identified USP15 as a crucial factor in TNBC progression by stabilizing PARP1, which enhanced DNA repair mechanisms and tumor cell survival. The research suggested that the absence of hormone receptors in TNBC contributed to PARP1 overexpression and implied that targeting USP15 could be a potential therapeutic strategy, especially in enhancing the efficacy of PARP inhibitors [16]. Recent study revealed that EMSY, a protein overexpressed in TNBC, enhanced tumor stem cell properties, and disruption of EMSY’s role in methionine metabolism combined PARP inhibitor and methionine deprivation therapy could target cancer stem cells and improve patient prognosis [17]. Several potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets have been identified, such as PD-L1 for immunotherapy, BRCA1/2 mutations for PARP inhibitors, and Trop-2 for antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) [18]. The previous study identified SNORD33, a small nucleolar RNA, as a potential biomarker for predicting the efficacy of platinum-based chemotherapy in TNBC [19]. Additionally, the exploration of TGF-β inhibitors, Notch signaling pathway inhibitors, and STAT3 signaling pathway inhibitors highlights the ongoing efforts to identify new therapeutic approaches for TNBC [10]. In this study, we integrated RNA sequencing, whole-exome sequencing and public data analysis to compare TNBC with non-TNBC, and our research aimed at exploring new therapeutic targets for TNBC. Crucially, we identified CLIC3 as a new potential biomarker for TNBC, which was specifically upregulated and correlated with patient prognosis in TNBC. This study aims to enhance our understanding of the disease’s molecular intricacies, leading to more precise and effective treatment strategies, and to reveal novel vulnerabilities that may be targeted therapeutically.

Materials and methods

Clinical cohorts

A set of paired tumors and normal breast tissue derived from 8 patients was used in this study. The clinical features, as well as the tumor pathological diagnoses which were independently confirmed by two pathologists were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Samples were obtained from the patients undergoing initial total resection of breast cancer from June 2023 to December 2023 in the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Polytechnic University. The samples were stored at −80℃.

Ethics approval statement

The current study was conducted in accordance with both the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and the rules of China government, which was reviewed then approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Polytechnic University (KY2020-11-002). The authors are accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved.

Patient consent statement

All patients signed an informed consent approved by the institutional Review Board.

RNA sequencing library Preparation

Total RNA was used as input material for the RNA sample preparation. Briefly, mRNA was extracted from the total RNA using magnetic beads that had poly-T oligos attached to them. The mRNA was then subjected to fragmentation facilitated by divalent cations at an increased temperature within a First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5×). For the synthesis of the first strand of cDNA, a random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase devoid of RNase H activity were employed. The creation of the second strand of cDNA was performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. The residual overhangs on the DNA fragments were transformed into blunt ends with the help of exonuclease and polymerase enzymes. Subsequently, the 3′ ends of the DNA fragments were adenylated, and adapters with a hairpin loop were attached to prepare the fragments for hybridization. To isolate cDNA fragments that were predominantly 370 to 420 base pairs in length, the AMPure XP system was used to purify the library fragments. PCR amplification was then conducted using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase in conjunction with Universal PCR primers and an Index (X) Primer. Finally, the PCR products were subjected to purification using the AMPure XP system, and the library’s quality was determined with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

Whole-exome sequencing library Preparation

Genomic DNA was prepared for exome sequencing using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit with the following steps: Initially, 0.4 µg of high-quality genomic DNA was randomly sheared to a mean size of 180–280 base pairs using a Covaris S220 sonicator. Any remaining DNA overhangs were made blunt by exonuclease and polymerase treatment. Subsequently, the DNA fragments underwent end repair and phosphorylation, followed by the addition of a single ‘A’ base (A-tailing) and ligation with paired-end adaptors at the 3′ ends. Fragments with adaptors on both ends were amplified by PCR. Post-PCR, the library was hybridized with biotinylated probes in solution and then captured using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads to selectively enrich for gene exons. The captured libraries were subjected to an additional PCR to incorporate index tags, preparing them for sequencing. The PCR products were purified with the AMPure XP system, and DNA concentration was determined using a Qubit®3.0 Flurometer. The library size distribution was assessed with NGS3K/Caliper, and the library was quantified by real-time PCR to a final concentration of 3 nM. Ultimately, the DNA library was sequenced on an Illumina platform to achieve paired-end 150 base pair reads.

Cell culture and construction of CLIC3 knockdown TNBC cell lines

The human TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 was derived from ATCC and cultured in an incubator containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. To construct a stable CLIC3 knockdown system, two short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting the human CLIC3 mRNA sequence were inserted respectively into the lentiviral vector pLKO.1-puromycin (Supplementary Table 2). The plasmids were co-transfected with the packaging vectors psPAX2 and pVSVG at a ratio of 10:7:3 into HEK293T cells and harvested at 48 and 72 h respectively. The harvested supernatant was used to infect MDA-MB-231cells with polybrene (10 µg/mL) for 2 days. Subsequently, the target cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 7 days to establish new stable cell lines [20, 21].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from the cells was extracted using the TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and then was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, , China). qPCR was performed with FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Rox) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System [20, 22]. All reactions were set triplicate duplications and were calculated by the comparative threshold method (2–ΔΔCt). The sequences of primers for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Colony formation assay

Cells were evenly plated into 6-well plates (1000 cells per well) and cultured for 14 days at 37℃ with 5% CO2. Each experiment was repeated three times. The cell culture medium was changed every two days. The colonies were fixed with methanol at room temperature for 15 min, stained with crystal violet (0.1% w/v) at room temperature for 1 h, and then counted.

Western blot

Cells were lysed on ice for 15 min using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing the protease inhibitor phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Beyotime). Then, cell lysates were ultrasonicated for 10 s. After centrifugation for 10 min at 4℃, the supernatant was collected to measure the concentration using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein samples were isolated using a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), which was then blocked with 5% skimmed milk in 1×TBST (Tris buffered saline-Tween)for 1 h before incubation with primary antibodies (1:1000) overnight at 4 ℃ and secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was washed with 1×TBST three times after each antibody was used. Protein levels were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Merck Millipore) [20, 22].

CCK-8 cell viability assay

Cells were seeded at 1 × 103/well in a 96-well plate with three replicates. After culturing for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was mixed with 100 µL fresh medium and added to each well. Then the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37℃ with 5% CO2 [23]. Cell proliferation was analyzed by measuring the absorption at 450 nm.

Transwell assay

Transwell assay was carried out to assess cell migration and invasion. Matrigel was used to coat the upper chamber of the well in the invasion experiment. A total of 3 × 104 cells(migration)or 8 × 104 cells (invasion) per well in the upper chamber with serum-free DMEM medium (200 µL), while the lower chamber contained 600 µL DMEM medium with 10% FBS. Each experiment was repeated three times. Next, the migrated or invaded cells were fixed with methanol for 15 min and then stained by 0.1% crystal violet for 1 h. The cells were photographed under an inverted microscope.

Wound healing assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were plated in a 6-well plate with 5 × 105 cells per well. When cells grew to 100% confluence, a 200 µL pipette tip was used to make a straight wound in each well, and the cells were washed with 1×PBS. Then DMEM (without FBS) was added, and images of the same position were taken by a inverted microscopy at 0, 24 h and 48 h after wounding. Each experiment was repeated three times [24].

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE breast cancer samples from 8 patients (received tumor resection from 2022 to 2023) were selected and subdivided into 2 subgroups according to the molecular subtype classification. The TNBC subgroup consisted of 4 samples, and the other 4 Non-TNBC were set as control. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the 1: 100 diluted CLIC3 antibody (ab128941, Abcam; Cat. No. EPR8243) in conjunction with the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Roche; Cat.No. 760 − 700). Except sectioning, all steps were automatically completed on the Benchmark IHC system (Roche). All procedures were carried out in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

RNA-seq analysis

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using fastp [25] (v0.11.9), which removes adapter sequences, trims low-quality bases, and discards reads shorter than 50 bp. High-quality clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg38, GENCODE version 34) using HISAT2 [26] (v2.1.0), and alignment files were processed and indexed with SAMtools [27] (v1.17).

Transcript assembly and abundance estimation were performed using StringTie [28] (v2.2.1), and raw read counts per gene were extracted with featureCounts [29] (v2.0.6). For downstream analyses, only uniquely mapped reads were retained. Expression levels were normalized as raw counts for differential expression analysis, while fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM) values were used for visualization.

For the public cohort, RNA-seq expression matrices (generated on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform) and matched clinical metadata of 115 TNBC, 976 non-TNBC, and 113 normal breast tissue samples were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://genome-cancer.ucsc.edu/) on March 23, 2022. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using the DESeq2 [30] package (v1.38.0). Genes with an adjusted p-value (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC| >1) were considered statistically significant. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing was performed using the “survminer” package (v0.5.0).

Alternative splicing analysis

Aligned RNA-seq reads in BAM format, generated by HISAT2 [26] (v2.1.0) and processed with SAMtools [27] (v1.17), were subjected to splicing event quantification using vast-tools [31] (v2.5.1). Vast-tools calculates the Percent Spliced In (PSI) value for each annotated event, providing a quantitative measure of exon inclusion. Events with low coverage (fewer than 10 informative reads) or marked as “VLOW” in the vast-tools quality score were excluded to ensure reliability. We focused on four major categories of AS events, including cassette exon (CEx), intron retention (IR), alternative 5′ donor site (ALT5), and alternative 3′ acceptor site (ALT3). To identify differentially spliced events between groups, stringent thresholds were applied: absolute change in PSI (|ΔPSI|) ≥ 10 and a minimum PSI range of 5 across samples. These criteria minimize false positives while capturing biologically meaningful splicing alterations.

Whole-exome sequencing analysis

Raw paired-end reads were subjected to quality control using fastp [25] (v0.11.9) to remove adapter sequences, reads with >10% ambiguous bases, and low-quality bases. Only high-quality reads were retained for downstream analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, release v34) using BWA [32] (v0.7.17-r1188), followed by sorting and indexing with SAMtools [27] (v1.17). PCR duplicates were marked, and base quality score recalibration was performed according to the GATK best practices workflow.

Somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were identified using the GATK MuTect2 [33] (v4.4.0.0) pipeline with default settings. A matched normal sample panel and standard filtering criteria were applied to reduce sequencing artifacts and germline contamination. Functional annotation of the detected mutations was performed with Maftools [34] (v2.24.0), which also enabled visualization of mutational profiles, mutational signatures, and summary statistics.

To identify putative driver genes, we separately analyzed TNBC and non-TNBC cohorts using MutSigCV [35] (v1.3.01), which estimates background mutation rates while accounting for gene-specific covariates such as expression level and replication time. Genes with significant q-values after false discovery rate (FDR) correction were considered candidate driver genes for each subtype.

Weighted gene co-expression analysis (WGCNA)

To explore the co-expression patterns among differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we applied the WGCNA R package (v1.71) [36]. A total of 4,287 DEGs were used as input after variance-stabilizing transformation and removal of genes with excessive missing values. Pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients were first calculated to generate a similarity matrix, which was subsequently transformed into an adjacency matrix using a power adjacency function. To satisfy the scale-free topology criterion (scale-free R² >0.85), a soft-thresholding power of β = 3 was selected. The adjacency matrix was further converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), which considers not only the direct connections between genes but also their shared neighbors, thereby enhancing network robustness. Genes were hierarchically clustered based on TOM dissimilarity, and modules were identified using a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm with a minimum module size of 50 genes. Similar modules were subsequently merged if their eigengene correlation exceeded 0.75. Module eigengenes (the first principal component of each module) were calculated to summarize the expression pattern of modules, and their associations with clinical traits or phenotypic variables were assessed using Pearson correlation. Modules showing absolute correlation coefficients |R| ≥ 0.3 with adjusted p < 1E-10 were considered significantly associated and selected for further functional enrichment and hub gene analyses.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

The R package clusterProfiler [37] (v4.16.0) from the bioconductor (v3.15) project was used for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, and the adjusted p-value less than 0.05 was considered as the statistical significance. The results were visualized using ClueGO [38] (v2.5.8) and CluePedia [39] (v1.5.8) plugins in Cytoscape (v3.6.1) software.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 [40] was used for statistical analyses. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD)/standard error of the mean (SEM). Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA were performed to analyze differences between groups. P-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Gene deployment and expression in TNBC

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is generally considered to be more aggressive compared to other subtypes of breast cancer (BRCA) mainly due to lack of targeted treatment options, higher grade and proliferation rates, and increased risk of metastasis. To investigate the gene deployment and expression regulation of TNBC, the expression matrix consisting of 115 TNBC and 11 adjacent normal samples was downloaded from the TCGA database. By performing differential expression analysis, we identified 2709 up-regulated genes and 1578 down-regulated genes (Fig. 1A). To further characterize the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were conducted. The up-regulated genes were significantly enriched with the functions related to immune response, such as ‘regulation of leukocyte activation’, ‘lymphocyte activation’, ‘primary immunodeficiency’, ‘antigen processing and presentation’ (Fig. 1B, D). Besides, the expressions of genes associated with cell cycle, such as ‘mitotic cell cycle process’, ‘DNA-templated DNA replication’, were also significantly increased in the TNBC samples. The results suggested that upregulation of cell cycle genes in TNBC may accelerate cell division, reduce cell death, and consequently accumulate genetic alterations, leading to the rapid proliferation and progression of TNBC. For the down-regulated genes, the functional enrichment analysis revealed the significant enrichment with cell communication, development, and metabolism, such as ‘regulation of cell communication’, ‘circulatory system development’, ‘nucleic acid metabolic process’, ‘focal adhesion’ (Fig. 1C, E). Cell communication is essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and coordinating various cellular processes, implying that dysregulation of cell communication may result in uncontrolled cell growth, evasion of cell death, and enhanced invasive properties. Taken together, we discovered a group of DEGs associated with overactivation of immune response, dysregulation of cell cycle and cell communication, and all of these are hallmarks of TNBC.

WGCNA revealed specific gene expression pattern in TNBC

To identify genes uniquely characteristic of TNBC, we compared the transcriptional profiles of TNBC with those of other BRCA subtypes, including human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positive (HER2+), Luminal A, Luminal B, and the less common subtypes (others). The gene expression matrix consisting of 115 TNBC, 37 HER2+, 361 Luminal A, 98 Luminal B and 477 others was retrieved from TCGA databases. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was employed to delineate gene expression patterns across distinct breast cancer subtypes. To refine the gene set, we included only those genes differentially expressed in at least one breast cancer subtype when compared to adjacent normal samples. The optimal soft threshold (power) of 3 was selected to construct a co-expression network (Fig. S1). Adjacencies among DEGs were assessed and depicted in a hierarchical clustering tree based on the Topological Overlap Measure (TOM). Employing the Dynamic Tree Cut Merged Dynamics algorithm, module eigengenes (MEs) with similarities above 0.75 were consolidated to simplify the network complexity (Fig. S2A). In total, 11 modules were identified, and the dendrogram and heatmap based on the eigengenes revealed two distinct clusters among these modules (Fig. S2B, C). Then, the correlations between the 11 MEs and five subtypes of BRCA were evaluated. As a result, four modules containing 2047 genes (MEbrown with R = 0.5 and p value = 7e-69, MEpurple with R = 0.4 and p value = 1.3e-42, MEred with R = 0.37 and p value = 4.4e-36, MEturquoise with R = 0.41 and p value = 1e-45) were significantly positively correlated with TNBC (TNBC-positive modules) and two modules including 338 genes (MEgreenyellow with R = −0.48 and p value = 2.2e-63, MEyellow with R = −0.49 and p value = 1.6e-67) were significantly negatively correlated with TNBC (TNBC-negative modules) (Fig. 2A). The gene significance (GS), an efficient measure quantifying the relationship between the expression profile of an individual gene and a particular phenotype or trait of interest, was calculated for each module with respect to TNBC. Consistently, the six modules related to TNBC showed the higher GS values than other modules (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the GS values and the module membership (MM) values, a method quantifying the correlation between the expression profile of an individual gene and a specific module within the gene co-expression network, displayed strong correlation in the six modules (Fig. 2C, D). In summary, the six modules showed a significant correlation with TNBC, and the genes within these modules exhibited distinct expression patterns in the TNBC samples.

Additionally, GO enrichment analysis of genes associated with TNBC-positive modules indicated significant enrichment in DNA replication and heterochromatin, suggesting the dysregulation of cell cycle and chromatin accessibility in TNBC (Fig. 2E). Genes within the TNBC-negative modules were significantly enriched in cilium-related and microtubule-based movement processes, suggesting potential disruptions in intercellular communication and signal transduction in TNBC (Fig. 2F). Moreover, the previous studies have reported that dysregulation of cilia genes may disrupt normal cilia function and cellular signaling pathways, potentially contributing to tumor development and progression [41–45].

Whole exome sequencing revealed distinct somatic mutations in TNBC

As a powerful tool for exploring the genetic basis of cancers, whole-exome sequencing (WES) has been utilized to dissect the somatic mutations and genetic alterations driving tumor development and progression. In this study, we conducted whole-exome sequencing (WES) on 8 breast cancer (BRCA) samples, comprising 4 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and 4 non-TNBC cases, with 8 corresponding adjacent normal samples serving as controls. In total, we identified 11,540 somatic mutations, including 9452 SNVs and 2438 indels, with an average of 2328 mutations per sample (Fig. 3A-C). Most somatic mutations identified were missense, potentially impacting protein function and thereby disrupting key cellular processes that could contribute to cancer development (Fig. 3A). The most prevalent mutations were cytosine-to-thymine transitions, followed by guanine-to-adenine transitions, indicating a significantly higher frequency of transitions compared to transversions (Fig. 3B). Noticeably, we observed the high frequency of somatic mutations in several mucin and taste receptor type 2 (TAS2R) genes across both TNBC and non-TNBC samples, with MUC6 being the most frequently mutated gene overall (Fig. 3D).

Performing principal component analysis (PCA) based on presence and absence variation (PAV) of somatic mutations among these samples, we found that the four TNBC samples clustered together and were distinctly separated from the non-TNBC samples (Fig. 3E). We then predicted cancer driver genes separately in the TNBC and non-TNBC samples. Interestingly, a considerable number of these driver genes were specific to either subtype, suggesting that TNBC may have accumulated or be driven by distinct somatic mutations. (Fig. 3F). Among the 49 TNBC-specific driver genes, several have been reported in TNBC or breast cancer, such as AJAP1, CLOCK, AHNAK2 [46–51]. In addition, we also identified 8 genes exclusively mutated in the TNBC samples, including CTDP1, AHNAK2, TXLNG, SFTPB, C16orf74, C9orf43, PCP4L1, DLX2 (Fig. 3G). In aggregate, we employed WES to characterize and compare the somatic mutations and driver genes between TNBC and non-TNBC, and the results showed that TNBC may be specifically driven by certain somatic mutations and gene.

TNBC showed distinct gene expression and splicing patterns to non-TNBC

We subsequently conducted RNA-seq analysis on eight BRCA samples, using 8 corresponding paracancerous samples as control. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed three distinct groups corresponding to TNBC, non-TNBC and normal samples (Fig. 4A), indicating that these groups exhibited varied gene deployment and expression patterns. Differential expression analysis identified 1,586 up-regulated and 1,073 down-regulated genes in TNBC compared to adjacent normal samples, and 289 up-regulated and 325 down-regulated genes when TNBC was compared to non-TNBC samples. (Fig. S3A, B). Then, we screened out 170 up-regulated and 133 down-regulated genes, which represent the genes that were specifically differentially expressed in TNBC (Fig. 4B). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the up-regulated genes were significantly enriched in immune response pathways, such as Antigen processing and presentation, Th17 cell differentiation, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation. In contrast, the down-regulated genes were associated with key signaling and metabolic pathways, such as TGF-beta signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (Fig. S3C, D).

A PCA of transcriptome profiles from four TNBC, four non-TNBC, and eight paracancerous tissue samples, showing clear separation of TNBC samples from the other groups.B Differentially expressed genes uniquely dysregulated in TNBC were identified, with up-regulated genes highlighted in red and down-regulated genes in blue; genes with no significant changes in non-TNBC are excluded.C Summary of the number of differential alternative splicing events detected between TNBC and non-TNBC samples, classified by splicing type.D PCA based on percent spliced-in (PSI) values of differential cassette exon (CEx) events, further distinguishing TNBC from non-TNBC.E Three TNBC-specific up-regulated genes associated with poor prognosis in TNBC, but showing no prognostic relevance in non-TNBC, were highlighted.F One TNBC-specific down-regulated gene associated with favorable prognosis in TNBC, but showing no prognostic relevance in non-TNBC, was highlighted.G Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showing the correlation between expression of these four TNBC-specific genes and patient prognosis within TNBC cohorts

Alternative splicing (AS) is a dynamic and complex process that significantly contributes to the molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer and plays a significant role in tumorigenesis, progression, and response to therapy. We analyzed the alternative splicing, including cassette exon (CEx), intron retention (IR), alternative 5′ donor site (ALT5) and alternative 3′ acceptor site (ALT3), and compared among the TNBC, non-TNBC and normal samples. Overall, we identified 1223, 1020 and 648 differentially splicing events between TNBC and normal groups, non-TNBC and normal groups, TNBC and non-TNBC groups, respectively (Fig. 4C). Compared with the normal samples, both TNBC and non-TNBC showed large amounts of exon spliced out and intron retention events. Particularly, a considerable number of intron retention events were observed in TNBC and non-TNBC, suggesting that alteration transcript isoforms via affecting intron-exon structure may represent a key AS event in breast cancer. Additionally, PCA utilizing the percent spliced in (PSI) values of differential CEx events consistently showed three distinct clusters matching the TNBC, non-TNBC and normal samples, indicating that TNBC and non-TNBC may be distinguished by their unique AS events (Fig. 4D).

CLIC3 was a key risk factor affecting the proliferation, invasion and migration of TNBC cells

We conducted Kaplan–Meier analysis to identify potential biomarkers associated with the prognosis of TNBC. Initially, we identified 178 genes associated with poor prognosis and 3 with good prognosis in TNBC. These genes were then filtered based on their prognostic relevance in non-TNBC. Subsequently, we correlated these genes with TNBC expression data. Ultimately, we selected three genes (chloride intracellular channel 3 (CLIC3), progestin and adipoQ receptor family member 5 (PAQR5), SH2B adapter protein 2 (SH2B2)) that were associated with poor prognosis in TNBC, showed no correlation with non-TNBC prognosis, and were exclusively upregulated in TNBC. Additionally, we found one gene (Ly6/PLAUR domain-containing protein 6 (LYPD6)) that indicated good prognosis in TNBC, had no correlation with non-TNBC prognosis, and was exclusively downregulated in TNBC (Fig. 4E-G, Fig. S3E).

To precisely select the most relevant prognostic marker for TNBC, CLIC3 with the smallest P-value related to poor prognosis in TNBC in survival analysis was selected for further experimental validation. We used MDA-MB-231, a TNBC cell line with high CLIC3 expression, to construct two stable CLIC3 knockdown cell lines (Fig. 5A). In the Western blot assay, the protein expression in the sh-CLIC3−1 and sh-CLIC3−2 groups was significantly downregulated compared to the control group (Fig. 5B), indicating that the two independent short hairpin RNAs had a high silencing efficiency. In the CCK8 and colony formation assays, the number of cells in the CLIC3 knockdown groups were significantly less than that of in control group (Fig. 5C-D), which indicated an inhibited cell proliferation ability. Additionally, Trans-well assay showed that the invaded and migrated cells was significantly reduced after CLIC3 silencing (Fig. 5E-F). Meanwhile, the wound-healing assay showed that the cell migration ability was suppressed after CLIC3 silencing (Fig. 5G). These results validated that the CLIC3 was a key risk factors affecting the cell proliferation, invasion and migration in TNBC, and that high-expressed CLIC3 promoted TNBC progression.

Identification of CLIC3 as a novel biomarker in TNBC

To further validate the potential of CLIC3 as a prognostic biomarker for TNBC, we performed immunohistochemical analysis on tumor tissues from 4 TNBC and 4 non-TNBC patients (Fig. 6A, Fig. S4). After randomly selecting three fields of view, we observed significantly elevated CLIC3 protein levels in TNBC tissues compared to non-TNBC tissues, which exhibited extremely low CLIC3 expression (Fig. 6B). These findings suggested that CLIC3 could be a specific prognostic biomarker for TNBC and may represent a promising target for TNBC diagnosis and therapy.

Discussion

Recent advancements in high-throughput sequencing technology has enabled a comprehensive investigation of TNBC. However, research on TNBC remains challenging due to its aggressive clinical behavior, pronounced heterogeneity, lack of targeted therapeutic options, complex tumor microenvironment, and difficulties in predicting treatment responses. Previous studies have uncovered several potential biomarkers for TNBC. For example, overexpression of PD-L1 could be indicative of a potential therapeutic target [52–54]. A significant association between BRCA mutations and TNBC was discovered, with implications for personalized treatment strategies [55, 56]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) has been proposed to be a valuable predictive biomarker for response to immunotherapy in TNBC [57, 58]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has been explored as a non-invasive biomarker for monitoring TNBC progression and treatment response [59, 60]. There is an urgent needs to identify more biomarkers for TNBC to advance personalized treatment strategies, improve early detection and prognosis, overcome drug resistance, and guide more effective and cost-efficient clinical decision-making, ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

In this study, we employed a multi-omics approach to systematically compare TNBC with non-TNBC subtypes at the genomic, transcriptomic, and splicing levels. Our analyses revealed distinct molecular landscapes that underscore the unique biology of TNBC. Using RNA-seq data from TCGA, we identified widespread differential gene expression in TNBC compared to normal tissue, with up-regulated genes significantly enriched in immune response and cell cycle pathways, and down-regulated genes involved in cell communication and metabolic processes. These findings align with the known aggressive phenotype of TNBC, characterized by rapid proliferation and immune activation.

To further delineate subtype-specific gene expression patterns, we applied Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). This analysis identified six gene modules significantly correlated with TNBC status—four positively and two negatively correlated. Functional enrichment of these modules highlighted dysregulation in DNA replication, chromatin organization, and cilium-related processes in TNBC. The latter finding is particularly intriguing, as ciliary dysfunction can impair critical signaling pathways and intercellular communication, potentially contributing to tumor progression. These WGCNA results provide a nuanced view of the coordinated gene expression programs that underlie TNBC pathogenesis, which were not fully explored in our initial differential expression analysis and represent a key addition to the discussion of transcriptional regulation in TNBC.

Whole-exome sequencing of matched tumor-normal pairs revealed a distinct somatic mutation profile in TNBC compared to non-TNBC. Notably, we observed a high frequency of mutations in mucin genes (e.g., MUC6, MUC16) and taste receptor type 2 genes (TAS2R family), which have been implicated in tumor microenvironment modulation and cancer progression [61–63]. Principal component analysis clearly separated TNBC from non-TNBC based on their mutational profiles. More importantly, the prediction of cancer driver genes revealed that a substantial number were specific to TNBC, including previously reported genes like AJAP1 [46] and CLOCK [64], as well as novel candidates such as CTDP1 and C16orf74. The identification of eight genes harboring TNBC-specific somatic mutations further underscores the unique genetic drivers of this subtype. The role of CTDP1 in DNA repair and its interaction with BRCA1 suggest a potential mechanism for genomic instability in TNBC [65], warranting further functional investigation. C16orf74 has been discussed in relation to pancreatic cancer, specifically being upregulated in most patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and associated with poor prognosis, implying a potential role as a biomarker or therapeutic target in PDAC [66].

Beyond genetic and expression differences, our study uncovered significant alterations in alternative splicing patterns between TNBC and non-TNBC. We identified numerous differentially spliced events, with a notable abundance of intron retention events in both cancer types compared to normal tissue. However, TNBC exhibited a unique AS signature that clearly distinguished it from non-TNBC in PCA based on percent spliced in PSI values. This suggests that post-transcriptional regulation via alternative splicing contributes significantly to the molecular heterogeneity of TNBC and may influence its pathogenesis and treatment response.

By integrating prognostic analysis with subtype-specific expression data, we identified four genes, CLIC3, PAQR5, SH2B2, and LYPD6, as potential TNBC-specific biomarkers. Particularly, CLIC3 emerged as a strong candidate due to its significant association with poor prognosis in TNBC, exclusive upregulation in TNBC, and lack of prognostic relevance in non-TNBC. CLIC3 is a member of the CLIC protein family, which are involved in various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cell migration. Studies have shown that CLIC3 expression is significantly elevated in bladder cancer tissues compared to normal bladder tissues. Overexpression of CLIC3 mRNA has been linked to the prognostic value in bladder cancer, suggesting that it could be used as a biomarker for patient stratification and treatment planning [67, 68]. Additionally, CLIC3 has been shown to have glutathione-dependent oxidoreductase activity, which may drive cancer progression through its effects on cellular redox balance and the stabilization of mRNA transcripts [69]. The known roles of CLIC3 in cellular redox balance and mRNA stability may provide mechanistic insights into its oncogenic functions in TNBC. Experimental validation in TNBC cell lines demonstrated that knockdown of CLIC3 significantly depressed cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that CLIC3 protein was predominantly expressed in TNBC, with markedly lower levels in non-TNBC tumors. These consistent findings across bioinformatic and experimental approaches strongly support CLIC3 as a novel prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target for TNBC.

Despite the robust findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. the sample size for our in-house WES and RNA-seq analyses was relatively small (4 TNBC and 4 non-TNBC), which may limit the statistical power to detect rare mutations or subtle transcriptional differences. Future studies with larger, independent cohorts are needed to validate our findings. Second, the prognostic value of CLIC3 was identified and validated within the TCGA dataset but requires confirmation in a large, independent prospective cohort to solidify its clinical utility. Third, our in vitro models provide strong evidence for CLIC3’s role in tumor cell aggressiveness, but they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. Future work should include in vivo models to validate these findings in a more physiologically relevant context. Lastly, the precise molecular mechanisms downstream of CLIC3 that drive TNBC progression remain to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, to enhance clinical utility, future work should aim to develop a multi-marker prognostic panel combining CLIC3 with other candidate genes identified here (e.g., PAQR5, SH2B2) or established markers. Such a panel could provide superior predictive power and clinical utility for managing TNBC patients. Additionally, while our data robustly positions CLIC3 as a prognostic biomarker, its utility for early diagnosis or staging requires further investigation. Future studies should analyze CLIC3 expression in a large, well-annotated cohort encompassing all TNBC stages to determine if its upregulation occurs in pre-invasive or early-stage lesions, which would bolster its potential as an early diagnostic marker.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of CLIC3 as a novel prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Using an integrated multi-omics approach, we identified distinct molecular signatures of TNBC, including dysregulations in immune response, cell cycle, and alternative splicing. The overexpression of CLIC3 was significantly associated with poor prognosis in TNBC patients, and experimental validation demonstrated its role in promoting TNBC aggressiveness by enhancing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings suggest that targeting CLIC3 may offer a promising strategy for improving TNBC treatment outcomes. Further studies with larger cohorts and in vivo models are warranted to confirm the clinical utility of CLIC3 as a therapeutic target and to explore its mechanisms in the progression of TNBC.
