Multi-region brain transcriptomes uncover two subtypes of aging individuals with differences in Alzheimer risk and the impact of APOEε4
Authors: Annie J Lee, Yiyi Ma, Lei Yu, Robert J Dawe, Cristin McCabe, Konstantinos Arfanakis, Richard Mayeux, David A Bennett, Hans-Ulrich Klein, Philip L De Jager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe heterogeneity of the aging population suggests the existence of molecularly distinct subgroups that differ in vulnerability to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet this population structure remains poorly defined. We performed unsupervised clustering of multi-region brain transcriptomes to assess whether integrating data across regions involved in cognition could uncover such subgroups. Canonical correlation-based analysis in a discovery cohort of 459 participants with RNA-sequencing data from three regions (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and anterior caudate), followed by replication in 690 additional participants with partial data, identified two meta-clusters (MC-1 and MC-2). These groups differed in cognitive trajectories, with MC-2 showing a three-year delay in dementia onset relative to MC-1. This may reflect, in part, a greater impact of tau pathology on neuronal chromatin architecture, white matter loss, and APOEε4-related cognitive decline in MC-1. These findings reveal a molecular population structure of the aging brain that modulates vulnerability and resilience to AD and may inform targeted therapies and trials.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, aging brain, multi-region transcriptomics, unsupervised clustering, canonical correlation analysis, cognitive decline, molecular vulnerability

Introduction

There is extensive heterogeneity among older individuals in terms of their cognitive ability, trajectory of cognitive decline, and the accumulated burden of aging-related brain pathologies. In the religious order study (ROS) and memory and aging project (MAP)—collectively referred to as ROSMAP1,2,3—over 230 unique combinations of brain pathology have been found,4 and in these longitudinal studies of cognitive aging, even after accounting for six known types of brain pathology, more than 50% of the variance in cognitive decline remains unexplained.5 This suggests that there are many yet unknown biological factors that affect the performance of the aging brain.

Heterogeneity is also evident in the effects of established Alzheimer’s disease (AD) risk factors. For example, the APOEε4 allele strongly increases AD susceptibility in individuals of European ancestry but has a weaker effect in African American and Latino populations,6 and its impact diminishes in advanced age7: there may thus be both inter-individual and temporal heterogeneity in the effects of APOEε4 on AD risk. We now have a large set of genetic,8 environmental,9 and experiential (such as menopause) risk factors10 associated with susceptibility to AD or AD-related traits, yet it remains unclear whether these factors exert modest to moderate effects in all individuals or whether they have strong effects in some subgroups and limited or no effects in others, yielding only modest effects at the population level. Recent work has also highlighted additional molecular processes contributing to AD pathogenesis, including impaired autophagy and mitophagy,11,12 as well as altered cell-cell communication,13 which may interact with tau- and APOE-related mechanisms to influence disease progression. This question has been challenging to address because there is no established strategy for defining biologically meaningful subgroups of aging individuals, and high-dimensional datasets suitable for this question have only recently become large enough to enable robust stratification. Recent clustering efforts in AD have focused on identifying subsets of individuals within diagnosed AD using data from a single brain region,14,15 revealing 2–3 subgroups that differ in pathological burden. A complementary multi-omics study integrating five modalities from the frontal cortex also detected molecular subtypes associated with cognitive decline.16

Here, we pursue a different question and leverage a rapidly growing collection of multi-region transcriptomic profiles from ROSMAP participants who are all non-demented at study entry. By sampling individuals across the entire spectrum of the aging brain (from non-impaired to advanced AD), we aim to define the data-driven structure of the older population that reflects global brain state: we are looking for broader patterns than those which can be found in a single brain region and may relate to the specialized function of that region. We jointly analyze gene expression from three brain regions—the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and the anterior caudate (AC)—from the same individuals, providing an opportunity to examine large-scale cognitive and memory circuits variably affected by aging and AD, allowing simultaneous multi-region analysis and a more integrative view of the aging brain. This strategy identified two “meta-clusters” of aging individuals in ROSMAP that differ in their rate of cognitive decline, white matter integrity, the consequences of Tau pathology, and the impact of the APOEε4 risk factor, highlighting differences in the impact of AD risk factors across individuals. These meta-clusters represent data-driven partitions of a continuum of brain states rather than strictly discrete biological subtypes. Here, “vulnerability” and “resilience” refer to differences in the magnitude of the effects of risk factors on cognitive decline, rather than representing intrinsic predisposition. We also demonstrate that these meta-clusters may generalize to other datasets with similar brain regions.

This is the first report of newly expanded and novel RNA sequence datasets from ROSMAP—including DLPFC (n = 1,092), AC (n = 731), and PCC (n = 661)—with AC and PCC data not previously analyzed for gene expression changes in relation to AD traits: only ∼10% of these data were used in a recent study focused on RNA editing.17 We perform a de novo multi-region clustering approach to define transcriptome-based subgroups without relying on prior knowledge of AD pathophysiology. Unlike earlier efforts that analyze each region independently or focus on features that define the presence or stage of AD, for the first time, we integrate 3 regions simultaneously to define our subgroups, providing a more comprehensive view of the biological heterogeneity in the aging brain.

Results

Description of the study participants and data

ROS and MAP are two prospective studies of aging that recruit non-demented participants at baseline, follow each participant annually with neurologic and neuropsychological evaluations, and collect brains at the time of death since agreement to brain donation is a prerequisite for participation in the study. Thus, participants capture the diversity of the older population at the time of death, ranging from cognitively non-impaired, to mildly impaired, to dementia in the cognitive spectrum. The characteristics of ROSMAP participants with transcriptomic data derived from postmortem brains are presented in Table 1. A total of 1,149 ROSMAP participants had transcriptomic data in at least one brain region (Figure 1A). A subset of 459 participants had RNA-seq profiles in all three brain regions and served as the discovery cohort (Figure 1B). The remaining 690 participants had RNA-seq profiles in only one or two regions and were used as a replication cohort (Figure 1C).

Defining the population structure of aged human brains

We considered statistical approaches to define the structure of the ROSMAP participants based on transcriptomic profiles across three cognitively relevant brain regions. Unlike prior efforts that evaluated individual regions separately and focused on AD cases,14,15 we considered the full spectrum of aged brains—from non-impaired to advanced AD—and explored the hypothesis that there may exist broader patterns in the molecular state of the brain that may not be as apparent if one focuses on a single brain region. Importantly, since cognition is supported by a distributed network of functional centers, we were interested in a more comprehensive view of the brain and thus wanted to simultaneously consider all three regions in our clustering efforts. Brain regions are functionally specialized, and certain types of pathology in the older brain have a predilection for specific regions; since we elected to focus on certain regions involved in cognition, we did not select regions that capture the Braak staging for tau proteinopathy. Selecting such regions would yield an interesting but different study; here, we evaluate the relation of Braak staging to our clusters as part of the downstream analyses.

We used canonical correlation analysis (CCA)18 followed by unsupervised k-means clustering. CCA finds combinations of genes, known as canonical variates, which capture shared expression patterns across regions. We deployed CCA in the discovery cohort of individuals that have transcriptional profiles in all three regions and performed k-means clustering on the resulting canonical variates within each region to identify participant subgroups (Figures 1B and 2A). In contrast to principal-component analysis (PCA), which captures variance within each region independently, CCA emphasizes shared structure across regions. We then projected the clusters of participants identified from each region using CCA into a shared, low-dimensional space using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF),19,20 deriving two “meta-clusters”, which arise from an integrative analysis that incorporates all three regions simultaneously and reflect shared molecular states across the brain (Figures 1B, 2B, and 2C). Across all three regions, two clusters of participants were optimal in the discovery cohort. We evaluated clustering solutions from 2 to 10 clusters across three clustering methods (k-means, hierarchical clustering, and partitioning around medioids), and assessed model fit using internal validation measures (connectivity,21 Dunn index,22 and silhouette width23). For all regions, two clusters minimized connectivity and maximized Dunn index and silhouette width under both k-means and hierarchical clustering (Figure S1A). When we compared k-means and hierarchical clustering, clustering stability evaluated through clusterwise Jaccard bootstrap mean (CJBM)24 showed that two k-means clusters were the most stable in all three regions, with CJBM above 0.85 (Figure S1B). The two clusters in each region were integrated into two meta-clusters using NMF (Figures 2B and 2C). Two meta-clusters were optimal, supported by the highest cophenetic coefficient25 (Figure 2D). The meta-clusters were stable, as the subdivision was clearly evident in the consensus matrix (Figure 2E). These results indicate that the two-cluster solution is stable across methods and resampling procedures. In the discovery cohort, AC and PCC clusters mapped cleanly onto one of the two meta-clusters, while the DLPFC clusters showed partial but high concordance (85%) (Table S1). This highlights the value of integrating multiple region, although region-specific variation remains informative, analyzing regions jointly prevents it from obscuring broader molecular patterns that influence distributed cognitive networks and contribute to symptomatic changes in older individuals.

We next projected participants in the replication cohort—those with data from one or two regions—using the CCA-derived feature weights from the discovery cohort to determine their region-specific cluster membership (Table 1; Figure 2F; STAR Methods). These regional assignments were then integrated across brain regions using NMF, which resolved two stable meta-clusters (Figures 2G–2J). While data generation in each region was done on as many samples as were available at the time RNA was extracted, without any phenotypic selection criteria, replication participants had a higher prevalence of clinical and pathological AD than those in the discovery cohort. Relation of meta-clusters to clinical and pathological traits

To evaluate the relevance of our transcriptomically defined meta-clusters, we assessed whether clinical and pathologic features differed between the two meta-clusters, using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05 to account for correlations among traits (Table S3). Analyses were first conducted in the Discovery cohort. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B and Table 3, individuals in meta cluster 1 (MC-1) exhibited a steeper rate of cognitive decline than those in MC-2 after adjusting for age and sex, and this result was validated in the replication samples. A meta-analysis of both cohorts (Figure 3A; Table 3) provided the final results, with the larger sample size also yielding significance for an increased risk of clinical AD dementia at death (b = 0.556, pFDR = 0.001), consistent with the more rapid cognitive decline in MC-1 (b = −0.025, pFDR = 0.0001) (Figure 3B). Similarly, residual cognition—a measure of global cognition unexplained by known brain pathologies26 (i.e., cognitive performance at the last evaluation before death adjusted for all pathologic indices)—was also reduced in MC-1 (b = −0.224, pFDR = 0.001). We also observed a difference in pathologic diagnosis of AD (b = 0.359, pFDR = 0.015), driven by higher β-amyloid load in MC-1 (b = 0.210, pFDR = 0.006); tau pathology burden was only marginally higher in MC-1 (b = 0.198, pFDR = 0.023) (Figures 3A; Table 3). Neocortical Lewy bodies were also higher in MC-1 (b = 0.476, pFDR = 0.019). By contrast, the burden of several other pathologies contributing to cognitive decline—cerebrovascular pathologies, TDP-43, and hippocampal sclerosis—did not differ significantly between the meta-clusters.

To determine whether the difference in the rate of cognitive decline between MC-1 and MC-2 was driven by differences in AD pathology, we repeated the analysis adjusting for β-amyloid and tau burden, the two defining pathologic characteristics of AD. The association remained significant (b = −0.020, pFDR = 0.000104) (Table S3), indicating that the difference in cognitive decline is not fully explained by these pathologies and may reflect an additional, uncharacterized mechanism. This is also illustrated in Figure 3C, where meta-cluster membership remains associated with cognitive decline after adjusting for all neuropathologies and covariates. The meta-clusters did not segregate individuals with and without dementia (Figure 3D): both MC-1 and MC-2 included many demented participants. Although dementia was more frequent in MC-1, this was not a defining characteristic of that group. Results were similar when using the pathologic diagnosis of AD using National Institute on aging-Reagan diagnostic criteria (Figure 3D). In secondary analyses stratified by AD status, MC-1 had a steeper rate of cognitive decline than MC-2 among participants with pathological AD (b = −0.025, pFDR = 0.004), after adjusting for age and sex, and the result remained significant after additional adjustment for the burden of β-amyloid and tau pathology (b = −0.023, pFDR = 0.002) (Table S4).

Differences in other factors related to cognitive decline

To further explore what could be driving the differences in cognitive decline between MC-1 and MC-2, we evaluated a summary measure of epigenomic perturbation caused by Tau pathology that we derived from genome-wide histone 3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) data.27 This score, calculated by aggregating tau-associated H3K9ac signals across the genome, reflects large-scale chromatin alterations and transcriptional activation, capturing a proximal functional consequence of tau proteinopathy, particularly pronounced in neurons. Although a single chromatin mark cannot fully capture the state of chromatin, our carefully selected mark does capture important changes in chromatin structure that relate to Tau pathology. Other chromatin changes may also be present, but the H3K9Ac-related changes are large, widespread across the genome, and have been validated in vitro.27 This epigenomic tau score was associated with the rate of cognitive decline (b = −0.0004, p = 0.0006) in ROSMAP participants. Interestingly, the overall burden of tau pathology, which is based on the amount of pathologic phosphorylated tau aggregates that have accumulated in the brain,28 was only marginally different between the two meta-clusters (b = 0.198, pFDR = 0.023) (Table 3). Yet, the epigenomic tau score was greater in MC-1 than in MC-2 (b = 16.3, p = 1.04 × 10−6), and this difference remained highly significant even after adjusting for tau pathology burden (Table S5). These findings suggest that molecular events downstream of tau associated with cognitive decline are not fully captured by neuropathological quantification of pathologically phosphorylated tau fibrils.

Most of the outcomes evaluated so far relate to cortical function and neuropathology. Since our multi-region modeling approach aims to capture a comprehensive view of the brain, we also investigated whether the meta-cluster membership reflects aging-related alterations in white matter function. In ROSMAP, postmortem magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to evaluate the loss of brain tissue integrity that relates to cognitive decline. One previously characterized MRI measure is the transverse relaxation rate R2, an important tissue characteristic that is the multiplicative inverse of T2 and is thought to reflect the ratio of free to bound water molecules in tissue. We had previously defined a region of frontal white matter where R2 explains variance in cognitive decline after accounting for known neuropathologies.29 This region showed a difference in R2 level between the two meta-clusters (b = −0.217, p = 0.007) (Figure 3G; Table S5), suggesting that meta-cluster classification, based solely on three gray matter RNA-seq profiles, may also capture an element of disruption in white matter integrity. Moreover, the association between MC-1 and accelerated cognitive decline remained significant after adjusting for white-matter R2 (b = −0.028, p = 2.88 × 10−(4) (Table S3), indicating that white-matter disruption is only one aspect of the different state of MC-1 brains. In stratified secondary analyses, MC-1 had a steeper rate of cognitive decline than MC-2 among participants with pathological AD (b = −0.026, pFDR = 0.005) (Table S4), after adjusting for age, sex, amyloid, tau, and R2. Among participants with pathological AD, MC-1 also had lower R2 level than MC-2 (b = −0.280, pFDR = 0.012) (Table S4). These subgroup analyses are limited by smaller sample sizes.

In terms of genetic risk factors leading to cognitive decline, we found no difference in APOEε4 haplotype frequency between the two meta-clusters (Tables 2 and S4). Some haplotype combinations showed nominal differences, though not significant after multiple-testing correction (Table S5): MC-1 participants were more likely to be APOEε4 homozygous and less likely to carry the APOEε2/ε3 combination (b = −0.476, p = 0.014), compared with MC-2. Furthermore, we evaluated previously derived polygenic scores for AD and the first two genotype principal components (PCs),30 which have been associated with diverse clinical and molecular outcomes in ROSMAP.31 MC-1 and MC-2 showed similar genetic propensities for AD based on the polygenic risk for AD or in the genetic PCs (Table S5). Among common AD susceptibility variants32 (Table S6), only the clusterin (CLU) variant displayed nominally different allele frequencies between the meta-clusters, with MC-1 more likely to carry the risk allele, but this association did not remain significant after FDR correction (b = 0.126, p = 0.007, pFDR = 0.129). However, the allele frequency difference was small, and, when we added this SNP as a covariate, MC-1 remained strongly associated with cognitive decline (b = −0.029, pFDR = 4.86 × 10−(5) (Table S3), suggesting that this variant is not a major driver of the two clusters of participants. This suggests that the meta-cluster structure may not primarily reflect genetic susceptibility, but rather differences in responses to risk factors and pathological events.

To summarize and contextualize the relative importance of meta-cluster membership, Figure 3E presents the variance in cognitive decline explained by known neuropathologic factors and the additional variance explained by the meta-clusters. The meta-clusters accounted for an additional 1.1% of variance (p = 0.003), beyond the known neuropathologic factors and the measure of frontal white matter integrity. For comparison, APOEε4 did not significantly explain variance in cognitive decline in a similar model, consistent with prior reports that the haplotype’s effect is primarily mediated through pathology.33 For comparison, neocortical Lewy body burden explained another 2.4% of variance (p = 1.6 × 10−6). Thus, meta-cluster membership captures a component of variation in cognitive decline that is distinct from known neuropathologic factors, although modest in magnitude.

Pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes

To annotate the genes distinguishing the two meta-clusters, we first identified transcripts that were differentially expressed between MC-1 and MC-2 in each of the three regions (Figures 4A and 4B; Table S7), and then performed pathway enrichment analysis using the functional Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway database34 with FDR correction (FDR<0.05). The estimated proportion of neurons35,36 was included as a covariate in the regression model to account for potential differences in neuronal cell populations across samples.

In the DLPFC, 12,723 genes were differentially expressed between MC-1 and MC-2 (Figures 4A and 4B), and these genes displayed enrichment for 87 KEGG pathways (Figure 4C). Enriched pathways included those related to several neurodegenerative diseases (including AD, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Parkinson’s disease), suggesting that the meta-clusters may capture shared elements of CNS vulnerability or resilience. There was also less pronounced enrichment for the endocytosis pathway, which has been implicated in AD. Results from the AC and PCC were highly similar (Figure 4C). Notably, APOE-related pathways did not emerge in any of these analyses.

We also conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each region, comparing MC-1 to MC-2 (Figure 4D). Prominent GO categories included protein targeting and neuronal signatures related to synaptic transmission and trans-synaptic signaling, suggesting that neuronal processes may contribute to the observed differences, even though neuronal proportion was accounted for in the analysis.

Transcriptome-derived cluster structure propagates into the proteome

Although proteomics data are available for a smaller subset of participants and only in the DLPFC region of ROSMAP, we nonetheless evaluated whether the transcriptomic differences that define the meta-clusters propagate to the protein level (Figure 4E). Among 335 samples profiled for 8,817 proteins in the DLPFC,37 we identified proteins that were differentially expressed between MC-1 and MC-2 (Figure 4E). When comparing protein abundance with RNA expression levels for the same genes in the DLPFC, the detected proteins were distributed across the RNA expression spectrum, with a skew toward transcripts expressed at higher levels. Comparing fold-changes between MC-1 and MC-2 at both the RNA and protein levels for the same genes revealed a correlation of 0.23 across all genes (p < 2.2 × 10−(16) and a stronger correlation of 0.55 (p < 2.2 × 10−(16) among genes and proteins that were differentially expressed (FDR <0.05), indicating that the molecular distinctions between the meta-clusters are present at the protein level and consistent with the RNA level (Figure 4F). GO enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed proteins revealed pathways that were consistent with those identified at the RNA level, including cellular protein complex disassembly (p = 7.60 × 10−(11) and protein-containing complex disassembly (p = 1.28 × 10−(8) (Figure 4G), further supporting a concordant proteomic signature of the two meta-clusters.

Differences in cell type proportions

To assess the possible role of differences in cell type proportions influencing the meta-cluster definition, we evaluated each cell type using signatures derived from purified cell populations.27,36 We observed that the inferred proportion (from bulk DLPFC profiles) of almost all cell types differed between MC-1 and MC-2 (p < 1 × 10−(9) (Table S5). There appears to be a broad effect on the cellular composition of the CNS parenchyma that differentiates MC-1 from MC-2. To address whether this is a late manifestation of AD due to greater neuronal or other cellular loss in individuals with AD, we repeated the analysis in a subset of ROSMAP participants who died without cognitive impairment (no dementia and no mild cognitive impairment). In these cognitively unimpaired individuals, all cell types except oligodendrocytes also differed between the meta-clusters (p < 0.01). Thus, the lower inferred neuronal proportion and higher proportions of other cell types in MC-1 appear to be early features, present prior to the cognitive impairment. Although these analyses cannot determine whether these differences in cell types arise from the product of brain development or early, asymptomatic neurodegeneration, they demonstrate that the observed cell population differences are clearly not simply a reflection of the terminal phase of AD. These findings suggest that variation in cell-type composition is part of the biological signal captured by the meta-clusters, rather than solely a confounding factor.

To confirm these results, we accessed targeted proteomic data from the DLPFC in 1,092 ROSMAP participants, many of whom were included in the RNA-based analyses, focusing on peptides encoded by the synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP25), an important synaptic protein and proxy of synaptic integrity. SNAP25 levels were reduced in MC-1, consistent with the reduced inferred proportion of neurons, and remained significant after adjusting for the proportion of neurons estimated from the transcriptome (b = −0.056, p = 6.38 × 10−(4) (Table S5), suggesting that this difference may not be largely driven by neuronal loss. When we adjusted for SNAP25, the proportion neurons remained reduced in MC-1 (b = −0.057, p = 5.06 × 10−(28) (Table S5), indicating that SNAP25 levels capture synapse loss—a phenotype that is related to, but different from, loss of neuronal soma. This pattern aligns with our gene set enrichment results, which highlight alternations in synaptic signaling pathways in MC-1, supporting synaptic vulnerability as a distinguishing feature of this meta-cluster.

For microglia, we further tested the HuMi-Aged gene set, which we previously defined as enriched in microglia isolated from aged ROSMAP brains.38 This signature was also increased in MC-1 (b = 0.282, p = 1.84 × 10−30), with a stronger association than the inferred microglial proportion measure (b = 0.008, p = 6.82 × 10−(16) (Table S5). While all of these signatures derived from bulk tissue homogenates imperfectly captured cell type proportions, the microglia-related results suggest that, even if the proportion of microglia are modestly more abundant in MC-1—potentially due to reduced neuronal proportions—the underlying transcriptional state of these microglia is substantially more altered. In particular, microglia in MC-1 exhibit a more “aged” transcriptional profile than those in MC-2.

Heterogeneity in the effect of known AD genetic risk factors in the two subgroups

To investigate potential mechanism underlying the differences in clinical phenotypes between MC-1 and MC-2, we investigated whether known AD genetic risk factors could have a different effect sizes on the rate of cognitive decline in the two meta-clusters. Such heterogeneity could contribute to the more severe cognitive symptoms observed in MC-1. We tested differential genetic effects using a meta-analysis of the discovery and replication cohorts. We first investigated the APOEε4 haplotype given its unique role in AD and found a significant difference (p = 0.003) in its effect on cognitive decline between the two meta-clusters (Figure 3G; Table S9). Specifically, APOEε4 was associated with a more rapid cognitive decline in MC-1 individuals relative to MC-2. At least part of this difference in effect may be attributable to enhanced accumulation of tau pathology in MC-1, as suggested by a modest interaction between meta-cluster and APOEε4 on tau burden (p = 0.037) (Table S9). However, even after adjusting for β-amyloid and tau pathology, the interaction of meta-clusters with APOEε4 remained significant, although attenuated. Thus, we find that the meta-clusters differ in the magnitude of the effect of the APOEε4 haplotype, with MC-2 individuals having a diminished impact of APOEε4. When we evaluated all APOE haplotype combinations, the effect of APOE on cognitive decline did not differ significantly between the meta-clusters (p = 0.190) (Table S9). Among participants with pathological AD, however, the effect of APOEε4 on cognitive decline differed significantly between MC-1 and MC-2 (p = 0.005) (Table S8). To assess whether this interaction is driven by differences in aged microglia, we adjusted for the HuMi-aged gene set, but the interaction between APOEε4 and meta-cluster remained significant (Table S9). Similarly, we adjusted for SNAP25 expression to assess whether the interaction may relate to neuronal proportion or synaptic health; the interaction effect was attenuated but remained significant (Table S9).

Secondarily, we evaluated other associations with AD-related traits for evidence of interaction. There was suggestive evidence (p = 0.024) that MC-1 individuals accumulate more tau proteinopathy per unit of β-amyloid burden (Table S9), although this observation will need further validation. We found no evidence for significant differences in the effects of the R2 white-matter integrity measure or the other neuropathologic traits.

Finally, we considered the epigenomic factor of activated microglia (EFAM),39 which we recently reported; it is an epigenomic factor related to microglial activation that enhances the effect of APOEε4. We therefore hypothesized that diminished impact of APOEε4 in MC-2 might reflect lower EFAM activity. While the EFAM levels did not differ significantly between MC-1 and MC-2 (p = 0.087) in the frontal cortex of the 456 ROSMAP participants with DNA methylation data (Table S5), higher EFAM was associated with a more rapid cognitive decline only in MC-1, providing some evidence of interaction (p = 0.029) (Table S9). However, EFAM did not explain the difference in APOEε4 effect: when considering both EFAM and APOEε4 in the same model, we found that the interaction between APOEε4 and meta-cluster on cognitive decline remained significant (Table S9).

Clinical relevance of meta-cluster assignment

To assess the clinical impact of belonging to one of the two meta-clusters of aging brains, we evaluated age at AD onset by conducting a survival analysis using the onset of AD dementia as the primary outcome. As shown in Figure 3H, individuals in MC-1 tend to develop dementia earlier than those in MC-2. In particular, if we look at the median AD dementia-free survival, there was a 3-year delay in the median age of dementia onset for MC-2 compared with MC-1. Using a complementary approach, we estimated the cumulative risk of AD dementia by age 75. The estimated risk was 16.3% for MC-1 (95% CI: 5.2–30.1%) and 11.6% for MC-2 (95% CI: 4.2–21.4%) (Figure 3I; Table S10), after adjusting for sex and education. These results indicate a meaningful difference in clinical outcome that arises from belonging to MC-2. These findings demonstrate the prognostic value of the meta-cluster membership for estimating the risk and expected timing of AD onset.

Validation

To assess the generalizability of our findings, we accessed neocortical RNA-seq datasets from the AD knowledge portal. These datasets differ from ROSMAP in study designs, as samples were collected through traditional brain banks and selected to fulfill certain categorical criteria. We retained 186 participants with inferior frontal gyrus data from the Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB)40 and 262 participants with temporal cortex data from the Mayo Clinic cohort (Mayo),41 both brain regions broadly comparable to the DLPFC. Using the signature genes and weights from the DLPFC applied to the ROSMAP replication set, we obtained canonical variates for MSBB and Mayo samples and identified two participant clusters in each cohort using k-means clustering. We then tested whether pathological AD differed between meta-clusters and whether the effect of APOEε4 on pathological AD also differed between meta-clusters in each cohort. Results from all three cohorts were combined in a meta-analysis.

Despite smaller sample sizes and the absence of cognition-related traits in these external datasets, we found evidence of replication in both Mayo and MSBB (Figure S3). Pathological AD differed between MC-1 and MC-2 in Mayo (b = 1.565, p = 1.83 × 10−5), and, although not significant in MSBB, the effect estimate was directionally consistent. A meta-analysis across ROSMAP, MSBB, and Mayo was significant (b = 0.237, p = 0.045) (Table S11; Figure S3A). We note that MSBB and Mayo included fewer participants and only provide pathological outcomes, which have weaker associations in ROSMAP than cognitive measures, yet the replication remains encouraging that our meta-cluster structure may be generalizable. Mayo also contains younger brains, which may partly explain the findings, as MC-1 participants appear overrepresented in AD cases at younger ages, consistent with our observation that MC-1 participants are diagnosed with AD about 3 years earlier on average. Finally, we did not find evidence for replication of the interaction of the effect of APOEε4 on a pathological AD with meta-clusters in Mayo and MSBB; however, the limited sample sizes in these cohorts make these results difficult to interpret (Figure S3B; Table S12).

Discussion

We explored the structure of the older population based on the molecular state of their brain at the time of death. Empirically defined subtypes of individuals were determined and found to be associated with differences in cognitive decline. While earlier attempts have been made to define subtypes of individuals with AD in other sets of brain data and earlier versions of ROSMAP data,15,42 we improved on prior work in two important ways: (1) we had a much larger sample size, with up to 1,149 individuals with brain transcriptomes, expanding previous samples15,42 by 88%, and (2) we included a large number of ROSMAP participants (n = 459) with transcriptomes from three distinct brain regions involved in cognition (AC, DLPFC, and PCC). Thus, we had greater statistical power to resolve the presence of small subsets of subjects, if present, and we could deploy a different approach that considered the three regions simultaneously. The latter approach may provide a better, integrative view of the state of an individual’s brain that may overcome technical factors and biological idiosyncrasies of the data from each region. We also considered the full range of aged ROSMAP participants and not just the subset with AD, as other groups have done. This strategy, implemented using a CCA with NMF approach, identified two meta-clusters as the model that best fit the data, and these two meta-clusters appear to have clinical relevance since cognitive decline is more rapid in MC-1. Importantly, we replicated this observation in an independent set of ROSMAP participants that were not used in the discovery CCA analysis (Figures 2G and 2H; Tables S2 and S5) and found some consistent results in two other external sample collections from similar brain regions (Figure S3).

Our population structure captures an aspect of the molecular state of the brain that is associated with cognition and may be independent of the underlying pathologies that accumulate with older age (Figure 3; Tables 3 and S2). While tau accumulation between the two groups is only marginally different, its biological impact as measured by the epigenomic perturbation score appears to be greater in MC-1 (Table S5): the same amount of tau pathology is associated with a greater effect on the neuronal nucleus and, later, on the cognitive trajectory of MC-1 individuals.

Our epigenomic perturbation score is a summary measure of large-scale chromatin alterations.27 Although we cannot fully capture the state of chromatin with a single chromatin mark, our carefully selected mark does capture important changes in chromatin structure related to tau pathology. Consistent with the results for quantitative measures of tau presented in Table S5, we see a skew toward higher Braak stages in MC-1. However, the population structure we identified across the three regions related to cognition is not driven by Braak stages. Further, the difference in cognitive decline between MC-1 and MC-2 remains significant after adjusting for Braak stage (pFDR = 0.0001; Table S3).

As shown in Figure 3D, a large proportion of individuals in both meta-clusters had AD at the time of death. Thus, we were not simply separating cognitively impaired and non-impaired individuals. Rather, we identified a subset of individuals that may be less resilient and more likely to decline for a given exposure: for example, our interaction analyses reveal that the APOEε4 haplotype risk factor has a different effect size in MC-1 than in MC-2 brains. This elaborates the narrative that the effect of APOEε4 can be influenced by other factors,39 including genetic ancestry, and that not all APOE genotypes are equivalent,43 although our analysis did not uncover significant differences in genotype frequencies between MC-1 and MC-2. Specifically, MC-1 subjects are more strongly impacted by this factor, which is associated with multiple features of AD, including the accumulation of AD pathologies, cognitive decline, and dementia. These results suggest that the MC-1/MC-2 partition captures molecular processes present in the transcriptome that modulate the impact of risk factors on cognitive decline; therefore, we use the term “vulnerable” to describe individuals in whom risk factors (such as APOEe4) have a greater impact, rather than implying a direct causal predisposition. One interesting observation was that frontal white-matter integrity (R2), which was previously associated with cognitive decline,29 was also more disrupted in MC-1 participants, suggesting that these gray-matter-derived meta-clusters may be capturing a broader state of brain disruption that affects the aging brain.

The nature of the signature differentiating MC-1 and MC-2 is not well defined. It seems to contain genes previously annotated across multiple neurodegenerative diseases signatures, suggesting that the meta-clusters are not specific to AD but reflect broader properties of the aging brain. These meta-clusters can be interpreted as data-driven partitions of a continuum of brain states rather than strictly discrete subtypes. Consistent with this, there were large differences in cellular composition between MC-1 and MC-2, with fewer neurons and, interestingly, greater expression of the aged microglia gene set (HuMi-aged) in MC-1. Since cognitively non-impaired individuals showed similar differences, these differences in cell type proportion and signatures were not limited to the late phase of the disease, but we could not resolve whether they may have occurred during development or during the course of early brain aging. While the roles of APOEε4, tau pathology, and white-matter changes in AD are well established, our findings highlight differences in their impact across transcriptomically defined brain states.

Are there only two subtypes of aging brains? Given the heterogeneity in cognitive performance and the wide variety of combinations of pathologic traits in ROSMAP subjects,4 we considered the possibility that we would uncover multiple subsets of subjects. However, differences in AD pathologic features were not the primary distinction between our meta-clusters (Figures 3A; Table 3), and two meta-clusters were the best empirical solution from the transcriptomic data. We used an unsupervised clustering method to identify clusters solely based on gene expression patterns, ensuring they were not influenced by predefined characteristics such as AD diagnosis. The two recent efforts to explore subsets of these data focused on individual regions and investigated a different question: how many subsets of brains existed among individuals diagnosed with AD.14,15 Thus, they did not consider the full spectrum of neocortical states in older adults, particularly those with no or mild cognitive impairment who do not fulfill AD criteria. While one of these efforts found no phenotypic differences among clusters, the other found inter-group differences driven by pathology, which is quite different from the cognition-related changes captured by our meta-clustering approach. Overall, these approaches are likely complementary to the one deployed here, and further work can integrate these models.

Overall, we identified two major subsets of older brains associated with biological processes that influence the impact of risk factors such as APOEε4. These meta-clusters capture ∼1% of the variance in aging-related cognitive decline, similar in magnitude to other established factors such as neocortical Lewy body burden, which explains another 2.4% of variance, beyond what is explained by brain pathology and loss of white matter integrity (R2). A better understanding of the biological basis of this process may provide insight into the mechanisms that distinguish the more resilient MC-2 individuals from the MC-1 individuals, in whom AD factors have a greater impact. Finding proxy markers for MC-1/MC-2 in peripheral blood or cerebrospinal fluid will be important to assess the relevance of this classification in younger populations, across the AD continuum, and in clinical trials.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, the CCA approach may de-prioritize data structures present in only one region, as it seeks to achieve consensus across the three regions, minimizing region-specific effects. As shown in Figures 2A and 2G, there are some differences between region-specific and meta-cluster assignments, so further work in individual regions is warranted to resolve local effects. Second, the molecular subtypes were derived from transcriptomic data from three brain regions (AC, DLPFC, and PCC). While these regions are clinically meaningful for cognition and vulnerable to AD pathology, they do not capture the full heterogeneity of the brain. Leveraging transcriptional similarity across regions allowed us to identify broader molecular states but also suggests that subtype generalizability may depend on sampled regions. Large-scale resources such as GTEx and the Allen Brain Atlas demonstrate substantial yet incomplete cross-regional similarity in gene expression, highlighting both shared and region-specific molecular variation. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as region- and cohort-specific and may not generalize to other cortical or subcortical regions or to cohorts with different sampling or assay modalities. Third, our large RNA-seq dataset was generated over many years in different batches; this potential technical confounder was carefully considered and incorporated into our trait analyses to avoid overcorrecting the data at earlier stages, such as when the expression residuals are calculated. Finally, ROSMAP participants are non-demented at the time of recruitment (usually in their mid-70s) and had an average age of 89.6 years at the time of death. Thus, our results may not generalize to younger individuals.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study aimed to identify transcriptome-defined subsets of aging individuals using a data-driven approach and a large collection of individuals with RNA sequence data from three brain regions involved in cognitive circuits. Participants in this study were from the Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP),3 two clinical-pathologic cohort studies of aging and dementia, one from across the United States and the other from the greater Chicago area conducted by investigators at the Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Center. ROSMAP participants span the full cognitive spectrum of the older population at the time of death—from cognitively non-impaired to mildly impaired to dementia—providing a continuous distribution that enables analytical designs independent of diagnostic categories and supports the data-driven clustering approach used in this study. After rigorous quality control and our preprocessing pipeline, we retained transcriptome RNA sequencing data from postmortem brains, including 17,143 unique genes (19,147 transcripts) in 731 participants for the AC, 16,674 unique genes (18,629 transcripts) in 1,092 participants for the DLPFC, and 16,996 unique genes (19,017 transcripts) in 661 participants for the PCC for downstream analyses. Data generation was attempted on all available samples, so there was no selection of individuals for data generation in a given region. A total of 1,149 ROSMAP participants had transcriptomic data in at least one brain region. A subset of 459 participants had RNA-seq profiles in all three brain regions and served as the Discovery cohort (Figure 1A). The remaining 690 participants had RNA-seq profiles in only one or two regions and were used as a Replication cohort. Participants were older adults recruited free of known dementia at study entry (mean age at entry = 81.0 ± 7 (SD) years), including both males and females, who underwent annual clinical and neuropsychological evaluations and signed an Anatomical Gift Act allowing brain donation at the time of death. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Sex was included as a covariate in statistical analyses where appropriate. Written informed consent and a repository consent for data sharing were obtained from all participants, and both studies were approved by an Institutional Review Board of Rush University Medical Center (IRB protocol number: AAAR4962). ROSMAP data are available through the Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Center (https/www.radc.rush.edu).

Method details

Clinical and pathological traits

H3K9ac (histone 3 lysine 9 acetylation) is a histone modification associated with open chromatin and transcriptional activity and has been shown in prior studies to be strongly associated with tau pathology in the human prefrontal cortex.27 H3K9ac data were generated using chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) in DLPFC samples from ROSMAP participants. DNA fragments bound to acetylated histones were isolated, sequenced, and mapped to the genome. An individual-level epigenomic tau score was calculated by aggregating tau-associated H3K9ac signals across the genome, providing a summary measure of tau-related chromatin remodeling in each participant. Pathological AD in ROSMAP was defined using modified National Institute on Aging–Reagan criteria, which incorporates Braak stage and CERAD score, and participants were classified as AD present (intermediate or high likelihood) or AD absent (low likelihood or no AD). We define vulnerability as the state within which risk factors have a greater impact on cognitive decline, reflecting differential modulation of risk factor effects rather than a direct causal predisposition.

RNA sequencing data quantification, pre-processing and normalization

Data was generated in multiple batches from different sequencing centers and experimental protocols. RNA sequencing of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was first done in 10 batches for 739 subjects at the Broad Institute. Samples were extracted using Qiagen’s miRNeasey Mini Kit (cat. no. 217004) and the RNase-Free DNase Set (cat. no. 79254). RNA was quantified using Nanodrop, and RNA quality was evaluated using the BioAnalyzer (Agilent) as previously described.44 Samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) score greater than 5 and a quantity threshold of 5 μg were submitted for library construction. 569 subjects in 8 batches were processed using the dUTP method and 170 subjects in 2 batches were processed using the newer Illumina TruSeq method modified by the Broad Institute Genomics Platform. Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina HiSeq2000 with 101 bp paired-end reads for a targeted coverage of 50 million paired reads. Subsequently, 124 subjects in 2 batches were sequenced at the New York Genome Center. The RNA-Seq library was prepared using the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Kit with RiboErase (Kapa Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was carried out using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequence with 2 × 100bp cycles targeting 30 million reads per sample. Details of RNA sequencing was described previously.44 Moreover, 229 samples in a single batch were sequenced at the Rush Alzheimer’s Decease Center. RNA was extracted using the Chemagic RNA tissue kit (PerkinElmer, CMG-(1212) and the RNA Quality Number (RQN) was calculated using the Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, DNF-471). 500 ng total RNA was used to generate the RNA-Seq library and rRNA was depleted with RiboGold (Illumina, 20020599). TruSeq stranded sequencing libraries (Illumina, 20020599) with custom unique dual indexes (IDT) were generated by the Zephyr G3 NGS workstation (PerkinElmer). Libraries were normalized and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 2 × 150bp paired-end reads at 40–50 million reads. Detailed RNA sequencing was described in Yu et al., 2020.44 RNA sequencing of the anterior caudate was done in 76 subjects in a single batch at the Broad Institute using the Illumina TruSeq method, as described above for the DLPFC. Subsequently, 655 subjects in 2 batches were sequenced at the New York Genome Center using the same experimental protocols described for the DLPFC. RNA sequencing of the posterior cingulate cortex was done in 79 subjects in a single batch at the Broad Institute using the Illumina TruSeq method. Subsequently, 487 subjects in 2 batches were sequenced at the New York Genome Center and 95 subjects in a single batch were sequenced at the Rush Alzheimer’s Decease Center. The experimental protocols of each sequencing center are the same as described for the DLPFC.

Each brain region was pre-processed separately. We used gene-level transcription values derived from the RNA-seq data. The transcripts per million (TPM) values were used to measure the gene expression levels and subsequently normalized using Trimmed Means of M-values (TMM) to create a frozen dataset that is available on Synapse (Synapse: syn25741873). Outlier samples were removed based on quantified expression profiles, and lowly expressed genes with a median TPM less than 10 were filtered out to reduce the influence of technical noise.

To identify potentially important covariates that should be considered for adjustment for expression modeling, for example as in the DLPFC, we first looked at the distribution of participants by batch in Figure S4A, which is generated from an MDS plot of gene transcriptions of TPM values. We observed that there was a difference between samples by batch. Next, when we explored the correlation between various variables and the first 20 principal components (PCs) from the gene transcript, we observed that some of the technical covariates and pathologic variables were highly correlated with the first few PCs (Figure S4B). We then used a forward selection method to identify covariates to be adjusted in the model. We started with a model including age, sex, and batch and then successively added more technical variables if the technical variables were significantly associated with the PCs calculated from the residuals of the previous model. The selected variables for the DLPFC were age at death, sex, and technical confounding factors, which were batch, library size, percentage of coding bases, percentage of aligned reads, percentage of ribosomal bases, percentage of UTR base, median 5 prime to 3 prime bias, median CV coverage, PMI, and study index of ROS or MAP. Technical confounding factors for AC and PCC were very similar. We adjusted for the selected variables in a linear regression and fitted for each transcript with the log2-transformed normalized TPM values as the outcome to account for differences between samples, studies, experimental batch effects, and unwanted RNA-sequencing specific technical variations. We used the residual from the transcript as our final transcription dataset. The residuals represent a quantitative trait capturing variability in transcript outcomes not captured by known demographics or technical factors.

As a sanity check, when we explored the correlation between various variables and the first 20 PCs of residuals of the final model, the selected variables that we adjusted in the model were no longer associated with PCs of residuals (Figure S4C). Adjusting for all these technical variables is more powerful than adjusting for batch alone. We adapted this approach from another reprocessing effort of the ROSMAP data.45

We elected not to over-correct our data as adjusting for unknown surrogate variables could eliminate biological signals of interest (such as meta-clusters). To address the possibility that the observed population structure could be driven by technical factors, we (1) show that this structure is associated with meaningful clinical outcomes such as cognitive decline, (2) identify concordant biological differences across non-RNA modalities (for example, the epigenomic scores and MRI measures), and (3) replicate our findings in independent ROSMAP participants and external cohorts. When we performed a surrogate variable analysis (SVA) using the R package sva46 conditioned on age, sex, amyloid, tau, and batch effects to capture latent confounders for gene expression data, we did not find surrogate variables for the three regions.

Integrating transcriptomes from multiple brain regions using sparse multiple CCA

To facilitate interpretation, we briefly summarize the overall approach. We first identify shared transcriptional features across regions using sparse multi-CCA, then group individuals within each region, and finally integrate these groupings into global meta-clusters using NMF. To identify shared transcriptional features that are jointly correlated across the three brain regions, we applied a sparse multiple canonical correlation analysis (multi-CCA) to the Discovery cohort, which includes participants with gene-expression data available for all three regions. Sparse multi-CCA estimates sets of projection vectors (canonical variates) that generate linear combinations of genes that maximize the shared correlation structure across regions, capturing cross-regional coherence in gene expression while restricting the model to a subset of informative genes. The sparsity parameters control the degree of regularization and determine which genes are retained as contributors to each canonical variate.

To determine the appropriate sparsity level, we evaluated eleven sets of sparse parameters in which the sparsity parameter for each region ranged from 2 to 20 in increments of 2 (i.e., (2,2,(2) through (20, 20, 20) for DLPFC, AC, and PCC). We used the MultiCCA.permute function in the R package PMA to select the optimal parameters. This range ensured adequate shrinkage of coefficients toward zero and prevented over-section of genes. The optimal sparsity parameters were (4,4,4), yielding the maximum sum of pairwise correlations between the first canonical variates across regions (ρˆ1 = 1.14; Figure S2A). Examination of the correlation sums across the first six canonical variates under various sparsity setting (Figures S2C and S2D) further supported (4,4,(4) as the optimal parameter set, yielding the largest overall correlation. Under this sparsity setting, approximately 30 genes contributed to each canonical variate in all three regions (Figure S2E).

The number of canonical variates was selected using the maximum eigenvalue ratio criterion.47 After applying sparse multi-CCA in the Discovery cohort, we estimated the summed pairwise correlations for each canonical variate across the three regions (Figure S2A). We considered a maximum of 10 canonical variates, consistent with other high-dimensional applications.48 We then identified where the ratio of successive correlations was highest, which corresponded to the sixth canonical variate (Figure S2B). Accordingly, we retained the first six canonical variates in each region. Sparse multi-CCA was implemented using the MultiCCA function in the R package PMA with sparse parameters (4,4,(4) and expression data were standardized to have a mean of zero and unit variance.

Integrating clusters of participants from different brain regions

We subsequently deployed k-means clustering to the first six canonical variates in each region obtained from the sparse multi-CCA to identify subgroups of participants in the Discovery cohort, using the R package stats. To determine the optimal number of clusters in each region, we evaluated models ranging from 2 to 10 clusters across three clustering methods (k-means, hierarchical clustering, and partitioning around medoids), and evaluated model quality using three internal clustering validation measures (connectivity measure, the Dunn index, and Silhouette width). As shown in Figure S1A, two clusters were optimal in all three regions, minimizing connectivity21 and maximizing both the Dunn index22 and average silhouette width23 for k-means and hierarchical clustering.

To assess cluster stability, we used the clusterwise Jaccard bootstrap mean (CJBM)24 across 1,000 bootstrapped samples, considering two to five clusters in each region under the k-means and hierarchical clustering (Figure S1B). CJBM24 measures the Jaccard similarities of the original clusters to the most similar clusters in the bootstrap-derived clusters. As shown in Figure S1B, the two-cluster solution obtained by k-means clustering was the most stable in all regions, with both clusters achieving CJBM values above 0.85. Accordingly, we selected the two-cluster k-means solution as our optimal model. These results consistently supported a two-cluster solution across methods and resampling procedures, indicating that the identified meta-clusters are robust to methodological choices. We therefore interpret these subgroups as capturing shared sources of variation across brain regions.

The two clusters identified in each brain region of the Discovery cohort were then integrated across the regions into two meta-clusters using a NMF19 approach. Cluster assignments for each participant across three regions were combined into a matrix, and NMF was applied to decompose this matrix into a basis matrix, representing how region-specific clusters map onto the meta-clusters, and a coefficient matrix, representing each participant’s meta-cluster membership. Choosing too few meta-clusters risks merging unrelated patterns, whereas too many would fail to integrate shared structure. Ideally, each region-specific cluster maps cleanly to one of the meta-clusters. The coefficient matrix in Figure 2C illustrates participant membership after NMF projection. For the Discovery cohort, the optimal number of meta-clusters was two, supported by the highest Cophenetic coefficient,25 which reflects overall clustering robustness (Figure 2C). Cluster stability was further supported by the consensus matrix, which showed a clear subdivision into two groups (Figure 2D). NMF results for the two meta-clusters are shown in Figure 2A.

To partition the Replication cohort, consisting of individuals with data from only one or two regions, we applied the CCA-derived feature weights from the Discovery cohort to generate canonical variates for each available region. Participants were then assigned to region-specific clusters based on the nearest cluster centroid defined in the Discovery cohort, leading to two clusters for each region (Figure 2). These region-specific clusters were subsequently integrated through NMF, producing two stable meta-clusters in the Replication cohort (Figure 2G).

Association test between traits and clusters

To assess whether clinico-pathologic traits were different between two transcriptomically defined meta-clusters, a regression model was fitted on the Discovery and Replication cohorts of individuals separately and evaluated the effect of meta-clusters on the trait. A linear regression model was fitted on a continuous trait and a logistic regression model was fitted on a binary trait after adjusting for age and sex. We analyzed the square root of β-amyloid and tau to transform it to follow normal distributions for modeling. We then conducted a meta-analysis of the two association studies obtained from the Discovery and Replication cohorts through a fixed-effects model with inverse-variance weighted average method. We tested the effect of meta-clusters to increase statistical power and created Figure 3A and S3A, Table 3; Tables S3–S5 and S10.

To further investigate whether the association between two clinico-pathologic traits vary by two meta-clusters, two separate regression models were fitted on individuals within each meta-cluster and evaluated the association between two traits, for each Discovery and Replication cohorts separately. A linear regression model was fitted on a continuous trait and a logistic regression model was fitted on a binary trait after adjusting for age and sex. We then conducted a meta-analysis of the two associations studies from the Discovery and Replication cohorts for each meta-clusters separately. We tested the difference in the association between two meta-clusters by fitting a single fixed-effects meta-regression model with meta-clusters as a moderator using the R package metafor and created Figure S3B; Tables S8, S9 and S12.

While we assessed the difference in clinico-pathologic traits between MC-1 and MC-2 using the meta-analysis of association results from the Discovery and Replication cohorts, for visualization purposes, we simply evaluated the difference for 1,149 ROSMAP participants with transcriptomic data in one or more brain regions to compute the p-values in Figures 3B, 3D, and 3F, and Table 2. The mean difference in a continuous trait between MC-1 and MC-2 was assessed using an independent t test or the non-parametric equivalent Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Proportions of measured variables by the two meta-clusters were assessed by chi-square tests. The normality of the data distribution was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A list of ROSMAP participants with cluster classification obtained from each brain region and meta-clusters is provided in Table S13.

To investigate the proportion of variance in cognitive decline attributable to meta-cluster membership beyond known neuropathologic determinants, we fit a sequence of linear regression models for the slope of cognitive decline in 1,149 participants (Figure 3E). First, we fit a baseline model adjusted for age and sex. Second, we added neuropathologic traits jointly, including amyloid, phosphorylated tau, TDP-43 (limbic or neocortical), cerebral atherosclerosis (moderate or severe), cerebral amyloid angiopathy (moderate or severe), arteriolosclerosis (moderate or severe), hippocampal sclerosis, and frontal white-matter R2. Third, we added meta-cluster assignment to the second model, and the incremental variance explained was quantified by the change in adjusted R-squared. For comparison, we also fit additional models that added APOEε4 or neocortical Lewy body burden to the same second model to evaluate their incremental contributions. We used analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare nested regression models and determine whether inclusion of each additional variable significantly improved model fit, and reported the corresponding p-values in Figure 3F.

Differential expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted by fitting a linear regression model on the residuals of the normalized TPM values using the R package limma.49 The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in MC-1 compared to MC-2 were identified with FDR adjusted p-value < 0.05 within each brain region. A tandem mass tag (TMT) mass spectrometry approach was used to obtain quantitative proteomics data for 596 ROSMAP participants in the DLPFC.37 Differential expression analysis for the proteomics data in the DLPFC was conducted by fitting a linear regression model on the residuals of the normalized TMT protein abundances, with FDR adjusted p-value <0.05. Spearman correlation was used to compute the correlation between gene and protein expressions in the DLPFC. Gene set enrichment analysis of DEGs comparing MC-1 to MC-2 was conducted using the enrichGO and enrichKEGG functions from the R package clusterProfiler,50 as shown in Figure 4.

Survival analysis

To estimate the age at AD onset in MC-1 and MC-2, we used the extended accelerated failure time model51 as described in Wilson et al., 2021.52 ROSMAP participants were all non-demented at study entry which can bias estimates of survival parameters due left-truncation. To adjust for the left-truncation, we used the extended accelerated failure time model with a generalized gamma distribution for a baseline age distribution52 controlling for sex and education using the flexsurvreg function from the R package flexsurv.51 We estimated the age at AD onset and the cumulative risk of AD onset for MC-1 and MC-2 as presented in Figures 3H, 3I and Table S10, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.3. Statistical methods included canonical correlation analysis, k-means clustering, non-negative matrix factorization, linear regression, logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, random-effects meta-analysis, differential expression analysis, and pathway enrichment analyses. Differential gene and protein expression analyses were adjusted for relevant covariates, and multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided, and FDR <0.05 or p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Details of these analyses, including statistical tests, sample sizes, and significance thresholds, are provided in the corresponding method details sections, results, figure legends, and tables.
