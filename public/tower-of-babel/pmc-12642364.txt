Safety and efficacy of pimavanserin in patients with Lewy body dementia experiencing dementia-related psychosis in the HARMONY study
Authors: Yasar Torres-Yaghi, Lambros Chrones, Victor Abler, Greg Brunson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPimavanserin is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson’s disease. We analyzed the effect of pimavanserin on psychosis in patients with Lewy body dementia, including Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), from the phase 3 HARMONY trial.MethodsThis subgroup analysis included patients with PDD or DLB from HARMONY, a previously completed, multicenter, randomized, discontinuation study of pimavanserin for dementia-related psychosis. Patients with moderate to severe psychosis received pimavanserin 34 mg once daily for 12 weeks (open-label treatment). Those who achieved a sustained response at weeks 8 and 12 were randomized to continue treatment with pimavanserin or discontinue treatment and receive placebo for up to 26 weeks in the double-blind period. The primary endpoint was the time to psychosis relapse as defined by prespecified criteria. Safety was evaluated by adverse events; effects on motor symptoms and cognitive abilities were also assessed.ResultsOverall, 392 patients were enrolled in HARMONY; 49 patients with PDD and 27 patients with DLB were included in the open-label safety analysis, of whom 46 met response criteria and were randomized to receive pimavanserin (n = 22) or placebo (n = 24) during the double-blind period. Psychosis relapse risk was significantly lower with pimavanserin vs. placebo (hazard ratio, 0.031; 95% CI, 0.010–0.103; 2-sided nominal P < 0.0001). Fewer than 50% of patients experienced an adverse event in either treatment period; pimavanserin did not negatively affect motor or cognitive function.ConclusionsPsychosis relapse risk was significantly lower with pimavanserin vs. placebo in patients with Lewy body dementia. Pimavanserin was also well tolerated and did not worsen motor or cognitive function. Results should be interpreted cautiously since this study is a post hoc analysis of a previous trial that was not originally designed or powered to draw clinical conclusions based on dementia subgroups.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03325556 (registered 18 October 2017).

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) are types of Lewy body dementia characterized by the presence of abnormal deposits of α-synuclein in the brain [1]. Other sporadic neurodegenerative diseases that comprise α-synucleinopathies include multiple systems atrophy, pure autonomic failure, and rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (previously reviewed in Calabresi et al. and Koga et al. [2, 3]).

Lewy body dementia is the second most common cause of neurodegenerative dementia (reviewed in Taylor et al. [4]). A meta-analysis that evaluated original epidemiological studies of dementia subtypes and DLB identified 18 population prevalence studies and 10 clinical prevalence studies. The authors found a mean prevalence of DLB of 0.36% in population studies of individuals over age 65 years (N = 26,137), and a mean prevalence of 7.5% (N = 3144) in clinical populations [5]. Additionally, cognitive impairment can exist as a presenting feature in Parkinson’s disease (reviewed in Aarsland et al. [6]). PDD was reported in approximately 80% of individuals diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease [4], with an estimated 45% to 68% of these patients exhibiting symptoms of psychosis [7]. A systematic review of neurodegenerative diseases found that the prevalence of psychosis in DLB ranges from 55.9% to 73.9% [8].

Pimavanserin, an inverse agonist and antagonist of the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor (and to a lesser extent, 5-HT2C), is currently the only treatment approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson’s disease psychosis [9]. Pimavanserin is pharmacologically distinct from other atypical antipsychotics that are sometimes used off-label for Parkinson’s disease psychosis because it has negligible binding at dopaminergic, histaminergic, adrenergic, and muscarinic receptors [9–13]. Because of its high degree of selectivity for 5-HT2A receptors, pimavanserin lacks many of the adverse effects associated with activity at off-target receptors [14]. All other atypical antipsychotics exhibit greater binding affinity for off-target receptors, potentially leading to negative off-target clinical effects that can adversely affect motor, cognitive, and autonomic functions, particularly in individuals with PDD and DLB [15, 16]. Like all other antipsychotics, pimavanserin includes a boxed warning for increased mortality when used in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis.

Pimavanserin was previously investigated for the treatment of patients with psychosis related to Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, vascular dementia, PDD, or DLB in the phase 3 HARMONY trial [17]. HARMONY was stopped early for efficacy at a prespecified interim analysis. In the full trial, patients with dementia-related psychosis who had a response to pimavanserin had a lower risk of relapse with continuation of the drug than with discontinuation (double-blind phase) [17]. The objective of this post hoc subgroup analysis was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of pimavanserin (34 mg, once daily) in patients with Lewy body dementia (PDD and DLB) from the HARMONY trial.

Methods

Study design

HARMONY (NCT03325556) was a phase 3, placebo-controlled, randomized, discontinuation study of patients with dementia and moderate to severe psychosis [17]. The full study population enrolled 392 patients with PDD (n = 59), DLB (n = 28), possible or probable Alzheimer’s disease (n = 260), frontotemporal dementia (n = 7), or vascular dementia (n = 38) [17]. The study design, eligibility criteria, and results for the primary analysis have been described in detail in a previous publication [17]. Participants included in this post hoc subgroup analysis met the clinical criteria for a diagnosis of PDD or DLB.

In HARMONY, patients received open-label pimavanserin (34 mg/d) for 12 weeks. Based on tolerability, a dose reduction to 20 mg/d was permitted during weeks 1 to 4. Those who met the prespecified criteria for treatment response at weeks 8 and 12 vs. baseline were randomized 1:1 to continue pimavanserin or receive placebo (double-blind) for ≤ 26 weeks. Prespecified criteria for treatment response required improvements in both the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms–Hallucinations + Delusions (SAPS–H + D) score (determined by a ≥ 30% reduction) [18] and Clinical Global Impression-Improvement (CGI-I) score (determined by a score of 2, much improved, or 1, very much improved) [19]. Randomization was stratified by dementia subtype, including PDD and DLB, based on clinical diagnostic criteria [20, 21]. HARMONY met the prespecified stopping boundary at the time of interim analysis, and the study was stopped early for efficacy [17].

Ethical considerations

The HARMONY trial was conducted in accordance with ethical principles of Good Clinical Practice derived from the Declaration of Helsinki in addition to other applicable and local regulatory requirements. Prior to the initiation of the HARMONY trial, the protocol was approved by institutional review boards, and all patients or authorized representatives provided written informed consent.

Study assessments

The primary efficacy endpoint in HARMONY was time from randomization to psychosis relapse during double-blind treatment. Relapse of psychosis was defined as ≥ 1 of the following: Relative to the double-blind baseline at week 12, worsening in SAPS–H + D total score (≥ 30% increase) and CGI-I score of 6 (much worse) or 7 (very much worse), treatment with an antipsychotic other than pimavanserin for dementia-related delusions and/or hallucinations, cessation of the study drug or withdrawing due to lack of efficacy, or hospitalization for worsening dementia-related psychosis. The secondary endpoint was the time from randomization to trial discontinuation for any reason.

Safety endpoints included reporting of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), physical and neurological examinations, and other clinical and laboratory evaluations. Motor-related function was assessed using the Extrapyramidal Symptom Rating Scale-Abbreviated (ESRS-A), where higher scores indicate worsened motor function (range, 0–120) [22]. Cognitive function was measured via the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), where lower scores indicate reduced cognitive function (range, 0–30) [23]. MMSE permissions were obtained via license agreement with Psychological Assessment Resources, Inc. (PAR).

Subgroup analysis

This subgroup analysis of patients with Lewy body dementia includes participants with PDD and DLB who received pimavanserin at 34 mg/d without dose reduction throughout their participation in the study. The intention-to-treat (ITT) population for the double-blind phase included patients randomized to receive placebo or to continue pimavanserin. Efficacy data (time to psychosis relapse during the double-blind period) are from the ITT population in the interim analysis with a data cutoff of July 31, 2019. Safety data (TEAEs, ESRS-A, and MMSE) were collected from the open-label and double-blind safety analysis sets, which included all patients who received ≥ 1 dose of pimavanserin or placebo.

Statistical methods

A Cox regression model was used to assess the relative risk of psychosis relapse between treatment groups for the primary and secondary efficacy endpoints. Descriptive statistics were used for safety data and baseline characteristics.

Results

Patient disposition

Among 392 patients enrolled in HARMONY, 87 had Lewy body dementia, including 59 with PDD and 28 with DLB, and were considered for this subgroup analysis. Pimavanserin was reduced to 20 mg for tolerability issues in 10 patients with PDD and 1 with DLB; 76 patients with Lewy body dementia (PDD, n = 49; DLB, n = 27) remained on 34-mg pimavanserin throughout their participation in the open-label period (open-label safety analysis set). An additional 30 patients discontinued the study in the open-label period due to lack of response per predefined criteria (n = 15), adverse event (n = 4), death (n = 1), noncompliance with the study drug (n = 2), sponsor termination (n = 6), or withdrawal of consent (n = 2). Thus, 46 of 76 patients (60.5%) with Lewy body dementia who maintained 34-mg pimavanserin throughout the open-label period met the prespecified response criteria and were randomized to receive pimavanserin (n = 22) or placebo (n = 24) (double-blind safety analysis set). At the time of the interim analysis, 7 of these patients (pimavanserin, n = 3; placebo, n = 4) had not yet begun the double-blind period and were ineligible for the efficacy analysis. The remaining 39 patients (pimavanserin, n = 19; placebo, n = 20) who had begun the double-blind phase were evaluated for efficacy in this post hoc analysis (interim ITT analysis set; Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

At the open-label baseline, the mean age of patients with Lewy body dementia was 72.8 years. Among them, 29 (38.2%) were female, 49 (64.5%) had PDD, and 27 (35.5%) had DLB. Investigator-assessed dementia severity was mild for 20 (26.3%) patients, moderate for 49 (64.5%), and severe for 7 (9.2%). The mean (SE) duration of cognitive impairment was 4.2 (0.33) years.

At the double-blind baseline, the mean age of patients with Lewy body dementia in the placebo and pimavanserin groups were 73.2 and 70.1 years, respectively. Among them, 11 (45.8%) and 8 (36.4%) were female, 20 (83.3%) and 16 (72.7%) had PDD, and 4 (16.7%) and 6 (27.3%) had DLB, respectively. Investigator-assessed dementia severity (determined by MMSE range) in the placebo and pimavanserin groups were mild for 7 (29.2%) and 8 (36.4%) patients, moderate for 14 (58.3%) and 14 (63.6%), and severe in 3 (12.5%) and 0, respectively. The mean (SE) duration of cognitive impairment was 3.6 (0.51) years in the placebo group and 4.2 (0.49) years in the pimavanserin group. Clinical efficacy

After receiving 34 mg of pimavanserin for 12 weeks during the open-label period, participants were randomized to receive either placebo or continue 34-mg pimavanserin in the double-blind period. When the trial was stopped early due to efficacy in the double-blind period, 1 patient (5.3%) in the pimavanserin group experienced a relapse of psychosis compared with 11 patients (55.0%) in the placebo group (Table 2). The risk of relapse was significantly lower with pimavanserin vs. placebo (hazard ratio [HR], 0.031; 95% CI, 0.010–0.103; 2-sided nominal P < 0.0001; Fig. 2). The time to discontinuation for any reason in patients receiving pimavanserin was significantly reduced compared with those in the placebo group (HR, 0.177; 95% CI, 0.066–0.478; 2-sided nominal P = 0.0006; Fig. 3).

Safety

Overall, < 50% of patients experienced a TEAE in the open-label or double-blind periods, and most were mild to moderate (Table 3). The most common TEAEs occurring in ≥ 2 patients during the open-label period were decreased weight, decreased appetite, and urinary tract infection (6.6% each). During the double-blind period, 4 (18%) patients treated with pimavanserin and 4 (18%) receiving placebo experienced a TEAE. In the pimavanserin group, TEAEs included prolonged QT interval (mean ± SE prolongation calculated using Fridericia’s formula) and anxiety, each occurring in 2 (9.1%) patients. In the placebo group, 2 (8.3%) patients experienced hypertension, and 2 (8.3%) patients experienced decreased weight. TEAEs leading to discontinuation occurred in 7 (9.2%) patients during the open-label period and 3 (6.5%) patients during the double-blind period (placebo, 2 [8.3%]; pimavanserin, 1 [4.5%]).

Motor and cognitive function assessments

In the open-label period, the mean ESRS-A change from the open-label baseline to week 12 was − 1.6 (SE, 0.60) among patients receiving pimavanserin. In the double-blind treatment period, the mean ESRS-A scores remained similar from the double-blind baseline through week 26 for patients randomized to pimavanserin and placebo (Fig. 4), thus suggesting no negative effect on motor function. The mean MMSE change from the open-label baseline to week 12 was 0.6 (SE, 0.55). As with ESRS-A scores, the mean MMSE scores in the double-blind period were similar between the double-blind baseline and week 26 for both groups (Fig. 5), thus suggesting no negative impact on cognitive function.

Discussion

In this post hoc subgroup analysis of patients with Lewy body dementia (including PDD and DLB) in HARMONY, 60.5% (74% with PDD and 37% with DLB) met the prespecified response criteria for psychosis improvement during 12 weeks of open-label treatment with 34 mg of pimavanserin. During the double-blind period, treatment with pimavanserin also reduced the risk of psychosis relapse in these patients by approximately 97% compared with placebo (HR, 0.031; 95% CI, 0.010–0.103; 2-sided nominal P < 0.0001) and reduced the risk of discontinuation for any reason by approximately 88% compared with placebo (HR, 0.177; 95% CI, 0.066–0.478; 2-sided nominal P = 0.0006). Taken together, these data indicate that initial efficacy was maintained throughout the study duration. Additionally, pimavanserin was well tolerated and did not worsen motor or cognitive function, thus suggesting that tolerability may have been a factor in the reduced risk of discontinuation.

Efficacy and safety data from this subgroup analysis are consistent with those of the full HARMONY population [17] and of another HARMONY subgroup analysis in patients with only PDD [10]. In the full population, psychosis relapse was reported in 13% of patients in the pimavanserin group and 28% in the placebo group (HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.17–0.73; P = 0.005) vs. 5.3% of patients with Lewy body dementia (DLB and PDD) in the pimavanserin group and 55.0% in the placebo group (HR, 0.031; 95% CI, 0.010–0.103; 2-sided nominal P < 0.0001) as reported herein [17]. Discontinuation for any reason in the full HARMONY population occurred in 22% of patients in the pimavanserin group vs. 38% in the placebo group (HR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.26–0.79; P = 0.005) vs. 26.3% of patients with DLB and PDD in the pimavanserin group and 70.0% in the placebo group (HR, 0.177; 95% CI, 0.066–0.478; 2-sided nominal P = 0.0006) reported in the present analysis [17]. In the previously published subgroup analysis of patients with PDD, 7.7% in the pimavanserin group and 52.9% in the placebo group experienced relapse (HR, 0.052; 95% CI, 0.016–0.166; 1-sided nominal P < 0.001) [10]. Although subgroup analysis of patients with DLB is not available owing to small sample size, favorable responses in this subset indicate that pimavanserin may offer therapeutic value in select cases.

HARMONY expanded the patient population and treatment duration originally explored in the pivotal phase 3 trial that supported US approval of pimavanserin in patients with Parkinson’s disease psychosis [24]. The pivotal trial included only patients with Parkinson’s disease psychosis with MMSE scores ≥ 21 to 30, whereas HARMONY also enrolled patients with dementia and MMSE scores between 6 and 24. This accounts for the differences in mean MMSE scores between the pivotal trial and the HARMONY trial, as well as our subgroup analysis in patients with PDD and DLB. Furthermore, HARMONY included a 12-week open-label period with an additional 26 weeks of treatment in a discontinuation design [17] compared with 6 weeks of treatment in the phase 3 pivotal trial in patients with Parkinson’s disease psychosis [24].

Lewy body dementias have several overlapping features and little comprehensive guidance for patient management (reviewed in Taylor et al. [4]). Clinical features of Parkinson’s disease and DLB, along with indicative biomarkers identified through imaging studies, drive diagnosis in clinical settings. DLB is diagnosed when cognitive decline presents before or within 1 year after motor symptom onset, while PDD is characterized by cognitive decline manifesting in the presence of established PD ≥ 1 year after motor symptom onset [25, 26]. In research, pathological α-synuclein is more commonly assessed. Development of the seed amplification assay to allow detection of α-synuclein in clinical samples and other recent evidence have led to the proposal for a biological definition of Parkinson’s disease and DLB as neuronal α-synuclein diseases rather than by clinical features alone [1]. Additionally, skin biopsies for detecting phosphorylated α-synuclein are now used in clinical practice as a specimen biomarker for patients who exhibit clinical features consistent with α-synucleinopathies [27].

Our subgroup analysis demonstrates that the efficacy and safety of pimavanserin may extend to patients with Lewy body dementia, warranting further research in these patients beyond the indicated population of patients with hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson’s disease psychosis.

As a subgroup analysis of the HARMONY trial, this analysis carries many of the limitations of the results from the full analysis set, with additional considerations. HARMONY was not designed or powered to demonstrate effects by dementia type; therefore, results from this post hoc analysis should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, clinical features were used as diagnostic criteria for DLB and PDD, as biomarkers were not available as a diagnostic tool at the start of HARMONY [20]. This may have led to possible misdiagnoses in the study population [28]. Similar to the full population study, this analysis is subject to potential selection bias inherent in randomized discontinuation trials, which typically include only patients who seem to have responded to treatment, resulting in a sample enriched with responders. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable. Moreover, the study was halted early due to efficacy, resulting in a lower total number of participants evaluated than initially anticipated; only a small number of patients completed the 26-week double-blind period (pimavanserin: n = 6; placebo: n = 2). The small number of patients with DLB in the safety analysis (n = 10) may result in an underestimation of the side effects associated with pimavanserin in this subgroup.

Conclusion

This post hoc subgroup analysis offers additional evidence supporting the use of pimavanserin in the treatment of hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson’s disease, for which the treatment is approved by the FDA. This analysis emphasizes the necessity of a longer and larger clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of pimavanserin in the management of dementia-related psychosis in patients with Lewy body dementia.
