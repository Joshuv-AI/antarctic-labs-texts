The Effects of Acute Caffeine Supplementation in Combination with Postactivation Performance Enhancement on Performance and Fatigue in High-Level Male Volleyball Athletes
Authors: Marjan Shidai, Hamid Rajabi, Neda Khaledi, Aref Basereh, Somayeh Ahmadabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile both post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) and caffeine (CAF) independently enhance performance, their combined effects on physical performance and the levels of central and peripheral fatigue remain unexplored.ObjectivesThis study assessed the impact of the PAPE method and CAF supplementation on central and peripheral fatigue following a fatigue-inducing protocol in elite male volleyball athletes.MethodsThe study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit 40 voluntary participants (18–30 y), who were then divided into 4 groups: PAPE group (N = 10), CAF group (N = 10), PAPE + CAF group (CAF + PAPE, N = 10), and control group (CG, N = 10). The CAF group received a CAF supplement of 6 mg/kg of body mass 20 min before the warming up for 10 min, whereas the PAPE group performed 2 sets of back squats at 80% of 1RM (4 reps/set) 2 min before the protocol.ResultsBetween-group comparisons revealed that the PAPE + CAF group demonstrated a significant improvement in the performance variables, including time to exhaustion, number of rounds, and serum dopamine concentrations, compared with all other groups (P < 0.05), whereas serum ammonia, serotonin, and prolactin concentrations decreased significantly compared with all other groups (P < 0.05). The CAF group also showed higher values in these performance and biochemical measures than the CG and PAPE groups, but these were lower than those in the CAF + PAPE group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found for rating of perceived exertion or uric acid (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThis study confirms CAF's primary role in enhancing performance and reducing fatigue. The combined CAF + PAPE intervention provided a distinct advantage only for the number of rounds completed, but showed no significant benefit over CAF alone for time to exhaustion or most biochemical markers.Keywords: voluntary conditioning activity, fatigue, volleyball, caffeine, central fatigue

Introduction

Volleyball, as a team sport characterized by explosive movements such as jumping, defense, and spikes and short-time rally, predominantly relies on anaerobic metabolism. Although volleyball is inherently high-intensity and frequently induces fatigue, variables such as match intensity, opponent skill level, and the natural pauses between rallies are also key determinants of a player's fatigue [1]. Sports professionals and coaches are always looking for methods to boost physical performance. One of the widely studied approaches is postactivation performance enhancement (PAPE), known for enhancing voluntary muscle performance [2,3]. This effect is typically achieved by performing a voluntary conditioning activity (CA) before a similar, explosive movement. Although the precise mechanisms behind PAPE remain uncertain, factors such as increased muscle activation, elevated temperature, and heightened muscle water content are thought to play roles in the observed performance gains [2,4].

Although PAPE alone provides an effective strategy for enhancing muscle performance, combining it with other methods may offer an additive effect [5]. In particular, caffeine (CAF) administration has shown potential for further enhancing explosive exercise performance [6,7]. CAF acts as an adenosine receptor antagonist in the central nervous system, which helps maintain neuron excitability, neutralizes central fatigue, and reduces pain perception during activity [8]. In addition, CAF promotes the release of calcium and its uptake and enhances the activity of the calcium-potassium pump. Therefore, CAF plays a supportive role in delaying fatigue by affecting the central and peripheral nervous systems [9]. Studies by Cardoso et al. [10] and Pereira et al. [11] have shown that CAF can decrease both nerve and muscle fatigue through its modulation of the central nervous system. In addition, the advantages of PAPE, which enhance muscle response and power, have been documented across various sports-specific tasks [[12], [13], [14]].

By targeting different aspects of fatigue (CAF for energy and endurance, PAPE for power and muscle activation), combining these methods could provide a more comprehensive way to sustain performance during prolonged or intense matches. A combination of plyometric exercises and sled towing with CAF ingestion (5 mg/kg body mass) significantly enhanced jumping performance compared with the same CA with a placebo among male soccer players [14]. In contrast, another study demonstrated that a single set of heavy-loaded back squats (at 80% 1RM) with controlled velocity, used as a CA, enhanced subsequent countermovement jump performance in female volleyball players. However, they found no additional effect of CAF (6 mg/kg) on performance [13]. Although previous studies have provided valuable insights, some limitations remain [15,16]. One such limitation is the use of a moderate CAF dose (3 mg/kg). Although effective and common in research, a higher dose might have elicited a more pronounced ergogenic effect [14]. However, a more fundamental gap exists, as no research has yet examined the combined effects of CAF and PAPE on both performance and physiological markers, leaving a critical gap in understanding their integrated impact.

Considering that the combined use of the PAPE protocol along with CAF ingestion may be a promising strategy for acute sports performance enhancement, this study aimed to investigate whether combining CAF supplementation with PAPE can reduce fatigue and improve performance in volleyball players undergoing a fatiguing protocol, such as the Wikstrom protocol. It was hypothesized that the combination of these 2 interventions would significantly reduce fatigue and enhance performance compared with either method alone or a control condition.

Methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G∗Power version 3.1.9.7 (University of Kiel), which determined that a minimum sample size of 8 participants per group was necessary. To enhance the robustness of the study, 10 participants were recruited for each group, representing a 20% increase over the calculated minimum. The power analysis was based on an assumed statistical power of 0.95, an alpha level of 0.05, an effect size (ES) of 1.46, and a correlation coefficient of 0.5 for repeated measures. The selected ES was derived from prior research evaluating the effects of CAF combined with PAPE compared with placebo on physical performance outcomes [15]. The study involved 40 elite male volleyball players from the Iranian volleyball league, who volunteered to participate (Table 1). Participant recruitment took place between 20 October, 2023 and 05 January, 2024. Before the commencement of the study, participants were briefed on the research objectives and provided written Informed consent. The study protocol also adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Sport Sciences Research Institute review board (SSRI.REC-2302-2118). Athletes were initially selected based on the following inclusion criteria: 1) training and competing regularly in the past and current year; 2) ≥18 y old; 3) signing the Free and Informed Consent Form; 4) commitment to participate in all phases of this study; 5) ≥7 y’ experience in performance training. Also, to account for individual differences in habitual responsiveness to CAF because of tolerance, only participants with a daily CAF intake of ˃50 mg/d were included [17]. Participants underwent physical examinations, testing, and medical interviews conducted by a physician to confirm their fitness for the experiment and rule out any injuries or neurologic/musculoskeletal conditions that could impact the results. Failure to complete all phases of the study because of side effects, injury, or other unforeseen circumstances was treated as an exclusion criterion. In addition, for clearer and more reliable results, participants were instructed to abstain from consuming nutritional supplements, alcohol, and antioxidant medications for ≤1 mo before the study. Also, participants were instructed to avoid consuming caffeinated beverages for 48 h before the test, and compliance was verified by asking them (by prestudy questionnaire) about their adherence just before the testing session.

Study design

A quasi-experimental research approach utilizing a parallel design and pretest posttest design was employed in this study. The participants arrived in the laboratory at the same time of day as the upcoming experimental sessions (in the evening between 16:00 and 18:00). A familiarization session took place 1 wk before the main protocol, during which participants were introduced to the study procedures and became accustomed to the equipment and tasks. During this session, anthropometric measurements such as height, body mass, and body composition were recorded. Body mass was assessed using a scale with a 300 kg capacity and 20 g precision (Platform Scales 8501AL-150; Co. Iran). Height was recorded to the nearest millimeter using a Seca Stable Stadiometer 220 (Co. Germany). Body composition was estimated via tetrapolar bioelectrical impedance (Biodynamics 310 model A; biodynamics corp), with participants in a supine position on a stretcher. All standard guidelines for this measurement were carefully adhered to. Before enrollment, all participants were screened to confirm resistance training experience and technical proficiency in the barbell back squat exercise, as it was a fundamental component of their habitual training programs. Following a standard 10-min warm-up, the 1-RM test was conducted. During the test, if a lift was successful, the load was increased by 5–20 lbs (2.5–9 kg) for the subsequent attempt. This process was repeated until the participant failed to complete the lift. The last successfully lifted weight was then recorded as the 1-RM. This protocol was typically completed within 3–6 attempts, with rest intervals of 3–5 min allowed between each attempt [18]. After confirming no significant baseline differences following the 1RM test (Table 2), participants were randomly (computer-generated randomization process) assigned to the 4 experimental groups: the PAPE group, the CAF consumption group, the PAPE + CAF group, and the control group (CG) (Figure 1).

CAF supplementation

The CAF group received a CAF supplement (Sobhan Daro Co.) in gelatin capsules containing ≤600 mg anhydrous CAF, which were uncoated for rapid dissolution and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. Participants ingested pure CAF at a dosage of 6 mg/kg of body mass, packaged in capsules, 20 min before warming-up for 10 min [11,19].

Voluntary CA protocol

In this study, the CA protocol involved performing 2 sets of back squats at 80% of the participant’s 1RM, each consisting of 4 repetitions. The back squat was chosen as it engages large muscle groups, particularly the lower body, which is highly relevant to volleyball performance. This squat-based PAPE was performed 2 min before the Wikstrom fatiguing protocol to maximize the potentiation effect [20].

Wikstrom protocol

The Wikstrom 6-station protocol exhibits a closer resemblance to sports activities such as volleyball and competitive events compared with other protocols, enhancing its applicability to fatigue induced by sports training and competition [21]. To facilitate familiarization and warm-up, participants engaged in practice sessions at all 6 stations:

Exhaustion time and rating of perceived exertion measurement

During the familiarization and subsequent testing sessions, participants' performance was evaluated through exhaustion time and the rating of perceived exertion (RPE). Exhaustion time was defined as the duration taken to complete the Wikstrom protocol until the onset of fatigue, which was operationally defined as a 50% increase in completion time compared with each participant's initial maximal effort run. Timings were recorded using an infrared timing device to ensure precision in measurements. Also, participants were instructed to assess their perceived exertion using the Borg Scale (6–20 scale) immediately after each trial of the Wikstrom protocol. This subjective measure offers valuable insights into the athletes' perceived effort and fatigue levels during the exercise [23]. Because the groups' number of rounds during the exhaustion period was unequal, the RPE was divided by the number of rounds.

Biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected 1 h preintervention and postintervention (end of Wikstrom protocol). After collecting 10 mL of blood from a forearm vein into BD Vacutainer K2 EDTA tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company), the samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 × g using a Combi-514R centrifuge (Hanil). Following centrifugation, plasma was isolated for analysis. The collected plasma was used to analyze peripheral fatigue markers, including ammonia and uric acid, using a Cobas c 311 biochemical autoanalyzer (Roche Diagnostics), employing spectrophotometric methods. For the analysis of central fatigue parameters, such as serotonin, dopamine, and prolactin, an ELISA was conducted using specific ELISA kits and measured with a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices).

Statistical analysis

Data processing was conducted using SPSS software version 23.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics, including mean and SD, were computed for the measured variables. The normality of data distribution was checked using Shapiro–Wilk tests. Group comparisons for time to exhaustion were performed using a 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni's post hoc test because the data met normality assumptions. In contrast, RPE and the number of rounds were examined by the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test because the Shapiro–Wilk test revealed a significant departure of data from normality. In addition, comparisons between groups for peripheral fatigue parameters (ammonia and uric acid) and central fatigue parameters (serotonin, dopamine, and prolactin) were analyzed using a repeated measures ANOVA (group × time) test, followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. ES was estimated by Kendall’s coefficient of concordance, and the magnitude of mean differences was expressed with standardized ES; thresholds for qualitative descriptors of Cohen’s d were defined: ≤0.20 as “small”, 0.21–0.8 “medium,” and >0.80 as “large” [24]. Also, the percentage difference was determined by calculating the difference between the posttest value and the pretest (initial) value, dividing this difference by the initial value, and subsequently multiplying by 100.

Results

Effect of interventions on time to exhaustion, number of rounds, and RPE

The results showed that there is a significant difference between the groups in the time to exhaustion (F(3,39) = 14.64, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.549). Bonferroni's post hoc test showed that the time to exhaustion in the CAF + PAPE and CAF groups showed a significant increase of 76% [741.40 compared with 420.30, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.58, 95% confidence interval (CI) for Cohen’s d = 1.07, 4.09] and 69% (711.60 compared with 420.30, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.34, 95% CI for Cohen’s d = 0.87, 3.81) compared with the CG and 36% (741.40 compared with 542.90, P = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 1.59, 95% CI for Cohen’s d = –0.24, 2.95) and 31% (711.60 compared with 542.90, P = 0.022, Cohen’s d = 0.24, 95% CI for Cohen’s d = –1.01, 1.49) compared with the PAPE group, respectively (Figure 3).

The results showed no significant difference between the groups in RPE at the time of reaching exhaustion X2(3, 40) = 5.907, P = 0.116. Also, for the number of rounds, the results showed that there was a significant difference in the number of rounds between the groups (F = 28.59, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.731). Discussion

The results of our study indicated that the consumption of CAF alone and in combination with PAPE before exercise led to an extension in time to exhaustion among the participants. Despite no significant difference in the RPE, the number of rounds in the PAPE, CAF, and CAF + PAPE groups exhibited better outcomes compared with the CG. Previous studies have shown that exercise performance improves after CAF consumption [9,[25], [26], [27], [28]], performing a precontraction warm-up (PAPE) [29,30], and a combination of CAF and PAPE [14]. The performance enhancement after CAF consumption may be attributed to the ergogenic effects of CAF in diminishing pain perception [31,32], reducing RPE, and consequently delaying the onset of fatigue [33]. It has been found that recreational runners selected faster treadmill velocities (∼5 m/min) when producing prescribed RPE levels following CAF ingestion (6 mg/kg) compared with placebo [32]. In alignment with our study's findings, it has been found that 6 mg/kg CAF doses significantly improved 60-min time trial performance (time to exhaustion) by 4.2% and 2.9%, respectively [34]. Furthermore, another study demonstrated that after 60 min of CAF ingestion (3 mg/kg), participants performed a 15-s maximal jump test, a repeated sprint test (7 × 30 m; 30 s of active recovery), and played a simulated soccer game, which resulted in increased high-intensity running distance and sprint frequency in male semiprofessional players [35]. In contrast, other studies have shown that CAF (6 mg/kg) provides no ergogenic benefit for explosive performance in females, which may be attributed to sex-based differences in muscle fiber type and the resultant PAPE response [13]. Improvements in exercise performance and time to exhaustion following CAF ingestion appear to be attributable to a reduction in pain perception and a delay in the onset of fatigue [8]. The reduction in pain perception may be due to an increase in β-endorphin concentrations in the participants' bodies, although the measurement of β-endorphin concentrations was not conducted in the current study. However, previous studies have shown that CAF consumption increases β-endorphin concentrations [36]. On the other hand, it seems that using more fat instead of carbohydrates after consuming CAF can be effective in reducing fatigue. This is due to the increase in cellular AMP through the inhibition of the enzyme phosphodiesterase by CAF, which leads to ATP production and stimulation of lipolysis in fat tissues [37]. This process results in a greater utilization of fatty acids instead of muscle glycogen, thus delaying the onset of fatigue [38]. In addition, CAF functions as an adenosine receptor antagonist. By blocking these receptors, it attenuates adenosine's inhibitory effects on the central nervous system. This mechanism consequently enhances neural excitability, diminishes the perception of fatigue, and ultimately improves exercise performance [39].

Furthermore, the present study demonstrated that performing PAPE alone resulted in a nonsignificant increase in time to exhausting compared with the CG (29%). This nonsignificant increase in time to exhausting, along with a significant increase in the number of repetitions performed during the Wikstrom fatigue test, suggests that performing PAPE during the warm-up phase can lead to relative improvements in performance. Various studies have shown that sports performance improves after a preparatory activity [1,29,30]. For example, a vertical jump height increased in both female and male volleyball players after a preparatory contraction. From the perspective of these researchers, athletes can benefit more from the beneficial effects of PAPE to improve their performance [1]. Despite the coexistence of fatigue and PAPE, the interaction between these 2 factors determines whether there will be a decrease or an increase in sports performance. For instance, another study examined the effects of PAPE on endurance activities and demonstrated that if central and peripheral fatigue decreases or if PAPE can overcome fatigue, it can be utilized in endurance activities [40]. However, the results of the current study showed an increase in central and peripheral fatigue levels after the Wikstrom fatigue protocol in the control and PAP groups. On the other hand, the lower fatigue levels observed in the CAF + PAPE group were associated with an enhancement in sports performance. On the basis of previous research, PAPE mechanisms can be classified into functional and mechanistic approaches. In endurance activities lasting over a minute, functional factors such as increased blood flow, muscle temperature, and motivation are thought to have a more significant impact on performance enhancement [4].

This study assessed markers such as prolactin, serotonin, and dopamine to measure central fatigue, and uric acid and ammonia to measure peripheral fatigue [[41], [42], [43]]. The current study showed that regardless of the group type, the fatigue protocol (Wikstrom) led to elevated levels of all research variables in the posttest except for uric acid. Although direct comparisons with previous studies are challenging because of the unique protocol, it is known that high-intensity exercise can increase serotonin concentrations contributing to fatigue [44]. Other studies have also shown that variables such as dopamine and prolactin increase [42,45]. The present study also showed that the concentrations of prolactin in the CAF + PAPE and the CAF groups were significantly lower by 32% and 38% compared with the CG, and by 30% and 35% compared with the PAPE group, respectively. In addition, the serotonin concentrations in the CAF + PAPE and the CAF groups were significantly lower by 23% and 25% compared with the CG, and by 20% and 22% compared with the PAPE group, respectively. On the other hand, the dopamine concentrations in the CAF + PAPE and the CAF groups were significantly higher by 9% and 3% compared with the CG, and by 2.5% in the CAF + PAPE group compared with the PAPE group.

The study revealed a significantly lower concentration of prolactin associated with CAF intake, without any difference between the PAPE and CG. It appears that the implementation of PAPE had no effect on altering this marker, similar to other central and peripheral fatigue variables, and the majority of these changes can be attributed to the ergogenic effect of CAF. CAF's mechanism involves suppressing prolactin secretion from lactotroph cells located in the anterior pituitary gland [46]. Prolactin may serve as a marker of the peripheral balance between serotonergic and dopaminergic activity in the brain. Previous research has suggested that various factors, including psychological, environmental, and physical stimuli, can elevate prolactin concentrations [42,43]. A study demonstrated that prolactin concentrations increase after exhaustive exercise [42]. In the present study, prolactin was significantly higher in the PAPE and CG, whereas they were lower in the other 2 groups because of lower CAF consumption. In contrast to our study's results, previous findings indicated comparable prolactin concentrations in both CAF and placebo groups, possibly attributed to the minimal CAF dosage (3 mg) administered [47]. CAF induces a decrease in prolactin secretion from lactotroph cells in the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland [46].

On one hand, the observed higher prolactin concentrations in both the control and PAPE groups may be attributed to the concurrent rise in serotonin concentrations, as indicated by a positive and significant correlation between serotonin and prolactin in the present study. Conversely, the correlation test results revealed a significant negative relationship between dopamine and serotonin, as well as between prolactin. Previous research utilizing animal models has demonstrated that prolonged physical activity leads to an increase in dopamine concentrations and a decrease in serotonin concentrations, a phenomenon consistent with the findings of this study [48]. Specifically, the lower dopamine concentrations are associated with increased serotonin synthesis and secretion, a relationship that has been linked to fatigue by other study [49]. The current study also showed that the ratio of dopamine to serotonin was significantly higher in the CAF and CAF + PAPE groups, indicating elevated dopamine concentrations alongside lowered serotonin concentrations. This aligns with a previous study, which reported that CAF intake results in increased dopamine and decreased serotonin concentrations [50]. However, there was no significant difference in this ratio between the CAF and CAF + PAPE groups (˃5%). Notably, the CAF + PAPE group exhibited significantly higher dopamine secretion compared with other groups, potentially due to higher dopamine secretion during physical activity, possibly stimulated by the tuberoinfundibular dopamine pathway [51]. Moreover, literature suggests that higher dopamine concentrations can suppress prolactin secretion [42,52]. Despite observing a slight decrease in dopamine concentrations in the PAPE and CG in the current study, this could be attributed to the short exercise duration and the timing of blood sample collection 1-h postactivity, whereas other studies have measured immediately [45]. The absence of metabolite measurements in this study could be considered a limitation, as metabolites such as lactate, which increase during fatigue protocols, can impact dopamine and serotonin secretion. The 1-h interval postactivity may have led to a reduction in these metabolites, potentially influencing the observed discrepancies in results [53]. In addition, higher dopamine secretion was noted in groups consuming CAF, likely due to CAF's inhibition of adenosine receptors [52]. Furthermore, our findings indicate that CAF supplementation resulted in lower serotonin concentrations measured 1-h after exercise. This suggests that CAF may enhance individual performance by modulating serotonin secretion. Although the ergogenic effects of CAF have been well-documented in various studies [10,34,35], it is important to note that serotonin, dopamine, and prolactin interact in a complex manner within the brain [54]. Specifically, an increase in serotonin synthesis and secretion typically corresponds to a lower dopamine concentrations [55]. Studies on mice subjected to exhaustive protocols have corroborated this relationship by demonstrating lower serotonin concentrations alongside higher dopamine concentrations in the brain [48].

Uric acid and ammonia have been utilized as indicators of peripheral fatigue in various research studies [43,56]. In the current study, no significant difference was found in uric acid concentrations between groups; however, ammonia, another marker associated with peripheral fatigue, was significantly lower (by 28.5% and 27%) in the CAF + PAPE group and the CAF group, respectively, in comparison to the PAPE and CG. The rapid elimination of fatigue-related compounds from the circulatory system plays a crucial role in delaying the onset of performance decline in sports activities and facilitating sustained physical activity. Previous literature has indicated that during intense activity, the accumulation of muscle acidity triggers the release of ammonia [57]. Consistent with this, the current study revealed a significant elevation in ammonia concentrations in the PAPE and CG postexercise compared with baseline measurements. Given the exhaustive protocol of the current study, it seems that the increase in lactate has caused a further increase in ammonia in these groups. Conversely, in the groups supplemented with CAF, a lower ammonia concentration was observed, potentially attributable to the ergogenic properties of CAF.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CAF supplementation is a primary and effective strategy for enhancing performance and attenuating markers of central and peripheral fatigue. The critical finding is that although the combined CAF + PAPE intervention was effective, its superiority was specific to the number of rounds completed, where it outperformed all other groups, including CAF alone. For the other key performance metric, time to exhaustion, as well as for most biochemical markers, the addition of PAPE to CAF did not yield a statistically significant additional benefit. Therefore, the ergogenic benefits observed are predominantly attributable to CAF, with the combination providing a distinct advantage only in a specific aspect of performance. Future research could explore optimal dosages, timing, and individual responses to CAF and PAPE to refine their application in sports performance further.
