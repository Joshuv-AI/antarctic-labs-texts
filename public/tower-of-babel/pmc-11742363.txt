Advances in the awareness of tuberculosis-associated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Xiaoyan Gai, Brian Allwood, Yongchang Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) significantly increases the risk of developing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), positioning TB-associated COPD (TB-COPD) as a distinct category within the spectrum of respiratory diseases prevalent, especially in low- and middle-income countries. This condition results from the body's immune response to TB, leading to prolonged inflammation and consequent persistent lung damage. Diagnostic approaches, particularly post-bronchodilator spirometry, are vital for identifying airflow obstruction and confirming TB-COPD. Furthermore, exploring potential biomarkers is crucial for a deeper insight into the pathogenesis of TB-COPD and the improvement of treatment strategies. Currently, this condition is primarily managed using inhaled bronchodilators, with cautious use of inhaled corticosteroids advised owing to the increased risk of developing TB. This review delves into the epidemiology, clinical manifestations, pulmonary function, and imaging characteristics of TB-COPD, scrutinizing current and prospective biomarkers and therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, it underscores the necessity for focused research to bridge the knowledge and treatment gaps in this complex condition.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Tuberculosis-associated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Post-tuberculosis lung disease, Lung function

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) poses a significant global health burden, characterized by chronic, persistent, and irreversible airflow limitation. According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2024 estimates, the global prevalence of COPD stands at approximately 10.3%,1,2 making it the third leading cause of death worldwide. In the mainland of China, nearly 100 million individuals suffer from COPD, with a prevalence rate of 13.7% among adults aged ≥40 years.3 Smoking remains the most common risk factor for developing COPD; however, the prevalence of non-smoking-related COPD (S-COPD) has gained recognition in recent years.

Tuberculosis (TB), an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), continues to pose a significant global health challenge. According to the World Health Organization's 2023 Global TB Report, there were approximately 10.6 million new TB cases and around 1.3 million TB-related deaths in 2022.4 Notably, 30–50% of individuals with TB exhibit chronic respiratory symptoms, pulmonary imaging abnormalities, and decreased lung function. The First International Post-Tuberculosis Lung Disease Symposium defines post-TB lung disease (PTLD) as “evidence of chronic respiratory abnormality, with or without symptoms, attributable at least in part to previous tuberculosis.” It encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including obstructive lung disease, bronchiectasis, cavitary disease, fibrotic lung disease, pleural disease, and pulmonary vascular disease.5 The relationship between TB and COPD is complex and dynamic.6, 7, 8 Allwood et al9 proposed the concept of TB-associated COPD (TB-COPD) based on the distinct pathophysiological differences between TB-related airflow limitation and S-COPD. TB-COPD, sometimes called tuberculosis-associated obstructive pulmonary disease (TOPD), is increasingly recognized as an independent subtype of COPD.10

Recent evidence suggests a strong association between a history of TB and chronic airflow obstruction. An analysis of the Burden of Obstructive Lung Disease study involving individuals aged over 40 years from 19 global research centers revealed that individuals with a history of TB showed more than twice the risk of developing airflow obstruction than those without a history of TB (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.83–3.42).11 Our previous studies indicated that 43.1% of Chinese patients with COPD exhibited computed tomography (CT) signs indicative of old pulmonary TB.12 In the Tibetan Plateau region of China, 22.6% of patients with COPD reported a previous history of TB, and 45.1% of lung CT scans of patients with COPD revealed “old pulmonary TB” lesions.13 A 2021 study in China involving a sample of 8680 individuals found that 610 participants (7.0%) had a history of TB or chest imaging signs of old pulmonary TB, and 21.3% (130/610) had airflow obstruction, which was significantly associated with a history of TB (OR, 1.31).14 The PLATINO study from Latin America showed that the overall prevalence of airflow obstruction in individuals with a history of TB was 30.7%, significantly higher than that in individuals without a history of TB (13.9%).15 A meta-analysis indicated that a history of TB increases the risk of developing COPD by 2.59 times.16

TB significantly increases the risk of COPD among non-smokers. A comprehensive epidemiological survey conducted in China revealed that the incidence of airflow obstruction in non-smoking individuals was 4.0% in women and 5.1% in men. Notably, for non-smokers with a history of TB, these rates increased substantially to 7.9% in women and 9.6% in men, underscoring the association between TB and COPD even in non-smokers.17

Low- and middle-income countries are significantly affected by TB and COPD. A comprehensive study involving cohorts from 13 countries and territories reported a COPD prevalence of 8.8%. Notably, the prevalence of COPD was three times higher in individuals with a history of TB than in those without (25.7% vs. 8.3%, P < 0.001).18 High-income areas, such as Denmark, have a relatively low TB prevalence of approximately 0.5%; however, approximately 52% of individuals with TB have TB-COPD, highlighting a considerable and critical disease burden.19

Pathology and pathophysiology

The pathogenic mechanisms of TB-COPD, which have not been fully elucidated, are predominantly associated with chronic inflammatory and repair responses. These mechanisms tend to be initiated by TB infection, leading to enduring airflow obstruction. Mtb inhalation triggers alveolar macrophages to engulf the bacterium, setting off a complex immune response that includes macrophage migration, apoptosis, and enlistment of additional immune cells, notably activating CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes. These immune cells release inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma, which further recruit immune cells and provoke localized inflammatory reactions. Although intended to restrict the spread of Mtb, this prolonged inflammatory response can cause significant damage to lung tissue. The immune defense against TB results in the formation of granulomas within which Mtb multiplies. Mtb is then released due to the death of alveolar macrophages and the rupture and leakage of these granulomas. This leads to spreading of the infection and ultimately to lung damage through the creation of pulmonary cavities. Owing to chronic inflammation, the immune system remains constantly activated. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), produced by macrophages, neutrophils, and stromal cells, play a pivotal role in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix. Enzymes such as MMP-1 and MMP-9 are closely linked to the development of pulmonary cavities and disintegration of alveolar partitions, thereby significantly driving the progression of emphysema.20 Furthermore, an increase in transforming growth factor-beta levels precipitates lung fibrosis and pleural thickening, potentially exacerbating lung compliance and function reduction.21,22 Environmental exposures, tobacco smoke, and repeated infections can further aggravate TB-COPD, exacerbating the disease's severity (Fig. 1).

Bronchial mucosal TB and non-specific inflammation are commonly detected in lung tissue samples obtained from patients after TB surgery. Not only does TB affect the larger airways but disseminated and regional involvement of the smaller airways is frequently observed in CT imaging of patients with TB, as evidenced by the tree-in-bud infiltrates and gas-trapping on expiratory scans.23,24 These conditions can lead to localized obstruction or narrowing of the bronchial and bronchiolar lumina, as well as pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema, consequently diminishing lung tissue compliance. Widespread fibrosis and calcification further decrease lung compliance during the repair phase following TB.25 Allwood et al26 initially coined the term “TOPD” to describe airflow obstruction resulting from pulmonary TB, leveraging the unique physiological and radiological distinctions between airflow obstruction caused by smoking and TB. Notably, a case involving a 23-year-old non-smoker who developed severe chronic airflow limitation after a bout of pulmonary TB has been reported.9 Lung biopsy findings revealed pathological changes related to post-TB sequelae affecting the small airways and lung vasculature, suggesting that TB-COPD is a distinct phenotype of chronic airflow-limiting disease, differentiated from S-COPD and bronchiectasis.9

GOLD update

Previously, the term COPD was exclusively applied to chronic airflow limitation associated with smoking, whereas other forms were termed “chronic airflow restriction”. The 2023 GOLD guidelines introduced the concept of COPD “etiotypes,” encompassing COPD-C (cigarette smoking-related) and COPD-I (infection-related, including TB-COPD). The 2024 GOLD statements on the pathogenesis of COPD highlighted that TB is a risk factor for COPD, and the pooled prevalence of COPD in patients with prior pulmonary TB was 21%.2,16

Clinical manifestations

TB-COPD and S-COPD exhibit overlapping clinical symptoms, such as coughing, expectoration, chest discomfort, and wheezing. However, the incidences of chest pain and night sweats are usually higher in individuals with TB-COPD than in those with smoking-induced COPD. These symptoms are often overlooked because of other respiratory conditions and a widespread lack of recognition by the public and health care professionals; hence, TB-COPD misdiagnosis is common.27 Distinctively, hemoptysis can differentiate TB-COPD from S-COPD, particularly in individuals younger than 40 years. The former condition is often due to concurrent bronchiectasis or fungal infection as a sequela of TB, while hemoptysis in the latter is relatively rare and often prompts investigation for underlying lung cancer.

Different diagnostic criteria for TB-COPD from clinical studies

Diagnosing TB-COPD relies on demonstrating persistent airflow limitation, characterized by a post-bronchodilator (BD) forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.70, aligned with COPD diagnostic standards. However, the definition of post-TB status varies across studies. Prospective research often defines a post-treatment FEV1/FVC ratio of <0.70 as indicative of TB-COPD, reflecting chronic airflow limitation after completing treatment.24 Retrospective and cross-sectional analyses have revealed a wide range of definitions of post-TB status. Some studies classify individuals with a clear history of TB who have received treatment as post-TB patients.26 By contrast, others base their classification on a history of TB infection, stipulating that COPD must develop only after an episode of TB.28 Additionally, some studies have implemented broader criteria for TB-COPD patient identification, including those with suspected TB based on historical and radiological evidence of previous TB lesions or individuals with positive interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results.29 This inclusive methodology combines clinical history, imaging, and immunological testing for TB to discern and incorporate patients with TB-COPD accurately. All the above methodologies have limitations, as they require a trade-off between achieving specificity and sensitivity in diagnosing the condition, depending on the study's underlying hypothesis. This underscores the need for uniform diagnostic criteria in subsequent TB-COPD research.

Difference between TB-COPD and S-COPD

The distinction between TB-COPD and S-COPD is rooted in the different etiologies of TB infection and smoking. These differences lead to distinct pathophysiological changes, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, and pulmonary function characteristics [Table 1]. The concurrent presence of TB and smoking in clinical cases further complicates the diagnosis because of their overlapping effects.

Pathophysiological divergence

In TB-COPD, gas retention, a result of TB healing, is commonly observed because of small airway lesions. The widespread involvement of bronchioles in over 90% of active pulmonary TB cases contributes to pronounced airflow limitations, air trapping, and severe respiratory symptoms observed in patients with TB-COPD. PTLD is characterized by a range of abnormalities in lung function, including diffusion impairment, airflow restriction, air trapping, and restrictive ventilation disorders. According to Oh et al,30 compared to those with S-COPD, patients with TB-COPD were predominantly women, had lesser history of smoking, and reported higher scores on the St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire. Although FEV1 levels may be similar between the groups, patients with TB-COPD typically have a lower FVC, total lung capacity (TLC), and functional residual capacity, highlighting the impact of TB on lung function.30

Radiographic differences

Post-TB conditions, such as bronchiectasis, are distinctly present in patients with TB-COPD but not in those with S-COPD. Jin et al31 noted a higher incidence of bronchiectasis in individuals with a history of pulmonary TB, particularly in those with concurrent TB lesions, leading to a more significant presence of severe bronchiectasis on chest CTs.31 Although the overall prevalence of emphysema remained consistent regardless of TB history, patients with TB showed more extensive emphysema in the middle and lower lobes, higher severity scores, and broader panlobular and centrilobular emphysema.31 Air trapping, often due to small airway involvement, occurs more frequently in patients with TB-COPD.24,26

Lung function differences

Patients with TB-COPD often experience more severe airway obstruction than those with S-COPD. This increased restriction can be attributed to the after-effects of TB, including pulmonary fibrosis, airway scar contraction, and decreased elastic recoil; these effects lead to reduced FEV1 values and a lower FEV1/FVC ratio. Allwood et al26 investigated lung function disparities between patients with post-TB chronic airflow limitation, that is, TB-COPD, and those with S-COPD. They found that patients with TB-COPD exhibited poorer diffusion and inspiratory capacities, more significant gas retention, and fibrosis in imaging studies without noticeable differences in emphysema scores, implying small airway involvement.26

Mechanistic differences

Autoimmunity plays a role in the pathogenesis of S-COPD, with an increase in peripheral blood autoantibodies in patients with S-COPD.32 A recent study analyzed the differences in peripheral blood autoantibodies among patients with COPD, TB, and TB-COPD. The findings revealed an elevated expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen, mitogen-activated protein kinase 3, and threonine protein kinase 1 in the peripheral blood of patients with TB-COPD. Flow cytometry revealed the upregulation of T helper 1 (Th1) cells and related cytokines in TB-COPD patients’ peripheral blood, suggesting distinct etiological and immune mechanisms between these diseases.28 Another study on the metabolic differences between patients with TB-COPD and those with S-COPD indicated higher levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) in serum, as well as increased St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire scores, in the TB-COPD group compared with those in the S-COPD group.33 Targeted metabolite analysis suggested that the heightened inflammatory response in patients with TB-COPD might be associated with fatty acid pathways and tryptophan metabolism.33

Assessment system of TB-COPD

Regarding the screening and assessment for TB-COPD, there are two main clinical scenarios to consider [Fig. 2]. First, in the TB clinic, after patients with active pulmonary TB have fully completed their anti-TB treatment, it is essential to not only conduct chest imaging but also prioritize lung function tests to assess if there is PTLD, such as TB-COPD. Second, in the respiratory clinic, the initial evaluation of patients diagnosed with COPD, who typically present with symptoms such as cough, sputum production, and dyspnea, should involve lung function tests to confirm irreversible airflow obstruction and establish the COPD diagnosis. Subsequently, it is important to inquire about the patient's history of TB and use imaging studies (e.g., chest X-rays or CT scans) to screen for TB lesions and determine the presence of TB-COPD. When necessary, additional tests, such as IGRA and sputum analysis, can be considered to exclude active TB.

Spirometry

Spirometry plays a pivotal role in diagnosing and evaluating the severity of TB-COPD, with a post-BD FEV1/FVC ratio of <70% serving as the gold standard for identifying airflow obstruction. The Second International Post-Tuberculosis Lung Disease Symposium advocated incorporating more comprehensive effectiveness assessments in future TB clinical trials, which should include, at a minimum, lung function and quality of life evaluations.34

Patients treated for pulmonary TB for at least 20 weeks were reported to have a 59% incidence rate of lung function impairment compared to 20% in patients with latent TB infection, showing that patients with active pulmonary TB were 5.4 times more likely to experience lung function damage after adjusting for risk.35 Several predictors of COPD severity have been identified in patients with TB, including a positive sputum smear, extensive pulmonary involvement before anti-TB treatment, minimal improvement in pulmonary imaging after treatment, and delayed anti-TB treatment.36 There is a correlation between the extent of TB lesion involvement and the rate of lung function decline. Patients affected in multiple lung lobes may experience a significantly greater annual reduction in FVC and FEV1 than those with localized pulmonary TB lesions.37 However, some studies have reported a decline in lung function in patients with minor residual TB lesions on chest X-ray (CXR)38 or no apparent residual lesions.39 A recent meta-analysis of 41,014 post-TB treatment patients found that 59% exhibited abnormal lung function compared to 5% in the control group.40 Among patients with PTLD, 18%, 21%, and 13% presented with obstructive, restrictive, and mixed lung function patterns, respectively.40 Approximately 10–15% of pulmonary TB survivors suffer from severe lung function damage.41 Another meta-analysis indicated that sputum TB mycobacterial resistance affects lung function, with patients with sensitive pulmonary TB having predicted FEV1% and FVC of 76.6% and 81.8%, respectively, and patients with multidrug-resistant (MDR) TB showing 65.9% and 76.0%, respectively. Among these groups, approximately 22.0% and 19.0% of the patients had airflow obstruction, 23.0% and 22.0% had restrictive ventilation disorders, and 15.0% and 43.0% had mixed ventilation disorders.41

In recent years, several prospective studies have explored lung function patterns in PTLD, categorized as airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC <70%), restriction (TLC <80% predicted), and diffusion impairment (DLCO <80% predicted). A prospective study of patients with newly treated active pulmonary TB undergoing 1 year of post-anti-TB treatment lung function and CT follow-up showed that anti-TB treatment led to improved lung capacity indicators and decreased scores for pulmonary fibrosis lesions. Nevertheless, 18.6% of the patients still experienced restrictive ventilation disorders, 16.3% had airflow obstruction, 32.6% had gas trapping (RV/TLC >45%), and 78.6% experienced diffusion impairment. The progression of small airway lesions during anti-TB treatment may be a potential cause of post-treatment gas trapping.24 A recent prospective study in the Malawi region involving 405 patients with pulmonary TB revealed that approximately 34.2% (125/365) had abnormal lung function at the completion of TB treatment, with 19.3% (59/305) and 14.1% (43/305) experiencing a decline in FEV1 or FVC of ≥100 mL after 1 year of anti-TB treatment.42 At the three-year follow-up, 27.9% (76/272) of the patients continued to exhibit abnormal lung function, with the rate of airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC <70%) being 14.4% (41/285) after 1 year and 15.8% (43/272) after 3 years. During this period, the absolute values of FEV1 and FVC increased by an average of 0.03 L and 0.1 L, respectively; however, 29.7% (73/246) of the patients experienced a decline in FEV1 greater than 0.1 L.43

CT images

PTLD imaging patterns have been categorized into five distinct types: airway (large and small airways), parenchyma, pleura, pulmonary vasculature, and other patterns, made more complicated by the fact that multiple patterns may co-exist in the same patient. TB-COPD prominently exists within the “airways” category, defined by “airway obstruction (FEV1/FVC ratio <0.7 or <lower limit of normal [LLN]) predominantly resulting from small airway disease”.44 The radiographic depiction of TB-COPD varies notably, illustrating emphysema and other signs denoting TB's multifaceted impact on lung architecture, such as bronchiectasis, fibrosis, and nodular lesions. These signs indicate underlying chronic inflammation, structural alterations, and tissue damage. Chest HRCT is instrumental in offering detailed insights into the structural changes and facilitating differentiation from other forms of COPD.

In our analysis of lung CT scans from 231 patients with COPD, of which 82.1% had a history of smoking, it was found that 45% exhibited “old” TB lesions. These lesions were identified as sharply defined nodular or linear fibrotic areas located in the dorsal sections of the upper or lower lobes. Occasionally, these lesions were accompanied by lymph node calcification. Notably, a higher prevalence of emphysema was observed in these cases, underscoring the severe impact of previous TB infections on lung conditions.31

Biomarkers

Inflammatory and fibrotic biomarkers play pivotal roles in the diagnosis and prognosis of TB-COPD. Elevated serum TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP levels in patients with TB-COPD reflect a sustained chronic inflammatory state induced by TB infection. TNF-α and MMPs are associated with the destruction of lung tissues and the formation of cavities in patients with TB.45 However, their association with long-term pulmonary damage remains unclear. High levels of profibrotic cytokines, such as transforming growth factor-beta, are linked to excessive pulmonary fibrosis following TB infection.46 MMPs, a group of 28 proteases, participate in lung injury and remodeling by degrading the components of the extracellular matrix. Increased MMP expression may partly explain the pulmonary tissue destruction observed in patients with TB.21 Our preliminary research on smokers with TB has identified macrophage M1/M2 polarization, changes in NK cell subtypes, and increased release of MMP9 as significant contributors to the exacerbation of lung destruction in these individuals.47,48

Current clinical studies on TB-COPD

TB-COPD has numerous aspects that are yet to be fully understood. Clinical research on TB-COPD has predominantly focused on elucidating the epidemiological connection between TB and COPD, and studies delving into its pathophysiological mechanisms and biomarkers are relatively limited.33 The present findings underscore the significance of a history of TB as a pivotal risk factor for COPD development, especially in areas with high TB prevalence. Meta-analyses have revealed that 22% of patients previously treated for drug-sensitive TB and 19% of patients with drug-resistant TB exhibited TB-COPD manifestations.41

Moreover, smoking has been identified as a factor that might exacerbate lung damage post-TB, potentially intensifying pulmonary injuries associated with TB-COPD.49,50 Additionally, exploring biomarkers to distinguish TB-COPD from COPD triggered by other causes seeks to refine diagnostic precision. Studies on management and treatment strategies suggest the necessity for personalized COPD interventions for individuals with a history of TB, including specific pharmacotherapy, while generally advising against the use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), marking a departure from traditional COPD treatment.

Treatment of TB-COPD

Anti-TB treatment

Effective anti-TB treatment for patients with active pulmonary TB is crucial for improving treatment outcomes, enhancing lung function, and reducing or mitigating the incidence of TB-COPD. Recently discovered drugs, such as bedaquiline and delamanid, have shown promise in treating drug-resistant TB, which exacerbates lung damage. These drugs can improve treatment outcomes and potentially reduce severe lung impairment by effectively targeting MDR bacteria, reducing bacterial load, and limiting chronic inflammation and tissue damage. To comprehensively evaluate treatment success, future clinical trials should incorporate measures of lung function and quality of life as key efficacy endpoints. This approach will help determine the impact of new treatments on lung function and overall patient well-being.

COPD treatment

Inhaled BDs, namely long-acting muscarinic antagonists and long-acting beta2-agonists, are the cornerstone for managing COPD. ICS, often combined with BDs, have shown potential benefits in patients with specific biomarkers, such as elevated eosinophil counts and enhanced T2 gene expression, enabling tailored pharmacological interventions.51 However, ICS use is associated with an increased risk of active TB and other mycobacterial infections in patients with COPD.52,53 A recent meta-analysis indicated a significant correlation between ICS usage and a heightened TB risk in patients with COPD (OR = 1.46; 95% CI, 1.06–2.01; P = 0.02; I2 = 96%).52 Thus, the use of ICS for the treatment of TB-COPD is currently discouraged. Identifying biomarkers that can predict treatment responses and enable personalized medication strategies is pivotal to effectively managing TB-COPD.

Pulmonary rehabilitation

Outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation has been validated as an effective measure for enhancing lung function in individuals with chronic pulmonary conditions, including COPD.54 Research on post-TB pulmonary disease has shown that outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation significantly ameliorates clinical symptoms, improves performance on the six-minute walk test, and elevates lung function measures, including FEV1, FVC, and oxygen saturation.55,56 To improve lung function and overall health status in patients with TB-COPD, a comprehensive pulmonary rehabilitation program that includes nutritional support and psychological counseling is required.

Current issues and challenges in TB-COPD

Definition of TB-COPD

The definition of TB-COPD remains ambiguous. The provisional definition relies on the manifestation of airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC <0.7 or <LLN), based on the criteria for PTLD, and is primarily associated with small airway disease. However, precise characterization of airflow obstruction resulting from post-TB sequelae is challenging, especially given the frequent co-existence of large airway bronchiectasis (which may also cause spirometric obstruction). Thus, there is a need for more high-quality clinical evidence to define these as potentially distinct or overlapping conditions.

Defining confounding factors in COPD etiology

Considering the common risk factors between COPD and TB, such as smoking, bacterial and viral infections, use of ICS, being underweight, type 2 diabetes, malnutrition, vitamin D deficiency, exposure to biomass fuel, and low socioeconomic status, distinguishing between S-COPD and TB-COPD in patients with histories of smoking and pulmonary TB is challenging.

Terminological inconsistency

The terminology for TB-related COPD across various studies is inconsistent, and a consensus is required. The literature employs terms such as TOPD, T-COPD, TB-COPD, TB with obstruction, or post-TB chronic obstructive lung disease, and now TOPD-I, introduced by the GOLD guidelines.

Future directions in this area

Research into TB-COPD should prioritize several crucial areas: (1) Unified terminology and definitions: unified terminology, clear disease definitions, and diagnostic criteria are urgently required, and standardization is vital to advance clinical research. (2) High-quality clinical studies: rigorous clinical studies are essential to delve into the epidemiological characteristics, pathophysiological mechanisms, and responses of patients with TB-COPD to various BD treatments. These studies should aim to develop scientific diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines. (3) Biomarker discovery: it is important to identify biomarkers for the early diagnosis and monitoring of TB-COPD, enabling timely and accurate interventions. (4) Personalized treatment strategies: research on personalized treatment approaches is necessary to refine TB-COPD management, aiming for more tailored and precise therapeutic options.

Future studies should aim to develop more effective prevention, diagnosis, and treatment methods for patients with TB-related COPD. These advancements are expected to significantly enhance the health status and quality of life of affected individuals.
