The structure of polynomial growth for tree automata/transducers and MSO set queries
Paul Gallot, Nathan Lhote, Lê Thành Dũng (Tito) Nguyễn

Abstract
Given an ℕ \mathbb{N} -weighted tree automaton, we give a decision procedure for exponential vs polynomial growth (with respect to the input size) in quadratic time, and an algorithm that computes the exact polynomial degree of growth in cubic time. As a special case, they apply to the growth of the ambiguity of a nondeterministic tree automaton, i.e. the number of distinct accepting runs over a given input. We deduce analogous decidability results (ignoring complexity) for the growth of the number of results of set queries in Monadic Second-Order logic (MSO) over ranked trees. In the case of polynomial growth of degree k k , we also prove a reparameterization theorem for such queries: their results can be mapped to k k -tuples of input nodes in a finite-to-one and MSO-definable fashion. We then apply these tools to study growth rates and subclass membership problems for tree-to-tree functions. Using new proof strategies, we recover and generalize known results concerning polyregular functions, total deterministic macro tree transducers, and partial nondeterministic top-down tree transducers. In particular, we give a procedure to decide polynomial size-to-height increase for both macro tree transducers and MSO set interpretations, and compute the degree. The paper concludes with a survey of a wide range of related work.

Conventions on trees

A
ranked alphabet
is a finite set
\Sigma
given together with a

rank function

\mathsf{rank}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
; we use the notation
\Sigma=\{a:\mathsf{rank}(a),\;\dots\}
to define concrete ranked alphabets. The set
\mathsf{T}\Sigma

of
trees

\Sigma

\text{for}\ a\in\Sigma\ \text{and}\ \overbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{\mathsf{rank}(a)}}^{\mathclap{\text{empty family when}\ \mathsf{rank}(a)=0}}\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma,\quad a(t_{1},\dots,t_{\mathsf{rank}(a)})\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma~\text{.}

This inductive definition means that trees are subject to structural equality:

a(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})=b(t^{\prime}_{1},\dots,t^{\prime}_{\ell})\iff a=b\ (\text{implying}\ k=\ell)\ \text{and}\ \forall i,\;t_{i}=t^{\prime}_{i}~\text{.}

A tree

has a set of

, with the usual meaning. We shall use the identification

\mathsf{nodes}(a(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}))=\{\text{root of}\ t\}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}\mathsf{nodes}(t_{i}),\quad\text{the union being disjoint.}

Even though it is formally incompatible with structural equality (in

,

2.1
A preliminary step: trimming in linear time (and reusable lemmas)

A tree automaton is
trim
if all its states are accessible and co-accessible. Tree automata can be trimmed by keeping only the states that are both accessible and co-accessible, and the transitions that do not involve some removed state. This transformation preserves the set of accepting runs over each input tree; therefore, it preserves the ambiguity, as well as the value in the
\mathbb{N}
-weighted case.

It is often stated without proof that
“trimming ranked tree automata is standard (and can be performed in linear time)”
(quoting Caralp et al.
[
32
, §1]
); for another occurrence of this claim, see e.g. Seidl’s
[

, which he uses to get a quadratic-time algorithm to decide unambiguity
[

, Proposition 1.2]
. For the sake of completeness, we sketch the folklore linear-time trimming algorithm.

First, to remove inaccessible states, we use:

Lemma 17
.

The set of accessible states of a tree automaton is computable in linear time.

Proof.

While there is an intuitive graph-traversal-like algorithm that runs in polynomial time (cf. e.g.
Tree Automata Techniques and Applications

[
42
, Theorem 1.1.8]
or the
Handbook of Automata Theory

[
101
, Section 2.3]
), it is not so trivial to achieve a linear running time. In the appendix (§
A.1
), we explain a folklore solution based on a reduction to
Horn satisfiability
, and relate it to the graph traversal intuition.
∎

Let us say that a one-hole context is

if its hole is a child of
the root.

Definition 18
.

The

of a tree automaton has all the states as vertices,
and it has an edge from
q^{\prime}
to

if and only if there is some shallow

such that
q^{\prime}\to_{C}q
.

Since a context whose hole is at depth

the relation ‘

Lemma 19
.

The shallow digraph can be computed in linear time.

Proof.

After precomputing the accessible states (
Lemma

17
),
we build the shallow digraph by successively adding edges: for each

(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},a,q)
,

•

if
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
are all accessible, we add all the edges

•

if they are all accessible except for a single

(q_{i},q)

•

otherwise we do not add any edge for this transition. ∎

The trimming algorithm first removes the inaccessible states using

Lemma

shallow digraph starting from the accepting states to determine the remaining
co-accessible states.

\{(c,q^{\prime}),\;(q^{\prime},b,q^{\prime}),\;(q^{\prime},b,q_{N}),\;(q_{N},b,q_{N}),\;(q_{N},q_{N},a,q_{N-1}),\;\dots,\;(q_{1},q_{1},a,q_{0})\}

(where
\mathsf{rank}(a)=2
,
\mathsf{rank}(b)=1
and
\mathsf{rank}(c)=0
), with accepting state
q_{0}
.

It has a single pair of states forming a barbell, namely

q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q_{N}
(this can be witnessed by the context
b(\square)
, or
by
b(b(\square))
, etc.).
So the ambiguity on the tree
b^{n}(c)
would be
n+1
(if
N=0
).
For
N=1
, the tree
a(b^{n}(c),b^{n}(c))
has ambiguity
(n+1)^{2}
because we have
n+1
possible runs for each
b^{n}(c)
.
For
N=2
, the tree
a(a(b^{n}(c),b^{n}(c)),a(b^{n}(c),b^{n}(c)))
has ambiguity
(n+1)^{4}
and so on.
In the end, the degree of growth of its ambiguity is
{2^{N}}
.

We now introduce patterns involving barbells that
witness lower bounds for the degree of growth. They are finite ranked trees over
an infinite alphabet that consists of the original ranked alphabet
\Sigma

plus, for each one-hole context
C
, a rank 1 node
\langle C\rangle
.

Definition 33
.

We define inductively the
pumping patterns
for each state of
\mathcal{A}
:

•

if
\mathcal{A}
contains a transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)
and

\Pi_{i}
is a pumping pattern for
q_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
then
a(\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{k})
is a pumping pattern for
q

•

if

and
\Pi^{\prime}
is a pumping pattern for
q^{\prime}
then

\langle C\rangle(\Pi^{\prime})
is a pumping pattern for
q
.

The

\deg(\Pi)
of a pattern

is its number of

\langle-\rangle
nodes. For

, by structural induction on a pumping

\displaystyle\mathsf{pump}(n,a(\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{k}))

\displaystyle=a(\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi_{1}),\dots,\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi_{k}))

\displaystyle\mathsf{pump}(n,\langle C\rangle(\Pi))

\displaystyle=C^{n}[\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)]

In particular, the base case is that if
\mathcal{A}
contains a transition
(c,q)
with
\mathsf{rank}(c)=0
, then

is a pumping pattern for
q
and
\mathsf{pump}(n,c)=c
.

Remark 34
.

If
t\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma
admits a run ending in state
q
at the root, then

can be seen as a pumping pattern of degree 0 for

. In particular, since
the tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be trim, for any state there is at
least one pumping pattern.

As a non-degenerate example, the automaton of Ex.
32

(parameterized by

) admits a pumping pattern
\Pi_{N}
of degree

, defined by
\Pi_{0}=\langle b(\square)\rangle(c)
and

\Pi_{i+1}=a(\Pi_{i},\Pi_{i})
, for

.

Claim 35
.

Let
\Pi
be a pumping pattern for a state

q\to_{C}q_{\mathsf{a}}
where

q_{\mathsf{a}}
is accepting. Then
|C[\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)]|=O(n)
while the ambiguity over

C[\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)]
is at least
n^{\deg(\Pi)}
.

{claimproof}

We only detail here the proof for the lower bound on the ambiguity (the proof of
O(n)
is in §
A.4
of the appendix). It suffices to
show that there are at least
n^{\deg(\Pi)}
runs on
\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)
with
q

at the root. By structural induction:

case
\Pi=a(\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{k})
:

by definition, there is a
transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)
such that each
\Pi_{i}
is a
pumping pattern for
q_{i}
. By induction hypothesis, there are at
least
n^{\deg(\Pi_{i})}
runs on
\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi_{i})
with
q_{i}
at
the root. These runs plus the aforementioned transition can be
assembled to yield

n^{\deg(\Pi_{1})}\times\dots\times n^{\deg(\Pi_{k})}=n^{\deg(\Pi)}

distinct runs on
\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)
.

case
\Pi=\langle C\rangle(\Pi^{\prime})
:

by definition,
\Pi^{\prime}
is a pumping
pattern for some state
q^{\prime}
such that
q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow_{C}q
. The key
observation (from
[
123

) is that
there are at least
n
runs over the context
C^{n}
with
q^{\prime}
at the
hole and
q
at the root, namely:

\overbrace{q^{\prime}\to_{C}\dots\to_{C}q^{\prime}\phantom{{}^{\prime}}}^{\text{$m-1$ times $C$}}\to_{C}\overbrace{\phantom{{}^{\prime}}q\to_{C}\dots\to_{C}q}^{\text{$n-m$ times $C$}}

There are
n^{\deg(\Pi^{\prime})}
runs over
\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi^{\prime})
with
q^{\prime}
at the root, each such run can be combined with each of the
n
runs over

to obtain a distinct run over the tree
C^{n}[\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi^{\prime})]
. In total we get
n\times n^{\deg(\Pi^{\prime})}=n^{\deg(\Pi)}
runs on tree
\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi)=C^{n}[\mathsf{pump}(n,\Pi^{\prime})]
.

\claimqedhere

The ambiguity of
\mathcal{A}
on an input is at most its value on the same input, and
we have assumed in this subsection that
\mathcal{A}
is polynomially bounded. Together
with
Claim

35
, this entails that there must be a global bound
on the degrees of pumping patterns — which ensures that the degree of a state,
introduced below, is a well-defined natural number. (It is an adaptation to
tree automata of the notion of
“height of a state”

from
[
50
, §4.4.2]
.)

Definition 36
.

The
degree

\deg(q)
is the maximum degree of a pumping pattern for
q
.

Claim 37
.

These inequalities follow from the definition of pumping patterns:

•

if
\mathcal{A}
contains a transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)
then

\deg(q_{1})+\cdots+\deg(q_{k})\leqslant\deg(q)
– in particular,

\deg
is monotone with respect to
\to
;

•

if
q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q
then
\deg(q^{\prime})+1\leqslant\deg(q)
.

The degrees of the states are lower
bounds on the polynomial degree of growth of
\mathcal{A}
. Next, we prove a
matching upper bound. To do so, we start by distinguishing some transitions.

Definition 38
.

A transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},a,q)
is
critical
when

\deg(q)>\deg(q_{1})+\cdots+\deg(q_{m})\qquad[=0\ \text{when}\ m=0].

A
critical node
for a run over some tree is a node where the transition
taken is critical.

We may draw an analogy with flows in directed graphs: non-critical nodes satisfy
the
“conservation of flow”
equation

\deg(q)=\deg(q_{1})+\cdots+\deg(q_{m})
while critical nodes serve as

“sources”
for the flow of degrees. Since the root is the only

“sink”
, we have:

Claim 39
.

The number of critical nodes in a (not necessarily accepting) run is bounded
by the degree of the state at the root.

{claimproof}

For each transition of the form
(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},a,q)
, the
“entering flow”
at that node
\deg(q)-\deg(q_{1})-\cdots-\deg(q_{m})
is a nonnegative integer; for critical nodes it is strictly positive. Therefore, the sum of these entering flows over all nodes bounds the number of critical nodes. In the expression for this big sum, for each non-root node, the degree of its state appears once negatively (from its parent’s flow) and once positively (from its own flow). Thus, the only term that does not get canceled out is the degree of the state at the root.

Furthermore, the set of critical nodes of an accepting run in our
\mathbb{N}
-weighted tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
almost determines
this run, and the weight of this run is bounded:

Lemma 40
.

The sum of weights of accepting runs in
\mathcal{A}
over any given input tree whose set of critical
nodes equals any prescribed value is bounded by a computable function of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

We build an
\mathbb{N}
-weighted tree automaton
\mathcal{B}
with the same states as
\mathcal{A}
, but with input alphabet
\Sigma\times\{0,1\}
(the ranks are inherited from
\Sigma
).
For each critical (resp. non-critical) transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},a,q)
in
\mathcal{A}
, we add a transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},(a,[\text{1 (resp.\ 0)}]),q)
to
\mathcal{B}
, with the same weight. This way, the runs in
\mathcal{A}
over an input
t\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma
whose set of critical nodes is
S\subseteq\mathsf{nodes}(t)
correspond precisely to the runs in
\mathcal{B}
over the tree
t_{S}
obtained by replacing the label
a
of each node
\nu
by
(a,1)
if
\nu\in S
or by
(a,0)
if
\nu\notin S
.

Therefore, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{B}
is bounded. To do so, we apply
Theorem

28
. Let us check its assumptions:

•

\mathcal{A}
is trim and has no heavy cycles (by assumption), therefore
\mathcal{B}
too.

•

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
q_{1}\Rrightarrow_{C}q_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}
. Then in
\mathcal{A}
,
q_{1}\Rrightarrow q_{2}
is witnessed by the context obtained from
C
by projecting on the first component; thus,
\deg(q_{1})<\deg(q_{2})
(we only speak of degrees in
\mathcal{A}
). Furthermore, by definition of
\Rrightarrow
, we have
q_{1}\to_{C}q_{1}
and
q_{1}\to_{C}q_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}
. These are jointly impossible because, by a
“conservation of flow”
argument (cf. above), if
q^{\prime}\to_{C}q^{\prime\prime}
, then
\deg(q^{\prime})<\deg(q^{\prime\prime})
if and only if
C
contains a node with label in
\Sigma\times\{1\}
.

Finally, now that we know that
\mathcal{B}
is bounded, its maximum value is computable because:

•

\mathcal{B}
can be translated into an
\mathbb{N}
-weighted visibly pushdown automaton (
\mathbb{N}
-VPA) with the same range
[
30
, §2.3.3]
;

•

the maximum value of a bounded
\mathbb{N}
-VPA is computable
[
31
, Theorem 4]
.

(Note that this final observation does not require knowing what an
\mathbb{N}
-VPA is!)
∎

We are now in a position to bound the value of
\mathcal{A}
over an input tree of size
n
:

•

there are
O(n^{\max(\deg(\text{states}))})
possible sets of critical
nodes for the runs by
Claim

39
;

•

for each of these possibilities, the contribution of the corresponding
runs to the total value is
O(1)
by the above lemma.

Combined with the matching lower bound obtained in
Claim

35
, we see that, using the definition of growth rate from the beginning of the introduction:

Claim 41
.

\mathsf{growth}[\mathcal{A}](n)=\Theta(n^{\max(\deg(\text{states}))})
.

Thus, to prove
Theorem

14
, we just have to efficiently compute the degrees.

2.5
Computing the degrees of all states in cubic time

To compute the degree map, we first characterize it more abstractly as the

smallest
map that satisfies the inequalities of
Claim

37
. In
other words:

Lemma 42
.

The function that maps each state to its degree is the least fixed point of

f\mapsto\left[q\mapsto\max\left(\bigl\{f(q)\bigr\}\cup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(q_{i})\middle|(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)\ \text{trans.}\right\}\cup\Bigl\{f\bigl(q^{\prime}\bigl)+1\,\Big|\,q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q\Bigr\}\right)\right].

Proof.

We sketch the high-level idea here and give details in §
A.5
of the appendix.

Let
S(q)=\{\deg(\Pi)\mid\Pi\ \text{is a pumping pattern for}\ q\}
. From

Definition

33
we can extract a direct inductive definition of
S
, that
can be expressed by general considerations as a least fixed point in a lattice
of set-valued functions. To translate this into an expression for the degree
map, observe that
S(q)=\{0,\dots,\deg(q)\}
(indeed,
S(q)
is
downwards-closed because
\langle C\rangle(\Pi)
can be replaced by
C[\Pi]
to
decrement the degree of a pattern).
∎

To compute the degree map, we can iterate the above operator (starting from

q\mapsto 0
) until we reach a fixed point. This naive algorithm terminates
because it builds a bounded increasing sequence of
\mathbb{N}
-valued functions
with finite domain: such a sequence must be finite.
Slightly less naively, we can precompute

\{(q_{1},q_{2})\mid q_{1}\Rrightarrow q_{2}\}
in cubic time
(cf.
Lemma

29
) before iterating. Subsequently, each
iteration can be carried out in quadratic time, by examining each of the

O(|\mathcal{A}|)
transitions once and each of the
O(|\mathcal{A}|^{2})
barbells once.

To get a cubic bound on the total running time, it now suffices to show that
the fixed point is reached after a linear number of iterations
.
We denote by
f_{\tau}
the map from states to
\mathbb{N}
obtained after
\tau

iterations of the aforementioned operator, and
Q(\tau)
the set of states that are mapped to the correct value after
\tau
iterations:
Q(\tau)=\{q\mid f_{\tau}(q)=\deg(q)\}
.
We call a set of states
S
a
trigger
for a state
q
if:

•

either
S=\left\{q^{\prime}\right\}
and
q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q
and
\deg(q)=\deg(q^{\prime})+1
,

•

or
S=\left\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\right\}

and
(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)
is a transition and
\deg(q)=\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}}\deg(q_{i})
.

Claim 43
.

Q(\tau+1)=Q(\tau)\cup\left\{q\mid q\text{ has a trigger included in }Q(\tau)\right\}
.

{claimproof}

The expression in
Lemma

42
gives us
f_{\tau+1}(q)=\max(\{f_{\tau}(q)\}\cup D_{\tau}(q))
where

D_{\tau}(q)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{\tau}(q_{i})\middle|(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)\ \text{trans.}\right\}\cup\{f_{\tau}(q^{\prime})+1\mid q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q\}

If a state
q
has a trigger included in
Q(\tau)
, then this trigger witnesses that
\deg(q)\in D_{\tau}(q)
,
hence
f_{\tau+1}(q)\geqslant\deg(q)
. Since
f_{\tau+1}
is part of an increasing sequence that eventually reaches
\deg
, we have the converse inequality, so
q\in Q(\tau+1)
. We also have
Q(\tau)\subseteq Q(\tau+1)
, again because the iterations are increasing.

Conversely, if
q\in Q(\tau+1)\setminus Q(\tau)
, then
\deg(q)=f_{\tau+1}(q)=\max(\{f_{\tau}(q)\}\cup D_{\tau}(q))
.

•

The case
\deg(q)=f_{\tau}(q)
is impossible because
q\notin Q(\tau)
.

•

If
\deg(q)=f_{\tau}(q_{1})+\dots+f_{\tau}(q_{k})
for some transition
(q_{1},\dots,q_{k},a,q)
, we also know that:

–

f_{\tau}(q_{i})\leqslant\deg(q_{i})
for all
i
, and therefore
\deg(q)\leqslant\deg(q_{1})+\dots+\deg(q_{k})
;

–

\deg(q)\geqslant\deg(q_{1})+\dots+\deg(q_{k})
by
Claim

37
.

This forces all these inequalities to be equalities, which means that
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}
is a trigger for
q
and is included in
Q(\tau)
(by definition).

•

If
\deg(q)=f_{\tau}(q^{\prime})+1
for some
q^{\prime}\Rrightarrow q
, then
Claim

37
again forces
f_{\tau}(q^{\prime})=\deg(q^{\prime})
, so
q^{\prime}\in Q(\tau)
and
\{q^{\prime}\}
is a trigger for
q
.
\claimqedhere

Claim 44
.

If
Q(\tau+1)=Q(\tau)
then a fixed point is reached in at most
\tau
iterations.

{claimproof}

As a consequence of the previous claim, whenever
Q(\tau+1)=Q(\tau)
, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have
Q(\tau)=Q(\tau+N)
. This means that the fixed point has been reached: indeed, by definition, the algorithm halts at the first time
\tau^{\prime}
when
Q(\tau^{\prime})
is the full set of states.

Thus,
Q(\tau)
has to be strictly increasing before it stabilizes. Therefore, the number of iterations is bounded by the number of states. This concludes the proof of
Theorem

14
.

Remark 45
.

In the case of word automata, the computation of the degrees can be seen as a
longest-path problem in a weighted graph without positive cycles: take the
transitions of the automaton as weight 0 edges, and barbells as weight 1
edges. This problem can be solved by the classical Bellman–Ford algorithm
(cf.
[
57
, §8.4]
), whose iterative relaxations are very similar to
our own procedure.

3
Reparameterization of MSO queries

Let us now deduce
Theorem

3
(stated in more detail as

Theorem

57
) from the properties of critical transitions that we
just saw in the previous section.

We use the standard definition of monadic second-order (MSO) logic and its
semantics over relational structures; see for instance
[
44
, Chapter 5]
. As
usual, uppercase letters
X,Y,\ldots
denote second-order variables (ranging
over subsets of the domain) whereas lowercase letters
x,y,\ldots
denote
first-order variables (ranging over elements of the domain). A formula is closed
when it has no free variables.

Trees and DAGs as relational structures.

To a ranked alphabet
\Sigma
, we associate a relational signature that consists of:

•

a unary symbol
a
for each
a\in\Sigma
;

•

a binary symbol
\mathsf{child}_{i}
for each
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,\max(\mathsf{rank}(\Sigma))\right\}
;

•

a binary symbol
\leqslant
.

Definition 46
.

We define a
rooted DAG (directed acyclic graph)
over
\Sigma
to be a finite structure
\mathfrak{G}
over the above signature, such that the following hold:

•

\mathfrak{G}
interprets the formula
\mathsf{child}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\lor\dots\lor\mathsf{child}_{\max(\mathsf{rank}(\Sigma))}(x_{1},x_{2})

as an
irreflexive and acyclic
binary relation over its domain, whose reflexive transitive closure is equal to
\leqslant
;

•

the partial order
\leqslant
has a unique minimum element, called the
root
;

•

for each vertex (i.e. domain element)
u\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G})
:

–

there is a unique
a\in\Sigma
such that
\mathfrak{G}\models a(u)
;

–

for
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{rank}(a)\right\}
, there is a unique
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G})
such that
\mathfrak{G}\models\mathsf{child}_{i}(u,v)
;

–

for
i>\mathsf{rank}(a)
, there is no
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G})
such that
\mathfrak{G}\models\mathsf{child}_{i}(u,v)
.

When
\mathfrak{G}\models u\leqslant v
, we say that
u
is an
ancestor
of
v
and that
v
is a
descendant
of
u
.

Remark 47
.

Strictly speaking, our rooted DAGs may be multigraphs, i.e. contain parallel edges: when
\mathfrak{G}\models\mathsf{child}_{i}(u,v)\land\mathsf{child}_{j}(u,v)
, it does not necessarily imply that
i=j
.

Remark 48
.

All these axioms are definable in MSO.

Let us illustrate the power of MSO for querying DAGs encoded as relational structures.

Example 49
.

A
rooted path
in
\mathfrak{G}
is a finite sequence
(u_{0},i_{1},u_{1},\dots,i_{n},u_{n})
such that:

•

u_{0}
is the root;

•

\mathfrak{G}\models\mathsf{child}_{i_{k}}(u_{k-1},u_{k})
for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Note that with this definition, because of
Remark

47
, a path is
not
uniquely identified by its sequence of vertices
(u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
.
This is why we are going to encode such a path not as a single set of vertices, but as an
r
-tuple of sets, for
r=\max(\mathsf{rank}(\Sigma))
:

(P_{1},\dots,P_{r})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G}))^{r}\quad\text{where}\quad P_{i}=\{u_{k-1}\mid k\in\{1,\dots,n\},\;i_{k}=i\}

(in particular, the path with only the root (
k=0
) is encoded as
(\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing)
).

Then
\vec{P}
is an encoding of a rooted path if and only if
\mathfrak{G}\models F_{\mathrm{path}}(\vec{P})

for some MSO formula
F_{\mathrm{path}}(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})
. We can then say that this formula defines an MSO set query whose results are the rooted paths.
Explicitly,
F_{\mathrm{path}}
may be defined as the conjunction of:

•

\forall x.\;X_{i}(x)\Rightarrow\displaystyle\bigvee_{\mathclap{a\in\Sigma,i\leqslant\mathsf{rank}(a)}}a(x)
(take one such subformula for each
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
);

•

\forall x.\;\displaystyle\bigvee_{\mathclap{1\leqslant i\leqslant r}}X_{i}(x)\Rightarrow\Big((\forall y.\;x\leqslant y)\lor\bigvee_{\mathclap{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}}\exists y.\;X_{j}(y)\land\mathsf{child}_{j}(y,x)\Big)
;

•

the predicates
X_{i}
define disjoint subsets (this can be expressed in MSO).

Remark 50
.

We work with structures whose interpretation of
\leqslant
is fully
determined by their interpretation of the other symbols. But from the point of
view of expressive power, it is well known that having direct access to the
descendent order makes a difference in first-order logic (see e.g. the
introduction to
[
14
]
) — this is important for the
decomposition of
Lemma

67
. It also matters for the structures
outputted by MSO interpretations (§
4
), as
explained in
[
24
, §2.2]
.

Any tree over
\Sigma
can be turned into a rooted DAG whose domain is the set
of nodes of the tree, with the intuitive interpretations of the relation
symbols. This defines an injection from trees to DAGs, whose range can be
characterized up to isomorphism of structures:

Definition 51
.

If every non-root vertex of a rooted DAG
\mathfrak{G}
has a unique parent
and in a unique way
— in other words, for every non-root
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G})
there are a unique
u\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathfrak{G})
and a unique
i
such that
\mathfrak{G}\models\mathsf{child}_{i}(u,v)
— then
\mathfrak{G}
is called a
tree structure
.

For
t\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma
and
F
a formula, we abbreviate
“
[\text{the
tree structure for}\ t]\models F
”
as
t\models F
. This abuse of
notation extends to formulas with first-order (resp. second-order) variables
instantiated by nodes (resp. subsets of nodes) of
t
— as used, for
instance, in the introduction when defining the set query
\wn F
specified
by an MSO formula
F(X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})
.

We recall a basic and useful syntactic property.

Proposition 52
.

From an MSO formula
F(X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
over
\mathsf{T}\Sigma
one can build an MSO formula
G(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})
over
\mathsf{T}(\Sigma\times\{0,1\}^{\ell})
such that

\forall t\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma,\;\forall\bigl(\vec{P},\vec{S}\bigr)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{nodes}(t))^{\ell+m},\quad t\models F\bigl(\vec{P},\vec{S}\bigr)\iff\mathsf{marked}\bigl(t,\vec{P}\bigr)\models G\bigl(\vec{S}\bigr)

where
\mathsf{marked}(t,P_{1},\dots,P_{\ell})
is the tree such that:

•

by projecting all labels to their first coordinate, one recovers the tree
t
(for this to make sense, we define the ranks on
\Sigma\times\{0,1\}^{\ell}
to be inherited from the ranks on
\Sigma
);

•

the
(i+1)

Logic-automata correspondence.

The fundamental theorem of MSO on trees is that the sets of trees defined by
closed MSO formulas are precisely the regular tree languages, that is, those recognized
by tree automata; cf. e.g.
[

. (The language recognized by an automaton is the set of inputs with an accepting run.) Furthermore, this
equivalence is effective, i.e. witnessed by computable translations.
Thanks to

queries, as follows:

Theorem 53

Let

and, for each

•

such that
\Xi_{t}=\wn F(t)
for all

;

•

the
“language of marked trees”

\{\mathsf{marked}(t,\vec{P})\mid t\in\mathsf{T}\Sigma,\,\vec{P}\in\Xi_{t}\}
is regular.

Remark 54

Actually, one classical translation from MSO to tree automata — which is the one given in

The reparameterization theorem.

In the case of polynomial growth, we have:

Example 58

(detailed version of

)
.

Consider the formula

where

\Sigma

An explicit reparameterization is given by the family of functions that maps
each rooted path in a tree to its endpoint (i.e. the last vertex in the

, and it is MSO-definable by:

G_{\mathrm{path}}(\vec{X},z)=F_{\mathrm{path}}(\vec{X})\land\forall z^{\prime}.\;z\leqslant z^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow\overbrace{\bigwedge_{\mathclap{1\leqslant i\leqslant r}}\forall x.\,\forall y.\,X_{i}(x)\land\mathsf{child}_{i}(x,y)\Rightarrow y\leqslant z^{\prime}}^{\text{\ltxml@oqmark@open\textquotedblleft\penalty\hskip-0.0002pt\hskip 0.0002pt$z^{\prime}$ is an upper bound of all vertices in the path\textquotedblright\ltxml@oqmark@close{}}}

This MSO formula selects the endpoint of a rooted path in any rooted DAG — a level of generality that will be convenient for later reuse. (Though this is not finite-to-one over DAGs; in fact, there
can be up to exponentially many rooted paths to a given endpoint.)

Proof of
Theorem

57
.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{F}
be obtained by trimming the tree automaton
\mathcal{B}_{F}
defined earlier.
By the assumption

,

is polynomially ambiguous: it satisfies the assumptions of

) for the runs of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{F}
, which are the same as the runs of
\mathcal{B}_{F}
.
Using the bijective correspondence of

55
, we can define a family of functions indexed by

\vec{P}\in\wn F(t)\mapsto\{\text{critical nodes for the accepting run of}\ \mathcal{B}_{F}\ \text{over}\ t\ \text{corresponding to}\ \vec{P}\}

By

Proposition 63

Any function defined by an MSO interpretation can also be defined by an MSO set interpretation (up to isomorphism of output structures).

Proof.

The basic idea is to replace each first-order free variable

Ambiguity of context-free grammars and languages.

A classical result of formal language theory is that the unambiguity of a
context-free grammar is undecidable, with three proofs appearing independently
in 1962
[
29
,
33
,
77
]
. Four decades later, Wich showed in
his PhD thesis
[
129
]
that exponential ambiguity is undecidable, even
with the promise that the input grammar is either unambiguous or exponentially
ambiguous
[
129
, Theorem 7.43]
(this also implies for instance that
finite ambiguity is undecidable). On the positive side, unambiguity is decidable
for context-free grammars that generate bounded languages in the sense of
Definition

92

[
84
, Section 4]
. Also, the
ambiguity of any context-free grammar is either exponential or

O(n^{\ell})

[
129
, Theorem 7.54]
for some
\ell
that is computable
from the grammar (in fact, defined by a simple closed-form formula), and there
is a (non-effective) criterion for exponential
ambiguity
[
129
, Theorem 7.22]
that is very similar to the

“heavy cycles”
of
Section

2.2
. But polynomially bounded does
not imply
\Theta(n^{k})
here: for any computable unbounded and non-decreasing
function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, e.g.
f=\log^{*}
, one can find a CFG
whose growth rate of ambiguity is below
f
, and yet tends to

+\infty

[
129
, Theorem 6.18]
.

A closely related topic is the
inherent ambiguity
of a context-free
language, quantifying over all grammars generating the language. It is
undecidable whether a context-free language is inherently
(un)ambiguous
[
84
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
85
, Corollary on
p. 4]
), but sophisticated techniques based on generating series
have been developed to prove that specific languages are inherently
ambiguous
[
76
,
99
]
. It turns out that if a function

\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is the growth rate of the ambiguity of some context-free grammar,
then it can also be realized as an inherent ambiguity function (in a suitable
sense) for some context-free language
[
129
, Theorem 8.1]
. Therefore,
some context-free languages are inherently exponentially ambiguous (this had
been proved earlier by Naji
[
109
, Satz 4.2.1]
) while some others
have an inherent ambiguity that grows very slowly. To wrap up this part, let us
mention that Wich has given a characterization of the context-free languages
with polynomially bounded ambiguity
[
129
, Theorem 7.39]
.

Min/max-automata & regular cost functions.

In our discussion of weighted automata throughout this paper, the semiring
structure was implicitly taken to be the usual addition and multiplication of
numbers.
One can also study the growth rate of weighted automata
over semirings that do not embed into fields, e.g. tropical semirings (for which the notion of joint spectral radius from §
6.1
can be adapted, cf. e.g.
[
48
]
).
The equivalence between weighted automata and linear/affine cost register automata
[
6
,
13
]
, mentioned earlier over
\mathbb{N}
in the context of streaming string transducers, extends to arbitrary semirings.

For instance, Simon
showed
[
125
, Theorem 13]
that when
\Sigma
has at least
two letters, these classes form a strict hierarchy for
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
covering all unbounded
(\mathbb{N},\min,+)
-automata:

\left\{f\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;f\ \text{recognized by some
$(\mathbb{N},\min,+)$-automaton},\;\mathsf{growth}[f](n)=\Omega\big(\sqrt[k]{n}\big)\right\}

As another example, Colcombet, Daviaud and Zuleger proved that

(\mathbb{N},\max,+)
-automata exhibit an asymptotic behavior with fractional
degree
[
38
,
47
]
, namely: if such a weighted automaton
recognizes a function
f\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
, then

\min_{|w|\geqslant n}f(w)=\Theta(n^{\alpha})\ \text{for some}\ \alpha\in(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1])\cup\{-\infty\}\ \text{computable from the automaton.}

See
[
47
, Ex. 4.1.1 and 4.1.2]
for concrete examples of max-plus
automata that realize the degrees
\alpha=1/2
and
\alpha=2/3
respectively.

In Colcombet’s theory of regular cost functions
[
36
]
, min-plus
automata and max-plus automata are both generalized by
“B-automata”

(which are no longer weighted automata). Cost functions are the equivalence
classes of functions
\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
for a suitable
equivalence relation, and those that have a representative computed by some
B-automaton are said to be regular. The point of this theory is that many

“quantitative”
computational problems on formal languages and automata
can be solved by deciding the boundedness of regular cost functions; cf. the
survey
[
37
]
. While the present paper also attempts to take a
unified look at several quantitative questions (of asymptotic growth), there is
no clear relationship at the moment between our work and regular cost functions.

Combinations of topics.

Weighted automata with restricted ambiguity
(e.g. finite or polynomial) are a common object of study. The purpose of this
restriction can be to control their expressive power
(e.g.
[
114
,
55
,
102
]
) or to make decision
problems easier (e.g.
[
98
,
115
]
).

There are also weighted tree transducers which generalize both weighted tree automata and some nondeterministic tree transducer models; they compute relations where each pair of trees is assigned a weight, and have been applied to natural language processing. Many references on the topic are given in
[

, p. 6]
. It is possible that

Given a tree automaton, consider the following set of clauses, whose atoms are the states:

\lnot q_{1}\lor\dots\lor\lnot q_{m}\lor q\quad\text{for each transition}\ (q_{1},\dots,q_{m},a,q).
