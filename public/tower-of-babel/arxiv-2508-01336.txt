Abstract
This paper considers large-amplitude, two-dimensional and rotational solitary waves propagating under a normal electric field. We present a new model for nonlinear systems with a free surface, governed by Euler and electric potential equations. Unlike previous works, our work allows internal stagnation points and overhanging profiles. We prove the existence of a continuous curve of large-amplitude solitary wave solutions using analytic bifurcation theory and conformal mapping. Furthermore, to establish compactness, more intricate nodal analysis and Schauder estimates are performed, yielding the well-behaved structure of the curve. Along this curve, either (i) an extreme wave forms and the stagnation point occurs, (ii) a singularity emerges on the free surface, or (iii) the dimensionless wave speed becomes unbounded. Keywords: Solitary waves; Electrohydrodynamics; Global bifurcation; Conformal mapping. AMS Subject Classification (2020): 76B25, 35Q35, 76B03.

Statement of main results

The main theorem of the paper is the following existence result for large-amplitude solitary electrohydrodynamic waves, which allows for internal stagnation points and overhanging profiles.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix the gravitational constant

(ii)

For

\min\left\{\inf_{\Gamma}|\nabla\eta(s)|,\;\left(\sup_{\Gamma}|\nabla\eta(s)|\right)^{-1},\;\frac{1}{F(s)}\right\}\longrightarrow 0,

(iii)

For
\gamma=0
,

\min\left\{\inf_{\Gamma}\left(1+\epsilon_{1}-\frac{2}{F^{2}}\frac{\eta-d}{d}\right),\;\frac{1}{F(s)}\right\}\longrightarrow 0,

where

Remark 2.2
.

Let us briefly explain the terms in Theorem

.

(i) If the Froude number

remains bounded, then

\inf_{\Gamma}\left(1+\epsilon_{1}-\frac{2}{F^{2}}\frac{\eta-d}{d}\right)\to 0,

which leads to

1+\epsilon_{1}-\frac{2}{F^{2}}\frac{\eta-d}{d}=\frac{U^{2}+\epsilon_{1}{E_{2}}^{2}}{F^{2}gd}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

This condition, together with (

), implies that both the fluid velocity and electric field vanish at the wave crest, i.e.,

(ii) If

(iii) If

(iv) If

)-(
2.5j

)-(

The second main result provides lower bound estimates for the Froude number.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\eta,\zeta,\vartheta,\alpha)
be a solution to (

. If

and

on the surface

Remark 2.5
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first bound of its kind in electrohydrodynamic waves.

3
Linearized operators

We rewrite (
2.5
) as a nonlinear operator equation for
w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})
in an appropriate Banach space. This section focuses on the analysis of the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
.

3.1
Functional analytic formulation

Define the Banach spaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\{w\in(C_{\mathrm{b}}^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})\cap C_{0}^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{R}}))^{3}:\Delta w=0\,\text{in}\,\mathcal{R},\,w=0\,\text{on}\,\mathcal{B}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=(C_{\mathrm{b}}^{3+\beta}(\Gamma)\cap C_{0}^{2}(\Gamma))\times(C_{\mathrm{b}}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)\cap C_{0}^{1}(\Gamma))\times(C_{\mathrm{b}}^{3+\beta}(\Gamma)\cap C_{0}^{2}(\Gamma))

and let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
are larger spaces whose elements do not vanish at infinity.
That is,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
no longer intersect with
C_{0}^{1}
and
C_{0}^{2}
.

Equation (
2.5
) can be reformulated as a nonlinear operator equation

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}(w,\alpha)=0,

(3.1)

where the mapping
\mathscr{F}:=(\mathscr{F}_{1},\mathscr{F}_{2},\mathscr{F}_{3}):\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{1}(w,\alpha)

\displaystyle=(w_{2}+\gamma w_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma w_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{2}(w,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\big(\gamma(w_{1}+w_{1y}+w_{1}w_{1y})+w_{2y}+1\big)^{2}+\epsilon_{1}\big(2w_{3y}+w_{3y}^{2}+1\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-(1+\epsilon_{1}-2\alpha w_{1})\big(w_{1x}^{2}+(w_{1y}+1)^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{3}(w,\alpha)

\displaystyle=w_{3}.

We seek solutions in the open subset

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}:\alpha<1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1},\,\lambda(w,\alpha)>0\right\}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle\lambda(w,\alpha):=\inf_{\mathcal{R}}4(1+\epsilon_{1}-2\alpha w_{1})^{2}\big(w_{1x}^{2}+(1+w_{1y})^{2}\big)>0.

(3.2)

This reformulates assumption (
2.5n
) in terms of
w
. Notably, (
3.2
) provides a sufficient condition for applying linear Schauder estimates to the linearized problem.

By linearizing the operator, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathscr{F}_{1w}(w,\alpha)\dot{w}=c_{i}\dot{w}_{i},\\
&\mathscr{F}_{2w}(w,\alpha)\dot{w}=a_{ij}\partial_{j}\dot{w}_{i}+b_{i}\dot{w}_{i},\\
&\mathscr{F}_{3w}(w,\alpha)\dot{w}=d_{i}\dot{w}_{i},\end{split}

(3.3)

where
\partial_{1}=\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{2}=\partial_{y}
, and the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle b_{1}=2\gamma(w_{1y}+1)\big(w_{2y}+1+\gamma(w_{1}+w_{1y}+w_{1}w_{1y})\big)+2\alpha(w_{1x}^{2}+(1+w_{1y})^{2}),

\displaystyle b_{2}=0,\quad b_{3}=0,

\displaystyle a_{11}=-2(1+\epsilon_{1}-2\alpha w_{1})w_{1x},

\displaystyle a_{21}=0,\quad a_{31}=0,

\displaystyle a_{12}=2\gamma\big(w_{2y}+1+\gamma(w_{1}+w_{1y}+w_{1}w_{1y})\big)(1+w_{1})-2(1+\epsilon_{1}-2\alpha w_{1})(1+w_{1y}),

\displaystyle a_{22}=2\big(w_{2y}+1+\gamma(w_{1}+w_{1y}+w_{1}w_{1y})\big),

\displaystyle a_{32}=2\epsilon_{1}(1+w_{3y}),

\displaystyle c_{1}=\gamma(1+w_{1}),

\displaystyle c_{2}=1,\quad c_{3}=0,\quad d_{1}=0,\quad d_{2}=0,\quad d_{3}=1.

We note that (
3.3
) is the type of operator considered in appendix. Linearizing about the trivial solution
w\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{1w}(0,\alpha)\dot{w}=\gamma\dot{w}_{1}+\dot{w}_{2},

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{2w}(0,\alpha)\dot{w}=2\big(\dot{w}_{2y}+(\gamma-1-\epsilon_{1})\dot{w}_{1y}+(\gamma+\alpha)\dot{w}_{1}+\epsilon_{1}\dot{w}_{3y}\big),

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{3w}(0,\alpha)\dot{w}=\dot{w}_{3}.

(3.4c)

Remark 3.1
.

By (
2.5m
), the limit operator of
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
as
x\to\pm\infty
is
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
.

3.2
Local properness and invertibility properties

We begin by showing that the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
satisfies the conditions necessary for applying Schauder estimates.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha):\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
in (
3.3
) satisfies the Schauder estimate

\|\dot{w}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}}\leq C\big(\|\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)\dot{w}\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}}+\|\dot{w}\|_{C^{0}(\mathcal{R})}\big),

where the constant
C
depends on
\|w\|_{\mathcal{X}}
,
\alpha
and the minor constant
\lambda(w,\alpha)
.

Proof.

To apply Lemma
A.1
, we must verify that

\begin{split}\inf_{\Gamma}&\Big((a_{11}c_{2}d_{3}+a_{21}c_{3}d_{1}+a_{31}c_{1}d_{2}-a_{31}d_{1}c_{2}-a_{21}c_{1}d_{3}-a_{11}c_{3}d_{2})^{2}\\
&\quad+(a_{12}c_{2}d_{3}+a_{22}c_{3}d_{1}+a_{32}c_{1}d_{2}-a_{32}d_{1}c_{2}-a_{22}c_{1}d_{3}-a_{12}c_{3}d_{2})^{2}\Big)>0.\end{split}

Based on coefficients given in Subsection 3.1 and condition (
3.2
), we derive

\begin{split}\inf_{\Gamma}&\Big((a_{11}c_{2}d_{3}+a_{21}c_{3}d_{1}+a_{31}c_{1}d_{2}-a_{31}d_{1}c_{2}-a_{21}c_{1}d_{3}-a_{11}c_{3}d_{2})^{2}\\
&\quad+(a_{12}c_{2}d_{3}+a_{22}c_{3}d_{1}+a_{32}c_{1}d_{2}-a_{32}d_{1}c_{2}-a_{22}c_{1}d_{3}-a_{12}c_{3}d_{2})^{2}\Big)\\
&=\inf_{\Gamma}4(1+\epsilon_{1}-2\alpha w_{1})^{2}\big(w_{1x}^{2}+(1+w_{1y})^{2}\big)>0.\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

For solitary electrohydrodynamic waves, the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}})
is not a Fredholm map from
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
. In particular, its range is not closed (see
[
34
]
). To overcome this difficulty, we employ a center manifold reduction method. However, before proceeding, we first need to establish several preparatory results.

Lemma 3.3

(Injectivity)
.

The linear operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha):\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
defined in (
3.4
) has a trivial kernel if and only if
\alpha<1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}
.

Proof.

Using separation of variables, it suffices to rule out solutions
(\dot{w}_{1},\dot{w}_{2},\dot{w}_{3})\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
of the form

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{1}=c_{1}\cos(kx)\sinh(ky),\quad\dot{w}_{2}=c_{2}\cos(kx)\sinh(ky),\quad\dot{w}_{3}=c_{3}\cos(kx)\sinh(ky),

(3.5)

where
k
is a real wave number and
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
. Substituting (
3.5
) into (
3.4a
) and (
3.4c
), we obtain
c_{2}=-c_{1}\gamma
and
c_{3}=0
. Inserting (
3.5
) into (
3.4b
), we find that nontrivial solutions exist if and only if the dispersion relation

\displaystyle\gamma+\alpha=(1+\epsilon_{1})k\coth(k)

(3.6)

is satisfied. Observe that
k\coth(k)
attains its minimum value of 1 at
k=0
. Therefore, (
3.6
) admits no real solution whenever
\alpha<1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

(i) Lemma
3.3
demonstrates that the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
is injective when mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
, given that
\alpha<1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}
.

(ii) If
\alpha=1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}
, the linear operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
becomes singular.

We proceed to analyze the operator’s local properness and its invertibility properties.

Lemma 3.5

(Local properness)
.

For
(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
is locally proper both from
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
and from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.

As the argument for Lemma
3.5
closely resembles that of
[
23
, Lemma 5.5]
, we omit the details here.

Lemma 3.6

(Invertibility)
.

For
(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the linear operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
is invertible both from
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
and from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5
establish that
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
has a trivial kernel and is locally proper as a mapping from both
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
and
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
. To show invertibility on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
, it remains to verify that the operator has Fredholm index zero.

To this end, we consider a one-parameter family of linear operators
\mathscr{L}(t):\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
, defined by

\mathscr{L}(t)\dot{w}:=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\dot{w}_{1}+\dot{w}_{2}\\
2\big(\dot{w}_{2y}+(\gamma-1-\epsilon_{1})\dot{w}_{1y}+(\gamma+\alpha)t\dot{w}_{1}+\epsilon_{1}\dot{w}_{3y}\big)\\
\dot{w}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\text{ for }t\in[0,1].

A modification of the argument in Lemma
3.3
shows that
\mathscr{L}(t)
is injective from
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
, provided
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}})
. In particular, the dispersion relation (
3.6
) takes the form

t(\gamma+\alpha)=(1+\epsilon_{1})k\coth(k),

which implies that the kernel is trivial whenever
t(\gamma+\alpha)<1+\epsilon_{1}
.

Using the argument from Lemma
3.5
, we deduce that
\mathscr{L}(t):\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
is locally proper and thus semi-Fredholm with a trivial kernel for all
t\in[0,1]
. Consequently, the Fredholm index of
\mathscr{L}(t)
remains constant throughout the interval. The result of
[
8
, Lemma A.8]
extends to our setting, implying that
\mathscr{L}(0)
is invertible and therefore has Fredholm index zero. By the continuity of the Fredholm index, we deduce that
\mathscr{L}(1)=\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
also has index zero. Together with local properness and a trivial kernel, this implies that
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha):\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{b}}
is invertible.

It remains to show that
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
is also invertible as a map from
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
. Since
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
, injectivity on
\mathcal{X}
follows immediately. To prove surjectivity, let
\dot{f}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Because the operator is invertible on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
, there exists
\dot{w}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{b}}
such that
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)\dot{w}=\dot{f}
. A translation argument ensures that
\dot{w}\in\mathcal{X}
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

For
(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the linear operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
is Fredholm with index
0
as a map from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

Fix
(w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
. By Remark
3.1
, the coefficients of
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w,\alpha)
approach those of
\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha)
as
|x|\to\infty
. The proof is completed by combining Lemma
3.6
with
[
36
, Lemmas A.12 and A.13]
.
∎

4
Existence theory

4.1
Small-amplitude

Our objective in this subsection is to construct small-amplitude solutions for values of

near
\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}

\alpha=\alpha^{\varepsilon}:=\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}-\varepsilon=1-\gamma+\epsilon_{1}-\varepsilon,

where

\displaystyle\eta_{x}<0\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{R}\cup\Gamma)\cap\{x>0\},\quad\eta_{x}>0\quad\text{in }(\mathcal{R}\cup\Gamma)\cap\{x<0\},

(4.1)

where
\eta_{x}=\frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}x}
. We now state the main result of this subsection as the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{*}>0
and a continuous local curve

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\{(w^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}):0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{*}\}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}

(4.2)

consisting of nontrivial symmetric solutions to
\mathscr{F}(w,\alpha^{\varepsilon})=0
, with the asymptotic expansion

w_{1}^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=\frac{3\varepsilon}{3-3\gamma+\gamma^{2}+\epsilon_{1}}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3\varepsilon}}{2}x\right)+O(\varepsilon^{2+\frac{1}{2}})

in
C_{\mathrm{b}}^{3+\beta}
. Moreover, the following properties hold:

(i)

(Monotonicity): Every solution on
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}
satisfies the nodal property (
4.1
).

(ii)

(Uniqueness): If
w\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
are sufficiently small, and
w
satisfies (
4.1
), then the equation
\mathscr{F}(w,\alpha^{\varepsilon})=0
implies
w=w^{\varepsilon}
.

(iii)

(Invertibility): The linearized operator
\mathscr{F}_{w}(w^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon})
is invertible as a map from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{*}
.

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we employ the center manifold reduction method developed in
[
7
]
. This approach refines and extends the classical framework introduced by Kirchgässner
[
25
]
and further developed by Mielke
[
29
,
30
]
. A principal advantage of this methodology is that the entire analysis is formulated in Hölder spaces, thereby allowing the reduced equations on the center manifold to be solved explicitly via a direct power series expansion.

As is standard in center manifold theory, we first expand the function space to allow for small exponential growth in the spatial variable
x
. To this end, we define exponentially weighted variants of the spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mu}:=\Big\{(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})\in(C_{\mu}^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}}))^{3}:\Delta w_{i}=0\;\text{in}\;\mathcal{R},\;w_{i}=0\;\text{on}\;\mathcal{B}\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mu}:=C_{\mu}^{3+\beta}(\Gamma)\times C_{\mu}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)\times C_{\mu}^{3+\beta}(\Gamma).

For any
\mu>0
, the linearized operator around the trivial flow is given by

\mathscr{L}:=\mathscr{F}_{w}(0,\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}).

As stated explicitly in (
3.4
), this operator is a mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{\mu}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mu}
. For sufficiently small
\mu>0
, it is straightforward to verify that the kernel of
\mathscr{L}
is two-dimensional and is given by

\ker\mathscr{L}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}(A+Bx)\varphi_{1}(y)\\
(A+Bx)\varphi_{2}(y)\\
(A+Bx)\varphi_{3}(y)\end{pmatrix}:A,B\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}(y)\\
\varphi_{2}(y)\\
\varphi_{3}(y)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
-\gamma y\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 4.2
.

(Center manifold reduction)
There exists
0<\mu\ll 1
, neighborhoods
\textbf{U}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\textbf{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

coordinate map

with

satisfying

\Upsilon(0,0,\varepsilon)=\Upsilon_{A}(0,0,\varepsilon)=\Upsilon_{B}(0,0,\varepsilon)=0~\text{ for all }\varepsilon,

such that the following hold:

(i) Suppose that
(w,\varepsilon)\in\bm{U}
with
\alpha=\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}-\varepsilon
solves (
3.1
). Then
q(x):=w_{1}(x,1)
solves the second-order ODE

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}=f(q,q^{\prime},\varepsilon),

(4.3)

where

is the

mapping defined as

\displaystyle f(A,B,\varepsilon):=\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\right|_{x=0}\Upsilon(A,B,\varepsilon)(x,1),

(4.4)

and admits the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle f(A,B,\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle 3\varepsilon A-\frac{3}{2}(3-3\gamma+\gamma^{2}+\epsilon_{1})A^{2}

\displaystyle+O\big((|A|+|B|)(|A|+|B|+|\varepsilon|)^{2}\big).

(4.5)

(ii) Conversely, if

for solution

of (

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 4.3
.

It is straightforward to verify that equation (

. As a result, we have

\Upsilon(A,B,\varepsilon)(x,1)=\Upsilon(A,-B,\varepsilon)(-x,1),

which implies that the function

Proof.

Based on the results in
[

[

that the coordinate map

).

Explicitly, the index set

Thus, we expand

in the space

We now compute the coefficients

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle A^{2}\big((2+2\epsilon_{1})\Upsilon_{200}^{1}+(2\gamma-2-2\epsilon_{1})\partial_{y}\Upsilon_{200}^{1}+2\partial_{y}\Upsilon_{200}^{2}+2\epsilon_{1}\partial_{y}\Upsilon_{200}^{3}+3-2\gamma+\gamma^{2}+\epsilon_{1}\big)

The third boundary condition,

0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{L}\Upsilon_{101}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\\

Proof of Theorem

Under this transformation, the equation (

We now verify the three properties:

5.2
Nonexistence of monotone bores

In this subsection, we establish the nonexistence of monotone bores, as stated in Theorem

Traditionally, the flow force defined by

\int\left(P-P_{\text{atm}}+U^{2}\right)\,dY,

plays an important role in the analysis of steady waves, where
P_{\text{atm}}
is atmospheric pressure at the surface. It is straightforward to verify that this quantity is independent of

\displaystyle S=\int_{x=\mathrm{constant}}\begin{pmatrix}P-P_{\text{atm}}+U^{2}+\epsilon_{1}{E_{2}}^{2}\end{pmatrix}dY-(UV-\epsilon_{1}{E_{1}}{E_{2}})dX.

In terms of the dimensionless variables, (

P+\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}+v^{2})+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}({e_{1}}^{2}+{e_{2}}^{2})+\alpha(\eta-1)=\gamma\psi+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}+P_{\text{atm}}-\gamma(1-\frac{1}{2}\gamma),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle P-P_{\text{atm}}+u^{2}+\epsilon_{1}{e_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}({e_{2}}^{2}-{e_{1}}^{2})-\alpha(\eta-1)+\gamma\psi+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}-\gamma(1-\frac{1}{2}\gamma).

Then, we have

\begin{split}S=\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(&\big(\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}({e_{2}}^{2}-{e_{1}}^{2})-\alpha(\eta-1)+\gamma(\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\eta^{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}\gamma)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\big)\eta_{y}+(uv-\epsilon_{1}{e_{1}}{e_{2}})\eta_{x}\bigg)dy.\end{split}

(5.13)

A careful calculation yields

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})\eta_{y}+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}({e_{2}}^{2}-{e_{1}}^{2})\eta_{y}+uv\eta_{x}-\epsilon_{1}{e_{1}}{e_{2}}\eta_{x}\\

(5.14)

From (

\begin{split}S(x;\eta,\zeta,\alpha):=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\frac{\eta_{y}\left(\zeta_{y}^{2}-\zeta_{x}^{2}\right)+2\eta_{x}\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}\bigg)\,dy+\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\frac{\eta_{y}\left(\vartheta_{y}^{2}-\vartheta_{x}^{2}\right)+2\eta_{x}\vartheta_{x}\vartheta_{y}}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}\bigg)\,dy\\
&-\left.\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}\eta^{3}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\eta^{2}-\frac{2\alpha+1+\epsilon_{1}}{2}\eta\right)\right|_{y=1}.\end{split}

To demonstrate that

is independent of

\begin{split}&\frac{{(\zeta_{y}+i\zeta_{x})}^{2}}{\eta_{y}+i\eta_{x}}=\frac{\eta_{y}(\zeta_{y}^{2}-\zeta_{x}^{2})+2\eta_{x}\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}+i\frac{2\eta_{y}\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}-\eta_{x}({\zeta_{y}}^{2}-{\zeta_{x}}^{2})}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}},\\
&\frac{{(\vartheta_{y}+i\vartheta_{x})}^{2}}{\eta_{y}+i\eta_{x}}=\frac{\eta_{y}(\vartheta_{y}^{2}-\vartheta_{x}^{2})+2\eta_{x}\vartheta_{x}\vartheta_{y}}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}+i\frac{2\eta_{y}\vartheta_{x}\vartheta_{y}-\eta_{x}({\vartheta_{y}}^{2}-{\vartheta_{x}}^{2})}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}.\end{split}

Then, by differentiating under the integral and using the Cauchy-Riemann equations, we conclude that

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta_{y}(\zeta_{y}^{2}-\zeta_{x}^{2})+2\eta_{x}\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}dy=\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\eta_{y}\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}-\eta_{x}(\zeta_{y}^{2}-\zeta_{x}^{2})}{\eta_{x}^{2}+\eta_{y}^{2}}\bigg|_{y=1},\\
