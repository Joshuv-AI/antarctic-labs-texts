Triple potentiator combinations increase the functional correction of several CFTR gating mutants
Authors: Guido Veit, Christian Vaccarin, Márton A. Simon, Haijin Xu, Houriyeh Hajizadeh, Tina He, Adriana Chilin, Elias Matouk, László Csanády, Giovanni Marzaro, Gergely L. Lukacs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Trikafta drug combination, comprising the corrector tezacaftor (VX-661), the potentiator ivacaftor (VX-770) and the dual corrector/potentiator elexacaftor (VX-445), has been FDA-approved for treatment of cystic fibrosis caused by ~300 cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) mutations. Nevertheless, several CFTR variants exhibit limited response to Trikafta. To address this therapeutic gap, we investigated whether the potentiator activity of VX-445 can complement the VX-770 and preclinical “co-potentiators” activity in partially responsive CFTR mutants. Functional clustering of clinical and preclinical potentiator profiles suggests that VX-445 represents a distinct potentiator class, an inference supported by its additivity with both VX-770/VX-770-like potentiators and co-potentiators across five CFTR mutants in bronchial epithelia. This concept was further validated in gene-edited 16HBE and primary human nasal epithelia, expressing G551D-, N1303K-, and W1282X-CFTR, the 3rd, 4th, and 6th most common CF-mutations, respectively, and was confirmed at the single-channel level. Moreover, we present the development of a novel series of co-potentiator compounds that are derived from our previously described 4172 corrector scaffold, which exhibit low micromolar potency. Our findings suggest that triple potentiation can significantly enhance functional restoration of poorly responsive gating mutants, thereby uncovering novel avenues for therapeutic development.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF), one of the most common lethal autosomal recessive diseases, is caused by mutations in the CF Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR), a cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA)-activated anion channel expressed at the apical membrane of secretory epithelia1. CFTR belongs to the ATP-binding cassette transporter C (ABCC) subfamily and consists of two homologous halves, each containing a membrane-spanning domain (MSD1 and MSD2) and a nucleotide-binding domain (NBD1 and NBD2), connected by a unique regulatory (R) domain2. Over 2000 mutations have been identified in the CFTR gene (Cystic Fibrosis Mutation Database, www.genet.sickkids.on.ca/app and CFTR2, www.cftr2.org). These variants are categorized into six functional classes according to their primary cellular phenotypes, although some mutations exhibit characteristics of more than one mutation class3. Class I-VI variants are associated with protein expression, folding/processing, gating, conductance, protein quantity, and peripheral stability defects, respectively3.

CFTR modulators have been developed that rescue the folding (correctors) or gating (potentiators) defects of CF mutations and direct binding of several modulators to the native-like conformation has been demonstrated4–7. The potentiator VX-770 (ivacaftor) has been FDA-approved for monotherapy of 96 mutants and, together with the correctors VX-661 (tezacaftor) and VX-445 (elexacaftor), form the triple drug triple drug combination Trikafta. Trikafta has been FDA-approved for ~300 CFTR variants, including the most common CF-causing mutation F508del8. The F508del mutation leads to a folding, stability, and gating defect of the CFTR channel, which is corrected to >60% of the wild-type function by the Trikafta combination in primary human bronchial epithelia (HBE) and human nasal epithelia (HNE) cells9,10.

We and others recently demonstrated that, besides functioning as a corrector, VX-445 has potentiator activity, which is at least additive to that of VX-770, thereby contributing to the functional rescue of F508del and selected gating mutants11–13. However, despite the dual corrector and potentiator activity of VX-445, the function of many missense mutations is only partially restored upon Trikafta therapy9,13,14. Another group of potentiators, termed co-potentiator, has been identified, which are additive or synergistic to VX-770 and increase the channel function of a variety of mutants including G551D-, W1282X-, and N1303K-CFTR15–18, the 3rd, 6th and 4th most common CF-causing mutations with allelic frequencies of 2.1, 1.2 and 1.6% in the CFTR2 database (CFTR2 project, www.cftr2.org), respectively. Although the binding sites and molecular mechanisms of co-potentiators remain to be elucidated, their additive functional effect led to the classification of potentiators into two distinct groups: VX-770-like potentiators include VX-770 and the investigational compound ABBV-974, and co-potentitators, which encompass compounds such as the flavonoid apigenin and the curcumin derivative bisdemethoxycurcumin (bDMC)16,19,20.

Here, we show that pairwise combinations of VX-445 with VX-770 or with preclinical co-potentiators at or near maximally effective concentrations lead to additive potentiation of several CFTR gating mutants. Together with the distinct cryo-EM binding sites of VX-445 and VX-770 on CFTR5,6, and the observation that the triple combination of VX-445, VX-770, and co-potentiator(s) produces significantly greater channel activation than VX-770 plus co-potentiators alone, these findings support the hypothesis that VX-445 represents a mechanistically distinct potentiator class. Furthermore, we developed a novel series of co-potentiators, characterized by favorable pharmacokinetic profiles and low-micromolar potencies. These results suggest new opportunities for modulator drug development aimed at targeting a subset of CFTR mutants that remain poorly responsive to approved therapies.

Results

VX-445, a novel potentiator class, additively acts with VX-770/VX-770-like potentiators and co-potentiators

Additive gating activation of several CFTR mutants, including the R352Q, S549R, S549N, G551D, G1244E, W1282X and N1303K, with VX-770/VX-770-like potentiators and co-potentiators has been previously reported (Supplementary Fig. 1)16,18,19. In addition, the combination of VX-770 and VX-445 at saturating concentrations additively potentiates the F508del-CFTR and selected gating mutants, such as G551D and G1244E11,12. To further investigate the VX-445 potentiator mechanism and to expand the list of mutants that respond to VX-445 potentiation, we expressed R352Q, S549R, S549N, W1282X, and N1303K-CFTR in the bronchial epithelial cell line CFBE41o-. CFTR channel function was assessed using the halide-sensitive YFP-quenching assay upon exposure to single potentiators or dual-potentiator combinations18. CFTR activation by PKA-mediated phosphorylation was initiated by syringe-pump-mediated injection of the adenylate cyclase activator forskolin and the phosphodiesterase inhibitor IBMX, in the presence of potentiators at or near their saturating concentrations. To further define the VX-445-dependent channel activation in relation to VX-770/VX-770-like and co-potentiators, combinatorial profiling was performed. The efficacy of all potentiator pairs was analyzed with respect to the additivity of their individual effects at or near saturating concentrations, followed by the clustering of their interaction fingerprints. As shown previously, VX-770 and ABBV-974 cluster together (Fig. 1B)12,18, consistent with their overlapping binding sites, formed by transmembrane helices (TM) 6 and 8 in the MSD1 and MSD2, respectively, as revealed by cryo-electron microscopy of CFTR4. Similarly, the co-potentiators bDMC and apigenin clustered together for most mutants (Fig. 1B). In contrast, VX-445 forms a distinct category, separated by variable but substantial distance from the other clusters (Fig. 1B). This is consistent with its recently identified binding site, formed by elements of MSD1 (TM2 and Lasso motif) and MSD2 (TM10-11), which is distinct from the VX-770 binding site5,6. Based on these observations, we propose that VX-445 does not belong to either the VX-770/VX-770-like potentiator or co-potentiator groups, but instead defines a separate functional potentiators class (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Triple potentiator combination hyperactivates G551D-CFTR

To investigate whether a combination of the three potentiator classes could additively stimulate the endogenously expressed G551D-CFTR, we used two epithelial cell models. The human bronchial epithelial cell line 16HBE14o- (16HBE), which was modified by CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing to contain the G551D mutation (16HBEge), was obtained from the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Therapeutics (CFFT)21. The G551D-CFTR channel function was assessed by using short-circuit current (Isc) measurements in polarized 16HBEge cells. As expected from its severe gating defect, the G551D mutant exhibited minimal response to forskolin as compared to the WT-CFTR (Fig.2A). Because VX-770 is a permissive factor for VX-445 potentiation12, VX-770 was added first, followed by the addition of VX-445 and apigenin as either the second or third potentiator. Sequential addition of saturating concentration of VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin increased the G551D-CFTR function with relative contributions of ~27%, ~40%, and ~28%, respectively, to the maximal Isc response (Fig. 2A). To determine whether the greater-than WT-CFTR activity of G551D-CFTR was due to increased mutant protein expression, we compared their abundance by immunoblotting. Densitometric analysis revealed that the expression of post-Golgi localized, complex-glycosylated band C form of G551D-CFTR was ~125% of that of the WT-CFTR in 16HBE14o- cells (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 5). This finding, together with the Isc data, indicates that G551D-CFTR phosphorylation combined with triple potentiation hyperactivates the channel. Since the single-channel open probability (Po) of WT-CFTR has been estimated at ~0.43 for channels reconstituted in lipid bilayers22 and ~0.38 in excised patches23, we estimate that the Po of G551D-CFTR under these conditions reaches ~0.56–0.64.

Similar results were obtained in primary HNE cultures isolated from a patient homozygous for the G551D mutation and differentiated at the air-liquid interface for three weeks. In these cultures, triple potentiation produced a maximal Isc of ~200% of the average forskolin-stimulated Isc recorded from HNE of 10 non-CF donors, with a relative contribution of VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin of ~24%, ~56% and ~20%, respectively (Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, HNE cultures isolated from compound heterozygous individuals carrying one G551D allele and a second allele encoding either Y1092X or F508del also reached >100% of WT-CFTR activity upon triple potentiator combination (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin additively increase the Po of G551D-CFTR single-channels

To further investigate the effect of the potentiator combination at the single molecule level, we measured the activation of transiently expressed G551D-CFTR in inside-out patches of HEK-293T cells. To approximate the unbound fraction of potentiators in physiological solutions, VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin were applied to the cytosolic bath at 50 nM, 1 µM, and 1 µM, respectively, and the excised patches were perfused in the presence of the catalytic subunit of PKA at 25˚C, as before24. This experimental setup allows the accumulation of the potentiators in excised membrane patches, as suggested by cellular concentration studies25,26.

Under these conditions, the negligible basal Po of G551D-CFTR (~0.006) was increased to 0.026, 0.103 and 0.166 by consecutive addition of VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin, respectively, indicating additive potentiation (Fig. 2D, E, Supplementary Fig. 2B). The Po values obtained from the single-channel measurements were lower than those inferred from macroscopic measurements described above, likely because the single-channel measurements were performed at lower temperature (25 °C) and may also reflect differences in post-translational modifications in the HEK-293T heterologous expression system.

Cross-talks among gating potentiator classes

Based on the relatively high affinity of VX-770 and VX-445 and their distinct binding sites in the purified CFTR channel, it is plausible that these drugs do not compete for binding4–6. Nevertheless, the presence of VX-770 did increase the VX-445 potentiator activity, as measured by enhanced Isc of G551D and F508del-CFTR12. This phenomenon could reflect dynamic rearrangement of allosteric networks that connect the VX-445 binding site, formed by the lasso motif, TM2, TM1, and TM12 helices, with structural and dynamic changes associated with increased pore opening in the presence of VX-7704,27. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that VX-770 did not alter the EC50 of VX-445-mediated G551D-CFTR potentiation. Indeed, the EC50 of the VX-445-induced activation was similar in absence (2.3 ± 0.6 nM) and presence (2.5 ± 0.2 nM) of VX-770 (Fig. 2F). In contrast, the EC50 of apigenin, 347 ± 55 µM, was reduced to 106 ± 11 µM and 56 ± 1 µM in presence of VX-770 and VX-445, respectively, suggesting that VX-770 and VX-445 not only improve the gating efficiency of G551D-CFTR but also enhance the apparent affinity of apigenin to its cognate binding site (Fig. 2G). The potency of VX-770 remained unchanged in the presence of apigenin or bDMC18.

Triple-potentiator combinations augment the function of poorly modulator-responsive CFTR mutants

The W1282X mutation introduces a premature termination codon (PTC), generating transcripts that are susceptible to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), which reduces the CFTR mRNA abundance by >70%28,29. The resulting low amount of C-terminally truncated protein can be partially corrected by Trikafta30 and is susceptible to dual potentiator combinations16,17. We first used gene-edited 16HBE cells expressing the W1282X-CFTR21 to determine whether apigenin, as a co-potentiator, can further enhance the effect of Trikafta potentiators. Under conditions where VX-661 was used as a corrector and VX-770, VX-445 and apigenin were applied as potentiators, functional correction of reached 11% of the phosphorylated WT-CFTR, with a relative contribution of 9%, 49% and 41% from VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin, respectively (Fig. 3A). When VX-445 was used both as a corrector and potentiator, VX-661 as a corrector, and VX-770 with apigenin as acute potentiators, functional correction increased to 14% of WT-CFTR current (Fig. 3B). In HNE cultures from a patient homozygous for W1282X-CFTR, NMD needed to be partially suppressed with an SMG1 inhibitor31 to elevate the low steady-state abundance of the W1282X-CFTR mRNA. Under these conditions, combined chronic VX-661 + VX-445 treatment and acute VX-770+apigenin exposure resulted in an Isc corresponding to ~9% of the WT (Fig. 3C).

The N1303K mutation is associated with defective CFTR processing, cell-surface trafficking, and channel gating, and is largely refractory to first-generation CFTR modulator combinations, such as V809 + VX-770 or VX-661 + VX-77016. The N1303K-CFTR function was partially corrected by combining first-generation modulator drugs with co-potentiators16 or with VX-4459,32,33, but the efficacy remains < 25% of the WT-CFTR activity in primary HNE cultures9 (Fig. 3E). We therefore next assessed the effect of adding a third potentiator in gene-edited N1303K-16HBE and primary HNE homozygous for the N1303K mutation (Fig. 3D, E).

In 16HBEge cells corrected with VX-661 and acutely potentiated with VX-770, VX-445 and apigenin, the N1303K current reached >100% of the WT with relative contributions of 8%, 44% and 46% from VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin, respectively (Fig. 3D). In HNE cultures, the absolute functional correction efficacy was lower, reaching 45% of the WT Isc, with a relative contribution of 1%, 34 and 46% from VX-770, VX-445, and apigenin-elicited currents, respectively (Fig. 3E). These results confirm recent observations in intestinal organoids and nasal epithelia from individuals with N1303K-CFTR34–36 and suggest that the efficacy of Trikafta-mediated for N1303K-CFTR functional correction, which has been shown to be substantial in several recent clinical studies33,37,38, could nearly be doubled by the introduction of a co-potentiator to complement the mechanism of action of VX-770/VX-770-like and VX-445 potentiators.

Development of more potent co-potentiators

The low potency and limited bioavailability of apigenin restrict its utility as a co-potentiator. We previously identified compound 4172 that synergistically rescued the folding defect of F508del-CFTR and selected rare missense mutants in combination with the first-generation corrector drugs VX-809 and VX-66139. Four sites of modification were considered: the pyrimidinone ring, site A, the thiophene ring, site B, the methylene bridge, site C, and the terminal phenyl ring, site D. The resulting SAR was subsequently used to design two compounds incorporating combined structural variations at both C and D sites, designated E in Fig. 4A. Modifications of the A and B rings did not increase the efficacy of the parent compound ML-014. Replacement of -CF3 moiety with other groups, including dimethyl, fused cyclohexyl, pentafluoroethyl, or aryl substitutions, yielded inactive compounds. Similarly, the substitution of the pyrimidinone ring with an aminopyrimidine was detrimental. Substitution at the thiophene ring, or its replacement with (substituted)phenyl or amines, markedly reduced the potentiator activity. Replacement of thiophene with a 3-N-Boc-aniline (ML-042) led to a compound active only on the N1303K-CFTR mutant with modest improvement relative to ML-014.

The methylene bridge (site C) turned out to be the most informative region of the molecule. Evaluation of previously published compounds40 showed that the functionalization of the methylene bridge with a spiro-cyclopentyl moiety produced an almost ineffective compound. The initial attempt to reduce the steric hindrance at site C with spiro-cyclopropyl (ML-050) was unsuccessful. In contrast, introduction of an isopropyl function generated the first improved analog of ML-014, ML-051, suggesting that a bulky hydrophobic function can be tolerated at site C, provided that a hydrogen atom is retained. Smaller substituents (either hydrophobic as chlorine or hydrophilic as hydroxy or hydroxymethyl) led to inactive compounds. Conversely, introduction of a pyrrolidine group yielded a second improved potentiator (ML-054).

We then focused on the phenyl moiety at site D. Replacement of the chlorine atom with a hydroxy function, ML-056, abolished potentiation of G551D-CFTR while improving activity toward N1303K-CFTR. The opposite profile was observed when the chlorophenyl moiety was replaced with coumaran, ML-064. Notably, potentiation of both mutants was achieved with the use of an indole substituent, ML-065, suggesting that the phenyl ring can be spatially displaced from the methylene bridge. Consistent with this interpretation, ML-016, from the previous series and bearing the 2-(chlorophenoxy)propyl-2-en bridge, exhibited low but detectable potentiator activity. Replacement of the chlorophenoxy moiety with the bromophenoxy generated a third improved analog of ML-014, ML-069.

Finally, we designed a compound that integrated the major SAR features so far identified: i) retention of a CH group at site C, ii) steric bulk hindrance at the bridge; and iii) the favorable contribution of phenoxy moiety at site D. This compound, ML-070, containing a dihydrobenzodioxane at site E, representing C + D optimization, emerged as the fourth improved potentiator in the series.

ML-014 and the improved analogs, ML-051, ML-054, ML-069, and ML-070, were submitted to preliminary pharmacokinetic evaluation (Supplementary Table 3). All compounds showed low aqueous solubility profiles, consistent with their high cLogP values, and near-quantitative plasma protein binding, >99%. ML-014, ML-054 and ML-070 were stable to human liver microsomes, with intrinsic clearance <10 μL/min/mg protein, and displayed promising half-lives of 300, 160, and 340 min, respectively.

Finally, an in vivo pharmacokinetic study was performed for ML-014 in mice following intravenous or oral administration (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 4). Non-compartmental analysis was used to determine the principal pharmacokinetic parameters, including Cmax (maximum plasma concentration), Clast (plasma concentration at last timepoint), Tmax (time of maximum plasma concentration), AUC0-last (AUC from time zero to the time of the last quantifiable plasma concentration), AUCinf (AUC from time zero extrapolated to infinity), MRT (mean residence time), and t½ (half life). Following intravenous administration at 2.5 mg/kg. ML-014 reached a Cmax of 3447 ng/mL and remained quantifiable in plasma after 24 h, with an MRT of 337 min similar to t½ of 252 min. ML-014 showed a low clearance with a medium-low steady-state volume of distribution (Vss). Following oral administration at 30 mg/kg, Tmax was 6 h with a plasma concentration of 11,368 ng/mL and an MRT comparable to that observed after intravenous dosing. Oral bioavailability was 33% (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Of the ~1200 disease-causing mutations in the CFTR2 database (https://cftr2.org), only ~300 mutations are approved for the highly-effective modulator therapy (HEMT) with the Trikafta triple drug combination. In a recent study of 655 disease-causing mutants, including 590 missense variants, >30% were classified as having no or borderline responses to Trikafta41. Moreover, in some people with CF (pwCF) who are eligible for Trikafta, mental health and other adverse effects require dose reduction, possibly reducing therapeutic efficacy42–44. Therefore, despite the impressive clinical benefit of HEMT therapy for pwCF with at least one copy of F508del45,46, there remains a need to achieve higher functional correction, particularly for mutants with limited responsiveness to established modulators. This may be accomplished by further increasing the folding/processing efficiency or the Po of the affected CFTR mutants.

In this study, we present a strategy to increase the Po of poorly responsive gating mutants by combining three potentiators with unique mechanisms. Based on their non-redundant mechanism of action at saturating concentrations, we propose to expand the classification of potentiators into three classes. This proposition is based on cluster analysis of the potentiators’ functional profiles in five severe gating mutants and the previously established additive effect of VX-770 with “co-potentiators”15–18, as well as the distinct binding sites of VX-770 and VX-4454,5. VX-770 and the investigational compound ABBV-974 are designated as VX-770-like potentiators, while apigenin and bDMC belong to co-potentiators16,18,19. VX-445 forms its own functional class.

It has been recognized that combining VX-770 with co-potentiators such as apigenin or curcumin additively increases the channel function of certain gating mutants, including W1282X- or G551D-CFTR16,18–20. Here we show that combining apigenin with VX-770 and VX-445 potentiators considerably increases the channel function of CF mutants associated with gating defects beyond that of dual potentiator combinations. In patient-derived HNE, acute addition of apigenin enhanced the current of VX-770 + VX-445-potentiated N1303K-CFTR from ~20% to >40% of WT levels. Triple potentiation reached > 100% of WT-CFTR activity from a single allele of G551D-CFTR, and addition of a third potentiator increased the Isc activation of W1282X-CFTR by >50%. However, the resulting W1282X current remained below the threshold for significant clinical benefit due to the reduced transcript level caused by NMD in 16HBE and primary cells21,28,29.

Although the low bioavailability of apigenin can, in principle, be augmented by nanoparticle-based formulation47 and its low CFTR potentiation efficacy is increased in the presence of VX-770 and VX-445 (Fig. 2E), its low potency may limit clinical applicability. We therefore developed potentiator compounds with improved potency. The trifluoromethyl analog ML-014, derived from the previously reported 4172 corrector scaffold39,40, unexpectedly exhibited potentiator rather than corrector activity. This observation suggests that, similar to the VX-445 binding site6, the as-yet unidentified binding site for this compound series may support both CFTR folding correction and gating activation. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of co-potentiator isolation for future clinical development.

Extensive medicinal chemistry optimization allowed the development of ML-014 analogs with improved potency in G551D- and N1303K-CFBE41o- cells. Evaluation of a library of ~70 compounds, established a structure-activity relationship, providing a rational framework for future analog design. The most potent compounds displayed favorable pharmacokinetic profiles. Despite limited aqueous solubility, ML-014 efficiently absorbed after oral administration, reaching 33% bioavailability.

Jointly, our findings support the concept that introducing a third mechanistically distinct potentiator can significantly increase the functional correction of a subset of CFTR variants with residual conductance/gating defects. This approach will likely provide therapeutic benefit for multiple CFTR mutants that remain only partially corrected by approved modulators.

Reagents and antibodies

CFTR modulators VX-445, VX-770 and ABBV-974 (former GLPG1837), as well as forskolin and CFTRInh-172, were purchased from MedChemExpress. Bisdemethoxycurcumin (bDMC), apigenin, amiloride, ATP, amphotericin B, and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) were from Sigma. The following antibodies were used: mouse anti-CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Foundation, 660, 1:1000) and mouse anti-Na+/K+-ATPase (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-48345, 1:5000). Include all reagents here.

Synthesis of small molecule compounds

Materials: All solvents and starting materials were purchased from commercial suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich and Carlo Erba) and used without further purification. The thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on pre-coated plates of silica gel 60 with fluorescent indicator UV254 (0.2 mm, Merck); column chromatography was performed with silica gel 60 (0.063–0.100 mm, Merck). Flash chromatography was performed on a Biotage Isolera One instrument (Uppsala, Sweden) using SNAP KP-Sil cartridges, eluting with the solvent specified for individual compounds. Detection of reaction products was performed by evaluating the absorbance at λ = 254 nm. The 1H- and 13C-NMR spectra were recorded at room temperature on a Bruker 400-AMX spectrometer (Billerica, MA, USA); chemical shifts are reported in ppm and are referred to residual solvent signals. Coupling constants are reported in Hz. Reported yield refers to the purified compounds and reactions that are not optimized. Purity for all the tested compounds was determined by elemental analyses and found to be equal to or greater than 95%.

Synthetic procedures: Detailed synthesis of compounds ML-001 to ML-025 is described in our previous paper40. See Supplementary Table 1 for chemical structures. Tested compounds were synthesized using the same synthetic strategy as previously reported40 and briefly resumed in Supplementary Fig. 3. Specific details and single reactions not included in the general scheme are described in Supplementary Table 2.

General method A for the synthesis of 2-mercaptopyrimidin-4-ones

To a solution of KOH (10.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) and thiourea (10.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq) in absolute EtOH (8 ml) was added the starting β-ketoester (10.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.). The resulting reaction mixture was heated at reflux for 3 hours. At reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 95/5), the solvent was removed by evaporation under reduced pressure, and the formed solid was redissolved in distilled water (10 ml). The resulting solution was then acidified to pH ≃ 4 with conc. HCl; the formed solid was then collected by filtration and washed with 3 ml of distilled water, affording the desired 2-mercaptopyrimidin-4-one.

General method B for the synthesis of 2-(methylthio)pyrimidin-4-ones

To a solution of NaOH (1.1 mmol; 1.10 Eq.) and 2-mercaptopyrimidin-4-one (1.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in water (3 ml) was added methyl iodide (1.2 mmol; 1.20 Eq.). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 hours. Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 95/5), the solid formed was collected by filtration and washed with 1 ml of distilled water, affording the desired 2-(methylthio)pyrimidin-4-one.

General method C for the synthesis of 2-hydrazineylpyrimidin-4-ones

To a suspension of 2-(methylthio)pyrimidin-4(3H)-one (10.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in n-butanol (2 ml) was added hydrazine monohydrate (45.0 mmol; 4.50 Eq.). The resulting solution was heated to reflux overnight. At reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10), the reaction mixture was cooled in ice bath and the solid formed was collected by filtration and washed with 2 ml of ice-cold n-butanol, affording the desired 2-hydrazineylpyrimidin-4-one.

General method D for the synthesis of 3-oxo-3-arylpropanenitriles

To an ice-cold solution of aryl ester (15.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in THF (25 ml) was added ACN (0.8 ml; 25.0 mmol; 1.67 Eq.) followed by NaH 60% in mineral oil (0.6 g, 16.5 mmol; 1.1 Eq.), and the resulting reaction mixture was heated to reflux overnight. Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 95/5), the reaction mixture was acidified to pH≃6 with conc. HCl. The formed solid was collected by filtration and washed with 10 ml of water, affording the desired 3-oxo-3-arylpropanenitrile.

General method E for the synthesis of 2’-(5-amino-3-aryl-pyrazol-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-ones

To a suspension of the hydrazine intermediate (1.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in MeOH (1 ml) was added the 3-oxo-3-propanenitrile intermediate (1.1 mmol; 1.10 Eq.) followed by TFA (1.0 mmol; 1 Eq.). The reaction mixture was placed under a nitrogen atmosphere and then microwave-irradiated at 120°C for 10 minutes (power setpoint 80 W). Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10), the precipitate formed was collected by filtration and washed with ice-cold methanol (0.5 ml). The solid was resuspended in saturated NaHCO3 solution (10 ml) and extracted with CHCl3 (3 × 5 ml). The organic phase was dried over Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed by evaporation under reduced pressure, affording the desired 2-(5-amino-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-one.

General method F for the synthesis of 2’-(5-amino-3-dialkylamino-pyrazol-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-ones

To a suspension of 2-carboxy-3,3-di(thiomethyl)propionitrile (2.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in MeOH (4 ml) cooled at 0 °C were added in the order triethylamine (2.2 mmol; 1.10 Eq.) and the appropriate dialkylamine (6.0 mmol; 1.50 Eq.). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours.

Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10 + 1% HCOOH), the appropriate hydrazine intermediate was added (3.6 mmol; 0.9 Eq.), and the reaction mixture was refluxed for 24 hours. Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10 + 1% HCOOH), the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the oily residue was triturated with diethyl ether (6 ml). The solid formed was collected by vacuum filtration and purified through flash column chromatography (eluant: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10), affording the desired 2-(5-amino-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)pyrimidin-4-one.

General method G for the amide coupling reactions with SOCl2

To a suspension of the acid intermediate (1.2 mmol; 1.20 Eq.) in DCM (5 ml) was added DMF (50 μl; catalytic amount) followed by SOCl2 (2.0 mmol; 2.00 Eq.) dropwise. The resulting reaction mixture was heated to reflux for 3 hours and, upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 95/5), the solvent was removed by evaporation under reduced pressure. The obtained oily residue was diluted with fresh DCM (1 ml) and slowly added to an ice-cold solution of the 5-aminopyrazole intermediate (1.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.), DMAP (10 mg; catalytic amount), and TEA (1.5 mmol) in 4 ml of DCM/THF (75/25). The resulting solution was stirred at room temperature overnight. Upon reaction completion (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 95/5), the organic solvents were removed by evaporation under reduced pressure, and the obtained residue was slowly poured into 20 ml of brine. The precipitate formed was then collected by filtration, purified through silica gel chromatography (Eluent: DCM/MeOH 99/1), and recrystallized from a dioxane-water mixture (70/30), affording the desired amide derivative.

General method H for the amide coupling reactions with HATU

To a solution of the 5-aminopyrazole intermediate (1.0 mmol; 1.00 Eq.) in dimethyl sulfoxide (5 ml) were added in the order: triethylamine (3.50 Eq.), the acid intermediate (1.10 Eq.), and HATU (1.20 Eq.). The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 3 hours (TLC: CHCl3/MeOH, 90/10 + 1% HCOOH). Upon reaction completion, the mixture was diluted in saturated aqueous ammonium chloride (20 ml x mmol) and the precipitate formed was then collected by filtration, purified through silica gel chromatography (Eluent: DCM/MeOH 99/1), and recrystallized from a dioxane-water mixture (70/30), affording the desired amide derivative.

Halide-sensitive YFP quenching assay

CFTR function measurement by halide-sensitive YFP quenching assay was performed as described48. Briefly, CFBE cells harboring the inducible expression of CFTR variants were generated to co-express the halide sensor YFP-F46L/H148Q/I152L52 by lentiviral transduction. During the assay, YFP-expressing CFBE cells were incubated at 32˚C in 50 µl per well phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-chloride (140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, 1.1 mM MgCl2, 0.7 mM CaCl2, and 5 mM glucose, pH 7.4) containing potentiator compounds. Well-by-well measurements were performed. CFTR was activated by 50 µl injection of activator solution [20 µM forskolin, 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methyl-xanthine (IBMX), 0.5 mM 8-(4-chlorophenylthio)-adenosine-3’,5’-cyclic monophosphate (cpt-cAMP)] in PBS-chloride at 0 seconds. By injecting 100 µl of PBS-iodide, in which NaCl was replaced with NaI, the quenching reaction was started at 60 seconds. With a 5-Hz data acquisition rate the YFP-fluorescence (485-nm excitation and 520-nm emission) was recorded between 57 and 93 seconds in a FLUOstar Omega (BMG Labtech) fluorescence plate reader. After background values were subtracted, the YFP signal was normalized to the fluorescence before NaI injection. To exclude the instantaneous quenching artifact upon NaI injection during the first second, the influx rates were calculated by linear fitting to the initial slope starting from 61 seconds.

Short-circuit current measurement

Short-circuit current measurement of polarized CFBE41o-48, 16HBE14o-53 and HNE9 has been described previously. Cells differentiated on The Snapwell filter were mounted in Ussing chambers (Physiologic Instruments) in Krebs-bicarbonate Ringer (KBR) buffer (140 mM Na+, 120 mM Cl−, 5.2 mM K+, 25 mM HCO3−, 2.4 mM HPO42-, 0.4 mM H2PO4-, 1.2 mM Ca2+, 1.2 mM Mg2+, 5 mM glucose, pH 7.4), which was mixed by continuous gas lift with carbogen (95% O2 and 5% CO2). CFBE41o-, 16HBE14o-, and HNE were measured in the presence of a basolateral-to-apical chloride gradient, generated by replacing NaCl with Na+ gluconate in the apical buffer. For CFBE epithelia, the basolateral membrane was permeabilized with 100 μM amphotericin B. After compensating for voltage offsets, the transepithelial voltage was clamped at 0 mV and measurements were recorded at 37°C in the presence of 100 μM amiloride with the Acquire and Analyze package (Physiologic Instruments). To estimate the open probability of the triple potentiated G551D-CFTR channels, we used the equation: G551D Isc (%WT))/G551D PM density (%WT) x WT open probability. This method, allowing to estimate the open probability from macroscopic measurements, has been validated before18,22. The single-channel open probability of WT-CFTR has been estimated at ~0.43 for channels reconstituted in lipid bilayers22 and ~0.38 in excised patches23.

Excised inside-out patch recording

The patch pipette solution contained 138 mM NMDG, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM PIPES, pH 7.4 with HCl. The bath solution contained 138 mM NMDG, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM PIPES, 0.5 mM EGTA, pH 7.1 with HCl. Following excision into the inside-out configuration patches (each from a different cell to enhance biological variability) were moved into a flow chamber in which the composition of the continuously flowing bath solution could be exchanged using electronic valves (ALA-VM8, Ala Scientific Instruments). CFTR channel gating was studied at 25°C in the continuous presence of 2 mM MgATP (A9187-1G, Sigma) plus 300 nM bovine PKA catalytic subunit (P2645, Sigma), first in the absence of drugs, and then following sequential addition of 50 nM VX-770 (Selleck Chemicals), 1 µM VX-445 (Selleck Chemicals), and 1 µM apigenin (MedChemExpress) in the same patch. To facilitate estimation of the number of active channels in the patch, at the end of each recording channels were maximally stimulated by exposure to a channel counting cocktail, containing 50 µM 2’-deoxy-N6-(2-phenylethyl)adenosine-5’-O-triphosphate (P-dATP, Biolog LSI), PKA and all three drugs. Currents were recorded at -60/-80 mV membrane potential and were amplified and low-pass filtered at 2 kHz (Axopatch 200B, Molecular Devices), digitized at a sampling rate of 10 kHz (Digidata 1550B, Molecular Devices) and recorded to disk (Pclamp 11, Molecular Devices).

Estimation of open probability from inside-out patch recordings

Maximal open probability (Po;max) in the presence of the channel counting cocktail (CCC) was estimated in patches containing small numbers of channels (N < 7). Segments of record in the presence of CCC were Gaussian filtered at 50 Hz, idealized by half-amplitude threshold crossing, and Po;max calculated as (Σklktk)/(NT), where lk and tk denote the conductance level and duration, respectively, of the kth event, and T = Σktk. Po under various test conditions (Po;test) was obtained as Po;max*(Itest/Imax), where Itest and Imax are the mean currents in the test segment and in the presence of CCC, respectively, obtained in the same patch. For test segments with extremely low Po (e.g., in ATP + PKA), in which Itest is significantly affected by baseline variance, events lists were obtained by half-amplitude threshold crossing, and Itest was replaced by i*(Σklktk)/T, where i is the unitary current amplitude.

Pharmacokinetic profile: Pharmacokinetic profile for ML-014 after IV or OS administration were determined as previously reported57. Results are reported in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, statistical analysis was performed by one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey HSD Test with the means of at least three independent experiments and the 95% confidence level was considered significant. Functional measurements in HNE were analyzed by paired two-tailed Student’s t-test followed by Bonferroni FDR-correction. Dose-response plots were fitted with the pharmacological dose-response equation y = A1 + (A2-A1)/(1 + 10^((LOGx0-x)*p)) using OriginPro 2015. Clustering was performed with the Heatmapper software (http://www2.heatmapper.ca/)58, using the average linkage method and distance calculation by Spearman’s rank correlation.
