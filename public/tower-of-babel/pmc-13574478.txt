Serum magnesium and depressive symptoms among U.S. adults
Authors: Dongze Wang, Xiaohan Wang, Wenyuan Wang, Yanyong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnesium participates in neurotransmission, stress regulation, and inflammatory signaling, but epidemiological evidence linking circulating magnesium to depression is inconsistent. Serum magnesium remains clinically accessible and objectively measured, yet recent nationally representative evidence is limited. We analyzed 4061 adults aged ≥20 years from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey August 2021 to August 2023. Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms were defined as Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score ≥10. Serum magnesium was modeled as survey-weighted quartiles and per 1-standard-deviation increase. Analyses used the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey phlebotomy weight (WTPH2YR), strata, and primary sampling units. Survey-weighted logistic regression, 3-knot restricted cubic splines, sex-stratified analyses, formal interaction testing, and sensitivity analyses were performed. Serum magnesium was not independently associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms in the overall U.S. adult population. A male-specific inverse signal was observed but was not supported by a statistically significant sex interaction and should be considered exploratory. Serum magnesium did not emerge as a sensitive population-level indicator of depressive symptom status. The weighted prevalence of PHQ-9 ≥10 was 11.7%. In fully adjusted models, serum magnesium was not associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms. Compared with Q1, odds ratios (ORs) for Q2 to Q4 were 0.82 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.59–1.14), 0.90 (0.67–1.20), and 0.92 (0.59–1.44), respectively. The OR per 1-standard deviation increase was 0.92 (0.83–1.03; P = .134). Overall restricted cubic spline tests were nonsignificant (P-overall = .273; P-nonlinear = .679). An inverse association was observed in men (OR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.64–0.96; P = .021) but not women (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.91–1.19; P = .544); however, the magnesium-by-sex interaction was not significant (P-interaction = .113). Findings were unchanged using PHQ-9 ≥5, excluding estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, adjusting for dietary-magnesium, or additionally adjusting for serum calcium and 25-hydroxyvitamin D.

1. Introduction

Depression is a leading contributor to disability and disease burden worldwide. Magnesium, an essential divalent cation, is biologically plausible in mood regulation because it participates in neurotransmission, neuroimmune signaling, and stress-response pathways.[1–3]

At the neurobiological level, magnesium acts as a voltage-dependent blocker of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor and thereby modulates glutamatergic signaling.[4,5] Experimental work also links magnesium availability to hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, inflammatory signaling, synaptic plasticity, and neurotrophic pathways.[2,3,6] These mechanisms have motivated observational studies and supplementation trials, but their findings remain heterogeneous. Higher dietary-magnesium intake has been associated with fewer depressive symptoms in some studies, and meta-analyses of randomized trials suggest possible symptomatic benefit from supplementation in selected populations; however, sample sizes, exposure definitions, and clinical contexts vary substantially.[1,7,8]

Serum magnesium is an imperfect surrogate for total-body or brain magnesium because circulating concentrations are tightly regulated and represent only a small fraction of body stores.[9] Nevertheless, serum magnesium remains worth evaluating epidemiologically because it is objective, standardized, widely available in clinical practice, and routinely used to identify overt dysmagnesemia. Establishing whether this readily available measure tracks depressive symptom burden therefore has direct relevance to its potential clinical and epidemiological utility.

The August 2021 to August 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycle is also informative from a contemporary nutritional perspective. An National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) analysis of the same cycle showed that U.S. adults obtained a mean of 53.0% of total energy from ultra-processed foods. Although this proportion was lower than in 2017 to 2018, ultra-processed foods still accounted for the majority of adult caloric intake, underscoring a persistently highly processed dietary environment during the late-pandemic and recovery period.[10]

Using nationally representative NHANES August 2021–August 2023 data, we therefore aimed to evaluate the association between serum magnesium and moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms; assess potential nonlinear dose–response patterns using 3-knot restricted cubic spline (RCS) models; examine sex-specific associations and formal effect modification by sex; and evaluate the robustness of the overall association across renal-function, dietary-magnesium, calcium/vitamin D, and alternative depression-threshold sensitivity analyses.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study used public-use data from NHANES August 2021 to August 2023, conducted by the NCHS to characterize the health and nutritional status of the civilian, noninstitutionalized U.S. population. NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design and combines household interviews with standardized examinations and laboratory testing.[11]

The NCHS Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES 2021 to 2022 protocol (Protocol #2021-05), and written informed consent was obtained from participants according to NCHS procedures.[12] Because this study was a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available NHANES data, additional ethics committee or institutional review board approval was not required.

2.2. Study population

Among 11,933 NHANES participants, 7809 were aged ≥20 years. The lower age limit was set at 20 years because the adult education and public-use marital status variables included in the adjusted models are available for this age range. Sequential exclusions were applied for unavailable serum magnesium or nonpositive phlebotomy weight, incomplete Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) data, and missing covariates required for the primary complete-case model. The final primary analytic sample comprised 4061 adults. The detailed selection process is shown in Figure 1. No a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a secondary analysis of a fixed, publicly available NHANES cycle; the final sample size was determined by prespecified eligibility criteria and availability of required variables. Missingness was handled through complete-case exclusion for the primary model, and the numbers excluded for unavailable serum magnesium, nonpositive phlebotomy weight, incomplete PHQ-9 data, and missing covariates are shown in Figure 1.

2.3. Exposure: serum magnesium

Serum magnesium (LBXMAGN) was measured in the standard biochemistry profile using the Roche Cobas 8000 platform at the University of Minnesota Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory.[13,14] The assay is based on the reaction of magnesium with xylidyl blue in an alkaline solution containing EGTA to mask calcium; the resulting complex is quantified photometrically.[14] Concentrations were reported in mg/dL.

Magnesium was parameterized in 2 primary ways. First, participants were classified according to survey-weighted quartiles, with cut points at 1.9, 2.0, and 2.1 mg/dL and Q1 as the reference category. Second, magnesium was modeled continuously per 1 survey-weighted standard deviation (SD) (0.1718 mg/dL).

2.4. Outcome: depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the 9-item PHQ-9, with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. The primary outcome was moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms, defined as PHQ-9 ≥ 10. An alternative threshold of PHQ-9 ≥ 5, representing at least mild depressive symptoms, was evaluated in sensitivity analysis.

2.5. Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori on the basis of prior literature and plausible relationships with magnesium status or depressive symptoms. Sociodemographic covariates included age (continuous), sex, race/ethnicity, education (<9th grade, 9–11th grade, high school/GED, some college/associate degree, or college graduate and above), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, or never married), and poverty-income ratio (PIR) (continuous). Lifestyle covariates included smoking status (never, former, current), lifetime alcohol use (yes/no), and meeting a leisure-time physical-activity threshold of ≥600 MET-min/week. Body mass index (BMI) was included as a continuous clinical covariate. Diabetes was defined from self-reported physician diagnosis and/or current glucose-lowering medication use; hypertension was defined from self-reported physician diagnosis and/or current antihypertensive medication use. These definitions reflect the questionnaire and medication data available in the analytic files and did not incorporate unmeasured fasting glucose, HbA1c, or examination blood-pressure criteria.

2.6. Statistical analysis

2.6.1. Survey design specification and statistical inference

All analyses incorporated the NHANES complex survey design using the survey package in R. Because serum magnesium is a blood-analyte, the August 2021 to August 2023 phlebotomy weight (WTPH2YR) was used for primary blood-analyte analyses, together with SDMVSTRA and SDMVPSU.[13] The primary analytic sample retained 15 strata and 30 primary sampling unit (PSUs), with no strata containing a single PSU. The option survey.lonely.psu=“adjust” was specified as a general variance-estimation safeguard for restricted/domain analyses, although it was not invoked for lonely PSUs in the primary sample. The primary design had 15 design degrees of freedom.

Coefficient-level inference used design-based Wald t statistics and 95% confidence interval (CIs) based on the survey design degrees of freedom; multi-parameter terms, including RCS and interaction terms, were evaluated with design-based Wald F tests. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < .05. To contextualize precision rather than relying on a post hoc power calculation, we also estimated the survey design effect for the weighted prevalence of PHQ-9 ≥ 10 and an approximate effective sample size.

2.6.2. Main regression models

Survey-weighted logistic regression estimated odds ratio (ORs) and 95% CIs for PHQ-9 ≥ 10 according to magnesium quartiles and per 1-SD increase. Fully adjusted models included age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, BMI, PIR, smoking status, lifetime alcohol use, physical-activity, diabetes, and hypertension.

2.6.3. Restricted cubic spline analysis

Potential nonlinear associations were evaluated using a true 3-knot RCS parameterization with survey-weighted knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of serum magnesium. In the overall sample these corresponded to 1.8, 2.0, and 2.2 mg/dL, with 2.0 mg/dL as the reference. The overall magnesium term (P-overall) and departure from linearity (P-nonlinear) were evaluated separately. Sex-specific RCS models were also fitted.

2.6.4. Sex-specific and subgroup analyses

Sex-stratified logistic models estimated the association per 1-SD increase in magnesium in women and men, and a magnesium-by-sex interaction term formally tested effect modification. Additional exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted by age (20–49, 50–64, ≥65 years), BMI (<25, 25–29.9, ≥30 kg/m2), PIR (<1.3, 1.3–3.49, ≥3.5), and smoking status. Formal interaction tests were used to assess heterogeneity across each subgroup variable.

2.6.5. Sensitivity analyses

Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed: redefining the outcome as PHQ-9 ≥ 5; (2) restricting the analysis to participants with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m2, with eGFR calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation; and additionally adjusting for Day-1 dietary-magnesium intake (DR1TMAGN), using the Day-1 dietary recall weight (WTDRD1). For the dietary model, an alternate analysis using WTPH2YR was also examined to assess weight-related robustness. In direct response to the reviewer, a fourth sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for serum total calcium and total 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) because of their metabolic relationships with magnesium and vitamin D biology.[15] Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2.

3.2. Primary association between serum magnesium and PHQ-9 ≥ 10

In fully adjusted models, serum magnesium was not significantly associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms (Table 2 and Figure 2). Compared with Q1, the ORs were 0.82 (95% CI, 0.59–1.14; P = .221) for Q2, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.67–1.20; P = .451) for Q3, and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.59–1.44; P = .713) for Q4. When modeled continuously, each 1-SD increase in serum magnesium was associated with an OR of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.83–1.03; P = .134). Thus, the modest unadjusted difference in mean magnesium shown in Table 1 did not translate into a statistically significant independent association after multivariable adjustment.

3.4. Sex-specific associations and interaction tests

Sex-specific analyses revealed a pattern that differed from the overall null estimate. Among women, serum magnesium was not associated with PHQ-9 ≥ 10 (OR per 1-SD increase, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.91–1.19; P = .544). Among men, higher serum magnesium was associated with lower odds of PHQ-9 ≥ 10 (OR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.64–0.96; P = .021) (Fig. 4). Consistently, the male RCS model had a significant overall magnesium term (P-overall = .0417) without evidence of nonlinearity (P-nonlinear = .527), whereas the female RCS model was nonsignificant (P-overall = .818; P-nonlinear = .859) (Fig. 5).

Importantly, the formal magnesium-by-sex interaction did not reach statistical significance (P-interaction = .113). Accordingly, the male-specific inverse association was treated as an exploratory sex-stratified signal rather than evidence of established effect modification by sex.

3.5. Sensitivity analyses

The overall conclusion was stable across sensitivity analyses (Table 3 and Figure 6). Using PHQ-9 ≥ 5 produced a per-1-SD OR of 1.00 (95% CI, 0.89–1.11; P = .939). Restricting to eGFR ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 yielded an OR of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.82–1.03; P = .128). Additional adjustment for Day-1 dietary-magnesium using WTDRD1 yielded an OR of 0.96 (95% CI, 0.84–1.10; P = .522); the alternate WTPH2YR-weighted dietary model was similar (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.84–1.08; P = .421). In the reviewer-requested model additionally adjusting for serum calcium and total 25(OH)D, the per-1-SD OR was 0.93 (95% CI, 0.83–1.04; P = .175), closely matching the primary estimate of 0.92.

4. Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, serum magnesium was not independently associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms in the overall population after accounting for the NHANES complex survey design and a broad set of sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates. The null overall result was supported by quartile, continuous, and RCS models and remained stable under alternative outcome definitions, renal-function restriction, dietary-magnesium adjustment, and additional adjustment for calcium and 25(OH)D. The analysis did identify an inverse association among men, but the formal sex interaction was not statistically significant; this signal should therefore be interpreted cautiously and requires replication.

4.1. Comparison with previous literature

Previous observational evidence has been inconsistent. A meta-analysis reported lower serum magnesium concentrations among patients with depression, and a primary-care study also described an association between serum magnesium and depressive symptoms.[16,17] Dietary studies have likewise suggested an inverse association between magnesium intake and depression in some settings.[18] Conversely, prospective dietary evidence has not uniformly supported a protective association.[19] Differences in population characteristics, depression definitions, magnesium exposure measures, and confounding structures may account for part of this heterogeneity.

Our data add an important distinction between descriptive and adjusted associations. Participants with PHQ-9 ≥ 10 had modestly lower mean serum magnesium in the weighted descriptive comparison, but the association attenuated after multivariable adjustment. This suggests that a crude between-group difference in serum magnesium does not necessarily represent an independent relationship with depressive symptom status. The finding is also compatible with the possibility that dietary-magnesium, supplementation, serum magnesium, and intracellular magnesium capture partly different biological and behavioral constructs.

4.2. Why serum magnesium can yield a null population association

4.2.1. Serum versus intracellular magnesium pools

Serum magnesium represents only a small extracellular fraction of total-body magnesium and is maintained within a narrow range by gastrointestinal absorption, renal handling, and exchange with bone and soft tissues.[9,20–22] Consequently, normal serum concentrations can coexist with variation in intracellular magnesium status, which may attenuate epidemiological associations with neuropsychiatric outcomes.

Red blood cell (RBC) magnesium provides an intracellular measure and has therefore been proposed as a complementary biomarker of longer-term magnesium status. However, the available evidence does not establish RBC magnesium as a universal gold standard. A systematic review concluded that assessment of magnesium status remains difficult and that several biomarkers: including serum/plasma, RBC, and urinary magnesium: can provide complementary information.[23] In a large French adult cohort, serum and red-cell magnesium were positively but only modestly correlated (r ≈ 0.17), illustrating that the 2 measures capture overlapping but nonidentical biological information.[24] Future depression studies that pair serum with RBC or ionized magnesium could therefore help determine whether intracellular measures reveal associations that routine serum testing misses.

4.2.2. Clinical value of serum measurement despite biomarker limitations

The limitations of serum magnesium do not make it clinically irrelevant. Serum measurement is standardized, widely available, inexpensive relative to specialized intracellular assays, and useful for detecting overt hypo- or hypermagnesemia.[9] The present results instead narrow its interpretation: a single serum magnesium concentration did not function as a sensitive population-level indicator of depressive symptom burden after multivariable adjustment.

4.2.3. Micronutrient interactions and residual confounding

Magnesium participates in vitamin D metabolism and interacts with calcium-related physiology.[15] In the reviewer-requested sensitivity analysis, adding serum calcium and total 25(OH)D changed the per-1-SD estimate only minimally (OR 0.92 in the primary model vs 0.93 after additional adjustment). Thus, these 2 micronutrients did not appear to materially confound the overall association in this dataset. Residual confounding nevertheless remains possible from inflammatory status, chronic stress, medication exposure (including diuretics and proton-pump inhibitors), supplement use, dietary quality, and other factors not fully captured in the analytic files.

4.3. Sex-specific findings

Sex differences in magnesium intake, regulation, hormonal milieu, and stress/inflammatory responses have been discussed in prior literature.[25,26] In the present study, the male per-1-SD estimate was inverse and statistically significant, and the male RCS overall test was significant without evidence of nonlinearity. These internally consistent results raise the possibility of a predominantly linear inverse association in men. However, the formal magnesium-by-sex interaction was not significant (P = .113), and statistical significance in one stratum but not another is not itself evidence that the stratum-specific effects differ. The sex-specific finding should therefore be considered exploratory rather than a confirmed biological sex difference. Replication in larger datasets with repeated and intracellular magnesium measurements would be needed before drawing sex-specific clinical conclusions.

4.4. Contemporary dietary and late-pandemic context

The August 2021 to August 2023 cycle captures a period in which U.S. dietary and psychosocial conditions were still evolving after major pandemic-related disruptions. Notably, adults obtained 53.0% of total energy from ultra-processed foods in this cycle; although this was lower than in 2017 to 2018, it still represented the majority of caloric intake.[10] This context supports the contemporary relevance of examining nutritional biomarkers but does not justify attributing the null association to pandemic-related exposures. A more conservative interpretation is that, even during a period marked by substantial psychosocial and behavioral change, serum magnesium did not emerge as a sensitive indicator of depressive symptom status in the overall population.

4.5. Strengths

Key strengths include the nationally representative sampling framework; use of the NHANES phlebotomy-specific weight for a blood-analyte exposure; correction for the complex survey design; validated PHQ-9 assessment; objective laboratory measurement of serum magnesium; survey-weighted quartile and SD definitions; a true 3-knot RCS analysis; formal interaction testing; and multiple sensitivity analyses. The reviewer-requested calcium/25(OH)D model and the alternate weighting check for the dietary-magnesium model further support the stability of the overall conclusion.

4.6. Limitations and statistical precision

Several limitations require consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference; depressive symptoms could influence diet, health behavior, or supplementation as readily as magnesium status could influence mood. Second, a single serum measurement may not reflect chronic or intracellular magnesium exposure. Third, complete-case analysis may introduce selection bias if missingness is related to both magnesium and depressive symptoms. Fourth, residual confounding by medications, supplements, inflammatory markers, chronic stress, sleep, dietary patterns, and other psychosocial factors remains possible.

Fifth, the nominal sample size of 4061 should not be interpreted as equivalent to a simple random sample. The prevalence design effect was approximately 2.30, corresponding to an approximate effective sample size of 1764 for that estimate, and the primary design had 15 degrees of freedom. The study therefore had useful precision for excluding many moderate associations but cannot rule out small effects. Wide CIs at the extremes of the magnesium distribution and in some subgroups further limit inference. Finally, several sex-specific and subgroup analyses were examined; the isolated male and current-smoking findings should be interpreted in the context of multiple comparisons and nonsignificant formal interaction tests.

4.7. Summary

Overall, these results support a distinction between magnesium as a biologically plausible nutrient and serum magnesium as a population biomarker of depression-related status. The data do not demonstrate a robust independent association in the overall U.S. adult population. The male-specific inverse signal is hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. Future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated serum measurements, RBC or ionized magnesium, detailed supplementation and dietary data, and inflammatory or neuroendocrine biomarkers may better resolve whether specific magnesium compartments or population subgroups are relevant to depression. Given the nationally representative sampling framework, the overall findings are most applicable to the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized adult population represented by this NHANES cycle, and extrapolation to other populations should be made cautiously.

5. Conclusion

In U.S. adults participating in NHANES August 2021 to August 2023, serum magnesium was not independently associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms in the overall population, and no nonlinear dose–response relationship was detected. The overall result was robust to alternative depression thresholds, renal-function restriction, dietary-magnesium adjustment, and additional adjustment for serum calcium and 25(OH)D. An inverse association was observed among men, but the formal sex interaction was not statistically significant; this finding should be considered exploratory. These results do not support serum magnesium as a sensitive stand-alone population indicator of depressive symptom burden. Prospective studies incorporating intracellular or ionized magnesium and repeated measurements are warranted.
