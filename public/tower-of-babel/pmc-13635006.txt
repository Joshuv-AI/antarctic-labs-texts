Annual operational data for the 10 MWe ELLO Fresnel concentrated solar power plant
Authors: Valentine Grange, Fabien Roget, Valéry Vuillerme, Quentin Falcoz, Sylvain Rodat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article introduces a unique yearly dataset from the 10 MWe eLLO Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) plant, a commercial-scale Linear Fresnel Reflector (LFR) facility using Direct Steam Generation (DSG) and steam storage, located in Llo, France. The dataset, collected via a supervisory system, includes real-time operational data from 124 sensors distributed across the plant, categorized into seven types: meteorological, solar field (east/west), turbine/aero-condenser, feedwater, electricity production and storage system. Those data were collected under standard operational conditions at two different acquisition times:•a 24-hour snapshot (from July 16th, 2025, 04:00 to July 17th, 2025, 04:00) recorded with a 10-second interval.•a year-long variable data (from January 1st, 2025, 00:00 to January 1st, 2026, 00:00) with a 1-hour interval. Erroneous data were flagged as NaN, resulting in 338 usable days for 2025.The dataset is organized into three sheets:•“Description”: metadata (sensors IDs, variables, units, annotated schematics).•“24-hour period”: data for all variables over the 24-hour period described earlier.•“Year 2025″: data for all variables over the year 2025.This is the first open yearly dataset for a commercial LFR-DSG plant, enabling experimental validation of numerical models, simulations of part-load operation, thermal storage dynamics, and efficiency, as well as optimization of plant sizing, operational strategies, and techno-economic analyses. A reference case study for researchers, engineers and industrial stakeholders in a field where real-world data are scarce.Keywords: Concentration solar technology (CST), Linear fresnel reflector (LFR), Standard daily operations, Thermal energy storage (TES), Direct steam generation (DSG)

Background

Concentrated Solar Technologies (CST) are critical for advancing renewable energy solutions. However, a significant gap exists in the availability of comprehensive operational datasets for these systems. Existing datasets present notable limitations. For instance, Sergio et al [3] provide meteorological data and images only, while Soto et al [4] covers a few days of operational data at most. Kraft et al [5], though potentially comprehensive, is not publicly accessible due to confidentially restrictions. Some are limited to prototypes [6], partial measurements [7], or single-field studies (such as optics [8]). Consequently, these datasets primarily address simulations, short-term experiments, or specific technical aspects, rather than full-scale, long-term operational performance.

To address this gap, a comprehensive operational dataset was recovered from the eLLO plant, a commercial, first-of-a-kind Linear Fresnel Reflector (LFR) - Direct Steam Generation (DSG) plant. The dataset was collected under normal operational conditions, using standard monitoring and control systems. The plant features a unique 4-hour steam storage system.

Following COP30 in Brazil, which highlighted the urge to accelerate industrial decarbonization and renewable energy deployment [9], this dataset supports CST system design, performance assessment, and scalability studies. It enables reproducible models, techno-economic analyses, and optimization tools for new CSP/CST projects.

The solar field is oriented northwest/southeast, covering an area of 152,769 m², and is divided into two subfields: the east field (10 solar lines) and the west field (17 solar lines). Each subfield has a separator tank that directs steam to either the power block or storage (for the west field), while water is returned to the solar field.

The power block features a two-stage turbine with a nominal capacity of 10 MWe, equipped with an air-cooled condenser.

The storage system consists of 9 steam accumulators, each with a capacity of 120 m³. The steam is sourced from the west field, and the feedwater comes from the power block.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Location

The eLLO Concentrated Solar Power plant is located in Llo, Pyrénées-Orientales department, in southern France. Covering an area of approximately 33 hectares (see Fig. 4), the plant features a hilly topography as well as a thalweg and a 63 kV high-tension line (see Fig. 5). Its design is specifically adapted for this site [10] (for an exhaustive treatment, see [11]), which features a hostile environment with cold winters, strong winds, and storms.

b. Solar Field.

The solar field is oriented North-West/South-East due to the specific topography of the location, and divided into two subfields, with the thalweg and high-tension line being the dividing line between both solar fields. Altogether, there are 27 solar lines, 10 in the East solar field, and 17 in the West solar field. Each solar line is formed of 4 to 8 modules associated in series: the East field comprises a total of 54 modules, covering an area of 48,526.6 m², while the West field consists of 116 modules, totaling 104,242.4 m². One module is 67 m x 14 m, and consists of 140 primary reflectors, arranged in 10 longitudinal and 14 transversal spans. Each module is divided into two heliostats with 7 transversal spans, each equipped with its own sun tracking system, including an actuator, a transmission beam and a position encoder, allowing precise adjustment of the mirrors throughout the day.

The fluid exits the solar field at a nominal outlet temperature of 286 °C. The system is designed to operate at a working pressure of 70 barg.

i. Primary reflector and solar receiver.

Each primary reflector at the eLLO plant consists of four mirrors bonded under stress to the top plate of a supporting frame, creating a slightly curved reflective surface of about 6.42 m², which enables better concentration compared to flat reflectors.

Key parameters, such as the spacing between rows and primary reflectors, their dimensions, and shape, have been thoroughly investigated and optimized through existing research [12,13]. Moreover, the mirrors used in primary reflectors undergo specialized optical treatments: they are designed to maximize spectral reflectivity (0.94) while minimizing spectral absorptivity.

ii. Solar receiver.

The solar receiver is an assembly of components (see Fig. 6) positioned 8.5 m above the mirror’s axis. Its primary function is to intercept concentrated solar radiation and transfer thermal energy to the heat transfer fluid (water/steam).

Schematic of a single-tube receiver equipped with a CPC-type reflector. This schematic illustrates the design of a single-tube receiver with a Compound Parabolic Concentrator (CPC)-type reflector.

The solar receiver consists of a 1.4301 (AISI 304) stainless steel absorber tube with selective coating, which converts the radiative solar energy into thermal energy transferred to the heat transfer fluid. It also features a Compound Parabolic Concentrator (CPC-type) secondary reflector, which redirects incoming solar radiation into the absorber tube in order to maximize the collection of diffuse and misaligned solar rays. This overall system is protected by an enclosure and a protective glass in order to minimize thermal losses. In this configuration, the absorber tube is not surrounded by vacuum and the cavity is not insulated.

All surfaces involved in solar concentration are optically treated within the solar radiation wavelength range to minimize optical losses in the system (these optical treatments must withstand mechanical and thermal stresses over time):

•Secondary reflectors are designed to maximize spectral reflectivity (0.89) while minimizing spectral absorptivity.

Secondary reflectors are designed to maximize spectral reflectivity (0.89) while minimizing spectral absorptivity.

•Protective glass is designed for high spectral transmittance (ranging from 0 to 0.952 depending on the angle of incidence), and both low spectral reflectivity (ranging from 0 to 0.524 depending on the angle of incidence) and absorptivity. Furthermore, the material used is transparent to solar radiation, but reflective to infrared radiation emitted by the absorber tube (creates a greenhouse effect inside the receiver).

iii. Solar tracking system.

The solar tracking system orients the primary reflectors to effectively concentrate solar radiation onto the receiver, based on the sun’s position. In the linear concentrating system, such as linear Fresnel modules, tracking is performed following a single axis, aligned parallel to the receiver. The rows of primary reflectors are mechanically linked along the rotational axis. The optimal angle for each reflector row is calculated based on the sun’s position (computed in real time by an automated system using specialized algorithms [[14], [15], [16]]) and the row’s relative location to the target (solar receiver).

c. Direct steam generation (DSG).

i. DSG in linear concentrating systems.

Unlike systems that rely on thermal oils or molten salts as heat transfer fluids, DSG uses water/steam directly within the absorber tubes, simplifying the thermal cycle and reducing operational costs. It offers several advantages, such as eliminating the need for expensive heat transfer fluids and separate steam generator, thus reducing capital and operational costs and exploiting a non-toxic and non-flammable environmentally friendly working fluid.

However, DSG also presents challenges, such as two-phase heat transfer within the absorber tube, which complicates thermal management, but also less efficient internal convection in the gas phase, reducing overall heat transfer performance. Additionally, large-scale thermal storage remains difficult to achieve due to the high volume of steam required and the technical constraints of storing it efficiently.

ii. Flow configuration.

The solar fields flow configuration relies on a recirculation architecture, including a steam tank and pumps, to ensure operational continuity and safety in case of a malfunction. The liquid-vapor mixture produced in the solar field, which should reach 80% steam quality, is separated within the steam tank, with the steam routed either to the power block through a superheater, or to the thermal storage system, depending on the pressure differential and the operational strategy, while the liquid water is pumped back into the solar lines by the recirculation pump (see Fig. 7).

d. Storage.

While various thermal storage technologies have been explored for CST plants with DSG, steam accumulators stand out due to their rapid-response times and high discharge rates, making them ideal for mitigating the intermittency of solar resources. Steam accumulators store sensible energy in pressurized water: during periods of excess steam production, surplus steam is injected into the liquid phase of the accumulator (see Fig. 8). As the steam injected condenses, it heats the pressurized water to saturation temperature. To maintain the operating pressure, the accumulator’s pressure is controlled via steam injection or extraction, and the water level is regulated by adding or removing liquid. During periods of insufficient steam production, the steam accumulator’s pressure drops, causing flash evaporation of the stored pressurized water, thus releasing saturated steam.

Steam accumulator. This schematic shows a simplified schematic of the steam accumulator showing the inlet and outlet connections for water and steam flow.

This plant’s storage system uses 9 steam accumulators of 120 m³ each, arranged in parallel. This type of storage allows storing surplus steam, with a total capacity of approximately 4 h of full-power operation. The operational strategy depends on the weather: on cloudy days, the storage serves as a buffer, and on sunny days, it acts as isolated storage, used once the solar lines can no longer produce steam.

e. Power Block.

The power block operates using a Hirn thermodynamic cycle, a variant of the Rankine cycle. It includes a thermodynamic superheater, a turbo-generator unit, an air-cooled condenser, and several water tanks and heaters.

The turbine consists of two stages, each featuring a dedicated heat exchanger: the superheater (for steam from the solar field) and the reheater (for steam from the first turbine stage). Before entering the turbine, the steam generated in the solar field is superheated. To superheat the steam exiting the first turbine stage, an expansion valve reduces its pressure to 40.5 bar (251 °C), lowering its saturation temperature. This steam is then further heated as it passes through the superheater, which is fed by a portion of the high-pressure saturated steam from the solar field (70 bar, 286 °C). Additionally, the reheater superheats a portion of the steam extracted from the first turbine stage (R1 extraction) to provide the second stage with superheated steam.

The steam expands through the turbine’s multi-stage, driving a 10 MWe turbo-generator unit coupled to an 11 kV alternator to produce electricity. The site self-consumption can be determined by taking the net power production, removing the electricity sent to the grid, and adding the electricity drawn from the grid, using the data provided in the dataset. This gives the actual electricity used on-site.

Condensate is then cooled in the condenser and recirculated through feedwater heaters and pumps, completing the closed cycle. The cycle includes extractions at intermediate turbine stages, used to heat the condensate and facilitate the removal of non-condensable gases.

Acquisition procedures

The dataset was collected from the eLLO concentrated solar power plant, instrumented with multiple sensors, distributed across the solar field, storage system, and power block. The acquisition system is centralized, with all sensors connected to a supervisory data acquisition system, ensuring synchronized logging and remote monitoring. All sensor data are logged by a single, centralized datalogger equipped with a GPS-synchronized clock, ensuring uniform timestamp alignment across the entire site. The datalogger records data at a 1-second resolution, while transmitters apply a 3-second rolling average (damping) to smooth signals for control loops. As such, no inter-logger synchronization is required, and cross-variable timestamp alignment is inherently trustworthy.

a. Sensors and Instrumentation.

The sensors and transmitters used in this study are grouped by the type of process variable they measure or transmit, including temperature, pressure, flow, heliostat focusing, meteorological data, tank levels, and electrical power.

Temperature measurements are performed using the Rosemount 148 Temperature Transmitter (model 148HNNAN0Q4XA), a versatile device designed to accept multiple sensors inputs to improve measurement accuracy (with an uncertainty of ±0.3 °C). The 148 series features a 4–20 mA output signal and ambient temperature compensation to ensure reliable performance in diverse environments [17]. In this setup, the transmitter is linked to different temperature sensors (e.g.: 0065J05F0110N0405A1XA) and thermowells (e.g.: EB486D00170100K5N01X) in order to adapt specific conditions such as temperature range and pressure.

For pressure monitoring, Rosemount 2088 Gauge and Absolute Pressure Transmitters (models 2088G3S22A2D4B4Q4Q8S5 and 2088G4S22A2S1D4B4Q4Q8) are used. These transmitters use advanced piezoresistive sensor technology, providing a total accuracy of ±0.075% of span, including combined errors from reference accuracy, ambient temperature effects, and line pressure effects [18]. In this setup, the 2088G3S22A2D4B4Q4Q8S5 is used in most locations, while the 2088G4S22A2S1D4B4Q4Q8 is specifically installed in the storage tank system and the west steam tank.

Flow measurements are conducted with the Rosemount 2051CD Coplanar Pressure Transmitter (model 2051CDCD3A02A1BS5D4V5Q4Q8), part of the 2051 series. It is engineered with patented coplanar technology, enabling direct mounting for flow measurements and providing high accuracy (reference accuracy of ±0.065% of span) and flexibility for a wide range of conditions [19]. The 2051CD is suited for differential pressure flow applications.

Meteorological data are collected using a suite of sensors strategically placed across the plant. It includes measuring Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI), wind speed (0° means south to north, 90° means west to east), ambient temperature and wind directions at various locations. For the identification of usable periods, a strict criterion was enforced: time steps containing any NaN values were excluded from the analysis to determine the usability ratio. This resulted in 338 usable days for the year 2025. However, the published dataset retains all time steps, including those with NaN values, to provide a complete and continuous record of the entire year (see Fig. 9).

Acquisition data process flowchart. This flowchart shows the process that was used to collect the data.

The anomaly detection process relied on automated flagging performed by the plant’s acquisition system. Only values already identified as NaN (due to system crashes or missing measurements) were retained in their original form. No manual verification or modifications were applied, ensuring that all measurement, including potentially erroneous values, were preserved to maintain internal consistency. Users are advised to assess data coherence for their specific analysis periods, as physically implausible values, such as −15 °C for the “West-field recirculation pump discharge water temperature” parameter, remain unchanged in the dataset. Additionally, small negative values, such as those observed in “Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI)”, may represent sensor noise and could be treated as zero for analytical purposes, if deemed appropriate.

Operating strategy

The operating conditions reflect the standard daily plant operation. The primary goal is to supply electricity to the grid, with a nominal electrical power target of 9.9MWe. Once this objective is met, the remaining thermal energy will be used to charge the thermal storage system.

All operating setpoints are determined by algorithms, but under continuous operator supervision to ensure safe and reliable plant operation.

The initial phase of an operating day would be the recirculation within the solar field. The fluid will be circulated through the solar field in order to progressively heat the system until the temperature required for power generation is reached.

On-site self-electricity consumption is preferred whenever possible.

The mass flow rate in each solar line is individually regulated as a function of day, time and number of modules. This regulation is achieved through valve opening control to maintain a target steam quality of 0.8 at the outlet and to preserve constant mass in the separator tank of each solar field. The steam quality at the solar field outlet cannot be directly measured and is therefore not included in the published dataset. However, it can be estimated as the ratio between the steam flow from the separator tank and the recirculation flow entering the solar field, using the published variables.

When the thermal storage system is fully charged and the turbine is operating at nominal load, solar modules will be defocused to limit steam production and to ensure stable operating conditions.

Limitations

The dataset’s main limitation relates to both data availability and data quality. Due to a bug in the central supervision system, some data were not recorded, and entire time periods are missing from the dataset. Additionally, measurement noise and potential issues arising from defective sensors further affect reliability.

Moreover, the dataset lacks critical maintenance records, such as the dates of mirror cleaning operations, details about missing protective glass on the receptor, or documented damage to some mirrors. These gaps introduce uncertainties in interpreting long-term trends and may impact the accuracy of analyses dependent on continuous or high-fidelity data. These limitations have to be taken into account when interpreting these “real world” data.
