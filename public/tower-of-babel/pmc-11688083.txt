A single epidural administration of butorphanol combined with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia relieves postpartum contraction pain after cesarean section: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Ling Qiu, Xu-Hui Chen, Jia Fu, Lu Hua, Di Fan, Ying-Ying Zhang, Xiao-Bin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Numerous studies have shown that butorphanol exerts an inhibitory effect on visceral pain. The aim of this study was to observe the effects of a single epidural administration of butorphanol combined with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia on postpartum uterine contraction pain after cesarean section.Methods:We randomly allotted 121 women undergoing cesarean section surgery procedure to 3 groups: 31 cases were excluded; and 90 cases were divided into group S (blank control, n = 30), group I (intravenous butorphanol, n = 30), and group E (butorphanol administered into the epidural cavity, n = 30). We then observed changes in the indices of uterine contraction pain and intraoperative stretch pain for the 3 groups.Results:(1) In terms of the uterine contraction pain, the visual analog scale scores of group I were lower than those for group S at 10 minutes (P = .001), 20 minutes (P < .001), 30 minutes (P < .001), 1 hour (P < .001), 2 hours (P < .001), and 4 hours (P < .001) after the epidural administration. The visual analog scale scores of group E were lower than those of group I at 10 minutes (P < .001), 20 minutes (P < .001), 30 minutes (P < .001), 1 hour (P < .001), 2 hours (P < .001), 4 hours (P < .001), and 6 hours (P < .001) after the administration. (2) In terms of intraoperative traction reactions, sedation, and comfort during the operation, group E was superior to groups S and I at any time points (all P < .043).Conclusions:Butorphanol single epidural administration combined with intravenous patient-controlled analgesia can effectively reduce the pain of uterine contraction and intraoperative traction reaction and improve perioperative comfort of the parturient.

1. Introduction

Postpartum uterine contraction pain is post-paroxysmal pain after delivery and is generally located in the lower abdomen. Unlike the body pain caused by the cesarean incision,[1] the uterine contraction pain is a visceral pain. The pain is located deep in the body, hard to pinpoint accurately, is intermittent, and paroxysmal. It is often manifested as abdominal colic in the suprapubic area. Uterine contraction pain is related to the use of postpartum oxytocin, postpartum breast-feeding, delivery times, and other factors. However, the specific underlying mechanisms for this phenomenon remain unclear.[2,3] Strong uterine contractions increase maternal discomfort and also affect maternal postoperative recovery as a consequence of pain, refusal to breast-feed early,[4] and increased maternal risk of postoperative depression.[5–7] With improvements in anesthetic techniques, however, the pain of incision related to a cesarean section (C-section) delivery has become increasingly better controlled. Nevertheless, there is still dissatisfaction with the postoperative analgesia as the parturient’s uterine contraction pains have yet to be reduced or completely alleviated. Therefore, it is essential to relieve the visceral pain caused by uterine contractions. At present, the clinical treatment of postpartum uterine contraction pain can be divided into 2 categories: drug treatment and nondrug treatment.

Drug treatment primarily comprises opioid analgesics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and the application of traditional Chinese medications.[8–10] While many opioid drugs used for analgesia after C-section are delivered via intrathecal or intravenous routes (e.g., morphine, fentanyl, and sufentanil, which can maintain good analgesic effects for lengthy periods), adverse reactions such as itching, nausea, vomiting, urinary retention, and respiratory depression often occur.[11,12] Abbud et al found that a relatively long duration of butorphanol delivery was needed to achieve analgesia, and a faster onset of action of morphine was noted compared with a single epidural dose[13]; these actions may be related to dosage. Therefore, morphine used alone is rather risky and its current use rate is low.[14,15] The major adverse drug reactions of NSAIDs were gastrointestinal ulcers, bleeding, perforation, renal perfusion, and decreased glomerular filtration rate.[16–18]

The nondrug treatment methods include acupuncture points, local hot compresses, massage, physical therapy, and psychological intervention.[19] Although Chinese studies have proven that the aforementioned modalities generated certain effects on uterine contraction pain, these methods and drugs did not satisfactorily alleviate uterine contraction pain.

Numerous studies have shown that butorphanol exerts an inhibitory effect on visceral pain.[20] For example, Simonin et al demonstrated that epidural injection of butorphanol prevented traction in patients undergoing total hysterectomy.[21,22] In other studies, investigators inhibited the expression of the κ-receptor after deleting the κ-receptor gene in rats, and by analyzing the stimulatory response of these mutated rats to various kinds of pain, they then demonstrated that butorphanol use was still associated with visceral pain.[23–25] Studies by Miller et al showed that butorphanol sensitivity was greater in females, and showed fewer adverse reactions.[26,27] Therefore, butorphanol appears to be more effective in relieving postoperative pain in women. Hence, the present experiment was conducted to observe the effect of epidural butorphanol on postpartum uterine contraction pain after C-section. We aimed to observe the effects of a single epidural administration of butorphanol combined with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) on postpartum uterine contraction pain after C-section, which may accelerate postoperative recovery, reduce the risk of postoperative depression, and improve overall patient satisfaction.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

For the study, we included women who were primipara or multipara of single birth and had undergone C-section delivery under anesthesia between 2017 and 2018 in this randomized double-blind prospective study, at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University. The patients signed informed consent forms. Upon approval by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (KY2021153), we strictly implemented inclusion and exclusion standards. Our inclusive criteria were singleton term-pregnant woman, no contraindication to epidural anesthesia, grade American Society of Anesthesiologists I–II, no history of drug allergy, no history of hypertension or diabetes, consent of the patient and her family, and a signature of informed consent. Exclusion criteria were complications during pregnancy such as gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes, major organ damage such as liver dysfunction and renal dysfunction, a history of diseases of the blood system such as anemia and hemophilia, multiple pregnancies, withdrawal from the study due to various reasons after the operation, and failure to correctly understand visual analogue scale (VAS), Ramsay, Bruggrmann comfort scale (BCS), or the use of analgesic pumps due to cognitive impairment.

2.2. Randomization and blinding

All women scheduled for C-section were randomly assigned to the 3 groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence concealed in consecutively numbered, opaque, and sealed envelopes that were opened on the morning of the surgery. This study’s primary outcome, the VAS score, is a continuous variable. Sample size (N) was calculated using the formula N=Ψ2[∑ki=1si2/k]/[∑ki=1(X¯i−X¯)2/(k−1)], Si was the initial estimate of the mean and standard deviation of the i-th sample, and k was the number of groups, ψ was obtained from the value query statistical table. Based on previous literature and using PASS 2021 software, the sample size was calculated to be 30 cases per group, totaling 90 cases. The 3 groups comprised of 121 cases of women undergoing C-section, of which 31 cases were excluded, and 90 cases were ultimately included. Group S (n = 30) women were given normal saline intravenously and into the epidural space. In group S, after umbilical cord clamping after delivery, 5 mL of normal saline (sodium chloride, batch number 17101713, from Sichuan Mekangjiale Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) was slowly injected intravenously and into the epidural space. Group I (n = 30) women were administered butorphanol by intravenous injection and normal saline into the epidural space. In group I, 5 mL of butorphanol (1 mg, batch number 170609 BC, Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) was slowly injected intravenously, and 5 mL of normal saline was slowly injected into the epidural space. Group E (n = 30) women were given normal saline intravenously and butorphanol into the epidural space. In group E, 5 mL of normal saline was slowly injected intravenously, and 5 mL of butorphanol (1 mg) was slowly injected into the epidural space. Participants in all the 3 groups were administered epidural anesthesia. A PCIA pump (Fig. 1) was used after the operation and prepared as follows: 100 µg sufentanil (sufentanil citrate injection, batch number 1170501, Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) + 8 mg of butorphanol 8 mg + 13.44 mg of tropisetron (tropisetron mesylate injection in 200 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride, batch No. 1706121, Beijing HuaSu Pharmaceutical Co, Ltd., China). Parameter settings of the analgesic pump were a continuous background infusion dosage of 3 mL/h, a single dose of 0.5 mL, and a locking time of 15 minutes. All women were treated by the same experienced anesthesiologist who specialized in obstetric and gynecologic anesthesia, and who did not participate in data collection. Another anesthesiologist, blinded to group allocation, then conducted the postoperative data collection. Patients, surgeons, and nurses were also blinded to group assignment and the types of pain killers applied. Flow chart of the study as shown in Figure 2.

After analgesic pump installation, the analgesic pump equipped with analgesic drugs is directly and uniformly pumped into the drugs through the intravenous indwelling needle.

Flow chart of the study.

Clinical evaluation: the VAS score, Ramsay sedation score, BCS comfort score, adverse reactions, intraoperative tenderness assessment, frequency of patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) presses within 48 hours, postoperative bleeding volume within 24 hours, and postoperative uterine reactivation after 10, 20, and 30 minutes, and at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 hours were recorded by clinical evaluation of the pregnant women.

To ensure the accuracy of the experimental data and to eliminate any interference by researchers, 3 different experimenters provided the drugs after umbilical cord clamping, and these designated the pain ratings and analyzed the data generated. The staff who participated in evaluations using the pain-rating scale were trained and completed their related sample evaluations independently.

2.3. Statistical methods

Data were recorded, sorted, and plotted employing Excel 2007, and the experimental results were statistically analyzed with SPSS 20.0. We expressed measurement data that followed a normal distribution by means ± standard deviation. The measurement data between the 2 groups was tested by independent sample t test and the measurement data in repeated measurement were analyzed by variance in repeated measurement.

3.8. Adverse reactions

We noted 1 case of nausea and vomiting in group S, 2 cases of lethargy in group I, and 1 case of nausea and vomiting in group E. We did not observe adverse reactions such as hypotension, respiratory depression, pruritus, headache, urinary retention, headache, nerve injury, or chills among the 3 groups (all P > .05).

4. Discussion

Postpartum pain after C-section is different from pain experienced in other common operations. Postpartum pain after C-section, in addition to the conventional postoperative pain experienced, also encompasses the uterine contractions induced by the postoperative pain. Therefore, although postpartum analgesic measures have been created for postpartum pain after C-section,[28] the majority of puerperas still complain of moderate or higher levels of pain,[29] that are related to uterine contraction pain. This latter pain is also 1 of the principal reasons leading to postoperative discomfort and overall diminished comfort perceived by postpartum women.[30] Although scholars in this specific area of pain have adopted measures to alleviate postpartum pain,[31] the resultant pain relief remains unacceptable. In this investigation we therefore observed the actions of epidural analgesia with butorphanol for puerperal women using self-controlled administration in combination with intravenous injection.

PCIA has been widely used in clinical practice for postoperative analgesia. Appling 2 or more kinds of analgesic drugs through PCIA achieve good analgesic effect and reduce drug dose.[32] Sufentanil, a derivative of fentanyl, is currently the most common postoperative analgesic drug in clinical practice. However, the increased dose is associated with an increase in nausea, vomiting, and other adverse effects, for which combined utilization with other analgesics may reduce adverse effects. It was found that butorphanol may activate κ-receptor to inhibit visceral pain. In this study, we utilized different anesthesia patterns and compared the anesthetic effects and associated adverse effects. The results showed that the VAS score of incision pain and uterine contraction pain in all 3 groups was about 3 points, which was within the range of mild pain, suggesting that intravenous PCIA provided a good analgesic effect. The VAS score of the uterine contraction pain in group I was significantly lower than that in group S at majority of tine points after administration with VAS score of about 3, indicating that intravenous butorphanol administration could alleviate uterine contraction pain. Butorphanol mainly entered the blood–brain barrier to act on κ receptor and produce central analgesic effect. In addition, the VAS scores of uterine pain in group E were significantly lower than those in group S at almost timepoints with a VAS score of 0 to 1, suggesting that epidural butorphanol could relieve uterine contraction pain. Butorphanol acted on dorsal horn κ receptor of spinal cord, resulting in peripheral analgesia. The VAS scores of uterine pain in group E at almost timepoints after administration were lower than those in group I, indicating that epidural butorphanol was better than intravenous administration in relieving uterine pain and the analgesia lasted longer. It might be that the concentration of butorphanol given through epidural administration was larger. The concentration of intravenous drugs is significantly reduced after passing the blood–brain barrier, and butorphanol confined to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord is metabolized more slowly than intravenous administration. Commiskey et al analyzed the response of mutant rats to various pain stimuli, and showed that after silencing the β-receptor gene of rats, κ-receptor was inhibited. In addition, β-receptor gene expression products were associated with visceral pain.[33–35]

Reports have shown that the visceral reactions induced by traction during the operation cause obvious discomfort to the parturient. Group I showed that it can reduce the traction reaction at 10, 20, and 30 minutes, relative to group S; group E could reduce the traction reaction at 10, 20, and 30 minutes, and at 1 hour compared with group S; and group I showed an obvious traction reaction at 10, 20, and 30 minutes, and at 1 hour relative to group E. The results indicated that intravenous or epidural administrating of butorphanol could alleviate the intraoperative stretch reaction, while epidural administration was more effective. Studies by Reddi et al showed that clinical doses of butorphanol lessened uterine smooth muscle cramps relative to morphine,[36] suggesting that butorphanol was superior to other opioids in tractive the uterus. Preemptive analgesia with butorphanol has been postulated to reduce or eliminate the sensitization of the central nervous system (CNS) caused by tissue injury, by preventing harmful stimulation induced by CNS impulses or by halting CNS excitation prior to feeling pain, so as to achieve a beneficial analgesic effect.[37] At the same time, we observed that the use of butorphanol had no significant adverse effect on the recovery of postpartum uterus.

There were several limitations to our study. Constraints on time and case numbers in our study may have resulted in limitations to appropriate statistical analysis, and we will therefore expand the sample size and conduct a multicenter study to further confirm our conclusions. Secondly, simple random is not optimal for this study, stratified randomization is more appropriate. Finally, our study lacks observation and evaluation of the safety of breast-feeding infants.

In conclusion, intravenous or epidural butorphanol combined with intravenous controlled analgesia effectively relieved the pain of uterine contractions and intraoperative traction, and epidural administration of butorphanol was superior to intravenous administration in reducing uterine contraction pain and traction reactions.
