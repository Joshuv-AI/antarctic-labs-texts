Advances in therapeutic approaches to gastric cancer
Authors: Feng Wang, Zi‐Xian Wang, Yu‐Jing Zhang, Ye He, Jia‐Fu Ji, Yuan‐Fang Li, Xiu‐Juan Qu, Yi Ba, Shu‐Qiang Yuan, Rui‐Hua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, with nearly 1 million new cases diagnosed annually. Despite innovations in diagnosis, most patients still present at advanced stages, contributing to a poor prognosis. For early gastric cancer, endoscopic resection or minimally invasive surgery has become standard, offering comparable oncologic outcomes with faster recovery compared with open surgery. In locally advanced disease, resection of the primary lesion and D2 lymphadenectomy, defined as dissection of lymph nodes along two levels of blood vessels supplying the stomach, remains the cornerstone of curative surgery, whereas perioperative chemotherapy, particularly oxaliplatin‐based regimens, has demonstrated improved survival benefits compared with surgery alone. The integration of immunotherapy into perioperative therapy for locally advanced disease and first‐line palliative therapy for metastatic disease represents a paradigm shift, with programmed death 1/programmed death‐ligand 1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy significantly improving pathologic complete response rates and overall survival, especially in programmed death ligand 1–positive and microsatellite instability‐high tumors. Targeted agents include claudin 18.2 antibody, with zolbetuximab establishing a new first‐line standard for claudin 18.2–positive disease, and antibody–drug conjugates, such as trastuzumab deruxtecan for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–positive disease, both of which have revolutionized treatment paradigms. Emerging strategies include dual immune checkpoint inhibition, bispecific antibodies, chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy, and fibroblast growth factor receptor 2b–targeting agents. Ultimately, the field is moving toward personalized, biomarker‐driven approaches within multidisciplinary frameworks, although overcoming acquired resistance and addressing global disparities in therapeutic access remain important challenges.Keywords: chemotherapy, gastric cancer, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, targeted therapy

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer, including gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) cancer, remains one of the most common malignant tumors and imposes substantial burden on human health, society, and the economy. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 statistics, gastric cancer ranks fifth globally, with both incidence and mortality among all cancer types, accounting for 968,350 new cases and 659,853 deaths annually. 1 Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the overall prognosis of gastric cancer remains poor. The management of gastric cancer is challenged by late diagnosis, aggressive tumor biology, technically demanding treatments, and the severe functional consequences of therapy. Success depends on centralized, multidisciplinary care within specialized centers, together with personalized treatment strategies based on disease stage and molecular profile, as well as robust supportive care throughout the treatment course. For physicians in community hospitals where multidisciplinary resources are limited, following the latest national cancer treatment guidelines remains the most effective approach to delivering standardized care because these guidelines recommend optimal treatment options based on available evidence and drug accessibility. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the most recent treatment strategies as well as future challenges and opportunities. By integrating the latest research findings and clinical insights, it aims to help alleviate the burden of this disease.

EPIDEMIOLOGY AND PATHOLOGY

Gastric cancer incidence exhibits substantial geographic variation globally, with the highest rates observed in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and South America. Among these regions, East Asia has the highest incidence, with China, Japan, and South Korea collectively accounting for approximately 53% of global cases in 2022. 1 In contrast, Western Europe, North America, and Africa have comparatively lower incidence rates. 2 Approximately 660,000 people die from gastric cancer worldwide each year. Consistent with the global incidence pattern, mortality rates are highest in East Asia and Central and Eastern Europe, whereas North America has the lowest mortality rate. 1

The development of gastric cancer results from a multistage process involving the synergistic effects of multiple factors. Helicobacter pylori infection is the primary etiologic factor for gastric cancer, particularly noncardia gastric cancer. 3 Lifestyle factors also play an important causal role in the development of gastric cancer. A high‐salt diet, consumption of preserved foods, smoking, heavy alcohol consumption, and obesity are all associated with an increased risk of gastric cancer. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In addition to infectious and lifestyle‐related risks, several disease‐related conditions further increase susceptibility to gastric cancer, including precancerous lesions, such as chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and mucosal changes in the remnant stomach after partial gastrectomy. 8 Beyond these acquired risk factors, familial predisposition has also been identified as a contributor to gastric carcinogenesis, with approximately 10% of gastric cancers exhibiting familial clustering. 9

The Lauren classification system is one of the most widely used pathologic typing systems for gastric cancer. It classifies gastric cancer into two main subtypes: intestinal type and diffuse type. 10 Intestinal‐type gastric cancer is typically associated with environmental factors, is characterized by glandular formation (Figure 1A), and is linked to H. pylori infection, dietary habits, and other exposures. 11 In contrast, diffuse‐type gastric cancer exhibits discohesive tumor cell infiltration, absent glandular structures, and loss of cell adhesion (Figure 1B). This subtype shows a stronger genetic predisposition and is associated with a markedly poorer prognosis. 12

Pathomorphology‐based classification alone is insufficient to capture the molecular and genetic heterogeneity of gastric cancer and fails to provide the precision needed to guide clinical management. Advances in genomic technologies have enabled the development of seminal molecular classification frameworks. Based on data from Western populations, The Cancer Genome Atlas classification system categorizes gastric cancer into four principal molecular subtypes with distinct molecular and clinicopathologic features: Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)‐positive, microsatellite instability (MSI), genomically stable, and chromosomal instability tumors. The EBV‐positive subtype is characterized by male predominance, preferential location in the gastric fundus/body, clonal EBV amplification, frequent PIK3CA/ARID1A mutations, programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) overexpression, and genome‐wide hypermethylation. The subtype with MSI predominantly affects elderly females and is characterized by MSI because of epigenetic silencing of mut‐L homolog 1, a high mutational burden, and PD‐L1 expression. The genomically stable subtype occurs more frequently in younger patients, features CDH1/RHOA mutations, and presents with diffuse histology in 73% of patients. The subtype with chromosomal instability is primarily localized to the GEJ and is marked by RTK/RAS pathway activation and TP53 mutations. 13 Although the original analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas primarily established a molecular taxonomy rather than a prognostic classification, subsequent studies have explored the clinical relevance of these subtypes, including their potential associations with survival and treatment benefit. 14 A novel, multi‐omics, comprehensive subtyping system developed at the Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) can integrate genomic and tumor microenvironmental features to stratify patients with gastric cancer who receive anti–PD‐1 therapy. In this multi‐omics subtyping (MOS) system, MOS1 is characterized by an activated immune environment, with high IL‐12 pathway activity, enhanced antigen presentation, and infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells. MOS2 represents an immune‐resilient subtype, characterized by activation of metabolism‐related pathways, high FDXR expression, and the absence of TP53 mutations at baseline. MOS3 corresponds to an immunosuppressive subtype, marked by high APOA1 expression, enrichment of TREM2‐positive macrophages, and activation of both fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) and angiogenesis pathways. 15

With advances in bioinformatics and omics technologies, several key driver genes for gastric cancer have been progressively identified. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)/ERBB2 is a proto‐oncogene encoding a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase involved in signaling pathways that regulate cell differentiation and proliferation. 16 , 17 Its overexpression/amplification appears to be an early event in gastric adenocarcinogenesis. 18 Approximately 15% of patients harbor HER2 aberrations and thus represent a potential population for anti‐HER2–targeted therapy. 19 Claudin 18 (CLDN18) is a member of the claudin family and a constituent of tight junctions that regulate paracellular barrier function. One splice isoform, CLDN18.2, is specifically expressed in normal gastric mucosal cells and maintains the gastric mucosal barrier. CLDN18.2 is overexpressed in approximately 40% of gastric cancers, making it an important therapeutic target. 20 CDH1, which encodes E‐cadherin, is a tumor suppressor gene, and heterozygous germline mutations in CDH1 are closely associated with hereditary diffuse gastric cancer; CDH1 mutations are detectable in approximately 30%–50% of patients who have hereditary diffuse gastric cancer. 21

Diagnosis and staging

Patients with early stage gastric cancer are often asymptomatic or may present only with subtle symptoms, such as early satiety or loss of appetite. Consequently, most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage. 19 Common clinical manifestations of gastric cancer include dyspepsia, reflux, unintentional weight loss, and abdominal pain. Patients with tumors located at the GEJ or in the proximal stomach tend to experience dysphagia, whereas anemia frequently develops in individuals with bleeding gastric tumors. 22 Esophagogastroduodenoscopy with histopathologic biopsy remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of gastric carcinoma because it enables direct visualization of lesions and tissue sampling to confirm the histologic subtype and differentiation grade.

Accurate staging is crucial for the precise treatment of patients with gastric cancer. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is important for tumor staging, namely, tumor (T) staging, enabling differentiation between T1/T2 and T3/T4 gastric cancers and identifying candidates for endoscopic resection. 23 For evaluation of nodal status, namely, (N) staging, EUS may detect lymph node metastases; however, its accuracy remains limited, and it is unable to detect distant organ metastases. Therefore, complementing EUS with computed tomography (CT) imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is essential to evaluate metastatic disease in lymph nodes, liver, lungs, and peritoneum. 24 Although magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography‐CT are not routinely used for gastric cancer diagnosis, they offer distinct advantages in specific scenarios. Magnetic resonance imaging provides superior differentiation between hepatic metastases and primary liver tumors, whereas positron emission tomography‐CT provides metabolic information through fluorodeoxyglucose uptake for the detection of occult metastases. 25 , 26 When EUS or CT evaluation indicates tumor penetration through the muscularis propria into subserosal connective tissue (T3 stage) or serosal penetration/invasion of adjacent structures (T4 stage), diagnostic laparoscopy with peritoneal lavage cytology is recommended to better detect peritoneal metastases that are not visible on imaging techniques. 27 , 28

Therapeutic approaches for early gastric cancer

With the wider adoption of screening, the detection rate of early gastric cancer (EGC) has risen steadily, 29 and endoscopic resection (ER) has become a preferred treatment option in high‐volume centers experienced in accurate staging evaluation and ER techniques, whereas upfront surgical resection remains the ultimate curative backup. Endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) are the two main ER techniques. 30 Initially, ER indications were restricted to differentiated intramucosal carcinomas <2 cm. 31 In light of advances in ESD and accumulating evidence, the fifth edition of the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association guidelines expanded the absolute indications for ESD based on the Japan Clinical Oncology Group trial JCOG0607 to include differentiated intramucosal cancers >2 cm without ulceration and ≤3 cm with ulceration. 32 , 33 Subsequently, based on the JCOG1009/1010 trials, intramucosal undifferentiated carcinomas <2 cm were also included as absolute indications. 34 Compared with conventional gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy, ESD provides comparable oncologic control while offering lower cost, faster postoperative recovery, more precise lesion excision with preservation of normal gastrointestinal anatomy, and better postoperative quality of life. Nevertheless, when ER fails to achieve curative resection, salvage gastrectomy remains necessary.

Tumor depth of invasion and lymphovascular invasion are key determinants of treatment selection in patients with EGC. However, these features cannot be directly and reliably assessed by endoscopy alone. Laparoscopic and endoscopic cooperative surgery and laparoscopic sentinel node navigation surgery, therefore, represent promising minimally invasive strategies in EGC, enabling precise intraoperative margin localization and real‐time nodal mapping to optimize the extent of resection and lymphadenectomy while preserving gastric function. 35 , 36 In a retrospective analysis of 205 patients with EGC who underwent surgical resection, the metastatic rates to regional lymph nodes were 8.8% for intramucosal gastric cancer and 16.6% for submucosal gastric cancer, respectively, even among patients in whom preoperative imaging revealed no enlarged or suspected metastatic lymph nodes. 37 Therefore, ER is prudently recommended only for differentiated intramucosal carcinomas <2 cm without suspected metastatic lymph nodes at SYSUCC; otherwise, patients are advised to undergo surgical resection with extensive lymphadenectomy. Overall, treatment selection for EGC should balance oncologic safety with functional preservation while recognizing that accurate staging, strict indication control, and timely salvage surgery remain essential to achieving optimal long‐term outcomes.

Therapeutic approaches for locally advanced gastric cancer

Surgery

Locally advanced gastric cancer (AGC) is best managed within a multidisciplinary framework. For example, a multidisciplinary team protocol guides the approach at SYSUCC. Within this approach, curative‐intent gastrectomy remains the therapeutic backbone, combined with postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with clinical stage II disease and perioperative chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy administered in the setting of clinical stage III disease.

The optimal extent of lymphadenectomy has long been a focal point of debate in gastric surgery. A large, multicenter Dutch trial demonstrated higher postoperative morbidity (43% vs. 25%; p < .001) and mortality (10% vs. 4%; p = .004) after D2 resection, which includes removal of the perigastric lymph nodes along the lesser and greater curvatures (stations 1–6) and secondary nodes along the major feeding arteries (stations 7–12a), compared with D1 resection, which includes removal of the perigastric lymph nodes along the lesser and greater curvatures (stations 1–6). 38 Although no significant overall survival (OS) difference was observed at 5 or 10 years, at 15 years, D2 resection was significantly associated with a reduction in gastric cancer‐specific mortality compared with D1 resection (37% vs. 48%, respectively; p < .01). 39 , 40 , 41 Regarding whether to extend dissection beyond D2, the JCOG9501 phase 3 trial demonstrated that adding prophylactic para‐aortic nodal dissection to standard D2 did not improve survival. 42 The JCOG9502 trial further indicated that, for tumors with ≤3 cm of esophageal invasion, transhiatal resection with lower mediastinal lymphadenectomy is a safe and effective standard approach, and thoracotomy with more extensive dissection confers no practical benefit. 43 Together, these studies established D2 lymphadenectomy as the standard for curative gastrectomy. 44 Consistent with this evidence, the 2022 guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology recommend D2 dissection, preferably at centers with experienced surgical teams. 45 , 46

With advances in minimally invasive techniques and devices, together with accumulating evidence from multicenter randomized trials, laparoscopic surgery for resectable early and locally AGC is becoming the standard of care. Survival outcomes are similar to those of open surgery, whereas perioperative recovery and long‐term quality of life are superior. The Korean KLASS‐01 trial demonstrated that laparoscopic distal gastrectomy achieved oncologic efficacy comparable to open surgery for stage I gastric cancer, with a lower complication rate. 47 Consistent results were reported in Japan; both JCOG0703 and JCOG0912 demonstrated that 5‐year disease‐free survival (DFS) and 5‐year OS after laparoscopic distal gastrectomy were equivalent to those after open distal gastrectomy in stage I disease. 48 , 49 In the setting of locally AGC, the multicenter randomized CLASS‐01 trial led by Chinese investigators indicated that laparoscopic distal gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy is safe and feasible, with oncologic outcomes noninferior to open surgery. 50 Five‐year follow‐up data from the JLSSG0901 trial likewise support this conclusion. 51 In Western populations, the Dutch multicenter LOGICA trial, which included a substantial proportion of patients who received preoperative chemotherapy, demonstrated no significant difference in 1‐year OS between minimally invasive and open gastrectomy. 52 Overall, surgical treatment for locally AGC should be guided by accurate staging, multidisciplinary decision‐making, adequate D2 lymphadenectomy, and appropriate use of minimally invasive techniques in experienced centers.

Perioperative chemotherapy

In recent years, several key studies from East Asia have demonstrated that adding postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy after D2 gastrectomy further improves both relapse‐free survival (RFS) and OS (Table 1). The Japanese ACTS‐GC trial, which enrolled patients with pathologic stage II–III gastric cancer who had undergone curative D2 resection, was the first pivotal study to evaluate the effect of postoperative chemotherapy with the oral fluoropyrimidine anticancer agent S‐1 after D2 resection. That trial demonstrated that D2 plus S‐1 improved 5‐year OS from 61.1% to 71.7% (hazard ratio [HR], 0.67; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.54–0.83) and improved 5‐year RFS from 53.1% to 65.4% (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.54–0.79) compared with D2 resection alone. 53 , 54 The Korean CLASSIC trial, which enrolled patients with stage II–IIIB gastric cancer after curative D2 gastrectomy, further confirmed the benefit of postoperative capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (CAPOX) after D2 surgery, demonstrating a 9% absolute improvement in 5‐year OS compared with D2 surgery alone, with rates of 78% versus 69% (HR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.51–0.85). 55 , 56 On the basis of these findings, postoperative S‐1 monotherapy or CAPOX after gastrectomy became a standard regimen for patients with pathologic stage II–III gastric cancer. Subsequent trials further refined the optimal postoperative treatment strategy after D2 surgery. In JCOG1104, which enrolled patients with stage II gastric cancer after D2 surgery, reducing postoperative S‐1 from eight to four courses failed to preserve the efficacy of the longer regimen, with 5‐year OS of 88.6% versus 89.7% (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.72–1.75). 57 , 58 The Japanese JACCRO GC‐07 trial, conducted in patients with pathologic stage III gastric cancer after D2 surgery, demonstrated that adjuvant S‐1 plus docetaxel achieved higher 5‐year RFS and OS than S‐1 alone, with RFS rates of 59.8% versus 50.6% (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.60–0.88) and OS rates of 67.9% versus 60.3% (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.61–0.92). 59 , 60 Similarly, in the Chinese CAPITAL trial of patients with pathologic stage II/III gastric cancer after D2 surgery, postoperative S‐1 plus oxaliplatin (SOX) outperformed S‐1 alone, with higher 5‐year DFS (66.2% vs. 55.6%; HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.61–0.96) and 5‐year OS (70.9% vs. 62.9%; HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.58–0.95). 61 In contrast to evidence from East Asia, extensive studies in Western countries have not demonstrated a clear survival benefit from postoperative chemotherapy alone after gastrectomy for gastric cancer. 62 , 63 , 64 This discrepancy may reflect biologic heterogeneity, differences in surgical practice, and historically variable uptake of D2 lymphadenectomy. Therefore, international guidelines have incorporated these regional differences by adopting pathway‐specific recommendations. East Asian guidelines continue to recommend postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy after curative D2 gastrectomy for stage II–III disease, largely reflecting the ACTS‐GC and CLASSIC evidence base. In contrast, Western guidelines generally prioritize perioperative chemotherapy, partly because many historical Western adjuvant trials were conducted in settings where D2 lymphadenectomy was less consistently performed. However, adjuvant chemotherapy remains recommended when patients have undergone upfront surgery without preoperative treatments. 65

Preoperative chemotherapy can reduce tumor burden, treat potential micrometastases at an earlier stage, and provide an opportunity to assess chemosensitivity before selecting postoperative therapy (Table 2). Before D2 lymphadenectomy became widely adopted as the standard surgical approach, the UK MAGIC trial established the role of perioperative chemotherapy in Europe, in which patients received three preoperative and three postoperative cycles of epirubicin, cisplatin, and fluorouracil. The 5‐year OS rate was 36.3% with perioperative chemotherapy versus 23.0% with surgery alone (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.60–0.93). 66 Building on the European triplet chemotherapy paradigm, the German FLOT4 trial demonstrated that eight cycles of perioperative docetaxel, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (FLOT) were superior to six cycles of epirubicin, cisplatin, and fluorouracil in gastric cancer. 67 In FLOT4, the FLOT group achieved higher R0 resection rates (84% vs. 77%) and pathologic complete response (pCR) rates (16% vs. 6%), together with longer median DFS (30 vs. 18 months; HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.62–0.91) and median OS (50 vs. 35 months; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.63–0.94). 67

Because triplet regimens are associated with greater toxicity, doublet‐based perioperative approaches have predominated in Asian populations with locally AGC. The multicenter phase 3 RESOLVE trial from China compared adjuvant CAPOX, adjuvant SOX, and perioperative SOX and indicated that perioperative SOX yielded a clinically meaningful survival improvement compared with postoperative adjuvant CAPOX (5‐year OS, 60.0% vs. 52.1%; HR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.62–1.00). 68 The subsequent RESONANCE study likewise demonstrated that preoperative plus postoperative adjuvant SOX outperformed adjuvant SOX alone (3‐year DFS: 61.7% vs. 53.8%; HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.61–0.96). 69 In Korea, the PRODIGY trial indicated that three preoperative cycles consisting of docetaxel, oxaliplatin, and S‐1, conferred a significant progression‐free survival (PFS) advantage (3‐year PFS: 66.3% vs. 60.2%; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.53–0.94) and OS (5‐year OS: 66.8% vs. 63.0%; HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.54–0.96), supporting combined docetaxel, oxaliplatin, and S‐1 as a recommended preoperative option for locally AGC. 70 , 71 When evaluating different perioperative treatment models, comparisons are meaningful only when surgical quality is homogeneous, particularly regarding the adequacy of D2 lymphadenectomy. As D2 gastrectomy becomes more widely adopted in Western practice, global perioperative strategies are likely to converge. Collectively, current evidence supports a stage‐adapted and regionally informed treatment framework in which perioperative or postoperative chemotherapy is selected according to surgical quality, disease stage, treatment tolerance, and local standards of care, with D2 gastrectomy remaining the essential foundation of curative‐intent treatment.

Perioperative immunotherapy

Perioperative treatment for gastric cancer has now entered the immunotherapy era. Deficient mismatch repair (dMMR)/MSI‐high (MSI‐H) gastric and GEJ adenocarcinomas represent a biologically distinct subgroup with high sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade, providing the rationale for chemotherapy‐free preoperative immunotherapy strategies. Among patients with locally advanced dMMR/MSI‐H disease, the single‐arm phase 2 NEONIPIGA and INFINITY studies evaluated preoperative combinations of PD‐1/PD‐L1 and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4) inhibitors, respectively, and reported pCR rates of 59% and 60%, respectively. However, postoperative management differed between the two studies, with adjuvant nivolumab incorporated in NEONIPIGA but not routinely used after surgery in INFINITY cohort 1. 72 , 73 Given the absence of randomized comparator arms, the early follow‐up outcomes were favorable but do not yet establish a definitive RFS or OS advantage. Nevertheless, the magnitude of pathologic response has prompted the NCCN to list perioperative immunotherapy as a preferred option for patients with dMMR/MSI‐H gastric cancer. 65

For patients with proficient mismatch repair disease, chemotherapy remains the backbone of perioperative therapy, and the integration of immunotherapy throughout the perioperative course to act synergistically with chemotherapy has become a strategy of growing interest. In the randomized phase 2 NEOSUMMIT‐01 study, patients with locally AGC received preoperative chemotherapy with or without toripalimab; the addition of toripalimab increased the pCR rate from 7% to 22% 74 and was also associated with improved 3‐year event‐free survival (EFS) and 3‐year OS (3‐year EFS: 74.7% vs. 56.2%; HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.27–0.98; 3‐year OS: 81.3% vs. 72.2%; HR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.21–0.95). 75 Another phase 2/3 trial, DANTE, evaluated perioperative atezolizumab plus FLOT versus FLOT alone in patients with esophagogastric adenocarcinoma, clinically staged as T2 or higher or node‐positive disease, and showed greater pathologic downstaging and an increase in pCR rate from 15% to 24% with the addition of atezolizumab. 76 The DRAGON‐IV study further suggested that incorporating anti‐angiogenic therapy into immunochemotherapy significantly improved pCR compared with chemotherapy (pCR, 18.3% vs. 5.0% p < .0001), with acceptable tolerability. 77 Phase 3 trials have further clarified whether perioperative immunochemotherapy can translate improved pathologic response into long‐term survival benefit. In the final analysis of KEYNOTE‐585, pembrolizumab plus perioperative chemotherapy increased the pCR rate from 2.8% to 14.2%; however, this pathologic benefit did not translate into a clear long‐term survival advantage, with 5‐year OS rates of 55% versus 49% (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.71–1.03). 78 , 79 In contrast, MATTERHORN indicated that adding durvalumab to perioperative FLOT increased the pCR rate from 7.2% to 19.2%, improved 2‐year EFS from 58.5% to 67.4% (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.58–0.86), and improved 2‐year OS from 70.4% to 75.7% (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.50–0.90), compared with perioperative FLOT alone. 80 Recently, ASTRUM‐006 demonstrated that adding serplulimab to perioperative SOX improved the 3‐year EFS rate from 49% to 57% (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.56–0.94) in patients with a PD‐L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥5. 81 A key difference may lie in the chemotherapy backbone: MATTERHORN and ASTRUM‐006 used the oxaliplatin‐containing regimen, which may be more immunogenic, potentially enhancing the benefit of immunotherapy. In summary, perioperative immune checkpoint inhibitors have consistently improved pCR rates in locally AGC, although long‐term survival benefits have not been uniform across trials. Deeper exploration of predictive biomarkers may facilitate more precise patient selection and optimization of treatment regimens.

Despite the promising activity of immunochemotherapy in first‐line treatment of AGC and in the preoperative setting for locally advanced disease, this approach is not currently recommended as adjuvant therapy after upfront resection in patients who have resectable, locally AGC. ATTRACTION‐5, a multicenter phase 3 trial conducted in Japan, Korea, and China, demonstrated no statistically significant difference in 3‐year DFS among patients with pathologic stage III gastric cancer. 82

In resectable disease, the addition of preoperative radiotherapy to chemotherapy has been shown to improve tumor response and downstaging. In the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group RTOG 9904 study and the CROSS trials, pCR rates reached 26% for gastric cancer and 23% for esophageal and GEJ adenocarcinoma, respectively. Furthermore, trials such as POET and TOPGEAR demonstrated superior pCR and downstaging with preoperative CRT compared with chemotherapy alone. 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 Perioperative CRT has also been confirmed to be safe, with acceptable toxicity profiles reported in the preoperative POET and TOPGEAR trials as well as in the adjuvant ARTIST/ARTIST2 and CRITICS trials. 86 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 Despite these advantages in pathologic response and local disease control, most phase 3 trials have failed to demonstrate a survival advantage of perioperative CRT over perioperative chemotherapy alone. 88 , 92 The phase 2 CRITICS‐II trial reported improved pathologic response, favorable toxicity, and superior 1‐year EFS in both the preoperative chemotherapy plus CRT arm and the preoperative CRT arm compared with the preoperative chemotherapy arm, thereby supporting the total preoperative strategy for further investigation. 93

At the time of this publication, CRT is not routinely recommended as perioperative treatment for resectable gastric cancer. Exceptions include postoperative CRT for patients with inadequate lymph node dissection (D0 or D1); incomplete microscopic tumor clearance at the resection margin; or high‐risk pathologic features such as lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, metastases in three or more regional lymph nodes, or a high proportion of positive lymph nodes, for example, >25%. In contrast, perioperative CRT remains a promising strategy for resectable GEJ adenocarcinoma, supported by evidence from the CROSS trial indicating improved OS compared with surgery alone (10‐year OS rates, 38% vs. 25%, respectively; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.55–0.89) and from the POET trial showing higher pathologic response, better locoregional control, and a favorable survival trend in esophageal or GEJ adenocarcinoma. 87 , 94

The primary benefit of radiotherapy in gastric cancer may lie in improved locoregional control, which could represent a potential pathway toward survival benefit. In trials in which CRT reduced locoregional recurrence (LRR), such as CROSS and POET, survival gains were observed; and, even in ARTIST, LRR‐free survival was improved. Conversely, in studies like CRITICS and TOPGEAR in which the reduction in LRR was minimal or absent, CRT did not prolong survival. To make locoregional control clinically meaningful and translate it into survival benefit, pretreatment laparoscopic staging is particularly important because administering preoperative radiotherapy to patients who have occult peritoneal seeding may expose patients to treatment burden without realistic curative benefit and may also underestimate the true value of radiotherapy.

Looking forward, as immunotherapy becomes increasingly integrated into gastric cancer treatment, it will be essential to clarify recurrence patterns, particularly LRR rates and associated risk factors, after preoperative immunochemotherapy. This may help identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from combined radiotherapy. Molecular subtyping and total preoperative therapy approaches may also help optimize efficacy while minimizing toxicity.

Therapeutic approaches for advanced gastric cancer

Palliative chemotherapy

Cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the therapeutic backbone in the management of AGC, with fluoropyrimidine–platinum doublets constituting the global first‐line standard. However, East–West differences in regimen selection persist, largely reflecting regional epidemiologic patterns, clinical practice traditions, and drug availability. In Asia, the oral fluoropyrimidine S‐1 is widely used, and the G‐SOX trial indicated that SOX is as effective as cisplatin plus S‐1 in the first‐line setting (median PFS: 5.5 vs. 5.4 months; HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.84–1.20; median OS: 14.1 vs. 13.1 months; HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.80–1.14). 95 Furthermore, in the Chinese population, the SOX regimen demonstrated superior survival outcomes compared with the cisplatin plus S‐1 regimen in patients with diffuse or mixed‐type gastric cancer (median PFS: 5.7 vs. 4.9 months; HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.63–0.89; median OS: 13.0 vs. 11.8 months; HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.64–0.92). 96 Conversely, Western practice favors capecitabine or fluorouracil plus oxaliplatin (CAPOX/folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin [FOLFOX]) because S‐1 remains largely unavailable outside Asia. The global shift from cisplatin to oxaliplatin is well established, driven by comparable efficacy and superior tolerability.

In later line settings, taxanes (paclitaxel and docetaxel) and irinotecan are established global standards of care. In Japan, weekly nanoparticle albumin–bound paclitaxel is approved as another second‐line option based on its noninferior efficacy and superior safety profile compared with solvent‐based paclitaxel in the phase 3 ABSOLUTE trial (median OS: 11.1 vs. 10.9 months; HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.76–1.23). 97 In addition, trifluridine‐tipiracil represents a third‐line standard in the United States, the European Union, and Japan based on the TAGS trial (median PFS: 2.0 vs. 1.7 months; HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.47–0.70; median OS: 5.7 vs. 3.6 months; HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56–0.85). 98

Overall, cytotoxic doublets remain the mainstay backbone for combination strategies. When integrated with immunotherapy or targeted agents, these regimens require nuanced adjustment to deliver more effective and personalized treatment for patients with AGC.

Palliative targeted therapy

The landscape of targeted therapy for gastric cancer has significantly evolved toward a biomarker‐driven era of medicine. Two major milestones, HER2 and CLDN18.2, now define the first‐line standard of care, whereas vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/VEGF receptor (VEGFR) inhibition remains a validated regimen in later line settings. However, significant East–West divergences in drug availability and regulatory approvals continue to require region‐specific treatment algorithms (Table 4).

HER2‐targeted therapy: From monoclonal antibody to antibody–drug conjugate revolution

HER2 overexpression can be detected in approximately 13%–22% of patients with gastric cancer, typically identified by an immunohistochemistry score of 3+ or by an immunohistochemistry score of 2+ with fluorescence in situ hybridization positivity. 99 Trastuzumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the extracellular domain IV of HER2, thereby blocking HER2‐regulated signaling. The phase 3 ToGA trial established trastuzumab combined chemotherapy as a standard first‐line treatment for HER2‐positive AGC, with a median OS of 13.8 months versus 11.1 months for patients who received chemotherapy alone (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.60–0.91) and a median PFS of 6.7 versus 5.5 months, respectively (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.59–0.85). 99 More recently, in KEYNOTE‐811, which enrolled patients with HER2‐positive AGC, the addition of pembrolizumab to trastuzumab and chemotherapy further improved clinical outcomes (median OS: 20.0 vs. 16.8 months; HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.67–0.94; median PFS: 10.0 vs. 8.1 months; HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.61–0.87). 100 , 101

The major advance in later line therapy is trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd), a next‐generation antibody–drug conjugate (ADC). 102 In contrast to earlier HER2‐targeted strategies that failed to provide consistent benefit beyond trastuzumab, T‐DXd demonstrated unprecedented efficacy in the Asian DESTINY‐Gastric01/06 trials 102 , 103 and the Western DESTINY‐Gastric02 trial, 104 significantly prolonging survival outcomes in later line settings compared with physician’s choice chemotherapy, either paclitaxel or irinotecan (median OS: 12.5 vs. 8.4 months; HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.39–0.88; median PFS: 5.6 vs. 3.5 months; HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.31–0.71). In particular, the pivotal DESTINY‐Gastric04 trial further confirmed the superiority of T‐DXd over the previous second‐line standard, ramucirumab–paclitaxel (median OS: 14.7 vs. 11.4 months; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.55–0.90; median PFS: 6.7 vs. 5.6 months; HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.59–0.92), 105 establishing T‐DXd as the new preferred option after trastuzumab. In addition, disitamab vedotin (RC48), an alternative HER2‐ADC approved in China, yielded an objective response rate (ORR) of 24.8% (95% CI, 17.5%–33.3%), a median PFS of 4.1 months (95% CI, 3.7–4.9 months), and a median OS of 7.9 months (95% CI, 6.7–9.9 months) in a single‐arm phase 2 trial. 106

CLDN18.2‐targeted therapy: A major therapeutic advance since HER2

CLDN18.2, a tight‐junction protein aberrantly expressed in 30%–40% of gastric cancers, 107 represents a significant therapeutic advance in gastric cancer treatment since HER2‐targeted therapy. CLDN18.2 expression shows limited overlap with HER2 positivity in gastric cancer, 108 thereby broadening the population eligible for targeted therapy.

Zolbetuximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody against CLDN18.2, has emerged as a new first‐line option for patients who have CLDN18.2‐positive, HER2‐negative AGC, defined as ≥75% of tumor cells showing moderate‐to‐strong staining on immunohistochemistry. Two pivotal phase 3 trials confirmed its benefit in this population. In SPOTLIGHT, 565 patients were randomized to receive either zolbetuximab plus modified FOLFOX (mFOLFOX6) or placebo plus mFOLFOX6, and the addition of zolbetuximab prolonged median PFS from 8.67 to 10.61 months (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.60–0.94) and prolonged median OS from 15.5 to 18.2 months (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.60–0.94). 109 In GLOW, 507 patients were randomized to receive zolbetuximab plus CAPOX or placebo plus CAPOX, with median PFS prolonged from 6.80 to 8.21 months (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.54–0.87) and median OS prolonged from 12.16 to 14.39 months (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.62–0.97). Across both trials, the main added toxicities were gastrointestinal, particularly nausea and vomiting. 110 Future research should prioritize trials evaluating zolbetuximab‐based combinations in both HER2‐negative and potential CLDN18.2/HER2 co‐expressing gastric cancers, with the aim of further improving survival outcomes.

Palliative immunotherapy

Immunotherapy has revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for AGC. First‐line options for previously untreated patients now involve combination strategies, including chemotherapy plus PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade for HER2‐negative tumors and chemotherapy, trastuzumab, plus a PD‐1 inhibitor for HER2‐positive disease. With these advances, the median OS has increased by approximately 3 months, and the risk of death has decreased by approximately 25%. 100 , 111 , 112 , 113 , 114 , 115 (Table 5) Building upon this foundation, the sections below address several key clinical questions, including how to optimize chemotherapy backbone regimens, how to improve patient selection using biomarkers, and which future combination therapies may hold promise.

Role of cytotoxic backbone in enhancing immunotherapy

The integration of immunotherapy with chemotherapy is not uniformly synergistic because the choice of chemotherapy backbone appears to influence treatment efficacy. The disappointing outcomes of the KEYNOTE‐062 trial served as an early setback, demonstrating that the addition of pembrolizumab to a cisplatin/fluoropyrimidine regimen did not translate into a significant survival advantage for PD‐L1–positive AGC compared with cisplatin/fluoropyrimidine alone (median OS: 12.5 vs. 11.1 months; HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.70–1.03). 116 However, this narrative was reshaped by subsequent landmark trials, including CheckMate 649 (median OS: 14.4 vs. 11.1 months; HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.59–0.86), RATIONALE‐305 (median OS: 15.0 vs. 12.9 months; HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.70–0.92), ORIENT‐16 (median OS: 15.2 vs. 12.3 months; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.63–0.94), and GEMSTONE‐303 (median OS: 15.6 vs. 12.6 months; HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.61–0.92), in which PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade combined with oxaliplatin‐based chemotherapy, such as FOLFOX or CAPOX, produced significant improvements in OS compared with the corresponding chemotherapy control arms. 111 , 112 , 113 , 114 This discrepancy suggests a potentially more favorable interaction between immunotherapy and oxaliplatin, which is hypothesized to induce immunogenic cell death more effectively than cisplatin, thereby increasing tumor visibility to the immune system through the release of damage‐associated molecular patterns. 117 , 118 The clinical relevance of this mechanism is further supported by post‐hoc analyses of the RATIONALE‐305 and KEYNOTE‐859 trials, in which efficacy outcomes appeared numerically more favorable in cohorts receiving oxaliplatin‐containing combinations than in those receiving cisplatin‐containing combinations. 112 , 115 Collectively, these findings suggest that chemotherapy selection in the immunotherapy era should move beyond historical efficacy alone and toward a biologically informed approach that also considers the immunomodulatory potential of the cytotoxic backbone.

PD‐L1 combined positive score: An essential but imperfect predictive biomarker

The selection of patients with AGC for first‐line immunochemotherapy is predominantly guided by PD‐L1 expression, most commonly assessed using the CPS. A substantial body of clinical trial data demonstrates that the most pronounced survival advantage achieved by adding a PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitor to chemotherapy is primarily observed in AGC patients with PD‐L1–positive or higher PD‐L1–expressing tumors, often defined using CPS thresholds such as ≥1 or ≥5, depending on the trial and guideline context, a finding that is broadly consistent across different HER2 statuses. 100 , 111 , 113 , 115 , 116 , 119 Consequently, leading international oncology guidelines, such as those from the NCCN and the European Society for Medical Oncology, recommend first‐line immunochemotherapy for patients whose tumors express PD‐L1 with a CPS ≥1. 44 , 45 , 65

Determining the optimal therapeutic approach for patients with low PD‐L1 expression (CPS‐low) remains a substantial challenge in clinical practice. Although CheckMate 649 demonstrated a higher ORR in the CPS <5 subgroup, this did not translate into a statistically significant OS benefit, thereby limiting the strength of this signal for routine treatment selection. 111 Nevertheless, this ORR signal suggests the potential for meaningful clinical benefit in a subset of these patients. Reflecting this complexity, some clinical guidelines, such as those from the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology, provide a more nuanced and conditional recommendation that immunochemotherapy may be considered for individuals with a low CPS who present with high disease burden or limited subsequent treatment options. 44

Emerging biomarkers for patient stratification beyond PD‐L1

Although PD‐L1 expression is a cornerstone for patient selection, refining the prediction of treatment response to immunochemotherapy in AGC requires consideration of a broader biomarker landscape. Established genomic features, such as MSI‐H status and high tumor mutational burden, serve as strong indicators of tumors with high intrinsic immunogenicity, which consistently derive the greatest benefit from immunochemotherapy. 120 , 121 The remarkable efficacy of pembrolizumab monotherapy and nivolumab plus ipilimumab in MSI‐H cohorts, as reflected by prolonged survival and high ORRs in CheckMate 649 and KEYNOTE‐062, has catalyzed a paradigm shift toward chemotherapy‐free strategies. 116 , 122 Consequently, dMMR/MSI‐H status has emerged as a tissue‐agnostic indicator for frontline immunotherapy or dual checkpoint inhibition, namely, PD‐1 plus CTLA‐4 blockade, in major international guidelines, irrespective of other molecular markers like HER2 or CLDN18.2. 45 , 65

Because AGC can vary substantially between tumor sites and evolve over time during treatment, a single biomarker, such as PD‐L1, MSI, or tumor mutational burden, may not fully capture the likelihood of a response to immunochemotherapy. Therefore, the field is rapidly moving toward holistic biosignatures that synthesize diverse data streams, including genomic variants, transcriptomic profiles, exosomal proteomes, and gut microbiota, into unified predictive models. 15 , 123 , 124 , 125 The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning is pivotal in this transition. For instance, the multi‐modal deep‐learning framework, which fuses radiomic features with digital pathology and clinical parameters, has demonstrated high accuracy in forecasting treatment responses in HER2‐positive patients with AGC who are anti‐HER2 plus anti–PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy. 125 This evolution from static, one‐dimensional testing to dynamic, data‐driven artificial intelligence classifiers represents the next stage of precision oncology, enabling the identification of subtle patient subgroups that may benefit from tailored next‐generation immunotherapy combinations.

Emerging future therapies

The therapeutic frontier for treatment‐naive AGC is rapidly expanding beyond the established paradigm of first‐line chemotherapy combined with a PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitor. Current research is exploring more potent immunotherapy doublets and the blockade of complementary immunosuppressive pathways to further improve response depth, durability, and long‐term survival.

Dual immune checkpoint inhibition, exemplified by the combination of anti–PD‐1 and anti–CTLA‐4 antibodies, aims to achieve more comprehensive activation of antitumor T‐cell responses. Although nivolumab plus ipilimumab in CheckMate 649 did not achieve a survival advantage in the overall cohort, the delayed separation of OS curves in favor of the nivolumab/ipilimumab arm underscored the potential value of CTLA‐4 co‐inhibition. 120 This concept has been further refined with the advent of bispecific antibodies. Notably, the phase 3 COMPASSION‐15 trial demonstrated that cadonilimab, a PD‐1/CTLA‐4 bispecific antibody, plus chemotherapy significantly extended OS in patients with treatment‐naive, HER2‐negative AGC (15.0 vs. 10.8 months: HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.50–0.78). 119 It is important to note that this benefit persisted across all PD‐L1 expression levels, suggesting that the enhanced activity of bispecific agents may reduce dependence on high baseline PD‐L1 expression, thereby expanding the population eligible for frontline immunotherapy.

The integration of anti‐angiogenic agents into the immunochemotherapy framework represents a conceptually potent strategy to normalize tumor vasculature and enhance lymphocyte infiltration. Early phase studies evaluating triplet regimens consisting of chemotherapy, a PD‐1 inhibitor, and a potent VEGFR inhibitor, such as fruquintinib or anlotinib, have reported encouraging ORR and PFS data, suggesting potential gains over immunochemotherapy alone. 126 However, the failure of the LEAP‐015 trial, which evaluated lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab and chemotherapy, to meet its primary OS end point exemplifies the complexity of this approach (median OS: 12.6 vs. 12.9 months; HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.71–1.00). 127 , 128 This discrepancy likely stems from a narrow therapeutic window in which cumulative toxicities may increase treatment discontinuation, together with the distinct pharmacologic profiles of different VEGF‐targeting agents.

Targeting the transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β) pathway has emerged as a promising approach to remodel a desmoplastic or immune‐excluded tumor microenvironment. The bifunctional fusion protein SHR‐1701, which simultaneously traps TGF‐β and blocks PD‐L1, recently demonstrated superior survival outcomes when added to first‐line chemotherapy (median OS: 15.8 vs. 11.2 months; HR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.53–0.81). 129 By countering TGF‐β–mediated T‐cell exclusion and fibroblast activation, this dual‐targeting approach may prove more effective than standard immune checkpoint inhibitors in converting resistant tumors into an immune‐responsive state.

Beyond zolbetuximab for highly CLDN18.2‐positive disease, ongoing studies are exploring strategies to expand the population eligible for CLDN18.2‐targeted therapies. The phase 2 ILUSTRO trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03505320) suggests a potential role for zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy plus a PD‐1 inhibitor as a first‐line treatment strategy. Patients with high CLDN18.2 expression achieved a median PFS of 18.0 months, underscoring the importance of biomarker‐driven patient selection in maximizing therapeutic benefit. 130 CLDN18.2‐targeted ADCs represent another strategy to extend treatment beyond the highly expressing population eligible for zolbetuximab. In the phase 1 KYM901 trial, CMG901, an anti‐CLDN18.2 antibody conjugated to the microtubule inhibitor monomethyl auristatin E, demonstrated a confirmed ORR of 29% among 109 efficacy‐evaluable patients with previously treated AGC or GEJ cancer. The median PFS and OS were 3.7 and 10.1 months, respectively. 131 Similarly, the phase 1 study of SHR‐A1904, which carries a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, enrolled 95 previously treated patients and reported confirmed ORRs of 24.2% and 25.0% at doses of 6.0 and 8.0 mg/kg, respectively, with corresponding median PFS durations of 5.6 and 5.8 months. These findings demonstrate clinically meaningful activity across two distinct ADC payload classes and support further evaluation of CLDN18.2‐directed ADCs, although randomized trials are required to establish their survival benefit, optimal biomarker threshold, and position in the treatment sequence. 132 More recently, AstraZeneca announced positive topline results from the phase 3 CLARITY‐Gastric01 trial, reporting that sonesitatug vedotin (CMG901) met the prespecified OS end points 133 ; however, detailed efficacy and safety data have not yet been disclosed and are awaited before the magnitude and clinical relevance of the benefit can be fully assessed. CLDN18.2‐specific chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy has also demonstrated the ability to improve PFS in heavily pretreated populations, including patients who had previously failed anti–PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy, offering a promising and potentially more potent alternative. The randomized phase 2 CT041‐ST‐01 trial enrolled 156 patients with CLDN18.2‐positive, HER2‐negative advanced gastric or GEJ adenocarcinoma who had received at least two prior lines of therapy. Compared with the physician’s choice of treatment, satri‐cel prolonged median PFS from 1.77 to 3.25 months (HR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.24–0.56) and increased the ORR from 4% to 22%, and the median OS was 7.92 versus 5.49 months, respectively (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.46–1.05). 134 Based on these findings, satri‐cel was approved in China in June 2026 for this patient population, representing an important advance in CLDN18.2‐targeted therapy and cellular therapy for solid tumors. 135 Compared with CLDN18.2‐targeted monoclonal antibodies, such as zolbetuximab, which are currently limited to patients with high CLDN18.2 expression (≥75% tumor cell staining) and are administered in combination with chemotherapy, satri‐cel offers the possibility of a more durable response and uses a broader eligibility threshold (≥40% staining), potentially expanding the treatable population to nearly 60% of patients with gastric cancer. A pivotal phase 3 trial comparing satri‐cel with standard therapy in patients with previously treated AGC is ongoing.

FGFR2b is a clinically relevant splice variant of FGFR2 that promotes tumor proliferation through activation of downstream signaling cascades, including the RAS‐MAPK and PI3K‐AKT pathways. 136 Bemarituzumab, a specific immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody targeting FGFR2b, initially demonstrated clinical promise, but not statistical significance, in the phase 2 FIGHT trial, in which its combination with chemotherapy prolonged survival in patients with previously untreated AGC (median PFS: 9.5 vs. 7.4 months; HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.49–1.08; median OS: 19.2 vs. 13.5 months; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.52–1.14). 137 , 138 , 139 These findings prompted the initiation of the phase 3 FORTITUDE‐101 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05052801) and FORTITUDE‐102 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05111626) trials to further evaluate the potential of bemarituzumab in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy. 140 However, subsequent phase 3 development has been less definitive. FORTITUDE‐101 evaluated bemarituzumab plus mFOLFOX6 versus placebo plus mFOLFOX6 in previously untreated, FGFR2b‐overexpressing, HER2‐negative patients with AGC or GEJ cancer; although the prespecified analysis indicated an OS benefit (median OS: 17.9 vs. 12.5 months; HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.43–0.86), this benefit was attenuated with longer follow‐up (median OS: 14.5 vs. 13.2 months; HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.62–1.08). 140 In parallel, FORTITUDE‐102, which evaluated bemarituzumab plus chemotherapy and nivolumab in the first‐line setting, was stopped for inadequate efficacy after an ad‐hoc analysis requested by the data monitoring committee. It is important to note that these clinical setbacks do not negate the scientific validity of FGFR2b as a therapeutic target but, instead, highlight the need for a strategic evolution in clinical development. Realizing the full therapeutic potential of FGFR2b will require more granular patient‐selection strategies and the deployment of next‐generation modalities, particularly ADCs and biparatopic antibodies, to overcome existing barriers.

Concurrently, a deeper understanding of immune resistance mechanisms is yielding more sophisticated combination strategies. One example is targeting Dickkopf‐1 (DKK1), an immunosuppressive factor upregulated in some tumors that impairs CD8‐positive T‐cell function. This rational, biology‐driven approach has demonstrated early clinical success; in a phase 2 study, the combination of a DKK1 inhibitor with tislelizumab, an anti–PD‐1 antibody, and first‐line chemotherapy yielded an ORR of 90% in patients with DKK1‐high AGC, highlighting the potential of tumor microenvironment‐directed strategies to overcome resistance to PD‐1 blockade. 141 , 142

Targeting the c‐MET pathway has emerged as a promising strategy, particularly for the aggressive subset of tumors driven by MET gene amplification. ABBV‐400 is an ADC that delivers a potent cytotoxic payload directly to c‐MET–expressing tumor cells, thereby enhancing tumor‐selective activity while limiting damage to healthy tissue. In a phase 1 trial involving 42 heavily pretreated patients, ABBV‐400 achieved an ORR of 29%, including one complete response, demonstrating strong potential as a targeted therapeutic option for this molecularly selected population. 143

For molecularly defined scenarios, the NCCN guidelines suggest considering entrectinib, larotrectinib, or repotrectinib for NTRK fusions; dabrafenib combined with trametinib for BRAF V600E mutations; and selpercatinib for RET fusions. 64 The therapeutic horizon is expanding further to include emerging targets, such as TROP2, as well as next‐generation immunotherapeutic targets, including TIGIT and CD73. 144 , 145 , 146

Conclusions

Driven by advancing insights into the molecular mechanisms of gastric cancer, the diagnostic and therapeutic landscape is undergoing a paradigm shift (Figure 2). First, the incorporation of immunotherapy into the perioperative setting represents a landmark breakthrough. Expanding from the palliative setting to the preoperative and adjuvant settings, the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy has significantly improved pCR rates and may further enhance prognosis and long‐term survival outcomes for patients with locally AGC. The field of targeted therapy is also reaching a pivotal turning point. Positive data from large‐scale clinical trials targeting key pathways, such as CLDN18.2 and VEGFR2, have validated their clinical utility, moving the field beyond the historical reliance on HER2 as the dominant therapeutic target. However, the expanding array of targets and the co‐expression of biomarkers, such as CLDN18.2 and PD‐L1, have complicated decision‐making regarding optimal treatment sequencing and combination strategies. Despite the broadening therapeutic landscape, the long‐term efficacy of these novel agents remains constrained by acquired resistance. Consequently, effectively overcoming resistance mechanisms to targeted therapies and immunotherapies represents a central challenge in translating these innovations into sustained survival benefits. 147 Ultimately, gastric cancer management is evolving toward a personalized treatment era within the framework of precision medicine. This requires not only improving the efficiency of early screening and detection but also moving beyond single‐agent development toward innovative clinical trial designs and extensive international collaboration. By integrating multi‐omics data to refine molecular classification and implementing multidisciplinary team models, precise, whole‐course management strategies can be further established. Furthermore, addressing global disparities in therapeutic accessibility will be essential to achieving the dual goal of maximizing efficacy while minimizing toxicity.
