Tensor cross interpolation for global discrete optimization with application to Bayesian network inference S.D. is supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council New Investigator Award EP/T031255/1.
Sergey Dolgov, Dmitry Savostyanov

Abstract
Global discrete optimization is notoriously difficult due to the lack of gradient information and the curse of dimensionality, making exhaustive search infeasible. Tensor cross approximation is an efficient technique to approximate multivariate tensors (and discretized functions) by tensor product decompositions based on a small number of tensor elements, evaluated on adaptively selected fibers of the tensor, that intersect on submatrices of (nearly) maximum volume. The submatrices of maximum volume are empirically known to contain large elements, hence the entries selected for cross interpolation can also be good candidates for the globally maximal element within the tensor. In this paper we consider evolution of epidemics on networks, and infer the contact network from observations of network nodal states over time. By numerical experiments we demonstrate that the contact network can be inferred accurately by finding the global maximum of the likelihood using tensor cross interpolation. The proposed tensor product approach is flexible and can be applied to global discrete optimization for other problems, e.g. discrete hyperparameter tuning.

Introduction

Global optimization of functions that may have multiple local optima is an infamously challenging problem, particularly in high dimensions.
Continuous global optimization is more researched, and many well–performing methods exist, such as
genetic algorithms
tulu-stga-2004
;
venkatraman-ga-2005
;
weise-2009
,
consensus–based optimization
hajinkim-cbo-2021
;
totzeck-cbo-2022
;
Kalise-CO-2023
;
pareschi-cbo-2023
;
riedl-cbo-saddle-2024
;
riedl-cbo-global-2024
, or
Markov Chain Monte Carlo
jejeva-mcmcopt-2020
;
jejebe-mcmcopt-2021
.
In contrast, discrete global optimization of multivariate functions is short of state of the art methods, for the following two main reasons:

1.

the absence of gradients — and more generally the lack of continuity in the discrete setting, which makes many efficient methods from the list above impossible to apply;

2.

the curse of dimensionality — the search space size grows exponentially with the number of variables, hence a brute force search is unfeasible in more than a dozen of dimensions.

Methods based on the Markov process framework (now over a discrete state set) are one of the few feasible approaches.
However, without gradient enhancements they may converge slowly and get stuck in local optima frequently.

A novel and promising approach to multivariate optimization, and generally to solving problems in high dimensions, is based on tensor product approximations.
To introduce the concept, consider a function
f(\mathrm{x})
defined on a Cartesian product of
N
discrete sets

\mathbb{X}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{X}=:\mathbb{X}^{N}\ni\mathrm{x}.

The function values
f(\mathrm{x})
can be collected into a
N
-dimensional array

\begin{bmatrix}f(\mathrm{x})\end{bmatrix}_{\mathrm{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{N}},

also called a
tensor
.
The tensor has
(\#\mathbb{X})^{N}
entries, making it unfeasible to compute or store for large
N,
which is known as the curse of dimensionality.
However, if the tensor admits a low–rank approximation, it may be possible to compute it efficiently and work with this representation instead.

In this paper we use the Tensor-Train (TT) approximation
osel-tt-2011
, noting that other tensor product formats and algorithms are available, see e.g.
hackbusch-2012
.
Even if the tensor itself is large and inaccessible in full, its TT approximation can be computed efficiently by tensor cross interpolation
ot-ttcross-2010
;
sav-qott-2014
;
ds-parcross-2020
from a number of adaptively selected samples, which shape one–dimensional lines, or
fibers
, in the given tensor.
A fiber is a subset of positions

\mathrm{x}^{(s)}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n}^{(s)},x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{N}),

s=1,\ldots,(\#\mathbb{X})

in the tensor, such that only one index
x_{n}
is varying, and the other
N-1
indices are frozen.
This approach is inspired by alternating least square optimization algorithms used in statistics
harshman-parafac-1970
;
cc-parafac-1970
;
tuckerals-1980
;
zhang-mals-2003
and quantum physics
schollwock-2011
;
ds-amen-2014
;
schollwock-dmrg3s-2015
.
On each step, positions of the next fiber(s) are chosen adaptively in order to maximize the volume (absolute value of the determinant)
gtz-maxvol-1997
;
gostz-maxvol-2010
of the matrix of tensor elements formed by the intersection in each cross.
Maximum–volume submatrices are empirically known to contain large elements, hence the entries selected for cross interpolation can also be good candidates for the globally maximal element within the tensor.
Hence, a natural idea is to approximate a given tensor using tensor cross interpolation algorithm, and look for the maximum (in modulus) value of tensor among the entries chosen by the maximum–volume principle.
Crucially, the number of samples per iteration is linear in
N
and
\#\mathbb{X}
, hence the outlined procedure is computationally feasible and allows a direct search for the maximum among the evaluated samples.
The concrete way to generate fibers may vary.
One option is to apply the so-called
maxvol
iteration algorithm
gostz-maxvol-2010
to reshaped factors (
cores
) of the TT decomposition directly
ot-ttcross-2010
.
Optimization based on this method was used successfully to speed up
the search for molecular docking configurations
ZheOfeKat-ttdock-2013
;
Sulimov-docking-2017
;
sulimov-dock-2019
;
Zheltkov-ttopt-proc-2020
;
Zheltkov-ttopt-2020

and reinforcement learning
scspco-ttopt-2022
.

In this paper we apply a slightly different tensor cross interpolation with a greedy search for fibers
sav-qott-2014
;
ds-parcross-2020
to global discrete optimization which arises in Bayesian inference of a contact network from the observed dynamics of epidemic evolving on this network.
The problem boils down to finding the network with the largest a posteriori estimate, which in absence of prior knowledge is equivalent to maximization of the likelihood function.
Evaluating likelihood for each contact network is rather numerically expensive, since it requires
either solving a high–dimensional system of ODEs, known as Markovian master equation, or
accurately estimating the likelihood by extensive application of the Gillespie’s stochastic simulation algorithm (SSA)
gillespie-ssa-1976
.
Thus, we prefer the greedy cross interpolation instead of the
maxvol
TT cross algorithm
ot-ttcross-2010
, because in our numerical experience the former tends to require fewer samples than the latter.

Although the main contribution of this paper consists in testing the efficiency of the existing method applied to a relatively novel problem, we also suggest, test and evaluate a number of technical improvements to the greedy tensor cross interpolation algorithm
sav-qott-2014
, namely tempering and caching.
Tempering introduces a parameter called temperature, which modifies the likelihood function and changes the high–dimensional landscape for interpolation and optimization.
Caching simply means keeping all evaluated elements of the tensor, even those that are tested but not chosen by the adaptive tensor cross interpolation algorithm, as they may be tested again (and even selected) at later steps of the method.
We demonstrate numerically that both enhancements considerably speed up the global optimization of the likelihood.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
In Sec.
2
we describe the general framework for the Bayesian inference of the contact network from epidemiological data.
In Sec.
3
we present the specific epidemiological model, denoted
\varepsilon
-SIS, that we use for the experiments in this paper.
In Sec.
4
we recall the tensor cross interpolation algorithm from
sav-qott-2014
;
ds-parcross-2020
and state it using notation related to the specific problem considered in this paper to simplify the presentation.
In Sec.
we describe tempering and caching.
In Sec.
we apply the proposed method to Bayesian network inference of a simple contact network. We demonstrate that the tensor cross interpolation can be efficient as a heuristic algorithm for global discrete optimization, and that caching and tempering additionally improve its performance.

2
Bayesian network identification model

Following from our previous work
ds-infer-2024
,
we consider the problem of identifying the network of contacts between nodes (species, individuals, groups, etc.)
from observed discrete states of these nodes at given time points of a continuous–time Markov process.
The network is an unweighted undirected graph

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}),

consisting of a set of nodes

\mathcal{V}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}

and links (or edges)

\mathcal{E}=\{(m,n):\>m\in\mathcal{V},n\in\mathcal{V},\>m\neq n\},

such that

(m,n)\in\mathcal{E}\>\Leftrightarrow\>(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}.

We will say that nodes
m,n\in\mathcal{V}
are connected,

m\sim n,

iff

(m,n)\in\mathcal{E}.

Each node can take one of discrete states,

x_{n}\in\mathbb{X},

e.g. susceptible, infected, recovered.
The state of the whole network is therefore a vector

\mathrm{x}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{N}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{X}^{N}.

We consider a continuous–time Markov jump process on the state space
\Omega=\mathbb{X}^{N},

assuming that the probability to switch from a state
\mathrm{x}
to a state
\mathrm{y}
in a infinitesimal time interval
dt
is
p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\mathcal{G}}dt

with some known transition rate
p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\mathcal{G}}

that depends on the connectivity of the given network
\mathcal{G}
.
For example, the probability of infection spreading between nodes
n
and
m
can be nonzero only if
m\sim n
.
The system dynamics is described by the probabilities of network states

p(\mathrm{x},t|\mathcal{G})=\mathsf{P}(\text{system on a network $\mathcal{G}$ is in state $\mathrm{x}$ at time $t$}).

This probability function satisfies a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs),
known as
Markovian master equation

vankampen-stochastic-1981
;
chen-stoch-sir-2005
,
Chapman
or
forward Kolmogorov equations
:

p^{\prime}(\mathrm{x},t|\mathcal{G})=\sum_{\mathrm{y}\in\mathbb{X}^{N}}\left(p_{\mathrm{y}\to\mathrm{x}}^{\mathcal{G}}\cdot p(\mathrm{y},t|\mathcal{G})-p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\mathcal{G}}\cdot p(\mathrm{x},t|\mathcal{G})\right),\qquad\mathrm{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{N}.

(1)

In practice, we can measure only particular states at particular points in time, which we collect into a dataset

\mathcal{X}=\{t_{k},\mathrm{x}(t_{k})\}_{k=0}^{K}

of samples of the random variable
X(t)
with the probability distribution function
p(\mathrm{x},t|\mathcal{G})
.
We seek the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of the network configuration,

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\text{opt}}&=\arg\max_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{G}),\end{split}

(2)

where the likelihood of the dataset
\mathcal{X}
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{G})&=\mathsf{P}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{G})=\mathsf{P}(X(t_{1})=\mathrm{x}_{1},\cdots,X(t_{K})=\mathrm{x}_{K}|\mathcal{G}),\end{split}

(3)

and
\mathbb{G}
is some prior set of admissible networks, for example, all networks with unweighted edges.
If a uniform prior is used,

\mathcal{G}_{\text{opt}}

is also the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator.
Since the system dynamics is Markovian, the likelihood can be expanded as follows

\begin{split}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{G})&=\mathsf{P}(X(t_{1})=\mathrm{x}_{1}|X(t_{0})=\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathcal{G})\cdots\mathsf{P}(X(t_{K})=\mathrm{x}_{K}|X(t_{K-1})=\mathrm{x}_{K-1},\mathcal{G}),\\
&=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\mathsf{P}(X(t_{k})=\mathrm{x}_{k}|X(t_{k-1})=\mathrm{x}_{k-1},\mathcal{G})}_{\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{x}_{k-1}\to\mathrm{x}_{k}|\mathcal{G})}.\end{split}

(4)

The latter conditional probability is the solution to the master equation (
1
) started from
100\%
probability assigned to
\mathrm{x}_{k-1}
as the initial state, and integrated over
[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
,

\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{x}_{k-1}\to\mathrm{x}_{k}|\mathcal{G})=p(\mathrm{x}_{k},t_{k}|\mathcal{G}),\quad\text{given}\quad p(\mathrm{x},t_{k-1}|\mathcal{G})=\begin{cases}1,&\mathrm{x}=\mathrm{x}_{k-1},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5)

The master equation (
1
) can be solved
by stochastic simulation algorithms
gillespie-ssa-1976
;
Gillespie2001
;
hemberg-perfect-sampling-2007
;
AndersonHigham-MLCME-2012
;
Yates-MLCME-2016
,
or directly, approximating the probability function
p(\mathrm{x},t|\mathcal{G})
for example by sparse grids
hegland-cme-2007
,
adaptive finite state projections
munsky-fsp-2006
;
jahnke-wavelet-cme-2010
;
Cao-FSP-2016
,
radial basis functions
Schuette-RBF-CME-2015
,
neural networks
Khammash-NN-CME-2021
;
Grima-CME-NN-2022
,
and tensor product approximations, such as
canonical polyadic (CP) format
jahnke-cme-2008
;
Ammar-cme-2011
;
hegland-cme-2011
,
and TT format
kkns-cme-2014
;
dkh-cme-2014
;
ds-amen-2014
;
d-tamen-2018
;
Sidje-TT-CME-2017
;
Dinh-QTT-CME-2020
;
Ion-TT-CME-2021
;
Schuette-CME-CO-2016
;
ds-ttsir-2024
.
In this paper we use the latter approach
d-tamen-2018
;
ds-ttsir-2024
since the likelihoods require both
breaking the curse of dimensionality for large
N
, and
estimating even small probabilities accurately, such that the error is below
10^{-3}
–
10^{-4}
.

As a particular model, we consider the so-called
\varepsilon
-SIS process, as shown in Fig.
1
.
Each node can take only one of two states:

x_{n}\in\mathbb{X}=\{\text{susceptible},\text{infected}\}=\{0,1\},

i.e. there is no recovered state.
The system exhibits only two types of transitions:

p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\mathcal{G}}=\begin{cases}p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\text{(inf)}},&\text{if $\exists n\in\mathcal{V},$ such that $\mathrm{y}=\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{e}_{n}$}\qquad\text{(infection);}\\
p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\text{(rec)}},&\text{if $\exists n\in\mathcal{V},$ such that $\mathrm{y}=\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{e}_{n}$}\qquad\text{(recovery);}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(6)

where
\mathrm{e}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
n

For the simplicity sake, recovery rates
p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\text{(rec)}}=\gamma
are considered to be the same for all infected nodes.
The infection rate for a node
n
(nonzero only if
n
is susceptible, i.e.
x_{n}=0
) is set to

p_{\mathrm{x}\to\mathrm{y}}^{\text{(inf)}}=I_{n}(\mathrm{x})\beta+\varepsilon.

Note that it grows with the number of infected neighbouring nodes,

I_{n}(\mathrm{x})=\#\{m\in\mathcal{V}:m\sim n,x_{m}=1\},

at the constant per-contact rate
\beta,

on top of the baseline infection rate
\varepsilon
introduced from outside the network.

With known number of nodes and constant rates
\beta,\gamma,\varepsilon
of the only possible transitions in the
\varepsilon
–SIS process, the last bit of information comes from a contact network
\mathcal{G}.

The unweighted network can be represented by adjacency matrix of edges,
G\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n},
where
g_{m,n}=1
indicates that
m
and
n
are connected, and
g_{m,n}=0
means they are not.
Since the graph
\mathcal{G}
is undirected, its adjacency matrix is symmetric,
G=G^{T}.

Without loss of generality we take
g_{n,n}=0.

Under these assumptions the graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined uniquely by the elements of the adjacency matrix
G
above the diagonal.
We will collect the
\tfrac{1}{2}N(N-1)
elements from the upper triangular part of
G
and stretch them in a single
adjacency vector

\mathbf{\hat{g}}\in\{0,1\}^{d},
where
d:=\tfrac{1}{2}N(N-1),
and the elements are arranged as follows

\mathbf{\hat{g}}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{g}_{1}&\ldots&\hat{g}_{d}\end{pmatrix}^{T}:=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1,2}&g_{1,3}&g_{2,3}&\ldots&g_{N-1,N}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\in\{0,1\}^{d}.

(7)

Since
\mathcal{G}\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{\hat{g}},
the likelihood (
3
) can also be seen as a function of the adjacency vector,

\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{G})=\mathsf{L}(\mathbf{\hat{g}}):\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},

and the array of all likelihoods

\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{G})\end{bmatrix}_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{G}}

can be seen as a
2\times 2\times\cdots\times 2
tensor with
d
binary indices.

4
Tensor Train cross approximation and optimization

All possible likelihood values
\mathsf{L}(\mathbf{\hat{g}})
for all possible combinations of
\mathbf{\hat{g}}
can be collected into a tensor of order
d
, with elements

\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\ldots,\hat{g}_{d})=\mathsf{L}(\mathbf{\hat{g}}).

In this section we consider low–rank cross approximation methods for approximation and optimization of this tensor.

4.1
Matrix cross interpolation

Consider first only two variables
\mathbf{\hat{g}}=(\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2})
, such that the likelihood tensor is a matrix with elements
\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2})
.
To introduce a nontrivial low–rank matrix approximation (which will be necessary in higher-dimensional case), let us relax the assumption
\mathbf{\hat{g}}\in\{0,1\}^{2}
for the moment, and consider
\mathbf{\hat{g}}\in\{1,\ldots,\hat{M}\}\times\{1,\ldots,\hat{N}\}.

If the matrix
\mathsf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{M}\times\hat{N}}
has rank
r\leqslant\min(\hat{M},\hat{N})
, it can be interpolated exactly via any
r
of its linearly independent rows and columns,

\begin{split}\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2})=\tilde{\mathsf{L}}(\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2})&:=\sum_{s=1}^{r}\sum_{t=1}^{r}\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}^{(t)})[\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})]_{t,s}^{-1}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{(s)},\hat{g}_{2})\\
&=\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})[\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})]^{-1}\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\hat{g}_{2}),\end{split}

(8)

where
\mathcal{I}_{1}\subset\{1,\ldots,\hat{M}\}
is a set of row indices and
\mathcal{I}_{2}\subset\{1,\ldots,\hat{N}\}
is a set of column indices.
In general, one can aim at approximating
\tilde{\mathsf{L}}\approx\mathsf{L}
.
The approximation (
8
) is still exact on the positions of the computed rows
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and columns
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, which together form a cross of elements of
\mathsf{L}
, hence the name
cross interpolation
.
However, to minimize the error in the other elements
it becomes crucial to select
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}
that draw not just linearly independent, but as well-conditioned as possible rows and columns.
Theoretical guarantees are available if
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}
are chosen such that the intersection matrix has maximum
volume
,

|\det\mathsf{L}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})|\to\max

over all possible
\mathcal{I}_{1}\subset\{1,\ldots,\hat{M}\}

\mathcal{I}_{2}\subset\{1,\ldots,\hat{N}\}
of size

gtz-maxvol-1997
;

In practice, cheaper heuristic algorithms are used.

\mathsf{L}

The greedy algorithm for the matrix cross interpolation that we use in this paper can be seen as a version of the
adaptive cross approximation

4.2
TT cross interpolation algorithm

Matrix methods can be applied to tensors reshaped to matrices.
We introduce

\mathsf{L}^{\{k\}}(\hat{g}_{\leqslant k},\hat{g}_{>k})=\mathsf{L}^{\{k\}}(\hat{g}_{1}\hat{g}_{2}\ldots\hat{g}_{k};\hat{g}_{k+1}\ldots\hat{g}_{d})=\mathsf{L}(\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2},\ldots,\hat{g}_{d}),

\begin{split}\mathsf{L}^{\{k\}}(\hat{g}_{\leqslant k},\hat{g}_{>k})&\approx\tilde{\mathsf{L}}^{\{k\}}(\hat{g}_{\leqslant k},\hat{g}_{>k})\\

For example, for

Without a better initial guess, we can initialise it at random.
The left factor

This sets the base for a recursive factorization of
