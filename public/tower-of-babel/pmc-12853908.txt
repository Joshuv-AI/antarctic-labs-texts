Cold climate dual threats: lagged and nonlinear effects of air pollution and meteorological extremes on acute myocardial infarction risk
Authors: Hongxin Xu, Leilei Yin, Yingtao Zhang, Haiyu Zhang, Hongyan Zhao, Kai Du, Minghui Li, Danyang Yu, Kai Dong, Sizheng Peng, Yuhui Sun, Tao Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a major health concern in cold regions, where extreme meteorological conditions and air pollution can jointly elevate cardiovascular risk. However, the nonlinear and delayed impacts of diverse environmental stressors on different AMI subtypes remain insufficiently understood.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective time-series study including daily ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI, ST refers to the ST segment on electrocardiograms) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) cases recorded in Heilongjiang Province, China from 2014-2023. Environmental exposures included key meteorological indicators (mean air temperature, surface temperature, snow depth, atmospheric pressure, sunshine duration, and ultraviolet intensity) and major air pollutants (PM, PM, NO, SO). Extreme cold and heat were defined primarily using mean air temperature thresholds based on regional climatic characteristics (<-20C and >30C), while the full spectrum of non-extreme weather conditions was also assessed. Distributed Lag Nonlinear Models (DLNMs) were used to characterize nonlinear and lag-response relationships.ResultsSTEMI risk significantly increased under stress, with acute effects observed at extremely low (<-20C) and high (>30C) mean air temperatures. NSTEMI showed a more gradual response, particularly to sustained heat exposure. Short-term PM exposure had a stronger effect on STEMI, whereas NO was more strongly associated with delayed NSTEMI risk. Atmospheric pressure between 990-1010 hPa elevated risk, while greater snow depth appeared inverse association. Non-linear patterns were also noted for sunshine duration and ultraviolet intensity.ConclusionIn cold-climate regions, both meteorological extremes and air pollution substantially affect AMI, but with distinct temporal and exposure-response characteristics between STEMI and NSTEMI. These findings highlight the need for subtype-specific environmental early warning systems and climate-adaptive cardiovascular prevention strategies.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Meteorological extremes, Air pollution, Distributed lag nonlinear models, STEMI, NSTEMI

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) continues to be a leading cause of morbidity and mortality on a global scale. In colder regions, variations in meteorological conditions and heightened levels of air pollution can significantly influence cardiovascular events [1]. Exposure to cold triggers both systemic and localized inflammatory responses, which can destabilize atherosclerotic plaques and increase the risk of rupture, thereby elevating the incidence of AMI [2]. Temperature changes directly affect the body’s physiological functions, and both low and high temperatures have adverse effects on AMI hospital admissions [3]. Additionally, winter months in urbanized areas are characterized by increased air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM, PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO), and Sulfur dioxide (SO), which contribute to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation—further exacerbating cardiovascular risk [4, 5]. PM and PM refer to inhalable particulate matter with aerodynamic diameters of <2.5 um and <10 um, respectively, which differ in their penetration depth and impacts on the cardiovascular system.

To better characterize cold-climate environments, snow depth and surface pressure were also included in this study. Snow accumulation may alter individual outdoor behaviors and cold exposure patterns, while atmospheric pressure fluctuations can influence blood viscosity, coronary perfusion, and autonomic regulation—potentially promoting acute cardiovascular events [6, 7]. Initially, a broader set of meteorological indicators such as humidity and wind speed was considered. However, correlation screening revealed strong multicollinearity with existing variables and limited added explanatory value in cold regions. Therefore, the final selection of environmental exposures was guided by prior evidence and model performance considerations.

Heilongjiang Province in Northeast China, located near 45N latitude, represents one of the world’s most severely cold inhabited regions. Winters are long, dry, and characterized by high atmospheric pressure and minimum temperatures often below −30C. The six-month heating season results in considerable emissions of inhalable particulate matter and other pollutants. Utilizing a decade of AMI hospitalization records from the largest tertiary hospital in this province and meteorological data during the same period, this study provides a rare opportunity to analyze environment-cardiovascular associations in an extreme cold setting. Although studies in Nordic and Canadian regions have also demonstrated heightened cardiovascular vulnerability during severe cold events, most have focused on total AMI or mortality rather than on subtype-specific response profiles [8–10]. Evidence quantifying nonlinear and lagged effects of meteorological extremes and pollution mixtures remains limited in low-resource, cold regions such as Northeast China.

We will systematically analyze the non-linear and lagged effects of key environmental variables, including temperature, snow depth, surface pressure, sunshine duration and ultraviolet intensity as well as quantify the influence of air pollutants(PM, PM, NO and SO) on AMI risk. By integrating high-resolution environmental data with clinical outcomes, we seek to elucidate the complex interactions and temporal dynamics governing these associations.

A deeper understanding of these relationships is crucial for developing climate-adaptive cardiovascular prevention strategies, improving healthcare resource allocation during critical weather periods, and guiding environmental health policies in high-latitude regions. These insights may also contribute to global initiatives aimed at mitigating cardiovascular risks in the context of climate change.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This retrospective study analyzed patients diagnosed with AMI at the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University between 2014 and 2023. AMI cases were identified based on the 10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) coded as I21.0–I21.3 and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) as I21.4. AMI cases coded as I21.9 (Acute myocardial infarction, unspecified), totaling 2,281 cases and accounting for only 5.5% of all AMI cases, were excluded from the analysis, as these cases typically could not be definitively classified as either STEMI or NSTEMI and therefore could not be assigned to any clinical subtype for the DLNM analysis. Patient data were retrieved from the hospital’s Chest Pain Center according to these ICD codes. Clinical information was collected in accordance with the Chinese Society of Cardiology guidelines, with all diagnoses confirmed by cardiology specialists based on clinical symptoms, electrocardiograms, and laboratory findings.

Data processing and exclusion criteria

To ensure data accuracy and reliability, cases were excluded if they had missing demographic or clinical information, resided outside Heilongjiang Province, were transferred from other hospitals, or lacked recorded symptom onset times. Hospitalization records included key demographic and clinical variables such as patient ID numbers, registration numbers, sex, age, identification number, residence, admission and discharge dates, mortality status, confirmed diagnosis, and comorbidities. All personally identifiable information was anonymized, and data processing adhered to stringent privacy and ethical standards. The final dataset comprised daily AMI hospitalization counts, structured into a time-series database for subsequent statistical analysis.

Data categorization and analysis

Based on established epidemiological research and cardiovascular guideline criteria, the study population was stratified into several predefined subgroups to explore potential effect modifications [11]. These subgroups included sex (male, female), age categories (65 years, <65 years), place of residence, mortality status, seasonality of onset (heating, non-heating season), and the presence of major cardiometabolic comorbidities. The selection of these subgrouping variables reflects their recognized relevance to AMI susceptibility. The widely used age cut-off of 65 years enables the distinction of older individuals at higher cardiovascular risk from the general adult population. Further stratification into narrower age ranges was considered, however, due to sample size constraints and uneven subgroup distributions, we adopted this binary approach to ensure statistical power and model stability. Meteorological and air pollution data were obtained from the China Integrated Meteorological Information Service System (CIMISS), managed by the China Meteorological Data Service Center. These data were collected from 54 national meteorological monitoring stations across Heilongjiang Province and averaged to generate representative environmental exposure values for the study region, ensuring comprehensive assessment. To ensure the reliability of environmental exposure metrics, standardized national quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures were applied across all stations. A completeness check was conducted, and days with more than 25% missing data across monitoring stations were excluded. Outlier detection was performed based on established environmental plausibility ranges and interquartile range (IQR) thresholds, with measurement-error–related extreme values corrected or removed when necessary. To minimize spatial instability and reduce the influence of anomalies from a single station, daily exposure values were aggregated using multi-station means to represent the environmental conditions of the study region. Meteorological data indicators

Therefore, extreme temperature events were identified using regional climatological criteria provided by the Heilongjiang Provincial Meteorological Bureau. Specifically, extreme low temperature events were defined as daily mean temperatures below the 5th percentile of the long-term distribution (approximately C), while extreme high temperature events were defined as daily mean temperatures above the 95th percentile (C). These definitions were applied consistently in the analysis of both STEMI and NSTEMI. This approach ensured that the classification of extreme weather events reflected meaningful and locally relevant environmental exposure conditions. Mean air temperature is the most commonly used meteorological indicator in cardiovascular epidemiology, as it reflects the overall level of ambient thermal stress. In contrast, surface temperature better represents the thermal conditions at ground level where human exposure actually occurs. This distinction is particularly relevant in cold regions, where factors such as snow cover or ground heating can markedly alter surface thermal environments and thereby influence actual exposure. Therefore, to comprehensively capture temperature-related exposures across different environmental layers, we initially included both variables in our modeling framework.

To facilitate subsequent analyses, we leveraged the geocoding API from Baidu Maps to transform patients’ current residential addresses into geographical coordinates (longitude and latitude), enabling precise localization. Then, to assess meteorological and air pollutant exposures near patients’ homes, we applied the Haversine formula. This formula calculates distances between two points on a sphere based on their geographical coordinates and helped us identify the closest meteorological monitoring station to each patient’s residence. We used this nearest station as the source of meteorological and air pollutant exposure data for the patient. With this method, we combined historical data from meteorological stations to match each patient with precise meteorological and air pollutant exposure information.

Research methods

This study employed DLNM [12] to simultaneously account for the non-linear effects and lagged responses of exposure variables [13, 14]. To identify relevant meteorological factors influencing AMI incidence, we performed a Spearman rank correlation analysis to examine the relationship between daily AMI cases and meteorological variables, while addressing potential collinearity among meteorological indicators [15]. Based on this analysis, we selected the most relevant meteorological factors for inclusion in the model.

In the model, meteorological factors were used as independent variables, and daily AMI incidence was set as the dependent variable, while adjusting for potential confounders such as holiday effects and long-term trends [14]. The degrees of freedom and lag duration were determined using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and references to relevant literature. Ultimately, the maximum lag period was set at 30 days [16]. Results

The time series distribution of air pollutants and meteorological factors from 2014 to 2023 is displayed in Fig. 1. The air quality index (AQI) and major air pollutants, including PM, PM, SO, and NO, exhibit significant temporal variations with recurrent seasonal peaks. The AQI and PM concentrations display periodic surges, particularly during winter months, suggesting the influence of seasonal meteorological conditions such as lower temperatures and stagnant atmospheric conditions that favor pollutant accumulation. Similarly, PM and gaseous pollutants (SO and NO) also show fluctuations over time, with occasional extreme peaks likely associated with industrial emissions, traffic-related pollution, and meteorological anomalies. NO levels exhibit more stable trends compared to SO, which demonstrates sporadic spikes, possibly due to specific industrial activities or atmospheric dispersion variations. Meteorological factors, including surface temperature, mean air temperature, Sunshine duration, and Ultraviolet intensity, reveal strong seasonal cycles, with higher values during summer and lower values in winter. In Heilongjiang Province, the massive amount of snowfall during winter results in extremely deep snow depth. Surface pressure displays relatively stable patterns but with slight fluctuations over time. The observed temporal trends suggest potential interactions between air pollutants and meteorological variables, which may contribute to variations in the incidence of AMI. The seasonal and long-term trends in pollution levels align with fluctuations in temperature and solar radiation, indicating that meteorological conditions may modulate pollution exposure and its potential health effects.

STEMI and NSTEMI displayed distinct risk patterns in response to inhalable particulate matter exposure (Fig. 2). The 3D graph delineates the single-point lag effect subsequent to exposure, whereas the line chart elucidates the cumulative lag effect. For STEMI, RR remained low at mild pollution but surged above 1.6 when PM exceeded 400 g/m3, peaking at certain lag periods. Initially, a transient association was observed, but cumulative exposure led to an exponential increase in risk, with RR spiking sharply beyond 200 g/m3. In contrast, NSTEMI risk remained stable below 500 g/m3 but fluctuated at higher levels, reaching its peak at 10–20 days (RR 1.6), suggesting a delayed effect. These findings highlight the stronger and more prolonged influence of PM on STEMI compared to NSTEMI. Similarly, PM10 exposure also led to distinct risk patterns for both conditions. At low concentrations (<500 g/m3), both showed minimal risk. For STEMI, RR gradually increased between 500–1500 g/m3, peaking at specific lags before declining. Notably, risk was highest around 1000 g/m3 but unexpectedly dropped above 1500 g/m3, suggesting adaptation or environmental interactions. Similarly, NSTEMI risk rose with PM levels, peaking at 1000–1500 g/m3, with a weaker cumulative effect than STEMI. However, at extreme concentrations (>2000 g/m3), RR declined, mirroring STEMI’s nonlinear trend. Widened confidence intervals at high pollution levels indicated greater uncertainty in risk estimates.

The impact of inhalable particulate matter on STEMI and NSTEMI

In contrast, both conditions exhibited similar nonlinear risk patterns when exposed to gaseous pollutants (Fig. 3). At low concentrations (<100 g/m3), both showed minimal risk, but RR peaked sharply at 150–200 g/m3, indicating a critical threshold. Beyond 300 g/m3, RR declined. STEMI showed a pronounced lag effect, with risk persisting up to 20 days before decreasing, while NSTEMI had a stronger cumulative effect within the 150–200 g/m3 range. Wider confidence intervals at high SO levels indicated greater uncertainty in estimates. For NO2 exposure, STEMI and NSTEMI followed comparable trends, showing minimal risk at lower concentrations (<50 g/m3). Risk increased gradually, peaking at 50–100 g/m3, where both conditions saw sharp rises in RR, indicating that moderate NO levels are critical for acute onset. Beyond this peak, RR declined at higher concentrations (>200 g/m3), suggesting a threshold effect or physiological adaptation. Cumulative analysis confirmed that moderate exposure (80–120 g/m3) had the most pronounced effect, with a declining risk at extremely high concentrations. However, NSTEMI exhibited greater sensitivity to NO, with a stronger cumulative risk increase at moderate pollution levels compared to STEMI.

The impact of gaseous pollutants on STEMI and NSTEMI

The effects of surface temperature and mean air temperature on STEMI and NSTEMI were nonlinear (Fig. 4), though key differences emerged in their respective impacts. For STEMI, surface temperature as well as mean air temperature had a minimal effect within the −20C to 20C range, with relative risks (RR) remaining stable (0.90–1.10). However, at extreme temperatures (< −20C or > 30C), RR increased, suggesting temperature extremes influence STEMI risk. Specifically for NSTEMI, both temperature metrics demonstrated a consistent pattern: lower temperatures had little influence, whereas warmer conditions (above 20C) significantly elevated RR. Cumulative analysis revealed that mean air temperature had a stronger long-term effect, especially at high temperatures, where RR continued to rise, while surface temperature had a more stable, less pronounced impact. Compared to STEMI, NSTEMI showed a more gradual response to temperature changes, with cold exposure having a weaker effect.

The impact of temperature on STEMI and NSTEMI

We have further investigated the impact of extreme weather events on STEMI and NSTEMI (Fig. 5). Regarding atmospheric pressure, both STEMI and NSTEMI showed a similar trend, with low pressure (<990 hPa) associated with a reduced risk. As pressure increased, the RR gradually increased (RR >1.2). However, at pressures exceeding 1020 hPa, RR declined, indicating that very high atmospheric pressure may not continuously elevate risk. Cumulative effect analysis showed a gradual increase in RR within the 960–1000hPa range, followed by a decline at higher pressures. A comparable pattern was observed with snow depth, where minimal snow cover (<10mm) had little effect on the risk of either condition (RR close to 1). As snow depth increased, RR gradually declined, reaching about 0.5 at depths exceeding 30 mm, suggesting a potential favorable association of deeper snow accumulation. This declining trend remained stable across different lag periods, indicating that the effect is sustained rather than a short-term trigger. Cumulative effect analysis confirmed this pattern, with the lowest RR values observed at snow depths of 40–50 mm or more.

The impact of extreme weather events on STEMI and NSTEMI

Additionally, both conditions exhibited a nonlinear relationship with solar radiation (Fig. 6). When sunshine duration was short (<3 hours), the relative risk (RR) of both conditions remained low. As sunshine duration increased, RR gradually rose, peaking at 5–7 hours, suggesting moderate sunshine exposure may contribute to both STEMI and NSTEMI risk. However, beyond 7 hours of sunshine, RR declined, indicating that prolonged exposure may not continuously elevate risk and could exert a favorable association. This trend was more pronounced for NSTEMI, which showed a more stable risk pattern across sunshine durations, suggesting better adaptation to sunlight variations. Similarly, STEMI and NSTEMI displayed a comparable nonlinear response to ultraviolet (UV) intensity. At low UV intensity (<500,000 J/m2), the RR for both conditions remained low, suggesting that reduced UV exposure may lower risk. As UV intensity increased, RR gradually rose, peaking at moderate levels, indicating that moderate UV exposure may slightly influence both STEMI and NSTEMI risk. However, when UV intensity exceeded 2,000,000 J/m2, RR declined, implying that extreme UV exposure may not continuously elevate risk.

The impact of extreme weather events on STEMI and NSTEMI

Notably, subgroup analyses were performed based on various factors, including age, sex, comorbidities (such as diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia), mortality status, urban versus rural residency, and whether the individuals were in the heating season. These analyses aimed to explore potential subgroup differences and effect modifications. However, no statistically significant differences were identified.

Discussion

This study utilized the DLNM to systematically investigate the effects of air pollution and meteorological factors on the incidence and prognosis of AMI in cold regions, with a particular focus on STEMI and NSTEMI under varying environmental exposures. The findings indicate that both air pollution and extreme meteorological conditions significantly contribute to AMI occurrence, with distinct exposure-response patterns for STEMI and NSTEMI. Notably, their risk trajectories diverged under high pollution levels or extreme weather events, highlighting the heterogeneous impact of environmental stressors on AMI subtypes.

Espite no significant statistical differences found in our subgroup analyses (e.g., by age, sex, comorbidities, whether dead or not, whether it is the heating period or not), this likely stems from sample size limits, small exposure differences between subgroups, or insufficient model power. Although demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, and comorbidity status can influence individual vulnerability to environmental exposures, several factors may help explain the lack of statistically significant subgroup differences observed in this study. First, the overall exposure contrasts between subgroups were relatively small within the cold-climate setting of Heilongjiang Province, where most residents experience sustained and intense winter conditions. This shared environmental context may lead to broadly similar physiological stress responses, thereby reducing detectable heterogeneity across subgroups. Second, the cold-related cardiovascular mechanisms—such as sympathetic activation, vasoconstriction, and increased blood viscosity—tend to operate at a population-wide level rather than being highly subgroup-specific. Third, the statistical power to detect subtle effect modification may have been limited, particularly in subgroups with smaller sample sizes. These findings are consistent with previous research conducted in other cold regions, which has similarly reported modest or non-significant subgroup variation in the associations between meteorological factors, air pollution, and acute cardiovascular events [18, 19]. Together, this evidence suggests that in severely cold environments, extreme weather and pollution exposures may exert a pervasive cardiovascular burden across the population, resulting in relatively uniform risk patterns among different demographic and clinical subgroups.

The impact of air pollutants on STEMI and NSTEMI

In this study, we observed a notable impact of PM on STEMI. However, NSTEMI appears to be more strongly associated with long-term exposure to PM. Even at relatively low concentrations of PM, chronic cumulative exposure may contribute to a gradual increase in the risk of NSTEMI. This suggests that the pathogenesis of NSTEMI may be more closely related to chronic inflammation and sustained vascular endothelial damage. This difference may stem from PM-induced acute inflammatory responses and vascular constriction [20], making STEMI patients more susceptible to short-term exposure, whereas NSTEMI may be driven by prolonged low-level exposure. PM exposure also significantly impacted AMI risk, with the strongest effects observed at concentrations between 500–1000 g/m3, suggesting a critical threshold for AMI risk. However, at PM levels exceeding 1000 g/m3, RR declined, indicating potential adaptive or defensive mechanisms, such as enhanced airway defenses or reduced outdoor exposure. A large-scale population cohort study (USA) reported the association between long-term average PM exposure and ischemic heart disease (IHD) as well as overall cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. The study found that for each 10 g/m3 increase in PM, the risk of IHD mortality (and cardiovascular mortality) significantly increased [21], which is consistent with our findings. In addition, a study employing a time-stratified case-crossover design to assess the association between short-term PM exposure and AMI hospitalization also found a significant correlation between PM exposure and STEMI hospitalizations [22]. Inhalable particulate matter may increase the risk of cardiovascular disease mortality through mechanisms such as pulmonary and systemic inflammation, accelerated atherosclerosis, and altered cardiac autonomic function [23–25]. PM may trigger myocardial infarction by affecting hemodynamics and blood coagulation mechanisms. Specific mechanisms include increasing blood viscosity, accelerating heart rate, reducing heart rate variability, inducing ventricular fibrillation, and increasing the frequency of therapeutic interventions in patients with implanted cardioverter-defibrillators [26].

Among gaseous pollutants, SO and NO were strongly associated with elevated AMI risk, particularly during winter heating periods, when their concentrations were highest. Given that the primary sources of SO and NO are traffic-related pollution, chronic exposure may lead to endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammation, increasing cardiovascular risk [27]. After inhaling NO, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses occur in the lungs. These inflammatory responses may further lead to a systemic inflammatory state, accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis [28]. Previous studies have demonstrated that traffic-related air pollutants, particularly NO, may significantly increase the risk of myocardial infarction within a few hours of exposure, highlighting the acute impact of short-term traffic pollution on cardiovascular health [29]. This supports the notion that living near major roads and high-traffic areas may have adverse effects on cardiovascular health, which is consistent with our findings. Other studies have also shown that short-term exposure to NO is significantly associated with cause-specific mortality across multiple regions, particularly at different lag periods, highlighting the acute harm NO poses to cardiovascular and respiratory health [30]. Additionally, SO has been found to be significantly associated with an increased risk of AMI on the same day [31]. Our study also exhibits a similar short-term lag effect. SO may affect macrophage function as well as alveolar clearance and mucus transport capacity, thereby contributing to the occurrence of AMI [32, 33].

The impact of meteorological factors on STEMI and NSTEMI

Regarding meteorological influences, this study identified a U-shaped relationship between mean air temperature and STEMI risk, where in both extreme high and low temperatures increased STEMI incidence. However, the risk response patterns varied in NSTEMI: STEMI patients were more susceptible to temperature fluctuations, particularly at high temperatures, where RR increased significantly. This may be due to heat stress-induced vasodilation, dehydration, and increased blood viscosity [34]. In contrast, NSTEMI patients exhibited a more limited risk increase under cold conditions, suggesting that cold exposure may have a weaker impact on NSTEMI. This discrepancy could stem from the fact that acute physiological stressors like sympathetic activation and blood pressure fluctuations caused by cold exposure are more likely to trigger STEMI [35]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that, in addition to air quality, meteorological factors such as temperature fluctuations (both extreme cold and extreme heat) can trigger AMI [36]. Additionally, the risk of hospitalization for cardiovascular disease significantly increases under extreme temperature conditions, particularly during short periods of rapid temperature fluctuations [3]. These findings are consistent with our study.The potential mechanism may be that Cold and high temperatures can lead to increased blood pressure, elevated cholesterol levels, accelerated heart rate, increased plasma fibrinogen concentration, enhanced platelet viscosity, and peripheral vasoconstriction, these physiological changes place additional stress on the heart, thereby increasing the incidence of ischemic heart disease events [37].

Atmospheric pressure fluctuations also influenced AMI risk. This pattern suggests that pressure variations may induce cardiovascular stress responses, influencing AMI occurrence. Increased snow depth was associated with reduced AMI risk, particularly when snow depth exceeded 30 mm, leading to a downward trend in both STEMI and NSTEMI incidence. This could be attributed to reduced outdoor activity, lower exposure to cold and pollution, or the stabilizing effect of snow on environmental conditions.

Previous studies have indicated that the relationship between sunlight duration and AMI hospitalizations follows a U-shaped curve, suggesting that both excessively short and long sunlight exposure can increase the risk of AMI [38]. While our findings share similarities with this pattern, there are slight differences, which may be influenced by factors such as geographic location, seasonal variations, or population behaviors (e.g., outdoor activity habits). Further research is needed to account for potential confounding factors. UV intensity had a relatively minor effect on AMI, but RR slightly increased at moderate UV levels before declining at higher exposures. This suggests that UV exposure may influence AMI through immune regulation or vascular function modulation.

Public health implications

Based on this study’s precise quantification of the lagged and nonlinear effects of air pollution and meteorological factors, public health interventions can be optimized by targeting critical risk thresholds and time windows [39]. On extreme pollution days, a regional cardiovascular health early warning system should be activated [40]. Regarding meteorological risks, when the temperature plummets below - 20C or soars above 30C, the emergency medical system needs to enhance resource allocation for STEMI treatment within 48 hours (e.g., increasing catheterization laboratory on - call shifts). For sustained moderate pollution, long - term protective education should be implemented for high - risk NSTEMI populations, emphasizing the use of home air monitoring devices. At the healthcare system preparedness level, the lagged effect revealed by the study requires hospitals to dynamically adjust cardiology bed capacity and thrombolytic drug reserves in the third week following severe pollution events, particularly during the winter heating season [41]. Observations of snow depth > 30 mm show a favorable association, which can be used to collaborate with meteorological departments to develop a snow depth index. This can guide communities in cold regions to strengthen health checks for the elderly during periods of low snowfall. In addition, early warning signals of air pressure fluctuations between 990 - 1010 hPa should be linked with the emergency medical.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, causal relationships cannot be fully established and residual confounding may remain. Second, exposure assessment was based on fixed-site monitoring data at the population level, which may introduce exposure misclassification, as actual exposure can vary with individual indoor–outdoor activity patterns, ventilation and heating conditions, and personal protective behaviors. Third, some subgroup analyses did not show statistically significant effect modification, which may be partly due to limited sample sizes and insufficient statistical power, rather than a true absence of heterogeneity. Fourth, due to the restricted data availability, we were unable to perform more extensive and deeper sensitivity analyses to fully address potential behavioral confounding. Finally, the findings are derived from a single cold-region province, which may limit their generalizability to other regions with different climates, pollution characteristics, and population structures.

Future research should adopt higher-resolution exposure modeling approaches (e.g., satellite-based estimates, land-use regression) and, where feasible, integrate personal exposure monitoring combined with time–activity information to improve exposure accuracy and strengthen causal inference. Expanding study populations and establishing multicenter collaborations are also needed to enhance statistical power, validate subgroup findings, and further explore underlying biological mechanisms. Moreover, biomarker-based investigations and experimental or translational studies focusing on inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and autonomic regulation will be essential to provide direct mechanistic evidence. Continued enhancement of air quality policies and interdisciplinary collaboration will remain crucial to mitigating environmental risks to cardiovascular health.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the incidence of AMI in cold regions is influenced by subtype-specific environmental pathophysiology. Specifically, STEMI exhibits acute sensitivity to short-term pollution spikes and thermal shocks. In contrast, NSTEMI is associated with cumulative damage resulting from prolonged exposure to pollutants and meteorological stressors. Importantly, we identify nonlinear risk thresholds that exceed current regulatory standards and illustrate how favorable association factors, such as snow cover and optimal sunlight, modulate these relationships. The distinct lag structures—acute for STEMI and protracted for NSTEMI—highlight the different biological mechanisms underlying environmental triggers. These findings underscore the need for climate-adapted cardiovascular prevention strategies, including dynamic early-warning systems tailored to subtype vulnerabilities and healthcare resource planning that aligns with exposure-delayed risk peaks. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanistic pathways specific to each subtype and validating intervention frameworks across various climatic regions.
