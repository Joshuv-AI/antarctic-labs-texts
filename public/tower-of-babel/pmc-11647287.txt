Knowledge about diabetic retinopathy, eye check-up service utilisation and associated factors among adult diabetic patients at public hospitals of southeastern Ethiopia, 2022
Authors: Abdulaziz Temam Ahmed, Muhammadawwal Abduku, Ahmednur Adem Aliyi, Anwar Tahir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe objective of the study is to assess knowledge of diabetic retinopathy, eye check-up service utilisation and associated factors among adult diabetic patients at public hospitals in southeastern Ethiopia in 2022.SettingThe study was conducted at Ginnir General and Goba Referral Hospital in southeast Ethiopia.DesignThe study was designed as a facility-based cross-sectional study.ParticipantsSystematically selected adult diabetic patients were the participants of the study.Primary outcomeThe primary outcomes are knowledge about diabetic retinopathy and eye check-up service utilisation.ResultsOf the 422 samples, 401 study participants participated with a 95% response rate. 254 (63.3%) (95% CI 58.5, 68%) and 37% (95% CI 32.5, 42%) of the participants had good knowledge of diabetic retinopathy and good uptake of eye check-up service, respectively. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), the leading cause of blindness in adult diabetic patients, is a microvascular complication caused by pathological changes in the retinal microvasculature accompanied by structural changes in the retina due to persistent poor glycaemic control.1 The global prevalence of DR in adult diabetic patients is estimated at 27%, leading to blindness in up to 4.8% of cases.2 The International Diabetic Federation recognises DR as a major eye disease for which there is a need to address the knowledge gap in adult diabetics, close the gap in eye check-up service use, ensure positive attitudes and gain urgent attention from professionals and governments.3

In addition, the recommended clinical guidelines of the American Academy of Ophthalmology recommend that every person with diabetes should have an eye examination at least once a year to prevent, detect early and treat diabetes-related vision problems promptly.4 In Africa, the overall prevalence of DR in adult diabetics was reported to be 31.6%.5 In addition, studies conducted in African countries (eg, a study in South Africa6 and Ethiopia7) reported that the proportion of eye check-up services utilisation in adult diabetics was 49% and 31.5%, respectively. Ethiopia remains one of the top five African diabetic countries,8 and the national prevalence of DR among adult diabetics was reported to be 34.1%.9

The global proportion of knowledge about DR and uses of eye check-up service utilisation varies in different studies. Facility-based cross-sectional studies conducted in Asian countries (eg, Bangladesh, India and Saudi Arabia) have shown that the proportion of knowledge about DR among adult diabetic patients ranges from 15%,10 40%11 to 63.5%.12

In addition, the proportion of knowledge about DR varies in different studies in African countries. For example, a study conducted in Nigeria found that the proportion of knowledge about DR was 29.7%.13 A recent study conducted in Ethiopia found that the level of knowledge about DR among adult diabetic patients was 47.4%.14

In Ethiopia, the level of knowledge about DR among diabetic patients was 47.4%, and the utilisation of eye examinations was 39.6%.14 Another study conducted in northeastern Ethiopia found that the proportion of utilisation of eye examinations among adult diabetic patients was 31.5%.7 Various studies conducted on factors associated with knowledge of DR and utilisation of eye examination services among adult diabetic patients show conflicting results regarding the extent of knowledge of DR and utilisation of preventive measures.914,16 Despite these reasons, previous studies had several limitations: they were conducted in a single study area with a small sample size, and attitudes towards preventive measures for DR were not considered. These reasons mandate setting specific evidence generations.

Therefore, this study assessed the knowledge level about DR, eye check-up service utilisation and associated factors among adult diabetes patients attending diabetic chronic follow-up units at public hospitals of Bale and East Bale zones, southeast Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area, design and period

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among diabetic patients from three randomly selected public hospitals of Southeast Ethiopia, namely, Ginnir General Hospital, Delomena General Hospital and Goba Referral Hospital, from 1 April to 01 May 2022. Diabetic patients receive care in the hospital setting. These hospitals are providing both primary and secondary care in their respective wide catchment area.

Population

All adult diabetic patients who came to chronic follow-up units of public hospitals of Bale and East Bale zones in Southeast Ethiopia were the source population for this study. All adult diabetic patients who came to chronic follow-up units, who were willing to participate and who were available during data collection were included. Adult diabetic patients with a psychiatric disorder, who could not give information (could not read and had a hearing problem) and with gestational diabetes were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The sample size was calculated by using a single population proportion formula for sample size calculation. Accordingly, taking the proportion of knowledge of DR and eye check-up service utilisation was found 47% with a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 5% and a non-response rate of 10%, resulting in the final sample size of 422.

To select study participants from all diabetic patients eligible for the study, a systematic random sampling procedure was used after determining the omission interval (k) for each hospital. Thus, the k-interval was calculated by dividing the number of baseline populations by the proportionally allocated sample size, which resulted in 347/212 equal to ‘2’ for Goba Referral Hospital (GRH), 288/140 equal to ‘2’ for Ginnir General Hospital and 114/70 equal to ‘2’ for Delomena General Hospital. Unfortunately, the calculated k-interval for all three selected public hospitals was two. This meant that study participants were selected with an interval of two, starting with the first study participant and that the first study participant was selected by lot on each day of data collection.

Operational definition

Knowledge about DR

Knowledge about DR was assessed using 20 questions. Respondents who scored greater than or equal to ‘9’ on the knowledge questions were considered well-informed, whereas those who scored less than ‘9’ were considered poorly informed about DR. This cut-off point is the individual respondent median score for knowledge questions.

Attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation

Attitudes towards eye check-up service utilisation were also assessed using a 5-point Likert scale with four questions. The median score was used to categorise attitudes towards eye check-up service utilisation. Accordingly, respondents who scored greater than or equal to the median or ‘8’ on the attitude questions were considered to have a good attitude, while those who scored less than the median or ‘8’ were considered to have a poor attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation.

Eye check-up service utilisation

Seven questions were used to assess eye check-up service utilisation among adult diabetics. Participants who had a greater than or equal to ‘1.5’ mean score were considered as having good eye check-up service utilisation while those who had a less than ‘1.5’ mean score were labelled as having poor eye check-up service utilisation.

Regular diabetic eye check-up utilisation

Participants who undertook investigations at least every 1 month since diagnosis of diabetes were considered as having regular utilisation of eye check-up services.17

Variables

Dependent variables

Knowledge of DR and utilisation of eye check-up services among adult diabetic patients were dependent variables.

Independent variables

Social demographic characteristics such as age, educational status, occupation, marital status, income, residence, attitude towards utilisation of eye check-up services, duration of illness, types of diabetes mellitus and history of eye disease were considered.

Data collection and data quality control

The questionnaire was adapted from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia14 and Saudi Arabia.18 The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and translated into the local language Afan Oromo and Amharic and translated back to English to check for consistency.

The questionnaires were pretested on 5% of the sample size outside the study area at Robe General Hospital and modified accordingly. Through this, pretest validity and reliability of the questionnaires were also checked. The questionnaire consisted of sociodemographic variables, health profile variables, knowledge about DR, attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation and eye check-up service utilisation status. Data was collected by six BSc nurses and supervised by two supervisors. Two days of training were given for data collectors and supervisors regarding the objectives of the study, consent form, respondents’ rights and actual data collection procedures. The questionnaire was checked thoroughly for its completeness before leaving each respondent by data collectors and at the end of each data collection day by assigned supervisors. The participants were informed about the objective of the study before giving their responses.

The study was approved by the Madda Walabu University Research Development Directorate Review Board with approval reference number AA-271/1/2028, and written informed consent was obtained from each study participant after an explanation of the study objectives, risks and benefits. Their right not to participate was also explained. Respondents were also assured that their answers would be kept confidential so that they could provide truthful answers. The respondent’s identification information was not recorded for their privacy, and investigators have no access to information that could identify the participant’s identity.

Investigators and supervisors closely followed and frequently reviewed the data collection process to ensure the completeness and consistency of the information collected.

Data processing and analysis

Collected data were coded and entered into EpiData 3.1 and exported into SPSS V.25. Descriptive statistics were performed to examine the distribution of each study variable using frequency distribution and measures of central tendency.

A binary logistic regression model was also used to identify factors associated with both dependent variables (knowledge and utilisation of eye check-up services for prevention of DR). In the bivariate logistic regression, the variables that had a p value of less than 0.25 were selected as candidate variables for the multivariable logistic regression analysis. In the multivariable analysis, the adjusted odds ratios (AOR) were calculated with the corresponding 95% CI, and those variables that had a p value of less than 0.05 were declared factors significantly associated with the outcome variables. The fit of the model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test of fit.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 401 adult diabetic patients participated in this study, representing a response rate of 95%. The remaining 21 (5%) diabetic patients have interrupted replying to our questionnaire. The median age of study participants was 40.0 years (with an SD of 10.991). Of the study participants, 223 (55.6%) were male, and 230 (57.4%) were urban residents. Regarding marital status, participants who were (single) were never married, married, divorced and widowed were 38 (9.5%), 344 (85.8%), 16 (4%) and 3 (0.7%), respectively.

Furthermore, the finding of this study showed that those who could not read and write, read and write with no formal education and taught primary school (1–8) and secondary school (9–12) and above were 129 (32.2%), 73 (18.2%), 109 (27.2%) and 90 (22.4%), respectively. The majority of the participants were farmers (128, 31.9%) with the fewest of them being merchants (28, 7%). Moreover, the study yielded the median monthly income was 1050.0 ETB (with an SD of 2314.609) (see table 1).

Factors associated with knowledge about DR

Residence, marital status, educational status, income, duration of DM, previous eye disease, distance from a health facility, health insurance and good attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation were significant from bivariate logistic regression analysis with p value<0.25 and then entered to multivariate binary logistic regression analysis. Three variables remain significant after analysing multivariate binary logistic regression at a p value of <0.05. Consequently, this study revealed that the odds of good knowledge about DR were 10 times higher (AOR=10, 95% CI 2.99, 35.381) among participants with secondary school (9–12) and above level of education when compared with those who can not read and write, after controlling the effects of other variables in the model. It also showed that the odds of having good knowledge about DR were two times higher (AOR=2, 95% CI: 1.304, 4.473) among participants who had health insurance compared with those who had not it after controlling the effects of other variables in the model. Furthermore, the odds of good knowledge about DR were 61% less likely (AOR=0.385, 95% CI 0.220, 0.673) among participants who had a poor attitude towards the uses of eye check-up services for prevention of DR as compared with their counterparts (see online supplemental table 5).

Factors associated with utilisation of eye check-up services for prevention of DR among adult diabetic patients

As shown in online supplemental table 6, age, residence, educational status, duration of diabetes, hypertension, previous eye diseases, health insurance, good knowledge about DR and good attitude towards eyes check-up service utilisation were selected for multivariable logistic regression based on the results from bivariate logistic regression analysis. After multivariate logistic regression analysis, five variables remain significant factors associated with eye check-up service utilisation. Accordingly, the odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants from the urban residence were two times higher (AOR=2.332, 95% CI 1.168, 4.654) when compared with their counterparts after controlling the effects of other variables in the model. The odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants with secondary school (9–12) and above were 11 times more (AOR=11.363, 95% CI 3.876, 33.31) when compared with those who can not read and write, after controlling the effects of other variables in the model. Additionally, this study reported that the odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants who had health insurance were five times higher (AOR=4.954, 95% CI 2.455, 9.99) as compared with their counterparts. Moreover, when compared with their counterparts, the odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants having a poor attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation were 53% less likely (AOR=0.47, 95% CI 0.249, 0.879). On the other hand, the odds of having good eye check-up service utilisation were 15 times less likely (AOR=15.122, 95% CI 5.426, 42.139) among respondents who had poor knowledge about DR when compared with their counterparts after controlling the effects of other variables in the model.

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus can be responsible for preventable non-traumatic vision-threatening complications that demand good knowledge about DR and eye check-up service utilisation for the prevention of DR.

This study was targeted to assess knowledge about DR, eye check-up service utilisation and associated factors among adult diabetic patients at public hospitals of Bale and East Bale zones, southeastern Ethiopia, 2022.

In this study, therefore, 63.3% (95%CI 58.5, 68%) of adult diabetic patients had good knowledge about DR. This finding is consistent with the study conducted in Saudi Arabia, which reported that adult diabetic patients have good knowledge about DR (64%).12

However, the result of this study was lower than the study done in Japan,19 Australia20 and Oman21, which reported that 98%, 96% and 72.9%, respectively, of adult diabetic patients had good knowledge about DR. This variation might be due to differences in literacy rates among these countries and the lack of organised diabetic health education campaigns particularly awareness creation on DR in the current study setting of this study.

251 (62.6%) study participants reported that diabetes can affect the eyes. This finding is in line with the study conducted in India22 and Ethiopia14, which revealed 74.3% and 72.6%, respectively. However, this is lower than the study conducted in South Africa23 and India24, which reported 97% and 98.7%, respectively. This discrepancy is because the participants from India and South Africa were from diabetic eye centres that came for regular eye check-ups and got access to information about DR.

On the other hand, the findings from the present study showed that 55.6% and 30.4% of participants mentioned poorly controlled blood glucose levels and hypertension, respectively, as risk factors for DR. This is consistent with the study conducted in Ethiopia14 that reported 59.8% and 39%, respectively. This might be the similarity of study design and sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. On the contrary, the finding of this study is higher than the study conducted in Nigeria,13 in which 29.7% of participants reported poorly controlled blood glucose levels, a known risk factor for DR. This is because the study done in Nigeria13 used a smaller sample size (148 samples) than this study.

In the present study, 278 (69.3%) participants reported the importance of regular diabetic eye check-ups. This is in line with the study conducted in Nigeria13 and Ethiopia14, which showed 66.9% and 66.6%, respectively. This similarity might be due to the study participants’ good adherence to the care provider’s diabetes health advice.

The proportion of good eye check-up service utilisation in this study was 37% (95% CI 32.5, 42%). This study is supported by the findings from a study conducted in Bangladesh25 and South Africa23, which reported 37% in both. On the contrary, the present finding is lower than reports from studies conducted in Switzerland26 and Turkey27, which revealed 70.5 % and 77.3%, respectively. This discrepancy is due to the variation of study participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and patients from Switzerland and Turkey who easily accessed diabetic eye centres rather than those in our study.

The reasons for not using eye check-up services in the present finding were participants reported that they did not think that it was necessary (67.2%), followed by not being advised by a doctor (57.9%). This result is similar to the finding reported from Ethiopia.14 This is possibly explained by the lack of awareness and alertness of participants about the need for a regular eye examination in both studies.

In this study, the odds of good knowledge about DR were 10 times higher among participants in secondary school9,12 and above compared with those who can not read and write. The result of this is supported by the study conducted in India28 and Nigeria13, which realised that educational level is positively associated with good knowledge about DR.

The study conducted in Ethiopia14 reported that educational level did not significantly associate with good knowledge about DR knowledge. This might be due to variation in the study setting and sample size where the present study used one referral and two general hospitals with a relatively large sample size, and this could shine a light on the association of educational level with good knowledge of DR.

Moreover, the current finding shows that the odds of good knowledge about DR were two times higher among participants who had health insurance compared with those who did not. This is supported by the result of the study conducted in Germany29, which showed that participants who had health insurance had a good awareness of DR and good eye check-up service utilisation. This is because secured health insurance can increase the tendency towards the use of eye check-up services for the prevention of DR, which might also facilitate an arena for DR-related information flow.

Additionally, this study reported that participants with a poor attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation were 61% less likely to have good knowledge about DR compared with their counterparts. This might be because having a positive perception towards eye check-up service utilisation for the prevention of DR brings good adherence to health-seeking behaviour and increases accessibility to DR-related information.

The odds of having good utilisation of eye check-up services among participants with good knowledge about DR were 15 times higher compared with their counterparts in this finding. This result is supported by the study conducted in Turkey27 and Ethiopia30, which revealed that those participants who had poor knowledge about DR were less likely to use eye care services than those who had good knowledge about DR. This might be due to having a good knowledge of DR creating a positive attitude towards the need for annual eye check-up service utilisation.

Similarly, the odds of having good eye check-up service utilisation among participants who had a poor attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation were 53% times less likely than those with a good attitude. This result is supported by the study done in Ethiopia (30), which reported that participants with good attitudes increased health-seeking behaviour of DR prevention practices which in turn facilitates access to diabetic-related information flow. Furthermore, this study reports that the likelihood of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants from the urban residence was two times more compared with their counterparts. This result is supported by the study done in Bangladesh25 and Ethiopia.14 This is possibly described by participants from urban residences prone to access diabetic information.

The odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants with secondary school (9–12) and above were 11 times higher compared with those who can not read and write in this study. This is because literacy is associated with good knowledge about DR, which increases the tendency of good adherence to eye check-up service utilisation. Eventually, the finding of this study showed that the odds of good eye check-up service utilisation among participants who had health insurance were five times higher compared with those who did not. This is supported by the result of the study conducted in Germany29, which showed that participants who had health insurance had good eye check-up service utilisation.

The findings of this study imply the importance of policy and programme focus in addressing knowledge about DR and eye check-up service utilisation by giving due attention to factors that promote as well as hinder these occurrences. Healthcare providers need to provide prompt diabetic health education at the follow-up unit level. Beyond and above this, it is important to arrange to deliver DR-related lesson plan campaigns for adult diabetic patients. Healthcare planners should launch diabetic eye check-up service centres locally at every hospital.

Strength and limitation

The study was completed with a high response rate, a relatively large sample size was used, probability was used to select study participants and data quality was assured by close and careful monitoring thoroughly. In this study, patients with gestational diabetes and psychiatric disorders were not addressed. The study also shares the limitations of a cross-sectional study.

Conclusion

More than half (63.3%) and below half (37%) of the study participants had good knowledge about DR and had good eye check-up service utilisation, respectively. The factors positively associated with good knowledge about DR were high educational status, good attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation and having health insurance. On the other hand, being from an urban residence, having high educational status, having health insurance, having good knowledge about DR and good attitude towards eye check-up service utilisation were significant variables with good eye check-up service utilisation.
