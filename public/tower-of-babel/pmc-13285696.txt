Osteosynthesis versus arthroplasty in the management of radial head fractures: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mariana Martins Sousa, Luís Pedro Vieira, André Rodrigues Pinho, Vitorino Veludo, Francisco Serdoura, Maria João Leite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadial head fractures are among the most common elbow injuries in adults. The choice between osteosynthesis and radial head arthroplasty (RHA) remains controversial, particularly in comminuted or unstable fracture patterns. This study aims to compare functional and radiographic outcomes, complication rates, and patient satisfaction following osteosynthesis or arthroplasty in the surgical management of radial head fractures.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 35 patients treated surgically for radial head fractures between 2018 and 2022 was analyzed. Fractures were classified using the Mason classification and stratified by complexity. Clinical outcomes included the Mayo Elbow Performance Score, visual analog scale for pain, range of motion, and patient satisfaction. Radiographic follow-up identified complications such as pseudarthrosis and implant migration. Statistical comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests, with significance set at P < .05.ResultsOf 35 patients, 22 (63%) underwent osteosynthesis and 13 (37%) underwent RHA. The osteosynthesis group showed higher mean Mayo Elbow Performance Score (82.7 vs. 74.6) and lower mean visual analog scale (1.52 vs. 2.54) compared to the RHA group, although differences were not statistically significant. Satisfaction scores were similar (mean = 2.0) in both groups. Range of motion limitations were more frequent in complex fractures but not significantly associated with treatment type. Complication and revision rates were descriptively higher in the RHA group.ConclusionOsteosynthesis may yield favorable functional outcomes and comparable satisfaction relative to arthroplasty, especially in noncomplex, reconstructable fractures. In our study, the trends favored screw fixation, but overall, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as no statistically significant differences were observed and the analysis is subject to substantial confounding, supporting an exploratory interpretation. Treatment should be individualized, and long-term follow-up is crucial to assess recovery and detect complications.

The radial head is a pivotal component of the elbow joint, articulating with both the capitellum of the humerus and the proximal ulna. Its unique anatomy, featuring a 40-degree central concavity and an oval shape, facilitates a 180-degree range of forearm pronation and supination. Additionally, the radial head contributes significantly to the elbow’s valgus stability, particularly when the medial collateral ligament complex is compromised. Notably, the anterolateral third of the radial head lacks subchondral bone, rendering it more susceptible to fractures.12

Radial head fractures are prevalent, accounting for approximately one-third of all elbow fractures and 1% to 4% of all fractures in adults.11 These injuries typically result from a fall onto an outstretched hand and patients commonly present with pain, swelling, and limited elbow range of motion (ROM). Fractures can occur in isolation or as part of complex injury patterns involving elbow dislocation, ligamentous disruption, or associated osseous injuries such as the terrible triad.13 As such, a comprehensive evaluation of the interosseous membrane, medial elbow structures, and distal radioulnar joint is essential, particularly in high-energy or atypical presentations.

Historically, the radial head was considered dispensable and often excised. However, long-term studies have shown that excision is associated with instability, proximal radial migration, and degenerative changes.8 This recognition has led to a paradigm shift favoring fixation or replacement to preserve elbow biomechanics.

Treatment options include conservative management, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), or radial head arthroplasty (RHA), depending on fracture severity, displacement, and associated injuries. While ORIF is typically favored in reconstructable fractures, arthroplasty is recommended in non-repairable comminuted patterns, especially in the setting of instability.3,9

In this study, we aim to compare the clinical and functional outcomes of osteosynthesis versus arthroplasty in the treatment of radial head fractures. By analyzing complication rates, functional scores, and patient satisfaction, we seek to clarify the role of each approach and provide evidence to guide clinical decision-making.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study of patients with radial head fractures who underwent surgical treatment with either osteosynthesis or arthroplasty at a tertiary trauma center between 2018 and 2022. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined to select patients with displaced or unstable radial head fractures requiring surgical intervention, while patients with minimally displaced or stable fractures were managed nonoperatively and thus excluded.

Data collection included demographics (age, sex, and comorbidities such as smoking status, diabetes, and osteoporosis), mechanism of injury (categorized as high-energy trauma—eg, motor vehicle collision or fall from height—versus low-energy trauma—eg, fall from standing height), fracture classification and associated lesions, treatment type, complications, and revision surgeries.

Fractures were classified using the Mason classification based on anterior-posterior and lateral elbow radiographs obtained at presentation. All patients underwent computed tomography, which was retrospectively reviewed to support and confirm fracture classification and help surgical planning. Although a minimum follow-up of 12 months was intended, the actual follow-up duration ranged from 3 to 48 months due to variability in patient availability. Complications were evaluated through clinical records and follow-up radiographs and included delayed union, nonunion, pseudoarthrosis, iatrogenic fracture, joint stiffness, implant-related issues (eg, overstuffing, migration), and refracture. All complications were independently reviewed by a surgeon not involved in the index procedure.

Surgical technique was documented and categorized as screw fixation (single screw, parallel screws, tripod, and configuration), plate fixation, or RHA. Post-operative rehabilitation followed a standardized, phase-based protocol aligned with current recommendations for radial head fractures. During the first 1–2 weeks, the elbow was immobilized in a splint or brace for pain and swelling control, while gentle passive and active-assisted ROM exercises were initiated. From weeks 2–6, progression to active motion and active-assisted exercises was encouraged, with gentle strengthening introduced as tolerated. After 6–8 weeks, focus shifted to restoring full ROM and progressive resistance training to regain strength and function. Return to unrestricted activities was permitted based on radiographic healing and patient tolerance for both osteosynthesis and RHA cases. Functional outcomes were assessed at a mean follow-up of 12 months during outpatient visits by the treating surgeon. Evaluations included the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, a patient satisfaction score, and ROM.

The MEPS evaluates pain, stability, mobility, and functional use of the elbow. Scores >90 are considered excellent, 75–89 good, 60–74 fair, and <60 poor. The VAS pain score was recorded on a 100 mm scale, with 0–4 mm indicating no pain, 5–44 mm mild pain, 45–74 mm moderate pain, and 75–100 mm severe pain. Satisfaction was rated using a 4-point ordinal scale (very satisfied, satisfied, indifferent, and unsatisfied). ROM was assessed as the total degrees of elbow flexion-extension and forearm pronation-supination.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical variables. Continuous data are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) and ranges, while categorical variables are reported as absolute and relative frequencies.

Inferential statistical analyses were performed to evaluate associations between treatment type and outcomes:•The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare MEPS and VAS scores across the 3 surgical groups due to nonparametric data distribution and small sample size.•The Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons (eg, tripod vs. parallel screw configuration).•Associations between categorical variables (eg, complication rates across treatment types) were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.•Correlations between continuous variables (eg, age and MEPS) were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Results

A total of 35 patients with radial head fractures were included in this study. The mean age of the cohort was 56 years (SD = 15.4), ranging from 28 to 81 years, with a predominance of female patients (60%, n = 21). The most common comorbidities were hypertension (n = 14), dyslipidemia (n = 7), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (n = 5).

A substantial proportion (43%) had no documented comorbidities.

Concerning the mechanism of injury, low-energy trauma was the predominant cause, accounting for 30 out of 35 cases (86%), while high-energy trauma was responsible in only 5 cases (14%).

The mean follow-up was 12 months (SD = 10.9, range: 3 to 48), deemed sufficient for the assessment of midterm functional outcomes and complications.

Fracture severity was classified according to the Mason classification. The majority of the fractures were categorized as Mason type III (n = 13), followed by Mason type II (n = 11), type IV (n = 9), and type I (n = 2) (Fig. 1). This reflects a predominance of comminuted or displaced fractures, often requiring surgical intervention.Figure 1Distribution of fracture types according to Mason classification.

Fractures were further stratified by complexity, based on the presence of associated injuries. A total of 9 patients (26%) presented with isolated radial head fractures without any associated injuries and were classified as simple fractures.

In contrast, 26 patients (74%) had complex fractures, characterized by concomitant injuries. The most frequent associated injury pattern was the terrible triad of the elbow (n = 12), followed by combined fractures of the coronoid process and olecranon (n = 2), proximal radius and ulna (n = 1), and various types of fracture dislocation injuries. Other less frequent but clinically significant combinations included Monteggia fractures and Essex-Lopresti lesions.

Surgical treatment and fracture patterns

Overall, 22 patients (63%) underwent osteosynthesis (19 with screws, 3 with plates), and 13 (37%) were treated with RHA. Osteosynthesis was the most frequent surgical technique, with 19 cases (54%) treated using isolated screws and 3 cases (9%) treated with plate fixation. In terms of screw configuration, 12 patients had a “parallel” screw configuration, whereas 4 underwent fixation with a “tripod” configuration.

Functional outcomes and pain

In our sample, the mean MEPS was 82.7, and the mean VAS for pain was 1.52.

A subgroup analysis was performed comparing outcomes between patients who underwent screw fixation (n = 14) and those who received RHA (n = 11). Due to limited data availability, patients treated with plate fixation were excluded from this comparison.

The mean MEPS was higher in the screw fixation group (82.7) compared to the arthroplasty group (74.6), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (U = 143.5, P = .267; Mann-Whitney U test).

Similarly, pain scores (VAS) were lower in the screw fixation group (1.52) than in the arthroplasty group (2.54), although the difference was also not statistically significant (U = 94.5, P = .394).

Although screw fixation tended to yield better functional and pain outcomes, these differences were not statistically significant, possibly due to the small sample size. Nonetheless, these findings support a trend favoring osteosynthesis in select cases, particularly when native anatomy can be reconstructed.

Complications and revision surgeries

Thirteen patients (37%) experienced complications, most frequently joint stiffness (n = 11), nonunion (n = 2), and implant-related issues (n = 3). Complication rates were 26% for screw fixation, 67% for plate fixation, and 46% for arthroplasty (Fig. 4). Differences were not statistically significant (P = .283). Regarding screw fixation, the most common complications were nonunion or pseudarthrosis (n = 3) and elbow stiffness (n = 1). For plate fixation, pseudarthrosis (n = 1) and stiffness (n = 1). Eight patients (23%) required revision surgery. These included radial head excision, elbow arthrolysis, neurolysis, ulnar osteosynthesis revision, and bone grafting. Most revisions occurred in patients with complex fractures (6/8) and Mason III injuries (4/8), but no significant associations were observed with fracture complexity (P = 1.000) or classification (P = .479).

Complications were identified at a mean follow-up of 19 months, with 75% detected within 24 months, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up.

Radiological complications and findings

Radiological issues were documented in 9 patients (26%), including pseudarthrosis (n = 4), implant migration (n = 1), overstuffing (n = 2), and metaphyseal remodeling (n = 1). These complications occurred predominantly in Mason III-IV and complex fractures and were more common after arthroplasty (38%) than screw (16%) or plate fixation (33%).

Radiological complications were typically detected between 11 and 48 months post-operatively, with a median follow-up of 22 months among affected patients.

Although the overall sample size is limited, the data indicate a trend toward higher radiological complication rates in arthroplasty cases, although without statistical significance.

Patient-reported satisfaction was categorized into 4 levels: very satisfied, satisfied, indifferent, and unsatisfied. Screw fixation had the highest proportion of “very satisfied” responses (55%), while arthroplasty showed more variable outcomes, including one report of dissatisfaction (Fig. 6). Higher satisfaction outcomes tended to correlate with less severe fractures and screw fixation techniques. Patients with complex or high-grade fractures, especially those treated with arthroplasty, reported more mixed outcomes. However, the sample size remains limited.

The analysis revealed a moderate negative correlation between satisfaction and Mason classification (ρ = −0.44), indicating that patients with more severe fractures (Mason III–IV) tended to report lower satisfaction. Additionally, fracture complexity (ρ = −0.27) and type of surgical treatment (ρ = −0.24) also showed weak negative correlations with satisfaction (Fig. 7). These results suggest that fracture severity and overall injury burden are stronger predictors of patient-reported outcomes than the choice of fixation.

These findings suggest that simpler fractures and less invasive procedures (eg, screw fixation) may be associated with higher patient satisfaction, although the correlation strength is limited. This reinforces the clinical observation that fracture severity and treatment burden may negatively impact subjective outcomes, but also highlights that satisfaction is multifactorial, potentially influenced by patient expectations, rehabilitation quality, and overall recovery trajectory.

Discussion

Radial head fractures represent a significant proportion of adult elbow injuries, and the optimal treatment strategy remains debated in the orthopedic literature. In this study, we compared the clinical and functional outcomes of osteosynthesis and arthroplasty in the management of radial head fractures, evaluating pain, function, complications, and patient satisfaction.

Indications for arthroplasty vs. osteosynthesis (open reduction and internal fixation)

In our series of 35 patients, osteosynthesis was the predominant treatment modality (63%), particularly for Mason I–II in the context of simple fractures, whereas arthroplasty was mainly reserved for complex Mason III–IV fractures. Our findings showed a statistically significant association between the Mason classification and the type of surgical treatment (P = .0056), consistent with current literature, which supports the use of arthroplasty for non-reconstructable fractures, especially in high-grade injuries.11,12

While traditional algorithms propose RHA for fractures with ≥3 fragments, our data revealed that in Mason III fractures, 6 out of 13 patients were treated successfully with osteosynthesis (either screws or plates). This suggests that some comminuted fractures, when not associated with bone loss or instability, may still be amenable to fixation, aligning with recent evidence supporting ORIF in selected Mason III cases.13

Interestingly, although low-energy trauma was the predominant mechanism of injury in our cohort (86%), a substantial proportion of patients (74%) sustained complex fractures, characterized by associated injuries such as dislocations or additional osseous lesions. This apparent paradox underscores a crucial clinical insight: even in the context of seemingly benign, low-energy mechanisms, significant soft tissue, and osteoarticular damage may occur. These associated injuries have critical implications for both surgical planning and prognosis. Therefore, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and ensure a comprehensive diagnostic assessment, including advanced imaging when necessary, to identify concomitant injuries that may influence treatment strategies and outcomes. Our findings reinforce the need for thorough evaluation of radial head fractures, irrespective of the initial mechanism of trauma.

Mason III: universal arthroplasty?

Our results contradict the notion that all Mason III fractures should be managed with prosthetic replacement. Five of the 13 Mason III cases underwent screw fixation, with functional outcomes (MEPS) comparable to those treated with arthroplasty. This echoes recent studies indicating that the decision should be based on reconstructability and joint congruity rather than fracture type alone.5

Radial neck fractures and pseudarthrosis risk

Radial neck involvement raises concerns regarding pseudarthrosis. In our cohort, pseudarthrosis occurred in 4 cases, mostly in patients with complex Mason III–IV fractures with high-energy trauma. Although these findings may suggest a rationale for arthroplasty in neck fractures, our data showed successful outcomes in some patients treated with plate or screw fixation, indicating that arthroplasty should be considered selectively, not systematically.4

Tripod screws vs. plates in radial neck fixation

Among patients submitted to radial head fracture fixation, those who received tripod configuration (n = 4) reported better ROM and satisfaction than those treated with plates (n = 3), with fewer complications. This observation, while limited by sample size, aligns with recent evidence showing that tripod screw techniques may lead to earlier union and fewer complications than plate fixation in neck fractures.8 The minimally invasive nature of screw fixation helps preserve soft tissue integrity and vascular supply, and the absence of extracortical hardware reduces the risk of impingement during forearm rotation, potentially resulting in improved ROM and greater patient satisfaction.

Management of comminuted fractures in young patients

In young patients with comminuted fractures (including Mason III and IV), where long-term concerns about prosthesis durability arise, our data support attempting fixation with screws—especially tripod configurations—when feasible. Despite the trend toward higher complication rates in arthroplasty (46%) versus screws (26%), these differences were not statistically significant but reinforce the importance of careful case selection.3,11

Functional outcomes and pain

Patients treated with osteosynthesis showed a trend toward higher MEPS (mean 82.7 vs. 74.6) and lower VAS pain scores (mean 1.52 vs. 2.55) compared to those treated with arthroplasty. According to the MEPS grading system, a score between 75 and 89 is classified as “good,” while scores between 60 and 74 are considered “fair.” Therefore, both treatment groups achieved at least fair to good functional outcomes, with the screw fixation group tending toward better function. Although not statistically significant (MEPS: P = .267; VAS: P = .394), the 8-point difference in MEPS may still be clinically relevant, especially in active patients where small functional gains can impact quality of life and daily activities. Satisfaction was also slightly higher in the osteosynthesis group (mean 2.35 vs. 2.11; P = .573). These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting better subjective and functional outcomes in selected fracture types treated with head-preserving techniques.6

Range of motion

These findings highlight that residual deficits in forearm rotation and elbow extension represent the most prevalent post-operative functional impairments, particularly relevant in activities requiring full arm articulation. As such, restoration of physiological motion arcs, especially rotational, should remain a central goal in the surgical and rehabilitative management of radial head fractures.

The most frequently observed limitation was elbow extension deficit, ranging from 10° to 20° when present, followed by deficits in supination and pronation, which were also typically 10° to 20°. While no statistically significant associations were found between ROM limitations and the treatment modality, literature suggests better motion preservation with screw fixation compared to plating, attributed to lower surgical invasiveness.8

These results suggest that individual variability and surgical/recovery factors may play a more influential role in functional outcomes than fracture severity alone.

Complications and revision surgeries

Complications were identified at a mean follow-up of 19 months, with 75% detected within 24 months, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up. Radiological complications and findings were typically detected between 11 and 48 months post-operatively, with a median follow-up of 22 months among affected patients.

The overall complication rate was 37%, with stiffness and nonunion being the most common. Although arthroplasty had a higher complication rate (46%) than osteosynthesis (26%), this difference was not statistically significant. These observations are supported by recent evidence indicating that arthroplasty may offer functional advantages in irreparable comminuted fractures (Mason III–IV), but carries a higher risk of complications, especially in younger or more active patients.5,7 Radiological complications (26%) were more frequent after arthroplasty (38%) than screw fixation (16%), reinforcing concerns about overstuffing and implant migration.2,10

Several studies have reported a higher complication rate associated with plate fixation in radial head fractures when compared to screw fixation or RHA. Plate osteosynthesis, particularly in comminuted fractures or in cases requiring extensive dissection, has been linked to complications such as nonunion, hardware prominence, implant failure, and restricted forearm rotation due to soft tissue irritation and impingement on the proximal radioulnar joint.11,12 In contrast, isolated screw fixation, when feasible, tends to preserve the soft tissue envelope and radial head vascularity, leading to lower complication rates and better preservation of motion arcs, particularly in simple or partial head fractures.13

RHA, while effective in addressing non-reconstructible fractures, carries its own set of complications, including overstuffing, implant loosening, joint stiffness, and radiocapitellar arthritis—especially in the long term.4,7 Nevertheless, plate fixation appears to be the technique most consistently associated with early complications, a fact that has led many authors to advocate against its routine use, particularly when simpler, less invasive fixation techniques are feasible.8,11 As such, current trends in the literature favor screw fixation for reconstructible fractures and arthroplasty for unreconstructible or highly comminuted lesions, reserving plate fixation for selected, technically demanding cases.

Although the overall sample size is limited, the data indicate a trend toward higher radiological complication rates in arthroplasty cases, reinforcing the need for careful implant selection, positioning, and long-term radiographic surveillance, especially in patients with severe or complex fractures.

Patient satisfaction

Screw fixation was associated with higher levels of patient-reported satisfaction compared to arthroplasty, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fisher's exact test, P = 1.000). This observation is consistent with the findings by Ahmad et al, who reported that patients undergoing head-preserving procedures tend to express greater subjective satisfaction, likely due to the preservation of native joint biomechanics and proprioception.1 Further analysis using Spearman's rank correlation demonstrated a moderate negative correlation between satisfaction and Mason classification (ρ = −0.59) and weaker correlations with fracture complexity (ρ = −0.23) and treatment modality (ρ = −0.39), suggesting a general trend toward lower satisfaction in more severe fractures and after prosthetic replacement. However, when tested categorically, no statistically significant associations were found between satisfaction and Mason classification (chi-square test, P = .281), fracture complexity (Fisher's exact test, P = 1.000), or treatment type (Fisher's exact test, P = 1.000). These results suggest that satisfaction may be more closely influenced by the type of treatment provided rather than by fracture severity alone. Nonetheless, since fracture severity is often a key determinant of the treatment strategy—particularly in high-grade or comminuted fractures—these 2 variables are inherently linked and cannot be completely dissociated when interpreting satisfaction outcomes.

Study limitations

Some several important limitations must be emphasized. First, treatment selection was not randomized, introducing potential selection bias, particularly in less severe fractures where the choice between fixation and arthroplasty may have been influenced by surgeon preference or fracture characteristics. Second, the cohort included heterogeneous injuries in terms of fracture complexity and associated lesions, which may affect outcomes and complicate comparisons between surgical techniques. Third, follow-up duration was variable across patients, ranging from 3 to 48 months, potentially influencing the assessment of functional recovery and complications. Fourth, post-operative rehabilitation was not fully standardized, which may have contributed to variability in ROM, strength, and patient-reported outcomes.

Despite a moderate cohort size, this study's sample (n = 35) still falls within the range of comparable surgical series in the literature, which typically include 30–100 patients for radial head fracture studies.9,14 Larger analyses such as Grewal et al (n = 52) and Lee et al (n = 67) have helped clarify outcomes of screw versus plate fixation. However, randomized controlled trials with sample sizes of 40–60 patients (eg, Murphy et al) have been powered to detect only large effect sizes. Thus, while our sample is not unusually small for this surgical context—orphan fracture types are typically low incidence—it remains underpowered to identify small-to-moderate differences in rare complications or satisfaction metrics. The retrospective design and variability in surgical technique and surgeon experience further introduce potential bias. Therefore, prospective multicenter randomized controlled trials with n ≥ 100 per arm will be required to more definitively assess the relative benefits and drawbacks of screw fixation, plating and arthroplasty in radial head fractures.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of tailoring the surgical approach for radial head fractures based on fracture type, complexity, and patient-specific factors. While arthroplasty remains a reliable solution for irreconstructible, complex Mason III–IV fractures—particularly those associated with instability or bone loss—our results support the use of osteosynthesis in a broader range of scenarios with good functional results, high patient satisfaction, and low-complication rate. Mason III fractures, traditionally considered for arthroplasty, were effectively managed with screw fixation in selected cases, aligning with recent literature.5,13 Fracture severity and complexity appear to be the dominant determinants of outcome.

Tripod screw configurations demonstrated promising outcomes in radial neck and comminuted fractures, especially in younger patients for whom long-term prosthesis durability is a concern.9 Although complication and revision rates were higher in the arthroplasty group, functional scores and satisfaction favored osteosynthesis, especially in simple or reconstructable injuries.

Clinical decision-making should be individualized, considering reconstructability, patient age, and expected functional demands. Long-term follow-up remains essential to evaluate complications, radiological outcomes, and patient-reported satisfaction. Future prospective, randomized studies are needed to refine indications and optimize treatment algorithms.
