Delayed diagnosis leading to severe complications in primary hyperparathyroidism caused by atypical parathyroid tumor
Authors: Francesca Lacchè, Alessandro Ciarloni, Monia Bordoni, Gianmaria Salvio, Giancarlo Balercia, Gilberta Giacchetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere skeletal complications at the onset of primary hyperparathyroidism are uncommon in contemporary Western clinical practice because routine serum calcium measurement usually allows early diagnosis. We describe a case of primary hyperparathyroidism in which delayed diagnosis, despite suggestive symptoms, led to an extremely severe presentation characterized by multiple fractures, osteolytic lesions, and prolonged postsurgical hungry bone syndrome requiring several months of intensive treatments and hospitalization. During hospitalization, the patient developed potentially life-threatening infectious and thrombotic complications. Management was further complicated by the diagnosis of an atypical parathyroid tumor, an uncommon and potentially aggressive cause of hyperparathyroidism.Keywords: hyperparathyroidism, hungry bone syndrome, fracture, atypical parathyroid tumor

Introduction

The diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is based on hypercalcemia with elevated or inappropriately normal parathyroid hormone (PTH) level [1]. Primary hyperparathyroidism is most commonly caused by parathyroid adenoma [1]. Atypical parathyroid tumor (APT) accounts for only 2% to 3% of PHPT [2]. Parathyroidectomy is recommended when PHPT is associated with fractures, osteoporosis, severe/moderate hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, or kidney disease [1]. Hungry bone syndrome (HBS) is a potentially life-threatening complication of surgical treatment, characterized by prolonged hypocalcemia [3].

We report a case of PHPT caused by APT that presented with multiple bone fractures and osteolytic lesions. Management was challenging because severe HBS developed after parathyroidectomy and required prolonged hospitalization.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 47-year-old man presented in September 2021 with back pain and walking difficulties. Electromyography and electroneurography were performed, and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy was suspected. Magnetic resonance imaging of the pelvic and shoulder girdle and muscle biopsy were recommended. In June 2022, he was admitted to the emergency room after an accidental fall. Computed tomography (CT) revealed bilateral subtrochanteric femoral fractures, bilateral humeral shaft fractures, multiple rib fractures, T11 vertebral body wedging (Fig. 1), a bone cyst, and a “salt-and-pepper” appearance of the skull (Fig. 2), and diffuse osteolytic lesions with cortical disruption throughout the appendicular skeleton and in many vertebral bodies (Fig. 1). Hypercalcemia (ionized calcium 7.47 mg/dL [Système International (SI): 1.86 mmol/L]; reference range 4.5-5.3 mg/dL [SI: 1.12-1.32 mmol/L]), elevated serum PTH (4950 pg/mL [SI: 525 pmol/L]; reference range 14-85 pg/mL [SI: 1.48-9.02 pmol/L]), hypophosphatemia (1.1 mg/dL [SI: 0.36 mmol/L]; reference range 2.5-4.5 mg/dL [SI: 0.81-1.45 mmol/L]), severe hypovitaminosis D (9.9 ng/mL [SI: 24.7 nmol/L]; reference range >30 ng/mL [SI: >75 nmol/L]), and increased bone turnover markers (BTMs; alkaline phosphatase 373 μg/L [SI: 1238 U/L], reference range 4.0-21.0 μg/L [SI: 15.0-41.2 U/L]; type 1 procollagen N-terminal propeptide 2290 ng/mL [SI: 2290 μg/L], reference range 15-75 ng/mL [SI: 15-75 μg/L]; and C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen 3369 pg/mL [SI 3.369 μg/L], reference range 130-600 pg/mL [SI: 0.130-0.600 μg/L]) led to PHPT diagnosis.

Thrombosis of the right popliteal and renal veins and embolism of the right pulmonary artery required anticoagulant therapy and placement of an inferior vena cava filter before orthopedic surgery.

Diagnostic assessment

Thoracic CT revealed a left cervical lesion measuring 2 × 2.5 cm compatible with a parathyroid gland (Fig. 3). After admission to our Endocrinology Department, parathyroid scintigraphy was performed using technetium-99m sestamibi and technetium-99m pertechnetate. A large area of potassium-mimetic and non-iodine-mimetic uptake below the left thyroid lower pole with late persistence was described. It was suggestive of a 26 mm hyperfunctioning parathyroid area (Fig. 4). Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry previously performed in May 2022 had shown reduced bone mineral density (BMD) with Z-score L1 to L4 −3.6. Femoral BMD had not been measured.

Treatment

Initial treatment of PHPT consisted of intravenous hydration, furosemide 20 mg/day, cinacalcet 30 mg 3 times daily, oral cholecalciferol 50 000 IU/week, zoledronate 4 mg IV twice a month, and a single 60 mg injection of denosumab. Calcium and vitamin D levels normalized, and BTMs decreased by ∼50% within 30 days. Meanwhile, the patient was admitted to the Orthopaedics Department. Bilateral subtrochanteric femur fractures were treated with intramedullary nailing (Fig. 5), whereas bilateral humeral fractures (Fig. 6) were managed conservatively. Because parathyroid cancer (PC) was suspected, the patient underwent left parathyroidectomy with left hemithyroidectomy and dissection of left lateral and central cervical compartment lymph nodes. No evidence of invasive growth, such as neoplastic infiltration of adjacent tissues or neoplastic lymph node involvement, was found. Histological examination reported a brownish neoformation with polycyclic margins of 3 × 2 cm. It presented growth in solid nests, separated by dense acellular fibrous bands with hemosiderin deposits. A fibrous capsule of variable thickness surrounded the lesion. It showed a trabecular growth pattern and was composed of cells with nuclear atypia and prominent nucleoli. Mitotic activity was <1 mitosis/10 high-powered fields, with a 2% Ki67 proliferation index. Immunohistochemistry staining was negative for thyroglobulin, TTF1, and calcitonin and positive for GATA3, with focal positivity for synaptophysin and chromogranin. The absence of vascular, capsular, and extracapsular invasion supported the diagnosis of APT. Germline testing for a CDC73 mutation was performed, yielding negative results.

After surgery, PTH decreased from 3210 pg/mL (SI: 340.45 pmol/L) to 192 pg/mL (SI: 20.36 pmol/L), whereas calcium decreased from a maximum of 14.9 mg/dL (SI: 3.72 mmol/L) to a minimum of 4.5 mg/dL (SI: 1.12 mmol/L).

Given the high risk of postoperative HBS, intravenous calcium gluconate infusion (20 mg/hour of elemental calcium), cholecalciferol (100 000 IU IM/day), and calcitriol (0.25 μg/day) were administered. Cinacalcet was discontinued the day before neck surgery. Nevertheless, the patient developed severe HBS. After surgery, renal function deteriorated (serum creatinine 0.54 mg/dL [SI: 47.5 µmol/L] on July 14, 2022 vs 1.36 mg/dL [SI: 119.7 µmol/L] on July 19, 2022). In contrast, the BTMs did not show significant reduction (alkaline phosphatase 232 µg/L [SI: 770 U/L] on July 14, 2022 vs 465 µg/L [SI: 1543 U/L] on July 21, 2022, type 1 procollagen N-terminal propeptide 1289 ng/mL [SI: 1289 µg/L] on July 14, 2022 vs 1482 ng/mL [SI: 1482 µg/L] on July 29, 2022, C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen 1635 pg/mL [SI: 1.635 μg/L] on July 14, 2022 vs 1031 pg/mL [SI: 1.031 μg/L] on July 21, 2022). Prolonged calcium gluconate infusion (up to 50 mg of elemental calcium/hour), oral calcium citrate (up to 6 g/day) and calcium carbonate (up to 2.4 g/day), calcitriol (up to 3 μg/day), oral cholecalciferol (100 000 IU/day), oral phosphate (500 mg/day), and oral magnesium (300 mg/day) were required. Serum calcium normalized after 2 months of pharmacological treatment.

Clinical course was complicated by septic shock from Acinetobacter baumannii requiring transfer to the intensive care unit.

Outcome and follow-up

After a 4-month hospital stay, the patient was discharged with oral calcium, calcitriol, and cholecalciferol supplementation. Follow-up included periodic measurements of calcium, phosphate, renal function, and PTH levels and annual neck ultrasound. No evidence of recurrent disease was detected. At the 1-year follow-up, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry showed an increase in BMD (Z-score L1-L4 −1.1), and imaging confirmed resolution of osteolytic lesions.

Discussion

We describe a man with PHPT due to an APT who presented with multiple bone fractures and osteolytic lesions. In Western countries, osteitis fibrosa cystica, the typical bone complication of PHPT, has become rare [4]. Its radiographic features include a demineralized skeleton, salt-and-pepper appearance of the skull, subperiosteal resorption, bone cysts, and lytic lesions. Reduced BMD is the most common bone manifestation [4]. Although PHPT is associated with increased risk [5, 6], presentation with pathological fracture is currently uncommon in developed countries [7]. This change has been attributed mainly to routine serum calcium measurement, which allows earlier diagnosis [8, 9]. A distinctive feature of our case is the failure to measure serum calcium despite the performance of electromyography and electroneurography and the recommendation for muscle biopsy. Measurement of serum calcium might have prevented this dramatic presentation. The patient had skeletal and neuromuscular manifestations, including fatigue, muscle pain, walking difficulties, multiple bone fractures, and osteolytic lesions. Lumbar BMD was also reduced (Z-score L1-L4 −3.6). The presence of multiple major fractures in a relatively young male without significant risk factors was most likely attributable to prolonged exposure to markedly elevated PTH levels [5, 6]. The first symptoms potentially attributable to PHPT had appeared several months before hospital admission [10, 11]. Biochemical abnormalities such as hypophosphatemia may also have contributed to initial symptoms, including severe weakness [10]. The simultaneous occurrence of multiple major fractures required prolonged immobilization, which likely contributed to a prothrombotic state and the subsequent pulmonary embolism [12]. The cumulative physiological stress may also have promoted systemic inflammation and immune dysfunction, thereby contributing to the development of severe infectious complications and sepsis [13]. The severe presentation may also have been related to the APT diagnosis and the markedly elevated PTH levels. Atypical parathyroid tumors are clinically hardly distinguishable from PC. It is a parathyroid neoplasm with uncertain malignant potential that displays concerning cytological and architectural features resembling those of PC [2]. The diagnosis depends on the absence of unequivocal evidence of invasive growth and malignancy, including capsular, perineural spaces, lymphatic and/or vascular invasion, neoplastic infiltration of adjacent tissues, or metastasis [14]. In contrast to PC, APT rarely recurs locally or metastasizes [15]. Parafibromin is a nuclear tumor-suppressive protein encoded by the CDC73 gene. Assessment of parafibromin expression may aid in the diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of parathyroid neoplasms. CDC73 alterations may result in loss of parafibromin expression. Despite observation of CDC73 mutation in up to 80% of PC, it can also be reported in some APT [15, 16]. Loss of parafibromin is correlated to aggressive behavior, local recurrence, or distant metastasis with high mortality risk [15]. According to the 2022 World Health Organization classification [2], parafibromin immunohistochemistry is recommended in APT to assist in risk assessment. Germline CDC73 testing is recommended for parafibromin-deficient APT, and long-term clinical and biochemical follow-up is indicated when a germline CDC73 mutation is identified [16, 17]. In our patient, there was no vascular, capsular, or extracapsular invasion, and the Ki-67 index was low (2%). Because parafibromin expression was not assessed, germline CDC73 testing was performed, yielding a negative result. However, the absence of tumor sequencing means that a somatic CDC73 alteration or another tumor-specific molecular abnormality cannot be excluded. This is a limitation in the management of our case. After surgery, the patient developed HBS. It is a prolonged hypocalcemia, often accompanied by hypophosphatemia and hypomagnesemia, following parathyroidectomy in patients with high bone turnover. Hungry bone syndrome probably results from the abrupt decrease in PTH secretion after parathyroidectomy, together with preoperative suppression of the remaining normal parathyroid gland by hypercalcemia. Removal of PTH stimulus shifts bone remodeling from predominantly osteoclastic resorption toward osteoblastic formation, resulting in substantial uptake of calcium, phosphate, and magnesium from the circulation into the skeleton [3]. Consistently, BTMs levels did not decrease as rapidly as PTH and calcium levels after surgery. The observed deterioration of renal function following parathyroidectomy is probably related to increased urine calcium levels caused by iatrogenic hypoparathyroidism [18]. Unfortunately, urine calcium was not evaluated after treatment. The patient had risk factors for HBS, including a large parathyroid tumor, extensive skeletal involvement, and elevated preoperative biochemical parameters [3, 19]. Given the high risk, prophylactic treatment with intravenous calcium gluconate, cholecalciferol, and calcitriol was administered. Nevertheless, HBS developed anyway. Treatment of hypocalcemia is based on intravenous and oral calcium supplementation. During hospitalization, our patient required up to 1200 mg/day of intravenous elemental calcium. After parathyroidectomy and resolution of HBS, a marked improvement in BMD was observed (Z-score L1-L4 from −3.6 to −1.1). Similar improvements were reported in case series of HBS [20]. Osteolytic lesions often regress after parathyroidectomy; the literature suggests 90% resolution within 6 to 10 months [21]. In our patient, all the osteolytic lesions had resolved after 1 year.
