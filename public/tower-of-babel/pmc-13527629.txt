Nutritive Value and Nutrient Digestibility of Silages From Different Parts of Fodder Beet With or Without Lactobacillus buchneri Inoculants for Sheep
Authors: Ali Abdi Saray, Alireza Aghashahi, Abolfazl Zali, Mehdi Ganj Khanlou, Einar Vargas‐Bello‐Pérez, Navid Ghavipanje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the suitability of diets containing silages from different parts of fodder beet (FB) with or without Lactobacillus buchneri (LB) when fed to sheep.MethodsSeven adult ruminally fistulated rams (2 years old; 74 ± 2.5 kg body weight) were randomly allocated to seven experimental diets in a changeover design. Treatments were as follows: whole FB without LB (WFB − LB); whole FB with LB (WFB + LB); FB tops without LB (FBT − LB); FB tops with LB (FBT + LB); FB bulbs without LB (FBR − LB); FB bulbs with LB (FBR + LB) and corn silage (CS) as control.ResultsLB inoculation did not affect silage chemical composition. FB bulb silage had lower pH than FB top silage. Dry matter intake (DMI) was highest for WFB + LB and lowest for FBT − LB (p < 0.001). Dry matter (DM) and organic matter (OM) digestibility were highest for CS and lowest for FBT − LB (p < 0.001). Ruminal pH and NH3‐N concentration were highest in sheep fed whole FB silage (p < 0.001). The acetate:propionate ratio was highest in FBT + LB and FBT − LB, followed by whole FB silage. FBT + LB and FBT − LB showed higher pH and NH3‐N but lower lactic acid concentration and flieg point value than other treatments.ConclusionSilage made from whole FB (with or without LB) could be a promising alternative to CS for sheep, based on digestibility and rumen function parameters.Keywords: digestibility, fodder beet, lactobacillus, silage quality, sustainability

Introduction

The continued and accelerated growth of the world's population is driving intensified ruminant production, a sector already challenged by limited resources, climate change and environmental impacts (FAO STAT 2021). To ensure global food security, adopting more sustainable ruminant production practices is urgently needed. Consequently, recent research has focused on alternative feed options that can enhance sustainability, addressing public concerns about the environmental footprint of ruminant systems (Jalal et al. 2023). Fodder beet (FB, Beta vulgaris subsp. maritima), a member of the Amaranthaceae family (formerly Chenopodiaceae), is a biennial plant known for its fleshy, swollen roots (Yolcu et al. 2021). This high‐yield crop can produce over 100 t/hectare (ha) of green fodder within 4 months and is particularly outperforming other crops in saline water and salt‐affected soils (Fleming et al. 2021). FB is recognized as a viable alternative for ruminant feed due to its favourable agronomic traits, including salinity and drought resistance, reduced water requirements, and high‐quality forage and silage production under diverse environmental conditions (Waghorn et al. 2018; Pacheco et al. 2020). FB is highly palatable and digestible; its roots are rich in water‐soluble carbohydrates (WSC), whereas the leaves contain higher levels of protein and minerals (Fleming, Edwards, et al. 2021; Pacheco et al. 2020). Previous studies have reported that FB roots contain about 72% WSCs and relatively low concentrations of crude protein (CP) (7.9%), acid detergent fibre (ADF) (6.7%) and neutral detergent fibre (NDF) (11.7%). These contrasting characteristics between roots and leaves highlight the importance of evaluating whole‐plant, top‐ and root‐only silages separately. Different portions of FB differ markedly in nutrient composition, fermentation characteristics and digestibility. Tops contain more fibre and protein, whereas the storage root is rich in soluble carbohydrates. Lactobacillus buchneri (LB) inoculants are commonly applied to improve fermentation efficiency, reduce spoilage and enhance aerobic stability, especially (Filiya 2003). This nutritional profile may elevate the risk of metabolic and nutritional disorders, such as ruminal acidosis (Dalley et al. 2019; Fleming, Edwards, et al. 2021). Moreover, FB is highly digestible, with organic matter (OM) digestibility reported to be 87%–90% (Dulphy et al. 2000). Eriksson, Ciszuk, et al. (2004) found that supplementing grass silage with FB increased microbial protein synthesis (MPS) in vitro. However, they could not confirm these results in an in vivo trial (Eriksson, Murphy, et al. 2004). Notably, the oxalic acid content and laxative effect of FB are limiting factors in ruminant feeding (Goyal 2018). Beet tops contain 4.12%–9.7% oxalate, which can bind dietary calcium to form insoluble calcium oxalate, reducing mineral absorption and potentially leading to decreased milk production and growth (El Tawab et al. 2020; Rahman et al. 2013). FB conservation is challenging due to high moisture, which causes spoilage. However, ensiling beet leaves and roots allows for long‐term storage, providing a valuable feed source during winter (Dulphy and Demarquilly 2000). It has been well established that ensiling is an option to reduce the concentration of oxalic acid and its negative effects (El Tawab et al. 2020). The lactic acid bacteria can utilize oxalic acid as a carbon source, reducing its concentration and mitigating its negative effects (El Tawab et al. 2020). LB is used for the efficient use of WSCs, rapid lactic acid production and pH reduction. They also reduce antibacterial activity, inhibiting harmful microorganisms in silage and rumen (Gollop et al. 2005). The present study aimed to assess the nutritional value, digestibility and silage quality of different parts of FB, including the tops, bulbs (roots) and their mixture, in comparison with corn silage. Additionally, the study sought to evaluate the potential effects of LB on the aforementioned silages.

Materials and Methods

Silage Making

The FB used in this study was cultivated in Gavdasht Farm located in Babol City, Mazandaran Province (Iran). The crop was established as an autumn (October) sowing in prepared beds with a row spacing of approximately 50–60 cm to facilitate root development and agronomic operations. Standard agronomic practices, including irrigation, weed control and pest management, were followed during the growing season. The crop was manually harvested in March (spring). Separation was performed manually by cutting through the crown area with a sharp harvesting knife to detach the leafy tops from the storage root. Each fraction (tops and storage root) was then chopped (mechanical chopper) to a uniform particle size and subsequently used for preparing the respective silage treatments. Silage of different parts (tops, bulbs and whole‐plant [whole‐plant FB typically consists of approximately 70%–80% storage root and 20%–30% leafy tops on a fresh‐weight basis]) was prepared using chopped FB, with wheat straw added as an absorbent. A mixture of 12% wheat straw and 88% FB was used to achieve the desired (25%) final dry matter (DM) content (Table 1). The amount of ME and CP of ration was 2.64 (mcal/kg) and 7.8 (%), respectively, by average. The FB and straw were fed into a large horizontal chopper (Famco, Co. Ltd., Karaj, Iran). The chopper was equipped with a screen that produced beet pieces <8 × 8 cm and chopped straw <10 cm in length. LB (1 × 106 CFU/g fresh matter) was used as the silage inoculant because this heterofermentative strain is known to enhance aerobic stability by converting lactic acid into acetic acid and 1,2‐propanediol, which inhibit yeasts and moulds and reduce spoilage losses in silages with high moisture and sugar content (Si et al. 2019; Muck et al. 2018). The silages were prepared using polyethylene bag silos. Approximately 20 kg of chopped material was tightly packed into each heavy‐duty, oxygen‐limiting polyethylene bag (thickness 150–200 µm). The bags were compacted manually to expel as much air as possible and immediately sealed with airtight heat‐resistant plastic clips. All silos were stored indoors at ambient room temperature (18–22°C) and protected from sunlight and temperature fluctuations throughout the fermentation period (75 days).

Digestibility Trial

The digestibility trial was performed at the experimental farm of Animal Science Research Institute, Karaj, Iran (longitude and latitude, 35.8° N and 50.9° E, 1312 m above sea level, and an annual average rainfall of 251 mm) from June 2024 to September 2024.

In this experiment, seven adult rams (2 years old; 74 ± 2.5 kg body weight [BW], mean ± SD), fitted with a flexible rumen fistula, were housed in individual stalls in a ventilated enclosed barn. Rams were randomly allocated to experimental diets in a changeover design arrangement, so that each animal received each of the seven experimental diets once over the course of the trial. There were seven 20‐day experimental periods consisting of 15 days for diet adaptation and 5 days for sample collection. Thus, during any given 20‐day period each ram was offered a single test ration and then moved to the next treatment in the subsequent period. The experimental diets were silages from different parts of FB with or without LB inoculants as follows: whole FB (mixture of tops and bulbs) without LB (WFB − LB); whole FB with LB (WFB + LB); FB tops without LB (FBT − LB); FB tops with LB (FBT + LB); FB bulbs without LB (FBR − LB); FB bulbs with LB (FBR + LB), corn silage (CS; Control group). The digestibility of the basal diet was first assessed, and then the experimental silage was evaluated alongside the basic diet (Table 2). During each period, half of the basal diet was replaced with the FB silages. Animals were fed twice daily at 08:00 and 16:00 h and had free access to water during the trial. During the last 5 days of each experimental period, the amount of feed offered and refused, and the total daily faeces and urine for each ram were collected separately. All animal handling and laboratory staff were blinded to the study diets.

Individual urine samples were taken for five final consecutive days, and immediately the pH was measured using a portable pH meter (Metrohm 827; pH Lab, Herisau, Switzerland).

On the last day of each experiment period, individual ruminal fluid samples (50 mL/head) were collected at 0, 3 and 6 h after feeding using a stomach tube. After the pH was determined (portable pH meter, Metrohm 827; pH Lab, Herisau, Switzerland), the samples were filtered through nylon cloth. Two 5 mL aliquots of ruminal fluid were stored at −20°C for Volatile fatty acids (VFAs) and ammonia nitrogen (NH3‐N) determination. Ruminal VFA was determined via gas chromatography (Hewlett‐Packard, model 5890, Avondale, PA) as described by the Ottenstein and Butler method (1971). Ruminal NH3‐N concentration was determined by a UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV‐1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto 604‐8511, Japan) based on AOAC (2005).

Laboratory Analysis

The pre‐dried samples of diets, refusals and faeces were ground in a Willey Mill (Arthur Hill Thomas Co. Philadelphia, PA) with a 1‐mm mesh sieve and then analysed according to AOAC (2005) for DM (method no. 934.01), CP (method no. 981.10), ash (method 94205) and ether extract (EE; method 920.29). The content of ash‐free NDF and ADF was determined as described by Van Soest et al. (1991), with the modifications proposed in the Ankom device manual (Ankom Technology Corporation, Macedon, NY). For measuring silage pH, 50 g of fresh silage was blended with 125 mL of distilled water and allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 h (Faithfull 2002). After decanting the silage extract into a small beaker, the pH was measured using a digital pH meter (Sartorius PT‐10; Germany). Two millilitres of juice from the silages were pipetted into centrifuge tubes containing 0.2 mL of acid (25% meta‐phosphoric acid and 2‐ethyl butyric acid 2 g/L as the internal standard), then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C (Galyean 1997). VFAs in the supernatant were quantified using gas chromatography (UNICAM 4600; SB Analytical, Cambridge, UK) with a flame ionization detector (FID; 250 C), split‐injection port (1.0 µL injection), capillary column (Agilent J & W HP‐FFAP, 10 m by 0.535 mm by 1.00 µm, 19095F‐121; Agilent, CA) and helium as the carrier gas (column head pressure of 10 psi). To determine NH3‐N, an extract was obtained by squeezing the silage material, filtered using Whatman 54 filter paper, then a 9 mL of aliquot was taken, mixed with 1 mL of 7.2 N H2SO4 and stored at −20°C. After thawing, the silage extracts were analysed for NH3‐N using a phenol‐hypochlorite assay (Galyean 1997). The Fleig points of the silages were also calculated as: Fleig point = 220 + [(2 × DM) − 15] − 40 × pH (Kilic 1984). Fleig points indicate the following quality classifications: 85–100, very good quality; 60–80, good quality; 55–60, moderate quality; 25–40, satisfactory quality and below 20, worthless (Denek and Can 2006).

Statistical Analysis

All data were tested for normality using PROC UNIVARIATE of SAS software (SAS 9.2, SAS/STAT, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Data that were measured only once during the experiment were analysed with the GLM procedure, whereas data repeated over time were analysed with the MIXED procedure of SAS. The model accounts for the fixed effects of diets and the random effect of the animal as the experimental unit. Least‐square means were computed and tested for differences by Tukey's test, and the differences were declared significant at p ≤ 0.05 and trends at 0.05 < p ≤ 0.10.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Composition of Silages

The chemical composition of experimental treatments is shown in Table 3. The addition of LB did not affect the chemical composition of silages, however, the DM content for FB ensiled with LB was found to be slightly higher than that for FB without the additive (p > 0.05). This indicates that the addition of LB restricted the formation of volatile compounds and the loss of DM in the form of carbon dioxide (Cao et al. 2011; Muck et al. 2018). This preservation effect can be attributed to the enhanced fermentation efficiency achieved through LB inoculation, which accelerates the pH decline and creates an environment less conducive to spoilage microorganisms and secondary fermentation processes (Wilkinson and Davies 2013).

Furthermore, microbiological enumeration indicated that an increase in initial WSC content generally led to a decrease in LB and other bacteria (Gu et al. 2025). The addition of wheat straw resulted in increased DM. The protein content in silage made from whole FB was higher than that in silage made from tops alone, attributed to reduced proteolysis. A faster rate of lactic acid production results in less proteolysis, especially in crops with high WSC concentrations, where inoculants have been shown to provide a clear advantage (Oliveira et al. 2017). Specifically, the bulbs of FB prevent proteolysis due to their high WSC content and low pH, creating an environment where proteolytic enzymes are inhibited, thereby preserving the native protein structure and nutritional value (Tabacco et al. 2011).

Feed Intake and Nutrient Digestibility

The highest and lowest dry matter intake (DMI, p < 0.001) was for WFB + LB and FBT − LB, respectively (Table 4). The highest digestibility of DM (p < 0.001) and OM (p < 0.001) was observed in CS, whereas FBT − LB showed the lowest values. The DMI of whole silage from FB was higher than that of CS, indicating greater palatability. This enhanced palatability can be attributed to the sweet taste imparted by high sugar content in FB, which is known to stimulate feed intake in ruminants (Gerlach et al. 2017). FB is a highly palatable forage and is a popular feed choice for ruminants in Denmark and the Netherlands (Amrita and Chairman 2012). Similarly, it has been documented that an increase in dietary NFC levels may lead to enhanced DMI, nutrient digestibility and MPS in the rumen (Dong et al. 2021). The underlying mechanism involves the rapid fermentation of NFC, which provides readily available energy for ruminal microorganisms, thereby stimulating their growth and metabolic activity (Wei et al. 2021). The relationship between forage quality and feed preference has been examined by many researchers. It was reported by Arzani (2009) that the chemical composition of plants significantly influences their palatability. In this study, the highest digestibility was for CS. It was also found that FB silages were less digestible than CS due to the addition of 10% wheat straw. Higher digestibility of DM and OM was associated with FB bulbs compared to FB tops. Low NDF was shown to enhance DM and OM digestibility (Filiya 2004). The higher digestibility and metabolic energy of FB bulbs may be attributed to excessive WSC accumulation in the roots. Starch content and cell wall composition significantly affect digestibility (Li 2021). Our data showed that the FB tops had lower intake and digestibility of nutrients, which agrees with El Tawab et al. (2020). Reduced feed intake with beet tops silage has been attributed to the laxative effect of beet tops (A. El Tawab et al. 2017). However, this may not fully explain the low intake of treated diets, as FBT + LB has a reduced laxative effect (Rahman et al. 2013). Other factors may also contribute to the low feed intake, including the palatability of FBT + LB compared to CS, potentially due to the presence of oxalic acid, which can irritate the digestive tract (A. El Tawab et al. 2017; Rahman et al. 2013). Additionally, the relatively low OM content of FBT + LB may negatively impact ruminal microbial activity (El Tawab et al. 2020). It was confirmed by Rahman et al. (2013) that oxalate causes rumen dysfunction and inhibits ruminal microflora, which can affect nutrient digestibility. Additionally, it was noted by Bajaj et al. (2011) that the feeding of oxalate‐containing plants led to a decrease in the population of ruminal bacteria and protozoa in buffalo calves, as well as reduced rumen motility, resulting in decreased nutrient digestibility. The inhibitory effect of oxalate on ruminal microorganisms occurs through the chelation of essential minerals, particularly calcium and magnesium, which are crucial for microbial enzyme function and cell wall integrity (Goyal 2018). These findings may help to explain the observed lower nutrient digestibility in beet tops.

Rumen Fermentation and Urine pH

Several decades ago, investigations were conducted on FB; however, the recent rise in its popularity in certain regions seems to be attributed to high yields, a well‐defined establishment protocol, and its potential value as feed for ruminants during the winter months (Fleming et al. 2021). The effect of experimental diets on rumen fermentation and urine pH is presented in Table 5. The highest (p < 0.001) ruminal pH was recorded in sheep fed WFB + LB, whereas the lowest pH values were noted in those fed FBR‐LB. The NH3‐N concentration in FBR + LB or FBR − LB was lower (p < 0.001) than in other groups. The animals fed on FBT + LB or FBT − LB had the highest (p < 0.001) acetate/propionate (A:P) ratio, followed by FB whole silage. Moreover, the highest and lowest urinary pH were for WFB − LB and FBR − LB, respectively (p < 0.001). In the present study, the pH of ruminal fluid in FB bulbs silage was lower than the other groups, attributed to its high sucrose content (NFC). In confirmation, lambs fed diets with elevated NFC/NDF ratios experienced a rapid decline in ruminal pH after each feeding (Ma et al. 2015). Similar trends were also noted in heifers (Pina et al. 2009) and dairy cows (Agle et al. 2010). The risks of acidosis linked to the feeding of ruminants with FB that is high in WSC have been thoroughly investigated (Pacheco et al. 2020). It is a well‐known fact that a decline in rumen pH and an increase in total VFA concentration are expected when diets high in sugars are fed (Oba et al. 2015), due to the rapid fermentation of sugars into VFA. In line with our results, Fleming et al. (2021) reported that feeding FB, a source of readily fermentable carbohydrates, to lactation dairy cows reduced ruminal fluid pH and rumen's fibrolytic activity. In all diets, the pH of ruminal fluid was higher before morning feeding (Figure 1). This rise may be attributed to overnight rumination and the return of N to the rumen, which stimulates saliva production. Salivary buffering properties in the rumen environment can influence rumen pH (Reddy and Hyder 2023). The consistent decrease in ruminal fluid pH 3 h post‐feeding across all dietary treatments reflects the peak fermentation activity period, during which microbial populations actively utilize available substrates for growth and metabolic processes (Davis et al. 2021). This temporal pattern corresponds with the utilization of NH3‐N by microorganisms for MPS, representing an efficient mechanism for converting dietary nitrogen into high‐quality protein (Wilkinson and Davies 2013).

Rumen ammonia concentrations in all groups were found to exceed the limit of 3.4 mmol/L, as suggested by Satter and Slyter (1974). This threshold represents the minimum NH3‐N concentration required to prevent nitrogen limitation of MPS, ensuring efficient rumen function (Reddy and Hyder 2023). Our data showed that the highest concentration of NH3‐N was for FB tops silage, whereas the lowest concentration was associated with FB bulbs silage. The protein content of FB tops (16%) is higher than that of FB bulbs (6%), leading to greater NH3‐N production. FB bulbs have more easily digestible carbohydrates than their tops, which may increase the demand for available N by amylolytic microorganisms. This rise in digestible carbohydrates can subsequently reduce NH3‐N levels in the rumen.

The highest concentration of NH3‐N occurred 3 h after feeding, whereas the lowest levels were observed 6 h after feeding (Figure 1). In confirmation, Reddy and Hyder (2023) showed that NH3‐N concentrations peaked 2–3 h after feed intake, and a diet with a higher proportion of digestible protein was associated with a higher NH3‐N. The decrease in ammonia nitrogen 6 h after feeding is attributed to its utilization by microorganisms for growth and MPS. The FB bulbs produced more propionate and butyrate than the tops, whereas the FB tops silage produced more acetate due to its higher fibre content. Changes in VFA profiles in response to sucrose or FB supplementation are variable. Some studies have reported an increase in butyric acid synthesis when starch is replaced with sucrose (Ravelo et al. 2022) or when FB is used to supplement herbage (Waghorn et al. 2018). However, it was found by Pacheco et al. (2020) that propionate concentrations increased in response to FB supplementation. These responses reflect changes in the diet's chemical composition, as the formation of propionate is enhanced by glucogenic precursors such as starch, whereas butyrate levels are associated with higher dietary sugar content from FB. WSC has been shown to alter ruminal fermentation patterns, increasing butyrate while decreasing NH3‐N concentrations (Fleming et al. 2021).

Fermentative Characteristics of Silages

The quality of silages in terms of pH, ammonia nitrogen, lactic acid and flieg point is shown in Table 6. The lowest pH (p < 0.01) and highest lactic acid concentration (p < 0.01) and flieg point score (p < 0.01) were recorded for silage made from FBR + LB, whereas the highest amount of pH and lowest concentration of lactic acid and fleigh point score were for FBT + LB or FBT − LB (p < 0.01). Silage of FB bulbs had lower pH values than FB top silage primarily attributable to the higher amount of NFC content (Table 6), which provides readily available substrates for lactic acid fermentation. This relationship between substrate availability and pH reduction aligns with the fundamental principles of silage fermentation, where WSCs serve as the primary energy source for LB proliferation and organic acid production (Desta et al. 2016). Silages containing FB bulbs received higher scores on the flieg index. The superior performance of FB bulb silages can be attributed to the significantly higher sucrose content in the root tissues, which serves as a readily available substrate for lactic acid bacteria during the initial fermentation phase (Zhang et al. 2015). The root part of FB, due to its high sucrose content, causes a rapid decrease in pH and enhances the quality of silage. The mechanism underlying this enhanced fermentation involves the preferential utilization of sucrose by homofermentative lactic acid bacteria, which convert these simple sugars directly to lactic acid through the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway (Oliveira et al. 2017). This metabolic pathway produces two molecules of lactic acid per glucose equivalent, resulting in more efficient acidification compared to the heterolactic fermentation pathways that predominate when complex carbohydrates are the primary substrate (Muck et al. 2018).

Moreover, it is well‐recognized that LB plays a crucial role in silage fermentation. LB inoculants function as biological acidifiers by rapidly dominating the microbial population during the initial anaerobic phase, thereby accelerating the fermentation process and establishing favourable conditions for preservation (Oliveira et al. 2017). Consequently, silage prepared with LB inoculants resulted in lower pH, inhibited the growth of clostridia and aerobic bacteria and improved the overall quality of the silage (Cao et al. 2011).

A fundamental prerequisite for successful silage production is the presence of sufficient quantities of easily fermentable carbohydrates, which serve as the primary substrate for lactic acid fermentation (Muck et al. 2020). The WSC content must typically exceed 3% of DM to ensure adequate fermentation, though this threshold may vary depending on the buffering capacity of the forage material (Muck et al. 2020). Research has demonstrated that the supplementation of additional fermentable carbohydrates to forage materials significantly enhances fermentation efficiency and final product quality (Gao et al. 2021). Among the silages, the pH of FBT + LB and FBT − LB was higher than that of the other silage because of its lower content of lactic acid (Kaiser et al. 2004). The silages pH ranged from 3.8 to 4.5, which is considered low enough for an acceptable preservation (Faithfull 2002). Protein degradation during fermentation is minimized in good quality silage (McDonald et al. 1991), and the higher level of NH3‐N in FBT + LB and FBT − LB compared with the others probably resulted from its higher pH, as this increase protein breakdown (Kaiser et al. 2004). Silage lactic acid levels (69.3–84.9 g/kg DM) in the present study were within the range of good quality silage (30–140 g/kg DM) characterized by ZoBell et al. (2004). The flieg point system provides a comprehensive assessment of silage quality by integrating multiple fermentation parameters, including pH, organic acid concentrations, and ammonia nitrogen levels (Andrighetto et al. 2018). Scores above 80 points indicate excellent silage quality (Andrighetto et al. 2018). The superior performance of FBT + LB in this study aligns with established principles of silage science, where the combination of adequate substrate availability and appropriate microbial enhancement yields optimal fermentation outcomes (Carvalho et al. 2021).

Conclusion

The addition of LB at a dose of (1 × 106 CFU/g fresh matter) did not significantly alter the chemical composition of the silages. In practice, whole‐plant FB silage is recommended as a suitable alternative to corn silage, particularly under drought conditions.

Suggestion

The use of a higher bacterial dose and combining this strain with other Lactobacillus species (such as Lactobacillus plantarum) is suggested for future research.
