Honeysuckle branches and lactobacillus enhance quality by changing the composition and diversity of microorganisms in alfalfa silage fermentation
Authors: Xia Liu, Dong Li, Qiang Liu, Bin Yang, Xiaojing Wang, Yan Zhang, Yan Niu, Chunxia Yang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The incorporation of honeysuckle as a silage additive in alfalfa production has yielded promising results; however, the underlying microbial mechanisms during fermentation remain poorly understood. This study leveraged high-throughput sequencing and nutrient profiling to elucidate microbial population dynamics over 45 days of anaerobic fermentation within a vacuum-assisted co-culture system comprising varying fresh weight ratios of honeysuckle branches, Lactobacillus, and alfalfa. The experimental design encompassed a Lactobacillus-supplemented treatment (M group) and an unsupplemented control (R group), each with five honeysuckle percentages (5 %, 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 25 %) and respective negative controls. Our findings reveal that the combined use of honeysuckle branches and Lactobacillus significantly impacts microbial community composition and diversity during alfalfa silage fermentation. The Mantel test underscores Lactobacillus's absence-dependent correlation of bacterial communities with pH and lactic acid, whereas its presence redirects these correlations towards total nitrogen, NH4+-N, neutral detergent fiber, and crude protein as key bacterial population drivers. Fungal populations exhibited analogous trends. Moreover, the combined additives reshaped microbial populations during anaerobic fermentation, altering interaction networks and intensifying microbial interplay. Notably, Lactobacillus introduction on a honeysuckle branch base diminished fungal core OTUs, thereby mitigating fungal contamination risks. This study also identified biomarkers unique to each treatment condition. Collectively, our research provides a molecular framework for employing honeysuckle branches and Lactobacillus as innovative silage additives in alfalfa production, fostering the development of eco-friendly and hygienic feed and farming industries.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a premium forage for dairy cattle and herbivores, underpins livestock industry growth. However, China's hay-based alfalfa production faces challenges from rainfall-induced losses during harvest [1]. Silage emerges as a cost-effective alternative, harnessing a diverse microbial community (Lactobacillus, spoilage bacteria, yeasts, molds, Bacillus) that impacts silage quality and ruminant rumen microbiota stability [2,3]. Alfalfa's low soluble carbohydrates and high buffering capacity hinder strong acidification during silage, rendering it vulnerable to harmful bacterial fermentation by Clostridium and Bacillus [4]. Traditional silage methods struggle with stability, spoilage, and leakage, necessitating additives to enhance quality and reduce nutrient loss [5,6].

A range of silage additives exists, with Lactobacillus and green juice fermentation broth topping the research agenda from 2005 to 2022, followed by cellulase [7]. While these improve silage success, controlling the fermentation process remains challenging, leading to secondary fermentation post-opening, mold growth, nutritional degradation, and even biotoxin production [8,9]. Chemical inhibitors like formic acid and potassium sorbate combat harmful bacteria but pose safety concerns due to their irritancy, corrosiveness, odor, and residual issues [10]. Thus, the quest for green, hygenic, residue-free additives to boost alfalfa silage quality and stability is pressing [11].

Honeysuckle (Lonicera japonica Flos. L), a herbal medicine, is increasingly used as a feed additive due to its abundance in chlorogenic acid, isochlorogenic acid, lignans, and other bioactive compounds offering antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antioxidant properties [12]. It inhibits pathogens while promoting beneficial flora like bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus [13]. Its bioactive components exhibit potent antibacterial activity against various pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Aspergillus flavus, Candida albicans, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Aspergillus niger [14]. Honeysuckle's antioxidant prowess surpasses BHT, and its leaf extracts significantly inhibit E. coli and S. aureus ([15,16]; Sun et al., 2002).

Despite these successes, the microbial dynamics and interaction mechanisms during honeysuckle-mediated silage fermentation remain understudied. Microbial activity is crucial, converting feed carbohydrates, proteins, and fats into organic acids, enhancing nutritional value and preventing spoilage [17]. Functional microbiota break down proteins into amino acids, modulating the intestinal environment and flora composition of feeding animals [18]. Additionally, microbial activity decomposes cellulose, generating oligosaccharides and monosaccharides, boosting feed's energy value and digestibility [19]. Microbial interactions, either antagonistic or synergistic, significantly impact silage quality, with beneficial microorganisms' antimicrobial secretions inhibiting harmful bacteria, regulating pH, and ensuring feed quality and storage safety [20,21]. Thus, unraveling these microbial intricacies holds the key to optimizing alfalfa silage with honeysuckle additives.

In summary, this study explores cost-effective honeysuckle branches as alfalfa silage additives, utilizing various percentages mixed with lactobacillus for anaerobic fermentation. High-throughput sequencing analyzes fermentation quality, microbial diversity, interactions, and aims to address issues such as: (i) fermentation and nutritional variability, (ii) microbial composition and dominant groups, (iii) optimal honeysuckle addition ratio, ensuring a theoretical and technical foundation for the sustainable feed industry.

Material and methods

Experimental design and materials

The experimental honeysuckle branches (harvest at 2021-07-18), naturally air-dried, were kindly provided by Zhongwei Sunshine Mu Farm Co., Ltd (No. 93, Ping An East Road, Shapotou District, Zhongwei City). The specific nutrient and bioactive compound compositions of various honeysuckle parts were referenced from supplementary materials (Tables S1 and S2) published by Li et al. [22]. The alfalfa samples (harvest at 2021-07-18) were sourced from Ningxia Qianyeqing Agricultural Technology Development Co., Ltd (Gaozhuang Township, Pingluo County, Ningxia) and subsequently air-dried to attain a moisture content reached 63.8 %. The lactobacillus inoculum, containing Lactobacillus plantarum and other lactobacilli species with a viable count of 1.0 × 1011 cfu g⁻1, was supplied by Taiwan ASIM Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

The raw materials in distinct treatment groups were homogenously packed into identical silage containers (polyethylene bags), evacuated using a vacuum packaging machine, and securely sealed. These containers were then subjected to anaerobic fermentation at ambient temperature for a period of 45 days. The mixed mass ratios for each treatment group were determined with reference to supplementary material (Table S3). Each treatment was replicated three times, ensuring statistical rigor. At the end of the fermentation period, samples were uniformly collected: a portion for the assessment of nutritional indicators, and another for DNA extraction.

Production of silage system

The experimental design incorporated three silage preparation methods: honeysuckle branch silage (M group), lactobacillus-inoculated silage, and a combination of both honeysuckle branches and lactobacillus (R group). For the M group, harvested honeysuckle branches were chopped into 1–2 cm segments and incorporated into the alfalfa mixture at varying fresh weight percentages (5 %, 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, and 25 % of alfalfa). These mixtures were then vacuum-packed and stored at room temperature for fermentation. For lactobacillus-inoculated silage, 500 g of green forage was uniformly mixed with 5 mL of bacterial suspension (control group RCK received 5 mL of sterile water instead). Following vacuum packaging, the mixtures were stored under identical room temperature conditions for fermentation. The R group combined both approaches, where honeysuckle branches (1–2 cm segments) were mixed with alfalfa at the same percentages mentioned above, and subsequently inoculated with lactobacillus. Determination of nutrients and active ingredients

Sample pH was measured using a Remagnet S-3C Precision Meter (Shanghai Precision Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.), following a protocol that involved blending 10 g of sample with 90 mL of distilled water, homogenizing for 1 min on a juicer, and filtering through filter paper and four layers of gauze to obtain the filtrate for pH analysis. Dry matter (DM) content was determined by the method outlined in "Determination of Moisture in Feed" [9], with DM content calculated as 1 minus moisture content. Water soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content was quantified spectrophotometrically, adhering to "Determination of Total Sugars in Feeds" [9]. Total nitrogen (TN) content was assessed using the Kjeldahl method, as specified in "Determination of Crude Protein in Feeds" [9], with crude protein (CP) content derived by multiplying TN by 6.25. Acid detergent fiber (ADF) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) were determined in accordance with NY/T 1459–2007 and GB/T 20806-2006 respectively. Ammoniacal nitrogen content was measured via the phenol-sodium hypochlorite colorimetric method. The quantification of organic acids (lactic acid, formic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid) was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). All nutrient and bioactive component analyses were conducted at the Ningxia Institute of Agricultural Products Quality Standards and Testing Technology, China, adhering to relevant industry standards.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was extracted from the experimental samples utilizing the Power DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio Laboratories Inc., Carlsbad, USA), adhering strictly to the manufacturer's protocol as described in Liu et al 2022. Subsequent to the completion of genomic DNA extraction, the quality and presence of the extracted DNA were verified through 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. For the amplification of bacterial 16 S rRNA V3-V4 region and fungal ITS1 gene fragment, universal primer pairs 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′)/806R (5′-GGACTAACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) and 1737 F (5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′)/2043R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′), respectively, were employed, following the guidelines of Xu et al. [23] and Zhang et al. [24]. The PCR amplification was conducted under optimized conditions, initiated with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 35 s, annealing at 55 °C for 35 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. A final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min was performed to ensure complete amplification. The PCR reactions were executed in triplicate, each reaction mixture containing 20 μL of a blend comprising 2 μL of 2.5 mM deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate (dNTPs), 4 μL of 5 × Fast Pfu buffer, 0.4 μL of Fast Pfu polymerase, 0.4 μL of each primer at a concentration of 5 μM, and 10 ng of template DNA, as outlined in Liu et al. [25].

Illumina library construction and sequencing

Following the PCR-mediated addition of the Illumina adapter sequence to the exterior termini of the target region, the resulting PCR product was excised from the gel using a dedicated gel extraction kit. The extracted DNA was then eluted in Tris-HCl buffer and validated through 2 % agarose gel electrophoresis to construct the sequencing library. Subsequently, PCR synthesis was initiated using the purified DNA fragment as template, with the fixed base sequence present on the sequencing chip serving as the primer for on-chip synthesis of the target DNA fragments. After denaturation and annealing steps, the opposite end of each DNA fragment on the chip underwent random complementation with a nearby primer, facilitating PCR amplification to generate DNA clusters. These clusters were subsequently linearized into single-stranded templates. By introducing DNA polymerase and fluorescently labeled dNTPs, the nucleotide species incorporated during the initial round of polymerization for each template sequence were detected. Chemical cleavage of the "fluorescent group" and "termination group" ensued, restoring the 3′ end for further elongation. The sequences of the template DNA fragments were deduced by counting the fluorescence signals accumulated in each sequencing cycle. The entire library preparation and sequencing process was expertly conducted by Shanghai Majorbio (http://www.majorbio.bioon.com).

Sequencing data quality control and filtering

To generate high-quality sequences suitable for downstream analysis, paired-end (PE) reads were merged based on an overlap of at least 10 base pairs (bp), allowing for a maximum mismatch ratio of 0.2 in the overlapping region. Mismatched sequences were subsequently filtered out to ensure sequence accuracy. Sample discrimination was achieved by identifying unique barcode and primer sequences at the termini of each read, followed by sequence orientation adjustment. From the optimized dataset, non-repetitive sequences were extracted, and singleton sequences (i.e., those appearing only once) were discarded in accordance with established protocols (http://drive5.com/usearch/manual/singletons.html). The remaining non-repetitive sequences were then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a similarity threshold of 97 %, a standard practice for microbial community analysis. During this clustering process, chimeric sequences were identified and removed to maintain the integrity of the OTU dataset. Representative sequences for each OTU were selected, and the optimized sequences were mapped against these representatives. Sequences displaying 97 % or greater similarity to an OTU representative were assigned to that OTU, thereby constructing the final OTU table for subsequent statistical and ecological analyses, as described by Lucas et al. [26].

Data analysis

To ensure consistency across samples for subsequent analyses, the minimum number of randomly extracted sequences from each sample was utilized to compute the alpha diversity index, adhering to the methodology described by Ze-Bin et al. [27]. Statistical significance in differences among indices was assessed using the LSD-test implemented in DPS software, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Box plot visualizations of the α-diversity index, species composition, and Venn diagrams were generated using R software (version 3.5.2). Principal coordinate analysis (PcoA), phylogenetic tree construction, and Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) were performed on the Major's BioCloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com), applying a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) threshold of ≥2 for both bacterial and fungal taxa. This platform facilitated a comprehensive exploration of microbial community structures. To investigate microbial interactions, Spearman correlation coefficients and Mantel tests were employed, leveraging R software (version 4.0.3), to quantify associations among microbial populations. The resulting co-occurrence networks were visually represented using Gephi software (version 0.10), providing insights into the intricate relationships within the microbial community.

Results

In the treatment group devoid of lactobacillus (M), the inclusion of varying percentages of fresh honeysuckle branches significantly altered the pH, NH4+-N, TN, and crude protein content (p < 0.05). Specifically, an increasing proportion of honeysuckle branches led to a marked reduction in pH, with the 25 % inclusion rate resulting in a significant 6.8 % decline compared to the control (CK). Concurrently, NH4+-N, TN, and crude protein levels exhibited a downward trend with increasing honeysuckle percentages. Following the introduction of lactobacillus (R), notable changes were observed in the measured chemical composition (p < 0.05), except for crude ash content. The previously observed decreasing trends persisted, albeit with one notable exception: dry matter content increased significantly (p < 0.05) with rising honeysuckle proportions, peaking at a 20.02 % enhancement in the 25 % treatment group compared to CK (Table 1). Employing honeysuckle branches as an additive in alfalfa silage resulted in substantial variations in microbial populations after 45 days of anaerobic fermentation, with distinct treatments incorporating alfalfa (Fig. 1a). PCoA analysis of bacterial (Fig. 1b) and fungal (Fig. 1c) communities revealed discernible differences in distances upon dimensionality reduction of the microbial data. Notably, the sample points were significantly segregated across the treatment groups, indicating that the experimental conditions had a profound impact on the microbial populations. Furthermore, the principal components PC1 and PC2 collectively accounted for over 60 % of the total variability observed, underscoring their importance in explaining the microbial community structure.

The analysis of alpha diversity illuminated the influence of species richness and the incorporation of Lactobacillus at varying concentrations on the overall microbial community. In the absence of Lactobacillus, the concentration stratification had a significant effect on the bacterial Shannon index (p < 0.05), while the fungal alpha diversity remained largely unaffected by this variable (Fig. 2a). Notably, the introduction of Lactobacillus markedly altered the Ace index of bacteria and the Sobs index of fungi (Fig. 2b), introducing a gradient of variation across concentrations. These findings suggest that Lactobacillus addition modifies the consistency and interactions within the entire microbial community. Discussion

Impact of honeysuckle branches on the quality and fermentation characteristics of alfalfa silage

In this study, we explored the alterations in silage nutrient and bioactive component profiles using a vacuum-assisted mixed culture system integrating honeysuckle branches, Lactobacillus, and alfalfa, leveraging high-throughput sequencing techniques [28]. Our findings revealed that incorporating a low percentage of honeysuckle branches significantly decreased pH, NH4+-N, TN, and crude protein levels in the culture system, with a more pronounced pH reduction observed with increasing honeysuckle proportions. This decline in NH4+-N, TN, and crude protein content could be attributed to their binding with lignin and cellulose in honeysuckle branches, thereby limiting microbial access [29,30]. Notably, Lactobacillus addition further modulated the chemical composition, except for dry matter content, which paradoxically increased with honeysuckle proportion, potentially due to microbial catabolism releasing nutrients bound to lignin and cellulose [31,32].

Moreover, prior to Lactobacillus introduction, the maximum honeysuckle proportion (25 %) led to peaks in propionic acid, soluble carbohydrates, neutral detergent fiber, and acid detergent fiber contents, attributed to the high fiber and carbohydrate content of honeysuckle branches enhancing water retention and fiber content [33,34]. However, post-Lactobacillus addition, these peaks shifted to 20 % honeysuckle treatment, indicating a synergistic effect with other microorganisms in converting soluble carbohydrates to propionic acid and modulating fiber degradation [35].

Regarding organic acids, lactic acid peaked in the 10 % treatment, reflecting Lactobacillus's homolactic fermentation pathway, while acetic acid peaked in the 5 % treatment, suggesting involvement of the heterolactic fermentation pathway [35]. These results underscore the potential of combining Lactobacillus with honeysuckle branches to optimize silage fermentation, enhancing nutrient profiles and bioactive components through microbial mediation and substrate utilization.

Impact of honeysuckle branches on microbial composition and diversity during silage fermentation, along with biomarker screening

The utilization of honeysuckle branches as an additive in alfalfa silage fermentation profoundly influenced microbial populations, as evidenced by significant alterations in community composition [36]. These changes can be attributed to the antimicrobial properties inherent in honeysuckle, primarily due to bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and saponins, which exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity [37,38]. During fermentation, these compounds selectively foster the proliferation of beneficial microbial species while suppressing undesirable microorganisms.

Furthermore, the study demonstrated that Lactobacillus introduction markedly impacted the α-diversity of both bacterial and fungal communities, reflecting a degree of selectivity within the experimental systems. It is postulated that this selectivity arises from Lactobacillus's diverse metabolic activities, which modulate environmental pH and nutrient availability, thereby creating favorable niches for specific microbial taxa. The validation of sequencing depth and microbial diversity patterns through dilution curves of α-diversity indices underscores the robustness of the findings and excludes sequencing artifacts as a contributing factor to the observed α-diversity differences across treatment groups [39].

Species abundance maps illuminated the prevailing microbial populations under varying treatment conditions. Notably, the dominance of Enterococcus in bacterial communities without Lactobacillus supplementation and its subsequent decline upon Lactobacillus introduction underscore Lactobacillus's pivotal regulatory function in shaping the fermentation system's microbial ecology [40]. In contrast, fungal populations exhibited differential responses, with Clavispora demonstrating heightened sensitivity to concentration gradients compared to Lactobacillus-supplemented treatments, indicating species-specific environmental adaptability [41]. The reduction in fungal core OTUs post-Lactobacillus addition suggests Lactobacillus plays a crucial role in modulating fungal interactions, potentially leading to a loss of core diversity [42].

Regarding biomarkers, bacterial groups such as Oceanobacillus and Staphylococcales emerged as significant in non-Lactobacillus treatments, whereas Staphylococcales were excluded post-Lactobacillus and honeysuckle-alfalfa co-culture, highlighting Lactobacillus's influence on bacterial community structure [28,43]. Fungal biomarkers like Wickerhamomyceanomalus and Issatchenkia, though understudied in animal gut microbiology and silage systems, emerged as characteristic markers in this study, implying their potential importance in microbiota regulation during anaerobic fermentation and presenting avenues for future research and application [31,44].

Interaction between nutrient-active substances and microbiota during fermentation, along with functional prediction

The Mantel test was employed to assess the correlation between selected nutrient active ingredients and the matrices of bacterial and fungal communities, along with their predicted functional profiles, within the alfalfa silage culture system augmented with honeysuckle branches [45]. In the absence of Lactobacillus, bacterial communities exhibited significant correlations with pH and lactic acid content. However, Lactobacillus addition shifted the drivers of bacterial population structure to include TN, NH4+-N, neutral detergent fiber, and crude protein, enhancing the sensitivity of bacterial functional substrates to perturbations by neutral detergent fiber and acetic acid [46]. Fungal populations mirrored these trends, with pH, NH4+-N, and crude protein becoming significantly correlated with population and function in the presence of Lactobacillus. These findings suggest that the integration of honeysuckle branches with modified microbial communities and functional substrates holds promise for enhancing silage quality and nutritional value, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms [47].

Database-guided functional variability analysis traced potential functional shifts in microorganisms induced by experimental conditions. Post-Lactobacillus introduction, the addition of fresh honeysuckle branches elicited specific functional variations in both fungi and bacteria, indicating microbial metabolic alterations within the fermentation system. These functional changes likely correlate with alterations in silage's active ingredients, necessitating rigorous protocols to validate functional-active substance interactions [41].

Interactions among microorganisms during the fermentation process influenced by honeysuckle branches

Previous research has focused on silage additives inhibiting harmful bacterial growth through direct chemical inhibition and environmentally induced production of bacterial inhibitory active substances by microorganisms [47]. However, the competitive dynamics among microbial populations for survival resources has been overlooked [48]. The incorporation of honeysuckle branches during fermentation significantly altered the original microbial interaction network. Specifically, the combination of honeysuckle branches and Lactobacillus augmented negative correlations within the bacterial network, with a similar trend observed in the fungal network. This suggests heightened microbial activity and intensified interspecific competition within the silage fermentation system, possibly contributing to the suppression of harmful bacterial blooms [49]. Additionally, fermentation of honeysuckle-like herbs is postulated to rupture cell walls, releasing active substances and degrading larger macromolecules into smaller, more bioavailable forms [50]. These metabolites may serve as intermediates, modulating microbial interactions within the system, reshaping the reciprocal network, and ultimately inhibiting bacterial proliferation [51].

Conclusion

The integration of honeysuckle branches and Lactobacillus during alfalfa silage fermentation exerts a profound influence on microbial community composition and diversity. Through a vacuum-assisted incubation approach incorporating varying ratios of honeysuckle branches, we delved into the microbial mechanisms underlying their synergistic inhibition of harmful microorganisms during fermentation. Our findings reveal that Lactobacillus addition mitigates the adverse chemical effects of an optimal honeysuckle proportion while amplifying its beneficial impacts. This combined additive strategy remodels the microbial landscape during anaerobic fermentation, reshaping interaction networks and intensifying microbial interplay. The observed α-diversity disparities among treatments underscore the selective enrichment of beneficial microbial species and the suppression of detrimental bacteria like Staphylococcaceae. Species abundance maps underscore Lactobacillus's pivotal regulatory function in crafting microbial communities, while Lactobacillus introduction subsequent to honeysuckle branches diminishes fungal core OTUs, mitigating fungal contamination risks. LEfSe analysis pinpointed bacterial biomarkers including Lactobacillus and Enterococcus, alongside fungal markers such as Wickerhamomyceanomalus and Issatchenkia, offering potential research avenues. This study underscores the potential of Lactobacillus-honeysuckle branch blends to enhance silage quality and process efficiency, with broader implications for microbial-driven strategies aimed at bolstering animal health and productivity.
