Strengthening Indigenous community engagement in clinical research: a formative evaluation in Northern British Columbia
Authors: Júlia Campos Clímaco, Laura McNab-Coombs, Rheanna Robinson, Paul Winwood, Anurag Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBuilding meaningful relationships with Indigenous communities is essential to effective clinical research in Northern British Columbia. This formative evaluation describes a journey of cocreating a culturally grounded framework for clinical research partnerships. Through extensive engagement with rural and remote Indigenous communities, this initiative identified community perspectives on research priorities, systemic barriers and principles essential to building trust, reciprocity and respect. The resulting framework offers practical guidance for researchers, institutions and partners committed to decolonising research and promoting Indigenous health equity through community-led approaches.Keywords: Health Services Accessibility, Public Health, Descriptive study

Introduction

In Canada, approximately 18% of the population resides in rural settings, often facing increased costs and limited access to essential services, including healthcare. These disparities disproportionately affect Indigenous Peoples and accentuate the critical need for policies and interventions to improve access and address systemic inequities.1,3

Northern British Columbia (BC) spans approximately 650 000 km², covering two-thirds of the province. The Northern Health (NH) region includes 55 First Nations communities with 17 distinct Indigenous linguistic groups, underscoring the region’s rich cultural and linguistic diversity. Healthcare access is challenged by both distance and systemic inequities.4 5

Healthcare disparities in the NH region are particularly pronounced when compared with the region’s more urban areas.6 For Indigenous Peoples, these rural health disparities are compounded by the historical and contemporary harms and injustices committed through the Canadian Healthcare System.3 Indeed, most notably, through the establishment of Indian Residential Schools and ‘Indian hospitals’,7 8 non-consensual clinical trials and medical experimentation have violated ethical standards and contributed to a legacy of trauma and scepticism among Indigenous Peoples toward medical systems, institutions9,11 and medical research initiatives.812,15

Clinical research is vital in advancing healthcare by developing and distributing effective health products, such as drugs, vaccines and diagnostics. It allows for the development of personalised, targeted therapies that have the potential to alter the trajectory of disease progression and prognosis. Further, clinical research is crucial for ensuring the safety and efficacy of new and emerging treatments by providing essential data to support regulatory approvals. Therefore, ensuring equitable access to clinical research for vulnerable populations while maintaining tailored protections based on the risk level of the study is essential for improving healthcare outcomes.16 17 Despite the complexities in demonstrating the direct benefits of clinical research, participation can improve care processes, enhance infrastructure and attract professionals to the healthcare systems in need.18 There is a distinct need for diverse populations to be included in clinical research to ensure that safety and efficacy apply to all who may need the intervention.12 17

Indigenous Peoples in Canada demonstrate remarkable strength and resilience, rooted in their rich cultural traditions, knowledge systems and deep connections to the land. Despite enduring the ongoing impacts of colonisation, systemic inequities and intergenerational trauma, Indigenous Peoples persevere to realise the revitalisation of languages, assert their self-determination and implement community-led health and wellness initiatives. Self-determined healthcare models like the First Nations Health Authority in BC demonstrate how Indigenous leadership has reclaimed control over healthcare delivery, cultural safety and health services that honour community values and traditions.19 20

Through clinical research cocreation with Indigenous Peoples and their communities, there is an opportunity to learn and inform the development of a culturally safe pathway for testing new health interventions. Here, clinical trials may become a platform for integrating Indigenous knowledge systems, ensuring they are culturally appropriate and contextually relevant.21 Shifting the approach in clinical research to one that values co-leadership with communities ensures the development of scientifically sound, respectful health interventions that support the priorities and needs of Indigenous Peoples and communities.22 23

In 2022, the Northern Centre for Clinical Research (NCCR) was established as a tripartite partnership between the University of BC, NH and the University of Northern BC to promote the cohesion of existing clinical and life sciences programmes, enhance capacity for clinical research in Northern BC and address health disparities among rural and Indigenous Peoples. Located on the ancestral and unceded territory of the Lheidli T’enneh First Nation, members of the Dakelh Peoples, in the community now known as Prince George, the NCCR leadership recognised the need for community engagement as a foundational step towards advancing clinical research in Northern BC.

Community engagement is recognised as a central component of ethical and effective health research, yet it remains conceptually underdeveloped and methodologically heterogeneous, with limited consistency in how it is defined and operationalised. Definitions vary across the global health literature, with terms such as participation, mobilisation and collaboration often used interchangeably.24 25 While some frameworks position engagement along a continuum from outreach to shared leadership, others emphasise its ethical role in promoting trust, legitimacy and equitable partnerships.26 27 Both ‘community’ and ‘engagement’ are therefore context-dependent, reflecting dynamic, internally diverse and overlapping identities within and across communities.28 In response, this study understands community engagement as a relational, collaborative and iterative process through which communities and researchers co-shape research priorities, processes and outcomes.

Design and processes

Laying the groundwork

The engagement activities described were undertaken as a quality improvement initiative, distinct from traditional research. The primary objective was to build foundational relationships and understand community health priorities to inform future clinical research.

Indigenous community engagement must respect Indigenous rights, values and knowledge systems while addressing cultural safety and power imbalances to support a decolonised research process and prioritise self-determination.824 29,32 By adhering to these principles, the NCCR aimed for inclusive, respectful and effective community engagement with rural, remote and Indigenous populations.

A dedicated ‘Community Liaison Worker’ (CLW) position was created and supported by NCCR staff, leadership and a postdoctorate research associate trained in qualitative research methodology, who created interview guides for community engagement. The CLW’s role was to build relationships with communities to gather knowledge through conversations, interviews and participation in community events. Community engagement was defined as involving communities in decision-making33 and actively informing the process of determining the priorities, identifying solutions and developing resources. In practice, this was approached as a relational and iterative process, shaped through ongoing interactions with community members rather than implemented as a discrete or standardised set of activities. This approach aligns with community-engaged and formative research traditions, which emphasise continuous learning, adaptation and responsiveness to community context.34 Engagement activities included relationship-building, ongoing dialogue and iterative feedback across these interactions, reflecting a range of involvement from information-sharing to more collaborative exchanges.26 34 By describing how engagement was enacted through these interactions, this study contributes to addressing gaps in the literature related to the limited clarity in how community engagement is implemented and documented in practice.25

Alongside identifying over 10 Indigenous communities for NCCR engagement, the process initially involved diverse institutions and associations throughout Northern BC, including the BC Rural Health Network, Carrier Sekani Family Services, First Nations Health Authority and the Prince George Native Friendship Centre, among others (the complete list of 21 engaged institutions and organisations is provided in online supplemental table 1). These organisations provided valuable preliminary insights and perspectives, contributing to an understanding of the unique healthcare needs and challenges faced by Indigenous communities in the region.

Listening and learning approach

This formative evaluation initiative used a grounded theory-informed approach to systematically reflect on and learn from community engagement experiences. These principles guided the iterative and reflective process of community interactions, facilitating the emergence of key themes and insights without formal coding of interview transcripts or structured data analysis. Instead, iterative reflection and synthesis were based on detailed notes, meeting summaries and team discussions. Key learnings

The outreach activities established a foundation for NCCR’s ongoing and future community engagement efforts, setting the stage for impactful and sustainable clinical research collaborations based on trust. Key learnings from this work include:

Trust as a foundation of ethical engagementEstablishing trust is crucial for effective engagement in clinical research, as it promotes open communication and collaboration. A trauma-informed and reconciliatory approach with community-driven strategies ensures active participation in decision-making and the use of culturally safe methodologies. Moreover, it is essential to recognise that Indigenous communities across Northern BC are distinct culturally and linguistically; therefore, a distinctions-based approach should always be considered.

Trust as a foundation of ethical engagement

Establishing trust is crucial for effective engagement in clinical research, as it promotes open communication and collaboration. A trauma-informed and reconciliatory approach with community-driven strategies ensures active participation in decision-making and the use of culturally safe methodologies. Moreover, it is essential to recognise that Indigenous communities across Northern BC are distinct culturally and linguistically; therefore, a distinctions-based approach should always be considered.

Structural barriers to access clinical research in Northern BCGeographical isolation, high transportation costs and limited access to timely emergency services continue to be structural barriers for rural and Indigenous Peoples and their communities. These are further complicated by longstanding experiences of systemic racism and historical trauma, which contribute to deep-rooted mistrust in healthcare systems.3 Opportunities to participate in clinical research are limited, with many individuals needing to travel to urban centres or even out of province, to access trials for conditions such as childhood illnesses, cancer, neurodegenerative diseases and rare genetic disorders. Clinical research activities were described as siloed, fragmented and not well integrated with the healthcare needs of rural and Indigenous communities.

Discussion

Our engagement activities revealed several interrelated themes that underscore the systemic challenges and transformative possibilities in clinical research with Indigenous communities. Central to this work is the recognition that trust cannot be presumed and must be earned through sustained relational ethics, cultural humility and community-driven processes. This emphasis reflects a broader imperative to reimagine clinical research as a scientific endeavour and a social contract grounded in respect, equity and justice.41,44

These findings also resonate with broader debates in global health on the role, scope and challenges of community engagement. While community engagement is widely framed as an ethical imperative that supports trust, legitimacy and more equitable research,26,28 it has also been critically examined as a practice that may reproduce existing power imbalances and place disproportionate burdens on communities,45 such as research fatigue, uneven distribution of labour and expectations of sustained community involvement without corresponding resources or institutional support.45 Community engagement in this initiative was enacted through relational, ongoing interactions that supported the identification of community priorities and helped inform future clinical research efforts in this regional context. By providing a grounded account of how engagement was enacted in practice, this work responds to calls for greater clarity and methodological detail in the implementation of community engagement.25 27 It also illustrates the time and relational effort required to support meaningful engagement, particularly in settings shaped by historical inequities and structural barriers to care.

A long history of colonisation, medical experimentation and systemic racism has shaped the structural exclusion of Indigenous Peoples from clinical research. These histories have not only eroded trust but have structurally shaped the methods, ethics and priorities of research in ways that continue to marginalise Indigenous knowledge systems. Our findings align with global critiques of extractive research models that privilege academic and institutional agendas over community-defined needs.12,1422 46 In response, our approach, which centred on community engagement from the outset, demonstrates that ethically sound and scientifically robust research must begin with community coleadership.8 22 24 42

The NCCR’s engagement efforts were not without challenges. Geographical and logistical barriers complicated access to over 50 Indigenous communities. More profoundly, the process of establishing trust with communities impacted by historical trauma and systemic racism demanded time, effort and a deep commitment to reconciliation. However, the outreach led by the NCCR laid a critical foundation of trust with local communities—an essential step for the success of future clinical research. These relationships require compassion, cultural humility and a long-term dedication to relationships and healing that goes beyond the life cycle of a particular project.

Authentic engagement in this context also requires reckoning with epistemic indebtedness, the moral obligation that researchers and institutions bear toward the communities whose knowledge, lived experience and relational generosity sustain the research enterprise.47 As in the context of Northern BC, where Indigenous knowledge systems have been systematically marginalised, this obligation demands concrete reciprocity: returning findings to communities, prioritising community-defined outcomes and ensuring demonstrable benefit. It is also known that power differentials in research operate not only through overt institutional structures but through internalised racism, the adoption, through repeated social conditioning, of beliefs about one’s own inferiority that can manifest at institutional, interpersonal and individual levels.48 It is therefore an ethical imperative to practice sustained and structured reflexivity throughout every phase of engagement, rather than a one-time disclosure of positionality.

These principles also align with findings from an environmental scan by Robinson and Best, which provides a comprehensive review of Indigenous engagement guidelines for health research and programme evaluation at national and BC-based community levels.8 The report synthesises best practices, principles and processes to promote meaningful, ethical and culturally safe engagement with Indigenous Peoples while highlighting critical gaps in existing frameworks.

Importantly, communities voiced a desire to move beyond participation toward governance. This reflects a profound shift: from communities being seen as research subjects to becoming cocreators of knowledge.23 46 49 Power-sharing mechanisms, such as embedding First Nations principles of Ownership, Control, Access and Posession (OCAP) are not just technical tools but provides a governanace mechanism that addreses historical imbalances essential to redistributing authority within clinical research .22 41 Such frameworks challenge conventional metrics of success, calling for more expansive indicators such as community capacity-building, sustained relationships and self-determined outcomes.42 50 51

We recognise this shift from participation toward governance requires a systematic analysis of the interests, values, tensions and power dynamics among all parties involved in the research.52 Effective engagement demands moving toward active conavigation: communities must be involved not only in data generation but in surfacing tensions, aligning interests across governance levels and shaping solutions from the outset.52 For the NCCR, this underscores the structural rationale of its phased framework, one in which governance structures, not merely engagement activities, are co-designed with Indigenous communities and adjusted iteratively in response to the interests and realities of each nation to enable authentic decision making authority.

Although geographically focused on Northern BC, the insights generated here have broader implications for global health. Across diverse Indigenous and historically excluded communities—from the Māori in Aotearoa to Arctic Sámi populations—calls for decolonised, participatory and community-led research models are intensifying.19 20 24 Our findings offer transferable principles for global health actors seeking to build equitable and accountable clinical research systems that respect Indigenous sovereignty and knowledge.

Insights from NCCR’s engagement activities revealed a critical gap: conventional research frameworks are often misaligned with the lived realities, governance structures and priorities of Indigenous communities in Northern BC. These gaps risk perpetuating extractive practices and eroding trust. In response, we developed a community-driven framework grounded in Indigenous principles, cultural safety and shared decision-making. It aims to embed equity and relational accountability across all stages of clinical research and support ethical, inclusive and locally relevant approaches.

To inform the creation of our tailored NCCR community-engagement framework, we reviewed existing Indigenous and community engagement frameworks to identify foundational best practices, adapt proven principles to our regional context and explicitly align our approach with culturally safe engagement strategies.2224 42,44 49 50 53 Core principles from these frameworks directly informed the development of the specific phases within our NCCR approach, as summarised in table 2.

The NCCR’s phased approach reflects core elements of effective community engagement identified in the global health literature. Adhikari et al drawing on experience implementing complex health research in remote and isolated communities, identified five evidence-based elements of effective engagement: multiscale authority and community engagement, integration of local human resources, formative research to understand the social and economic context, responsiveness to emerging community needs and shared control and leadership in decision-making.26 Each of these elements is embedded across the NCCR’s Exploration, Planning and Delivery phases. Cunningham-Erves et al further demonstrated that iterative formative research, incorporating community advisory structures, community-based organisation leadership and culturally grounded feedback cycles, is essential to designing clinical research programmes that are genuinely appropriate to and accepted by underserved communities.34 Collectively, these frameworks position NCCR’s community-driven approach within an established evidence base for equitable clinical research engagement.

Integrating Bharadwaj’s22 emphasis on reciprocal relationships into our exploration and planning phases ensured that initial community interactions were authentically centred on relational ethics, avoiding predetermined research outcomes. The principles of OCAP informed our Design and Delivery Phases, prioritising community ownership, data sovereignty and ethical standards. The Indigenous Research Level of Engagement Tool provided essential guidance for our initial trust-building and dialogue, validating our approach to relationship development. Similarly, the First Nations Health Authority’s guidelines shaped our planning and design strategies, emphasising cultural safety and distinctions-based approaches. Collectively, these frameworks validated our community-engagement strategies, enabling culturally safe, responsive and community-led approaches tailored to the diverse contexts of Northern BC.

Conclusion

Reimagining clinical research through indigenous partnership

This formative evaluation presents a community-driven framework to transform clinical research practices with Indigenous communities in Northern BC, responding to broader calls for conceptual clarity and practical guidance in the implementation of community engagement in health research.25 27 It offers a relational and practice-based pathway for embedding cultural safety, relational accountability and Indigenous self-determination into every phase of the research lifecycle. Yet its significance extends beyond regional relevance as it contributes to a growing global movement to decolonise health research and elevate Indigenous leadership.

Success will be measured through qualitative and quantitative metrics, including community participation, trust and relationship building, research relevance and impact and capacity building. Metrics can include the number and diversity of community members actively participating in clinical research projects, the level of trust and satisfaction among community members regarding their involvement in research and research outcomes addressing the specific health needs and priorities of the communities, as well as community members leading and contributing to research projects will be measured through their participation in training and leadership roles in research activities.

Charting a path forward

Despite the progress made, several questions remain unanswered. How can the framework be further refined to address emerging challenges and incorporate new insights from ongoing research? What are the long-term impacts of this framework on health clinical research in Northern BC’s rural, remote and Indigenous communities? How can we ensure sustained community engagement and prevent research fatigue over time? These questions highlight the need for continuous evaluation and adaptation of the framework to ensure its effectiveness and relevance towards health equity.

To answer these questions, future directions will include piloting this framework across additional communities, refining it through longitudinal feedback loops and evaluating its impact on community trust, health outcomes and research relevance.

Ultimately, this work calls for recalibrating clinical research as a relational, ethical and community-grounded practice. Only through such a shift can clinical research contribute meaningfully to health equity for Indigenous and marginalised communities in Canada and globally.

Community input also informed the design of outreach materials and will guide the dissemination of findings, including presentations back to participating communities in accessible formats. This manuscript reflects the shared priorities, values and voices of the communities engaged.
