ProcTex: Consistent and Interactive Text-to-texture Synthesis for Procedural Models CCS: Computing Methodologies Shape Modeling
Ruiqi Xu, Zihan Zhu, Xianghao Xu, Srinath Sridhar, Daniel Ritchie

Abstract
. Recent advancement in 2D image diffusion models has driven significant progress in text-guided texture synthesis, enabling realistic, high-quality texture generation from arbitrary text prompts. However, current methods usually focus on synthesizing texture for single static 3D objects, and struggle to handle entire families of shapes, such as those produced by procedural programs. Applying existing methods naively to each procedural shape is too slow to support exploring different parameter settings at interactive rates, and also results in inconsistent textures across the procedural shapes. To this end, we introduce ProcTex, the first text-to-texture system designed for procedural 3D models. ProcTex enables consistent and real-time text-guided texture synthesis for families of shapes, which integrates seamlessly with the interactive design flow of procedural models. To ensure consistency, our core approach is to generate texture for the shape produced by one setting of the procedural parameters, followed by a texture transfer stage to apply the texture to other parameter settings. We also develop several techniques, including a novel UV displacement network for real-time texture transfer, the retexturing pipeline to support structural changes from discrete procedural parameters, and part-level UV texture map generation for local appearance editing. Extensive experiments on a diverse set of professional procedural models validate ProcTex’s ability to produce high-quality, visually consistent textures while supporting real-time, interactive applications.

1.
Introduction

Humans perceive and interact with the world in three dimensions. To digitally replicate this experience, high-quality 3D asset creation methods are indispensable, underpinning a wide range of applications such as autonomous driving
(
Ost et al. 2021
;
Tancik et al. 2022
)
, robotics
(
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Authors 2024
)
, VR/AR
(
Avetisyan et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2024
)
, and video games
(
Raad et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
. Texturing plays a critical role in the creation of 3D assets, as it not only assigns appearance details to 3D objects but also integrates seamlessly with standard computer graphics pipelines, facilitating downstream tasks such as editing and rendering. While textures have traditionally been created manually, recent advances in 3D generative models have introduced mechanisms
(
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Cao et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023a
)
to synthesize textures for 3D objects based on high-level user control, such as text prompts describing the desired appearance.

Aside from text-guided texture synthesis, a popular approach of creating 3D assets is procedural modeling, which uses programs or rules to generate an entire parametric family of shapes. This approach offers two notable advantages: First, procedural parameters are often interpretable (e.g., the length and width of a car model), making the design process intuitive for users. Secondly, procedural modeling enables rapid generation of numerous assets for quick iteration and interactive customization. While text-guided texture synthesis and procedural modeling each merits their own strengths, combining them effectively remains vastly under-explored. Existing text-based texturing systems are not well suited to the interactive workflows that procedural models are designed to support. A straightforward approach is to employ these systems to independently texture each output of a procedural model; however, these outputs would be inconsistent with one another due to randomness in the texture generation process and incur flickering when users slide over parameters. In addition, rerunning the texture generation for each output would be too slow to support exploring different parameter settings at interactive rates.

In this paper, we consider the problem of developing a system for text-based texturing of procedural models. Specifically, given a procedural model and a user-defined text prompt, our goal is to synthesize appropriate texture for shapes generated by any setting of the procedural parameters. Such a system should support procedural models that exhibit both structural changes and continuous deformations in response to parameter changes. Moreover, to enable seamless design exploration, the generated textures must be consistent, in the sense that there is no visual flicker as the procedural parameters are smoothly altered.
The system should also generate the textures fast enough to deliver results at interactive rates, allowing users to modify parameters on the fly. Finally, the generated textures should be easily editable by users while preserving output consistency across parameter settings.

With the above objectives in mind, we present ProcTex, a system for text-based texturing of procedural models. Our approach builds on existing single-shape texturing frameworks that iteratively paint textures from multiple views using image diffusion models
(
Tang et al. 2024a
)
. We represent the generated texture as UV maps, allowing users to edit them easily.

To maintain consistency across various parameter settings, our system generates texture for a shape produced by one configuration of the procedural parameters and then transfer this texture to other configurations. Our model supports procedural models with both structural changes and continuous parametric variation. Concretely, we assume each procedural model outputs a set of mesh components, which may deform non-rigidly under continuous parameters. For discrete parameters, if a new component is introduced, our system synthesizes a texture map for it; if a component is removed, we detect newly exposed regions on existing components and inpaint their texture maps accordingly.

To achieve interactive rates, we first perform an offline preprocessing stage to generate a texture map and train a novel UV displacement network for each mesh component. Then, at runtime, given any procedural parameter setting, the procedural model generates output mesh components, and our trained UV networks compute UV coordinates in real-time for each component to map the geometry onto the precomputed texture images.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our model by evaluating it on a diverse set of real, professional procedural models. These models span a wide range of object categories, with varying degrees of geometric deformation and number of procedural parameters. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has studied text-based texture generation for procedural modeling. As a result, we adapt existing methods to condition on multiple geometries for comparison studies. Both qualitative and quantitative results show that our method outperforms the alternatives, producing high-quality textures with consistent visual coherence.

To summarize the main contributions of this work:

(1)

We propose ProcTex, a system that enables text-guided texturing for procedural models, building on top of existing text-based texturing systems for individual shapes. Our system supports procedural models structural and continuous variations, generates consistent textures, supports interactive parameter manipulation, and synthesizes UV texture maps that can be conveniently edited.

(2)

We introduce a novel UV displacement network as part of our ProcTex system, which effectively estimates dense shape correspondence and thereby UV assignments, enabling real-time texture generation and interactive applications.

Related Work

2.1.
Procedural Modeling

Procedural modeling is the process of using programs or rules to produce visual content. It has been studied since the early days of computer graphics
(
Sutherland 1964
)
and is used for various stages of the computer graphics pipeline, with the prominent uses in 3D geometry modeling
(
Nishida et al. 2016
;
Palubicki et al. 2009
;
Merrell et al. 2010
)
, material and texture design
(
Guerrero et al. 2022
;
Perlin 1985
;
Worley 1996
)
, and fabrication
(
Ghali 2008
;
Stroud 2006
)
.

The focus of this paper is procedural geometry modeling. One well-known approach in this field is
L-systems
, which employ context-free string rewriting systems to generate branching structures, such as trees and vegetation
(
Prusinkiewicz 1986
;
Prusinkiewicz et al. 1988
;
Prusinkiewicz et al. 1996
)
. Another widely used class of procedural modeling is
shape grammars
. Shape grammars consist of shape rules and a generation engine that selects and processes rules, which have been used to generate various assets such as facades
(
Müller et al. 2007
;
Martinovic and Van Gool 2013
)
, buildings
(
Müller et al. 2006
)
, and cities
(
Kelly 2021
)
. More recently, there has been growing interest in combining procedural geometry modeling with deep learning for 3D shape modeling, with applications in inferring 3D shape programs
(
Ellis et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Deng et al. 2022
)
and 3D shape generation
(
Nishida et al. 2018
;
Jones et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2021
)
. For a deeper exploration of procedural modeling techniques, we direct readers to two comprehensive reviews
(
Smelik et al. 2014
;
Ritchie et al. 2023
)
on the topic.

The goal of this paper is to enable text-guided texture synthesis for 3D geometric procedural models. These models can often be abstracted as interfaces that take a set of program parameters as input, and produce a set of deforming 3D components as output. Such interfaces allow users to randomize the program parameters and rapidly explore the design space of the procedural models in an interactive way. Our model is designed to leverage this interface, while ensuring the texture generation process is efficient enough to support real-time interaction. Our model also imposes minimal assumptions about the procedural models themselves, requiring only that they conform to the described abstraction. This generality ensures our model is agnostic to object categories, specific procedural features, degrees of deformation, and the number of parameters of the input procedural models.

2.2.
Text-guided texture generation systems

Inspired the recent success of 2D image generation using text-guided diffusion models, several works attempt to leverage pretrained 2D diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Saharia et al. 2022
)
to guide texture synthesis for 3D shapes. A popular line of work
(
Poole et al. 2022
;
Lorraine et al. 2023
;
Qian et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Qiu et al. 2024
)
is to represent the 3D object as a neural radiance field
(
Mildenhall et al. 2021
)
and optimize it through a score distillation sampling objective and its variants
(
Poole et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
. With the rise of Gaussian Splatting
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)
as an alternative 3D representation, researchers have also explored training Gaussian Splatting models via supervision from pretrained 2D diffusion models
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Yi et al. 2024
)
. While these methods achieve impressive results, extracting explicit 3D assets (e.g., meshes) from such implicit representations remains challenging, limiting their applicability to be integrated into standard computer graphics workflows.

Aside from implicit representations, A major line of work focuses on generative texturing of 3D meshes under text guidance. Early methods such as
(
Khalid et al. 2022
)
and
(
Khalid et al. 2022
)
use CLIP-based signals
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
to optimize textures that align with a given textual description. More recently,
(
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Deng et al. 2024
)
leverage the SDS objective to optimize texture and an optional hybrid tetrahedral grid
(
Shen et al. 2021
)
for a given mesh. There are also several works that represent appearance as texture images via UV parametrization, and then explicitly generating these texture images
(
Tang et al. 2024a
;
Cao et al. 2023a
;
Gao et al. 2024
;
Yeh et al. 2024
)
. This representation significantly enhances the editability of resulting assets. Furthermore, some of these methods avoid the lengthy and sometimes unstable SDS optimization by leveraging a depth-conditioned diffusion model to project multi-view images onto texture images, thereby greatly accelerating training time. However, these approaches typically process only a single mesh at a time. As a result, re-running the training for each new shape is prohibitively expensive in interactive settings, and there is no inherent guarantee of consistency across textures generated independently by these models.

Meanwhile, another line of research aims to develop generalizable 3D generative models
(
Yu et al. 2024
;
Bensadoun et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Hong et al. 2024
)
. These systems are trained on large-scale 3D datasets and can synthesize arbitrary textured 3D outputs quickly during inference. However, such approaches do not accept a family of shapes as input, which limits their use in the context of procedural modeling. To the best of our knowledge, existing works that specifically address text-driven texture generation for multiple shapes
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
are limited to simple primitive forms and do not readily scale to complex or large families of geometries. In contrast, our work bridges this gap by enabling text-guided texture generation for procedurally generated shapes, while simultaneously ensuring texture consistency and supporting real-time inference.

3.
Method

Figure 2
.
An overview of ProcTex
. ProcTex consists of three modules: (1) a texture synthesis pipeline to generate part-level UV texture images; (2) a shape matching module to establish dense surface correspondence across the sampled shapes; (3) a UV displacement network which estimates the difference between original and transferred UVs for target shapes. ProcTex is trained on a sampled set of meshes and procedural parameters during the pre-processing stage. For inference, ProcTex predicts the transferred UVs for any procedural mesh in real-time.

3.1.
Problem Statement and Approach

The goal of this paper is to develop a system to enable text-based texturing for procedural models. To integrate with the interactive workflow of procedural modeling, our system must satisfy two key properties. First, the generated textures across geometries need to be consistent, such that there is no flickering appearances when users adjust the procedural parameters. Second, the system needs to produce textures at interactive rates to support real-time exploration of the design space. The key design of our system is to generate texture UV images for a template mesh sampled from the procedural model, and then learn a UV displacement network to perform real-time texture transfer for all other meshes produced by the procedural model. Fig.
2
provides an overview of our system.

Our model decouples the task into two stages. In the pre-processing stage, our system starts by uniformly sampling the parameter space of the procedural model to produce a set of corresponding meshes. We then build on an existing texture inpainting method to generate part-level texture UV images for a template shape. Next, we build dense surface correspondence between the sampled meshes and the template mesh. The UV map is then transferred to other procedurally generated meshes according to the previously built dense surface correspondence. Then, we train a UV displacement network with the goal of learning the UV transfer behavior for the entire family of shapes produced by the procedural model. During the inference stage, given the same text prompt and any parameters from the procedural model, our learned UV displacement network predicts the corresponding UV displacement with just one single feedforward process and effectively transfers the generated texture to the procedural shapes in real time.

3.2.
Exploring the shape space of procedural modeling

The first step of our model is to sample meshes that span the parameter space of the procedural model. The key reason for this is to capture the full range of configurations and ensure that ProcTex can handle any procedural setting at inference. To achieve this, we systematically sample both continuous and discrete parameters of the procedural model.

3.2.1.
Continuous parameters

Continuous parameters typically control the size or dimensions of a shape, such as the length, width, and height of a sofa model. These parameters can often be viewed as applying an affine transformation to the generated shapes. In some other cases, it can also apply some non-linear deformation to the shape, for instance, when bending or twisting a particular region. To handle each continuous parameter, we first determine lower and upper bounds within which the model produces plausible shapes for the object category. We then generate a collection of meshes by uniformly sampling the parameter intervals from the procedural model.

3.2.2.
Discrete parameters

The discrete parameters, on the other hand, control the number of certain geometry components in the shape, such as the number of wings on an airship. Following a similar process, we begin by identifying a set of valid discrete parameter values that yield meshes appropriately matching the object category. For each valid configuration in this set, we then generate the corresponding meshes using the procedural model.

3.2.3.
Selecting template mesh

After sampling a diverse set of shapes from the procedural models, the next step is to select a template mesh for texture generation. While any sampled mesh can be used, we choose the mesh produced by the median set of the sampled procedural parameters as the template mesh. The reason is that the median mesh represents the central tendency of the procedural shape space, which inflicts minimal distortion for texture transfer on average.

3.3.
Generating texture for template mesh parts

One appealing advantage of procedural modeling is that it allows users to control the generated shapes by adjusting the procedural parameters. Motivated by this, we aim to provide users with a similar level of control over the generated textures. To achieve this, we synthesize textures in the form of UV images, a widely used representation in computer graphics that is intuitive and convenient to modify. Additionally, in this paper, we aim to enhance the texture editability further by generating texture images for individual parts of the 3D shapes to enable part-level customization. While semantic part segmentation for 3D shapes is inherently challenging, we propose a simple yet effective approach under the context of procedural modeling. We describe our method for part identification and grouping in Sec.
3.3.1
, and the texture generation pipeline in Sec.
3.3.2
.

3.3.1.
Part Identification & Grouping

Segmenting a mesh into semantically meaningful components is a fundamental problem in computer graphics
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Abdelreheem et al. 2023
)
. While semantic segmentation is challenging on arbitrary shapes without any prior knowledge, our key observation is that procedural models typically build complex geometries by first constructing simpler components and then systematically combining them. One prominent example of this is Geometry Nodes in Blender
(
Community 2018
)
, where each part is modeled individually and then linked together in a tree-based structure. Moreover, procedural models inherently avoid post-hoc connections between components. As a result, we can segment a geometry into parts simply based on their connectivity properties, which has proven effective across all the procedural models we tested. Specifically, we run a standard depth-first search algorithm based on the faces of the template mesh, and treat each connected component as a separate part.

When a single component instance is repeated multiple times (e.g., the four wheels of a car), having them share the same texture map helps avoid redundancy and visual inconsistencies. To enable this, we perform part grouping in the template mesh before running the texturing pipeline. In most cases, grouping is straightforward and relies on matching component topologies, as procedural modeling typically duplicates components directly. If this condition doesn’t hold, we align the pairs using rigid Iterative Closest Point (ICP)
(
Besl and McKay 1992
)
, measure their similarity with the Chamfer distance, and apply a predefined threshold to determine whether they belong to the same group. All parts within a group share the same UV space, ensuring that only one texture map is required.

3.3.2.
Texture generation

We apply an enhanced InTex
(
Tang et al. 2024a
)
pipeline as the text-to-texture method for the template mesh. The original InTex pipeline is an iterative method that generates textures for 3D meshes guided by a text prompt, using a depth-aware inpainting diffusion model. The 3D shape is rendered from a given viewpoint to produce an RGB image with an inpainting mask and a depth map. These inputs, along with a text prompt, are passed into the pre-trained diffusion model to generate an inpainted image, which is then projected back onto the texture map using UV mapping. This process is repeated for a set of camera poses surrounding the object, and the texture is refined at viewing angles that introduce less distortion. We refer readers to the original InTex paper for further details.

In this paper, we extend InTex to synthesize textures for each part group for part-level editing, as explained in Sec.
3.3.1
. During rendering, each component is rendered individually and combined into a single view. During back-projection, each inpainted image is projected onto multiple texture maps, one for each component group. Specifically, we store a rendered depth map for each component, and compare them with the global depth map. This ensures only the component nearest the viewpoint is selected for projection at each pixel, preventing incorrect projections.

We adopt an inpainting-based texture generation approach because it is both significantly faster and yields higher-quality results compared to SDS-based methods. Although we aim to use the most up-to-date texture generation technique as a foundational component of our system, improving texture quality for a single shape is not the main contribution of this work. Consequently, we experimented with various open-source texturing methods that generate UV images
(
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Tang et al. 2024a
)
and found that InTex provides the most suitable balance of speed and quality for our purposes.

3.4.
Transferring textures for procedural meshes

To synthesize textures for the entire family of procedural shapes, we first generate part-level texture images of the template mesh using the process described in Sec.
3.3
, and then transfer the textures onto other shapes. This approach ensures that all shapes share the same texture images and therefore guarantees visual consistency of the textured outputs. For texture transfer, our key design is to build a dense surface correspondence between the template mesh and other generated meshes, after which the template mesh’s UV coordinates are assigned to other shapes via the shape correspondence. For shapes with a single connected component, a single texture map is often sufficient for all instances. For shapes with multiple connected components, we keep track of the textures generated for each part component and the surface correspondence is performed for each part component independently.

3.4.1.
Identifying part correspondence

Before establishing dense surface correspondence, we need to know which component of a sampled mesh corresponds to which component of the template mesh. For example, consider two car meshes. Sec.
3.3.1
gives two unordered sets of their components. We need to know one car’s wheel correspond to the other car’s wheel rather than its headlight. To tackle this, we run ICP for each possible pair of the components between the sampled meshes and the template mesh, and map components of the training meshes to components of the template mesh that would give the lowest mean Chamfer distance after ICP transformation.

3.4.2.
Establishing dense correspondence

After corresponding parts are identified, ProcTex establishes dense correspondence through solving the shape matching problem between the source and target parts. Here, the part component in the template mesh if referred to as the source part, and the corresponding part in other sampled shapes is the target part. To account for different types of deformations, we categorize the shape correspondence problem into three scenarios and outline the methods used for each.

When the source and target parts share the same topology, the most efficient and accurate approach is to pair vertices directly in order. While topology consistency is a strong assumption, it often holds in procedural modeling where many parts remain unchanged despite parameter variations. For instance, adjusting the car length parameter does not affect the topology of the car wheels. In such cases, we can directly assign the UVs from the wheels of the template mesh to the corresponding wheels in other training meshes that differ only in car length.

If topology consistency does not hold but the deformations can be approximated by affine transformations, ProcTex employs ICP with non-uniform scaling. This approach effectively handles cases involving many continuous parameter changes, such as modifications to the height, length, or width of a sofa in a procedural model.

Establishing dense correspondence across shapes with non-affine deformations is a well-studied problem in geometry processing. One prominent class of methods utilizes the functional map framework to learn geometric features for shape pairing
(
Litany et al. 2017
;
Cao et al. 2023b
;
Sharma and Ovsjanikov 2020
)
. To this end, we adopt Smooth Shells
(
Eisenberger et al. 2019
)
, a state-of-the-art shape matching model that solves dense correspondence by aligning shapes iteratively in a coarse-to-fine manner. Similar to ICP, we run Smooth Shells between the source and target parts, and leverage the learned dense mapping to assign UVs effectively.

3.4.3.
Handling part addition and removal

As discussed in Sec.
3.2.2
, procedural models often include parameters that cause discrete geometric changes, such as adding or removing components. These changes present challenges for texture synthesis, as they introduce new geometries or expose regions on existing components that were previously occluded by removed parts. Both scenarios necessitate a re-texturing procedure to generate textures that are harmonious and consistent with the existing ones. Importantly, the re-texturing procedure must preserve the existing textures to ensure visual coherence with the template mesh.

For part addition, ProcTex first attempts to map the new parts to existing components using ICP. If a match is found, the texture maps associated with the corresponding components are reused, which eliminate the need for additional texturing. In cases where ICP fails to establish a reliable mapping, i.e., when the new component has no analogous part in the existing geometry, ProcTex re-runs the texturing pipeline described in Sec.
3.3.2
to generate new texture maps specifically for the added component. This ensures that the newly generated textures are visually coherent with the existing textures, while preserving the existing textures.

For part removal, the re-texturing process targets the dis-occluded regions of existing components. To maintain consistency, ProcTex employs an update counter for each texture image. During the re-texturing process, texels that were already updated during the initial generation stage for the template mesh remain unchanged. This ensures that previously generated textures are preserved, while new textures are synthesized only for the newly exposed regions. In this way, the re-texturing process selectively updates only the necessary areas, maintaining harmony among shapes with structural changes.

3.5.
UV displacement network

With the steps described in Sec.
3.4
, we can map the generated textures onto any shape produced by the procedural programs. However, there remains one more thing to address. Running ICP or Smooth Shells for each shape can take up to a few minutes per instance. While this runtime is manageable for preprocessing, it is prohibitively long for inference, especially in the setting of procedural modeling. A key advantage of procedural modeling is its interactive workflow, which allows users to rapidly explore the parameter space. To ensure that texture transfer can be done in real-time and preserve the interactive nature of procedural modeling, we introduce a UV displacement network to predict UV assignments for the entire procedural geometry family.

The goal of the network is to learn the shape correspondence behavior through estimating displacements in the UV space. Formally, let
\mathbf{U}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}\times 2}
denote the UV coordinates of the template mesh, and
\mathbf{U}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{S}\times 2}
denote the UV coordinates of a target mesh generated during preprocessing. The dense correspondence matrix between the template mesh and the target mesh is given by
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{S}\times n_{T}}
, where
\mathbf{C}_{IJ}=1
if vertex
i
of the target corresponds to vertex
j
of the template, and 0 otherwise. Using this, the transferred UVs for the target mesh are computed as:

(1)

\mathbf{U}_{S}^{\text{transferred}}=\mathbf{C}\cdot\mathbf{U}_{T}.

The UV displacement network
f
, parameterized by
\theta
, predicts a displacement vector
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{S}\times 2}
for each vertex of the target mesh. The network first augment the original UVs
\mathbf{U}_{S}
by projecting in into a high-frequency space via positional encoding, following techniques commonly used in recent works
(
Mildenhall et al. 2021
;
mueller2022instantsrinivasan2023nuvo
)
. Additionally, the network is conditioned on the procedural parameters
\mathbf{P}
, which serves as a well-structured encoding of the underlying geometry. The displacement
\mathbf{D}
is given by:

(2)

\mathbf{D}=f_{\theta}(\text{PE}(\mathbf{U}_{S}),\mathbf{P}).

This displacement aligns the target mesh’s original UVs,
\mathbf{U}_{S}
, to the transferred UVs,
\mathbf{U}_{S}^{\text{transferred}}
, such that:

(3)

\mathbf{U}_{S}^{\text{predicted}}=\text{Clamp}(\mathbf{U}_{S}+\mathbf{D},0,1).

We optimize the UV displacement network by minimizing the L2 distance between the transferred UVs and the final UVs predicted by the network. The loss function is given by:

(4)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{n_{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\left\|\mathbf{U}_{S}^{\text{predicted}}(i)-\mathbf{U}_{S}^{\text{transferred}}(i)\right\|_{2}^{2}

3.6.
Online Inference

ProcTex trains the UV displacement network on the sampled meshes during the proprocessing stage, and uses it for online texture transfer during inference, including for previously unseen meshes. At inference time, given a target mesh with procedural parameters
\mathbf{P}
and initial UV coordinates
\mathbf{U}_{S}
, the transferred UVs are computed as described in Eqs.
2
and
3
. Importantly, generating the transferred UVs for each mesh requires only a single feedforward pass through the network, making the process well-suited for real-time applications.

4.
Results and Evaluations

We conduct experiments on a selection of 8 procedural models available on Blender Market. These models represent a diverse range of object types and offer varying levels of control over geometry. We ensure that our selection covers four key categories commonly found in procedural modeling: models with consistent topology, models with components undergoing affine transformations, models with components undergoing non-affine transformations, and models containing discrete changes that require retexturing. We analyze the texture generation quality and the visual consistency in Section
4.1
, showcases the part-level editability and support for various UV parametrizations in section
4.3
, presents quantitative metrics for comparison with existing methods in Section
4.4
.

4.1.
Qualitative Analysis of Texture transfer

We present the qualitative comparison in Fig. 6 (after the reference section). For the baseline, we process each procedural mesh independently without any texture transfer in between, using our modified version of InTex. As there is no existing method designed specifically for text-guided texture synthesis for procedural models, we adapt alternative techniques from texture field optimization to serve as a secondary baseline. Specifically, we use a MLP with similar structure to
(
Michel et al. 2022
)
to learn a texture field through SDS
(
Poole et al. 2022
)
. This texture field is conditioned on the procedural parameters of the input shapes, and predicts per-vertex color for each mesh. We incorporate Lipschitz regularization
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
on the MLP weights to explicitly enforce smoothness in appearance. The SDS loss is guided by depth-conditioned Stable Diffusion via ControlNet
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
for better visual results.

It can be observed that the baseline method generates textures that vary significantly between different runs, resulting in noticeable appearance differences across procedural meshes. In contrast, our method demonstrates strong consistency and ensures uniform textures across all meshes. While the SDS + Lipschitz method achieves some level of visual consistency on certain models, it suffers from lower overall quality and can produce degenerate textures, as seen in the tumbler example. Furthermore, the SDS-based method requires a significant increase in training time and all meshes must be subdivided to approximately 100k vertices to achieve acceptable appearance resolution, leading to increased memory usage. Despite these additional resource requirements, detailed inconsistencies between meshes remain unsolved; for example, the cat decal on the mug, the patterns on the back of the sofa and some specular highlights in the pumpkin case remain inconsistent across geometries. We refer readers to the supplemental videos for best visual results.

4.2.
Re-texturing and Part-level Editability

As discussed in Sec.
3.2.2
, procedural models often support structural changes for the underlying meshes. For both part addition and removal cases, ProcTex leverages the fact that procedural models are typically assembled from perpetrate components that are not connected or merged post-hoc. It then automatically isolates each part based on the mesh topology, which enables localized texturing and editing without affecting the rest of the shape.

4.3.
Supporting different UV parametrization

ProcTex is designed to be independent of any particular UV parametrization, as long as it is a bijective projection from the 3D position space to 2D UV space for the purpose of obtaining unambiguous training data for the UV displacement network. Users may adopt off-the-shelf projection methods (e.g., spherical or cube mapping) or advanced neural approaches. We integrate
Nuvo

(
Srinivasan et al. 2023
)
into our workflow and provide an open-source implementation as part of this work. As demonstrated in Fig.
5
, ProcTex achieves consistently high-quality results regardless of the chosen UV mapping approach.

4.4.
Quantitative Evaluation

Table
1
compares training and inference times across three approaches. ProcTex requires a moderate setup phase, including part segmentation and grouping, establishing dense correspondence, and training the UV displacement network. Yet, it then achieves near instantaneous inference, making it practical for real-time exploration of the procedural model’s deisgn space. By contrast, the baseline method textures each geometry separately, so its training and inference speeds are identical but slow at the inference stage. Meanwhile, the SDS+Lipschitz approach offers comparably fast inference but suffers from significantly longer training times, coupled with inconsistent or degraded visual results. These results highlight ProcTex’s favorable balance between training and runtime performances for interactive procedural modeling workflows.

5.
Conclusions

We propose ProcTex, a novel text-to-texture system tailored to procedural models. Our model is capable of generating high-quality textures with remarkable visual consistency across a diverse set of procedural families of shapes. Our approach extends existing text-to-texture systems by introducing part-level texture UV image generation, enabling local appearance editing. Moreover, our re-texturing pipeline robustly addresses structural variations induced by discrete procedural parameters. A key innovation of ProcTex is the learned UV displacement network, which allows for textured outputs to be generated at interactive rates during inference. Potential directions for improvement include integrating more powerful single-object texture synthesis model, and enhancing more accurate dense correspondence solving, particularly for components with large-extent non-affine deformations.
