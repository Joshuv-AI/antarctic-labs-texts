Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100/Pt@CuS-induced self-accelerated dual-quenching ECL-RET strategy for sensitive sST2 detection
Authors: Xinrui He, Zhujun Ai, Diao Yu, Xiang Yu, Shijia Ding, Ling Zeng, Wei Yang, Qinggang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel dual-quenching electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunosensor based on ECL resonance energy transfer (ECL-RET) strategy was developed for sensitive detection of soluble suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (sST2) protein. The sensor developed a self-accelerated ECL emitter composed of Au-decorated graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanosheets and Materials of Institute Lavoisier-100(Fe) (MIL-100(Fe)). In this system, the reversible Fe2+/Fe3+ redox cycling in MIL-100(Fe) markedly enhanced the ECL emission from the g-C3N4/S2O82− pair by promoting the generation of sulfate radical. Simultaneously, the incorporated Au nanoparticles facilitated electron transfer and provided abundant sites for biomolecular immobilization, collectively establishing a robust “signal-on” state. Signal quenching was achieved using Pt@CuS nanoflowers as synergistic ECL quenchers. Their high antibody-loading capacity and efficient ECL-RET capability acted in concert to drastically reduce the signal, thereby inducing a pronounced “signal-off” state. The optimized “on–off” ECL immunosensor demonstrated a satisfactory linear range (0.001–100 ng mL−1) and an ultralow detection limit of 0.547 pg mL−1 for sST2. This work established a reliable platform for sST2 quantification, holding substantial potential for advancing heart failure research and clinical early diagnosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03866-1.Keywords: Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100, Pt@CuS, Immunosensor, ECL-RET, sST2

Introduction

Soluble suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (sST2) is a significant biomarker for heart failure (HF), widely used for prognostic assessment and disease monitoring in acute and chronic HF, which was officially incorporated into the HF management guidelines by the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association in 2013 [1]. Unlike brain natriuretic peptide, sST2 offers the dual advantages of evaluating myocardial fibrosis and predicting ventricular remodeling. Moreover, sST2 boasts superior clinical practicality, attributed to its lower reference change value, minimal individual variability, and independence from factors including age, gender, and renal function [2–4]. These properties make it an ideal biomarker for continuous monitoring and guiding heart failure treatment [5]. Currently, the clinical standard for sST2 detection is enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; however, it is compromised by insufficient enzyme stability and high cost [6, 7]. While emerging techniques such as amplified luminescent proximity homogeneous immunoassay [8], electrochemical immunosensing [9], and FRET aptasensors [10] have been developed, their widespread adoption is still hindered by limitations including operational complexity, insufficient sensitivity and specificity [11]. Thus, it is pivotal to determine a novel detection method to advance the clinical application of sST2 further.

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) biosensing analysis has been developed into one of the most accurate and mature analytical methods due to the advantages of both electrochemistry and chemiluminescence, including low background noise and superior spatiotemporal control [12, 13]. In biosensing analysis systems, many new luminescent nanomaterials (such as carbon dots, quantum dots, polymer dots, metal nanoclusters [14], and two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials [15]) have been extensively applied. Graphite-like carbon nitride nanosheets (g-C3N4 NSs) stand out among various ECL nanomaterials due to their exceptional biocompatibility, high quantum yield and remarkable chemical stability [16, 17]. Various ECL biosensors have been established to leverage the exceptional properties of g-C3N4 NSs. For instance, we previously illustrated a dual-quenched ECL immunosensor based on ECL resonance energy transfer (ECL-RET) for identifying protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II [18], wherein we found that Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) enhanced ECL signal due to their superior electrical conductivity [19]. Additionally, due to their unique physicochemical properties, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have received considerable attention in ECL-RET immunosensors [20]. Among these, Materials of Institute Lavoisier-100(Fe) (MIL-100(Fe)) stands out for its porous structure, high specific surface area, and good aqueous stability, attributed to the coordination bonds between Fe(III) and carboxyl groups [21]. Moreover, the Fe2+/Fe3+ redox pairs make it particularly suitable for oxidation reactions [22]. Bu et al. discovered that MIL-101, which also possessed Fe2+/Fe3+ redox pairs, could enhance the ECL signal of g-C3N4 in a K2S2O8 system [23]. Therefore, by reducing Fe(III) to Fe(II), MIL-100(Fe) can enhance electron transfer during oxidation processes while also intensifying the ECL signal by accelerating the generation of sulfate radical (SO4·−) from peroxydisulfate ion (S2O82−) and producing abundant ECL reaction intermediates near g-C3N4 NSs. The principle of ECL-RET involves the overlap of donor and acceptor, facilitating the transfer of energy from the excited donor state to the acceptor to diminish the ECL signal [24]. Because of its simplicity and high sensitivity, the “on–off” signal output system has garnered much interest in ECL biosensing [25–27]. Therefore, developing an effective quenching label is essential to achieve a “signal-off” state and detect minor analyte concentration changes with high sensitivity. For kanamycin biosensing construction, Wang et al. used an ECL-RET system comprising Au NPs and a novel kind of porphyrin-based MOFs (Fe TCPP (Zn) MOFs) [28]. Li et al. reported a CeO2@TiO2 nanocomposite and Cu2S ECL-RET biosensor for florfenicol detection [29]. However, the effectiveness of ECL-RET is limited by the fact that the aforementioned ECL-RET devices only contain one energy acceptor. Therefore, the ability of ECL-RET to quench can be directly enhanced by the introduction of numerous energy acceptors in the sensing systems. In quenching-typed system construction, platinum (Pt) nanomaterials have emerged as a type of qualified ECL acceptor due to their wide absorption ranges [30]. Additionally, copper sulfide (CuS) is a top-notch semiconductor material with outstanding visible-light absorption capabilities, which has found extensive application in photoelectric conversion and biosensing [31]. CuS exhibits size-dependent light absorption properties, and its absorption spectrum closely matches the ECL emission spectrum of g-C3N4 NSs, making it an ideal energy acceptor material in ECL-RET systems. Notably, CuS nanoflowers (CuS NFs) possess a unique and stable spatial structure, offering a larger surface area for biomolecule immobilization [32, 33]. Here, CuS NFs were firstly combined with Pt nanoparticles (Pt NPs) to create dual ECL-RET acceptors of g-C3N4 NSs, resulting in high quenching efficiency and enhanced detection sensitivity. Herein, a dual quenching strategy was introduced utilizing the donor/acceptor pair Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100/Pt@CuS, successfully developing a sensitive “on–off” ECL immunosensor for detecting sST2. Scheme 1 displayed the fabrication process of the sensor and its ECL enhancement and quenching mechanisms. Initially, Au NPs were covalently bonded to the amino groups of g-C3N4 NSs, forming Au-g-C3N4 composites. These composites were subsequently anchored onto MIL-100(Fe) through interactions between Au and carboxyl groups, resulting in Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100. When applied to the electrode, these composites exhibited strong ECL intensity, representing the “signal-on” state. Meanwhile, Pt@CuS hybrids were synthesized by decorating uniform CuS NFs with plentiful Pt NPs, which effectively quenched the ECL emission of g-C3N4 NSs through the ECL-RET mechanism. The developed “on–off” ECL, based on a sandwich immunoassay format, demonstrated sensitive detection of sST2, highlighting its potential for early clinical diagnosis of sST2-related conditions.

Experimental section

Preparation of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites

Firstly, 20 mL of synthesized Au NPs solution was applied to 5 mL of g-C3N4 NSs aqueous solution (1 mg mL−1) with 24 h of stirring. After the composites (Au-g-C3N4 composites) were spun (8000 rpm, 5 min) and rinsed with DI water, they were collected using 20 mL DI water. Next, 5 mL MIL-100(Fe) solution (9.2 mg mL−1) was applied to the aforementioned Au-g-C3N4 composites with 14 h of stirring. Following centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min and thorough rinsing, the Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites were dissolved in 25 mL of DI water.

Preparation of Pt@CuS-Ab2 bioconjugates

Scheme 1B showed the preparation of Ab2 bioconjugates. First, 20 mg of CuS NFs powder was added into 20 mL of absolute ethanol; then 5 mL of APTES was added into the above supernatant with stirring and heating at 70 °C for 4 h to adjust CuS NFs with a mass of amino groups. The obtained precipitates were re-dispersed in 20 mL of absolute ethanol after extensive centrifugation and multiple washings with absolute ethanol. Subsequently, 5 mL of synthesized Pt NPs solution was applied to the APTES-modified 5 mL CuS NFs supernatant with stirring for 12 h. Lastly, the hybrids (Pt@CuS hybrids) were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 5 min) and thoroughly rinsed with absolute ethanol and DI water. Re-dispersing the aforementioned precipitates was conducted in 5 mL DI water to create Pt@CuS solution. After that, 1 mL of Ab2 (10 μg mL−1) was added to 10 μL of this solution and left to react at 4 °C for 12 h. Then, the recovered Pt@CuS-Ab2 hybrids were centrifuged and washed with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4). To avoid nonspecific binding, they were exposed to BSA (5.0 wt%) at 4 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation and washing with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4), the Ab2 bioconjugates were collected and dispersed into 1 mL of PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for later usage.

Fabrication of ECL immunosensor

The glassy carbon electrode (GCE, Φ = 5 mm) was polished with alumina for 5 min to a mirror-like surface, then sonicated in DI water and dried under a nitrogen stream. Subsequently, the electrode was coated with 10 μL of the Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites and dried. The adjusted GCE was incubated with 10 μL of sST2 Ab1 (10 μg mL−1) at 4 °C for at least 12 h, rinsed with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4), and then blocked with 10 μL of 0.6 wt% BSA at 37 °C for 40 min. After being rinsed with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4), it was treated with 10 μL of varying concentrations of sST2 and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Unbound antigen was eliminated through rinsing with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4). Finally, following a 40-min treatment with 10 μL of Ab₂ bioconjugates at 37 °C, the electrode was rinsed with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) again.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites

TEM characterization and TEM-EDS elemental mapping were utilized to distinguish the shape and elemental makeup of the produced nanoparticles. The g-C3N4 NSs displayed a multi-layered stacking structure resembling 2D paper (Fig. 1A). HRTEM revealed that Au NPs had a lattice spacing of 0.239 nm, matching the (111) plane of Au (Figure S1A) [18]. On the other hand, pure Au NPs had an average diameter of 15.37 ± 1.32 nm and a roughly spherical shape (Fig. 1B and Figure S1B). MIL-100(Fe) was successfully synthesized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and FTIR spectrum analysis (Fig. 1C and Figure S2). After a combination of Au NPs with g-C3N4 NSs via Au–N covalent bonds, uniform-sized Au NPs were anchored on g-C3N4 NSs (Fig. 1D). In the TEM images of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 (Fig. 1E), the Au-g-C3N4 covered the surface of MIL-100(Fe) via the combination of Au and COOH. In the meantime, TEM-EDS mapping revealed the uniformly distributed C, N, O, Fe, and Au elements (Fig. 1F–L), indicating Au-g-C3N4 was evenly dispersed on MIL-100(Fe), and Au NPs were uniformly distributed on g-C3N4 NSs. Additionally, UV–vis spectra and zeta potential further established the successful synthesis of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 (Figure S3A, B).

The crystal structures of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 were characterized using XRD. Figure 1M displayed that g-C3N4 NSs exhibited characteristic peaks at 13.1° and 27.5°, corresponding to the (100) crystal plane of the internal heptazine structural unit and the (002) crystal plane of the graphite-like interlayer stacking (JCPDS NO. 87–1526), respectively [34]. The diffraction peaks of MIL-100(Fe) aligned well with the typical XRD patterns reported in the literature, establishing the successful synthesis of the MOFs (Figure S4) [35]. The absence of XRD peaks at 13.1 ° from g-C3N4 NSs was most likely caused by the characteristic peak fusion with MIL-100(Fe) at around 10.1 ° and 10.8 ° [36]. Furthermore, the distinct peaks at 38.18 ° and 44.39 ° in the Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 pattern were attributable to the Au 4f spectrum (JCPDS No. 04–0784) [37], indicating the successful formation of Au NPs on the surface of g-C3N4 NSs. Notably, the introduction of Au-g-C3N4 composites did not alter the crystal structure of MIL-100(Fe), confirming the structural integrity of the MOF framework upon modification.

XPS was employed to systematically assess the surface chemical bonding, electronic states, and elemental composition of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100. The XPS survey spectrum (Figure S5A–D) established the successful synthesis of MIL-100(Fe). Figure 1N showed that the XPS survey spectrum of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 indicated that the composites surface was primarily composed of C, N, O, Fe, and Au elements. The C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 1O) exhibited characteristic peaks at 288.63, 288.37, 285.12, and 284.64 eV, in line with its sp2 hybridized carbon, N = C-N bonds, C–C/C-H/C-N single bonds, and the C = O structure of the benzene ring, respectively. The N 1 s spectrum (Fig. 1P) displayed two characteristic peaks at 400.54 and 398.92 eV, attributed to C-N single bonds and sp2 hybridized N atoms in g-C3N4 NSs, respectively. The O 1 s spectrum (Fig. 1Q) showed two peaks at 532.30 and 531.60 eV, assigned to C = O and Fe-O-C bonds, respectively. The Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 1R) revealed three characteristic peaks at 724.65, 713.16, and 711.09 eV, where 724.65 and 711.09 eV correspond to Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2 peaks, respectively, and the satellite peak at 713.16 eV confirmed the existence of Fe(III) in the Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 structure [36]. Additionally, the Au 4f spectrum (Fig. 1S) displayed two distinct characteristic peaks at 87.73 eV (Au 4f5/2) and 84.01 eV (Au 4f7/2), further verifying the effective synthesis of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 [37].

Characterization of Pt@CuS hybrids

SEM and TEM were employed to analyze CuS NFs and Pt@CuS morphologies. CuS NFs were well-scattered and homogenous nanoflowers with an average diameter of 1.5 ~ 2.0 μm (Fig. 2A–C). TEM analysis revealed that Pt NPs were predominantly monodispersed and spherical (Fig. 2D). HRTEM further confirmed the high crystallinity of Pt NPs, with a lattice spacing of 0.24 nm consistent with the (111) plane of Pt (Figure S6A) [38]. Figure S6B displayed that the average particle diameter was 3.78 ± 0.93 nm. Assessment of Pt-modified CuS NFs indicated that spherical Pt NPs were distributed on the CuS NFs surface in a uniform manner (Fig. 2E–H). HAADF-TEM and EDS mapping confirmed the composition of Pt@CuS, revealing the presence of Cu, S, and Pt (Fig. 2I, J). These results demonstrated the homogeneous distribution of spherical Pt NPs on the CuS NFs surface. Additionally, UV–vis absorbance spectrum and zeta potential analysis of Pt@CuS were performed to validate the successfully fabricated Pt@CuS (Figure S7A, B).

XPS analysis was employed to assess the chemical composition of Pt@CuS hybrids. Cu, S, and Pt were the primary elements found on the surface of the nanoflowers, according to the Pt@CuS XPS survey spectrum (Fig. 2K). The Cu 2p spectrum (Fig. 2L) exhibited characteristic spin–orbit doublets at 951.59 eV (Cu 2p1/2) and 931.80 eV (Cu 2p3/2), while the S 2p spectrum (Fig. 2M) displayed peaks at 163.19 eV (S 2p1/2) and 161.96 eV (S 2p3/2) [39]. Metallic Pt was confirmed by Pt 4f peaks at 74.70 eV (4f5/2) and 71.42 eV (4f7/2), with a satellite feature at 76.80 eV suggesting coexisting Pt 4f5/2 2+-valence forms (Fig. 2N).

XRD analysis of Pt@CuS (Fig. 2O) revealed that CuS NFs exhibited seven distinct diffraction peaks at 27.7°, 29.3°, 31.8°, 32.9°, 47.9°, 52.7°, and 59.3°, corresponding to the (101), (102), (103), (006), (110), (108), and (116) crystal planes of CuS NFs (JCPDS No. 06–0464), respectively, confirming the successful formation of CuS NFs [31]. Additionally, the Pt NPs (JCPDS No. 04–0802) were identified as the source of the additional distinctive peaks at 39.8°, 46.2°, and 67.46° in the X-ray diffraction spectra of Pt@CuS [40]. This demonstrated that the attachment of Pt NPs on CuS NFs was successful.

The ECL mechanism of “on–off” immunosensor

Scheme 1C illustrated the proposed ECL mechanism for the immunosensor. In a typical cathodic ECL process with K2S2O8 as the coreactant, g-C3N4 NSs were directly reduced on the electrode, while S2O82− was primarily reduced to form SO4·−. The excited state g-C3N4* was then generated through the reaction between g-C3N4·− and SO4·−, resulting in light emission upon its relaxation to the ground state [41, 42]. The significant ECL enhancement of the g-C3N4/Au NPs/MIL-100(Fe) mixture (Fig. 3A, curve e) stemmed from the synergistic interaction between the components. As confirmed by XPS analysis (Fig. 1R and Figure S5D), the MIL-100(Fe) framework possessed reversible Fe2+/Fe3+ redox pairs. These sites served as efficient co-reaction accelerators by promoting S2O82− reduction and thereby increasing the yield of SO4·− radicals essential for g-C3N4 excitation [43]. Simultaneously, Au NPs provided abundant immobilization sites and enhanced electron transfer across the composite [19], facilitating the electrochemical reduction of both g-C3N4 and S2O82−. Control experiments in Fig. 3A and Figure S8A further validated this mechanism. Individual modification with MIL-100(Fe) (curve c) or Au NPs (curve d) improved the ECL intensity of g-C3N4 NSs, while the superposition of the same amounts of Au NPs and MIL-100(Fe) with g-C3N4 NSs (curve e) yielded the most intense signal, demonstrating an effect beyond the additive contributions of the single components. Moreover, the negligible ECL from bare Au NPs or MIL-100(Fe) alone (Figure S9) confirmed that their role was not emitting, but rather to synergistically enhance the g-C3N4/S2O82− system. Hence, MIL-100(Fe) and Au NPs functioned synergistically as co-reaction accelerators to enhance SO4·− generation from S2O82−, resulting in the significantly improved ECL intensity observed in the “signal-on” state.

The composites formed by combining g-C3N4 NSs with MIL-100(Fe) and Au NPs exhibited exceptional catalytic performance, as demonstrated by the significant improvement in the ECL signal in Fig. 3B and Figure S8B (curve a). The Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites exhibited significant ECL quenching upon the addition of Pt NPs and CuS NFs (curves b and c), while a simple mixture of the same amounts of these quenchers alone caused even more pronounced signal quenching (curve d). To explore the potential ECL-RET mechanism, the UV–vis absorption spectra of CuS NFs, Pt NPs, and Pt@CuS were analyzed (Figure S7A). CuS NFs had a broad UV–vis absorption range, whereas Pt NPs had a good capacity to absorb visible light. Therefore, the g-C3N4 NSs emission spectrum, with a maximum emission wavelength centering at approximately 470 nm, overlapped significantly with the absorption spectra of CuS NFs, Pt NPs, and Pt@CuS [44]. Simultaneously, Pt@CuS extended the absorption range of CuS NFs while enhancing the absorption intensity of both CuS NFs and Pt NPs in the UV–visible region. This could lead to a very efficient dual ECL-RET process between Pt@CuS and g-C3N4 NSs.

Electrochemical characterizations of the developed ECL immunosensor

Several modified electrodes were characterized by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) in a solution with 5 mM Fe(CN)63−/4− and 0.1 M KCl in order to determine the stepwise creation procedure of the ECL immunosensor. Figure 3C depicted that the electron-transfer resistance (Ret) was the same as the semicircle diameter in the EIS spectra. The least electron-transfer resistance was indicated by the bare GCE, which had the smallest semicircle (curve a). Upon modification with Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites, Ret increased (curve b) because of the semiconducting properties of g-C3N4 NSs and MIL-100(Fe), which hindered electron transfer. After the immobilization of Ab1, Ret further increased (curve c) as the antibody layer impeded electron conduction. Subsequent blocking with BSA led to a gradual rise in Ret (curve d), attributed to the electron-blocking effect of BSA [45]. Further incubation with sST2 and Ab2 resulted in additional increases in Ret (curves e and f), owing to the steric hindrance of the antibody-antigen complex and the insulating properties of the immunocomplex [46]. These results were consistent with the CV data (Fig. 3D), confirming the effective stepwise creation of the ECL immunosensor as designed.

Optimization of the reaction circumstances

To optimize the analytical performance for sST2 detection, the experimental conditions were systematically investigated. First, the effect of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites concentration on the sensing process was evaluated. The ECL intensity of electrodes modified with varying levels of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 adding the same concentration of Ab1 and BSA was assessed in PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) with 0.06 M K2S2O8 (Fig. 4A). The ECL signal increased with composite level and plateaued at 0.1667 μg mL−1, establishing this as the optimal concentration. Second, the level of Ab2 bioconjugates was optimized to balance quenching efficiency and reagent usage (Fig. 4B). The ECL intensity decreased with increasing concentration until stabilizing at 0.20 μg mL−1, which was chosen as the ideal level for the following trials. Finally, the incubation time of Ab2 bioconjugates with sST2 was optimized to maximize detection efficiency while minimizing time. Figure 4C showed that the ECL intensity decreased significantly as the incubation time elevated from 20 to 40 min, with negligible changes beyond 40 min. Hence, 40 min was considered as the optimal reaction time for sST2 detection.

Evaluation of the analytical performance of the immunosensor for sST2

Under optimized circumstances, the sensitivity of the system was evaluated by measuring sST2 levels (0.001–100 ng mL−1) (Fig. 5A). The ECL intensity exhibited a logarithmic correlation with sST2 concentrations within this range, as illustrated in the calibration curve (Fig. 5B). The linear regression equation was I = 6096–2393 log CsST2, where I signified the ECL intensity and CsST2 signified the sST2 level, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9953. The limit of detection (LOD) was 0.547 pg mL−1, which was significantly less than those illustrated in previous techniques for sST2 determination (Table S1). Leveraging the strong signal quenching capability of Pt@CuS hybrids and the synergistic enhancement of ECL intensity by MIL-100(Fe) and Au NPs as co-reaction accelerators, this “on–off” ECL immunosensor provided a robust and effective platform for sST2 detection. Its high sensitivity and stability made it a promising method for clinical diagnostics and practical applications in healthcare settings.

Specificity, stability, and reproducibility of the immunosensor

Subsequently, systematic investigations were conducted to evaluate specificity, stability, and reproducibility of the proposed biosensor, thereby assessing its potential for practical applications. Firstly, to show the specificity of the proposed immunosensor, its specificity for sST2 detection was assessed. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), myoglobin (MYO), cardiac troponin T (cTNT), and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) were among the compounds chosen as possible interferences for this aim. The level of all interfering substances was fixed at 10 ng mL−1, while the sST2 level was fixed at 1 ng mL−1, ensuring a clear contrast for detection within the system. Only in the presence of sST2 and its mixture did the ECL signal significantly drop (Fig. 5D). This suggested that the immunosensor had a very good selective response to sST2 in comparison to other pertinent biomolecules. Next, stability was a crucial consideration when determining whether the immunosensor could be promoted in clinical practice. With an average relative standard deviation (RSD) of 3.80%, a consistent ECL signal for sST2 (1 ng mL−1) within 77 s was seen (Fig. 5C), suggesting that the constructed immunosensor had outstanding stability. Simultaneously, to determine long-term stability of the ECL immunosensor, the Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100 composites were kept at 4 °C, and their ECL intensity was calculated daily. Notably, after five days, the ECL intensity retained 97.98% of its initial value, highlighting the exceptional storage stability of the composites. The detection results displayed a RSD of only 0.87%, further confirming the long-term stability of the developed immunosensor (Fig. 5E). Lastly, we assessed the repeatability of the fabricated immunosensor, five independent electrodes were tested under identical conditions for 1 ng mL−1 sST2 detection. The results showed a RSD of 0.82% (Fig. 5F), indicating excellent reproducibility of the proposed immunosensor. In conclusion, the developed biosensor demonstrated excellent specificity, remarkable stability, and superior reproducibility, highlighting its significant potential for clinical translation and practical implementation in diagnostic applications.

Recovery test

To explore the clinical applicability of the ECL immunosensor, recovery trials were conducted to observe sST2 in human serum samples. The dilution of healthy human serum was conducted 100-fold with PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) before spiking with known concentrations of sST2. Table 1 illustrated that the recovery range was 98.32% to 103.13%, with RSD (n = 3) between 1.93% and 3.23%. These outcomes established that the proposed biosensor had the potential to be applied for detecting sST2 in real-world samples.

Real sample detection

The ability of the constructed immunosensor to analyze complex biological materials was evaluated using 18 clinical human plasma samples. These samples were collected from Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital Bijie Hospital, comprising specimens from eleven healthy individuals and seven HF patients, and were characterized using the ELISA technique. The proposed ECL method was then applied to determine the concentration of sST2 in these samples. The results showed a strong correlation between the ECL method and the reference method, with a regression equation of y = 3.865 + 2.129 × and an R2 value of 0.9860 (Fig. 6). These findings demonstrated that the developed ECL immunosensor could reliably detect sST2 in real clinical samples.

Conclusion

In summary, a novel self-accelerated dual-quenching ECL-RET pair of Au-g-C3N4@MIL-100/Pt@CuS was developed for highly sensitive detection of sST2 based on ECL-RET strategy. The incorporation of MIL-100(Fe) and Au NPs as synergistic co-reaction accelerators significantly enhanced the ECL emission of the g-C3N4/S2O82− system by nearly twofold, mitigating the inherent limitations of stacked g-C3N4 NSs. Furthermore, the Pt@CuS hybrids achieved a nearly twofold increase in ECL quenching efficiency compared to single acceptors by leveraging their synergistic dual-acceptor function. This novel self-accelerated dual-quenching ECL-RET strategy resulted in an immunosensor with outstanding analytical performance, including a wide linear range (0.001–100 ng mL−1), an ultralow detection limit (0.547 pg mL⁻1), excellent specificity, stability, and reproducibility. This work not only provided a robust sST2 detection tool for HF monitoring but also established a versatile platform that could be adapted for other biomarkers by simply altering the specific antibodies. Future work will focus on simplifying the sensor fabrication process and integrating it with microfluidic devices to enhance its utility for point-of-care testing and real-time monitoring applications.
