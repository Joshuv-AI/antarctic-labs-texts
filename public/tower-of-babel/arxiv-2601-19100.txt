Reward Engineering for Reinforcement Learning in Software Tasks
Md Rayhanul Masud, Azmine Toushik Wasi, Salman Rahman, Md Rizwan Parvez

Abstract
Reinforcement learning is increasingly used for code-centric tasks. These tasks include code generation, summarization, understanding, repair, testing, and optimization. This trend is growing faster with large language models and autonomous agents. A key challenge is how to design reward signals that make sense for software. In many RL problems, the reward is a clear number. In software, this is often not possible. The goal is rarely a single numeric objective. Instead, rewards are usually proxies. Common proxies check if the code compiles, passes tests, or satisfies quality metrics. Many reward designs have been proposed for code-related tasks. However, the work is scattered across areas and papers. There is no single survey that brings these approaches together and shows the full landscape of reward design for RL in software. In this survey, we provide the first systematic and comprehensive review of reward engineering for RL in software tasks. We focus on existing methods and techniques. We structure the literature along three complementary dimensions, summarizing the reward-design choices within each. We conclude with challenges and recommendations in the reward design space for SE tasks.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for software engineering tasks, enabling agents to learn from execution feedback, test outcomes, and user preferences rather than relying solely on labeled examples
51
. Recent breakthroughs, including CodeRL for program synthesis
24
, DeepSeek-R1 for reasoning
12
, and RL-based agents for automated program repair
57
, have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on code generation, bug fixing, test synthesis, and repository-level issue resolution, as measured on benchmarks such as HumanEval, MBPP, and SWE-bench. Central to the success of these systems is the careful design of RL environments and reward functions. The environment defines how the agent interacts with code artifacts through states, actions, and transitions
46
, while the reward function translates complex notions of code quality, including correctness, efficiency, security, and readability, into scalar signals that guide learning
57
.

Despite the growing success of RL for software engineering, designing effective reward functions remains a fundamental challenge
51
. Unlike domains such as game playing where rewards are well defined
32
;
44
, software tasks involve multiple competing objectives that are difficult to quantify. A code snippet may compile successfully but fail test cases, pass all tests but contain security vulnerabilities, or be functionally correct but poorly readable. Existing approaches address these concerns in isolation, using execution-based rewards for correctness, similarity-based rewards for matching reference solutions, or learned preference models for subjective quality.
However, combining multiple signals introduces further challenges such as reward weighting, scale mismatch, and potential conflicts between objectives
39
;
1
;
6
;
30
. Moreover, reward design choices are tightly coupled with environment design, including how states are represented, what actions are available, and at what granularity rewards are assigned, yet these remain poorly understood across the literature.

Given these challenges, a systematic review of reward and environment design for RL in software engineering is needed, yet no such resource exists. Several surveys
61
;
56
;
42
;
51
;
68
have examined reinforcement learning or machine learning for software engineering, but none focuses on reward and environment design as central concerns. Existing surveys on RL for SE primarily address algorithm selection and application domains, treating reward functions as implementation details rather than core design choices that shape learning outcomes
51
. Recent surveys on RL for large language models
15
discuss reward modeling for alignment and reasoning, but do not address the unique challenges of software artifacts such as executability, test coverage, and semantic correctness
68
. As a result, practitioners lack a unified framework for understanding how different reward formulations compare, when to use execution-based versus learned rewards, and how environment design choices interact with reward effectiveness.

To address this gap, we present the first systematic survey of reward and environment design for reinforcement learning in software engineering. We review 50+ related papers published between 2018 and 2025 across major venues in software engineering, machine learning, and natural language processing. We organize the literature along three dimensions: reward source (execution-based, similarity-based, preference-based), reward granularity (token, line, function, program, trajectory), and aggregation strategy (single signal, weighted combination, learned mixing). Based on this taxonomy, we address the following research questions:

RQ1:
How can we categorize the existing reward schemes in reinforcement learning for software engineering tasks?
This question aims to establish a comprehensive taxonomy that organizes reward schemes by their underlying mechanisms and applicable task domains.

RQ2:
What are the recurring reward design patterns used across different SE tasks? This question identifies common reward formulations despite their distinct objectives.

RQ3:
How are rewards calculated at different granularities (token, line, function, program, or trajectory level) across SE tasks?
This question analyzes how granularity choices affect credit assignment and learning policy.

RQ4:
What strategies are employed to combine heterogeneous reward signals such as execution correctness, semantic similarity, and human preference?
This question examines how these diverse signals are weighted, aggregated, and balanced during training.

We close with challenges and future directions. Details of literature collection are in Appendix
A
.

Reward Methods.

In RL for software engineering (SE), rewards map task goals to optimization signals, reflecting objectives such as correctness, coverage, security, readability, and human preference. We group rewards into three classes:
execution-based
,
reference-based
, and
model-based
.

Execution-Based Rewards
are obtained by running the artifact (e.g., compiling and executing tests). They are widely used in code generation, program repair, and testing.
We use
Execution
to denote correctness feedback from compilation and tests, often binary or proportional to the fraction of tests passed. For testing and fuzzing, rewards can additionally encourage exploration via
Coverage
, e.g., line/branch coverage, mutation score, or newly reached states.

Reference-Based Rewards
compare outputs against ground-truth artifacts (e.g., gold code or summaries). We denote similarity-based metrics (BLEU/ROUGE, exact match, CodeBLEU, AST similarity) as
Similarity
. In retrieval-augmented settings,
Retrieval
rewards measure how well the agent fetches relevant items (e.g., NDCG, MRR, Precision@k).

Model-Based Rewards
come from learned reward models or external analyzers and can capture harder-to-specify objectives. We denote learned human-aligned judgments (e.g., code quality/readability/helpfulness) as
Preference
. These signals support multi-objective optimization but can be sensitive to reward incorrect specification.

RL Optimization Methods.

Most works fine-tune policies with on-policy methods such as
PPO

40
, using a clipped objective to stabilize updates under sparse, high-variance rewards. Recent alternatives include
GRPO
-style
41
updates that normalize rewards over groups of sampled completions (reducing reliance on a value critic) and
RLVR
(Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards)
22
approaches that integrate deterministic feedback loops to support heterogeneous, multi-objective optimization.

We use
Execution
,
Similarity
,
Retrieval
,
Coverage
, and
Preference
as shorthand labels throughout the paper.

understanding
, (iii)

, (iv)

testing

Code-Centric Tasks

3.1
Code Generation

3.1.1
NL2Code

For NL2Code, the model maps natural-language specifications (e.g., problem statements) to executable programs, where correctness is ultimately defined by program behavior. Accordingly, reinforcement learning methods predominantly rely on
execution
-based rewards from compilation and unit-test feedback, often complemented with auxiliary
similarity
or
preference
signals to improve credit assignment and training stability.
CodeRL
24
directly optimizes an
execution
-based scalar return obtained from compilation and unit-test outcomes,

r_{\text{exec}}(y)=\begin{cases}1.0,&\text{if $y$ passes all unit tests},\\
-0.3,&\text{if $y$ fails a unit test},\\
-0.6,&\text{if $y$ gets a runtime error},\\
-1.0,&\text{if $y$ fails to compile}.\end{cases}

(1)

and reduces variance through a greedy baseline and a learned critic that predicts test outcomes to provide dense token-level feedback. PPOCoder
43
extends this paradigm by combining execution rewards with structure-aware similarity signals relative to a reference solution
y^{\star}
, integrating compiler feedback with syntactic and semantic matching via

\displaystyle r(y)

\displaystyle=r_{\text{exec}}(y)+r_{\text{AST}}(y,y^{\star})

(2)

\displaystyle+r_{\text{DFG}}(y,y^{\star})-\lambda_{\text{KL}}\,r_{\text{KL}}(y),

thereby blending
execution
and
similarity
while regularizing deviations from the pretrained policy. In contrast, B-Coder
65
adopts a value-based formulation using Dueling DQN and relies on a sparse but granular
execution
signal, similar to CodeRL,
using the learned value function to guide decoding rather than explicit reward shaping. RLEF
11
further emphasizes outcome-level supervision by formulating multi-turn refinement as an MDP with a predominantly terminal
execution
reward,
penalizing invalid intermediate steps while deferring most learning signals to the final outcome. To address the sparsity of such rewards, PRM-based RL
5
introduces a learned Process Reward Model that predicts the correctness of partial code prefixes from unit-test supervision, combining sparse outcome rewards with dense process-level feedback as

r(y)=r_{\text{UT}}(y)+\lambda_{\text{PRM}}\,r_{\text{PRM}}(y),

(3)

where
r_{\text{UT}}(y)=1[\text{unit tests pass}]
. PRL-Coder
64
follows a similar philosophy but constructs compiler-verified step-level supervision via mutation and refactoring, using a PRM to produce an
execution
-aligned dense reward,
applied within PPO. Finally, Posterior-GRPO
8
integrates execution rewards with preference-based reasoning supervision, combining a format reward, an
execution
outcome reward, and a gated thinking reward from a
preference
-trained model,

r(y,z)=r_{f}(y)+r_{o}(y)+r_{o}(y)\cdot r_{t}(z),

(4)

where
r_{o}(y)=1[\text{all tests pass}]
ensures that reasoning traces are rewarded only when they lead to correct executions. Overall, NL2Code methods are unified by their reliance on
execution
signals as the primary source of supervision, while differing in how aggressively they mitigate reward sparsity through structural similarity, learned process models, or
preference
-based rewards.

3.1.2
Code Completion

Code completion aims to predict the next code tokens given the current context, ranging from local, single-file auto-complete to long-horizon suggestions that must remain consistent with a larger codebase. In repository-level code completion, outputs are often partial and non-executable, making
execution
-based rewards sparse or expensive; as a result, RL methods commonly rely on reference-derived
similarity
and learned
preference
signals.
IRCoCo
26
addresses sparse feedback by learning a critic
Q_{\varphi}
that provides dense token-level rewards by regressing BLEU and Edit Similarity scores against reference continuations,

r_{t}=r(s_{t},\hat{y}_{t})=\begin{cases}Q_{\varphi}(X;\hat{y}_{1:t})&\hat{y}_{t}\neq\texttt{</s>},\\
Q_{\varphi}(X;\hat{y}_{1:t-1})&\hat{y}_{t}=\texttt{</s>}.\end{cases}

(5)

yielding a signal that converts reference-based similarity metrics into a learned
preference
signal. By contrast, RLCoder
54
defines reward at the retrieval level using a binary objective

r(c_{i})=1\Big\{\mathrm{PPL}_{w}(y\mid x,c_{i})=\min_{c\in C}\mathrm{PPL}_{w}(y\mid x,c)\Big\}.

(6)

directly favoring retrieved contexts that minimize the weighted perplexity of the ground-truth completion and thus relying on a purely reference-based
similarity
criterion without a learned reward model. CoLT, which underlies aiXcoder-7B-v2
27
, further reframes the objective by applying DPO on synthetic preference triples constructed from reference code, optimizing

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}=-\mathbb{E}\big[\log\sigma\big(\beta(\Delta\log\pi_{\theta}-\Delta\log\pi_{\text{ref}})\big)\big]

(7)

which corresponds to an implicit
preference
-based reward that ranks ground-truth completions above degraded variants without execution feedback or an explicit critic.
Overall, these methods avoid execution-based supervision by deriving rewards from reference code, differing mainly in whether they use similarity directly, convert it into dense critic signals, or express it as pairwise preferences.

3.1.3
Code2Code

Code translation and migration convert programs across languages/APIs while preserving behavior, making RL naturally suited because rewards can be grounded in
execution
. CoTran
19
fine-tunes a CodeT5 translator with two execution-aligned signals: compilation feedback to encourage syntactic validity, and symbolic-execution/test feedback to measure functional equivalence via passing JUnit tests under round-trip translation. EffiReasonTrans
53
similarly uses a hybrid pipeline, adding GRPO with a dual reward that combines unit-test pass rate (
execution
) and a length-based efficiency term as a
preference
-style proxy. Overall, correctness is enforced mainly through execution rewards, with auxiliary preference objectives shaping efficiency.

3.2
Code Understanding

3.2.1
Code2NL

Code2NL tasks generate natural-language descriptions (e.g., docstrings or summaries) from code, where training is often dominated by teacher-forcing MLE and can suffer from exposure bias at generation time. RL can mitigate this by directly optimizing non-differentiable, sequence-level metrics (typically BLEU), yielding sparse, episode-level
similarity
rewards. Hybrid2Seq+Attn+DRL
49
and HAN
52
assign terminal BLEU rewards after full summary or comment generation, updating actor and critic via the corresponding advantage. TAG
2
extends this within a hierarchical RL framework, using BLEU-4 or ROUGE as similarity rewards and allowing reward selection to emphasize different metrics. Overall, Code2NL RL approaches rely on sparse, reference-based similarity rewards, aligning training with evaluation but providing limited guidance beyond textual overlap.

3.2.2
CodeRetrieval

Code retrieval aims to fetch relevant code snippets given a query, so success is naturally measured by ranking quality rather than exact textual matching. RL mainly optimizes ranking metrics, with
retrieval
rewards (MRR, NDCG@k) dominating and
similarity
rewards offering limited shaping benefit.
CoaCor
63
trains a code-annotation generator with a terminal MRR reward and shows it outperforms BLEU-based
similarity
. Cosoch
28
models session-level code search and directly optimizes
\mathrm{NDCG}@10
as a
retrieval
reward. QueCos
50
mixes MRR and BLEU-4 in a sparse end reward based on MRR. Overall, RL for code retrieval strongly favors retrieval-centric rewards, with similarity-based shaping largely ineffective.

3.3
Code Maintenance

3.3.1
Issue Resolution

Automated issue resolution targets end-to-end fixes for real repository issues, often requiring multi-step edits across files and interaction with tools, which makes it a long-horizon setting. Accordingly, recent RL approaches combine
similarity
,
execution
,
retrieval
, and
preference
rewards to guide both patch quality and the overall resolution process.
SWE-RL
57
uses a rule-based
similarity
signal (
-1
for malformed outputs;
[0,1]
for reference closeness) for scalable training. SWE-Gym
35
and DeepSWE
31
use sparse unit-test
execution
rewards, with DeepSWE adding a
preference
verifier for trajectory selection.

SWE-Swiss
13
uses a binary test reward (
R_{\text{repair}}=\pm 1
) and optionally adds retrieval/similarity signals, while Satori-SWE
66
trains retriever and generator separately with retrieval and learned preference rewards (plus format/similarity constraints). R2E-Gym
18
tackles issue resolution by generating a hybrid reward for patch ranking that combines an execution-free YES/NO verifier score with an execution-based test-pass score.
Overall, these methods trade off test-based correctness against similarity/preference signals for scalability and stability.

3.3.2
Code Refactoring

RL for code refactoring changes structure while preserving behavior, so rewards often target syntactic validity, compilability, and refactoring intent. Refactor-RL
34
trains with PPO on
extract-method
using a tool-based composite reward: tree-sitter syntax validity, successful compilation, and RefactoringMiner detection of the intended refactor, with a KL regularizer to limit drift—highlighting that fine-grained refactors benefit from multi-signal tool rewards when execution alone is insufficient.

3.3.3
Code Review

Code review comment generation involves open-ended natural language, limiting execution-based rewards and emphasizing preference alignment and tool-grounded supervision. CodeMentor
33
uses RLHF, training a reward model from expert-ranked comments and optimizing the generator with PPO to maximize this
preference
signal. CRScore++
21
combines preference optimization with static-analysis feedback, using linters and code-smell detectors to define expected review points and score candidate comments. Overall, RL for code review relies on preference-based rewards enriched with tool signals to balance subjective quality and verifiable critique.

3.3.4
Code Optimization

In compiler and auto-scheduling tasks, RL commonly uses
execution
rewards tied to end-to-end runtime speedups on target hardware. Pearl
23
rewards a sequence of loop transformations by stepwise speedups
\tau_{t}
and final
\tau_{f}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\tau_{t}
, using
r_{t}=\log_{4}(\tau_{t})
so that
\sum_{t}r_{t}=\log_{4}(\tau_{f})
. Illegal transformations are assigned
\tau_{t}=1
(thus
r_{t}=0
) and skipped, letting the agent learn legality implicitly without explicit penalties.

3.3.5
Bug Localization

Bug localization methods using RL mainly optimize
execution
-derived and ranking-quality signals, rather than direct patch correctness. RecBi
4
uses execution-trace
coverage
shaping measuring similarity/diversity of statement-coverage sets via Jaccard distance and rewards improvements in an aggregated quality score, with an
execution
oracle to validate passing tests. RLocator
3
, instead, treats localization as retrieval and optimizes
retrieval
rewards (MRR/MAP-style) with distance penalties, using developer interaction as implicit relevance feedback.

3.3.6
Program Repair

Program repair aligns naturally with
execution
-based rewards derived from compilation and unit-test outcomes, but recent work often densifies or augments these sparse signals using learned critics, preference models, or auxiliary objectives. RePair
71
trains a reward model (critic) from execution feedback, first defining a partial order over execution statuses, and then shaping PPO optimization via stepwise improvements,

\text{AC}>\text{PE}>\text{WA}=\text{TLE}=\text{MLE}>\text{CE}>\text{RE},

(8)

\Delta r_{\phi}(x_{i+1},x_{i})=r_{\phi}(x_{i+1})-r_{\phi}(x_{i}),

(9)

with a KL regularizer; this produces an
execution
-derived signal densified through a learned model rather than raw pass/fail outcomes. Repair-R1
14
jointly trains patch and test generation with rule-based rewards;
all optimized via GRPO, representing mainly
execution
-based signals. Repairity
48
uses RL from AI feedback, with a closed-source LLM generating pairwise preferences over candidate repairs and PPO optimizing this
preference
-based reward, while execution filters teacher-generated traces. Overall, program repair RL spans from pure execution feedback to hybrid approaches leveraging learned preference models for denser, more informative supervision.

3.4
Code Security

3.4.1
Vulnerability Detection

Vulnerability detection aims to identify security weaknesses in code. RL for vulnerability detection generally relies on labeled annotations to construct dense, reference-based rewards, as executing code to verify security properties is often infeasible at scale. ProRLearn
38
applies a binary reward
which constitutes a reference-based
similarity
reward without any
execution
or
coverage
component. RLFD
20
frames line-level localization with episode-level reward

R(\tau)=\frac{|V(\tau)\cap U|}{|V(\tau)\cup U|}+w\sum_{l\in U}\frac{1}{\mathrm{index}(l)+1},

(10)

where the first term measures IoU with ground-truth vulnerable lines (
similarity
) and the second term encourages earlier ranking of true positives (
retrieval
). Vulnerability-detection RL methods favor dense reference-based similarity signals, often augmented with ranking-based rewards.

3.4.2
Vulnerability Repair

Vulnerability repair often admits multiple valid patches and may lack reliable tests, making
execution
-based rewards sparse. PairCode
16
fine-tunes a causal LLM with PPO using a reference-based
similarity
reward that sums CodeBLEU and BERTScore against the ground-truth patch,
R=R_{\text{CodeB}}+R_{\text{BERT}}
, providing execution-free guidance but biasing toward surface/semantic similarity rather than behavior. SecRepair
17
instead applies RL to commit-comment generation, optimizing BERTScore similarity to preserve the semantics of the ground-truth comment. Vul-R2
59
combines a verifiable critic with reference shaping:

R=\begin{cases}-2,&\text{if the patch is judged incorrect},\\
1+\mathrm{Sim}(o,\mathrm{GT}),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(11)

where
\mathrm{Sim}\in[0,1]
measures similarity to the human patch. Overall, RL for vulnerability repair favors dense similarity and model-judged
preference
rewards due to sparse execution feedback and patch multiplicity.

3.5
Code Testing

3.5.1
Test Generation

Test generation aims to automatically produce tests that both execute correctly and expose faults in the target program. RL for unit-test generation largely uses
execution
-based rewards, often reshaping raw execution signals to promote informative, non-trivial tests.
AceCoder
67
computes pass rates by executing sampled programs, converts them into preference pairs to train a Bradley–Terry reward model, and RL fine-tunes with either
preference
-based reward or a binary execution reward.
CURE
55
co-evolves a coder and test generator using execution matrices over sampled programs, yielding
execution
-grounded rewards, and the resulting test generator can act as a reward proxy by producing discriminative tests. UTRL
25
optimizes a weighted sum of validity and discrimination rewards from compilation/runtime outcomes to generate tests for both correct and fault-revealing. Overall, RL-based test generation emphasizes execution-driven supervision, often augmented with learned preference models or shaping terms to produce more discriminative and informative test cases.

3.5.2
Fuzzing

Fuzzing generates large volumes of (often mutated) inputs to exercise a program and expose crashes or vulnerabilities. RL for fuzzing mainly uses
execution
-derived feedback, combining coverage signals for exploration with oracle-based outcomes to steer generation toward valid or vulnerability-triggering inputs. REINAM
60
uses a binary
execution
reward (
1
if the input is accepted,
0
otherwise).
RLF
45
combines an
execution
vulnerability reward,
R_{\text{bugs}}
, with a block-level coverage reward,
R_{\text{cov}}
, via

R_{\text{mix}}=\alpha R_{\text{bugs}}+(1-\alpha)R_{\text{cov}},

(12)

balancing vulnerability discovery with exploratory coverage.
CovRL-Fuzz
7

Overall, RL-guided fuzzing demonstrates how execution and coverage signals can be effectively combined to maximize both exploration and vulnerability detection in testing.

4
Discussion

4.1
Reward design patterns and granularity

(P1) Verifiable, tool-grounded outcome rewards remain the anchor.

Across SE tasks, the most dependable rewards are grounded in external oracles such as compilation, unit tests, or deterministic checkers. This is clearest in code generation:
CodeRL

24
ties return to functional correctness on tests and optimizes it with an actor–critic objective. Likewise,
RLEF

11
uses a sparse binary reward based on whether generated code passes a (hidden) test suite, underscoring the continued dominance of
sparse but verifiable
signals. In program repair,
Repair-R1

14
similarly relies on tool-checkable outcomes (e.g., test-based repair success) while adding shaping terms.

(P2) Hybrid rewards: combining execution and similarity to reduce brittleness.

Pure similarity rewards can miss functional correctness, while pure execution rewards can be sparse or costly; many works therefore adopt weighted mixtures. For instance,
B-Coder

65
combines an execution-derived term with a similarity term via an explicit weighting, reflecting a recurring “correctness + closeness” pattern. When execution is unavailable or impractical, translation-style settings instead hybridize metrics:
PairCode

16
mixes syntactic and semantic similarity (CodeBLEU and BERTScore). Overall, hybridization is repeatedly used to trade off (i) faithful functional objectives and (ii) dense shaping for learnability.

(P3) Reward granularity in SE: from token/line to trajectory-level objectives

Our surveyed works show that reward granularity is a core axis of reward design. Program-level (terminal) rewards dominate when correctness is only checkable at the end, such as unit-test-based correctness in CodeRL
24
or pass/fail signals in RLEF
11
. Step- or line-level rewards appear when authors explicitly address credit assignment: PRM-based approaches and PRL-Coder
64
provide intermediate, line-level supervision that nudges generation toward states more likely to compile or pass tests
17
. Trajectory-level rewards arise when actions form structured sequences and success is measured by set or rank-based metrics; for example, RLFD
20
defines reward over the predicted vulnerable-line subset using IoU and ranking penalties, while RLocator
3
rewards ranking quality (MRR/MAP). A consistent theme is that
granularity tracks observability and oracle availability
: when reliable intermediate feedback exists (e.g., compiler/test signals or PRM scoring), works shift toward denser step-level rewards; when only final correctness is meaningful, rewards remain sparse.

4.2
Challenges and recommendations

(C1) Reward misalignment and proxy failure.

Text/similarity rewards can be easy to optimize while failing to improve downstream behavior; conversely, purely execution-based rewards can be too sparse or expensive. A useful guideline is to prefer
downstream-metric rewards
when the task has a clear evaluation measure (e.g., MAP/MRR for ranking), and to justify any proxy reward by demonstrating correlation with that measure.

(C2) Sparse-and-expensive feedback loops.

Tool-grounded rewards (tests, compilation, symbolic checks) incur high evaluation cost, limiting exploration and increasing variance. Promising mitigation include caching, incremental evaluation (e.g., compile/test only affected components), curriculum schedules that gradually increase oracle strength.

(C3) Weight sensitivity and comparability across papers.

Because heterogeneous rewards differ in scale and noise, naive weighted sums can be unstable and hard to compare across studies. Future work should report normalization and scaling strategies, ablate weight sensitivity, and consider multi-objective views when objectives are genuinely competing from correctness, readability to security and coverage.

5
Conclusion

This survey presents a structured review of reward design in reinforcement learning for software engineering tasks. We propose a clear taxonomy that organizes the literature by five complementary reward feedback mechanisms, leveraged on different computation granularity (line/token/repo level), and categorizes existing works accordingly. Finally, we summarize reward design patterns, and discuss challenges and recommendations for future directions in reward design in SE.

Limitations

While this survey aims to provide a comprehensive view of reward design in reinforcement learning for software engineering tasks, the area is evolving rapidly and some very recent or adjacent works may have been missed. We intentionally scope our review to
reward schemes used to train or adapt policies
for SE tasks (e.g., code generation, repair, translation, retrieval/repo-level completion, testing, and localization), and do not attempt to cover reward design for large-scale pre-training or general instruction tuning.
We prioritize works where reward formulation is a central contribution. Works that apply RL only as a minor component, or where the reward is implicit/standard and not analyzed, are omitted. To help keep pace with new literature, we plan to maintain an accompanying repository that can be incrementally updated as the field progresses.
