Abstract
. We study topological versions of an independent set in an abelian group and a linearly independent set in a vector space, a topologically independent set in a topological group and a topologically linearly independent set in a topological vector space. These counterparts of their algebraic versions are defined analogously and possess similar properties. Let ℂ × {\mathbb{C}}^{\times} be the multiplicative group of the field of complex numbers with its usual topology. We prove that a subset A A of an arbitrary Tychonoff power of ℂ × {\mathbb{C}}^{\times} is topologically independent if and only if the topological subgroup ⟨ A ⟩ \langle{A}\rangle that it generates is the Tychonoff direct sum ⨁ a ∈ A ⟨ a ⟩ \bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle . This theorem substantially generalizes an earlier result of the author, who has proved this for Abelian precompact groups. Further, we show that topologically independent and topologically linearly independent sets coincide in vector spaces with weak topologies, although they are different in general. We characterize topologically linearly independent sets in vector spaces with weak topologies and normed spaces. In a weak topology, a set A A is topologically linearly independent if and only if its linear span is the Tychonoff direct sum ℝ ( A ) {\mathbb{R}}^{(A)} . In normed spaces A A is topologically linearly independent if and only if it is uniformly minimal. Thus, from the point of view of topological linear independence, the Tychonoff direct sums ℝ ( A ) {\mathbb{R}}^{(A)} and (linear spans of) uniformly minimal sets, which are closely related to bounded biorthogonal systems, are of the same essence.

1.
Introduction

One of the fundamental concepts in the theory of abelian groups is that of an independent set. A subset
A
of nonzero elements of a group

B\subseteq A
and every indexed set
\{z_{b}\colon b\in B\}
of integers, the equality
\sum_{b\in B}z_{b}b=e_{G}
implies that

for all

When we replace the algebraic relation of “being equal to zero” with a topological notion of “being close to zero,” we obtain a natural group-topological counterpart of an independent set from
[
3

:

Definition 1.1
.

A subset

of nonzero elements of a topological group

is

if, for every neighborhood

there is a neighborhood
U
of

such that for every finite subset

and every indexed set
\{z_{b}\colon b\in B\}
of integers, the inclusion
\sum_{b\in B}z_{b}b\in U
implies that

for all

is called a

-witness
of the topological independence of

.

Similarly, in vector spaces, we can replace the integer linear combination in the definition of an independent set with a linear combination with real numbers as coefficients to obtain one of the key notions of linear algebra – a linearly independent set. The definition of its topological counterpart was anticipated in
[
3
, Remark 4.13]

Definition 1.2

A subset

of a topological vector space

provided that for every neighborhood

of

there exists a neighborhood

of

such that for every finite

and every indexed set

of reals, the inclusion
\sum_{a\in F}r_{a}a\in U
implies that

. We call this neighborhood

-witness
of the topological linear independence of

In our paper, we explore the two previously defined notions. In the realm of topological vector spaces, where we have both, we compare them.

A further consequence is Theorem

Another consequence of Theorem

Namely, in Theorem

This is because, for a topologically (linearly) independent set in the presence of a weak topology, the topological vector space

In the following, we turn to the situation in normed spaces. In Theorem

Topological independence and Tychonoff direct sums

of the neutral element of
({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{I}
. There are finite
M\subset I
and positive
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\mathcal{U}\left({M},{\varepsilon}\right)\subset V.

By Lemma
2.6
, the set
F=\{a\in A\colon a(i)\neq 1\mbox{ for some }i\in M\}
is finite. Observe that
\langle{A\setminus F}\rangle\subset\mathcal{U}\left({M},{\varepsilon}\right)
. Therefore, (
1
) holds and
A
is absolutely Cauchy summable. This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
G
be a locally compact, compactly generated topological group. A subset
A
of
G
is topologically independent if and only if
\langle{A}\rangle
is the Tychonoff direct sum
\bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle
.

Proof.

By
[
6
, Theorem 9.8]
, the topological group
G
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{l}\times F
for appropriate nonnegative integers
k,l
and compact group
F
. Hence
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of the topological group
{\mathbb{R}}^{z}\times F
for some nonnegative integer
z
. In addition, the additive topological group
{\mathbb{R}}^{z}
is isomorphic to the multiplicative topological group
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{z}
and, by
[
1
, Corollary 11.5.2]
, the compact group
F
is isomorphic to a topological subgroup of
\mathbb{S}^{J}
for a suitable set
J
. Consequently, there is a set
I
such that
G
is isomorphic to a topological subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{I}\times\mathbb{S}^{I}
. Since the latter topological group is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{C}}^{\times})^{I}
, the conclusion follows from Theorem
2.7
.
∎

We end this section with Theorem
2.9
, which completely resolves the relation of topologically independent sets and Tychonoff direct sums in the realm of the underlying topological groups of topological vector spaces.
Before stating it, recall that a topology of a topological vector space
X
is
weak
provided that it is generated by the family
X^{*}
of all continuous linear functionals on
X
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A
be a topologically independent subset of a topological vector space
E
. Then
\langle{A}\rangle
is the Tychonoff direct sum
\bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle
if and only if the topology of
\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
is weak.

Proof.

Put
X=\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. If the topological vector space
X
has a weak topology, then the diagonal

\Delta_{f\in X^{*}}f\colon X\to{\mathbb{R}}^{X^{*}}

is an isomorphic embedding of topological vector spaces. Since the topological groups
{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
are isomorphic,
A
can be considered to be a subset of the topological group
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{X^{*}}
which in turn is (isomorphic to) a topological subgroup of
(C^{\times})^{X^{*}}
. Hence,
\langle{A}\rangle
is the Tychonoff direct sum
\bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle
by Theorem
2.7
.

If
\langle{A}\rangle
is the Tychonoff direct sum
\bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle
, then it is isomorphic to the Tychonoff direct sum
{\mathbb{Z}}^{(A)}
, because each
\langle{a}\rangle
is isomorphic to the discrete group
{\mathbb{Z}}
. The latter Tychonoff direct sum has an invariant linear span by
[
10
, Corollary 3.5]
, which means that its linear span is unique (up to an isomorphism of topological vector spaces), whenever it embeds as a topological group into a topological vector space. Therefore, the topological vector space
\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{(A)}
. Hence, it has a weak topology.
∎

Example 2.10
.

Every Schauder basis
A
of an infinite-dimensional Banach space is topologically independent, but
\langle{A}\rangle
is not the Tychonoff direct sum
\bigoplus_{a\in A}\langle{a}\rangle
.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Proposition 4.9]
, every Schauder basis in a Banach space is topologically independent. On the other hand, the topology of an infinite-dimensional normed space is never weak. The conclusion follows from Theorem
2.9
.
∎

3.
Topological (linear) independence and minimality in topological vector spaces

We start this section by recalling a minimal set in a topological vector space.

Definition 3.1
.

A subset
A
of topological vector space
V
is

minimal
if, for all
a\in A
, we have

(14)

a\not\in\overline{\langle A\setminus\{a\}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}}.

Remark 3.2
.

We have adopted the name

from
[
15
, Definition 6.1]
, where a minimal sequence was introduced in a Banach space.
Minimal sets in Banach spaces are defined in
[
4
, Definition 1.1]
, where the connection of minimal sets and biorthogonal systems is explained via the Hahn-Banach theorem.
In
[
5
]
, a minimal set is called

.
In the paper
[
10
]
, which we use substantially in this section, minimal sets are called
semi-basic
. This name comes from
[
7
]
, where the minimal sequences have this name.

In this section, we establish the following chains of implications.

In general, no other implication can be added to the above diagram as demonstrated in Examples

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A
be a subset of a Hausdorff topological vector space. Consider the following statements:

(i)

A
is topologically linearly independent.

(ii)

A
is topologically independent.

(iii)

is linearly independent.

\Rightarrow

is finite, then all three conditions are equivalent.

Proof.

The implication (i)

[

. Finally, if

Given an element

of a linearly independent subset
A
of a vector space, the symbol
\pi^{A}_{a}
denotes the unique projection from
\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
onto
\langle{a}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
such that

The proof of the following proposition can be found in the proof of

.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
A
be a linearly independent subset of a topological vector space
E
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

A
is minimal.

(ii)

The projection
\pi_{a}\colon\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\to\langle{a}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous for each
a\in A
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a linearly independent subset of a topological vector space
E
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

A
is topologically linearly independent.

(ii)

The collection
\{\pi_{a}^{A}\colon a\in A\}
is equicontinuous.

Proof.

It suffices to realize that given
x\in\langle{A}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, there is a unique indexed set
\{r_{a}\colon a\in A\}
of reals, where
r_{a}\neq 0
only for finitely many

, such that

x=\sum_{a\in A}r_{a}a.

Clearly,

\pi^{A}_{a}(x)=r_{a}a.

Now, given a neighborhood
W
of zero, the
W
-witness
U
of the topological linear independence of
A
is witnessing the equicontinuity of the collection
\{\pi^{A}_{a}\colon a\in A\}
and vice versa.
∎

The next statement is a direct consequence of Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Every topologically linearly independent set is minimal.

Example

provides a typical topologically linearly independent set. As shown in Theorem

, this is basically the only example of a topologically linearly independent set in a topological vector space with a weak topology.

Example 3.7
.

Let
A
be a set. For each

, let

satisfy
x_{a}(a)\neq 0

. The set

is topologically linearly independent, and

is canonically isomorphic to the topological vector space

.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let

be a subset of nonzero elements of a vector space

(ii)

is topologically independent;

(iv)

, we have (iv).

, and (v) implies (i) as shown in Example

.
∎

Example 3.9
.

Consider the Banach space
\ell^{\infty}
with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm topology and its subset

, where

for every

. The set

is topologically independent, but not minimal. In particular, according to Corollary

, it is not topologically linearly independent.

Indeed, let

be the open ball around

with a radius of

. If

. Therefore,

is a

for every neighborhood

of

On the other hand, if for any

is not minimal.

.

Consider the Banach space

4.
Topologically linearly independent sets in normed spaces

In the first part of this section, we link topologically linearly independent sets with bounded biorthogonal systems and uniformly minimal sets. In order to do so, let us recall some necessary terminology.

Given a nonempty set
I
, a normed space
X
and its dual
X^{*}
, a family
\{(x_{i},x^{*}_{i})\colon i\in I\}
of pairs from
X\times X^{*}
is called a
biorthogonal system
, provided that
x_{i}^{*}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
, where
\delta_{ij}
is Kronecker
\delta
. This biorthogonal system is called
bounded
if there is positive
K\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\left|\left|{x_{i}}\right|\right|\cdot\left|\left|{x_{i}^{*}}\right|\right|\leq K
for all
i\in I
.

Remark 4.1
.

It is a simple application of the Hahn-Banach theorem that given a faithfully indexed set
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
in
X
it is possible to find
x^{*}_{i}\in X^{*}
for each
i\in I
such that
\{(x_{i},x^{*}_{i})\colon i\in I\}
forms a biorthogonal system if and only if the set
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
is minimal.

Similarly, as remarked in
[
4
, Remark 1.24]
, the biorthogonal system above is bounded if and only if the set
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
is uniformly minimal in the sense of Definition
4.2
.

Definition 4.2
.

A subset
A
of a normed space is
uniformly minimal
provided that there exists a positive
K\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for every
a\in A
we have

(15)

\mbox{dist}\left(\frac{a}{\left|\left|{a}\right|\right|},\overline{\langle A\setminus\{a\}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}}\right)\geq K.

Remark 4.3
.

Uniformly minimal sequences in Banach spaces are studied, for example, in
[
16
,
4
]
. They also appear in
[
15
]
, however, the name “uniformly minimal” is suggested for a stronger property there (see
[
15
, Remark 7.2]
).

It turns out that a subset of a normed space is topologically linearly independent if and only if it is uniformly minimal (Theorem
4.5
). This result is based on Proposition
4.4
which provides a simple description of topologically linearly independent sets in normed spaces. Its straightforward proof, which is based on the absolute homogeneity of a norm, is omitted.

Proposition 4.4
.

A subset
A
of a normed space is topologically linearly independent if and only if there is positive
K\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for every finite
F\subset A
and indexed set of reals
\{r_{a}\colon a\in F\}

\mbox{if }\left|\left|{\sum_{a\in F}r_{a}a}\right|\right|<K,\mbox{ then }||r_{a}a||<1\mbox{ for all }a\in F.

Theorem 4.5
.

A subset of a normed space is topologically linearly independent if and only if it is uniformly minimal.

Proof.

Assume that
A
is a topologically linearly independent subset of a normed space, and let
K
be as in Proposition
4.4
. Pick
a\in A
arbitrarily. Since
\left|\left|{\frac{a}{\left|\left|{a}\right|\right|}}\right|\right|=1
, it follows from Proposition
4.4
that

\left|\left|{\frac{a}{\left|\left|{a}\right|\right|}-\sum_{b\in F}r_{b}b}\right|\right|\geq K

for every finite
F\subset A\setminus\{a\}
and all reals
r_{b}
, where
b\in F
. This gives us (
15
), and
A
is uniformly minimal.

To show the reversed implication, assume that
A
is uniformly minimal and take
K
as in (
15
). Pick arbitrarily a finite
F\subset A
, and for each
a\in F
a nonzero real number
r_{a}
such that

(16)

\left|\left|{\sum_{a\in F}r_{a}a}\right|\right|<K.

Fix
b\in F
arbitrarily. From (
15
) and (
16
) we get

K\leq\mbox{dist}\left(\frac{b}{\left|\left|{b}\right|\right|},\overline{\langle A\setminus\{b\}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}}\right)\leq\left|\left|{\frac{r_{b}b}{\left|\left|{r_{b}b}\right|\right|}+\sum_{a\in F\setminus\{b\}}\frac{r_{a}}{\left|\left|{r_{b}b}\right|\right|}a}\right|\right|<\frac{K}{\left|\left|{r_{b}b}\right|\right|}.

Multiplying the above inequalities by
\frac{\left|\left|{r_{b}b}\right|\right|}{K}
gives us
\left|\left|{r_{b}b}\right|\right|<1
. Hence,
A
is topologically linearly independent by Proposition
4.4
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

In this remark, we estimate cardinalities of topologically linearly independent sets in some topological vector spaces.

Recall that a
Markushevich basis
of a Banach space
X
is a biorthogonal system
\{(x_{i},x^{*}_{i})\colon i\in I\}
such that
\langle{\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
is dense in
X
and
\langle{\{x^{*}_{i}\colon i\in I\}}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
is dense in
X^{*}
in the
w^{*}
-topology. By Remark
4.1
and Theorem
4.5
, Markushevich basis is bounded if and only if the set
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
is topologically linearly independent.

(i)

Every infinite-dimensional Banach space contains a countably infinite topologically linearly independent set, as it contains a bounded Markushevich basis by a result of Pełczyński
[
9
, Theorem 1]
. See also
[
12
]
.

(ii)

Under the continuum hypothesis, there is a non-separable Banach space
X
such that every minimal (in particular, every topologically linearly independent) set in
X
is countable. This
is directly based on Kunen’s result, which can be found in
[
8
, Theorem 7.7]
.

(iii)

To find topologically linearly independent sets of uncountable cardinality in some Banach spaces, we can use the result of Plichko from
[
13
]
, which states that every non-separable Banach space with a Markushevich basis contains a bounded Markushevich basis (see also
[
4
, Theorem 5.13]
).

(iv)

As we will see in Proposition
4.7
, there are infinite-dimensional topological vector spaces such that every topologically linearly independent set is finite.

As usual, given a normed space
X
, we denote by
w
the topology of
X
generated by all continuous functionals on
X
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
X
be a normed space. Every topologically linearly independent subset of
(X,w)
is finite.

Proof.

Assume, for contradiction, that there is an infinite faithfully indexed set
A\colon=\{a_{n}\colon n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\subset(X,w)
that is topologically linearly independent. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
choose
r_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(17)

\left|\left|{r_{n}a_{n}}\right|\right|>n.

This is possible because all
a_{n}
’s are nonzero, being elements of a topologically linearly independent set. Since the topology
w
is weak, it follows from Theorem
3.8
that the set
A
is absolutely Cauchy summable. Therefore, for each neighborhood
V
of zero in
(B,w)
, there is finite
F\subset A
such that (
1
) holds. It follows that the sequence
(r_{n}a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges weakly to zero. Thus, it is norm-bounded by the uniform boundedness principle. This contradicts (
17
).
∎

Example 4.8
.

Continuous linear injections do not necessarily preserve topological linear independence.

Proof.

Let
B
be an infinite-dimensional Banach space with a bounded Markushevich basis
A
. Then
A
is an infinite topologically linearly independent subset of
B
by Remark
4.6
.
Consider the identity map
\iota\colon(B,||\hskip 2.84544pt||)\to(B,w)
. Then
\iota
is a continuous linear injection, and
\iota(A)
being infinite is not topologically linearly independent in
(B,w)
by Proposition
4.7
.
∎

We close this paper with an observation that combining topological linear independence with a property of being a free basis ends up with the notion of a (long) Schauder basis of the classical Banach space
\ell^{1}(N)
of all elements
x=(x_{n})_{n\in N}
such that
x_{n}
is nonzero for at most countably many
n\in N
and
\sum_{n\in N}|x_{n}|
converges unconditionally to the norm of
x
.

Recall that, as in
[
11
]
, a
free basis

N
of a Banach space
X
is a closed subset of
X
containing
0_{X}
with the property that for every Banach space
Y
and every Lipschitz map
f\colon N\to Y
such that
f(0_{X})=0_{Y}
there exists unique bounded linear extension
\hat{f}\colon X\to Y
of
f
.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
M
is a topologically linearly independent subset of a Banach space
X
and
M\cup\{e_{X}\}
is a free basis of
X
, then there is a canonical isomorphism
\phi\colon X\to\ell^{1}(N)
, where
N=\left\{\frac{m}{\left|\left|{m}\right|\right|}\colon m\in M\right\}
. In particular,
M
is a (long) Schauder basis of
X
.

Proof.

From
[
11
, Lemma 3.1]
, it follows that
N\cup\{e_{X}\}
is a free basis of
X
. From the definition, the set
N
is topologically linearly independent. Consequently, according to Theorem
4.5
, it is uniformly minimal. Hence,
N
is uniformly discrete by (
15
). Since
N
is also bounded as all its elements have norm 1, it is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to a metric space in which all elements have a distance equal to 2. To conclude the proof, it remains to apply
[
17
, Example 3.10]
together with a dictionary from
[
11
, Observation 2.2]
.
∎
