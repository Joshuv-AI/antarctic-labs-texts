Associations of Emotional Divergence in Risk Communication With Forecast Error Type During Typhoon Khanun
Authors: Kiru Kim, Jonghun Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForecast errors of severe weather events aggravate economic damage and degrade public mental health. Whether forecast errors are underestimated or overestimated can shape public emotional responses differently, which remains unknown. In this study, we investigate the socio‐psychological impacts of forecast errors during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun over the Korean Peninsula. We evaluate the predictive performance of multiple lead‐hour precipitation forecasts against observational data and conduct a sentimental analysis of over 43,000 online discourses from the NAVER Weather Report Talk platform. Multiple lead‐hour precipitation forecasts demonstrate underestimation in the eastern and southeastern regions of the Korean Peninsula and overestimation in the western and southwestern regions. The spatial discrepancies of precipitation forecasts are associated with distinct emotional responses: overestimation (underestimation) makes anxiety and worry (stress and confusion) the dominant emotion types in the discourses from the NAVER Report Talk platform. The findings of this study suggest that expectation–reality mismatch is a key mechanism in risk communication, and the direction of this mismatch differentiates public response. This study provides insights into the potential value of improved forecast accuracy on reducing emotional distress and strengthening public resilience during extreme weather events.

Introduction

Weather forecasts have intrinsic predictive limitations beyond 2 weeks. In the Earth's weather system, a small difference at the initial forecasting time can lead to a large difference later, which is known as the butterfly effect (Lorenz, 1994). Weather forecasts have two directions/types of errors: predicted rainfall that does not occur (false positive/Type I error) and actual rainfall that was not predicted (false negative/Type II error). These error types of forecast can alter public sentiment differently. In the case of false positive error, weather forecasts lose credibility with the public, leading to long‐term distrust (Burgeno & Joslyn, 2020). False positive errors in forecast also make people catastrophize the situation in advance, expecting worst possible outcomes from an emerging severe weather event, which is a type of cognitive distortion (Kienzler et al., 2025). These psychological impacts can permanently and/or temporarily degrade mental health, heightening the levels of anxiety and stress (Peterson & Moon, 1999). In the case of a false negative error, people may miss the window of opportunity, aggravating economic losses and fatalities from unpredicted intense rainfall. The lack of preparedness exacerbates mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression afterward (Vo et al., 2025). Unexpected severe weather events can disrupt daily routines and lead to significant psychological stress (H. Liu et al., 2024).

Land‐falling tropical cyclones (TCs) cause physical damage and socioeconomic losses (Krichene et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). The public have experienced devastating effects of tropical cyclone (TC) landfalls, leading to a high level of perceived risk and motivation to seek relevant information before, during, and after a TC landfall (Demuth et al., 2025). Limited prediction skill of extreme precipitation from TCs can degrade public mental health, for example, increasing the level of anxiety (Broos et al., 2023; Odabaşı, 2022). Research has shown that exposure to unexpected natural disasters can degrade mental health, causing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression (Afifi et al., 2012). Moreover, frequent false alarms can cause “alarm fatigue” such that the public intend to ignore future warnings/alerts (Lim et al., 2019). This “alarm fatigue” phenomenon causes inadequate public response during the emergence of actual extreme precipitation and altering community response dynamics (Johnson et al., 2017; LeClerc & Joslyn, 2015).

Prompt hurricane/typhoon risk communication enhances the resilience of affected communities and helps recover the regional immune system (Behnassi et al., 2021). Missing adequate risk communication aggravates the devastating effects of TCs beyond their destructive damage. For example, Hurricane Katrina in 2005 was the devastating event that caused unprecedented damage over the U.S. state of Texas, resulting in permanent mental health issues among survivors, such as post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression (Raker et al., 2019). While care communication was adequate, consensus communication and crisis communication were failed during the landfall due to inadequate clarity, insufficient credibility, and a failure to properly adapt to critical audiences (Cole & Fellows, 2008).

Risk communication systems with public participation can facilitate the management of a crisis and also strengthen a community's ability to cope with and recover from disasters (Agyepong & Liang, 2023). Recently, real‐time communication platforms have been a reliable data source that adjust the situational awareness of the public during severe weather events. For example, social media is an important information source that provides actionable information for disaster response and mitigation (Erokhin & Komendantova, 2024). Social media posts and online search queries have been used to gauge the level of various emotion types of the public (Neppalli et al., 2017; Thelwall et al., 2011). Social media platforms can capture real‐time public awareness and responses during extreme events, providing valuable insights into situational awareness and risk communication (Castillo et al., 2011; Vieweg et al., 2010). Individuals actively express emotions such as fear, anxiety, and anger during extreme events, enabling the tracking of temporal variations in public emotional responses through large‐scale social media data (Desai et al., 2020; Ruz et al., 2020). These studies have primarily focused on overall levels of public attention or aggregated emotional responses. Furthermore, prior approaches have relied on either social media discourse or search behavior, limiting the ability to directly link forecast accuracy with real‐time, experience‐based public communication.

Unstructured texts in social media provide an unprecedented opportunity to monitor and detect changes in public perceptions and emotional responses during an extreme weather event via natural language processing (NLP; Mi & Zhan, 2023) that can examine hidden patterns of risk communication from texts (Chu et al., 2024). Previous studies have applied NLP to classify and extract valuable information from Twitter/X as a social sensor of public opinion and real‐time events (Imran et al., 2014; Sakaki et al., 2010). More recently, NLP has been used to monitor emotional changes and public opinion in response to environmental issues (A. Liu et al., 2023) and extreme weather events over time (Tounsi & Temimi, 2023). These studies have provided insights into how authorities and policymakers tailor communication strategies, address public concerns promptly, and enhance the efficiency of risk communication in a timely manner. Different types of forecast errors (e.g., overestimation and underestimation) expectedly shaping public emotional responses remain largely unexplored.

Forecasts and relevant discourse data have been limited available, which is challenging to tackle the dynamics of public response during natural hazards. To mitigate their catastrophic impacts, government authorities and private companies have made significant efforts. For example, the Korean Meteorological Administration provides multiple lead‐hour precipitation forecasts, and NAVER, a South Korean web‐search engine company, operates the Report Talk platform that allows the users to post and share relevant information (texts, photos, and videos) to the ongoing natural hazard. The Korean Peninsula has experienced catastrophic damage and fatalities from landfalling TCs. In 2023, the landfall of Hurricane Khanun brought a record‐breaking August rainfall event over the eastern Korean Peninsula since 1968. According to the Ministry of the Interior and Safety Disaster Annual Report of the Republic of Korea (https://www.mois.go.kr/frt/a01/frtMain.do), Typhoon Khanun caused the temporary evacuation of over 15,000 residents and the destructions of 361 buildings and other facilities including road and residential flooding, soil loss, and bridge collapses. The nationwide property damage was about 56 billion KRW (43 million USD) during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun (Organization, 2023). While the economic damage from Typhoon Khanun has been reported, the socio‐psychological impacts of forecast errors on public mental health remain unknown.

This study extends the existing literature by explicitly investigating how the quality of information (e.g., forecast error types) is associated with distinct patterns of emotional divergence in real‐time public discourses through a case study of Typhoon Khanun. Here we aim to address the following three key questions: How do public communicate the risk online over the landfall of Typhoon Khanun? How does the accuracy of forecasts change over the study period? How does public emotion alter depending on the type of forecast error? To answer these questions, we collect over 43,000 discourses via the NAVER Report Talk platform (real‐time), and daily online search activity volumes from Google Trends and NAVER DataLab, and Wikipedia Pageview data. We retrieve hourly observational precipitation data from 613 weather stations and hourly precipitation forecast across South Korea. We evaluate rainfall forecast accuracy at one‐to‐six lead hours from categorical forecast performance metrics, such as the false alarm ratio (FAR) and hit rate, and assess public emotional changes through an NLP‐based sentiment analysis of texts in the online discourses. The findings of this study will unveil the hidden patterns of social response to time‐varying accuracy of weather forecasts during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun.

Data and Methodology

Data

Precipitation and Typhoon Track Data

Here we use hourly precipitation data from the Automated Synoptic Observing System (ASOS; https://data.kma.go.kr/data/grnd/selectAsosRltmList.do?pgmNo=36) and the Automated Weather System (AWS; https://data.kma.go.kr/data/grnd/selectAwsRltmList.do?pgmNo=56) to analyze observed precipitation patterns during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. The AOS and AWS systems provide hourly precipitation data from about 100 to 510 meteorological stations over the Republic of Korea, respectively. For spatial resolution consistency with the NAVER Report Talk data, we compute the district‐level averages of the hourly rainfall data among the meteorological stations for 250 districts. The temporal coverage of the observational precipitation data spans from 15:00 local time on 9 August 2023 through 09:00 local time on 11 August 2023. We compute district‐level hourly precipitation forecast data at one to six lead hours during the same period from the hourly forecast precipitation data from the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA; https://data.kma.go.kr/cmmn/main.do).

In this study, we use the hourly typhoon path data provided by KMA to monitor the temporal evolution of the typhoon's proximity to Seoul City Hall (37.5665°N, 126.9780°E). Seoul is the capital of South Korea with around 9.5 million people (over 25 million people including neighbor districts, Gyeonggi and Incheon) In the typhoon path data, we use the geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) of the typhoon center to calculate the hourly distances between the typhoon center and Seoul City Hall (37.5665°N, 126.9780°E).

Social Tracing Data: NAVER Report Talk, Google Trends, and Wikipedia

We collect 43,603 discourses posted on NAVER Report Talk (https://weather.naver.com/air/talk) over the study period (9,582, 32,759, and 1,262 posts on 9, 10, and 11 August 2023, respectively). In this context, the NAVER Report Talk platform functions where users upload texts, photos, and videos to validate ongoing weather conditions and their impacts. The NAVER Report Talk platform is embedded within one of the most widely used online ecosystems in South Korea, where users of diverse backgrounds—including general citizens, commuters, and residents—actively share real‐time observations, photos, and experiences. This participatory structure provides an unique opportunity to communicate relevant hazard risk to the ongoing extreme weather event. It is worth noting that discourse activity persists until the termination of the disaster, reflecting sustained public engagement throughout the event lifecycle.

We collect online search activity volume data from NAVER DataLab and Google Trends during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun, and the Wikipedia daily pageview data related to the typhoon event to assess the consistency of public behavior patterns of information seeking and sharing during the study period. The NAVER DataLab platform provides the relative daily search activity volume of NAVER users to the maximum daily volume over the search period (https://datalab.naver.com/keyword/trendSearch.naver). This platform provides daily search activity volume data depending on the user information including machine type (mobile/PC), sex (male/female), and age (≥12 years old, 13–18, 19–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, ≥60). This data has been used as a proxy indication of public interest in the ongoing disaster (Park et al., 2022).

The Google Trends platform provides the relative search activity volume of users based on the proportion of search queries for a given keyword within a specified region and time period (https://trends.google.com/trends/). The search activity is normalized on a scale from 0 to 100. 100 is the maximum search interest during the chosen search period. Although Google Trends does not provide absolute search counts, it enables the examination of temporal patterns in public attention. This data set has been widely used as a proxy for public attention and situational awareness during a natural disaster (S. Choi et al., 2025; Kam et al., 2021).

The Wikipedia webpage provides daily pageview statistics that represent the number of times specific articles are accessed by users (https://pageviews.wmcloud.org). Daily page views are available, which offers a different measure of public information‐seeking behavior from Google Trend. In this study, daily pageviews of typhoon‐related articles (https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/태풍 and https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/카눈) are collected to capture event‐specific public attention. It is worth noting that Wikipedia pageviews reflect active information consumption rather than query intent activities from Google Trends, providing a complementary perspective on public engagement. Prior studies have utilized Wikipedia pageviews as an indicator of collective attention during environmental and hazard events (Ahmad & Kam, 2024).

Methodology

Forecast Accuracy Metrics for Categorical Precipitation Forecasts

We use categorical forecast performance metrics such as the false alarm ratio, hit rate, critical success index (CSI) and bias score to evaluate the accuracy of weather forecasts during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. From the two‐by‐two contingency table, the false alarm ratio (FAR) is calculated using Equation 1. The two‐by‐two contingency table consists of four cases: True Positive (TP), True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), and False Negative (FN). A high FAR value indicates the ratio of the hours with no rain but forecasted rainfall to all hours with forecasted rainfall, that is, the percentage of the Type I error case of weather forecasts. (1) FAR=FPTP+FP, where TP is the number of forecasted rainfall situations with the occurrence of rainfall (true positive) and FP represents the number of situations where rain was forecasted but did not occur (false positive).

We compute the hit rate (H) from Equation 2. An H value indicates that the percentage of the success case to forecast rainfall (TP cases) over all hours with actual (nonzero) rainfalls (TP + FN cases). (2) H=TPTP+FN where FN is the no rain forecast with the occurrence of rainfall (false negative).

We compute the CSI and bias score (Equations 3 and 4). The CSI is the ratio of the success cases to forecast rainfall (TP cases) over all hours with actual or forecasted rainfall (TP + FP + FN cases). The bias score is the ratio of all hours with rainfall forecasts (TP + FP) to all hours with rainfall observation (TP + FN), which indicate the percentage of forecasted and observed rainfall durations, regardless of timing of success of forecasted precipitation. (3) CSI=TPTP+FP+FN (4) Bias=TP+FPTP+FN

Sentimental Analysis

We conduct a sentimental analysis of discourses from the NAVER Report Talk platform through the Korean comments Efficiently Learning an Encoder that Classifies Token Replacements Accurately (KcELECTRA) model trained by the Korean Online That‐gul Emotions (KOTE) Data set. The KOTE data set contains 50,000 Korean online comments labeled with 44 emotions (25 negative, 14 positive, and 5 neutral emotion types) under a new classification system (Jeon et al., 2024). The data set is constructed from 12 different online platforms, including news outlets, online communities, social media, e‐commerce, video platforms, movie reviews, microblogs, and forums. The model is developed by fine‐tuning the KcELECTRA (Korean comment ELECTRA) pretrained language model with the KOTE data set. For classification, the KcELECTRA model uses a multi‐label architecture in which a single input text can be associated with multiple emotion labels. The final layer applies a sigmoid activation for each of the 44 output nodes, producing a score (between 0 and 1) for each emotion label, indicating the model's confidence in the presence of that emotion.

The KcELECTRA model with the KOTE data set have shown the precision (true positive cases/(true positive cases + false positive cases from a two by two contingency table)) and recall (true positive/(true positive + false negative)) scores of 0.61 and 0.75, respectively, on average of the about 40,000 sample, indicating that the KcELECTRA model is reliable for sentimental analysis (Jeon et al., 2024). In this study, the dominant emotion of each discourse from the NAVER Report Talk platform is determined based on the highest score among the scores of 45 emotion types.

Bootstrapped Emotion Analysis by Forecast Error Type

We compare discourse samples from the regions with reliable precipitation forecasts (control group) with those with the limited prediction skill (an exposure group) to assess changes in emotional responses associated with forecast errors during the study period. Differences in emotional expression between these groups can be interpreted as being associated with discrepancies between forecasted and observed conditions.

We test the significance of emotional changes from the exposal group, comparing it with changes in the bootstrapping samples of the control group. Negative and positive emotions are analyzed separately. Let e denote each emotion category, and let We=xe,i,i=1…NeW, represent emotion scores in the control group. Similarly, Ue and Oe denote the underestimate and overestimate groups from the exposure group, with sample sizes NeU and NeO, respectively. For each emotion e, we compute the sample averages of each group, denoted as x‾eU (or x‾eO), and generated a bootstrap reference distribution from the control group. For the underestimate exposure group, we repeatedly sample with replacement bootstrap samples We∗(b) of size NeU from the control group 10,000 times (b=1,2,3,…,10,000 in Equation 5). For each bootstrap sampling, we calculate the average of x‾W∗(b) and define the difference as in Equation 5. (5) ΔeU(b)=x‾eU−x‾W∗(b)

From the bootstrapped sample, we construct an empirical distribution of the differences to keep the sample size consistent and estimate the 95th percentile range of the empirical distribution from the control group. The same procedure was applied to the overestimate exposure group. We detect significant change of the exposal group when the average of the emotion scores of the exposure group is beyond the 95th percentile range of the emotion scores of the control group.

Results

Spatiotemporal Variations of Risk Communication and Forecast Accuracy

Patterns of information seeking and sharing show different dynamic patterns during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun (Figure 1). NAVER discourses peaks on 10 August 2022 when the center of Typhoon Khanun is closest to Seoul. Online search activities from Google Trends and Wikipedia Pageviews peak on 9 August 2022. This temporal alignment demonstrates that public reporting behavior intensifies in response to the immediate physical threat and public seeking behavior surges for proactive mitigation and preparation. Such a behavior pattern supports that NAVER Report Talk is a real‐time indicator of situational awareness during the most critical phase of the event and Google Trends/Wikipedia Pageview provides valuable information to prepare for an emerging event (Figure 1). The observed and forecasted precipitation data exhibit different spatial distributions during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. Maximum rainfall rates exceeded 30 mm/hr along the eastern region (red boundaries in Figure 2b) where FAR is 0.4 regardless of the lead hour. In the southeastern region, including Gyeonggi‐do and Daejeon, FAR in the one lead hour forecasts exceeded that in the six lead hour forecasts. For example, Daejeon shows FAR of 0.47 (one lead hour) and 0.31 (six lead hours).

Forecasts overestimated rainfall duration by 4 hr on average during the landfall. After the landfall, hit rates dropped by up to 50% from six to one lead hour, reaching a minimum near 0.1 (Figure 2a and Figure S1a in Supporting Information S1). CSI in the six lead hours was 0.1–0.2, about 20%–30% lower than that in one lead hour (Figure 3e). The bias in the one lead hour forecasts was ∼1.0 (0.8–1.2) after the landfall, while the six lead hour forecasts was less than 0.2. For rainfall duration, the lead hour of forecasts is a critical factor to generate reliable information (Figure S1b in Supporting Information S1).

The one lead hour forecasts show both overestimation (false positive error) and underestimation (false negative error) from −8 to +40 mm/hr across the Peninsula (Figure 2a). One hour lead forecasts range from 15 mm/hr on average and up to 71 mm/hr (maximum) in the eastern and southern regions. The observational data show the regional maximum precipitation averaging 19 mm/hr (peaking at 88 mm/hr) (Figure 2a). The one lead hour forecasts underestimate intense local precipitation along the southern region and the eastern coastal regions, including Gangwon‐do and Gyeongsang‐do (Figures 1c and 2a).

The six lead hour forecasts overestimate precipitation by more than 10 mm/hr over 30% of the Peninsula, particularly the northern and western regions. The results show a general tendency toward overestimation over the districts where actual precipitation was low (Figure 2a). The six lead hour forecasts underestimate intense local precipitation along the eastern coastal region, and overestimate in the metropolitan areas including Seoul and Gyeonggi‐do, indicating a larger west‐east contrast than that from the one lead hour forecast (Figures 2c and 2d).

The eastern and southern regions experience rainfall events lasting over 20 hr (up to 39 hr) during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun (Figure 2e). Both one and six lead hour forecasts demonstrate long durations in the northwestern part of Korean peninsula, including Incheon, Seoul, Chungcheong‐do, by up to ±15 hr. In the one lead hour forecasts, 30% of the districts show overestimation of rainfall duration, up to 10 hr. Six lead hour forecasts overestimate the duration hours of precipitation up to 5 hr over 24% of the districts, particularly pronounced in the northwestern region, including Incheon and Gyeonggi‐do (Figures 2f and 2g).

Spatiotemporal Variation of Public Participants and Expressed Emotions

The NAVER Report Talk platform demonstrate that over 1,000 discourses are recorded spatially across the 11 districts of South Korea on average (Figure 4a). The northwestern districts of Seoul show the highest number of discourses (6,188 out of 43,603 discourses) during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. 12,000 and 3,500 discourses are posted in the southeastern and northeastern districts of the Korean Peninsula. Overall, 55% of the total discourses are negative. The dominant emotion types of the negative discourses show anxiety/worry (41%) and embarrassment/confusion (23%) (Figure 4b). The results reflect regional variations in public concern and reporting activity regarding the typhoon.

District‐level maps of total discourses and dominant emotion types during Typhoon Khanun. Spatial maps of the total number of discourses (a), dominant emotion types (b), percentages of negative discourses (c). Pie chart depict composition of five dominant and other emotion types (d).

The eastern districts show a significant volume of discourses from the districts with high rainfall intensities of over four mm/hour on average. Residents in metropolitan regions posted significant discourses, complaining that forecasted and actual rainfall durations were different even with weak intensity of observed precipitation. This result indicates that spatial distribution of discourses aligns well with the spatial distribution of not only the population but also the accuracy of precipitation forecasts. 72% of the districts of the Korean Peninsula show anxiety/worry as the dominant emotion type during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun, followed by embarrassment and confusion (6% of the Korean districts) (Figure 4b). Some districts in Gangwon‐do (Hwacheon), Gyeongsangnam‐do (Hadong), and Chungcheongnam‐do (Gongju) show that more than 70% of the discourses are negative (Figure 4c).

Dominant emotion types in the online discourses are negative but vary during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun (Figure 5a). When Typhoon Khanun approaches the Korean Peninsula (A in Figure 5), the dominant emotion types in the online discourses are anxiety and worry, which accounts for approximately 30% of the emotion types along the regions with overestimated forecasts, indicating a typical response from catastrophizing that often amplifies the level of stress and worry (Dodds, 2021). As the event progressed toward landfall (B in Figure 5), the proportion of anxiety‐related discourse gradually decreased as individuals began to reconcile forecasts with observed conditions; however, anxiety and worry remained a substantial component of the overall discourse, indicating the persistent influence of initial forecast expectations on public sentiment (West et al., 2020). Concurrently, when forecasts underestimated intense precipitation (Figures 2a and 5a), discourses characterized by embarrassment and confusion increased, suggesting a shift in emotional responses from anticipatory anxiety to cognitive dissonance during the realization of forecast inaccuracies. These results suggest that emotional changes are associated with the quality of precipitation forecasts before, during, and after the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. Positive emotion types of discourses are relief and reassurance over the regions with the records of moderate precipitation. The positive discourses express the gratitude that they had not yet experienced significant flooding or damage, often sharing messages such as “Luckily, we haven't been affected yet.” (Figure 5b).

Districts with moderate precipitation and localized damage show XX discourses with the positive emotion types (Figure 5c). In the central region of the Korean Peninsula, discourses embed gratitude for stable conditions with safety reminder messages (e.g., “Stay safe from the typhoon!”), indicating a high level of situational awareness with precautionary behavior patterns (Figure 5c). Discourses with a positive emotion type, reassurance, increase over time, becoming the third‐largest category (11%) until the termination period of Typhoon Khanun (C in Figure 5).

The public shows a shift in dominant emotion types in response to discrepancies between forecasted and observed precipitation (Figure 5). In the northern region of the Korean peninsula, the public exhibit relief in the discourses when the Typhoon Khanun is gone (e.g., “I'm so relieved that the typhoon is over”), indicating a transition stage toward post‐event recovery (Figure 5d).

Associations of Changes in Public Sentiment With Forecast Accuracy

We compare the emotion scores with a different emotion type given under‐ and overestimation in the forecasts with those with those with reliable forecasts (Figure 6). Over the regions with overestimation in precipitation forecasts, emotion scores for joy and Caring increase by 2% and 16%, respectively, while scores for gratitude and trust/relief, decrease by 14% and 11%, respectively. The regions with underestimation in precipitation forecasts show that emotion scores for joy, happiness, and caring, decrease by 16%, 19%, and 7%, respectively, whereas scores for expectation and interest/curiosity increase by 7% and 13%, respectively. The results indicate underestimation in rainfall forecasts heightens vigilance and curiosity at the expense of joy and comfort.

Associations of emotional score changes with forecast accuracy. Black filled circle and red cross in (a) and (b) depict the mean differences of the scores of the corresponding emotion type when forecasts are underestimated and overestimated, respectively. Error bars in (a and b) depict 95th percentile ranges of the bootstrapped samples.

The emotion scores of anxiety/worry increase regardless of the error type. Both overestimation and underestimation in precipitation forecasts increase the emotion scores of anxiety/worry by 5%. Underestimation in forecasts intensify adverse emotional responses of the public: Discourses with the dominant emotion types, sadness and embarrassment/confusion, increase by 9%, and 6%, respectively. This result indicates underestimation in the forecasts drives a negative effect such that the public confront a discrepancy between forecasted and observed precipitation that directly heightens personal and social discomfort. In contrast, overestimation in precipitation forecasts causes a mixed pattern of changes in public emotion. The forecasts with overestimation, the emotion scores of exhaustion and sympathy increase by 9.5% and 3.8%, respectively, and the emotion scores of disgust and anger decrease by 7%. Taken together, this mixed pattern might align with psychological adaptation to false alarms—repeated overstatement fosters fatigue and modest empathetic concern rather than acute hostility, shifting responses toward passive or resigned negativity. Discussion

This study highlights the utility of a two‐way (online) risk communication tool in tracing public sentiment and communication behavior in real time during extreme weather events. Online platforms, such as the NAVER Report Talk, provide essential data for improving crisis communication and management strategies deepening our understanding of public reactions. While the platform may preferentially attract users with higher interest in weather conditions, its temporal dynamics and reporting behavior closely align with broader public attention patterns observed from independent data sources such as Google Trends and Wikipedia pageviews. This suggests that NAVER discourse reflects not only weather‐aware users but also general societal responses to real‐time hazard impacts. Nevertheless, potential sampling bias from NAVER Report Talk's users should be acknowledged, and future studies should further validate the representativeness of such platforms using additional independent data sources.

This study reveals that online information seeking patterns are different dynamic patterns during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. We find that the information seeking volume is highest before the landfall on 9 August 2022 when Typhoon Khanun approaches the Korean Peninsula, and the relevant online discourse volume is highest during the landfall on 10 August 2022 when Typhoon Khanun penetrated the Korean Peninsula. The comparison result suggests that the purpose of risk communication determines the speed of public response to an emerging disaster. Integrating these data with weather forecast information can provide a comprehensive view of the dynamics of the public's emotional and behavioral responses during the emergence of extreme weather events. This study highlights the importance of a balanced knowledge of weather forecasting skill and public risk communication pattern on disaster prevention and preparedness of our society.

This study is a case study of the landfalling of Typhoon Khanun over the Korean Peninsula to evaluate the prediction skill of precipitation from Typhoon Khanun and its association with sentimental changes of the public over time. This study provides an opportunity to investigate associations of forecast accuracy with expectation formation and risk perception (Lazo et al., 2009). This study focuses on precipitation as the primary variable for the following reasons. First, in the Korean Peninsula, the majority of typhoon‐induced damages are associated with precipitation‐driven hazards such as pluvial flooding, river overflow, and landslides, rather than storm surge or wind alone (J.‐W. Choi et al., 2016; Kim & Jung, 2021). Second, hourly precipitation forecasts for precipitation are available, which constitutes the most widely accessed and communicated weather information to the public, making them a dominant driver of expectation formation and risk perception in risk communications (Lazo et al., 2009). This single variable approach can miss the multi‐hazard nature of tropical cyclones. Landfalling tropical cyclones bring not only intense rainfall but also strong wind speed and high storm surge that can influence public perception during the landfall of tropical cyclones, which should be addressed in future research, particularly for coastal communities that are vulnerable to both pluvial and fluvial floods.

This study finds that the accuracy of weather forecasts of both the intensity and duration of rainfall significantly influence public engagement on online risk communication about Typhoon Khanun. NAVER Report Talk demonstrates that discourses are posted by residents in the southeastern regions where intense precipitation persists during the landfall. The predominant emotion types in narratives are worry and anxiety. Inaccuracies of weather forecasts intensified these emotions and further transformed into emotion types, uncertainty and fear. Another common negative emotion type is embarrassment/confusion, particularly during periods of substantial rainfall but with underestimation forecasted rainfall, indicating that underestimations can lead to confusion and a sense of unpreparedness. On national average, the dominant emotion types of discourses are frustration and complaint (distrust and fatigue) during the hours with underestimation (overestimation) of forecasted precipitation. The findings of this study indicate that emotional expressions are associated with perceived discrepancies between expected and observed conditions and forecast error type alter public sentiment differently.

This study investigates how emotional responses change in alignment with variations in forecast accuracy since the influence of long‐term or chronic factors is likely constant during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. Our analysis focuses on the temporal co‐variations between forecast error and emotional expression at an hourly scale, capturing short‐term, event‐specific dynamics under an emerging hazard condition (Houben et al., 2015). It is worth noting that the observed emotional patterns should not be interpreted as being driven only by forecast inaccuracies. To understand actual causal attribution of changes of public emotion to forecast error type, future research is necessary incorporating longitudinal or experimental approaches that are carefully designed to disentangle the relative contributions of forecast error to other factors, such as general disaster‐related stress and daily routine.

The real‐time Naver Report Talk data hints how diurnal patterns of posing discourses interact with the daily routine (Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1). Inaccurate precipitation forecasts are associated with an increased proportion of negative discourses, particularly the emotion type of anxiety/worry before and during commuting hours (6:00 to 9:00 local time) compared with non‐commuting hours. These patterns suggest that the association between forecast accuracy and public sentiment may vary across different temporal scales, especially during periods of routine activity. However, these results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, as multiple interacting factors may influence public emotional responses during extreme weather events, for example, social events such as marathons (Fujiyama et al., 2016). It should be noted that daily routine is only one of several potential factors, and other factors such as information exposure, prior experience, and local vulnerability may also contribute to the observed temporal patterns. While this result hints a diurnal association of emotional changes with daily routines, future research incorporating independent approaches, such as human behavioral surveys or mobility data analysis, is needed to advance the current understanding of the underlying mechanisms of sociopsychological changes during an emerging disaster.

Conclusions

This study succeeds in assessing associations of public emotion changes with forecast error type during the landfall of Typhoon Khanun. Results confirm that a long lead hour of forecast degrades the prediction skill of precipitation from Typhoon Khanun with spatial variations of prediction skill across the study region. Over the western and northern regions of the Korean peninsula, forecasts overestimate actual (moderate) precipitation. Over the eastern and southeastern regions, intense precipitation persists during the landfall, but the current forecasts demonstrated limited prediction skill. Based on the findings of this study, underestimated rainfall forecasts are a potential direct stressor that intensifies specific emotion types such as fear, sadness, and confusion. Overestimated rainfall forecasts are a potential indirect stressor by having the public feel over‐prepared, which possibly alter the dominant emotion types into fatigue and sympathy. The results affirm that the social consequences of forecast inaccuracy differ depending on the forecast error type, suggesting the sociopsychological impacts of expectation–reality mismatch on public health. This study emphasizes the value of forecast accuracy as not only a technical metric but also a critical factor shaping public perception and emotional response during extreme weather events. Incorporating information about forecast uncertainty into risk communication therefore improves public trust and reduces emotional distress during future disasters.
