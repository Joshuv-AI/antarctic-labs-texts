AutoXAI: a meta-learning approach for recommendation of explanation techniques
Authors: Radwa El Shawi, Leila Jamel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The absence of a universally optimal global explanation technique for machine learning models presents a significant challenge in ensuring interpretability across diverse tasks and domains. Existing methods vary in their strengths and limitations, and selecting the most suitable technique often requires manual trial-and-error, which is inefficient and prone to bias. This study introduces AutoXAI, a meta-learning framework designed to automate the recommendation of global explanation techniques for supervised learning tasks on tabular data. The framework aims to optimize interpretability by aligning recommendations with user-defined quantitative metrics. AutoXAI leverages optimal transport tos identify datasets with similar underlying distributions and applies multi-objective optimization to select explanation methods that best satisfy the chosen metrics. The framework currently supports four widely adopted model-agnostic techniques: LIME, Anchor, RuleFit, and RuleMatrix. AutoXAI was evaluated across 21 benchmark datasets, where its recommendations matched the best-performing explanation techniques in 19 cases. Comparative analysis against static heuristics demonstrated superior performance. Robustness experiments showed that AutoXAI maintained consistent recommendations under noise perturbations in over 90% of scenarios, confirming its reliability. AutoXAI offers a scalable, data-driven solution for selecting explanation techniques, reducing manual effort and enhancing trust in model decisions. Its adaptability to user preferences and resilience to noise make it a promising tool for real-world deployment, including critical domains such as healthcare and intrusion detection systems.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a swift evolution and widespread adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML)1,2. The expansion of these models in terms of size and complexity has been driven by the pursuit of enhanced predictive performance. Nevertheless, the emphasis on accuracy as the sole metric is facing scrutiny. This is due to the emergence of large black-box models characterized by opaque decision-making processes, hindering users from evaluating, comprehending, and possibly rectifying the system. The need for interpretable and explainable AI (XAI) arises with the objective of enhancing the understandability of AI systems and their outcomes for humans3. The advent of deep learning over the last decade has heightened interest in devising techniques to elucidate and explain black-box models.

Conventional perspectives on interpretability often frame it as a binary property, overlooking its detailed nature with multiple dimensions4. Emphasizing a quantitative approach, we advocate for a multi-dimensional perspective that meticulously evaluates specific interpretability properties. The integration of a set of quantitative metrics, ideally automated and encompassing various interpretability facets, holds the potential to significantly empower researchers and practitioners across different dimensions. Firstly, it allows for the thorough evaluation and validation of the interpretability of individual explanation methods and their outputs. Secondly, it facilitates an objective and fair comparison between different explanation methods, offering insights into their respective strengths and weaknesses. Moreover, the metrics enable the recommendation of interpretability techniques based on user-specified metrics, presenting a diverse array of options to cater to specific preferences. Lastly, the integration of these interpretability metrics as an optimization criterion during model training serves to enable a fine-tuned balance between accuracy and interpretability.

Related work

The domain of explainable AI methods has arisen more recently within academic circles in comparison to the broader expanse of AI literature. This emergence has been prompted by the widespread integration of AI models across diverse sectors in contemporary society, signifying its growing importance1,5–10. XAI tools are differentially categorized into local or global methods, each designed to explain the decision-making processes of AI models across varying levels. The local methods specifically target the comprehension of an AI algorithm’s behavior at a more granular, low-level hierarchical tier1,11–13. The global set of XAI approaches, in contrast, are directed towards comprehending the behavior of AI algorithms at a higher hierarchical level14. These approaches assist users in gauging how features contribute to predictions across entire datasets or collections of datasets. Another way to categorize explanation techniques is based on whether the technique adopted to explain can work only on a specific black-box model (model-specific) or can be adopted on any black-box (model agnostic). Model-agnostic techniques are applicable to any model, offering valuable insights into the factors influencing their decisions15. These tools operate post-hoc, meaning they are applied after the model has been trained. Importantly, they do not require access to the model’s internal details; rather, they only necessitate the capability to test the model predictions16.

Another branch of research focuses on evaluating explanation methods both quantitatively and qualitatively, assessing aspects like their fidelity, stability, and overall utility3,6,17–21. Quantitative evaluation emphasizes metrics such as fidelity22 and stability23, which measure the ability of explainers to mimic model behavior and provide consistent outputs. Despite various metrics, no consensus exists on the best set of measures due to the inherent complexity and subjectivity in evaluations. Wilson et al.24 propose three key metrics: completeness, correctness, and compactness, to gauge explanation quality. Qualitative assessments, on the other hand, focus on usability, safety, trust, and social acceptance, often involving criteria proposed by Doshi et al.25–27. These criteria include functionally-grounded, application-grounded, and human-grounded evaluation methods, which range from formal definitions to domain-specific tasks and user-centered assessments, respectively.

Despite the proliferation of explanation methods and evaluation metrics, notable gaps persist in the literature. Firstly, most studies focus on either local or global explanations, often neglecting systematic guidance for selecting the most suitable method for a specific dataset or task12,14,23. Additionally, quantitative and qualitative evaluation metrics are frequently applied independently, lacking an integrated framework that simultaneously considers multiple dimensions of interpretability17,18,21. These limitations highlight the need for automated, data-driven frameworks capable of recommending robust and reliable global explanation methods, motivating the development of the proposed AutoXAI framework.

Recent studies in applied domains such as intrusion detection systems (IDS) have emphasized the importance of interpretability, robustness, and real-time performance. For instance, Zouhri et al.28,29 proposed hybrid approaches combining CTGAN and ENN, as well as dimensionality reduction techniques, to enhance the interpretability and responsiveness of IDS pipelines. These methods are typically tailored to specific model architectures and operational constraints. In contrast, AutoXAI provides a model-agnostic, metric-driven recommendation framework that can complement such domain-specific solutions by selecting explanation techniques aligned with user-defined interpretability goals. This flexibility enables AutoXAI to support IDS applications where explanation quality must meet stringent requirements for clarity, speed, and trustworthiness.

In our work, we use optimal transport to learn a mapping over joint feature and label spaces.

Since computing exact optimal transport distances can be computationally intensive for large collections of datasets, AutoXAI integrates several optimizations. First, class-conditional distributions are modeled as Gaussians, which allows the Bures-Wasserstein distance to be computed in closed form, avoiding costly linear programming solvers33,34. Second, when empirical distributions are compared, we employ entropic regularization (Sinkhorn approximation), which provides scalable solutions with near-linear complexity while maintaining accuracy35,36. Finally, all computed similarities are cached for reuse, avoiding redundant computation for previously seen datasets. Together, these strategies ensure that AutoXAI can efficiently compute similarities even as the knowledge base grows.

Multi-objective optimization

Traditionally, addressing multi-objective problems involves leveraging Pareto Theory methods, with the objective of identifying the Pareto front-namely, the set of non-dominated vectors for a given problem. This set delineates vectors where improvement in one dimension can only be achieved at the expense of a decrease in performance in another. The Pareto front proves instrumental in revealing the intricate relationship between objectives, leading to the identification of optimal solutions. The cornerstone concept in Pareto Theory is dominance, which establishes how two instances are compared to ascertain one’s superiority over the other. Mathematically, given two vectors in the objective space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}^{(1)} \in \mathbb {R}^m$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}^{(2)} \in \mathbb {R}^m$$\end{document}, the point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}^{(1)}$$\end{document} Pareto dominates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}^{(2)}$$\end{document} if and only if: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\forall i \in \{1, \ldots , m\} : \textbf{y}^{(1)}_i \le \textbf{y}^{(2)}_i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exists j \in \{1, \ldots , m\} : \textbf{y}^{(1)}_j < \textbf{y}^{(2)}_j$$\end{document}. In simpler terms, this criterion ensures that the first vector is superior in each objective or at least in one37.

Identity: Aims to ensure that explanations for identical instances are also identical38.

Separability: Aims to ensure that explanations for dissimilar instances differ, assuming that all features used in the model being explained are relevant to prediction and the model does not have degree of freedom38.

Speed: Measures the efficiency of the explainability framework by quantifying the average time required for generating an explanation6.

Correctness: Gauges the truthfulness of the explanation concerning how well it aligns with the black-box model’s workings39. Ideally, an excellent explanation strives for utmost truthfulness. It is imperative to clarify that this characteristic pertains to the descriptive accuracy40 of the explanation, independent of the predictive accuracy of the black-box model being explained.

Compactness: Measures the size of the explanation, guided by human cognitive constraints41. To enhance comprehension, explanations should prioritize sparsity, brevity, and non-redundancy, steering clear of overly extensive presentations. In this work, compactness assessment for rule-based explainability techniques considers the length of generated rules, while for feature attribution techniques, it is quantified by the count of features with non-zero attributions. The compactness scores are expressed as percentages, reflecting the proportion of concise elements in the overall explanation. Higher compactness percentages signify more efficient and succinct explanations.

Reference global explainability frameworks

In the following, we present an overview of the reference global explanation techniques supported by AutoXAI.

LIME: The Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)12 is a local model-agnostic explainer that generates feature importance scores to explain an instance x. LIME assigns weights to randomly generated samples based on their proximity to the instance x, prioritizing nearby examples. Utilizing the proximity measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _{x}$$\end{document} to capture the local context, LIME perturbs x by selecting non-zero elements uniformly. The perturbed instances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z\in \mathbb {R}}$$\end{document} are employed to train the explanation model g(.), typically a sparse linear model, by inputting them into the black-box model b and obtaining predictions b(z). The resulting local explanation encompasses the weights assigned to the coefficients of the linear model. Moreover, LIME supports global explanations through the Submodular Pick module (SP-LIME), which selects representative instances and their explanations, ensuring non-redundancy and global representativeness.

ANCHOR: Anchor is a model-agnostic technique that provides rule-based explanations called anchors43. Anchors are applicable when alterations in other feature values of an instance have no impact on the outcome. For a given instance x and anchor r, if r(x) equals the prediction b(x) from the black-box model b, it constitutes an anchor. The methodology involves perturbing instance x to generate synthetic instances and subsequently extracting anchors with precision surpassing a user-defined threshold. Anchor employs a multi-armed bandit algorithm for synthetic data generation and employs a bottom-up approach with beam search for anchor identification.

RuleFit: RuleFit, a framework for global explainability geared towards constructing rule ensembles44, undergoes two principal phases: rule extraction and weight optimization. In the extraction phase, RuleFit trains a tree ensemble model on a sample S and breaks down each tree into a collection of rules, forming a set R. In the weight optimization phase, RuleFit optimizes a weight vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in \mathbb {R}^{M}$$\end{document} for the rules in R using sample S. To uphold sparsity and ensure interpretability, RuleFit incorporates L1-regularization (Lasso penalty). Navigating through these phases, RuleFit constructs a rule ensemble model that captures data complexity while providing a globally interpretable representation through rules and their associated weights.

RuleMatrix: RuleMatrix represents a model-agnostic explanatory approach to provide both local and global insights while emphasizing the visualization of extracted rules45. This technique involves four key steps. AutoXAI

Given a new dataset and a set of metrics for measuring the quality of the explanation, AutoXAI selects an optimal explainability technique from a collection of previously evaluated techniques. The AutoXAI framework comprises two key phases: a meta-training phase, wherein it identifies the optimal explanation algorithm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E^{*}$$\end{document} for each dataset in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\text {meta}} = \{\mathscr {D}_1,...,\mathscr {D}_n\}$$\end{document}, and a subsequent meta-testing phase that recommends the optimal explanation method for new, unseen tasks. The architecture of AutoXAI is illustrated in Fig. 1.

In the offline phase, also recognized as the meta-training phase, we systematically process each dataset within our knowledge base, totaling 100 real and synthetic datasets sourced from the OpenML repository47. For each dataset, our procedure unfolds in the following steps. First, we leverage AutoSklearn48, an automated machine learning tool, to determine the optimal machine learning model optimized for accuracy. Next, we explain the resulting model using various global explanation techniques. Subsequently, we evaluate the explanations derived from distinct frameworks, based on multiple quantitative metrics specified in Sect. 4. Finally, we store, for each metric, the best-performing explanation method.

In the online phase, also referred to as the meta-testing phase, we begin by identifying the most similar dataset in our knowledge base to the given new dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\text {new}}$$\end{document}. Specifically, we calculate similarity scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_i = d_{\text {OT-N}}(\mathscr {D}_{\text {new}}, \mathscr {D}_i)$$\end{document} for each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_i \in \mathscr {D}_{\text {meta}}$$\end{document}. The most similar dataset is then obtained as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma = \arg \min _{i} \, d_{\text {OT-N}}(\mathscr {D}_{\text {new}}, \mathscr {D}_i),$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} denotes the dataset in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\text {meta}}$$\end{document} most similar to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\text {new}}$$\end{document}.

Subsequently, a multi-objective optimization framework directs the selection of the optimal explanation method(s) according to user-specified metrics. For a new dataset, the metric values of all explanation methods for the most similar dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} are retrieved. Each metric is treated as an objective, and explanation techniques represent solutions within a multi-dimensional space defined by these metrics. The application of non-domination sorting categorizes solutions into non-dominated fronts, highlighting optimal trade-offs between conflicting metrics. Pareto fronts, representing non-dominated solutions, provide a comprehensive view of feasible solutions. The selection process involves considering these Pareto fronts and choosing explanation techniques that align with specific preferences or demonstrate a desirable compromise between evaluated metrics.

Experimental setup

Datasets: The datasets utilized in this study are widely acknowledged for assessing the effectiveness of explainability techniques. We utilized 21 datasets with their original train/test split, as outlined by Feurer et al.49, and it is important to note that these datasets are not included in our knowledge base.

Tasks and Models: Our focus is on classification tasks, and we have adopted a data-driven approach to ensure the selection of high-performing models for explanation. For each of the evaluated datasets, we employed AutoSKlearn50 to choose machine learning models solely based on their performance with the provided data, ensuring a fair and unbiased platform for assessing explanation techniques. For each dataset, we proceed with the following steps: (i) employ AutoSKlearn to identify the optimal pipeline based on the training data while optimizing for accuracy, and (ii) explain the black-box model using each of the supported global explanation methods based on the training data. Subsequently, we assess the quality of the explanation using the metrics outlined in Sect. 4. Note that AutoSKlearn automatically applies standard preprocessing steps as part of its pipeline, including handling missing values, encoding categorical features, and feature scaling where necessary. No additional manual preprocessing was performed on the datasets.

Reference global explainability framework: AutoXAI supports the following global explanation techniques: LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)12, Anchors43, RuleFit44, and RuleMatrix45.

Hardware resources: Our experiments were conducted in a CPU environment running on Ubuntu 22.04 LTS, equipped with a 8-core Intel i7 Processor @ 2.80GHz and 16 GB of RAM.

Limitations and threats to validity

Despite the promising results, several limitations and potential threats to validity should be acknowledged. First, internal validity may be affected by the fixed pool of explanation methods and evaluation metrics; adding or excluding methods could influence the Pareto-optimal sets and AutoXAI’s recommendations. Second, external validity is limited by the evaluation datasets; while 21 diverse benchmarks were used, generalization to other data modalities (e.g., text, images) or ML tasks beyond supervised classification is not guaranteed. Third, construct validity may be threatened because explanation quality is inherently multidimensional, and the chosen metrics may not fully capture human interpretability or domain-specific trustworthiness. Finally, conclusion validity could be affected by randomness in AutoML pipelines and noise injection, although repeating experiments and aggregating across seeds mitigates this risk. Other limitations include the focus on four widely adopted global explanation methods, leaving out other families of techniques. Evaluation metrics provide a structured means of assessing explanation quality, but cannot fully capture subjective human-centered notions of interpretability. Additionally, since AutoXAI relies on AutoSKlearn for model selection, it inherits the design biases and preprocessing strategies of the underlying AutoML framework. Finally, computational scalability for very large datasets is an open challenge that warrants further study. Another potential limitation is how “unimportant” features were identified in the noise robustness evaluation. Features were labeled unimportant based on consensus across explanation methods, which helps avoid perturbing features that might carry explanatory value but may also introduce bias or circularity.

Conclusion and future work

This paper introduces AutoXAI, a robust framework designed to recommend model-agnostic global explanations for supervised tasks on tabular data. By leveraging a meta-learning framework, AutoXAI consistently tailors explanation techniques to specific tasks and demonstrates resilience to noise, underscoring its reliability across diverse datasets. The contributions of this work include introducing a systematic approach for automating explanation method selection and establishing a robust evaluation protocol validated on 21 datasets. From a practical perspective, AutoXAI provides clear advantages for practitioners: it reduces trial-and-error in selecting explanation methods, delivers recommendations aligned with multiple quality metrics, and ensures robust performance even in the presence of noisy or less relevant features. This facilitates faster deployment of interpretable models in real-world settings and enhances trust in model decisions. Future research directions include: (1) extending AutoXAI to incorporate local explanations and additional families of methods (e.g., counterfactuals, concept-based), (2) generalizing the framework to new data modalities such as images, text, and multimodal settings, (3) advancing criteria for explanation evaluation, including automated inference of metrics from system constraints such as computational budgets or explanation size limits, and (4) exploring model-driven alternatives (e.g., permutation importance or feature relevance scores) to define feature irrelevance independently of explanation techniques, and (5) adapting AutoXAI for deployment in critical domains such as intrusion detection systems (IDS), where interpretability must be balanced with robustness and real-time constraints. AutoXAI’s ability to optimize explanation selection based on metrics such as compactness, correctness, and speed positions it as a valuable tool for enhancing transparency in IDS workflows. Future work will explore integrating AutoXAI into IDS pipelines, evaluating its performance under streaming data conditions, and extending its capabilities to support local explanations and concept-based reasoning for anomaly detection and threat classification.
