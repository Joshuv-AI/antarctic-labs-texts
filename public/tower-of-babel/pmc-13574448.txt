Ferroptosis-related SOCS1 as a candidate host-response biomarker in tuberculosis
Authors: Kun Xu, Yingying Li, Jian Wu, Shaoshao Mo, Ming He, Tuodi Li, Ning Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1) has been implicated in immune regulation and has been identified as a ferroptosis-related candidate gene in tuberculosis (TB) transcriptomic studies. However, its expression characteristics in independent clinical samples and its relationship with disease activity-related indicators remain insufficiently defined. This study integrated public transcriptomic datasets with clinical validation to evaluate SOCS1 expression in TB, assess its potential adjunctive diagnostic value, and explore its clinical associations. Public datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus were used to identify and validate ferroptosis-related candidate genes associated with TB. SOCS1 expression was evaluated across independent datasets, including whole-blood datasets and an in vitro Mycobacterium tuberculosis-stimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cell model. Peripheral blood SOCS1 mRNA expression was measured by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction in 36 patients with active pulmonary TB and 21 healthy controls. SOCS1 protein expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry in an independent lung tissue cohort. Plasma malondialdehyde and total glutathione levels were measured as oxidative stress-related biochemical indicators using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Receiver operating characteristic curve and Spearman correlation analyses were used to assess discriminatory performance and clinical associations. SOCS1 was consistently upregulated across independent TB-related datasets and was significantly elevated in peripheral blood from patients with TB compared with healthy controls. In the clinical cohort, SOCS1 showed modest discriminatory performance for distinguishing TB patients from healthy controls, with an area under the curve of 0.731 (95% confidence interval = 0.599–0.864). Immunohistochemistry showed stronger SOCS1 protein expression in TB tissues than in control tissues, whereas pneumonia tissues showed a nonsignificant increasing trend. SOCS1 expression was positively correlated with C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and sputum culture grade. Additional biochemical analyses showed higher malondialdehyde and lower total glutathione levels in TB patients, with corresponding correlations with SOCS1 expression. SOCS1 may serve as a ferroptosis-related candidate host-response biomarker with potential adjunctive diagnostic value in TB. Its expression may reflect inflammatory burden, microbiological burden, and oxidative imbalance-related host responses in the context of TB, although further validation and mechanistic studies are required.

1. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), remains a major global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization’s Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, the global TB burden remains high, and disease control remains challenging.[1] Despite advances in microbiological testing, molecular diagnostics, and imaging, early recognition of TB remains difficult, microbiological confirmation is not always achievable, and disease activity is often insufficiently assessed in routine practice. Host-response biomarkers that are stable and clinically accessible may therefore provide useful support for TB diagnosis and disease activity assessment. Peripheral blood biomarkers are particularly attractive for translational studies because they are readily accessible and can be repeatedly measured.[2,3]

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has recently gained attention as a potential contributor to TB pathogenesis.[4,5] Experimental evidence suggests that MTB infection promotes lipid peroxidation, impairs glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-mediated protection, and induces iron-dependent injury, which may contribute to tissue necrosis, bacterial dissemination, and immunopathology.[6–9] These findings provide a biological rationale for exploring ferroptosis-related molecules as candidate biomarkers in TB.

Suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1), a key member of the SOCS family, is a well-established negative regulator of cytokine signaling and plays an important role in immune homeostasis and inflammation.[10–13] Bioinformatics-based studies have repeatedly identified SOCS1 as an upregulated ferroptosis-related candidate gene in TB patients or MTB-related models.[14–16] SOCS1 can also be induced during MTB infection and may regulate anti-TB immunity by modulating interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-related signaling and the activation of macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells.[17–19] Nevertheless, evidence on SOCS1 in TB mainly comes from public database analyses or in vitro studies, and its expression in independent clinical samples, especially the combined evaluation of peripheral blood mRNA, tissue protein levels, and clinical correlations, remains relatively limited.

Therefore, this study identified SOCS1 from ferroptosis-related transcriptomic screening and validated its expression in TB using independent clinical samples at both the peripheral blood mRNA and tissue protein levels. We further assessed its potential adjunctive diagnostic value and explored its associations with inflammatory markers, microbiological burden, and oxidative stress-related indicators, aiming to provide evidence for host-response biomarker research in TB.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Public dataset collection and bioinformatics analysis

The GSE83456, GSE28623, GSE19491, GSE39940, and GSE174566 datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and their characteristics are summarized in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. Datasets were eligible if they contained active TB patients and healthy controls, other disease controls, or MTB-stimulated and unstimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) controls; included more than 10 samples per comparison group; and provided genome-wide mRNA expression profiles from whole blood or PBMC samples. A total of 964 ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) were retrieved from FerrDb (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb).

Each dataset was processed separately in R version 4.5.3. Probe identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols, duplicated probes were averaged, and matrices were log2-transformed and quantile-normalized when required. In GSE83456, TB-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted P < .05. These genes were intersected with FerrDb-derived genes to define ferroptosis-related DEGs. Protein–protein interaction analysis was performed using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (https://string-db.org/), and hub genes were ranked in Cytoscape using cytoHubba. Candidate gene expression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) performance were assessed in GSE28623, GSE19491, GSE39940, and GSE174566.

2.2. Study population and clinical sample collection

This observational validation study included 2 independent clinical sample sets: a peripheral blood cohort used for reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and biochemical analyses, and a separate formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung tissue cohort used for immunohistochemical validation.

The peripheral blood cohort comprised 36 patients with active pulmonary TB and 21 healthy controls enrolled at the People’s Hospital of Qianxinan Prefecture, Guizhou Province, China, from February 2024 to February 2026. Baseline and TB-specific clinical characteristics of the peripheral blood cohort are summarized in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 2. Patients were included if they were aged ≥18 years and had microbiological, molecular, or clinical evidence of active pulmonary TB and were treatment-naïve or had received anti-TB therapy for <7 days before enrollment. Healthy controls were adults undergoing routine health examinations, with no known history of TB or TB exposure, no infection-related symptoms, normal chest imaging, and normal routine examination results. Participants with other infections, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, severe organ dysfunction, human immunodeficiency virus infection or other immunodeficiencies, pregnancy or lactation, recent glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy, incomplete clinical data, or inadequate sample quality were excluded from the peripheral blood cohort. During patient selection, co-infection with other pathogens was considered based on available medical records and routine microbiological testing. Patients with clinically documented co-infection at the time of sample collection were excluded when such information was available.

The independent tissue cohort comprised formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung specimens from patients with pulmonary TB, patients with pneumonia, and histologically normal-appearing adjacent non-tumor lung tissues from patients with lung adenocarcinoma, with 6 specimens in each group. The tissue specimens were obtained from the archives of the Department of Pathology at the same hospital. Diagnoses were established on the basis of histopathological findings and relevant clinical or microbiological information.

No formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was based on the availability of eligible peripheral blood samples and archived lung tissue specimens during the study period. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Qianxinan Prefecture (approval number: 2024-3-S-93), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.3. Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from whole blood using TRIzol reagent (Sangon Biotech). RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry, and cDNA was synthesized using Maxima Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific). RT-qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system (Thermo Scientific) using SGExcel FastSYBR Mixture (Sangon Biotech). Reverse transcription was performed at 25°C for 10 minutes, 50°C for 30 minutes, and 85°C for 5 minutes. PCR cycling consisted of 95°C for 3 minutes, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 45 seconds, with melting curve analysis for specificity. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 3.

SOCS1 expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with one healthy control sample used as the calibrator. For correlation analyses, −ΔCt was used, with higher −ΔCt values indicating higher relative expression. An anonymized summary of SOCS1 Ct, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase Ct, −ΔCt, and relative expression values is provided in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4.

2.4. Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue specimens from the independent tissue cohort described above were used for immunohistochemical analysis. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide and goat serum, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-SOCS1 primary antibody (1:200; Bioss). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000; Bioss), 3,3′-diaminobenzidine staining and hematoxylin counterstaining were performed.

SOCS1 staining was quantified using ImageJ. For each sample, 3 representative nonoverlapping high-power fields with a 50 μm scale bar were analyzed under identical imaging conditions. Fields with folding, edge artifacts, hemorrhage, or extensive necrotic debris without evaluable cellular structures were excluded. Sample-level SOCS1 mean optical density (MOD) was calculated as the summed 3,3′-diaminobenzidine-positive integrated optical density across the 3 fields divided by the summed analyzed tissue area across these fields. Quantification was performed blinded to clinical grouping, and detailed sample-level values are provided in Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5.

2.5. Clinical variables

Routine clinical laboratory variables were collected at enrollment, including white blood cell, neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), lymphocyte percentage (LYM%), platelet count (PLT), and hemoglobin (HGB). Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count, and systemic immune-inflammation index as the PLT multiplied by the absolute neutrophil count and divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and sputum culture grade were available only for patients with TB and were therefore included only in within-TB correlation analyses. Positive sputum cultures were graded semiquantitatively according to the proportion of the solid-medium slant covered by colony growth: 1+, one-quarter; 2+, one-half; 3+, three-quarters; and 4+, the entire slant. Grades were coded ordinally from 1 to 4, with higher grades indicating a greater semiquantitative mycobacterial burden. These clinical variables were obtained from routine tests performed at enrollment.

2.6. Measurement of oxidative stress-related indicators

Plasma malondialdehyde (MDA) and total glutathione (tGSH) levels were measured using a Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (S0131S) and a Total Glutathione Assay Kit (S0052), respectively (Beyotime Biotechnology), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MDA was quantified using the thiobarbituric acid colorimetric method at 532 nm. For tGSH measurement, 50 μL of plasma was mixed with an equal volume of protein removal reagent, incubated at 4°C for 10 minutes, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes. tGSH was subsequently measured using the glutathione reductase–5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) recycling method at 412 nm. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves and expressed in μM.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), GraphPad Prism (version 5.0; GraphPad Software), and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp.). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Overall differences in immunohistochemical measurements among the 3 tissue groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate discriminatory performance, and area under the curves (AUCs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Spearman rank correlation was used to assess associations between SOCS1 expression and clinical or biochemical variables. All tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. SOCS1 expression in public TB datasets

GSE83456 was used as the discovery cohort to identify ferroptosis-related candidate genes associated with TB. Using the predefined criteria, 231 DEGs were identified between patients with pulmonary TB and healthy controls, including 201 upregulated and 30 downregulated genes. By intersecting these TB-associated DEGs with FerrDb-derived FRGs, 10 ferroptosis-related DEGs were obtained. Protein–protein interaction network analysis and maximal clique centrality-based hub gene ranking identified SOCS1 as one of the core candidate genes (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 6). The expression patterns and discriminatory performance of other candidate genes are shown in Figures S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content 7.

The expression pattern of SOCS1 was further evaluated across independent public datasets and an in vitro MTB-stimulated PBMC model. SOCS1 was consistently upregulated in peripheral blood from patients with TB and in MTB-stimulated PBMCs compared with the corresponding controls (Fig. 1A–D). ROC analyses showed that SOCS1 had measurable discriminatory ability within each dataset-specific comparison, and the AUC values with 95% CIs are summarized in Figure 1E. Because GSE39940 included other disease controls and GSE174566 represented an in vitro MTB-stimulated PBMC model, these ROC analyses reflect discrimination between TB and other disease states or between MTB-stimulated and unstimulated conditions, rather than diagnostic performance against healthy controls alone. Considering its high maximal clique centrality ranking, consistent upregulation across independent datasets, and previous biological evidence supporting its role in host immune regulation and potential involvement in ferroptosis-related processes, SOCS1 was prioritized for subsequent clinical validation.

3.2. Clinical characteristics and peripheral blood SOCS1 expression

The clinical cohort included 36 patients with TB and 21 healthy controls. Baseline and TB-specific clinical characteristics are summarized in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 2. Age and sex distribution did not differ significantly between groups. Compared with healthy controls, TB patients had higher NEUT% and PLT levels and lower LYM% and HGB levels. CRP, ESR, and sputum culture grade were available only for the TB group and were not compared between groups. Peripheral blood SOCS1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in TB patients than in healthy controls (Fig. 2A), consistent with the public dataset findings. ROC analysis showed modest discrimination between TB patients and healthy controls, with an AUC of 0.731 (95% CI = 0.599–0.864; Fig. 2B).

3.3. SOCS1 protein expression in TB tissue specimens

Immunohistochemical staining showed that SOCS1 protein was mainly localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus. Compared with control tissues, TB tissues showed stronger SOCS1 staining, while pneumonia tissues also showed a tendency toward increased staining intensity (Fig. 3A–C). ImageJ quantification demonstrated significantly higher SOCS1 MOD values in TB tissues than in control tissues. Pneumonia tissues showed a trend toward increased MOD values; however, no significant differences were observed when compared with either control or TB tissues (Fig. 3D).

3.4. Associations between SOCS1 expression and clinical variables

Among TB patients, SOCS1 expression was associated with inflammatory and microbiological indicators (Fig. 4A). SOCS1 was positively correlated with CRP (r = 0.358, P = .03) and ESR (r = 0.428, P = .009; Fig. 4B and C), suggesting an association with systemic inflammatory burden. SOCS1 expression was also positively correlated with sputum culture grade (r = 0.579, P < .001; Fig. 4D), indicating a possible relationship with microbiological burden. SOCS1 showed a positive but nonsignificant trend with NEUT% (Fig. 4E). No significant associations were found between SOCS1 and white blood cell, LYM%, PLT, HGB, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, or systemic immune-inflammation index.

Analyses of oxidative stress-related indicators showed that plasma MDA levels were higher in patients with TB than in healthy controls, whereas plasma tGSH levels were lower (both P < .001; Fig. S4A and B, Supplemental Digital Content 8; Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 9). Among patients with TB, MDA levels were positively correlated with SOCS1 expression (r = 0.473, P = .004), whereas tGSH levels were negatively correlated with SOCS1 expression (r = −0.337, P = .04; Fig. S4C and D, Supplemental Digital Content 8; Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 9).

4. Discussion

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and has increasingly been linked to host cell injury and pathological processes in TB.[6–9,20,21] Previous evidence indicates that MTB infection can trigger ferroptosis in host cells, while ferroptosis inhibition may reduce infection-associated damage.[6] This provides a biological rationale for exploring FRGs as candidate biomarkers in TB. SOCS1 is a well-established negative regulator of cytokine signaling and plays an important role in immune homeostasis and inflammation.[10,11,13] Several ferroptosis-related transcriptomic studies have identified SOCS1 as a consistently upregulated candidate gene in TB.[14–16] In addition, evidence from non-TB models suggests that SOCS1 may participate in ferroptosis-related regulatory processes and modulate cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis through pathways involving solute carrier family 7 member 11, glutathione metabolism, and GPX4.[22–24] These findings support the consideration of SOCS1 as a ferroptosis-related candidate gene in TB and provide a rationale for its further validation. However, most existing evidence is based on transcriptomic screening or mechanistic inference, and validation in clinical samples remains limited. By integrating public datasets with independent clinical samples, this study evaluated SOCS1 expression and its potential adjunctive diagnostic value and examined its associations with inflammatory markers, microbiological burden, and indicators of oxidative imbalance, providing further evidence for SOCS1 as a candidate host-response biomarker in TB.

Our results showed that SOCS1 was consistently upregulated across public TB datasets, significantly elevated in peripheral blood from TB patients, and more strongly expressed at the protein level in TB tissues than in control tissues. These findings indicate that SOCS1 dysregulation is not confined to local lesions but is also detectable systemically in TB. The consistent expression pattern across cohorts, platforms, study settings, and clinical samples supports the reproducibility of SOCS1 upregulation in active TB and warrants further evaluation in clinical biomarker studies. ROC analysis indicated modest discrimination between patients with TB and healthy controls, suggesting that SOCS1 may have potential adjunctive diagnostic value. However, because the peripheral blood cohort did not include clinically relevant disease controls, such as patients with pneumonia or other infectious or inflammatory lung diseases, the ability of SOCS1 to distinguish TB from these conditions remains uncertain. Larger independent cohorts incorporating such controls are needed to further evaluate its potential adjunctive diagnostic value.

CRP and ESR are commonly used nonspecific markers of systemic inflammation and disease activity in active pulmonary TB.[25] In this study, SOCS1 expression was positively correlated with CRP and ESR, suggesting that SOCS1 upregulation is associated with systemic inflammatory burden in patients with TB. This interpretation is further supported by the tissue immunohistochemical findings. SOCS1 protein expression was stronger in TB tissues than in control tissues, while pneumonia tissues also showed an increasing trend, indicating that local SOCS1 expression may be influenced by the inflammatory microenvironment. Thus, SOCS1 should be regarded more appropriately as a candidate biomarker reflecting host response and inflammatory activity in TB, rather than as a TB-specific tissue diagnostic marker. Although TB tissues showed a higher trend of SOCS1 expression than pneumonia tissues, this difference did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the limited number of tissue samples and the overlapping inflammatory nature of pneumonia. Larger tissue cohorts including nontuberculous inflammatory lung diseases are therefore needed to determine whether SOCS1 expression differs quantitatively between TB and other pulmonary inflammatory lesions.

Although SOCS1 is a negative regulator, its positive association with inflammatory markers is not necessarily contradictory. A plausible explanation is that persistent pathogen stimulation and inflammation in active TB induce SOCS1 expression, and increased SOCS1 may act as an adaptive or dysregulated feedback mechanism that preferentially suppresses protective antimicrobial immunity, particularly interleukin-12- and IFN-γ-related signaling pathways that are essential for controlling bacterial burden,[26] while pathological inflammation may persist alongside ongoing bacterial load, chronic antigenic stimulation, and tissue-destructive immune responses. This interpretation is consistent with functional studies showing that MTB infection induces SOCS1 in macrophages, whereas SOCS1 inhibition can restore a proinflammatory immune milieu and reduce intracellular MTB activity.[17,27] In addition, SOCS1-targeted recombinant Bacillus Calmette-Guérin has been shown to enhance dendritic cell and T-cell activation and improve protection against TB in mouse models, further supporting SOCS1 as an important negative regulator of anti-TB immunity.[18,28]

Beyond inflammatory markers, we also observed a significant positive correlation between SOCS1 expression and sputum culture grade. Unlike nonspecific inflammatory markers such as CRP and ESR, sputum culture grade more directly reflects viable mycobacterial burden, with higher grades generally indicating greater pathogen load and stronger ongoing antigenic stimulation. Therefore, the association between SOCS1 and sputum culture grade suggests that SOCS1 upregulation may reflect not only systemic inflammation but also persistent MTB stimulation and higher bacterial burden in active TB. This finding is consistent with Masood et al, who reported increased SOCS1 transcript levels in patients with more severe pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB.[29,30] Taken together, our findings provide clinical microbiological support for the possibility that SOCS1 dysregulation participates in an immune imbalance characterized by persistent pathogen stimulation, enhanced negative immune regulation, and impaired protective antimicrobial immunity. However, sputum culture grade is still a semiquantitative measure, and our sample size was limited; the relationship between SOCS1 and bacterial burden therefore requires validation in larger cohorts using more precise microbiological quantification.

Biochemical analyses showed higher MDA and lower tGSH levels in patients with TB, with MDA and tGSH showing positive and negative associations with SOCS1 expression, respectively. These findings suggest that SOCS1 upregulation co-occurs with systemic oxidative imbalance. However, MDA and tGSH are oxidative stress-related biochemical indicators rather than ferroptosis-specific markers, and they may be influenced by infection-related inflammation, nutritional status, antioxidant capacity, and other metabolic factors.[31,32] Therefore, these findings support a potential association between SOCS1 and oxidative imbalance but do not demonstrate that SOCS1 directly regulates ferroptosis in TB. Further mechanistic studies incorporating GPX4, solute carrier family 7 member 11, lipid reactive oxygen species, and iron metabolism indicators are required to clarify this relationship during MTB infection.

Based on these findings and previous evidence regarding SOCS1-mediated regulation of cytokine signaling,[10,11,13] we added a schematic model to summarize the proposed biological context of SOCS1 in TB (Fig. 5). In this model, MTB infection may activate host immune and inflammatory responses through PRR/TLR signaling and IFN-γ/cytokine-related JAK/STAT signaling, which may subsequently induce SOCS1 gene upregulation. Increased SOCS1 may act as a negative feedback regulator of JAK/STAT signaling. In the present study, SOCS1 upregulation was clinically associated with inflammatory and microbiological burden, as reflected by increased CRP, ESR, and sputum culture grade, and with oxidative imbalance, as reflected by increased MDA and decreased tGSH. This schematic is intended to provide a proposed biological framework rather than direct mechanistic proof, and the causal relationships require further experimental validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical sample size was relatively small, and the peripheral blood cohort lacked disease controls such as pneumonia as well as individuals with latent TB infection; therefore, the diagnostic performance of SOCS1 and the observed clinical correlations should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger disease-control cohorts. Second, the immunohistochemical analysis included only 6 samples per group, and the control tissues were relatively normal adjacent lung tissues from lung adenocarcinoma cases, which may not fully represent healthy lung tissue. Although pneumonia tissues were included as inflammatory disease controls, the specificity of SOCS1 tissue expression for TB could not be fully determined. Third, although clinically documented co-infection with other pathogens was considered during patient selection, comprehensive screening for all potential coinfecting pathogens was not performed in all participants; therefore, the potential influence of undetected or subclinical co-infection on SOCS1 expression cannot be completely excluded. Finally, this was a cross-sectional observational study, and mechanistic validation was not performed. The MDA and tGSH analyses were indirect, and therefore cannot establish causal links between SOCS1 expression and ferroptosis-related cell death.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated SOCS1 upregulation in TB across public datasets, peripheral blood samples, and lung tissue specimens, and suggested its association with inflammatory burden, microbiological burden, and oxidative imbalance. Although the adjunctive diagnostic value and biological role of SOCS1 require confirmation in larger multicenter cohorts and functional studies, our findings provide a basis for future host-response biomarker research and suggest a potential avenue for adjunctive diagnosis and disease activity assessment in TB.
