Abstract
We consider cylindrical algebraic decomposition (CAD) and the key concept of delineability which underpins CAD theory. We introduce the novel concept of projective delineability which is easier to guarantee computationally. We prove results about this which can allow reduced CAD computations.



I
Introduction

Cylindrical algebraic decomposition (CAD)
is a well-known tool for computing with polynomial constraints over the reals, developed in the 1970s by Collins
[
5
]
as the basis for quantifier elimination (QE) over real-closed fields. CAD has been continuously researched, improved, and applied in the half century since its inception.

Recently, CAD theory has been adapted and specialized for
satisfiability modulo theories
(SMT) solvers which check the satisfiability of Boolean combinations of real polynomial constraints: e.g. the
NuCAD

[
4
]
, the
NLSAT

[
9
]
, and the
CAlC

[
1
]
algorithms.
The corresponding
QF_NRA
category of the SMT-LIB benchmarks contains instances of such problems originating from e.g. verification systems, the life sciences and economics.

Central to CAD is the notion of
delineability
of a polynomial over a connected set (
cell
). Roughly speaking, an
n
-variate polynomial
P
is delineable over a cell
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
if the set of zeros of
P
in the cylinder
S\times\mathbb{R}
is the disjoint union of the graphs of continuous real-valued functions of
n-1
variables (we call these
real root functions
). These graphs cut the cylinder
S\times\mathbb{R}
into cells which the sign of
P
is invariant within. We order these cells bottom-to-top to form a
stack
. The key observation is that the number of cells, their ordering, and the sign of
P
within cells, are all the same no matter which point of
s\in S
we are over. Thus the presence of delineability allows for construction at a sample point to be concluded valid over the cell.

We are particularly interested by the
single cell construction

[
13
]
, which is used as a subroutine by the three SMT algorithms mentioned earlier
[
4
,
9
]
,
[
1
]
. This uses CAD projection theory to compute a single CAD cell around a given sample point within which a set of polynomials is sign-invariant. Compared to traditional CAD algorithms, the single cell construction allows us to use the sample point to detect which projection polynomials are not relevant for the cell under construction, and omit them in the computation.

In this paper we introduce the new notion of
projective delineability
: easier to guarantee computationally than delineability but still useful. Our new notion allows us to further omit leading coefficients during single cell construction in cases where projective delineability of a polynomial is sufficient instead of its delineability. Indeed, projective delineability is equivalent to delineability in the case where the leading coefficient of
P
never vanishes, but may still be guaranteed when the leading coefficient has zeroes (see Proposition
3
for a more precise statement).

II
Delineability and Leading Coefficients

A CAD is a decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consisting of cells which maintain properties for the input; usually that a finite family of input polynomials
\mathcal{F}
in
n
variables are sign-invariant within each cell. These cells are
cylindrically arranged
, meaning that the projection of a CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and over each cell
S
of the latter, the cylinder
S\times\mathbb{R}
is decomposed by cells of the former.
A CAD may be computed recursively: for obtaining the CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we compute a
projection

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
in
n-1
variables of
\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
defines a suitable CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The following notion formalizes the property that the CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
needs to fulfil if we are to extend it to a CAD of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 1

(
[
5
,
10
]
)
.

We say that

is

on a subset

\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
if there exist
k\in\mathbb{N}
and some continuous functions
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}:S\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

the set of zeros of
P
in
S\times\mathbb{R}
is the disjoint union
\text{Graph}(\theta_{1})\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\text{Graph}(\theta_{k})
;

2.

for all
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\},
there exists
m_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for all
\textbf{x}\in S
, the multiplicity of the root
\theta_{l}(\textbf{x})
is
m_{l}
.

We say that
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
are the
real root functions
of
P
over
S
.
The graph of
\theta_{l}
is called a real

section
over

The computation of the projection
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has been an active area of research. Our work is based on

[

with the optimization by Brown

:

contains the discriminant and leading coefficient of each

and the resultant of each pair

The new notion of projective delineability is designed for the

]
discussed above.
Here the cell is already constructed using a partial CAD projection (given the sample point we identify that some projection polynomials for the full CAD are not needed for the region of interest). Prior work reduced the amount of resultants and some discriminants. Projective delineability allows detecting irrelevant leading coefficients also.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

The projective line

The points of the
real projective line
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
are the lines of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
passing through
(0,0)
. Such a line is determined by any of its nonzero vectors
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
, and so
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
is equivalently defined as the quotient set of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
by the equivalence relation given by proportionality. We denote the quotient map by
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
and the equivalence class of
(x,y)
by
\pi(x,y)
or
(x:y)
.

The topology of
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
is the usual quotient topology:
U\subset\mathbb{RP}^{1}
is open if
\pi^{-1}(U)
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
. The map
\psi(x:y)=\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}(x,y)
is a homeomorphism from
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
to a circle.

The real line
\mathbb{R}
is usually identified with an open and dense subset of
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
by first identifying
\mathbb{R}
to a (parameterized) line of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
and then composing with the projection:

\iota:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\mapsto(x,1),\quad\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}:x\mapsto(x:1).

(1)

The only point in
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
that is not in the range of the continuous injective map
\varphi
is the point
(1:0)
. For this reason it is sometimes denoted by
\infty
.
The inverse map

\varphi^{-1}:\mathbb{RP}^{1}\setminus\{\infty\}\to\mathbb{R}\ :(x:y)\mapsto\frac{x}{y}

is also continuous.
The choice to identify
\mathbb{R}
with a given line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(not containing
(0,0)
) is arbitrary: any other choice of parameterized line reads
\iota_{A}(\mathbb{R})
where
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in\{1,2\}}
is a nonsingular matrix (
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
) and

\iota_{A}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\to A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Setting
\varphi_{A}=\pi\circ\iota_{A}
, the point at infinity (with respect to
A
) is the point that is not in the range of
\varphi_{A}
, and thus corresponds to the first column of
A
. The maps
(\varphi_{A})^{-1}
, for
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{R}),
endow
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
with the structure of an analytic manifold.
Note that we have
\varphi_{A}=\mu_{A}\circ\varphi
, where for any
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, the map
\mu_{A}
is defined by

\mu_{A}:\mathbb{RP}^{1}\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}:(x:y)\mapsto\pi\left(A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\right).

III-B

Binary forms, homogenizations and pull-backs

Later we deal with roots of polynomials at
\infty
. The framework of binary forms allows us to deal with these roots at infinity on an equal footing with the real roots.

Definition 2
.

A
binary form
of degree
d
is a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
expressed as

g(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}x^{k}y^{d-k},\quad c_{0},\ldots,c_{d}\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

The set of binary forms of degree
d
is denoted by
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}
.

In view of
Definition

2

\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}
is clearly isomorphic to the vector space
\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}
of univariate polynomial functions of degree less than or equal to
d
. An isomorphism is given by the pull-back and its inverse, the homogenization with respect to degree
d
.

Definition 3
.

The
pull back of binary forms
of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
is the map

\iota^{*d}:\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}\to\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}:g\mapsto g\circ\iota
.
The
homogenization
with respect to degree
d
is
H^{d}=(\iota^{*d})^{-1}
.

Remark 1
.

1.

If
g
is a binary form of degree
d
given by (
2
), then
\iota^{*d}g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}x^{k}
. It has degree less than or equal to
p<d
if and only if
c_{d}=\cdots=c_{p+1}=0
.

2.

We have
H^{d}(P)(x,y)=y^{d}P(\frac{x}{y})
for every
P\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
y\neq 0
.

3.

By definition, pull-back and homogenization with respect to a same fixed degree are the inverse of each other. This fails to be true if they are not performed with respect to the same fixed degree. For instance, if
g(x,y)=x^{2}y+xy^{2}+y^{3}
, then
\iota^{*3}g(x)=x^{2}+x+1
, and we have
H^{2}(\iota^{*3}g)(x,y)\neq g(x,y)
.

As we continue it will be useful to consider the pull-back and homogenization with respect to other lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Definition 4
.

For every
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, the pull-back with respect to
A
is
\iota_{A}^{*d}:\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}\to\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}:g\mapsto g\circ\iota_{A}
and the corresponding homogenization is
H_{A}^{d}=(\iota_{A}^{*d})^{-1}
.
Finally, we set
A^{*d}:\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}\to\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leqslant d}:P\mapsto\iota_{A}^{*d}(H^{d}(P))
.

Remark 2
.

If
P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}x^{k}
, we compute

A^{*d}P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}(a_{11}x+a_{12})^{k}(a_{21}x+a_{22})^{d-k}

for
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
. When
a_{21}x+a_{22}\neq 0
, this expression also reads
(a_{21}x+a_{22})^{d}P\left(\frac{a_{11}x+a_{12}}{a_{21}x+a_{22}}\right)
.

The next lemma gathers useful formulas to deal efficiently with these operators.
We denote by
R_{A}
the right composition with
A
(seen as an operator from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), namely
R_{A}:\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{d}:g\mapsto g\circ A.

Lemma 1
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
A,B\in GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, we have
\iota_{A}^{*d}\circ H^{d}_{B}=\iota^{*d}\circ R_{B^{-1}A}\circ H^{d}
. In particular, we have
\iota_{A}^{*d}=\iota^{*d}\circ R_{A}
, and
H^{d}_{A}=R_{A^{-1}}\circ H^{d}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the particular cases. The first
one follows from the very definition of
\iota_{A}^{*d}
, while the second is obtained by inversion.
∎

We will analyze the behaviour of the order of polynomials with respect to homogenization and composition with a linear transformation. Note that the second part of
Proposition

2
justifies the definition of order of a binary form at a point of the projective line.

Proposition 2
.

1.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
and
A\in GL(m,\mathbb{R})
. We have

\text{ord}(f\circ A,v)=\text{ord}(f,Av)
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

2.

If
g\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n},y]_{d}
, then we have
\text{ord}(g,(\textbf{x},(x_{n},y)))=\text{ord}(g,(\textbf{x},k(x_{n},y)))
for every
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},(x_{n},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
and
k\neq 0
.

3.

If
P\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n}]_{\leqslant d}
. We have
\text{ord}(P,(\textbf{x},x_{n}))=\text{ord}(H^{d}(P),(\textbf{x},(x_{n},1)))
for every
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The first assertion of
Proposition

2
is a particular case of a general theorem (
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
). We give an elementary proof of the particular case under consideration.

Proof.

Using the chain rule, a simple induction shows that for
\forall k\in\mathbb{N},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant m
,

\partial_{i_{1}}\ldots\partial_{i_{k}}(f\circ A)=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{m}A_{j_{1}i_{1}}\cdots A_{j_{k}i_{k}}(\partial_{j_{1}}\ldots\partial_{j_{k}}f)\circ A.

It follows from the definition of the order that
\text{ord}(f\circ A,v)\geqslant\text{ord}(f,Av)
, and the first result follows by symmetry. The second one follows by using
A=\mathrm{id}_{n-1}\times(k\mathrm{id}_{2})
and noticing that
g\circ A=k^{d}g
has the same order as
g
. Finally, denote by
k
the order of
P
at
(\textbf{x}_{0},u)
. Using the multi-index notation, we have
P(\textbf{x},x_{n})=\sum_{|\alpha|+l\geqslant k}^{d}D_{\alpha,l}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{0})^{\alpha}(x_{n}-u)^{l}
and there exists
(\alpha_{0},l_{0})
such that
|\alpha_{0}|+l_{0}=k
and
D_{\alpha_{0},l_{0}}\neq 0
. We compute

H^{d}(P)(\textbf{x},(x_{n},y)){=}\sum_{|\alpha|+l\geqslant k}^{d}D_{\alpha,l}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{0})^{\alpha}(x_{n}-uy)^{l}y^{d-l}.

It is easily seen that
(\partial^{\beta}_{\textbf{x}}\partial^{a}_{x_{n}}\partial^{b}_{y}H^{d}(P))
vanishes at
(\textbf{x}_{0},(u,1))
whenever
|\beta|+a+b<k
and that it is nonzero if
\beta=\alpha_{0}
,
a=l_{0}
and
b=0
.
∎

IV
Projective delineability

In this section, we define the new concept of projective delineability of a polynomial over a subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, which extends the classical notion of delineability
[
10
]
by allowing the root functions to reach infinity.

Definition 10
.

For
P\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n}]
of degree
d_{n}=\deg_{x_{n}}(P)
in
x_{n}
and
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we define

\displaystyle Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P,S)

\displaystyle=\{(\textbf{x},x_{n})\in S\times\mathbb{R}\;|\;P(\textbf{x},x_{n})=0\},

\displaystyle Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)

\displaystyle=\{(\textbf{x},(x_{n}:y))\in S\times\mathbb{RP}^{1}\;|H^{d_{n}}(P)(\textbf{x},(x_{n},y))=0\}.

We say that
x_{n}
is a
real root
of
P
above
\textbf{x}_{0}\in S
if
(\textbf{x}_{0},x_{n})\in Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P,S)
. Similarly, we say that
(x_{n}:y)
is a
projective root
of
P
above
\textbf{x}_{0}
if
(\textbf{x}_{0},(x_{n}:y))\in Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
.

As already mentioned, the projective roots of
P
above
\textbf{x}_{0}
consist of the real roots above
\textbf{x}_{0}
(seen in the projective space) and the root at infinity above
\textbf{x}_{0}
. This latter root appears precisely when
\deg(E_{\textbf{x}_{0}}P)<d_{n}
as explained in Lemma
2
and the subsequent discussion.

Definition 11
.

We say that
P\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n}]
is
projectively delineable
on a subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
if there exist
k\in\mathbb{N}
and some continuous functions
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}:S\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
such that

1.

Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)=\text{Graph}(\theta_{1})\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\text{Graph}(\theta_{k})
;

2.

for all
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\},
there exists
m_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for all
\textbf{x}\in S
, the multiplicity of the projective root
\theta_{l}(\textbf{x})
of
E_{\textbf{x}}P
(w.r.t.
\deg_{x_{n}}(P)
) is
m_{l}
.

We say that
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
are the
projective root functions
of
P
over
S
.
The graph of
\theta_{l}
is a
projective

P
-
section
over
S
.

Requiring analyticity for
S
and the root functions leads to the notion of analytic projective delineability, extending the notion of analytic delineability from
[
10
]
.

Example 2
.

Polynomial
P(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}x_{2}-1)(x_{2}+x_{1}^{3})
is projectively delineable on
\mathbb{R}
. Indeed,
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,\mathbb{R})
is exactly the disjoint union of the graphs of the continuous functions
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
defined by
\theta_{1}(x_{1})=(1:x_{1})
and
\theta_{2}(x_{1})=(-x_{1}^{3}:1)
(respectively depicted in green and red in
Figure

2
, top). Furthermore,
\theta_{1}(x_{1})
and
\theta_{2}(x_{1})
are both simple roots of
E_{x_{1}}(P)
for all
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In general, the delineability of
P
on
S
does not imply the projective delineability of
P
on
S
, nor in the other direction. E.g. consider the polynomials
P(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+1
and
Q(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}x_{2}-1
and
S=\mathbb{R}
. The relations between these two concepts are investigated in the following.

Proposition 3
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
P\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n}]
of degree
d_{n}
in
x_{n}
be defined by
P({\bf{x}},x_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{d_{n}}c_{k}(\textbf{x})x_{n}^{k}
.
If there exists
k\in\{0,\ldots,d_{n}\}
such that for all
\textbf{x}\in S
, we have

c_{d_{n}-k}(\textbf{x})\neq 0\;\land\;\forall j\in\mathbb{N}:0\leq j\leq k-1,c_{d_{n}-j}(\textbf{x})=0,

(6)

then
P
is projectively delineable on
S
if and only if
P
is delineable on
S
.

Remark 4
.

(
6
)
means that for all
\textbf{x}\in S
,
P
admits
\infty
as a projective root of multiplicity
k
(w.r.t.
d_{n}
) above
x
.

Proof.

If
(
6
)
holds and
P
is delineable on
S
, then
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P,S)
is the disjoint union of the graphs of the continuous functions
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{p}:S\to\mathbb{R}
, and therefore

Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)=\begin{cases}\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}\text{Graph}(\varphi\circ\eta_{i})&\text{if $k=0,$}\\
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}\text{Graph}(\varphi\circ\eta_{i})\sqcup\text{Graph}(\theta_{p+1})&\text{if $k\neq 0,$}\end{cases}

where
\theta_{p+1}
is constantly equal to
\infty
on
S
and
\varphi
is defined in (
1
). Hence
P
is projectively delineable since the condition on multiplicities is satisfied.

If
(
6
)
holds and if
P
is projectively delineable on
S
, then
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
is the disjoint union of the graphs of the continuous functions
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}:S\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
. If
k=0
, there is no root at infinity. We thus have

Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P,S)=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}\text{Graph}(\varphi^{-1}\circ\,\theta_{i})
. Hence
P
is delineable since the condition on multiplicities is satisfied by Lemma
2
. If
k>0
, we introduce new projective root functions
\theta^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\theta^{\prime}_{p}
of
P
on
S
such that one of them is constantly equal to
\infty
in the following way. Considering for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
the closed set
C_{i}=\theta_{i}^{-1}(\{\infty\})
, we have
S=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}C_{i}
. We pick
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
C_{i_{0}}\neq\emptyset
and define the new functions by
\theta^{\prime}_{j}|_{C_{i}}=\theta_{\sigma_{i}(j)}|_{C_{i}},

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
where
\sigma_{i}
is the transposition
(i\;i_{0})
if

and the identity if
i=i_{0}
. It follows from the pasting lemma that these functions are continuous. The definition ensures that
\theta^{\prime}_{i_{0}}
is constantly equal to
\infty
and
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{p}\text{Graph}(\theta^{\prime}_{i})
. We thus have
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P,S)=\bigsqcup_{i=1,i\neq i_{0}}^{p}\text{Graph}(\varphi^{-1}\circ\theta^{\prime}_{i}).

We show that for
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\setminus\{i_{0}\}
, the multiplicity of
\theta_{i}^{\prime}(\textbf{x})
as a projective root of
E_{\textbf{x}}P
(w.r.t.
d_{n}
) is equal to that of
\theta_{i}(\textbf{x})
and hence, does not depend on
\textbf{x}\in S
. If
C_{i}=\emptyset
, then
\theta_{i}^{\prime}=\theta_{i}
on
S
and the conclusion is direct.
Otherwise, the mutliplicity of
\theta_{i}(\textbf{x})
is
k
for every
\textbf{x}\in C_{i}
, hence for every
\textbf{x}\in S
. The multiplicity of
\theta_{i}^{\prime}(\textbf{x})
is that of
\theta_{i}(\textbf{x})
or that of
\theta_{i_{0}}(\textbf{x})
, and both are equal to
k
. By Lemma
2
,
P
is therefore delineable on
S
.
∎

If we only assume the sign-invariance of the leading coefficient of
P
on
S

P
on

P
on
S
. A counter example is given by the polynomial

.

Corollary 1
.

Using the notation of Proposition
3

1.

if
c_{d_{n}}
never vanishes on

is projectively delineable on

is delineable on

if

is delineable on

Proof.

The first item is the particular case

of Proposition

on the connected set

implies that there is a projective root function, with a constant multiplicity

on

on

on

Corollary

While projective delineability is not a theoretically weaker property than delineability, the corresponding projection (discriminants and resultants only, see below)

Global result and coverings

In this section, we prove a global counterpart of Theorem
1
, finding this requires some additional assumptions which are classical in the theory of covering spaces
[
7
]
.

Definition 12
.

Let
X
be a topological space. A
covering space
of
X
is a topological space
Y
together with a continuous map
p:Y\to X
such that,
for each point
x\in X
, there exist an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
in
X
and a non-empty family of disjoint open sets
(V_{i})_{i\in I}
of
Y
such that
p^{-1}(U)=\sqcup_{i\in I}V_{i}
and
p|_{V_{i}}:V_{i}\to U
is a homeomorphism. The covering space
Y
is trivial if these conditions hold for
U=X
.

Remark 5
.

Note that in this setting, defining for
i\in I
the
local section

s_{i}=(p|_{V_{i}})^{-1}
we obtain a family of continuous functions
(s_{i}:U\to Y)_{i\in I}
, such that
p\circ s_{i}=\text{id}_{U}
,
s_{i}(U)
is open in
p^{-1}(U)
, and
p^{-1}(U)
is the union of these sets.

Recall that a topological space is simply connected if it is path connected (and thus connected), and for any
x\in X
, any loop of
X
based at
x
can be continuously deformed (preserving the base point) onto the constant loop.

Theorem 2
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1
, and the further assumption that
S
is simply connected, we have that
P
is projectively delineable on
S
and
H^{d_{n}}(P)
is order-invariant on each projective
P
-section over
S
.

Proof.

Theorem
1
guarantees that the set
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
is a covering space of
S
for the usual (continuous) projection

p:Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)\to S:(\textbf{x},(x_{n}:y))\to\textbf{x}.

Indeed, with the notation of Theorem
1
, for all
\textbf{s}\in S
, projective delineability of
P
over the open neighbourhood
N_{\textbf{s}}
implies that
p^{-1}(N_{\textbf{s}})=Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,N_{\textbf{s}})
is the disjoint union
\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}\text{Graph}(\theta_{i})
where
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
are the projective root functions from
N_{\textbf{s}}
to
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
.
Then for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},V_{i}=\text{Graph}(\theta_{i})
is homeomorphic to
N_{\textbf{s}}
through
p
and
\mathrm{Id}\times\theta_{i}
. Finally, since
\theta_{i}
is a continuous function from
N_{\textbf{s}}
to the Hausdorff space
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
, its graph is closed in
N_{\textbf{s}}\times\mathbb{RP}^{1}
, hence in
p^{-1}(N_{\textbf{s}})\subset N_{\textbf{s}}\times\mathbb{RP}^{1}
. Thus
V_{i}=p^{-1}(N_{\textbf{s}})\setminus\cup_{j\neq i}V_{j}
is open in
p^{-1}(N_{\textbf{s}})
, and therefore in
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
.

Since
S
is a simply connected analytic submanifold, any covering space over
S
is trivial
[
7
, Corollary 13.8]
, so there exist global sections
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}
of
p:Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)\to S
. These functions read
s_{i}(\textbf{x})=(\textbf{x},t_{i}(\textbf{x}))
and
t_{i}
are by definition continuous projective root functions globally defined on
S
, such that
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
is the disjoint union of their graphs. Finally for every
\textbf{s}\in S
the function
t_{i}
coincides over
N_{\textbf{s}}
with one of the functions
\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{k}
. Therefore, the multiplicity of
t_{i}
as a projective root of
E_{\textbf{s}}(P)
is constant on
N_{\textbf{s}}
as is the order of
H^{d_{n}}(P)
at
t_{i}
on
N_{\textbf{s}}
. We conclude by the connectedness of
S
.
∎

Remark 6
.

Observe that the projective root functions in Theorems
1
and
2
are analytic functions. Indeed, their local expressions in suitable (analytic) charts are exactly the root functions given by Theorem 2 of

]
.

Theorem
2
fails without the additional assumption:

Proposition 4
.

There exist a connected analytic submanifold
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
P\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2};x_{3}]_{\leqslant 4}
such that

1.

P
is never nullified on
S
;

2.

the discriminant
\text{Disc}_{x_{3}}^{4}(P)
of
P
is not the zero polynomial of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2}]
and is order-invariant on
S
;

3.

P
is not projectively delineable over
S
.

Proof.

We define
S=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;|\;x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-1=0\}
and
P\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2};x_{3}]_{\leqslant 4}
such that
P(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is given by

(1-x_{1})x_{3}^{4}+4x_{2}x_{3}^{3}+(2+6x_{1})x_{3}^{2}-4x_{2}x_{3}+(1-x_{1}).

See Figure
3
. Then
P
satisfies the requested properties: it is never nullified on
S
and we compute that

\text{Disc}^{4}_{x_{3}}(P)(x_{1},x_{2})=2^{14}(-1+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}).

Elementary computations show that it has order
2
on
S
.

To prove that
P
is not projectively delineable over
S
, we first compute the set of projective roots of
P
above every
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in S
. If we write
\textbf{x}=(\cos(t),\sin(t))
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
then we can compute that
H^{4}(P)(\textbf{x},(x_{3},y))
is

8\left(\cos\left(t/4\right)x_{3}-\sin\left(t/4\right)y\right)^{2}\left(\sin\left(t/4\right)x_{3}+\cos\left(t/4\right)y\right)^{2},

implying that
P
has exactly two projective roots over
(x_{1},x_{2})
of multiplicity two given by

\left(\sin\left(t/4\right):\cos\left(t/4\right)\right)\text{ and }\left(-\cos\left(t/4\right):\sin\left(t/4\right)\right).

If we assume that
P
is projectively delineable over

, then there exists two continuous functions
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}:S\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
such that

Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)=\text{Graph}(\theta_{1})\sqcup\text{Graph}(\theta_{2})
.
Hence,
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
is not connected because the graphs
\text{Graph}(\theta_{1})
and
\text{Graph}(\theta_{2})
are non-empty and closed. However
Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(P,S)
happens to be connected as the image of
\mathbb{R}
by the continuous function
\Upsilon:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{RP}^{1}
given by

t\mapsto\left(\cos\left(t\right),\sin\left(t\right),\left(\sin\left(t/4\right):\cos\left(t/4\right)\right)\right).\qed

We show that Theorem 2.2 of
[

extends naturally in the projective framework. In particular, the order-invariance of the resultant of two projectively delineable polynomials on

Theorem 3
.

Let
S
be a connected analytic submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
P,Q\in\mathbb{R}[\textbf{x};x_{n}]
of respective positive degree
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
in
x_{n}
. Assume
P,Q
are never nullified on
S
and
P
is projectively delineable over
S
. If
\text{Res}_{x_{n}}^{p_{n},q_{n}}(P,Q)
is not the zero polynomial and is order-invariant over
S
, then along each projective
P
-section over
S
, either
H^{q_{n}}(Q)
vanishes completely or it never vanishes at all.

Proof.

By assumption,
P
is projectively delineable over
S
and we denote by
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}:S\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
its projective root functions over
S
. For every
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we consider

\Omega_{l}=\{\textbf{x}\in S\;|\;(\textbf{x},\theta_{l}(\textbf{x}))\in Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(Q,S)\}

and we have to prove that this set is either empty or equal to
S
. We do it by using similar arguments as in Theorem
1
and the connectedness of
S
. We may suppose that
\Omega_{l}
is non-empty and take
s
in its closure.

Since
E_{\textbf{s}}(PQ)=E_{\textbf{s}}(P)E_{\textbf{s}}(Q)
is nonzero, we can choose
(a_{11},a_{21})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
such that
H^{p_{n}}(P)H^{q_{n}}(Q)(\textbf{s},(a_{11},a_{21}))\neq 0
and then
a_{12},a_{22}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\det A=1
. We then obtain a neighbourhood
N_{\textbf{s}}
of
s
as in the beginning of the proof of Theorem
1
(but for the polynomial
PQ
). The projective root functions of
P
above
N_{\textbf{s}}
are given by the restrictions of
\theta_{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
to
N_{\textbf{s}}
. By Lemma
3
the projective root functions of
A^{*p_{n}}P
above
N_{\textbf{s}}

are given by the continuous functions
\mu_{A^{-1}}\circ\theta_{i}
. Hence
A^{*p_{n}}P
is projectively delineable over
N_{\textbf{s}}
and has, by the choice of
A
, no root at infinity. By Proposition
3
, we obtain the delineability of
A^{*p_{n}}P
and denote by
\eta_{i}=\varphi^{-1}\circ\mu_{A^{-1}}\circ\theta_{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
its root functions.
We now check the hypotheses of Theorem 2.2 of
[
12
]
, with
A^{*p_{n}}P
and
A^{*q_{n}}Q
:

1.

They have respective degree
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
in
x_{n}
by the choice of
A
. Hence their degrees are positive.

2.

By Proposition
1
,
\text{Res}^{p_{n},q_{n}}(A^{*p_{n}}P,A^{*q_{n}}Q)=\text{Res}^{p_{n},q_{n}}(P,Q)
, which is therefore not the zero polynomial and order-invariant over the connected analytic submanifold
N_{\textbf{s}}
.

In particular, we conclude that
A^{*q_{n}}Q
is sign invariant on the graph of
\eta_{l}
over
N_{\textbf{s}}
. Since
s
is in the closure of
\Omega_{l}
, there exists
\textbf{s}_{0}\in N_{\textbf{s}}\cap\Omega_{l}
. Then
\theta_{l}(\textbf{s}_{0})
is a projective root of
Q
above
\textbf{s}_{0}
, so
\mu_{A^{-1}}\circ\theta_{l}(\textbf{s}_{0})
is a projective root of
A^{*q_{n}}Q
above
\textbf{s}_{0}
and finally
A^{*q_{n}}Q(\textbf{s}_{0},\eta_{l}(\textbf{s}_{0}))=0
. By the sign invariance
A^{*q_{n}}Q(\textbf{x},\eta_{l}(\textbf{x}))=0
for every
\textbf{x}\in N_{\textbf{s}}
, and it follows that
(\textbf{x},\theta_{l}(\textbf{x}))\in Z_{\mathbb{RP}^{1}}(Q,S)
for every
\textbf{x}\in N_{\textbf{s}}
. We just showed that
N_{\textbf{s}}\subset\Omega_{l}
for all
s
in the closure of
\Omega_{l}
, ensuring that the closure of
\Omega_{l}
is a subset of the interior of
\Omega_{l}
. Then
\Omega_{l}
is open and closed and therefore
\Omega_{l}=S
by connectedness.
∎
