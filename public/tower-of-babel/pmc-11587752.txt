Associations between cardiac arrhythmias and cardiovascular disease incidence and all-cause mortality: the Kailuan study
Authors: Xuemei Yang, Tingting Geng, Yinshun Peng, Liufu Cui, Shuohua Chen, Guodong Wang, Xiang Gao, Shouling Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCardiac arrhythmia is a rising public health issue. The aim of this study was to determine the associations of atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart block with cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence and all-cause mortality.Methods and resultsWe included 141,362 participants (mean age [49.3], 80.9% men) from the Kailuan study. Arrhythmias were diagnosed through a 12-lead electrocardiograph (ECG). Mortality and CVD events were ascertained through multiple sources, including a municipal social insurance institution, hospital records, death certificates, and regular active follow-ups. During a median follow-up of 12.5 years, 18,301 total deaths and 13,208 cases of CVD were documented. The multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) comparing participants with AF to those without arrhythmia were 1.76 (1.61–1.93) for all-cause mortality, 2.11 (1.86–2.39) for CVD, 3.99 (3.33–4.79) for heart failure, and 1.56 (1.30–1.90) for stroke. Further, comparing participants with heart block to those without arrhythmia, the multivariable-adjusted HRs (95% CIs) were 1.31 (1.24–1.38) for all-cause mortality, 1.26 (1.18–1.35) for CVD, 1.40 (1.23–1.59) for heart failure, and 1.25 (1.15–1.37) for stroke. Additionally, there were generally stronger associations for AF and heart block with all-cause mortality and CVD in younger participants compared with their older counterparts (Ps-interaction ≤ 0.02) and a stronger association between AF and CVD in women compared with men (Ps-interaction ≤ 0.006).ConclusionAF and heart block were associated with a higher risk of subsequent adverse CVD events and mortality. Our findings highlight the importance of strategies for preventing cardiac arrhythmias to reduce the risk of CVD and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20703-6.Keywords: Heart block, Atrial fibrillation, Cardiovascular disease, Mortality, Kailuan study

Introduction

Cardiac arrhythmia, referring to abnormal heart rhythm including tachycardia characterized by a fast heartbeat and bradycardia characterized by a slow heartbeat, is a rising public health issue. Among these arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation (AF) stands out as the most prevalent sustained arrhythmia, with around 44 million people estimated to have AF worldwide [1–3]. Mounting evidence has linked AF to the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, end-stage kidney disease, and premature death in general populations or patients with pre-existing conditions, such as myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease and diabetes [4–13].

In addition, heart block including atrioventricular block and bundle branch block are affecting a large number of individuals. In fact, around 4% of the general population experiences the first-degree atrioventricular block [14]. Furthermore, among various forms of heart block, high-degree atrioventricular block and left bundle branch block serve as primary indicators for pacemaker implantation [15]. However, evidence on the associations of heart block with cardiovascular events and mortality is limited.

To address the research gaps, we aimed to examine the associations of heart block with subsequent risks of all-cause mortality and incident cardiovascular disease including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke, using data from the Kailuan study, a prospective cohort of Chinese adults. And we further aimed to provide more evidence for AF and CVD and all-cause mortality. We also estimated population attributable risks (PARs), the proportion of deaths and cardiovascular events during follow-up that could have been avoided if individuals were free of heart block and AF.

Methods

Study population

The Kailuan study is an ongoing longitudinal cohort study conducted in the Kailuan community in Tangshan city, China. Detailed information about the study designs has been published previously [16]. Briefly, the study initially enrolled 101,510 Chinese adults (81,110 men and 20,400 women, aged ≥ 18 years) between 2006 and 2007, and additionally enrolled 51,639 participants (42,311 men and 9328 women, ≥ 18 years) between 2008 and 2012. The participants were followed up biennially including the administration of questionnaire, anthropometric measurements, clinical examinations, and laboratory tests [16]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Kailuan General Hospital following the guidelines outlined by the Helsinki Declaration. All participants agreed to participate in this study and provided written informed consent.

Ascertainment of atrial fibrillation and heart block

Diagnosis of atrial fibrillation and heart block was based on a 12-lead electrocardiograph (ECG) conducted during recruitment process according to the guidelines of the American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association and Heart Rhythm Society [17, 18]. Atrial fibrillation was identified if either atrial flutter or atrial fibrillation was present on ECGs. Heart block was diagnosed if any degrees of atrioventricular block, or any types of bundle branch block was detected on ECGs.

Assessment of covariates

Information on demographics (age and sex), socioeconomic status indicators (average monthly income and education levels), lifestyle factors (lifetime tobacco use, alcohol intake and physical activity), medical history (physician diagnosed hypertension and diabetes), and medication history (hypoglycemic, anti-hypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antiplatelet drugs) was obtained by well-trained interviewers using a structured questionnaire. For cigarette smoking, participants were classified as never, former, and current smokers. Participants were defined as current smokers if they had smoked at least one cigarette a day for more than six months. Regarding alcohol intake, participants were categorized as never, former, and current drinkers. Anthropometric parameters including Height, weight, and waist circumference were measured by trained nurses; body mass index (BMI) was calculated by body weight in kilogram divided by square of height in meter. Data on the amount of time spent on exercise per week were collected and participants who reported engaging in physical activity for more than 80-minutes per week were classified as physically active [19].

Fasting blood sample (8–10 h) were collected from the consenting participants, separated by components and analyzed at the central laboratory of Kailuan hospital. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured using the hexokinase/glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase method (Mind Bioengineering Co Ltd, Shanghai, China), with an upper limit of 30.07 mmol/L. Concentrations of total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), creatinine, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were determined by auto analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated from creatinine levels, following the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [20]. Prevalent diabetes was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, the use of hypoglycemic medications, or FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. Pre-existing hypertension was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed cases, undertaking the anti-hypertensive medications, or blood pressure measurements ≥ 140/90 mmHg.

Ascertainment of incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality

The primary outcomes of the study included all-cause mortality and a composite outcome of myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure. The secondary outcomes were subtypes of CVD. Detailed information regarding the ascertainment of outcomes can be found elsewhere [21–23]. The Kailuan study demonstrated comprehensive in CVD and mortality events recording through multiple sources including a municipal social insurance institution that covered all study participants, hospital discharge register centers, death certificates, and regular active follow-ups conducted annually since 2006.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of CVD outcomes, we included 141,362 participants after excluding individuals with missing values on ECG data (N = 6816), and those with a history of cancer (N = 528) or CVD (including myocardial infarction, heart failure and stroke) (N = 4443). For the analysis of all-cause mortality, prevalent CVD cases were not excluded leaving a total of 145,805 participants.

Patients were censored at occurrence of first endpoint, or end of follow-up (Dec 31, 2021), the end of follow-up of heart failure was Dec 31, 2020, whichever occurred first. We imputed missing values (< 4%) using means for numerical variables and modes for categorical variables. Differences between participants by atrial fibrillation or heart block were examined using Student t test, Mann-Whitney U test, and chi-squared test where appropriate. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to estimate the survival function of mortality and CVD events by AF and heart block status. Age and sex standardized CVD and death rates were estimated according to the arrhythmia status.

We used multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models to compute the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of AF and heart block with risks of outcomes of interest using those without AF or heart failure as the reference group. Three models were fitted. In Model 1, we adjusted for age at recruitment (years, continuous) and sex (men, women). In Model 2, we further adjusted for education level (college or university degree, others), BMI (kg/m2, continuous), alcohol intake (never, former, current), smoking status (never, past, current), physical activity status (yes, no), prevalent hypertension (yes, no), prevalent diabetes (yes, no), HDL-C (mmol/L, continuous), LDL-C (mmol/L, continuous), eGFR (ml/min per 1.73 m2, continuous), and hs-CRP (mg/L, continuous). In Model 3, use of anti-diabetic medications (none, oral drugs, insulin and others), anti-hypertensive medications (yes, no), cholesterol-lowering medications (yes, no), and antiplatelet drugs (yes, no) were additionally adjusted. In addition, we calculated the PARs to theoretically estimate the proportion of each outcome in this study population that could have been prevented if the population had no AF and heart block.

We also stratified the analyses by age (< 45, 45–65, ≥ 65 years), sex (men, women), smoking status (never, ever), history of hypertension and diabetes (yes/no for each). The interactions between exposures and the stratified factors on the outcomes were tested using the likelihood ratio test by including the multiplicative terms in the multivariable-adjusted Model 3. In addition, the joint effects of AF and heart block associated with risk of mortality and CVD events were also examined using participants who had no AF or heart block as the reference group.

To explore the robustness of our primary findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we performed a two-year lag year analysis to minimize the possibility of reverse causality on the observed associations. Second, to avoid the potential effect of the pre-excitation syndrome cases, we repeated the analysis after excluding patients with pre-excitation syndrome. Third, we also excluded patients who were using anti-hypertensive medications, cholesterol-lowering medications, and antiplatelet drugs. Fourth, to avoid the pace induced heart failure and mortality, we excluded patients with cardiac pacemakers. Fifth, to account for competing risks of death, we assessed the associations of AF and heart block with risks of incidence CVD events using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models. Finally, we performed the analyses in a 1:4 propensity score matched cohort to minimize differences between AF cases, heart block cases and non-cases.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina), and a two-sided p < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 141,362 participants, there were 885 (0.63%) AF cases and 6743 (4.77%) heart block cases, with 91 cases were defined as both AF and heart block. Baseline characteristics of prevalent AF, heart block, and no arrhythmia are shown in Table 1. Compared with the non-arrhythmia group, patients with AF and heart block were more likely to be older, men, have lower educational attainment, be physically inactive, and use medications for hypertension, lipid-lowering, and hypoglycemia. They tended to have a higher level of BMI, and a higher prevalence of hypertension, and diabetes. Patients with AF were also more likely to be non-current smokers and non-current drinkers.

Age and sex standardized CVD and death rates according to the arrhythmia status

After a median follow-up of 12.5 years, there occurred 18,301 (12.9%) deaths, and 13,208 (9.34%) CVD cases including 2025 (1.43%) myocardial infarction, 2967 (2.10%) heart failure, 7269 (5.14%) ischemic stroke, and 1316 (0.93%) hemorrhagic stroke. The rates for all-cause mortality and CVD events were substantially higher among individuals with AF and heart block compared to their counterparts. The age and sex adjusted rates of all-cause mortality were 29.3% among individuals with AF, 14.7% among individuals with heart block, and 13.1% among those without arrhythmias while the adjusted rates of CVD events were 23.5% for AF,10.7% for heart block, and 9.20% for those without arrhythmias (Table 2). The Kaplan-Meier curves showed that compared with non-arrhythmia group, patients with AF and heart block had lower survival functions (both Ps for log-rank test < 0.001; Supplemental Fig. 1).

Secondary analyses and sensitivity analyses

In the stratified analysis, there were generally stronger associations of AF and heart block with all-cause mortality and CVD in the participants at younger age compared with their older counterparts (Ps-interaction ≤ 0.02) and a stronger association between AF and CVD in women compared with men (Ps-interaction ≤ 0.006). We did not find significant heterogeneity in the risk estimates of other stratified factors with AF or heart block on the risk of outcomes (Fig. 1). In joint effect analysis, using those who were without AF or heart block as the reference, patients with heart block or AF had a significant higher risk of CVD and all-cause mortality. Moreover, patients with both AF and heart block had substantially higher risk of CVD (HR, 2.64; 95% CI, 1.82–3.83) and all-cause mortality (HR, 2.00; 95% CI, 1.54–2.61; Table 4).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we found that heart block and AF were each associated with a higher risk of mortality and CVD events. The observed association was stronger in participants aged younger than those who aged older. In addition, we estimated a significant proportion of all-cause mortality (27.9%) and CVD events (25.3%) could have been prevented if the participants did not have AF. Similarly, if the participants had no heart block, 6.72% of all-cause mortality and 3.47% of CVD events could have been avoided.

Our findings were consistent with those of previous studies, that AF was associated with a higher risk of mortality and CVD events. For instance, a cohort study conducted in the Netherlands including 8265 participants, showed that incident AF was associated with a substantially higher risk of CVD events and all-cause mortality [9]. Similarly, the Women’s Health Study found that incident AF was associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality [24]. Moreover, a recent study conducted using data from the UK Biobank also showed that incident AF was associated with a substantial higher risk of developing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure and all-cause mortality among patients with type 2 diabetes [13]. These consistent findings across multiple studies underscored the strong association between AF and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

However, the existing evidence on the relationship between heart block and CVD events and mortality is limited and inconsistent. The Copenhagen City Heart Study including 18,441 participants showed that right bundle branch block was associated with a higher risk of CVD events and all-cause mortality [25]. Another cohort study with 5687 Danish participants under the age of 50 years revealed that atrioventricular block of unknown aetiology, treated with cardiac pacemakers was associated with a four-fold higher risk of a composite of hospitalized heart failure and total death [26]. In contrast, null associations were also reported. For instance, a study of 855 men aged 50 years followed up for 30 years found no relationship between bundle branch block and ischemic heart disease or mortality [27]. A retrospective cohort study including 85 oldest-old patients hospitalized for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) showed that left bundle branch blocks were not associated with mortality [28].

Our study provides novel insights to the limited published literature on the associations of heart block with mortality and CVD events. We found that more prominent CVD and mortality risk associated with heart block among participants aged younger compared with their older counterparts, suggesting that the impact of arrythmias on cardiovascular health and mortality was more dramatic in younger participants. In addition, given the high prevalence of heart block in this population, we calculated the population attributable risk of heart block for the outcomes. In the Kailuan study, we estimated that 6.72% of all-cause mortality and 3.47% of CVD events could be attributed to heart block. Despite the high population risk, heart block is often not diagnosed unless patients are experience symptoms or during tests for other conditions. In order to mitigate the risk of CVD and all-cause mortality in patients with asymptomatic AF and heart block, regular electrocardiographic monitoring is imperative alongside appropriate exercise interventions [29, 30]. Although athletes engaged in long-term high-intensity exercise are at a heightened risk of developing AF [31], engaging in moderate-intensity exercise not only helps prevent AF [32, 33], but also demonstrates a protective effect against long-term cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in patients with AF [29]. Therefore, in asymptomatic patients with atrial AF and well-controlled ventricular rates ≤ 110 beats/min, appropriate exercise is recommended [29]. Additionally, individuals with asymptomatic first- or second-degree atrioventricular block at rest that normalizes during exercise may engage in all forms of physical activity [30]. Our findings underscore the importance of early prevention and detection of heart block in general population for CVD and premature death prevention, which have substantial public health and clinical relevance.

The potential link between arrythmias and mortality and CVD events may gain biological plausibility via the following mechanisms. First, arrythmias such as AF may contribute to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which could result in cardiac fibrosis through cardiac structural and electrical remodeling, and ultimately lead to cardiovascular phenotypes [34–36]. In addition, both AF and heart block may contribute to the development of atherosclerotic vascular disease through activating the prothrombotic related pathways and causing endothelia dysfunction [37, 38]. Finally, arrythmias, including both AF and heart block have the potential to cause cardiomyopathy and heart failure through septal hypoperfusion in left bundle branch block, molecular changes, e.g., reduction in SERCA2, phospholamban, and connexin43, and mitochondrial metabolism changes [39–41].

Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first investigations to assess the association of the heart block with the subsequent development of CVD events and all-cause mortality. Other strengths included its prospective study design, large sample size, and longer follow-up. However, some limitations must be acknowledged to aid in the interpretation of our results. First, the self-reported covariates were subject to measurement error and misclassifications were inevitable. However, given the prospective study design, such misclassification would probably be nondifferential in nature, which would then likely lead to an underestimation of the true associations. Second, one-time assessment of arrhythmia using ECG may have missed some cases of AF and heart block. Furthermore, we did not conduct follow-up with these patients, thus lacking information regarding their treatment regimens including anticoagulant usage. Third, we were unable to examine the duration of episodes for AF and heart block due to lack of the information. Fourth, both the relative risks and prevalence of risk factors must be considered when generalizing our findings of population attributable risk to other populations. Fifth, our work was confined to middle-aged and older Chinese adults, thus limiting the amenability of our findings to other ethnic and age groups. Finally, due to the nature of the observational study design, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results showed that arrhythmias, including both AF and heart block were associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality and CVD. These findings underscore the importance of strategies targeted on both AF and heart block prevention for reducing the risk premature death and CVD events.
