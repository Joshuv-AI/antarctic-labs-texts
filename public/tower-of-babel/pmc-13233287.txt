Cross-lingual transfer with multilingual language models for influenza-like-illness detection in social media texts
Authors: Niti Mishra, Joan Ballester, Rodrigo Agerri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent Language Models (LMs) can process multilingual texts, enabling social media-based Influenza-Like-Illness (ILI) surveillance across languages without requiring language-specific systems. Yet, it remains unclear whether a cost-effective multilingual classifier can reliably extract ILI signals from social media across countries, languages, and seasons. We introduce MuILI, the first class-balanced multilingual dataset annotated for ILI detection, comprising 4,284 tweets across five widely spoken European languages. Using MuILI, we investigated whether a single multilingual classifier can consistently extract ILI signals across nine ILI seasons for France (2014-2023) and five seasons for Italy, Spain, and Germany (2018-2023). We compared five fine-tuned Multilingual-Masked LMs (MMLMs), three Cross-Lingual Transfer (CLT) strategies, and four In-Context Learning (ICL) techniques with decoder-only Large LMs (LLMs). Domain-adapted MMLMs (Bernice, F1 = 0.87), set the benchmark performance. Comparable results were obtained with translate-train (F1 = 0.85) or model-transfer (F1 = 0.84) from French data alone. Spanish translate-train performed similarly (F1 = 0.84). Among LLMs, only Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct surpassed the benchmark (F1 = 0.88–0.90). Spearman cross-correlation between ILI signals derived from the benchmark classifier and official consultation rates demonstrated strong real-time alignment, though it varied during disruptions. Overall, multilingual LMs can expand ILI surveillance using social-media. CLT offers annotation-efficient solutions, while ICL with LLMs can eliminate annotation requirements but at substantial computational costs.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Seasonal and pandemic influenza are major public health concerns. While most people recover from influenza within a week, for others, such as the older population, pregnant women, children under five years, or those with underlying medical conditions, it can cause severe illnesses, hospitalization, and even death1,2. Up-to-date preparedness and response plans are crucial to minimizing influenza burden, and their successful implementation requires investment in monitoring and surveillance of influenza activity at multiple geographic levels3.

Currently in Europe, regional and national sentinel networks provide influenza surveillance data comprising syndromic proxies such as consultation rates for Influenza-Like-Illnesses (ILI) or Acute Respiratory Infection (ARI) recorded in medical clinics, alongside laboratory-confirmed influenza in respiratory specimens4. These data, however, have limitations relating to their timeliness and representation as incidences are reported days after the onset of symptoms and only capture populations that seek healthcare2,5.

Past studies have successfully explored the use of complementary alternative data sources to estimate trends in ILI activity, such as school absenteeism, telephone health-lines, pharmacy sales, electronic health records, digital participatory surveys, internet search queries, and social media posts5,6. Among these, X/Twitter, a popular micro-blogging social media, has been extensively used to research various real-world applications such as disaster prediction, monitoring diseases, hate-speech detection, poll prediction, and tracking stock market fluctuations7,8. Reviews on social media usage for applications in public health found X/Twitter to be the most frequently used alternative data source and the syndromic surveillance of ILI to be the most common application7,9.

Initial studies on ILI surveillance using X/Twitter extracted applicable tweets by matching keywords related to ILI and its symptoms. Such approaches captured a broad range of content, including general awareness, news, and public interest, in addition to expressions of ILI. To address this limitation, subsequent studies employed machine learning and natural language processing techniques to filter tweets reporting recent or ongoing ILI only. Broniatowski et al. showed that filtered English-language tweets from the United States strongly correlated with national and city-level influenza surveillance data, confirming the potential of social media as a complementary epidemiological tool10. Similar monolingual approaches were later applied in Portugal and Italy, which also reported strong correlation with official national ILI surveillance data11,12. Other studies further highlighted the value of ILI signals from social media by demonstrating an improved ILI prediction when incorporating tweets into an ensemble of heterogeneous data streams13,14.

More recent studies have explored the use of Transformer-based Masked Language Models (MLMs) capable of processing texts in one or more languages for text-classification, including ILI detection15. Magumba and Nabende showed limited cross-lingual generalization when Multilingual MLMs (MMLMs) were trained primarily on English-language tweets, whereas Mutuvi et al. demonstrated superior performance of MMLM on disease outbreak task when training on news articles in six languages16,17. Other studies applied parallel modelling strategies by training on ILI related tweets in English and Arabic separately and reported higher correlations with national influenza-related hospital visits in the United Arab Emirates when combining signals from both languages18. Finally, domain-adapted monolingual MLMs have been shown to outperform generic ones for ILI and COVID-19 detection in English-language tweets19.

Despite these advances, challenges remain regarding the applicability of existing approaches for global or large-scale regional ILI surveillance. First, prior studies reported a low ratio of tweets mentioning recent or ongoing ILI infection (ILI ratio hereafter) compared to general ILI discourse, with values ranging from 5.16% over two months to 17.5% over one year18,19. Models trained on such highly imbalanced datasets tend to be biased towards the majority class and underperform in the minority class, primarily of our epidemiological interest20. Conversely, utilizing a class-balanced dataset for training facilitates the learning of discriminative features across both classes, promoting robust generalization and reducing the risk of obscuring reliable epidemiological signals.

Second, existing approaches remain largely restricted to language-specific, predominantly English, datasets and models, limiting their utility for multilingual surveillance. As a result, a systematic benchmark for ILI detection in social media posts across multiple European languages is lacking. Moreover, independent modeling for each language is highly resource demanding in terms of time and costs associated with data collection, human annotation, and integration. Additionally, previous research has been largely confined to single-country validations over narrow temporal windows, providing limited evidence of their longitudinal and geographic generalizability. While emerging language technologies such as Cross-Lingual Transfer (CLT) and In-Context Learning (ICL) with Large Language Models (LLMs) have substantial potential to address these limitations, they have not yet been systematically evaluated for this purpose21,22. Thus, it remains unclear how fine-tuned MMLMs and LLMs perform in multilingual ILI classification, whether large-scale, language-specific annotations are required, and whether such models can consistently generate meaningful epidemiological signals across countries.

To address these limitations, this study evaluates the current capabilities of language models to support scalable, annotation-efficient, and epidemiologically valid ILI surveillance over four European countries. Specifically, we examine multiple modelling strategies with varying requirements for annotated data to assess their ILI detection performance across five languages. To our knowledge, an assessment of cross-lingual transferability and longitudinal validity for social media-based ILI surveillance has not previously been reported. Results

MuILI dataset

We present the MuILI dataset comprising annotated tweets related to ILI in five European languages and make its anonymized version publicly available online for reproducibility and reusability. Table 1 summarizes the 4,284 tweets in the MuILI dataset into its languages and annotated classes. Due to their low sample-size, tweets annotated as “Ambiguous” were removed, and tweets in “Likely COVID-19” class were combined with “Likely ILI” class. The core strength of our dataset lies in its inclusion of tweets related to ILI in multiple languages, annotated with the same guidelines, its substantial ground truth annotation quality, and balanced distribution of ILI vs non-ILI classes in all languages except Italian.

Fine-tuned MMLM performance

Figure 1 shows the performance of the five MMLMs fine-tuned on the MuILI dataset for ILI detection in multilingual tweets as measured by average macro F1-score over five runs with random seed initialization. Bernice, with the largest amount of tweet specific pre-training data, achieved the best all-language F1-score of 0.87. The all-language performance accounts for differences in test sample-size across languages. XLM-T, another tweet-based model, ranked second with an all-language F1-score of 0.86. It performed lower than Bernice in all languages except English, for which it performed similarly. It also performed slightly lower in French and German languages compared to mDeBERTav3, which had an all-language F1-score of 0.85. mDeBERTav3 had lower average performance in Italian compared to the other four languages. XLM-R had similar all-language performance as mDEBERTav3 but scored higher in Italian. mBERT had the lowest all-language F1-score of 0.82.

Macro F1-scores of ILI detection in tweets in multiple languages.

Each box plot shows the results of the five runs of an MMLM fine-tuned using training and validation tweets from all languages. The first box to the left shows results on the test data of all languages pooled together, and subsequent boxes to the right represent test data of each language individually. Each color represents one of the five MMLMs used in this study.

The best performance was observed in Spanish test data with all MMLMs. The performances on French test data were lower with XLM-T, XLM-R, and mBERT compared to Bernice and mDeBERTav3. The performances on German test data were fairly consistent throughout all MMLMs except mBERT, for which it was lower. The test data for Italian and English suffered from class imbalance and data scarcity, respectively, resulting in lower average performance compared to other languages. Still, F1-scores remained above 0.78 with the two tweet-based models.

Correlation with official consultation rates

Figure 2 shows the weekly comparison of normalized official consultation rates and ILI-tweets along with their Spearman rank cross-correlation over two lag periods across countries and ILI seasons. For ILI seasons from 2014/15 to 2017/18 (top row), correlations were consistently high in France (0.88 to 0.97), with slightly stronger associations at lag 0 compared to lag 1. ILI-tweets for other countries were not available for these periods. In 2018/19 (second row, first column), correlations were higher at lag 1 across all countries. France, Italy, and Spain showed strong correlation (0.83+), while lower values (0.71 at lag 0 and 0.75 at lag 1) were observed in Germany, where ARI consultation rates were reported. In 2019/20 (second row, second column), correlations declined slightly for France and Spain, but improvements were observed for Italy and Germany.

During the COVID-19 pandemic affected seasons, correlations varied across countries. By 2022/23 (second row, last column), correlations increased across countries, suggesting a return toward more typical seasonal patterns following pandemic-related disruptions.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced MuILI, a class-balanced dataset of manually annotated ILI-related tweets across five major European languages. Using MuILI, we fine-tuned state-of-the-art MMLMs and conducted cross-lingual ablation studies to assess ILI detection performance across languages with varying amounts of annotated data. Additionally, we compared seven LLMs of varying sizes to examine their capacity for ILI classification with a few or no manually annotated examples. Finally, we assessed the epidemiological validity of the signals derived from ILI-tweets by performing cross-correlation analyses with official consultation rates over two weekly lag periods across multiple ILI seasons in four European countries. Moving beyond prior monolingual studies confined to isolated temporal windows, this work establishes a systematic multilingual benchmark for ILI detection and demonstrates a scalable, annotation-efficient framework for large-scale surveillance. By extending social media-based ILI surveillance to multiple countries and validating ILI signals across consecutive seasons, we show that a multilingual framework can generate consistent digital syndromic proxies aligned with established surveillance indicators.

Consistent with past literature, we observed higher overall performance with models pre-trained on larger and/or domain-adapted data17,19,16,23,24. Across all ICL settings, the largest LLM, Qwen2.5-72B, achieved the best all-language performance, with highest performance observed in English and Spanish. Using the MuILI dataset, highest performance was observed with Bernice and XLM-T, which are specifically pre-trained on tweets, with the highest performance observed in Spanish. The higher performances in English and Spanish are likely attributable to the larger size of pre-training data in these languages23,25. Improvements with these models were also observed in Italian, despite class imbalance, and in German, despite differences in linguistic origin.

We observed variability in model performances between English and non-English languages under different ICL settings. Providing examples exclusively in English substantially improved performance in English, whereas providing examples in each target language increased performance mainly in non-English languages and only modestly in English. This highlights a tradeoff to consider when using LLMs with ICL in multilingual scenarios.

The benefit of multilingual examples for better overall performance also holds for fine-tuned MMLMs, and MT can be effectively leveraged to this end. Translate-train using French or Spanish data achieved comparable performance to fine-tuning on the original MuILI dataset, which required at least four times more resources to develop. While French model-transfer also performed close to the benchmark, further improvements were observed when the monolingual training data was augmented into multilingual using MT. These results underscore the practicality of MMLMs’ ability to transfer signals from one language to another and confirm the utility of MT to achieve those benefits22,26.

Larger LLMs ( ~ 70B parameters) demonstrated improvements over benchmark already with zero-shot instruction, with further improvements observed once a few examples were added. Qwen2.5-72B, trained on 29 languages, outperformed the benchmark both with and without examples27. Llama3.1-70B, trained on eight languages with a smaller vocabulary, showed notable gains in English over benchmark with examples26. Gemma3-27b, trained on 104 languages with the largest vocabulary, performed below benchmark in zero-shot settings but exhibited significant improvements once examples were added28. These results indicate that larger LLMs offer optimal performance without manually annotated data, although at high computational and deployment costs.

For CLT, the characteristics of task-specific annotated data are critical. First, improvements seen with translate-train using French, Spanish, and English data were not observed for Italian and German, likely due to lower representation in the pre-training data, and in Italian due to class imbalance15,20,29. Additionally, linguistic difference of German from Romance languages likely reduced lexicons, syntax, and morphological similarity, limiting cross-lingual alignment21,26. However, substantial cross-lingual transfer is observed when fine-tuning on languages with higher pre-training volumes, as evidenced by 0.79 macro-averaged recall on German when trained exclusively on Spanish data (Supplementary Table 1). Second, despite having the largest pre-training data, fine-tuning on only 117 English tweets showed limited performance, whereas simply translating non-English test data into English substantially improved results. These findings highlight the viability of translate-train and translate-test in data scarce scenarios, although translate-test incurs additional translation overhead22. Thus, language representation and data characteristics should be carefully considered for efficient multilingual ILI classifiers.

Collectively, these results highlight practical reasons to favor fine-tuned MMLMs over LLMs for routine, large-scale ILI surveillance. While the pre-training corpora for MMLMs like mBERT (138GB) and XLM-R (2.5TB) are significantly smaller than those utilized by LLMs, they outperformed smaller-sized LLMs in ICL settings, particularly for German and Italian languages. A similar pattern was observed with translate-train. These findings emphasize the cost-efficiency of using small, task-specific MMLMs with minimal annotation. Furthermore, MMLMs fulfill critical requirements, including reproducibility, lower latency, and operational sustainability, better positioning them for real-time epidemiological monitoring of high-volume data streams.

Thus, to evaluate operational utility, we selected Bernice, fine-tuned on the original MuILI dataset over the best performing LLM. The classifier effectively filtered ILI signals from multilingual tweets, enabling real-time tracking across time and locations. Strong correlation with the official consultation rates was observed across eight of nine ILI seasons in France, all five seasons in Italy, three of four seasons in Spain, and four of five seasons in Germany. During 2018/19 ILI season, correlations were higher at lag 1 compared to lag 0 across countries, highlighting early-warning capability. For subsequent seasons, higher correlations at lag 0 indicate real-time alignment with epidemiological trends. Given the 5–21 days reporting delay of official surveillance data, the real-time alignment underscores the potential of ILI-tweets as an early warning indicator of seasonal ILI dynamics12,14.

While the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted real-time associations between ILI-tweets and official consultation rates, the impact varied by country. Moderate correlations remained in four of seven affected ILI seasons (three ILI, one ARI), highlighting the resilience of signals derived from ILI-tweets. In Italy and Germany, ILI-tweets preceded official consultation rates in 2020/21, indicating early warning signals. However, declines in correlations observed in 2019/20 in France, Italy (lag 1), and Spain reflect the challenges posed by the growing volume of COVID-19 related posts. This pattern was not observed in Germany likely because the ARI indicator used there captures a broader spectrum of respiratory diseases30. An unusually low ILI ratio of 9% in France in 2020/21, driven by a heightened attention to COVID-19, weakened signals further (Supplementary Table 2). These lower ILI ratios, despite our filtering strategy, reflect reduced signal density and highlight the persistent signal-to-noise challenges inherent to social media10,18,19.

ILI-tweets align less closely with ARI consultation rates, likely because ARI includes a broader range of respiratory illnesses30. Overall lower correlations were observed in Germany, and a similar pattern was seen in Spain after the switch to ARI. Weak correlations were also observed in Spain and Germany during 2021/22, when ARI rates remained elevated due to ongoing COVID-19 circulation and influenza activity was atypical in both magnitude and timing31.

Finally, including multilingual tweets improved signals from ILI-tweets. In Spain, adding ILI-tweets in Catalan and Basque languages notably improved correlations. In Germany, adding English-language tweets was beneficial in 2018/19 when German-language tweets were limited. However, from 2019/20 onward, such augmentations were likely redundant as the volume of German-language tweets steadily increased and correlations remained strong without augmentation.

Although our results demonstrate clear benefits, they are not without limitations. One of them being the narrow range of European languages for which the data were collected and annotated. All languages are not equally represented in LMs due to their differences in pre-training size, linguistic structure, and language origins25,29,32. Also, despite expanding to multilingual regions, certain populations, regions, and demographics remained underrepresented. Therefore, future work should include additional languages and geographic areas to assess model generalizability and ILI surveillance capacity. We make both our data and model publicly available online to facilitate further research in this area.

MT process can also impact cross-lingual performance across languages and techniques, particularly for languages with distinct linguistic origins, such as German22. Future work should systematically compare different MT systems or translation quality levels to assess their impact on multilingual performance.

The smaller size of English data also limited a proper comparison of MMLM performances using English data. Although this study also demonstrated the benefit of MT and ICL with LLMs in such data scarce scenarios.

Additionally, LLM performance can vary substantially across different input instructions33–35. Future work is recommended to optimize multilingual classifier performance by tuning input instructions with more and/or different sets of examples.

While the largest LLMs demonstrated superior performance, their practical utility is limited by high computational costs and limited GPU availability. Furthermore, the low representation of non-English languages in pre-training data remains a persistent challenge in LLMs15.

Further research requiring integration of ILI-tweets with region and language-specific epidemiological factors is also needed to examine their forecasting potential beyond situational awareness.

Besides, limitations of using social media for ILI signals were also highlighted as low tweet volumes in German and Italian languages, online noise from other respiratory illnesses, and heightened public attention during pandemic seasons reduced correlations with official data.

Our reliance on a static, keyword-based strategy to obtain a class-balanced dataset may have limited coverage of ILI mentions, particularly for emerging terminology. Future work could explore dynamic ontologies or medical knowledge graph-based expansions to address this. Additionally, the system could be extended towards clinically informative monitoring by integrating ontology linking with medical definitions or frameworks like retrieval-augmented generation.

Finally, changes to X/Twitter API access increase implementation costs for X/Twitter-based ILI surveillance. This cost may be reduced by collecting tweets from select users with high centrality, as demonstrated in one study36. Other social media platforms, such as Facebook and Reddit, that offer free API with limited access should also be studied to avoid single-platform reliance risks.

Despite these limitations, our study advances scientific and public health understanding by demonstrating that scalable, annotation-efficient classifiers can detect ILI in multilingual social media posts with minimal annotated data. Crucially, fine-tuning with multilingual data improved performance for all languages, including English. By prioritizing MuILI—the first class-balanced multilingual ILI dataset, alongside MMLM fine-tuning and evaluation of both annotation-efficient strategies and current LLM capacities, we address critical linguistic, data, and methodological gaps. This work also establishes a rigorous performance benchmark and provides reproducible, computationally efficient, and sustainable alternatives to high-cost LLMs. This bridges the knowledge gap necessary to discern the operational trade-offs critical in real-world, resource-constrained public health settings. Moreover, we address the geographical and temporal gaps by evaluating the practical utility of our classifier to generate a scalable, digital syndromic proxy for ILI, as evidenced by the strong alignment between ILI-tweets and official consultation rates across four European countries over multiple ILI seasons.

These results are particularly significant for multilingual regions such as Europe. A multilingual ILI detection classifier enables efficient use of underutilized multilingual health-related online data for large-scale geographic surveillance, where deviations from usual trends may signal novel pathogens, facilitating earlier detection locally37. Furthermore, cross-correlation analyses demonstrate real-time insights and early signals of disease activity in populations not already covered by traditional systems5,6,9. Finally, it can serve as a non-sentinel data source for epidemiological modelling, offering additional insights on viral dynamics10–14. Integrating such classifiers into existing surveillance and forecasting frameworks can enhance preparedness and response to future epidemics and pandemics6,9.

Methods

Data collection

The high noise-to-signal ratio inherent in illness-related social media discourse makes it challenging to obtain a class-balanced dataset18,19. Therefore, we hypothesized that a high-precision keyword filtering strategy would match tweets mentioning actual infection, thereby increasing the ILI ratio in our dataset. To implement this, we obtained academic access to extract publicly available tweets in five most spoken languages in Europe: English, French, German, Italian, and Spanish. We used linguistic variations of influenza as primary keywords and a list of ILI symptoms defined by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) as secondary keywords. Native speakers translated English keywords to their equivalent non-English counterparts and included additional words and expressions colloquially used to convey ILI experiences. Finally, we also added as keywords verbs commonly used to communicate being sick, along with their conjugations, to specify numbers, tenses, and genders of the associated persons. Thus, each extracted tweet contains one primary keyword plus either an ILI symptom or an enlisted verb in the same language. An example each of primary, secondary, and verb keywords in English is “flu”, “fever”, and “caught”, respectively. A detailed keyword listing for each language can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Exceptionally, we also collected tweets in Catalan and Basque languages, widely used in some regions of Spain, by matching the primary keyword for influenza in these languages. We did not apply additional keyword filters due to their low volume of tweets.

We collected tweets for various time periods for each language (Supplementary Table 4). We limited the time period of data collection for tweets in English to 2017-10-29 to 2019-04-29, not including the COVID-19 period, due to a very high volume of tweets matching our query. For the remaining languages we extracted tweets posted in April/May 2023 going back until 2018-09-15. Prior to preparing the annotation guidelines, we reviewed a random sample of tweets from each year for each language. We observed a high volume of tweets between 2020 and 2022 related to general discussions around COVID-19 and influenza in all languages. The increase was significantly more for tweets in French and Italian languages, resulting in a notable reduction in the ILI ratio. Therefore, we extended the data collection period for tweets in French going back until 2011. We did not do the same for tweets in Italian due to the associated costs of attaining tweets after the removal of free academic access to Twitter API as of spring 2023. All data were collected between February and April 2023.

Data annotation

Our goal is to distinguish tweets indicative of someone experiencing ILI from those that are general chatter or awareness related to ILI. Since social media posts rarely provide complete symptom reports required for clinical diagnosis, we adopt an annotation strategy tailored to informal digital health mentions. Thus, tweets indicative of any first, second, and third person reports of recent ILI are annotated as “Likely ILI”. Due to the overlapping symptoms between ILI and COVID-19, tweets clearly suggestive of COVID-19 for the period on and after 2020 are annotated as “Likely COVID-19”. Tweets that indicate infection but are not clear if it is due to ILI or COVID-19 are still annotated as “Likely ILI”. Since the keywords we used to extract tweets follow ILI definition, we believe this to be the best strategy to handle such cases. Due to the brevity of tweets and ambiguity in language, it may often be unclear just from the tweet text if an infection has happened, when it happened, or who might be infected. Such tweets are annotated as “Ambiguous”. Finally, general comments, news, advertisements, and health promotions related to ILI or COVID-19 are considered “Not Related”. One exception to this is news items mentioning an ongoing influenza epidemic affecting humans or confirmed cases of hospitalization due to ILI or COVID-19. Examples of annotated tweets in each language are provided in Table 2.

We grouped tweets starting from year 2018 (2011 for French) into month and year of their post dates and randomly sampled them from each time interval for annotation. Tweets sampled for annotation were anonymized by replacing usernames with generic @user text and URLs with the keyword “URL”. A total of 16 annotators with native language proficiency annotated the same set of 400 (200 in English) tweets into one of the four categories. Another set of five annotators re-annotated a sample of 341 previously annotated tweets in their native language. 20 annotators had a formal academic or professional background in public health, including researchers, postdocs, technicians, and graduate students. One additional annotator, an information technology professional, was also included to meet the native-speaker requirement for one of the study languages. The inter-rater agreement scores between annotators annotating the same set of tweets in the first round using Fleiss Kappa, and the sample of previously annotated tweets in the second round using Krippendorff’s Alpha (Table 5) are 0.71 or higher, suggesting substantial consistency in labelling38. Disagreements were first resolved by reviewing them individually with each annotator and then using majority voting. The authors made the final decision by evaluating the annotators’ reasoning and closely following the annotation guidelines where majority could not be attained.

Due to the insufficient instances in the “Likely COVID-19” and “Ambiguous” categories to properly evaluate them, we conducted a second round of annotation to review them. Since labelling a large number of tweets is costly, time-consuming, and unlikely to resolve the class imbalance issue, we adopted a data preservation strategy to retain any tweets containing trustworthy signals of the “Likely ILI” cases in these two categories. Tweets still labelled as “Ambiguous” were removed from the dataset, and tweets in “Likely ILI” and “Likely COVID-19” categories were merged into a single “Likely ILI or COVID-19” category. The decision to merge these two categories was made due to the substantial overlapping similarities in their symptoms based on keyword usage, preventing clear differentiation. This decision aligns with the World Health Organization (WHO) findings that reported the sensitivity and specificity rate to detect COVID-19 using ILI case definition to be 20–55% and 38–90%, respectively30.

Multilingual masked language models

MLMs are trained on large unlabelled corpus of one or more languages with the objective of predicting masked words in a sentence based on the context provided by the surrounding words15. A major benefit of MMLMs over their monolingual counterpart is their ability to learn cross-lingual representations, especially for languages with shared vocabularies, genetic and/or contact relatedness15. Besides, performance differences between them are typically small, dependent on the task or domain, and can be offset by MMLMs trained on larger datasets23,39. Lower performances are generally observed for languages with lower proportion of pre-training or fine-tuning data, a challenge shared by both MLMs15,23,29,39. Moreover, MMLMs enable CLT by allowing inference in target languages not seen during fine-tuning21,22,23. Thus, MMLMs are often preferred over maintaining several monolingual MLMs.

We selected mBERT, XLM-R, mDeBERTaV3, XLM-T, and Bernice based on their performance on several multilingual downstream tasks, including cross-lingual classification, pre-training data, language coverage, and for research reproducibility15,39. mBERT, XLM-R, and mDeBERTaV3 are trained on Wikipedia or Common Crawl corpus comprising general text in over 100 languages23,29,40,41. They have the highest proportion of pre-training data in English and lowest proportion in Italian.

We also investigated the effects of fine-tuning domain-adapted models, Bernice and XLM-T, as such models have shown to provide better multilingual representation, leading to improved performance on tweet-related downstream tasks24,42. XLM-T is further trained using publicly available checkpoints of XLM-R on 198 million tweets scraped in over 30 languages, whereas Bernice is trained entirely on tweets using a tweet-specific tokenizer. Both models have the highest proportion of pre-training tweets in English, Spanish, and French, and comparatively lower in Italian and German.

Cross-lingual transfer

Fine-tuning MMLMs using labelled data in multiple source languages has data acquisition and annotation costs that increase with the number of languages. CLT can mitigate this cost by eliminating the need of annotated data in each target language. CLT benefits can be achieved through model-transfer and data-transfer strategies.

In model-transfer, an MMLM is fine-tuned on annotated data of a single source language and tested on different target languages. This is possible due to the aligned representations learned during pre-training for languages not seen during fine-tuning23–25. We implemented model-transfer by fine-tuning separate models on each source language to identify which one achieved the best overall performance.

In data-transfer, data of one language is translated to another language using Machine Translation (MT) prior to training or inference. We considered translate-train and translate-test data-transfer strategies that have been shown as effective alternatives to human annotation of multilingual text22,25. To implement these, we first translated the tweets from their original source language into the other four languages using DeepL translator. We chose DeepL due to its high translation quality and reproducibility. For translate-train, we fine-tuned an MMLM on the original tweets of a single language, pooled together with their translations to the other four target languages. We evaluated on the test data in its original source language. For translate-test, MMLMs fine-tuned on tweets of a single source language were evaluated on the test data translated to that language.

In-context learning with LLMs

As the size of both LMs and their pre-training data grew, their ICL ability also emerged. ICL refers to the process of obtaining output directly from the LLMs by providing it with instructions in natural language (mainly English), often supplemented with a few examples to demonstrate the problem and the expected solution33. The input instruction is commonly called prompt, and prompt engineering is an active area of research focused on designing prompts to maximize output performance33–35. Despite the time and costs of implementing LLMs, ICL is worth exploring for multilingual ILI detection task as it can reduce or even eliminate data acquisition and annotation costs15,34.

We selected seven open-weight decoder-only LLMs of varying sizes from Gemma, Qwen, and Llama families. Gemma3 can take both text and images to perform a wide variety of generation tasks28. Qwen2.5 is pre-trained on a dataset of up to 18 trillion tokens and can perform generation tasks, including structured outputs27. Llama3.1 is pre-trained on up to 15 trillion tokens from publicly available and synthetically generated data26.

We created four variations of prompts with instructions for our task in English. The prompts included explicit instruction to reason step-by-step based on illness mentions, contextual recency, and individual health status before generating a JSON-formatted classification and rationale. Additionally, we instructed the models to classify “Likely ILI or COVID-19” and “Not Related” classes as “sick” and “general”, respectively, to simplify the category names for LLMs to output and obtain more coherent results34,35. The various prompts differed only in their use of examples. First, we provided the prompt without any examples (zero-shot). Then, we added two examples of each class (two-shots) in English. Then, we modified the examples to include one example of each class in each language (one-multishot). Finally, we added two examples of each class in each language (two-multishots). The prompt used for two-multishots experiment is provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Experimental set-up

MMLMs are not task-specific by themselves and require fine-tuning with annotated labels to perform specialized tasks. To solve our task of ILI detection in multilingual texts, we formulated a two-category classification problem and fine-tuned variants of MMLMs described in Section Multilingual masked language model using the MuILI dataset. We obtained 60% train, 20% validation, and 20% test datasets by performing a class-based stratified split per language (Supplementary Table 5). For fine-tuning, we replaced the final layer of the pre-trained model network of each MMLM with a linear classification layer, defined binary cross entropy as the loss function, and trained the modified network using training and validation data only. We performed grid search optimization to obtain the best set of hyperparameters (Supplementary Table 4) based on macro F1-score on the validation dataset32.

For CLT experiments, we selected the best performing MMLM on the MuILI dataset and fine-tuned it following the same procedure, differing only in the data as described in Section Cross-lingual transfer.

We also implemented ICL with instruction-tuned versions of LLMs described in Section In-context learning with LLMs. The maximum number of output tokens and the temperature to control the randomness in the LLM output were set to 150 and 0.7, respectively. All other hyperparameters were kept at their default values.

For a comprehensive evaluation of the best performing classifier, we report performance as macro F1-score on the test set of each language separately and pooled together. Macro-F1 provides a balanced evaluation by equally weighting both classes and mitigating inflated performance from the majority class. This is particularly relevant for the Italian language in our dataset, where the “Likely ILI or COVID-19” class comprises only 15% of tweets. For fine-tuned MMLMs, we provide the average and the standard deviation of F1-score over five runs with random seed initialization.

All experiments were performed using Py-Torch, Transformers, and scikit-learn packages on Nvidia V100 and A100 graphics cards.

Operational evaluation

We selected the most efficient classifier and evaluated its potential for ILI surveillance over four European countries. First, we filtered tweets that matched locations within France, Italy, Germany, or Spain. Then, for each country, we further filtered tweets by the primary languages used in that country, including Catalan and Basque languages for Spain. Additionally, English-language tweets geolocated in Germany were included for the 2018/19 ILI season due to the low volume of German-language tweets. For subsequent seasons, English tweets were not available.

Next, we classified the selected tweets for each country and aggregated weekly counts of tweets classified as “Likely ILI or COVID-19” (ILI-tweets hereafter) for each ILI season. In accordance with ECDC guidelines, we defined an ILI season as spanning from week 40 of a given year through week 20 of the following year. We obtained official ILI consultation rates from the European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS). Where ILI rates were not available, as in the case of Germany and for Spain from 2021 onward, we used ARI rates instead. ILI and ARI consultation rates are syndromic indicators, representing population-level trends in respiratory illness rather than laboratory-confirmed diagnoses30.

To evaluate the temporal relationship between ILI-tweets and the official consultation rates, Spearman rank cross-correlation coefficients were calculated for each ILI season across two lag periods. Spearman correlation is robust to non-normal distributions and outliers typical of social media and ILI data. Examinations across two lag periods allow us to assess whether ILI-tweets precede official consultation rates, giving insights into its early-warning potential. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed t-test at the 0.01 level.

Ethical statement

Only publicly available tweets were collected and analyzed for this study. To ensure anonymity, usernames and URLs were replaced with unidentifiable texts. Annotators had access only to the anonymized tweet text and the year of the post. No metadata was provided. For reproducibility and verification, the anonymized annotated tweets are included in the MuILI dataset.
