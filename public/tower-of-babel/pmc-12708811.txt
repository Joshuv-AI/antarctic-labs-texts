Mind-Body Interventions for Comorbid Sleep and Gastrointestinal Concerns
Authors: Elyse R. Thakur, Tran Tran, Brooke A. Duarte, Jamie M. Horrigan, Elizabeth W. Lampe, Hyder Said, Jessica K. Salwen-Deremer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewSleep disturbances are common in individuals with gastrointestinal (GI) disorders. The connection between sleep and GI symptoms is bidirectional and influenced by shared biopsychosocial mechanisms and cognitive-behavioral factors, including alterations in the stress response. Treating sleep disturbances can effectively manage coexisting symptoms. This review explores current research on sleep treatments for GI conditions, emphasizing mind-body interventions that target stress.Recent FindingsBoth pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) benefit patients with sleep disturbance and concomitant GI symptoms; however, they have limitations. Other approaches, including mind-body interventions, may improve sleep quality, reduce GI symptoms, and enhance quality of life. SummaryMind-body interventions can complement other evidence-based approaches for patients with co-morbid sleep disturbance and GI symptoms. However, more rigorous research is warranted to fully understand their benefit. As research evolves, healthcare professionals can guide patients on how to best integrate these approaches into their care.

Introduction

Sleep disturbance is common among individuals with gastrointestinal (GI) disorders, including disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI) and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). DGBI include chronic gastrointestinal disorders characterized by disruptions in gut-brain signaling and the absence of structural or biochemical abnormalities. Definitions of common DGBI, and IBD, and symptom manifestations are outlined in Table 1. Sleep quality has been shown to be two to three times worse in patients with GI disorders [7–10] as compared to the general population [11]. Sleep disorders, such as insomnia, are prevalent among patients with both DGBI [12] and IBD [13]. Other sleep disturbances such as restless leg syndrome and obstructive sleep apnea are also common among patients with DGBI [14, 15]. The relationship between sleep and GI symptoms is best conceptualized within a bidirectional framework, shaped by shared biopsychosocial mechanisms and cognitive-behavioral factors [16]. One factor that is common among patients with GI disorders and those with insomnia is hyperarousal [17], which is a state of increased sympathetic nervous system activation. Heightened stress-related psychological states can result in an increased sensitivity to visceral pain [18] and fatigue [19] and contribute to GI symptom exacerbations in patients with DGBI and increased disease activity among those with IBD. Although mechanisms remain poorly understood, these changes likely stem from disruptions in pain processing pathways, leading to central sensitization and increased systemic inflammation [20–22]. Consequently, uncomfortable GI symptoms may then disrupt sleep [23]. Sleep disruptions can be particularly exacerbated among patients who have psychological comorbidities, such as anxiety, depression, and/or post-traumatic stress disorder [12, 17], and worsen GI symptoms through alterations in the stress response [24]. Moreover, disruptions in sleep and circadian rhythms may increase the risk for DGBI and IBD [25].

Improving Sleep Disturbance is an Important Treatment Target for GI Disorders

Consistent with literature on the relationship between sleep and pain [26], research across GI populations suggests that while sleep disturbances and GI symptoms are bidirectionally related, sleep may be the key driver in this relationship. For example, research using micro-longitudinal designs (e.g., daily diary studies) has demonstrated that poor sleep quality predicts next day abdominal pain in both people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and IBD [27–31]. While some aspects of sleep continuity were clearly bidirectionally associated with GI symptoms across these studies, poor sleep reliably predicted abdominal pain better than abdominal pain predicted poor sleep. Similarly, pilot intervention research suggests that insomnia treatments may improve not only sleep, but also GI symptoms [32, 33]. This focus on sleep is further supported by patient beliefs; research in people with IBD indicates that people want to talk to their gastroenterologists about sleep and believe sleep is an important part of their care experience [34]. Thus, given the degree to which sleep disturbances contribute to GI symptom experiences, sleep interventions represent a highly effective use of resources for people with comorbid sleep disturbances and GI conditions. Below, we review existing literature on sleep interventions for GI conditions, with a focus on emerging evidence related to integrative approaches which target stress to improve GI and sleep-related symptoms. We first introduce pharmacologic agents and then discuss non-pharmacologic treatments including behavior therapies, followed by mind-body interventions such as meditative movement practices, bodywork therapies, and nutritional approaches. Integrative sleep interventions used in GI disorders

Pharmacologic Approaches

Pharmacologic agents targeting sleep are quite popular, with 8.4% of U.S. adults endorsing having taken sleep medications over the past 30 days [35]. This practice is most common in older adults, with one study finding that 35.4% of subjects aged 65 years and older took sleep-related medications on an “occasional” or “regular basis.” [36] For individuals with concomitant GI symptoms, such as abdominal pain, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are utilized given their ability to improve abdominal pain while augmenting sleep duration and overall sleep efficiency [37, 38]. Other commonly used agents include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), although these are prescribed less often than TCAs, largely because of inconsistent evidence supporting their efficacy [39, 40]. Gabapentin and pregabalin have shown some efficacy in augmenting rectal compliance and mechanosensitivity in IBS but lack robust prospective data in improving abdominal pain and other sleep-related outcomes [41].

Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, neuromodulators regulate norepinephrine and serotonin transporters, leading to improvements in mood and visceral hypersensitivity, which are believed to modulate abdominal pain and sleep disturbance in patients with DGBI [28, 42]. For example, Lu et al. found that mirtazapine led to improvements in abdominal pain severity in patients with IBS, which correlated with decreased sleep disturbance [43]. Similarly, Liu et al. demonstrated that low dose amitriptyline led to improvements in epigastric pain and time to fall asleep as compared to pantoprazole [38]. The effects of these neuromodulators on sleep architecture remains less known, with some studies finding that antidepressants lead to changes in sleep quality due to their noradrenergic and serotonergic activity, influencing both REM and slow wave sleep [44, 45]. However, their specific effects on sleep architecture in populations with gut–brain axis dysregulation remain largely unexplored and requires further investigation.

Despite the prevalence of prescription sleep medication use, there has been an increased effort towards patient education regarding potential side effects over the past decade. This has led to a 31% decline in use [46]. In its place, patients have increased their use of over-the-counter sleep supplements, such as melatonin [47], and have begun utilizing non-pharmacologic interventions for sleep enhancement. Although evidence on the effects of sleep-specific supplements in DGBI is limited, Song et al. reported that nightly administration of 3 mg melatonin for two weeks significantly improved abdominal and rectal pain in patients with IBS, independent of changes in sleep quality [48]. Additional studies are needed to clarify the role of sleep-directed therapies in modulating DGBI symptom severity.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

CBT-I is Effective for Improving Sleep Disturbance

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a highly effective treatment for chronic insomnia disorder across populations [49], and clinical guidelines recommended it as a first-line of treatment in Europe and North America [50]. Meta-analyses have suggested that CBT-I can lead to significant and sustainable improvements in insomnia severity [51], as well as quality of life across diverse populations [52]. Moreover, research has indicated that CBT-I can lead to modest but meaningful reductions in chronic pain [53], and there is evidence to suggest that it is the most effective treatment option for individuals with co-morbid insomnia and chronic pain [54]. These findings indicate that CBT-I is a promising approach for patients with co-morbid sleep disturbance and GI symptoms, as sleep disturbance is an important driver of pain in this population.

CBT-I May Also Be Effective for Reducing GI Symptoms

CBT is also one of the most evidence-based brain-gut behavior therapies and is effective in reducing GI symptoms in DGBI [55–62]. Emerging research has explored the potential benefits of CBT-I for sleep and GI symptoms simultaneously. For example, Ballou and colleagues (2020) conducted a pilot RCT to examine the feasibility of a brief CBT-I intervention for insomnia in 25 patients with IBS and self-reported poor sleep [32]. Results indicated high treatment satisfaction, improvements in sleep quality and insomnia severity, and trends toward improvement for IBS-related symptom severity. Another RCT of 60 South Korean college students also demonstrated that CBT-I significantly decreased insomnia severity, unhelpful sleep habits, pre-sleep arousal, and IBS symptoms, while also improving sleep efficiency and IBS-specific quality of life [63]. Finally, a pilot trial demonstrated preliminary improvements in sleep, mood, inflammation and pain management among 10 individuals with IBD following 7-weeks of CBT-I [33]. While these findings are promising, there is limited research in this area. Further research is necessary to substantiate these findings and identify potential adverse effects.

CBT-I Can Improve GI Symptoms By Targeting the Interconnected Physiological Pathways of Sleep, Inflammatory Processes,and Pain Regulation

Poor sleep can lead to hyperalgesia [64, 65], which can heighten visceral hypersensitivity [27, 28] and exacerbate abdominal pain and discomfort. Thus, by enhancing sleep quality, CBT-I has the potential to reduce hyperarousal and central sensitization [66], and alleviate GI symptoms among patients with DGBI. Poor sleep has also been shown to increase inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α) [22], which are associated with certain GI conditions such as IBD. CBT-I has been shown to reduce inflammatory cytokine [67]. , to improve sleep and ease inflammation in this population. Moreover, CBT-I has also been shown to improve other aspects of chronic pain, such pain catastrophizing, pain interference, and pain-coping [68], which are modifiable cognitive-behavioral factors that may influence an individual’s risk for developing GI symptoms.

Pitfalls of CBT-I

While CBT-I is often considered the gold standard for the treatment of insomnia within the context of a wide array of co-occurring conditions, it may not always be the treatment of choice. As detailed below, limitations related to access, cultural appropriateness, and patient factors can influence treatment delivery and appropriateness [69].

First, demand for CBT-I providers drastically exceeds availability [70], and access is further complicated by issues related to feasibility of screening and referral within medical settings. This gap is further widened when identifying providers who have knowledge in both sleep medicine and GI conditions. The rise of non-clinician administered virtual interventions (e.g., Internet-delivered Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia, or iCBT-I) [71] and artificial intelligence (AI) enhanced CBT-I [72] have the potential to increase access, however, there remain unanswered questions related to reach, efficacy, and ethical implementation of these approaches [73].

Another persistent concern with CBT-I, and CBTs more generally, is cultural appropriateness for diverse patients. Core tenants of CBT are grounded in dominant Western cultural models; emphasizing independence over interdependence, assertiveness, verbal ability, and rationality that might run counter to an individual’s cultural norms and values [74]. Racial and ethnic engagement disparities in traditional mental health care services are well known [75] and non-white individuals drop out of CBT interventions at a higher rate that White individuals [76–78]. However, CBT-based interventions are similarly efficacious among racial and ethnic minorities compared to White individuals [74], and recent efforts have focused attention on culturally tailored adaptations.(e.g., 79).

Finally, there is a subset of people for whom CBT-I may not be the most appropriate treatment modality based on patient factors such as treatment history, health care engagement priorities, personal preference, and/or diagnosis and demographic factors. For example, someone who has already received a course of CBT-I and did not find benefit would be unlikely to reengage in CBT-I for a recurring or ongoing problem. Another example may be someone managing comorbid mental and/or physical health conditions who is limited in their ability to engage in the time-commitment of a 6–8 session CBT protocol due to treatment prioritization needs. Finally, research has shown differences in response to CBT-I depending on age, severity of mental health symptoms, number of comorbidities, concurrent antidepressant use, and circadian preference [80].

While CBT-I remains an evidence-based treatment for sleep disturbance in the context of GI conditions, there is a clear need for consideration of other treatment approaches to support individualized and effective treatment for comorbid sleep disturbance and GI conditions.

Gut-directed Hypnotherapy

Another evidence-based brain-gut behavior therapy is gut-directed hypnotherapy, a form of medical hypnosis specific to gastrointestinal conditions. In this treatment, the therapist guides the patients into a “trance-like” state, gives suggestions for autogenic (i.e., self-generated) relaxation, guides the patient through a visualization that corresponds to a gut-related symptom experience (e.g., waves at the beach come and go similar to peristalsis), then offers suggestions designed to alter perceptions of and responses to gut-related sensations. Evidence supporting the use of gut-directed hypnotherapy in refractory IBS dates back several decades, with recent work continuing to support its efficacy in IBS as well as other GI conditions [62, 81, 82]. Theorized mechanisms for GI symptom improvement via gut-directed hypnotherapy include changes in symptom-specific anxiety [83], normalization of pain/sensory thresholds [84], normalization of central processing of visceral stimuli [85], and reduced autonomic nervous system activity [86].

Given the impact of gut-directed hypnotherapy on arousal, it is possible there could also be a positive impact on comorbid sleep disturbance. While literature in this area is nascent, at least one trial to date has demonstrated improvement in sleep quality as a result of gut directed hypnotherapy [83]. In this trial, 119 patients with IBS were randomized to receive gut-directed hypnotherapy either individually or in a group. Patients in both groups experience significant improvements in sleep quality. However, recent reviews on the effectiveness of hypnotherapy for insomnia outside the context of GI conditions are inconclusive, citing heterogeneous and/or small samples and low quality methodology as likely reasons for these findings [87, 88]. Other reviews investigating the impact of hypnotherapy on sleep disturbance more broadly found that about half of the included studies demonstrated improved sleep as a result of hypnotherapy intervention [89, 90]. In addition, the number of studies demonstrating significantly improved sleep increased when sleep disturbance was part of the study inclusion criteria and hypnotic suggestions were sleep specific [89].

Also relevant is emerging literature on the impact of hypnosis on sleep quality in other chronic illness populations. For example, Dumain et al. found that a hypnosis intervention in 14 elderly women with chronic pain resulted not only in decreased pain intensity, but also pain interference, including large effect sizes for improved sleep quality immediately post-intervention and 9 months later [91]. Similarly, in a larger trial (N = 109) Eaton et al. randomized cancer survivors with chronic pain to listen to hypnosis or relaxation recordings [92]. While both interventions resulted in similarly large reductions in pain, only participants in the hypnosis group experienced significant improvements in fatigue and sleep disturbance. Finally, research comparing the impact of hypnotherapy compared to an attentional control in people with fibromyalgia pain demonstrated that hypnotherapy had a superior impact on measures of pain intensity, pain experience, and sleep quality [93]. As such, it is possible that gut-directed hypnotherapy may have effects on overall functioning beyond those specific to GI symptoms.

Meditative Movement Practices

Meditative movement practices, including yoga and tai chi, are mind-body interventions characterized by three core components: postures/movements, breath regulation, and meditation. These elements function synergistically to influence physiological and psychological systems offering potential therapeutic benefits for improving GI symptoms and enhancing sleep quality.

Yoga

Yoga, an ancient Indian practice, is one of the most popular meditative movement practices. There is evidence to support the use of this approach for patients with DGBI, such as IBS. For example, systematic review of six RCTs found yoga to have statistically significant decreases for IBS-related symptoms and anxiety compared to conventional care. Similar improvements in quality of life, global symptoms, and physical functioning were found when comparing yoga with non-treatment controls [94]. Yoga has also been studied beyond DGBI, with research demonstrating GI symptom reduction and safety for IBD, gastrointestinal cancer, and chronic pancreatitis [95]. Although mechanisms are not fully understood, research has shown that yoga reduces stress and arousal [96, 97], modulates immune responses [98], and enhances endogenous endorphin release [99], all of which can have a positive impact on brain-gut regulation and lead to better health outcomes. Research examining the impact of yoga on sleep quality among patients with DGBI is scant; however, one study examined the role of yoga therapy on digestive health and sleep quality in an elderly population with constipation. Ganesh et al. found that participants who engaged in a three-month yoga program (n = 48), experienced significant improvements in both constipation and sleep-related quality of life compared to those in the wait-list control group (n = 33) [100].

Tai Chi

Other forms of meditative movement practices including tai chi, a martial arts-based form of qi gong developed in China thousands of years ago, offer similar health benefits [101]. Tai chi has been shown to improve sleep quality among patients with related chronic conditions including fibromyalgia [102]. Tai chi is less well-studied in GI populations. To our knowledge, one virtual tai chi program was piloted for patients with IBS-C in a single arm trial. Participants engaged in eight, weekly, one-hour group based virtual sessions. Tai chi as described in Table 2 incorporated a component of self-massage, meditation and visualization tailored to IBS-C. Results revealed that this intervention was feasible, and patients reported improvements in IBS symptoms, IBS-related quality of life, abdominal diameter, and leg strength, however sleep outcomes were not examined. While published evidence for the impact of tai chi in other GI conditions is scarce, tai chi has been shown to improve systemic inflammation and lower IL-6 [103]. This outcome is especially relevant for patients with GI conditions, particularly IBD, where inflammation and immune dysregulation are central to disease pathology [104]. Thus, like yoga, tai chi may help to reduce disease activity in IBD [105] which may have a positive effect on sleep quality, although this has not been empirically studied.

Outside of GI and other chronic illnesses, meditative movement practices, including yoga and tai chi, have been shown to improve sleep quality and symptom severity among individuals with insomnia [106], a common comorbidity among GI patients [107]. In a RCT of older adults with insomnia, both Tai chi and CBT were shown to significantly reduce cellular and genomic inflammatory markers, which underscores the therapeutic potential of these approaches for patients with inflammatory-based GI conditions.

Further, it has been demonstrated that a personalized yoga practice improved sleep quality, sleepiness, fatigue, anxiety and depression in insomnia patients, as well as decreased arousal [108]. A personalized practice may be particularly appealing for patients with comorbid GI and sleep disturbance, as group-based practices may be less effective at targeting the unique combination of symptoms experienced by this population. A tailored approach may increase physical activity, reduce hyperarousal and counteract stagnation and atrophy caused by pain and fatigue, which can help to reset circadian rhythms, supporting improved gastrointestinal function and sleep regulation. However, further research is needed to fully understand the utility of meditative movement practices for this population, and how to best apply them.

Bodywork Therapies

Bodywork therapies, such as acupuncture and massage, have been increasingly implemented as mind-body interventions for sleep and GI concerns. Bodywork promotes healing by stimulating specific body parts; making it a highly relevant intervention to address insomnia and GI conditions.

Acupuncture

Meta-analyses of over 50 RCTs indicate that acupuncture, a traditional Chinese medical practice, can improve IBS symptom severity, IBS-specific quality of life, and comorbid anxiety and depression [109–111]. Studies have also suggested that acupuncture can address visceral hypersensitivity by regulating brain-gut peptides, promoting neuroendocrine functions and emotional state, and reducing bowel inflammation and hypersensitivity [112]. Specifically, acupuncture can alleviate chronic abdominal pain in IBS by targeting both peripheral and central pain pathways (e.g., the HPA-axis) [113]. Among IBD populations, acupuncture is theorized to provide symptom relief via the stabilization of inflammatory markers [114].

In addition to its potential benefits for GI conditions, acupuncture has also been investigated in the context of sleep disturbance. Two meta-analyses demonstrated that acupuncture improved total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and sleep architecture in patients struggling with primary insomnia [115, 116]. In addition, acupuncture has demonstrated effectiveness in obstructive sleep apnea by reducing hypoxia and inflammation, and decreasing daytime sleepiness [117]. While there are ongoing trials exploring acupuncture in GI populations with sleep outcomes as one of the measures [118], no results have been published to date. Thus, this indicates a fundamental direction for future research.

Massage

There is emerging evidence suggesting that different massage therapy approaches can also aid GI concerns by stimulating peristalsis, improving circulation, and promoting autonomic regulation [119]. Abdominal massage (AM) has been suggested as a safe and effective approach to multiple gastrointestinal functions across different populations (i.e., elderly individuals, ICU patients, post-orthopedic surgery, and individuals with multiple sclerosis) [120]. A systematic review of 10 RCT’s indicated that while short term AM (1–7 days) significantly reduced constipation, gastric residual volume, and abdominal distention, long-term AM (3–8 weeks) significantly reduced abdominal pain and constipation severity, improved quality and quantity of bowel movements, and promoted overall improvements in gastrointestinal functioning [120]. An experimental study of Swedish AM of 30 critically ill ICU patients also found that this approach significantly reduced gastric residual volume and abdominal discomfort [121]. Beyond AM, a meta-analysis of eight RCTs demonstrated that Tuina- a Chinese massage technique involving pressing, kneading, and rolling on acupoints, which resembles some aspects of acupuncture- meta-analysis of eight RCTs found that Tuina improved symptoms among patients with IBS-D when combined with routine treatment [122].

Full-body massage techniques have been shown to be effective at addressing sleep disturbance. ARCT examining the impact of a full-body Swedish massage in patients with multiple sclerosis (n = 70) found that this intervention significantly improved sleep quality, pain reduction, and psychological issues (i.e., anxiety and depression) after six weeks [123]. Other forms of massage have also been useful for sleep disturbance. A meta-analysis of 10 RCTs demonstrated that foot reflexology, significantly improved sleep quality among patients who were critically ill (i.e., admitted to the coronary care unit, intensive care unit, or burn unit; d = 1.02) [124].

There have been few studies examining sleep and outcomes concurrently. However, a quasi-experimental study found that aroma foot massage was associated with enhanced sleep quality, increased defecation frequency, and reduced constipation severity for older adults after four weeks [125]. It is possible that foot reflexology, may improve sleep and constipation by stimulating foot reflex points linked with the digestive system. This stimulation may enhance blood and lymphatic circulation, and ameliorate stress and anxiety; however, more research is warranted to fully understand the underlying mechanisms.

Nutritional Approaches

Dietary Changes

Nutritional approaches in the context of GI conditions are complex and individualized. To our knowledge, there are no studies that have investigated the impact of specific diets on sleep in patients with GI conditions. To improve nocturnal symptoms and sleep in patients with GERD, the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends avoiding late night meals, trigger foods (high fat foods, acidic foods, carbonated beverages, etc.,) and sleeping with the head of the bed elevated [126]. There are many diets that have been shown to improve symptoms of IBD. The American Gastroenterological Association recommends that patients with IBD follow a Mediterranean diet focusing on fresh foods, and decreasing processed food intake [127]. In patients with IBS, soluble fiber supplementation is recommended to improve symptoms. A trial of the low FODMAP (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) diet is also recommended by ACG to help improve IBS symptoms [128].

Meal Timing

Furthermore, meal timing and consistency may influence sleep quality. One study found that inconsistent breakfast or dinner times in patients with IBD was associated with markers of circadian misalignment, including greater sleep dept, increased social jet lag, and later chronotype [129]. Similarly, another study by Fowler et al. found that disrupting circadian rhythms, such as through irregular meal timing, can decrease sleep quality in patients with DGBIs and increase symptoms. This is partially thought to be due to irregular meal timing influencing melatonin production [130].

Dietary Supplements

While there is little known about the impact of specific diets on sleep in patients with GI conditions, there is more research available about dietary supplements on sleep. Magnesium deficiency is common in patients with Crohn’s disease and has been associated with poor sleep and increased intestinal inflammation. Magnesium supplementation may improve sleep quality by reducing sleep latency and lengthening sleep duration [131, 132]. Similarly, in patients with IBS, magnesium orotate may modulate the gut-brain axis, potentially improving anxiety, depression, pain, and sleep quality. Mechanisms proposed include altering the gut microbiome, decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and acting as a cofactor for synthesizing neurotransmitters such as serotonin [133]. Furthermore, melatonin supplementation may improve sleep quality and symptom burden, including pain, in patients with IBS [134, 135]. In IBD, melatonin may strengthen the intestinal mucosal barrier, improve dysbiosis, and decrease inflammation in addition to regulating sleep [136]. While melatonin is often sold in high doses, most studies use a dose of 3 mg as standard [137]. Importantly, supplements are not universally regulated and may be contaminated with other substances. Not all supplements have been extensively studied and can lead to adverse effects, drug-induced liver injury, making it important to discuss with a healthcare provider the safety of starting a particular supplement [138, 139]. Thus, there is an acute need to further study the impacts of dietary changes and supplements on sleep disturbance in the context of GI conditions for increased confidence in intervention efficacy and safety.

Conclusions

Mind-body interventions, including meditative movement practices, bodywork therapies, and nutritional approaches, offer a promising complement to care alongside pharmacologic (e.g., neuromodulators) and behavioral approaches (e.g., CBT and/or gut-directed hypnotherapy) for patients with co-morbid sleep disturbance and GI symptoms. Overall, research has shown that mind-body interventions can lead to improved sleep quality and GI symptom reduction. These approaches may attenuate the physiological and psychological impact of chronic stress by targeting risk factors such as pain sensitivity, fatigue, inflammation, sleep disruption, poor dietary habits, and physical inactivity. When sleep quality is improved, and GI symptoms are stabilized, patients may be more able to engage in adaptive coping strategies and health-enhancing behaviors, which may contribute to sustained improvements in overall quality of life.

While our review provides a comprehensive qualitative overview of mind-body interventions for sleep disturbance and GI conditions, more research is sorely needed. Rigorous RCT’s that evaluate the impact of these approaches for patients with co-morbid sleep disturbance and GI symptoms are warranted. In particular, studies that integrate objective measures of sleep and GI disease and which clarify mechanisms of action will be instrumental in improving our understanding of how to care for this population. As research continues to evolve, healthcare professionals can still provide patients with information on these interventions through shared decision-making approaches. Collaborative discussions that consider interventions in the context of disease severity, cultural beliefs, and access to care have the potential to improve health outcomes and the patient experience.
