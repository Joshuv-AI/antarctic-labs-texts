Effect of activated charcoal and subculture rates on a two-phase culture strategy involving biomass accumulation followed by phenolic production in date palm cell suspension cultures
Authors: Mansour A Abohatem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDate palm (Phoenix dactylifera) is an important medicinal plant rich in a wide range of phenolic compounds variety of high antioxidant values. To enhance the production of phenolic compounds in cell suspension cultures, this study examines the impact of activated charcoal (AC) and subculture rates on biomass accumulation and phenolic content.ResultsIndicate that culture medium with or without activated charcoal (AC) and subculture rates have a significant effect on the synthesis of phenolic compounds and the accumulation of biomass. The culture medium without AC showed a substantial increase in the synthesis of phenolic compounds without loss of biomass accumulation. Specifically, the intracellular phenolic content in somatic embryos increased more than threefold, while the extracellular phenolic content in the spent liquid medium rose more than fivefold. Additionally, when subcultured every three weeks, the intracellular phenolic content was fivefold higher and the extracellular phenolic content approximately sixfold higher than when subcultured once a week.The combination of subcultures with and without activated charcoal significantly enhanced the production of phenolic compounds and biomass accumulation. Subculturing every week with charcoal for three weeks, followed by two weeks without charcoal, and then three additional weeks without charcoal, resulted in increased biomass accumulation during the first four weeks and also significantly increased accumulation of intracellular phenolic content by sevenfold and extracellular phenolic content by ninefold in the last 3 weeks of the suspension cultures period.Conclusions This study establishes a reliable protocol for the large-scale production of phenolic compounds from date palm, which serve as raw materials for various pharmaceutical and food industries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07584-6.Keywords: Phoenix dactylifera, Cell suspension culture, Extracellular phenolic content, Intracellular phenolic content, Activated charcoal, Subculture number

Background

The date palm is one of the oldest and most economically important fruit tree species in the world’s hot, arid regions [1]. The fruits of the date palm are a rich source of several phytochemicals, including flavonoids, anthocyanins, tannins, sterols, carotenoids, and procyanidins. Additionally, they contain a variety of phenolic compounds with high antioxidant value. These compounds are particularly sought after because they can stabilize and deactivate free radicals, which helps prevent the harmful effects of these radical molecules. As a result, they play important roles in the prevention of many major chronic diseases prevalent today [2–7]. Phenolic compounds exhibit various functional activities, including antimicrobial effects [8–10], anticancer properties [11, 12], and anti-inflammatory actions [13–15], highlighting their crucial role in maintaining human health [16, 17].

In vitro studies have shown that plant cells can stimulate the production of phenolic compounds, which interfere with cell proliferation [18]. In vitro cell suspension culture is the most effective method for the large-scale production of somatic embryos in date palms [19–21]. Several commercial plant suspension cell cultures from various species have been successfully utilized to produce valuable secondary metabolites at a low cost [22–24]. This method offers a safe and eco-friendly way to enhance the production of bioactive compounds and provides a solid foundation for ongoing manufacturing. It also boasts excellent scalability for producing these compounds [24–26]. The variation in growth conditions significantly influences both the primary and secondary metabolism of plants, leading to numerous changes in the internal and external characteristics of date palms [27, 28].

The addition of abiotic or biotic elicitors to culture media is a primary method used in plant cell cultures. This strategy is effective for enhancing the production of secondary metabolites [29]. Abiotic elicitors are categorized into three groups: physical, chemical, and hormonal [28].

Secondary metabolites, such as phenolic compounds, are produced by plants as a defense mechanism against environmental stress. No studies consider activated charcoal (AC) as an elicitor, but it does act as a potential antioxidant in cell suspension culture. We are the first to study the effects of activated charcoal on the production of phenolic compounds and biomass accumulation in date palm cell suspension cultures [30–32]. Activated charcoal at 0.15 g/L has a positive effect on somatic embryo growth rate by reducing tissue and medium browning, phenolics, and peroxidase activity compared to the medium without charcoal [30–32].

Researchers in previous studies have focused on preventing or reducing in vitro oxidation (browning) by adding activated charcoal to the culture medium [33, 34] and by increasing the frequency of subculturing, which can limit phenolic production [21, 35]. Keeping in mind that additives preventing browning can simultaneously alter the primary and secondary metabolism of plant tissue, activated charcoal can have harmful and beneficial effects on the plant cells in vitro because, according to García and Tabarez [36], its addition to the medium can interfere in the absorption of nutrients considered essential, such as nitrogen, and in the action of endogenous hormones, which are fundamental for plant tissue culture formation. However, due to the adsorption of hormones exuded by plants and toxic metabolites, activated charcoal acts as a potential antioxidant [37]. It is also used to achieve high purity and yield of secondary metabolites, such as paclitaxel, from the plant cell cultures of Taxus chinensis [38].

Cell suspension culture can be influenced by various physical factors such as subculture rates, light, pH, temperature, and shaking speed [21, 39]. To maintain cell viability and ensure their healthy condition over an extended period, these cultures require regular subculturing into a new medium. The cells were able to grow and proliferate for more than five months with consistent subculturing [40, 41]. Similarly, Lo et al. [35] successfully maintained the cell suspension culture of Artemisia annua by subculturing the cells every 16 days for 33 subculture cycles.

This study examined the effectiveness of cell suspension culture of date palm as a model system for investigating the impact of elicitors, such as activated charcoal, and the number of subcultures on biomass accumulation and production of phenolic compounds. The influence of the combination of subculture rates with and without activated charcoal on intracellular and extracellular phenolic content and biomass accumulation in suspension cultures was also studied.

Methods

Callus induction and multiplication

Date palm offshoots of cultivar Sultana from Hadramout Valley were purchased and transported to the laboratory by the Ministry of Agriculture and Irrigation, Sana’a, Yemen, which were used as the plant material.

Shoot tips of date palm were disinfected according to Abohatem and Baaziz [42]. The shoot tips, which consist of very young leaves surrounding the apical dome, were cut into four to six pieces and used as explants for culture in the starting medium. They were cultured on a callogenesis induction medium according to our previous research [43], which contained MS salts [44] and Fossard vitamins [45] with 5 mg/L of 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP), 5 mg/L of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), 150 mg/L activated charcoal, and 30 g/L sucrose. For embryogenic callus induction, the friable callus formed after 6–8 months of culture was selected and transferred onto a multiplication medium containing 0.1 mg/L of BAP and 0.5 mg/L of 2,4-D. The cultures were incubated at 25 ± 2 ◦C in the dark and subcultured to fresh medium every 5 weeks until embryogenic callus was formed.

Establishment of cell suspension culture

For establishing the cell suspension, the method described in our protocol by Abohatem et al. [31] and Zouine and El Hadrami [19] was used in this study. 0.5 g of embryogenic callus was cut into the smallest possible pieces (fine pieces) using a sterile scalpel and then transferred to 50 ml liquid medium in a 250 ml Erlenmeyer flask. The contents of the Erlenmeyer are sieved through sieves with a 500 μm mesh size, and the filtrate is incubated on a rotary shaker (100 rpm) at 25 ± 2 °C under a 16/8-hour photoperiod (light/dark). The liquid medium is MS/2 supplemented with 0.3 mg/L of BAP and 0.1 mg/L of 2,4-D. Two elicitors were used in this study: activated charcoal (AC) and subculture rates. Each elicitor was tested individually, and the combination of subculture rates with or without activated charcoal was tested. AC was evaluated at concentrations of 0.0 (control), 150, 200, and 300 mg/L, and the subculture rates were tested by transferring the cells into fresh medium for one, two, and three weeks.

To measure the accumulation of embryogenic cell biomass (starting from 0.5 g of callus), the fresh weight (FW) of the total embryo biomass was measured and recorded after the 7th week, when somatic embryos reached the stationary growth stage. Extraction of intracellular phenolic compounds from somatic embryos

The extraction of phenolic compounds from date palm somatic embryos was conducted as described by Abohatem and Baaziz [42]. At the end of the seventh week of suspension culture, 250 mg of somatic embryo tissues were homogenized with 2 ml of 80% methanol at 4 °C. The mixture was then centrifuged three times at 7000 g for 3 min, with the supernatants collected each time. The final supernatant was stored in polypropylene tubes at −4 °C and protected from light.

Extraction of extracellular phenolic compounds from spent culture medium

For the extraction of extracellular phenolic compounds, 100 µl of the sample was taken directly from the spent liquid medium after filtration through nylon filters with a pore size of 30 μm, without any preceding extraction step.

Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content of both intracellular and extracellular samples was quantified based on the method described by Abohatem and Baaziz [42]. To 100 µl of each supernatant, 250 µl of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 20% sodium carbonate were added. The mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min, and the resulting blue color was measured at 760 nm. The phenolic content was expressed as catechin equivalents in mg/g of fresh weight (FW). The intensity of browning in the spent liquid medium was visually assessed and recorded as ++, +, or −, indicating high, medium, and low intensity, respectively, according to the scaling method by Pottino [46].

Extraction and analysis of peroxidases

Peroxidases were extracted by homogenizing 250 mg of somatic embryo tissues in a cold mortar using 2 ml of Tris–maleate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.5). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 9000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant collected represented the enzyme extract. Peroxidase activity was measured spectrometrically at 470 nm using guaiacol as the substrate. An aliquot of 20 µl of the enzyme extract was mixed with 2 ml of a reaction mixture containing 0.1 M Tris–maleate buffer (pH 6.5) and 25 mM guaiacol. The reaction was initiated with 20 µl of 10% H2O2 and was stopped after 3 min. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Results were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) test at a significance level of P = 0.05 to compare means. Each set of data included three replicates from two independent experiments.

Results

Effect of activated charcoal on biomass accumulation, intracellular and extracellular phenolic content and peroxidase activities in cell suspension cultures

Different concentrations of activated charcoal significantly influence cell growth. However, higher concentrations of charcoal result in reduced cell growth, indicating that an optimal level of charcoal is necessary for the successful induction of date palm cell suspension culture. The treatment with 150 mg/L of activated charcoal produced the highest fresh weight of biomass, averaging 38.6 ± 4 g. This was followed by the 200 mg/L treatment, which yielded 36.5 ± 2.4 g, and the control group without charcoal, which recorded 34.9 ± 1.7 g. No significant differences were observed in biomass among the treatments with 150 mg/L and 200 mg/L of charcoal compared to the control. However, increasing the concentration of activated charcoal to 300 mg/L resulted in a significant decrease in fresh biomass weight to 26.5 ± 2.9 g (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Activated charcoal is commonly used to adsorb phenolics and reduce oxidized phenols in cell suspension cultures, which can cause tissue browning and decrease the viability of the cultures. At the end of the seventh week, intracellular phenolic content and peroxidase activity were assessed in the biomass, while extracellular phenolic content was measured in the culture medium.

The addition of activated charcoal at varying concentrations resulted in a decrease in phenolic content in both the tissue and the spent liquid medium (Fig. 2), accompanied by a reduction in peroxidase activity. Notably, as the concentration of activated charcoal increased, both phenolic content and peroxidase activity showed a corresponding decline. In contrast, significant increases in intracellular phenolic content occurred in somatic embryos, and extracellular phenolic content increased in the spent liquid medium, in the absence of activated charcoal. Specifically, extracellular phenolic content in the spent liquid medium increased more than fivefold, rising from 0.27 ± 0.04 mg/g FW with 150 mg/L of activated charcoal to 1.4 ± 0.25 mg/g FW in the medium without activated charcoal (Table 2). Similarly, intracellular phenolic content in the tissue of somatic embryos increased more than threefold, from 0.15 ± 0.02 mg/g FW with 150 mg/L of activated charcoal to 0.48 ± 0.04 mg/g FW without activated charcoal (Table 1). The highest levels of phenolic content (0.48 ± 0.04 mg/g FW) and peroxidase activity (86.9 ± 10.5 UE/g FW) were observed in the medium without charcoal. Conversely, the lowest levels of phenolic content (0.09 ± 0.03 mg/g FW) and peroxidase activity (37.1 ± 3.8 UE/g FW) were recorded in the medium containing 300 mg/L of activated charcoal.

Effect of subculture rates on intracellular and extracellular phenolic content and biomass accumulation in the suspension cultures

The duration of the subculture period significantly affected the growth of suspension cultures. Specifically, subculturing weekly had a notably positive impact on somatic embryo development and minimized browning compared to subculturing every two or three weeks (Fig. 3). Weekly subcultures showed a steady increase in biomass from the second to the sixth week of culture. The highest fresh weight of somatic embryo biomass was recorded at 36.8 ± 5.3 g for weekly subcultures, followed by 21.6 ± 4.1 g for bi-weekly subcultures, and the lowest at 13.5 ± 3.1 g for tri-weekly subcultures (Table 3).

In this study, the cell suspension cultures that were subcultured every three weeks (Fig. 3C) exhibited higher levels of intracellular and extracellular phenolics compared to those that were subcultured weekly (Fig. 3A). Discussion

Effect of activated charcoal on intracellular and extracellular phenolic content and biomass accumulation in the suspension cultures

The culture medium without AC showed a significant increase in the synthesis of phenolic compounds without losing biomass accumulation. There was no significant difference in biomass accumulation between cultures treated with 150 mg/L of AC and those without AC. However, increasing the concentration of AC to 300 mg/L resulted in a notable decrease in the fresh weight of biomass.

The positive and negative effects of AC largely depend on its adsorptive properties [47]. AC features a fine porous network with a large inner surface area that can adsorb various substances [33]. The optimal level of AC contributes to biomass accumulation primarily by irreversibly adsorbing inhibitory compounds in the culture medium, which significantly reduces phenolic exudation, toxic metabolites, and brown exudate accumulation [33]. In contrast, high concentrations of AC may decrease biomass accumulation by adsorbing essential mineral nutrients and plant growth regulators (PGRs) from the culture medium [47]. Additionally, the concentration and type of PGRs, as well as the ratio of auxins to cytokinins, have a significant impact on biomass accumulation in date palm suspension cultures [48].

The culture medium without AC showed a significant increase in intracellular phenolic content in somatic embryos and extracellular phenolic content in the spent liquid medium.

There are three important results correlated with using the culture medium without AC in this study. The first significant result is the substantial accumulation of extracellular phenols, which was more than fivefold in the spent liquid medium. Plant cells in suspension cultures have the potential to release biologically active chemicals through the cell wall and membrane into the intercellular spaces or the spent medium [49]. This process simplifies the extraction and purification of these compounds, resulting in a high degree of purity with minimal losses after downstream processing [50].

The second important finding is the considerable accumulation of intracellular phenols, exceeding threefold in the biomass of cell suspension cultures free of AC. AC is typically added to adsorb phenolics and limit oxidized phenolics in the culture medium [35, 51, 52]. However, the most critical impact of adding AC is a drastic reduction in the concentration of plant growth regulators (PGRs) and other organic supplements, which are used as elicitors to produce phenolics and flavonoids [53]. For example, date palm cell suspension cultures grown with 5 mg/L of 2,4-D and 2.5 mg/L of 2iP yielded the maximum total phenolic content compared with 1 mg/L 2,4-D + 2.5 mg/L 2iP [48]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the culture medium without AC will facilitate a significant increase in the exudation of extracellular phenolic compounds and also enhance the production of intracellular phenolic compounds in somatic embryos.

The third important result is that the production of phenolic compounds was stimulated without a loss of biomass. High secondary metabolism is often negatively correlated with cell growth [54]. However, in this study, phenolic compound production was increased without sacrificing biomass, making this approach more suitable for obtaining valuable phenolic compounds from in vitro plant cell cultures. These results represent a breakthrough in the large-scale production of phenolic compounds.

Effect of the subculture rates on intracellular and extracellular phenolics content and biomass accumulation in the suspension cultures

The duration of subculture significantly affects the synthesis of phenolic compounds and biomass accumulation in suspension cultures. Conducting subcultures weekly positively influences the biomass of somatic embryos while also decreasing phenolic compound production, compared to subcultures every two or three weeks. Increasing the frequency of subculturing to fresh medium may be an effective strategy to lower phenolic compound levels, along with the soluble fractions of polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase [21]. The observed positive correlation between the high rate of subculturing and the growth of somatic embryos can be attributed to the reduced levels of phenolic compounds in the tissues and the culture medium [55]. This reduction leads to a decrease in tissue browning intensity and promotes cell proliferation, thereby enhancing somatic embryo production [34, 56].

In this study, a lower subculture frequency of every three weeks resulted in higher levels of both intracellular and extracellular phenolics when compared to weekly subcultures. There is a strong correlation between a lower subculture rate and higher levels of phenolics and peroxidase activity. A subculture interval of 20 days tends to increase the phenolic content in cell suspension cultures, leading to reduced proliferation of embryogenic cells [21]. Fewer subcultures in cell suspension cultures are associated with increased levels of phenolic compounds that accumulate in the tissues and the liquid culture medium [55]. This accumulation leads to the formation of quinones, which, through polymerization, exacerbate tissue browning and inhibit cell proliferation [34, 56]. The oxidation of phenolic compounds is linked to an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [57, 58] and the generation of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [59, 60]. This interaction occurs between the phenolic substrates and the enzymes peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase [21, 61]. In the case of date palms, tissue browning is associated with the high content of caffeoyl shikimic acids in the explants, which serve as effective substrates for oxidases [62]. Phenolic compounds are synthesized naturally in plants as a defense mechanism in response to stress. The total phenolic content and radical scavenging activity were increased in the 50 mg/L salicylic acid-treated cell culture of date palm medium [63].

Effect of the combination of subculture rates with and without activated charcoal on intracellular and extracellular phenolic content and biomass accumulation in the suspension cultures

The effects of combining different subculture rates, with and without activated charcoal (AC), on the intracellular and extracellular phenolic content, as well as biomass accumulation in suspension cultures, were examined.

The treatment (M3) began with weekly subculturing for three weeks using AC, followed by one subculture for two weeks without AC, and then one subculture for three weeks without AC. This approach resulted in an increased biomass accumulation during the first four weeks and a significant increase in intracellular phenolic content in the biomass of somatic embryos, which rose about sevenfold. Additionally, the extracellular phenolic content in the spent liquid medium increased approximately ninefold during the last three weeks of the culture period.

Evidence suggests that the production of secondary compounds in cell suspension cultures is most effective during the stationary growth stage. Therefore, an effective strategy for producing secondary compounds involves establishing suspension cultures using a two-stage method. This method first focuses on optimizing biomass accumulation in an appropriate medium before transferring the cells to a production medium that stimulates the synthesis of secondary compounds [29].

In the first stage, a protocol for producing secondary compounds requires, first and foremost, achieving high biomass accumulation during the exponential growth phase of plant cell suspension cultures. This can be accomplished by selecting the optimal medium and culture conditions. In this study, the significant biomass accumulation observed was attributed to the positive effects of weekly subculturing with AC during the initial three weeks, which improved the growth rate of globular embryos and reduced medium and tissue browning [21, 31].

In the second stage, the addition of elicitors to the culture medium during the peak yield period of the exponential growth phase is a primary method for producing secondary compounds. Our previous studies described the developmental stages of embryogenic cells in date palm suspension cultures [30, 31]. Cells in these cultures actively divided and formed globular embryos during the second and third weeks, eventually converting to cotyledonary embryos in the third and fourth weeks [30]. This information helped identify the optimal growth stage for adding elicitors to enhance the production of phenolic compounds from cell suspension cultures.

In this study, the increased accumulation of intracellular and extracellular phenolic content during the last three weeks of the suspension culture period was attributed to the combined effects of the two-week subculturing without AC, followed by subculturing for three weeks without AC. These findings demonstrate an efficient strategy that can be applied as a protocol for the large-scale production of phenolics from cell suspension cultures of date palms and other plants.

Conclusions

This study presents an efficient protocol for the large-scale production of phenolic compounds from the cell suspension of date palm at the shake flask scale. This methodology holds significant biotechnological value for the development of bioreactor-scale production of pharmaceutical phenolic compounds. The findings indicate an effective strategy for producing phenolic compounds by implementing a two-stage culture system. In the first stage, cell suspension cultures are multiplied in an optimal medium to produce biomass, with weekly subculturing with charcoal for four weeks. In the second stage, these cultures are transferred to a production medium designed to induce the synthesis of phenolic compounds. This phase includes two weeks of subculturing without activated charcoal, followed by three weeks of further subculturing without activated charcoal. This strategy can be applied to date palm and other plants.
