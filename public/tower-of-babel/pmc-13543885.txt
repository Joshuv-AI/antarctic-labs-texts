Clinical decision support tool evaluation: Correction of direct oral anticoagulant dosing errors by clinical pharmacists and physicians
Authors: Anish Saraswat, Shawna N Smith, Michael P Dorsch, Morris Fabbri, Jacob Seagull, Seo Youn Choi, Michael Lanham, Geoffrey D Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInappropriate dosing occurs in up to 20% of direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) prescriptions and is associated with bleeding, hospitalization, and death. Understanding why DOAC dosing errors arise after the initial prescribing period and how clinicians decide to adjust doses may help improve future antithrombotic stewardship efforts.ObjectivesThis study aimed to describe responses by clinicians to asynchronous electronic health record notifications regarding inappropriate DOAC prescriptions that develop after the initial prescribing period.MethodsAs part of a larger randomized trial to improve evidence-based DOAC prescribing, we conducted a manual chart review to assess reasons for inappropriate DOAC prescriptions, clinician response, and reasons for not changing DOAC prescriptions.ResultsMost apixaban prescribing errors occurred in patients aged 80 years or older (87.8%). Overall, dose changes occurred after 32.7% of notifications. Notifications to increase the dose of apixaban resulted in fewer dose changes (16.8%) than those to reduce the dose of apixaban (52%), increase the dose of rivaroxaban (43.2%), or decrease the dose of rivaroxaban (43.6%). The original DOAC prescribers were less likely than pharmacists to document a reason for not making a dose change (46.1% vs 90.5%). Renal function, weight, and bleeding-related reasons were commonly cited as reasons not to make a dose change.ConclusionVarious patient factors differentially influenced the likelihood that a clinician would change an inappropriate DOAC prescription. These factors can be used to tailor electronic health record-based notifications to reduce the burden on prescribers while maximizing the impact on patient safety by increasing evidence-based prescribing.Keywords: anticoagulants, apixaban, clinical decision support, pharmacists, rivaroxaban

Introduction

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have similar or superior efficacy and safety to older anticoagulant medications, such as vitamin K antagonists (eg, warfarin), for reducing thromboembolic complications of atrial fibrillation (AF), venous thromboembolism (VTE), and similar conditions [1,2]. Despite more than a decade of clinical experience with DOACs as first-line oral anticoagulant agents, inappropriate dosing occurs in up to 20% of cases [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Inappropriate DOAC dosing is associated with a higher rate of adverse events like bleeding, hospitalization, and death compared with on-label prescribing [3,4,8]. Reasons for these inappropriate DOAC doses include complex dosing criteria based on renal function, age, and body weight, variable dosing based on the incorrect indication, and underappreciated drug-drug interactions (DDIs) [9].

Clinical decision support (CDS) tools, such as medication notifications implemented within the electronic health record (EHR), have been proposed as an effective strategy to reduce prescribing errors. Several studies have shown positive effects of CDS tools on patient outcomes, including reduced adverse drug events [[10], [11], [12]]. However, poorly designed tools can lead to false positives and clinically insignificant messages, leading to clinician fatigue and overrides of potentially important notifications [13,14]. One study showed that 73.3% of medication notifications were overridden by clinicians, and 40% of these overrides were inappropriate [15]. In particular, several design flaws have been noted in DOAC-related medication CDS tools. These tools often only include low-yield information without actionable options for the end user, occur only at the time of prescribing (eg, ignoring later clinical changes that may warrant a DOAC dose change), and generally do not promote clinical prescriber-pharmacist collaboration [16].

A recently completed cluster-randomized implementation trial compared the effectiveness of sending automated asynchronous EHR notifications to the original DOAC prescriber vs a clinical pharmacist working in an anticoagulation clinic to determine which group was more effective at correcting inappropriate DOAC prescriptions [17]. Despite user-centered notification design that provided high-yield information with actionable tools, across both arms, only 23.2% of these notifications resulted in a prescription change within 7 days [18]. In this post hoc analysis, we assessed which clinical situations triggered notifications, how the dose-change rate differed by notification type and recipient, and why prescribers and pharmacists did not implement the recommended dose changes.

Methods

Trial Design

This follow-up study used data from a larger 2-arm comparative effectiveness randomized implementation trial that has previously been reported (NCT05351749) [17,18]. This trial, which compared the effectiveness of sending asynchronous notifications about an inappropriate DOAC prescription to the original prescriber vs a clinical pharmacist, ran from May 4, 2023 to Dec 15, 2024 and was conducted within 1 large academic health care system with an active anticoagulation clinic staffed by clinical pharmacists and nurses. Briefly, an automated EHR notification was triggered when a clinical change (eg, worsened renal function, a new DDI) rendered the dose of an existing DOAC prescription inappropriate (Figure 1). This notification was routed to either the original DOAC prescribing clinician or an anticoagulation clinical pharmacist and showed up as a direct message in their inbox to review. The recipient of the notification was determined by randomization at the prescribing clinician level. The primary aim of the trial was to compare if sending the notification to a prescriber or a pharmacist was more effective at improving appropriate DOAC prescribing. Regardless of the initial recipient, notifications explicitly encouraged prescriber-pharmacist collaboration. The notifications were also created using a user-centered design process, which has been shown to improve the effectiveness of CDS tools [16].

An example of a prescriber-directed notification. MiChart is the name used for the Epic electronic health record (EHR) at the study center. CrCl, creatinine clearance, eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate; FDA, Food and Drug Administration.

Prescriptions were deemed inappropriate if they did not follow the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) package label instructions or contained a clinically significant DDI (Supplemalentary Material 1). For example, apixaban is normally dosed at 5 mg twice daily for reduction of stroke risk in AF. However, if at least 2 of the 3 dose reduction criteria (age ≥ 80 years, body weight ≤ 60 kg, serum creatinine (Cr) level ≥ 1.5 mg/dL) are met, the dose is reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily. Rivaroxaban is normally dosed at 20 mg daily for reduction of stroke risk in AF but reduced to 15 mg daily if the dose reduction criteria are met (Cr clearance rate ≤ 50 mL/min). The Cr clearance rate was calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation with actual body weight [19].

This trial targeted DOAC prescriptions written in an ambulatory setting for adult patients. DOAC prescriptions written in an emergency department, inpatient hospital, or skilled nursing facility setting were excluded. Notifications focused on incorrect prescriptions for apixaban and rivaroxaban, the most widely used DOACs in the United States. Of note, pharmacists in our institution’s health system do not have independent prescribing authority. Instead, they prescribe and adjust medications under collaborative practice agreements with supervising physicians.

Data collection and outcomes

To better understand when and why certain notifications were effective in prompting prescribers and/or pharmacists to change inappropriate DOAC dosages, we completed a post hoc chart review of prescription changes and health care team member documentation (including prescribers, pharmacists, nurses, and medical doctors (MDs)/doctors of osteopathic medicine (DOs)/nurse practitioners (NPs)/and physician assistants (PAs) other than the original DOAC prescriber involved in the patient’s care) in response to notifications. One study team member (A.S. [medical student] or M.F. [research associate]) conducted a manual chart review for each notification, with review by a second study team member (G.D.B. [attending cardiologist]) to address areas of ambiguity (eg, if a specific situation would be classified as a dose change or how to classify different reasons for not making a dose change). Broadly, manual chart review identified the notification category, the reason for the notification, and the specific actions taken by any member of the health care team in response to the notification, including changes to the dose or documentation of the reason for not changing the dose. Further details about the variables that were evaluated via chart review for each notification are shown below.

Category of notification

Two major categories of notifications were identified. (1) Incorrect DOAC dose notifications, which identified a dose that was too high or too low based on clinical characteristics like renal function, age, or body weight; and (2) DDI notifications, which identified a potential interaction between a DOAC and another medication that the patient was taking.

Notification triggers

A chart review was conducted to determine the clinical situation that triggered each notification (Supplementary Material 1). For incorrect DOAC dose notifications, triggers included an age, weight, and/or renal function value over/under a dosing threshold. For DDI notifications, triggers included the specific interacting medication.

Dose changes

A chart review was conducted to determine whether a DOAC dose change occurred after the notification and, if so, how many days after the notification it occurred. The notification was considered to have resulted in a dose change if any change was made in the dosage, frequency, or type of medication prescribed when compared with the DOAC prescription that triggered the notification. A discontinued DOAC medication was also considered a change. The number of days until a change was made was extended beyond 7 days (as prespecified in the original protocol) because post hoc analysis found that some cases required >7 days to coordinate communications among various health care team members and the patient. If the dose change occurred after 30 days (up to 90 days), it was included only if a reliable link was found between the notification and the dose change (eg, the person who made the dose change viewed the notification on the day of the change, or follow-up documentation was created in response to the notification on the day of the dose change).

Reasons for not making a recommended dose change

When a dose change was not made in response to a notification, additional manual chart review was conducted to determine whether there was any documented reason not to make the recommended dose change.

Finally, we compared whether the dose change rate differed based on (1) whether the notification was initially sent to the original DOAC prescriber or to an anticoagulation pharmacist and (2) for different types of notifications. Within these categories, we compared the reasons given for not making a recommended dose change. All results will be shown by notification type (incorrect dose vs DDI).

Frequency data are presented with the t-test and chi-squared test, as appropriate, using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1). Statistical significance was set at P < .05. This study was reviewed by the Michigan Medicine Institutional Review Board as exempt for EHR data abstraction and a waiver of documented consent for clinician participation and randomization.

Results

Types of notifications

During the study period, 435 longitudinal notifications were sent regarding 361 patients. We excluded 14 (3.2%) notifications because those patients were hospitalized within 7 days of the notification and 1 (0.2%) notification due to a technical error. Of the remaining 420 notifications, 395 (94%) were for patients with AF and 25 (6%) were for patients with VTE. A total of 379 (90.2%) were classified as incorrect DOAC dose notifications, while 41 (9.8%) were classified as DDI notifications. All incorrect DOAC dose notifications (N = 379) were for patients with AF. Of the 41 DDI notifications, 16 (39%) were for patients with AF and 25 (61%) were for patients with VTE.

Most incorrect dose notifications were for apixaban-treated patients (287/379, 75.7%), including 102 (35.5%) when the apixaban dose was too high and 185 (64.5%) when the apixaban dose was too low. Dosing errors for rivaroxaban triggered notifications in 92 of 379 (24.3%) cases, with 55 (59.8%) for doses that were too high and 37 (40.2%) for doses that were too low (Figure 2).

Incorrect DOAC dose notification dose changes—rate and timing

This study’s primary results paper focused on dose changes made during the first 7 days following a notification [18]. Extending this window to within 90 days of the notification as noted in the Methods section, 124 of 379 (32.7%) of incorrect DOAC dose notifications sent to either a clinical pharmacist or a prescriber resulted in a change. This results did not vary significantly by recipient: 62 of 200 (31%) of the incorrect DOAC dose notifications sent to a clinical pharmacist resulted in a dose change, compared with 62 of 179 (34.6%) when sent to a prescriber. Most dose changes occurred within 1 to 7 days of the notification, including 41 of 62 (66.1%) dose changes for pharmacists and 48 of 62 (77.4%) for prescribers (Figure 4). Median time to dose change was 4 days (IQR, 1-12.25) in the pharmacist arm and 3 days (IQR, 0.75-6.25) in the prescriber arm, a difference that was not statistically significant (P = .74).

Documented reasons for not making a dose change following an incorrect dose notification

Of the 255 incorrect dose notifications that did not result in a change, 179 (70.2%) had a clearly documented reason for not changing the dose, with pharmacists significantly more likely to document (125/138 [90.5% notifications]) than prescribers (54/117 [46.1%]) (P < .001).

Table 1 summarizes documented reasons for not making a dose change following an incorrect DOAC dose notification, both overall and by prescriber/pharmacist recipient. The most common reasons for not making a dose change were related to renal function (76/179, 42.5%) and weight (38/179, 21.2%) thresholds. For example, one pharmacist noted, “Patient has fluctuating renal function with current (serum creatinine level) of < 1.5 mg/dL but has frequently been above that…given this, it is reasonable to continue on the lower dose. Will defer alert for 1 year and re-evaluate renal function at that time.” In another example, when a patient’s weight had crossed the 60 kg threshold but remained borderline, the provider elected to recheck the patient’s weight 1 month later, after which the patient’s weight returned to baseline.

Other reasons cited for not making a dose change in response to a notification included patient preference (14/179, 7.8%), medical history (15/179, 8.4%), adverse events on the recommended dose (14/179, 7.8%), and fall risk (12/179, 6.7%). No significant differences in reasons documented were seen between study arms (Table 1). Additionally, in 27 of 179 (15.1%) notifications, documentation included patient and/or provider hesitations to make a dose change due to bleeding risk.

DDI notification summary

There were 41 DDI notifications. Triggering medications included nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (32/41, 78%), posaconazole (3/41, 7.3%), phenytoin (1/41, 2.4%), carbamazepine/amitriptyline (1/41, 2.4%), dronedarone with CrCl < 80 (1/41, 2.4%), and verapamil with CrCl < 80 (3/41, 7.3%).

In total, 4 of 20 (20%) pharmacist DDI notifications and 2 of 21 (9.5%) prescriber notifications resulted in a dose change. Of the 35 DDI notifications that did not result in a dose change, 13 (37.1%) had a clear reason for not making a change documented (Table 2). Pharmacists were again more likely to document, with 11 of 16 (68.8%) pharmacist-directed notifications and 2 of 19 (10.5%) prescriber-directed notifications having a documented reason for not making a recommended change (P < .0004). The most common reason cited for not making a dose change was that another provider had already spoken with the patient about the interaction (9/13, 69.2%).

Discussion

This analysis highlights both the frequency of DOAC-related prescribing problems that develop after a correct prescription has been written and the varying responses to the resulting inappropriate prescriptions. Inappropriate DOAC prescriptions developed more commonly from changes in age, weight, and/or renal function issues than from DDIs. Overall, both clinical pharmacy specialists and prescribing clinicians made similar rates of prescription changes, but the level of documentation varied widely. Importantly, clinical pharmacy specialists overwhelmingly documented reasons for not making a dose change, whereas prescribing clinicians did not document reasons for over half of all inappropriate DOAC prescribing notifications.

The majority of apixaban prescribing errors occurred in patients over 80 years old who had some variation in weight or renal function that pushed them past a dosing threshold. Other studies have also shown that older age is frequently associated with inappropriate DOAC prescribing [3,6,20]. Increased longitudinal monitoring of DOAC dosing in this population is vital and guideline-recommended, as an aging population is more likely to have clinical changes like shifts in renal function that lead to errors in existing DOAC prescriptions [21].

Most DOAC notifications were triggered by inappropriate dosing based on renal function, age, and body weight. A minority of the notifications were triggered by DDIs. Of note, an EHR alert for the nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and DOAC DDIwas in place prior to the initiation of this research study, and most nirmatrelvir/ritonavir interactions had already been addressed by a provider before the notification was sent for this study. As such, removing these notifications in the future would likely reduce alert fatigue. Given the overall lower rate of change for DDIs than dosing errors in our study, health systems may wish to use other approaches for monitoring that do not add to the prescribing clinician’s burden.

The most common subtype of notification identified an apixaban underdosing error. Previous studies have shown that when off-label dosing of DOACs occurs, underdosing is much more common than overdosing [3,6,[22], [23], [24]]. Some studies have also shown that underdosing is more prevalent for apixaban than for other DOACs [3,20,24]. This observation could be related to the perceived low risk of bleeding associated with apixaban and therefore, its preferential use (and often underdosing) for patients at increased risk of bleeding [25,26].

Dose changes in response to notifications

A similar proportion of pharmacist- and prescriber-directed notifications resulted in a dose change. Most dose changes occurred in the first 7 days after the notification. Later dose changes generally occurred due to communication delays among health care team members, waiting for laboratory or weight rechecks, or delays in contacting the patient.

For both pharmacist- and prescriber-directed notifications, more changes decreased the apixaban dose than increased it. This finding may reflect a lack of understanding of how inappropriate underdosing affects bleeding and thromboembolic risk. Previous studies have shown that clinicians and patients sometimes focus on the potential harms of treatment, even when the potential benefits are equal to or greater than the harms. For example, the fear of potential bleeding events is commonly cited as the reason for using lower than recommended doses of anticoagulation [27]. Furthermore, the FDA did not approve a lower dose of dabigatran from the randomized evaluation of long-term anticoagulation therapy trial due to concerns that clinicians would favor the lower dose to minimize bleeding risk, leading to an excess of thromboembolic events [28].

In addition, several studies have shown that underdosing of DOACs is associated with increased adverse events like cardiovascular hospitalization, stroke, myocardial infarction, or death [3,4,29]. One meta-analysis showed that DOAC underdosing does not reduce bleeding risk but is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [30]. Another meta-analysis showed a higher risk of stroke without a reduction in bleeding for atrial fibrillation patients who were underdosed with a DOAC [31]. Including information about how underdosing and overdosing affect thromboembolic and bleeding risk directly in CDS tools, like notifications, may help reduce health care provider fears that lead to off-label dosing.

Reasons for not making a dose change

Common reasons for not making a dose change in response to a notification included renal function-related reasons, weight-related reasons, patient preference, medical history, adverse events, fall risk, and advanced age. In addition, in many cases no documentation was created in response to the notification. Previous work has shown that similar characteristics are common in those treated with off-label DOAC doses, including older age, higher bleeding scores, frailty, mild to moderate renal dysfunction, medical history including bleeding, and use of medications that increase bleeding risk [3,32]. Furthermore, several of the categories of reasons for not making a dose change identified in this study, like “patient preference,” “medical history,” “adverse event,” “fall risk,” and “advanced age,” can be grouped under a broader category such as “patient/provider concern over bleeding risk.” As above, including materials in CDS tools to improve patient and provider education about bleeding and thromboembolic risk is likely to improve compliance rates.

Prescribers were much more likely than pharmacists to make no dose change without documenting a reason why. Not documenting the rationale for overriding a medication notification poses a significant medical-legal risk. If an adverse patient event occurs, there is evidence that the clinician disregarded a warning that could have prevented harm. Moreover, the documentation process offers an opportunity to communicate to other clinicians and the patient why the selected DOAC dose was considered most appropriate for that individual clinical scenario. This documentation is also important as a way to mitigate potential medico-legal risk associated with using DOAC doses that do not follow evidence-based prescribing guidelines. Pharmacists are, therefore, an important addition to this workflow, as they are much more likely to provide documentation, regardless of whether a dose change was made. In more than one-third of cases with no apparent response to the notification, no reason for the off-label dosing was identified. Efforts to ensure that health care team members respond to notifications will likely further reduce inappropriate dosing.

Finally, renal- and weight-related factors were also significant reasons for notification recipients not making dose changes. The fluctuation of these values over time complicates dosing decisions, particularly when they are near a threshold. Further work is needed to understand how DOAC dosing decisions are made when these values are near a threshold to determine the utility of sending medication notifications in these situations.

Limitations

As this was a single-center study, not all the results may be generalizable. First, other health systems may not have a robust system in place for detecting certain DDIs. As such, these health systems may experience greater benefit from drug-drug interaction notifications than we experienced in our study. Due to the limited number of “DDI” notifications generated, it is difficult to conclude the efficacy of these notifications. Second, not all centers have an anticoagulation clinic with clinical pharmacists experienced in DOAC dosing. The lack of these specially trained clinical pharmacists makes it more difficult to implement a CDS strategy with pharmacist-prescriber collaboration. Additionally, the roles and responsibilities of clinical pharmacists and prescribers can vary greatly across health centers and in different countries, which could reduce the generalizability of the results. However, many countries do allow at least some form of independent prescribing for pharmacists, including the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia, Poland, Switzerland, and Denmark [33]. This can vary from using collaborative practice agreements and following state prescribing guidelines to full independent prescribing authority. In the United States, though prescribing authority is determined at a state level, all 50 states allow some form of pharmacist prescribing. Therefore, we believe that our results are relevant to many health systems both locally and internationally.

Additionally, a separate clinical system predated this trial to identify DDIs between apixaban or rivaroxaban and nirmatrelvir/ritonavir. This system likely reduced the total number of DDI notifications and altered how often this subgroup of notifications within our trial was reacted to. As the focus was on clinician response to the alerts, patient race and ethnicity data were not reported. Finally, this study was limited only to apixaban and rivaroxaban. It remains to be seen whether the results would have differed with other DOACs, such as dabigatran and edoxaban.

Conclusion

This study described the types of inappropriate DOAC prescribing errors that occur after the initial prescribing period and how various clinicians respond to an asynchronous CDS alert. While the overall rate of DOAC prescription change was similar between the prescriber- and pharmacist-directed notifications, the pharmacists more often documented responses to the notifications, representing an opportunity to reduce prescriber burden and improve medical-legal liability. Future studies should identify clinical situations or characteristics of patients or prescribers that are most likely to benefit from notifications or most likely to result in a prescribing change.
