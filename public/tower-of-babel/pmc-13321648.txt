Prognostic nutritional index and long-term outcomes after coronary artery bypass grafting
Authors: Haichang Xu, Weiqiang Li, Shen-an-nan Chen, Shuaishuai Zhao, Chengtao Peng, Kang Huang, Xueliang Zhou, Qicai Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study examined whether preoperative prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was related to long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study analyzed 550 patients with CHD who underwent CABG at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2014 and August 2025. PNI was treated as both a continuous variable and tertiles. Multivariable Cox models were used to evaluate its associations with study outcomes, and Kaplan–Meier as well as receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were additionally performed. Subgroup analyses, a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), and supplementary comparisons with the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) and EuroSCORE II were also undertaken.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 46.1 months, 113 patients developed MACE, 94 died from any cause, and 61 died from cardiovascular causes. After multivariable adjustment, higher PNI remained independently related to lower risks of MACE (HR per 1-unit increase: 0.948, 95% CI 0.917–0.979; P = 0.001), all-cause mortality (HR 0.938, 95% CI 0.904–0.973; P < 0.001), and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.939, 95% CI 0.900–0.980; P = 0.004). Patients in the highest tertile had lower risks of MACE and cardiovascular mortality than those in the lowest tertile. Similar overall patterns were observed in subgroup, quartile, and sensitivity analyses. Although PNI was significantly correlated with all three outcomes, its discriminatory performance was modest (AUCs: 0.596 for MACE, 0.616 for all-cause mortality, and 0.608 for cardiovascular mortality). GNRI and EuroSCORE II yielded numerically higher AUC values, and the combination of PNI with EuroSCORE II also showed a numerical improvement, but none of these differences reached statistical significance.ConclusionLower preoperative PNI corresponded to higher long-term risks of MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality after CABG. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the single-center retrospective design and the limited discriminatory performance of PNI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03807-1.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) continues to be a dominant driver of cardiovascular mortality worldwide. In 2021, CHD accounted for more than 9 million deaths, representing the single largest contributor to cardiovascular mortality and loss of healthy life-years [1]. CHD remains a major contributor to the global burden of cardiovascular disease, and cardiovascular mortality is expected to continue rising over the coming decades [2]. Despite substantial advances in pharmacotherapy and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is frequently selected for patients with anatomically complex multivessel disease or left main disease, and it offers superior long-term survival in appropriately selected patients [3, 4]. Nevertheless, postoperative complications may still occur, and the long-term clinical endpoints commonly monitored after CABG include repeat revascularization, stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), cardiovascular mortality, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and all-cause mortality [4, 5]. Therefore, accurate preoperative risk stratification tailored to individual patient characteristics is crucial for optimizing perioperative management, informing shared decision-making, and planning long-term secondary prevention strategies.

Beyond traditional risk factors, increasing attention has focused on the potential contribution of preoperative nutritional status and immune-inflammatory conditions to adverse postoperative outcomes after cardiac surgery, including CABG [6, 7].

Because nutritional status is informative for perioperative prognosis, numerous nutritional assessment tools have been introduced to support clinical stratification and management in surgical patients. Frequently used subjective or semi-subjective instruments include the Subjective Global Assessment (SGA), the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), and the Malnutrition-Inflammation Score (MIS), which is an extension based on components of the SGA [8, 9]. However, these approaches depend largely on clinician judgment guided by the patient’s clinical presentation, and their accuracy may be constrained by communication barriers between clinicians and patients, particularly in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. By contrast, laboratory-based nutritional indices provide a more objective appraisal of preoperative nutritional status in cardiovascular patients; commonly used examples include the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score, and the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) [10]. Compared with the CONUT score, PNI is a simpler composite derived only from lymphocyte count and serum albumin, and it is applicable to a broader population than GNRI, making it a more practical tool for routine use [11, 12].

PNI is a composite indicator based on serum albumin and lymphocyte count, integrating information on nutritional reserves and immune function [13]. As both serum albumin and lymphocyte count are routinely measured in clinical practice, PNI has promising potential as a simple and cost-effective bedside risk assessment tool. Accumulating evidence indicates that lower PNI tends to coincide with poorer outcomes across a range of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including heart failure (HF), stroke, and infective endocarditis [14–16]. Recent studies further suggest that PNI, when evaluated preoperatively, remains informative for mortality and MACE among patients with CHD [17–19]. Nevertheless, evidence on PNI in relation to long-term outcomes in patients undergoing CABG is still limited, and its value across multiple long-term endpoints—including MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality—requires additional investigation.

Accordingly, we set out to clarify how preoperative PNI relates to several long-term clinical outcomes—namely MACE, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality—in CHD patients undergoing CABG, with the goal of informing prognostic assessment and stratification in patients with CHD.

Methods

Study population and design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study in which consecutive patients with CHD who underwent elective on-pump CABG at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2014 and August 2025 were screened for eligibility. Both isolated and combined procedures were included, provided that complete coronary revascularization had been achieved. Eligibility criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a definitive diagnosis of CHD; and (3) receipt of CABG during the index hospitalization. Exclusion criteria included: (1) missing lymphocyte count or serum albumin data; (2) loss to follow-up; (3) severe hepatic or renal failure; (4) severe hematologic or immune system disease; (5) malignant tumors; and (6) missing baseline data. After application of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 550 patients were retained for the final analysis, and no missing baseline data remained in the final analytical cohort; therefore, the study was conducted as a complete-case analysis without imputation. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. 2026 − 109) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because all patients were analyzed anonymously and the study was retrospective in nature, the Ethics Committee granted a waiver of informed consent.

Determination and grouping of PNI

In this study, serum albumin was measured using the Medical System albumin assay kit and expressed in g/L, whereas lymphocyte counts were determined by laser flow cytometry and expressed as ×10⁹/L. The laboratory variables used to calculate PNI were obtained within 24 h of admission and before surgery. PNI was then calculated using the following formula: PNI = serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × lymphocyte count (10⁹/L) [20]. For categorical analyses, participants were stratified into three PNI tertiles: Group T1 (PNI ≤ 45.23, n = 183), Group T2 (PNI 45.23–50.17, n = 184), and Group T3 (PNI > 50.17, n = 183). The cohort was also classified into four PNI quartiles: Group Q1 (PNI ≤ 44, n = 139), Group Q2 (PNI 44–47.78, n = 136), Group Q3 (PNI 47.78–51.25, n = 139), and Group Q4 (PNI > 51.25, n = 136).

Covariate collection and definition

This study incorporated extensive clinical data, primarily comprising demographic information and past medical history. Demographic variables included age, sex, smoking history (defined as any prior smoking history, regardless of current smoking status), and type of CHD (categorized into three groups: angina pectoris, non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)). Past medical history encompassed hypertension, diabetes, CHD, atrial fibrillation (AF), HF, valvular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), multivessel disease (MVD), triple-vessel disease (TVD), as well as the use of antidiabetic drugs.

Patients were considered to have hypertension if they had a prior diagnosis of hypertension, were receiving antihypertensive medication, or had blood pressure readings of ≥ 140/90 mmHg during the index hospitalization [21]. Diabetes was determined by a prior diagnosis, use of glucose-lowering drugs, or in-hospital laboratory evidence meeting at least one of these thresholds: fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour glucose on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [22]. CHD was defined as symptoms suggestive of myocardial ischemia (e.g., typical angina or exertional dyspnea) together with anatomically significant coronary stenosis documented by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) or invasive coronary angiography. Obstructive CHD was defined as ≥ 50% diameter stenosis in a major epicardial coronary artery. High-risk CHD anatomy was defined as ≥ 50% left main stenosis or severe three-vessel disease with ≥ 70% stenosis in all three major epicardial vessels [23]. AF was characterized by chaotic atrial electrical activity resulting in ineffective atrial contraction and was diagnosed on electrocardiography by absent P waves replaced by fibrillatory waves with absolutely irregular RR intervals [24]. HF was defined as the presence of typical symptoms or signs, combined with B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) ≥ 35 pg/mL or N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) ≥ 125 pg/mL, and imaging evidence of cardiac structural or functional abnormalities [25]. Valvular heart disease was defined as structural or functional abnormalities of the cardiac valves resulting in hemodynamic alterations and, for the purposes of this study, included patients in Stage C (severe, compensated) or Stage D (symptomatic), as defined by imaging evidence of severe valvular lesions with significant hemodynamic impairment and ventricular remodeling, with or without symptoms such as dyspnea, angina, syncope, or fatigue [26]. For this study, CKD was defined as a persistent abnormality in kidney structure or function, characterized by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months, or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g (≥ 3 mg/mmol) persisting for ≥ 3 months [27]. TVD was defined by coronary angiography showing significant lesions, with ≥ 50% diameter stenosis involving all three major epicardial coronary arteries: the left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery [28]. MVD was defined by angiographic evidence of ≥ 50% stenosis affecting two or more major coronary arteries [29].

Biomarker data included body mass index (BMI), SBP, DBP, lymphocyte count, triglycerides, total cholesterol, albumin, creatinine, uric acid, FBG, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), D-dimer, fibrinogen. Imaging parameters included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). All biomarker and imaging measurements were obtained from the initial workup during hospitalization. Operative and perioperative variables included operative approach (isolated CABG, CABG plus valve replacement, CABG plus valve repair, and CABG plus valve repair and valve replacement), number of grafts, graft vessel type (saphenous vein graft, internal mammary artery graft, and saphenous vein graft plus internal mammary artery graft), blood loss, total allogeneic transfusion volume, allogeneic red blood cell transfusion volume, autologous blood reinfusion volume, cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, and postoperative complications. Total allogeneic transfusion volume was defined as the total volume of allogeneic blood products administered during the perioperative period, including allogeneic red blood cells, cryoprecipitate, and fresh frozen plasma. Autologous blood reinfusion volume was defined as the total volume of autologous blood returned to the patient during the perioperative period. These variables were obtained from the operative and perioperative records. Discharge medication regimens included aspirin, clopidogrel, statins, beta-blockers, furosemide, antihypertensive drugs, antidiabetic medications, spironolactone, and warfarin. For descriptive analyses, antihypertensive drugs were defined as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB) or calcium-channel blockers (CCB).

In addition, the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) and EuroSCORE II were calculated for supplementary comparative analyses. The GNRI was derived from serum albumin and the ratio of actual to ideal body weight according to the following formula: 14.89 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 41.7 × [body weight (kg) / ideal body weight (kg)]. Ideal body weight was estimated as 22 × height (m)2 [30]. In addition, EuroSCORE II, a standardized risk model used to estimate perioperative mortality risk in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery, was calculated for supplementary comparative analyses according to the standard EuroSCORE II model [31].

Follow-up and outcome ascertainment

Follow-up started at hospital discharge and continued until death or administrative censoring (October 2025). Among the 728 screened patients, 68 had no available follow-up outcome information and were therefore excluded from the final analytical cohort according to the predefined study criteria. The remaining 550 patients constituted the final analytical cohort. Outcome information was collected from subsequent medical records and discharge summaries, supplemented by telephone follow-up with patients or their relatives to confirm survival status and the occurrence and timing of predefined clinical events. No previously published interview instrument or study-specific structured questionnaire was used. Verbal consent for telephone follow-up and audio recording was obtained before each call. All calls were recorded, outcome events were assessed by two investigators, and disagreements were resolved by a third investigator. Randomly selected recordings were further reviewed by dedicated follow-up quality-control staff.

The study focused on three primary endpoints: MACE, death from any cause, and cardiovascular mortality. The composite MACE outcome consisted of cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, unplanned coronary revascularization, and rehospitalization for deterioration of HF. Nonfatal myocardial infarction was defined according to the 2008–2009 revision of the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of myocardial infarction, based on available clinical records, cardiac biomarker results, electrocardiographic findings, and relevant imaging or hospitalization information when available [32]. For events during follow-up with incomplete biomarker or electrocardiographic data, classification was based on the WHO framework for nonfatal myocardial infarction with incomplete information [32]. Unplanned coronary revascularization was defined as clinically indicated repeat coronary revascularization performed for recurrent angina symptoms and/or objective evidence of myocardial ischemia, including new ischemic electrocardiographic changes or reversible myocardial ischemia on noninvasive imaging [33]. Rehospitalization for deterioration of heart failure was defined as an unplanned hospital readmission due to new-onset or worsening signs and symptoms of heart failure requiring urgent therapy [34]. All-cause mortality encompassed any death recorded over the observation period. Cardiovascular mortality was classified as death resulting from cardiac etiologies, including sudden death, cardiac rupture, malignant arrhythmia, progressive HF, or death of undetermined cause [35].

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were primarily performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), whereas additional analyses in R (version 4.3.4) included proportional hazards assumption testing, multicollinearity diagnostics, comparative ROC analyses, time-dependent ROC analyses, and generation of Figs. 4 and 5, and Supplementary Figure 1. The Shapiro–Wilk procedure was applied to determine the distributional characteristics of continuous variables. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using parametric methods, including the independent t test for two groups and one-way ANOVA for three-group comparisons. For variables with skewed distributions, results were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, and nonparametric tests were employed, namely the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Comparisons of categorical data, presented as numbers and percentages, were performed using the chi-square test. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual-based tests and inspection of log-minus-log survival plots. A non-significant global test result (two-sided P > 0.05) together with no evident graphical departure was taken to indicate acceptable model fit with respect to the proportional hazards assumption.

Initial screening of candidate predictors was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression in a univariate framework for MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate regression analyses were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable regression analyses, and three regression models were subsequently constructed [36]. Multicollinearity among variables included in the final multivariable models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. Events-per-variable (EPV) ratios were calculated for the final multivariable Cox models to assess the potential risk of overfitting. Three sequentially adjusted models were subsequently constructed for each outcome. For the analysis of MACE, Model 1 included age only. Model 2 additionally incorporated diabetes and CKD. Model 3 further added LDL-C, TVD, allogeneic transfusion volume, cardiopulmonary bypass time and antihypertensive drugs to evaluate whether the findings were maintained after more extensive covariate control. Second, for all-cause mortality, Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and Model 2 additionally included diabetes and CKD. Model 3 further incorporated BMI, LDL-C, LVEF, allogeneic transfusion volume, aortic cross-clamp time and antihypertensive drugs to more fully account for clinical and treatment-related factors. Finally, for cardiovascular mortality, Model 1 adjusted for diabetes only; Model 2 additionally included CKD; and Model 3 further added LVEF, allogeneic transfusion volume, cardiopulmonary bypass time and antihypertensive drugs. Subsequently, subgroup analyses were performed after stratification by sex, age, smoking status, AMI (STEMI and NSTEMI), hypertension, diabetes, HF, valvular disease, BMI, MVD, and TVD to evaluate the consistency of results across clinically relevant subgroups. Besides, interaction terms between PNI and subgroup variables were additionally tested in the Cox models to assess effect modification. Robustness was further assessed by repeating the primary analyses after excluding patients with CKD. In addition, we examined PNI quartiles in relation to MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality using the same multivariable Cox regression framework. Additionally, differences in cumulative incidence across PNI categories were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. For censored time-to-event outcomes, time-dependent ROC analyses were further performed in supplementary analyses to evaluate the discriminatory performance of PNI for MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality at prespecified follow-up time points. Conventional ROC analyses with calculation of AUC values were retained mainly as comparative discrimination analyses to compare the performance of PNI with that of GNRI and EuroSCORE II, rather than as stand-alone evidence of prognostic utility for censored survival outcomes. Comparative ROC analyses were performed using DeLong’s method for correlated ROC curves to assess differences in AUCs between PNI, GNRI, EuroSCORE II, and the combined model of EuroSCORE II plus PNI. In addition, the discriminative performance of a combined model including both PNI and EuroSCORE II was evaluated to explore the potential complementary value of PNI beyond EuroSCORE II. Statistical significance was defined using a two-sided threshold of P < 0.05.

Clinical characteristics according to MACE, all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

MACE Major adverse cardiovascular events, CHD Coronary heart disease, NSTEMI Non-ST elevation myocardial infarction, STEMI ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, CKD Chronic kidney disease, BMI Body mass index, SBP Systolic blood pressure, DBP Diastolic blood pressure, UA Uric acid, HDL-C High-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL-C Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LVEF Left ventricular ejection fraction, MVD Multivessel coronary artery disease, TVD Three-vessel disease, CABG coronary artery bypass grafting, Antihypertensive drugs angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers or calcium-channel blockers, PNI Prognostic nutritional index

Relative to patients without MACE, those who experienced MACE were older and more likely to have AF and to use antidiabetic drugs. They also had higher D-dimer levels, greater total allogeneic transfusion volume, greater allogeneic red blood cell transfusion volume, and more frequent warfarin use. In contrast, they had lower BMI, albumin, total cholesterol, and triglyceride levels, and were less likely to receive clopidogrel, statins, beta-blockers, or antidiabetic medications at discharge (P < 0.05).

Similarly, patients who died from any cause were older and more likely to be male and to have AF. They also had higher D-dimer levels and greater total allogeneic transfusion volume. Conversely, they exhibited lower BMI, albumin, total cholesterol, triglyceride levels, and LVEF, and were less likely to receive clopidogrel, statins, beta-blockers, or antidiabetic medications at discharge (P < 0.05).

Likewise, patients who died from cardiovascular causes were older and more likely to have AF. They also had higher creatinine, uric acid, and D-dimer levels, as well as greater total allogeneic transfusion volume and allogeneic red blood cell transfusion volume. In contrast, they had lower BMI, lymphocyte count, albumin, total cholesterol, HDL-C, and LVEF values, and were less likely to be prescribed statins at discharge (P < 0.05).

Notably, patients who experienced MACE, all-cause mortality, or cardiovascular mortality had significantly lower PNI values, with corresponding P values of 0.002, < 0.001, and 0.006, respectively. In the tertile analysis, the MACE group (P = 0.018) and the all-cause mortality group (P = 0.012) included fewer patients in the T3 category, whereas the tertile distribution did not differ significantly according to cardiovascular mortality status (P = 0.096).

Clinical characteristics according to PNI tertile grouping

Table 2 presents the cohort stratified by PNI tertiles: T1 (PNI ≤ 45.23, n = 183), T2 (PNI 45.23–50.17, n = 184), and T3 (PNI > 50.17, n = 183). Across tertiles, significant differences were observed in age, BMI, CKD prevalence, and several laboratory parameters, including DBP, lymphocyte count, albumin, creatinine, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, LDL-C, D-dimer, fibrinogen, and LVEF (P < 0.05). Regarding operative and perioperative characteristics, graft vessel type also differed significantly across tertiles (P = 0.032). In addition, the use of aspirin and clopidogrel at discharge varied significantly among the three groups (both P < 0.05). Notably, the incidence of MACE (P = 0.018) and all-cause mortality (P = 0.012) showed a decreasing trend from T1 to T3.

Univariate Cox regression analysis of MACE, all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality

Table 3 summarizes the results of the univariate Cox regression analyses. For MACE, significant associations were observed for age, diabetes, CKD, use of antidiabetic drugs, lymphocyte count, albumin, creatinine, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, TVD, total allogeneic transfusion volume, allogeneic red blood cell transfusion volume, cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, and use of antihypertensive drugs at discharge (P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis of PNI and MACE risk

Formal interaction tests did not show statistically significant heterogeneity across most subgroups; therefore, these subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory. As presented in Table 5, subgroup analyses based on the fully adjusted multivariable Cox models showed that higher PNI was generally associated with a lower hazard of MACE across multiple clinical strata. When PNI was analyzed as a continuous variable, a 1-unit increase in PNI corresponded to a 5.1% lower hazard of MACE in males, a 7.6% decrease in females, a 6.2% decrease in patients older than 60 years, a 6.5% decrease in smokers, and a 5.1% decrease in non-smokers. A similar inverse association was observed in both the STEMI and NSTEMI subgroups, with a 5.6% and 5.8% lower MACE risk per unit increase in PNI, respectively. Among patients with comorbid conditions, a 1-unit increment in PNI corresponded to an 8.4% decrease in MACE risk in those with hypertension, a 6.8% decrease in patients with diabetes, and a 7.5% decrease in those with heart failure. Likewise, in patients without valvular heart disease, a 1-unit increase in PNI corresponded to a 7.5% lower hazard of MACE. In patients with a BMI < 24 kg/m², MVD, and TVD, each 1-unit increment in PNI corresponded to a 5.9%, 7.9%, and 8.6% decrease in MACE risk, respectively.

Subgroup analysis of PNI and cardiovascular mortality risk

Table 7 summarizes subgroup results from fully adjusted multivariable Cox models. Overall, the direction of the PNI–cardiovascular mortality relationship was similar across most subgroups, although effect sizes differed by subgroup. When PNI was treated continuously, a 1-unit increment in PNI corresponded to a 13.8% decrease in cardiovascular mortality hazard in females, a 6.0% decrease in patients older than 60 years, and a 6.6% decrease in non-smokers. A similar inverse association was observed in both the STEMI and NSTEMI subgroups, with an 8.5% and 7.7% lower risk of cardiovascular mortality per unit increase in PNI, respectively. Among patients with comorbid conditions, each 1-unit increment in PNI corresponded to an 11.1% decrease in cardiovascular mortality risk in those with hypertension, an 8.1% decrease in patients with diabetes, and a 6.8% decrease in those with heart failure. Likewise, a 1-unit increase in PNI corresponded to a 10.0% lower hazard of cardiovascular mortality in patients without valvular heart disease. In patients with a BMI < 24 kg/m² or ≥ 24 kg/m², each 1-unit increment in PNI corresponded to a 5.2% and 7.6% decrease in cardiovascular mortality risk, respectively. Similarly, higher PNI corresponded to an 8.5% lower risk of cardiovascular mortality in patients with MVD and an 11.0% lower risk in those with TVD.

Visual depiction of long-term cardiovascular and mortality outcomes according to PNI levels

Figure 2 presented Kaplan–Meier curves comparing cumulative event occurrence across the three PNI tertile groups. Significant separation was observed for MACE (Fig. 2A), all-cause mortality (Fig. 2B), and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 2C), with log-rank P values of < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.020, respectively. The curves showed a clear gradient, with the T3 group consistently displaying fewer events than the T1 group across these endpoints. Figure 3 summarized ROC analyses. PNI showed modest discrimination for MACE [Figure 3A: AUC = 0.596 (0.540–0.652), P = 0.002], overall mortality [Figure 3B: AUC = 0.616 (0.558–0.673), P < 0.001], and cardiovascular mortality [Figure 3C: AUC = 0.608 (0.539–0.677), P = 0.006]. In supplementary time-dependent ROC analyses, PNI also showed limited discrimination across the evaluated follow-up periods (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, as shown in Fig. 4, GNRI yielded numerically higher AUC values than PNI for predicting MACE (Fig. 4A: AUC = 0.631, 95% CI 0.574–0.688 vs. AUC = 0.596, 95% CI 0.539–0.652), all-cause mortality (Fig. 4B: AUC = 0.657, 95% CI 0.598–0.717 vs. AUC = 0.616, 95% CI 0.558–0.673), and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 4C: AUC = 0.628, 95% CI 0.555–0.701 vs. AUC = 0.608, 95% CI 0.538–0.677). However, comparative ROC analysis did not reveal a statistically significant difference between GNRI and PNI for any of the three outcomes (all P for comparison > 0.05). Similarly, EuroSCORE II yielded a numerically higher AUC than PNI for predicting MACE (Fig. 4A: AUC = 0.610, 95% CI 0.551–0.669 vs. AUC = 0.596, 95% CI 0.539–0.652), an identical AUC for all-cause mortality (Fig. 4B: both AUC = 0.616), and a slightly lower AUC for cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 4C: AUC = 0.603, 95% CI 0.527–0.679 vs. AUC = 0.608, 95% CI 0.538–0.677), with no statistically significant differences observed in the comparative ROC analyses. However, as shown in Fig. 5, after incorporation of PNI into EuroSCORE II, the model yielded numerically higher AUC values for predicting MACE (Fig. 5A: AUC = 0.628, 95% CI 0.570–0.686 vs. 0.610, 95% CI 0.551–0.669), all-cause mortality (Fig. 5B: AUC = 0.652, 95% CI 0.592–0.712 vs. 0.616, 95% CI 0.552–0.680), and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 5C: AUC = 0.630, 95% CI 0.560–0.700 vs. 0.603, 95% CI 0.527–0.679) compared with EuroSCORE II alone. However, comparative ROC analysis showed that these increases were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Using routinely collected hospital data in this hospital-based retrospective study, we found that lower preoperative PNI tended to accompany more frequent MACE, overall mortality and deaths from cardiovascular causes during long-term observation. These patterns remained evident after accounting for key covariates and were largely reproduced in subgroup and sensitivity analyses. In addition, when PNI was incorporated into EuroSCORE II, the combined model yielded numerically higher AUC values than EuroSCORE II alone; however, these increases were not statistically significant. Therefore, PNI may provide at most supplementary prognostic information when considered together with EuroSCORE II, rather than serving as an independent or clearly superior prognostic tool. Taken together, our results suggest that lower preoperative PNI corresponded to less favorable long-term outcomes in patients with CHD undergoing CABG, although the modest discriminatory performance and residual confounding warrant cautious interpretation.

Malnutrition plays an important role in postoperative outcomes after CABG. Clinical data indicate that approximately 20% of patients have established malnutrition before surgery, and preoperative malnutrition independently predicts multiple severe postoperative complications and mortality [37], showing a significant association with adverse postoperative outcomes [38]. In recent years, PNI, a low-cost immunonutritional marker derived from routinely available laboratory measures, has been increasingly used to support prognostic assessment in patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery [39]. Among individuals with CHD treated with revascularization by PCI or CABG, multiple reports suggest that PNI retains prognostic information for both early and late mortality [40, 41]. More recently, evidence focusing on CABG has also accumulated. Earlier single-center retrospective studies reported higher short-term mortality after CABG among patients with lower preoperative PNI. Specifically, lower preoperative PNI was linked to higher 30-day or in-hospital mortality in cohorts of 239 and 879 patients, respectively [42, 43]. In multicenter retrospective CABG cohorts, both preoperative and discharge PNI have been associated with survival outcomes. Liu et al. observed that lower discharge PNI independently corresponded to higher long-term mortality in 1,173 older patients, while Sun et al. observed that higher preoperative PNI corresponded to lower 30-day mortality and discharge PNI > 40 with lower long-term mortality in 2,889 patients [44, 45]. Collectively, these studies suggest that discharge PNI is closely related to long-term mortality and serves as a sensitive, objective prognostic marker after CABG, whereas so far, preoperative PNI has mainly been linked to short-term mortality and its long-term predictive value remains insufficiently characterized [42–45]. Because hospitalization itself can alter nutritional status [46], and the surgical process can cause marked fluctuations in PNI, its prognostic performance for long-term outcomes may be influenced by perioperative interventions and inflammatory stress responses [47, 48]. Accordingly, discharge PNI may better reflect post-CABG physiological reserve during recovery by integrating the effects of surgical trauma, perioperative complications, and in-hospital nutritional management, whereas preoperative PNI primarily represents baseline nutritional and immune reserves, more closely approximates the patient’s intrinsic long-term immunonutritional status, and is less influenced by perioperative factors. Therefore, the optimal application of preoperative and discharge PNI across the entire perioperative course of CABG remains to be further clarified. In this study, we focused on preoperative PNI and long-term adverse outcomes after CABG, providing additional evidence to inform long-term prognostic appraisal in this population. Besides, in a small single-center cohort of 131 patients undergoing emergency CABG for STEMI, Demirci et al. observed that higher preoperative PNI corresponded to lower long-term mortality, although the selected population and restricted sample size may reduce the generalizability of these findings [49]. Notably, in a large single-center retrospective cohort comprising 6,626 patients undergoing CABG, Gim et al. observed during long-term follow-up that patients in the low-PNI group had a 19% higher hazard of long-term death than those in the high-PNI group, and they also experienced a significantly higher incidence of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events [50].

Compared with previous studies, although our investigation was also single center and had a relatively smaller sample size, it has several advantages in study design and statistical analysis. First, regarding endpoint selection, in addition to all-cause mortality, we further evaluated MACE and cardiovascular mortality as separate outcomes. This approach separates all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and nonfatal cardiovascular events, thereby offering more outcome-specific insights for long-term management after CABG. Second, PNI was incorporated into Cox regression models both as a continuous variable and as tertiles and quartiles, which allowed us to explore the consistency of the observed associations across different analytic approaches. In the present study, the relationship between lower PNI and adverse outcomes was more consistent when PNI was treated continuously, whereas the categorical analyses appeared less robust after full adjustment. This was most evident in the tertile-based analysis of all-cause mortality, although the extreme-group comparison in the quartile analysis remained statistically significant. Third, subgroup and sensitivity analyses showed broadly consistent directions of association; however, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. In supplementary comparative analyses, both GNRI and EuroSCORE II demonstrated numerically higher predictive performance than PNI, but comparative ROC analyses revealed no statistically significant differences. In contrast, adding PNI to EuroSCORE II yielded numerically higher AUC values, although the differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that PNI may provide supplementary prognostic information rather than serving as an independent prognostic marker. Overall, our findings provide additional evidence that lower preoperative PNI is associated with poorer long-term outcomes after CABG, but they do not establish PNI as a standalone prognostic tool for clinical risk stratification.

Several biological pathways may help explain why a low PNI is linked to poorer long-term outcomes after CABG. Serum albumin is commonly viewed as a marker of both nutritional status and systemic inflammatory burden, and hypoalbuminemia often results from a combination of inadequate protein reserves, impaired hepatic synthetic function, increased capillary permeability, crystalloid fluid loading, and persistent systemic inflammation [51, 52]. These factors may contribute to tissue edema, impaired wound healing, and subsequent organ dysfunction [53]. Lymphocyte count, the other component of PNI, reflects cellular immune competence. Lymphopenia has been related to immunosuppression, increased susceptibility to infection, and poor outcomes across a range of cardiovascular disease [54–56]. Taken together, PNI is a simple and practical objective nutritional marker, and a lower value often reflects the combined presence of malnutrition, persistent inflammation, immune dysregulation, and overall frailty. These pathophysiological disturbances may reduce a patient’s tolerance to surgery-related physiological stress and impair recovery from perioperative injury after CABG, ultimately leading to higher long-term mortality and a greater likelihood of recurrent cardiovascular events.

Notwithstanding the study’s contributions, several limitations merit consideration when interpreting the results. First, this was a hospital-based retrospective cohort study carried out at a cardiovascular surgery center in China, and all participants were recruited from one institution within a single region. Therefore, differences in patient characteristics, disease spectrum, treatment strategies, and healthcare systems may reduce the applicability of our findings to other populations and clinical settings. Second, although patients with incomplete baseline data were excluded from the final analysis and outcome events were adjudicated using recorded telephone follow-up, dual-investigator assessment, third-reviewer adjudication, and random quality-control review, some events were still ascertained through telephone follow-up in addition to the institutional medical record database. Consequently, the ascertainment of certain nonfatal MACE components, including nonfatal myocardial infarction, unplanned coronary revascularization, and rehospitalization for worsening heart failure, may have been less precise, and some degree of outcome misclassification cannot be excluded. In addition, follow-up information partly relied on patients’ or their relatives’ recall, which may have introduced recall bias. Furthermore, cardiovascular mortality included death from undetermined cause, which may have further affected classification accuracy. In addition, because patients without available follow-up outcome information were excluded from the final analytical cohort rather than treated as censored observations, some degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Therefore, these findings warrant cautious interpretation. Third, PNI was measured only once at hospital admission and was used as the exposure indicator, thus primarily reflecting baseline preoperative nutritional status and the immune-inflammatory state. Because PNI was calculated from serum albumin and lymphocyte count, both of which may be influenced by non-nutritional factors such as hepatic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, infection, or immunosuppression, caution is warranted when interpreting PNI solely as a measure of nutritional status. Indeed, PNI may reflect not only nutritional condition but also, to some extent, overall disease severity. Fourth, we did not collect longitudinal PNI measurements after surgery, at discharge, or during extended follow-up. Therefore, we were unable to assess how perioperative and longer-term changes in nutritional status might cumulatively influence clinical outcomes, and the true relationship between nutritional status and long-term endpoints may have been under- or overestimated. Fifth, although we adjusted for a wide range of clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, discharge medications, and several operative and perioperative variables, some potentially important perioperative details and longer-term postdischarge management data were unavailable or not comprehensively captured. Owing to the retrospective design of the study, factors such as secondary prevention therapy, medication adherence, and lifestyle interventions could not be fully assessed. Accordingly, residual confounding remains unavoidable. Sixth, the cutoff values used for tertile- and quartile-based PNI categorization were derived from the internal distribution of our study population and have not been externally validated. In addition, the size of the overall cohort was moderate, and the number of events for some endpoints was limited, which may have reduced statistical power in fully adjusted categorical, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses. Although the fully adjusted models were simplified, the events-per-variable ratio for the all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality models remained slightly below the conventional empirical threshold, and some risk of overfitting or limited model stability cannot be excluded. Consistent with this, the relationship between PNI and all-cause mortality was more stable when PNI was treated continuously, whereas the tertile-based association was attenuated after full adjustment and no longer reached statistical significance in Model 3. A similar attenuation was also observed in the sensitivity analysis excluding patients with CKD. However, this attenuation should be interpreted cautiously, because quartile-based analyses still showed significant inverse associations in the extreme-group comparison, suggesting that the loss of statistical significance in tertile-based analyses may reflect information loss after categorization and reduced power after multivariable adjustment rather than the absence of an underlying association. Seventh, covariate selection for the multivariable Cox regression models was primarily based on the results of the univariate analyses rather than being fully determined by clinical relevance or prior literature. Although this strategy is commonly used, it has methodological limitations and may have increased the risk of model instability. In addition, although our study suggested that PNI may provide supplementary prognostic information for postoperative MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality after CABG, its discriminative performance was relatively limited. Therefore, although lower PNI was consistently associated with poorer outcomes across several analyses, the present findings should be interpreted as supporting an association rather than as evidence that PNI alone has sufficient clinical utility for long-term prognostic stratification after CABG. Despite these limitations, our findings provide additional evidence supporting the relationship of long-term outcomes after CABG with preoperative nutritional condition in patients with CHD.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with CHD undergoing CABG, lower preoperative PNI corresponded to less favorable long-term outcomes, including MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. By evaluating multiple clinically relevant long-term endpoints and examining PNI using continuous, categorical, sensitivity, and supplementary comparative analyses, this study provides additional evidence that preoperative immunonutritional status may contribute to long-term prognostic assessment after CABG. However, given the modest discriminatory performance, internally derived cutoff values, and residual confounding inherent to the study design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. PNI may offer supplementary prognostic information rather than serve as a standalone risk stratification tool, and further validation in larger multicenter prospective cohorts is warranted before broader clinical application.
