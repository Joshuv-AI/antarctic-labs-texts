Abstract
Leveraging the inherent connection between sensing systems and wireless communications can improve their overall performance and is the core objective of joint communications and sensing. For effective communications, one has to frequently estimate the channel. Sensing, on the other hand, infers properties of the environment mostly based on estimated physical channel parameters, such as directions of arrival or delays. This work presents a low-complexity generative modeling approach that simultaneously estimates the wireless channel and its physical parameters without additional computational overhead. To this end, we leverage a recently proposed physics-informed generative model for wireless channels based on sparse Bayesian generative modeling and exploit the feature of conditionally Gaussian generative models to approximate the conditional mean estimator.



I
Introduction

Generative models play a fundamental role in today’s rapid advancements of deep learning
[
1
]
. Most research focuses on large language models for text generation
[
2
]
and image generation models
[
3
]
, but also in wireless communication and, especially, wireless channel modeling, Generative models promise performance gains
[
4
]
. However, compared to text and image generation, Generative models for channel modeling are subject to very different restrictions and conditions. Unlike text and natural images, wireless channels follow the fundamental laws of electromagnetic wave propagation. Moreover, when using the Generative model during online operation, its computational complexity is more important than in standard text and image applications. Offloading the Generative model to mobile devices constrains the number of Generative model parameters. In addition, acquiring high-quality training data for the Generative models is difficult and potentially requires costly measurement campaigns.

To overcome these issues, the work in
[
5
]
builds upon sparse Bayesian generative modeling
[
6
]
and introduces a physics-informed Generative model for wireless channels, which simultaneously can learn from noisy channel observations, generalizes to arbitrary system configurations and obeys the underlying physics. This model is evaluated in
[
5
]
regarding its generation capability for, e.g., providing training data.

In our work, we show that the model from
[
5
]
can also be directly used for improving physical layer applications and, particularly, parameter and channel estimation. Specifically, conditionally Gaussian latent models, i.e., Generative models with a Gaussian conditioned on a latent variable, such as Gaussian mixture models and variational autoencoders have been used for channel estimation in
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Since sparse Bayesian generative modeling involves a conditionally Gaussian latent model
[
6
]
, the model in
[
5
]
can be used in the same way.

Our main contributions are as follows. First, we show that the Gaussian mixture model-based estimators from
[
11
]
, which use a discrete Fourier transform-based parameterization of the Gaussian mixture model covariances, are a special case of the model in
[
5
]
. Based on this reinterpretation, we show how the model in
[
5
]
can be used to jointly estimate parameters (e.g., directions of arrival and delays) and the channel without computational overhead, rendering it particularly interesting for applications in joint communication and sensing. In addition, by leveraging the integrated physics, we reduce our estimator’s complexity to be linear in the channel dimension (e.g., the number of antennas). Moreover, the number of model parameters does not scale with the channel dimension and is in the range of a few hundred while still achieving state-of-the-art performance.

Preliminaries

II-A

Gaussian Mixture Models for Channel Estimation

Gaussian mixture models are Generative models, which model the unknown distribution
p(\bm{h})
of interest as a mixture of
K
Gaussians, i.e.,

p(\bm{h})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{h};\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{C}_{k})

(1)

with the (prior) weights
\rho_{k}
, the means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
and the covariances
\bm{C}_{k}
being the model parameters. Learning these parameters based on a ground-truth dataset
\mathcal{H}=\{\bm{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N_{t}}
can be done by an expectation-maximization algorithm
[
12
,
13
]
. While not incorporating neural networks and, thus, not belonging to the deep learning-subfield of machine learning, Gaussian mixture models offer several benefits for wireless communications. First, they can be extended to learn from noisy observations
[
14
,
15
]
. Second and arguably more important, Gaussian mixture models enable a closed-form computation of the conditional mean estimator
[
14
]
, which minimizes the mean squared error and, thus, is a desired channel estimator, i.e.,

\E[\bm{h}|\bm{y}]=\E[\E[\bm{h}|\bm{y},k]|\bm{y}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(k|\bm{y})\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\bm{h}|\bm{y},k}

(2)

with
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{h}+\bm{n}
, measurement matrix
\bm{A}
and noise realization

\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})
. Moreover
[
7
]
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\bm{h}|\bm{y},k}=\bm{C}_{k}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}(\bm{A}\bm{C}_{k}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{\mu}_{k})+\bm{\mu}_{k},

(3)

\displaystyle p(k|\bm{y})=\frac{\rho_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{y};\bm{A}\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{A}\bm{C}_{k}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})}{\sum_{i}\rho_{i}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{y};\bm{A}\bm{\mu}_{i},\bm{A}\bm{C}_{i}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})}.

(4)

N\times 2N
-over-sampled discrete Fourier transform matrix. Former leads to
\bm{C}_{k}
being circulant, while latter leads to
\bm{C}_{k}
being Toeplitz.

II-C

Sparse Bayesian Generative Models for Wireless Channels

The work in
[
6
]
introduces sparse Bayesian generative modeling, which combines sparse Bayesian learning
[
17
,
18
]
with conditionally Gaussian latent models resulting in a simultaneously sparsity-inducing and learnable statistical model. Next to a variational autoencoder-based variant,
[
6
]
introduces a Gaussian mixture model-based implementation of sparse Bayesian generative modeling, the compressive sensing Gaussian mixture model, whose statistical model is given by

\displaystyle\bm{y}|\bm{s}\sim p(\bm{y}|\bm{s})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{y};\bm{A}\bm{D}\bm{s},\sigma^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}),

(6)

\displaystyle\bm{s}|k\sim p(\bm{s}|k)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{s};\bm{0},\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})),

(7)

\displaystyle k\sim p(k)=\rho_{k}

(8)

with
\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{n}
(
\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})
) being a noisy and compressed observation of some signal
\bm{x}
of interest. The signal
\bm{x}
is assumed to be compressible with respect to some pre-known dictionary
\bm{D}
with compressible representation
\bm{s}
, i.e.,
\bm{x}=\bm{D}\bm{s}
. For training,
[
6
]
derives an extended expectation-maximization algorithm which allows to learn the model parameters
\{\bm{\gamma}_{k},\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
in (
6
)-(
8
) solely based on a training dataset of noisy and compressed observations
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N_{t}}
.

In
[
5
]
, the statistical model (
6
)-(
8
) is applied to modeling wireless channels by associating the signal
\bm{x}
with the channel
\bm{h}
. While
[
5
]
considers spatial, as well as time-varying and wideband channels, we only discuss spatial channels in this work. However, our findings can be directly extended to the other cases. To incorporate the underlying physics,
[
5
]
utilizes that wireless channels are compressible with respect to a physics-related dictionary, which is typically utilized for parameter estimation (cf.
[
19
]
). Specifically, by assuming the base station (and/or user) antennas to be placed in a uniform linear array with the common
\lambda/2
spacing,
[
5
]
utilizes a grid
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
, whose grid-points lie in the corresponding angular domain
[\pi/2,\pi/2)
. The resulting dictionary
\bm{D}
then contains steering vectors of the form

\bm{a}(\delta)=[1,\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{j}\pi\text{sin}(\delta)},\ldots,\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{j}\pi(N-1)\text{sin}(\delta)}]^{\operatorname{T}}

(9)

as columns with
\delta\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
, and
N
is the number of antennas. Each
\bm{a}(\delta)
encodes a specific angle and is associated with one index in the vector
\bm{s}
in (
7
). Moreover, the corresponding entry in
\bm{s}
represents the associated path loss
[
5
]
. Thus, by learning the model parameters
\{\bm{\gamma}_{k},\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
in (
6
)-(
8
), we learn a statistical model for the physical channel parameters. Moreover, by considering
\bm{h}=\bm{D}\bm{s}
together with (
7
) & (
8
), we conclude that learning
\{\bm{\gamma}_{k},\rho_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
implicitly yields a statistical model for the channel
\bm{h}
itself, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{h}|k\sim p(\bm{h}|k)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{s};\bm{0},\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}),

(10)

\displaystyle k\sim p(k)=\rho_{k}.

(11)

III
Reinterpreting the Structured GMM Covariances

As a first step towards jointly estimating parameters and channels, we compare the covariance in (
5
) with the learned covariance by sparse Bayesian generative modeling for the channel
\bm{h}
in (
10
), i.e.,
\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}
. By choosing the number
S_{\mathrm{R}}
of grid-points in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
to be
N
(or
2N
), the dictionary
\bm{D}
matches in dimension with
\bm{Q}
in (
5
). The entries
\bm{Q}_{m,n}
of the discrete Fourier transform matrix are
\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{j}2\pi(m-1)(n-1)/N}
(or
\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{j}2\pi(m-1)(n-1)/(2N)}
for the over-sampled version). On the other hand, the entries
\bm{D}_{m,n}
of the dictionary defined by (
9
) equal
\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{j}\pi(m-1)\text{sin}(\delta_{n})}
.

For now, we choose
S_{\mathrm{R}}=N
and consider the case
n=1,\ldots,N/2
and
n=N/2+1,\ldots,N
separately. By utilizing
\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{j}\phi}=\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{j}\phi-2\pi(m-1)}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
in the second case, and setting the arguments in
\bm{Q}_{m,n}
and
\bm{D}_{m,n}
to be equal, we yield

\displaystyle\delta^{(\text{circ})}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left(2\frac{n-1}{N}\right)\ \forall\ n=1,\ldots,\frac{N}{2},

(12)

\displaystyle\delta^{(\text{circ})}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left(2\frac{n-1}{N}-2\right)\ \forall\ n=\frac{N}{2}+1,\ldots,N.

(13)

Thus, by choosing
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}=\{\delta^{(\text{circ})}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{S_{\mathrm{R}}=N}
, applying sparse Bayesian generative modeling (cf. (
6
)-(
8
)) leads to a statistical model for
\bm{h}
(cf. (
10
) & (
11
)) which matches the Gaussian mixture model investigated in
[
11
]
with the structured
N\times N
covariance (
5
) and the additional prior knowledge of setting the means
\bm{\mu}_{k}
to be zero
[
16
]
. Equivalently, when choosing the over-sampled discrete Fourier transform in (
5
), we derive

\displaystyle\delta^{(\text{toep})}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left(\frac{n-1}{N}\right)\ \forall\ n=1,\ldots,N,

(14)

\displaystyle\delta^{(\text{toep})}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left(\frac{n-1}{N}-1\right)\ \forall\ n=N+1,\ldots,2N.

(15)

The work in
[
5
]
chooses the grid-points in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
to be equidistant in
[\pi/2,\pi/2)
. However, from (
12
)-(
15
), we conclude that choosing
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
in a way that the
\text{sin}(\delta)
-term in (
9
) is uniformly sampled between -1 and 1 leads to discrete Fourier transform-based covariance decompositions of the Gaussian mixture model-based estimators investigated in, e.g.,
[
11
]
. While choosing
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}
in this form leads to the same statistical model for
\bm{h}
as in
[
11
]
, this reinterpretation offers the key advantage that we have explicit access to a statistical model for the channel parameters (
7
) & (
8
). This model can be used for, e.g., simultaneously estimating the channel parameters without computational overhead, making it particularly interesting for joint communication and sensing. We also show how this model can reduce the estimator’s complexity and model parameters.

IV
Joint Parameter and Channel Estimation with the CSGMM

Since the statistical model in (
7
) & (
8
) forms a Gaussian mixture model, we utilize the Gaussian mixture model-based decomposition of the conditional mean estimator described in (
2
) for estimating
\bm{s}
given a channel observation
\bm{y}
, i.e.,

\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]=\E[\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y},k]|\bm{y}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(k|\bm{y})\bm{\mu}^{\bm{s}|\bm{y},k}_{k}

(16)

with

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{k}^{\bm{s}|\bm{y},k}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}(\bm{A}\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}\bm{y}

(17)

\displaystyle p(k|\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{y};\bm{0},\bm{A}\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})}{\sum_{i}\rho_{i}\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\bm{y};\bm{0},\bm{A}\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{i})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})}.

(18)

Since
\bm{s}
encodes the angles of the corresponding channel
\bm{h}
, we can apply the generic DoA estimation scheme from compressive sensing
[
20
]
and identify the peaks in the element-wise squared absolute value
|\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]|^{2}
, which then correspond to our DoA estimates. While
\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]
can be used for parameter estimation in this way, we can also directly derive the conditional mean estimator for the corresponding channel
\bm{h}
by the simple matrix-vector multiplication

\E[\bm{h}|\bm{y}]=\bm{D}\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]

(19)

resulting in a joint parameter and channel estimator.

Equivalent to the Gaussian mixture model, training the compressive sensing Gaussian mixture model can be done in an initial offline phase. Moreover, most of the terms in (
16
)-(
18
) can also be pre-computed. In fact, the only operations in the online operation for channel estimation are the matrix-vector multiplication in (
17
), calculating the argument of the exponential function in the numerator’s Gaussians in (
18
), and (
19
).

2

(\bm{A}\bm{D}\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{k})\bm{D}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}
can be pre-computed for a desired range of noise variances.
We assume
N
to be the number of antennas,
S_{\mathrm{R}}
the number of grid-points (i.e., the dimension of
\bm{s}
), and
K
the number of Gaussian mixture model components. For simplicity and without loss of generality, we assume
\bm{A}=\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}
. The first operation takes
\mathcal{O}(NS_{\mathrm{R}}K)

\mathcal{O}(N^{2}K)
, and the third takes
\mathcal{O}(NS)

.

Note that each entry in
\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]
represents a distinct angle. Thus, the number of entries in
\E[\bm{s}|\bm{y}]
, which are non-negligible, is not to be expected to be significantly larger than the number of paths existent in the corresponding channel

in (
7
) & (

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{P}

(20)

\displaystyle\bm{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times S_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times P},

(21)

where we extract the
P

\bm{\gamma}_{k}

\bm{D}
to this transformation by extracting the corresponding

\bm{D}
for each component

\displaystyle(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}\text{diag}(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{k})\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}\left(\text{diag}(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{-1})+\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\bm{D}}_{k}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{A}^{\operatorname{H}}\right)

(22)

resulting in the argument of the exponential function in the numerator’s Gaussians in (

\mathcal{O}(NKP)

Conclusion & Future Work

In this work, we investigated how sparse Bayesian generative modeling can be used for joint parameter and channel estimation. We additionally utilized the integrated physics to reduce the complexity and number of model parameters. Next to channel estimation, conditionally Gaussian latent models have been investigated for other physical layer applications such as pilot design
