Effect of a patient education program focused on weight stigma on quality of life and self-efficacy in patients living with obesity: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Guillaume Ramsamy, Catherine Esnard, STEREOBES Consortium, Helena Mosbah, David Soussi Berjonval, Jean Pierre Faure, Marion Albouy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter a literature review and interviews with patients living with obesity, key psychosocial determinants such as coping strategies, weight bias internalization, body dissatisfaction and self-efficacy were identified as critical to address obesity-related stigma. The intervention was tailored using evidence-based techniques and input from health professionals to ensure relevance and avoid redundancy. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) aims to evaluate the effect of an intervention specifically designed to address weight stigma among individuals living with obesity.MethodsThe study compares two parallel arms following different interventions: Patient Education as Usual (PEU) program and the newly developed Patient Education STEREOBES (PES) program, which integrates additional components to counteract weight stigma. The PES program, crafted using the Intervention Mapping process, addresses key psychosocial determinants such as self-efficacy, body image, and coping strategies. It incorporates workshops focused on psychoeducation, physical activity, and emotional regulation through innovative techniques like mindfulness, assertiveness training, and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). The program emphasizes group activities and patient interaction to foster resilience against stigmatizing experiences and promote healthier lifestyle choices. This comprehensive intervention is designed to improve psychological, behavioral, and physical outcomes, particularly by reducing the internalization of weight bias and enhancing coping mechanisms.DiscussionPatients in the PES arm should demonstrate significant improvements in quality of life and self-efficacy compared to the PEU arm. Psychosocial factors should mediate the effect of the intervention on the outcomes involving quality of life and behaviors. This study will provide valuable insights into the role of weight stigma in obesity treatment and the effectiveness of targeted interventions.Trial registrationThis research protocol has been approved by the Individual Protection Committee Northwest IV (National n°2023-A00327-38) and registered on Clinicaltrial.gov (NCT05906238) on June 7, 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02274-8.Keywords: Obesity, Weight stigma, Weight bias, Patient education, Intervention, Mental health

Introduction

Obesity is characterized by a body mass index (BMI) over 30 kg/m2 and, more precisely, by an abnormal or excessive accumulation of body fat [1]. This is a multifactorial pathology, with a complex etiology whose maintenance is favored by socio-demographic and psychological factors (e.g. anxiety, stress, depression), as well as environmental, socio-economic, biological and physiological factors [2]. Although effective treatments exist, including adapted physical activities or psychotherapeutic support, one area that still receives little attention is weight stigma expressed through stereotypes and prejudices directed at individuals experiencing obesity [3, 4]. Stereotypes involve generalized beliefs, such as the notion that people with obesity are lazy or lack self-control, while prejudices reflect negative attitudes or biases, assuming that obesity results from a personal failure rather than a complex interplay of factors. These attitudes are based on the misconception that obesity is not a pathology and that overcoming it simply requires willpower, such as increasing physical activity or eating less [5, 6]. Consequently, the most common stereotypes are that people with obesity are lazy and lack self-control and self-discipline [7]. These stereotypes and prejudices result in discriminatory behaviors towards them [8, 9], which often lead to more depressive symptoms, increased perceived stress and reduced social support [10], all of which can contribute to a failure of care [11]. Ultimately, weight stigma negatively affects overall quality of life – which encompas physical health, psychological well-being, independence, social relationships, personal beliefs, and environmental factors according the World Health Organization [12] – by fostering discriminatory behaviors that contribute to increased depressive symptoms, heightened perceived stress, and diminished social support. There are two kind of weight stigma, that is constant and pervasive: Interpersonal weight stigma, addressed directly to people with obesity, i.e. coming from loved ones [13] or healthcare professionals [14–16]; Environmental weight stigma, addressed indirectly, i.e. devices or materials unsuitable for people with obesity [17–19]. In such a context, people with obesity can encounter numerous difficulties in various spheres of social life, as well as in the medical field. Many several studies attest that weight-related stereotypes or prejudices are frequently transmitted by physicians [8, 20, 21]. For instance, general practitioners believe individuals with obesity are less likely to comply the treatment compared to those of normal weight [22]. The consequences of this discrimination would lead to late diagnoses of other pathologies (e.g. cancer) and failures, when attempting to lose weight, which could be linked to a loss of confidence in the relationship with the physician [23]. Individuals living with obesity may frequently encounter stigmatizing situations related to obesity, which can affect the resources necessary for adopting salutogenic behaviors. Through constant exposure to these stigmatizing contexts, individuals tend to internalize weight bias, known as weight bias internalization (WBI), applying and considering these stereotypes to be true for themselves which can negatively modify their well-being and health behaviors [24, 25]. It increases stress, anxiety, and contribute to low self-esteem and body-dissatisfaction [26, 27], which in turn lead to harmful behaviors such as social isolation [28], reduced healthcare utilization [20], avoidance of physical activity [29], and binge eating [30]. Furthermore, weight stigma has been shown to significantly mediate the relationship between BMI, body dissatisfaction, and depressive symptoms, exacerbating these psychosocial issues [31].

Stereotype threat model [32] can help to better identify the relationship between weight stigma and deleterious behaviors. This threat is situational, with members of a group who are negatively stereotyped in one area feeling threatened to confirm a dimension of these stereotypes through their behavior. This fear of confirming the stereotype would result in cognitive overload due to efforts to regulate the stress induced by the threat. The consequences of this phenomenon have been observed in the case of obesity [33–36]. For instance, Major et al. (2014) demonstrated that when negative weight stereotypes are triggered by reading a stigmatizing article, participants who perceive themselves as overweight consume more snacks than those in the control group who read a neutral article.

While it seems relevant to incorporate an approach to remediate the harmful effects of weight stigma, few studies have explored its role within obesity treatment programs. Altough reducing the stigmatization of people with obesity in society is challenging, it is still possible and important to implement interventions aimed at lowering the incidence and addressing its deleterious consequences. A key intervention is to increase the resources available to individuals living with obesity, enabling them to better cope with stigmatizing situations. By doing so, they may become less sensitive and vulnerable to weight stigma, gaining greater agency over their behaviors and decisions – especially in adopting healthier lifestyle choices and managing stigmatizing experiences. For instance, focusing on aspects of social identity that are less threatened, or offering positive reinforcement through patient education (PE) programs, can help empower people with obesity [37]. These programs focus on the patient, helping them to modify their beliefs and representations of their condition and treatment, with the aim of bringing about a lasting change in their attitudes and behaviours [38]. The present STEREOBES program (acronym for “Stereotypes and Obesity”) aims to assess the effectiveness of a specific PE program to in addressing the adverse effects of weight stigma among patients living with obesity enrolled in a care program.

Development of the stereobes program

To develop the STEREOBES program, we relied on the Intervention Mapping methodology [39]. This approach is particularly well-suited for this study, as it provides a holistic, bio-psycho-social perspective on health. Furthermore, it has been successfully applied in several countries to address various public health challenges, including obesity [39, 40]. Intervention Mapping describes three main stages: the diagnostic stage (needs assessment and formulation of change objectives/change matrix), intervention design (selection of change techniques, program development and implementation), and intervention evaluation.

Firstly, through a literature review, we identified the most relevant psychosocial determinants to address in order to counteract the effects of weight stigma and influence behaviors, physiology, and psychological well-being. Stigmatizing situations appear to activate stress appraisals and diminish perceived social support, which in turn promote the use of ineffective coping strategies [41] and unhealthy eating behaviors [42]. Self-efficacy – the belief in one’s ability to manage situations and achieve goals – decrease in individuals with obesity due to weight stigma, resulting in lower levels of physical activity and healthy eating behaviors [30, 43]. Furthermore, body image is affected in a stigmatizing context, individuals become more preoccupied with their appearance, often setting unrealistic goals, which leads to greater disappointment when these goals are not achieved [44]. Additionally, positive self-esteem is associated with taking initiative and adopting effective coping strategies, but stigmatizing environments tend to lower self-esteem [45, 46]. Executive control, which governs the adoption of health-promoting behaviors, is also impaired by stigma due to a reduction in the cognitive resources necessary for optimal functioning [47]. Finally, weight bias internalization appears to be a critical determinant in this field of study. Frequent exposure to stigmatizing contexts can lead individuals to internalize these stereotypes to a greater extent, resulting in the adoption of more harmful health behaviors [24, 48].

The second part of the diagnostic phase involved selecting the most relevant and suitable determinants for the population targeted by this intervention. At the "Vie La Santé" (VLS) health promotion center, located within the University Hospital of Poitiers (France), the innovative environment is designed to promote physical, mental, social, and psychological well-being. Semi-structured interviews with patients at VLS were conducted to determine whether the determinants identified in the literature aligned with their lived experiences. Patients were encouraged to openly share their personal struggles with obesity and stigma, followed by rating the intensity of various issues on a scale from 1 to 10. These insights helped refine the intervention by grounding it in both research and patient experiences, ensuring that it addressed key health factors such as nutrition, chronic pain, stress management, and emotional well-being in a way that truly resonated with those affected. The interviews were transcribed and thematically analysed, though the detailed themes are not discussed in this article. The verbatim responses were categorized into nine themes: social support, self-esteem, weight bias internalization, body image, coping strategies, perceived stress, eating and physical activity behaviors, and others' perceptions. While certain factors – such as self-esteem, stress, and perceived social support – were excluded from the intervention's design, they remain important variables of interest for evaluation. The most relevant determinants identified for the intervention were coping strategies, weight bias internalization, body dissatisfaction, and self-efficacy. The intervention was then developed to positively influence these key factors.

To develop the intervention, it was necessary to review the various workshops already offered at the VLS to determine if these techniques were already being used. This approach helps avoid redundancy with workshops specifically designed for the STEREOBES program. In conclusion, the protocol outlined in this article represents the final phase of the intervention mapping process, which is dedicated to evaluating the intervention’s effectiveness. During this evaluation, variables identified in the literature but not integrated into the program’s design will be assessed (e.g. self-esteem, stress, and perceived social support). Additionally, evaluating patients' health literacy is both essential and highly relevant, as it plays a crucial role in the success of healthcare programs [62, 63].

Main objectives

Objective 1

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of an intervention addressing obesity stigma, integrated into a therapeutic PE program, on psychological (self-efficacy), behavioral (physical activity, diet), and physical (BMI) outcomes. By strengthening patients' skills, particularly in coping with stigmatizing situations, we seek to enhance their overall quality of life. We hypothesize that these improvements will result in better psychological and physical health by the program's conclusion. Finally, we aim for patients to adopt or modify behaviors that sustainably improve their quality of life and capacity to act.

Objective 2

This second aim, which is divided into two parts, is to evaluate the influence of psychosocial factors as mediators of the relationship between program follow-up and improved quality of life. First, we hypothesize that the intervention should lead to a decrease in the level of internalization of weight bias, which should then have a mediating effect on perceived benefits during and after the intervention. Secondly, we hypothesize that improvements in psychosocial factors such as self-efficacy, body image, self-esteem, stress, perceived social support and sense of coherence will have an impact on weight bias internalization (WBI). We expect that all these factors together will moderate the benefits on WBI, but also that WBI will have an influence on these factors, i.e. a bi-directional influence.

Secondary objectives

The study will also incorporate a qualitative dimension through semi-structured interviews to capture patients' subjective experiences at each evaluation stage. These interviews will provide deeper insight into the personal and interpersonal dynamics activated during the program, complementing and refining the quantitative results for a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention's impact. Then, we will seek to establish patient profiles by integrating characteristics such as BMI, education level, and lifetime experiences of stigma, alongside with qualitative data collected during the intervention. By combining these factors, we aim to identify which profiles are most receptive to change and evaluate whether the intervention has helped those experiencing the most stigma to regain a normal quality of life.

Methods and analysis

The STEREOBES study is a randomized controlled trial aimed at assessing the effect of a patient education (PE) program.

Procedure overview

The aim is to recruit 100 patients with obesity over a one and a half-year period between October 2023 and April 2025. Patients will be randomly assigned in two parallel arms: (1) PE as usual (PEU) program and (2) the PE-STEREOBES (PES) program developed specifically for this study. The two arms will be balanced at a ratio of 1:1. In order to create homogeneous cohorts, we will use stratification criteria such as age and body mass index (BMI). The randomization list is prepared by a biostatistician from the University Hospital of Poitiers who is not involved in the study, ensuring an unbiased randomization process. The allocated arm will not be blinded to the patients or the research team. Patients are recruited by various methods, including flyers, local press and referrals from their general practitioner to the Day Hospital at the Poitiers Obesity Center (POC). The first author (GR) will conduct regular visits to the POC to provide patients an oral presentation of the study and collect their contact details. Patients meeting the inclusion criteria will be informed of the STEREOBES study via the information sheet. Following this, they will be then contacted by telephone to discuss participation in the program. After a minimum consideration period of eight days, patients will have the opportunity to express their desire to participate in the study. Thus, they will take part to the shared educational assessment carried out by the nurse of the VLS, which is the usual part of procedure of the obesity course, and will be invited by the first author (GR) to sign the free and informed consent form. During the session, patients' specific needs related to obesity will be assessed (e.g. concern about nutrition, sleep issues, emotional management and others). Participants will then be randomly assigned to either the control or experimental arm. The first author (GR) and the last author (MA) are in charge of the randomization process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be included, patients must (1) be at least 18 years old, (2) have a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 kg/m2, which comprised patient in state of moderate obesity to morbid obesity. While obesity is typically diagnosed in individuals with a body mass index (BMI) above 30 kg/m2, there are cases where this diagnosis may not be applicable. For instance, a BMI exceeding 30 kg/m2 can be observed in elite athletes (e.g., boxers, rugby players, bodybuilders) whose muscle development is significantly above average. However, the patients included in this study are recruited through the Poitiers Obesity Center (POC), ensuring that obesity has been properly diagnosed by this service prior to inclusion in the study. Patients must also (3) be affiliated to a social security scheme and (4) sign the free and inform consent form. Individuals won’t be eligible if they (1) have an untreated psychiatric disorder, (2) benefit from enhanced protection (i.e. minors, persons deprived of their liberty by a judicial or administrative decision, persons staying in a health or social establishment, adults under legal protection and patients in emergency situations) and (3) are pregnant or breastfeeding, women of childbearing age without effective contraception (hormonal/mechanical: oral, injectable, transcutaneous, implantable, intrauterine device, or surgical: tubal ligation, hysterectomy, total oophorectomy). This last criterion is related to women included in bariatric surgery procedure.

Intervention

Patients will follow programs at the VLS, that include group workshop lasting an average of 90 min.

PE-STEREOBES (PES): this program was developed on the basis of the PE as Usual program (PEU) "Un Poids en Moins", so they have a common foundation. The specificity of the STEREOBES program lies in the workshops and self-help booklet developed specifically for this study. These tools include content on managing the adverse effects of weight stigma.

Patients included in the PES cohort will follow a patient education program at a frequency of at least 2 workshops per month for 12 months (N = 24). The twelve-month period appears to be the most appropriate for generating progressive and lasting benefits. Additionally, the intervention follow-up must be flexible enough to accommodate the daily constraints of patients' lives. It is preferable to offer a smaller, regular dose of workshops over an extended period, rather than an intensive program delivered in a short timeframe, when the goal is to achieve long-term benefits [64, 65]. The PES program will include workshops that are already offered by VLS, as well as workshops developed specifically for this study. A detailed presentation of the programme is available in the supplemental material.

All workshops generally run in the same way. The session begins with an ice-breaker where patients introduce themselves to others in a playful way (e.g. give 2 words that define them based on their initials). The patients then brainstorm on the theme of the workshop (e.g. self-esteem, emotions, physical activity) to establish their understanding of the topic. This is followed by a pedagogical section in which the facilitator shares his or her knowledge of the workshop theme. The last part of the workshop takes the form of a fun game to help the participants memorize and understand the concepts presented.

This program focuses on three key components:

Adapted Physical Activity (APA): The goal is to shift patients' perspectives on physical activity by highlighting its benefits. They engage in adapted exercises like gentle walking or gymnastics. Facilitators lead physical activity sessions and provide educational content, with the VLS offering workshops such as Nordic walking and gentle gymnastics.

Workshops have been specifically developed for the PE-STEREOBES program

STEREOBES workshop

This workshop is led by a clinical psychologist and incorporates techniques of psychoeducation on weight stigma, attribution modification, and self-compassion mediation. The goal of this workshop is to address the determinants of body image, coping strategies, and the internalization of weight biases. The workshop emphasizes a playful approach using games and group discussions to ensure an engaging experience and to encourage active participation from the patients.

“Let's ACT” workshop

This group workshop is led by a clinical psychologist and is based on Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), part of the third wave of cognitive-behavioral therapies. The aim is to help patients achieve greater psychological flexibility. The techniques used are designed to reduce the harmful effects of self-stigmatization and increase motivational factors for adaptive behavior change.

APA STEREOBES

These workshops will focus on simultaneous motor cognitive training [66], combining physical exercise with cognitive stimulation. The goal is to positively influence psychosocial factors such as self-esteem, self-efficacy, and chronic stress management, while improving executive functions and adherence to physical activity. The sessions will be of moderate intensity, featuring exercises based on cooperation and opposition. The emphasis is on a playful and gamified approach to physical activity, breaking away from the stereotypical association between exercise and high intensity. This will create a less intimidating environment, particularly for participants facing obesity, encouraging their engagement. Two workshops will be offered, one of which will focus on rhythm therapy dance. These sessions will foster a stimulating environment where participants can build social connections. The coordination of rhythmic movements, along with learning movement sequences, will physically and cognitively engage the participants.

STEREOBES booklet

The booklet is given to patients following the first individual assessment session, and the assessor also provides explanations for completing the exercises in the booklet. They are instructed to complete it throughout the course. The booklet is divided into three distinct sections: Physical Activity, Nutrition and Wellness. The nutrition and physical activity categories include techniques designed to improve our sense of self-efficacy. Exercises in the well-being category focus on coping strategies, internalizing prejudices and body image. For example, patients are given a worksheet to fill out, where they can describe a stigmatizing situation that caused them difficulty. They then indicate the strategies they employed to cope with the situation and specify whether these strategies were effective in the short and long term. This exercise is intended to help patients better identify whether the strategies they use in such contexts are effective, with the goal of reinforcing or modifying them as needed. These exercises are structured in a logical, step-by-step manner, and take the form of a table and a writing exercise. At each evaluation session, patients are asked about the exercises they have completed and any difficulties they may have encountered.

In order to ensure consistency in the follow-up of the workshops and in the evolution of the variables measured, the STEREOBES consortium divided the program into three steps, between each evaluation time, as shown in Fig. 1.

PE as usual (PEU)

Patients will follow the “Un poids en moins” program typically offered by Vie la Santé for the obesity pathway, including two workshops on weight management and bariatric surgery, as well as therapeutic education sessions tailored to their individual needs. For example, a patient who may need to improve their self-esteem or emotional management will be referred to workshops offered by the psychologist at the VLS. This program can last between three and six months, depending on the number of workshops patients attend based on their identified needs.

Data will be collected at the Vie La Santé, patients will complete a series of questionnaires using RedCap software. Sample size

To estimate the sample size required to achieve relevant statistical power, we used G.Power 3 software. This indicated that a sample size of 73 would give us 80% power to detect a significant difference (with a large effect size) between our arms, as well as potential interactions. This analysis was carried out using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) tests for repeated measures: within-between, interaction with a 4–2 model (four repeated measures and two conditions). This clinical study is anchored in an interventional approach and faces constraints due to the reality of patients' daily lives (e.g. distant distance from the facility, active professional life, family life…) we therefore increased the sample size by 35% to anticipate possible drop-out, so the sample should count 100 patients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed on an intention-to-treat and per protocol basis. Arms will be described and compared according to the following variables: socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, level of education), BMI, experience of stigmatization. A description of protocol deviations and causes of drop-out will be provided. Statistical analyses will be carried out using R and Statistica software. Clustering analyses will be carried out to determine whether, prior to the start of program follow-up, patients have characteristics that may influence perceived benefits.

To assess the effectiveness of the PE-STEREOBES program on the selected outcomes (objective 1), we will perform a 2 (Time: Before (T0) versus Pos-intervention) × 2 (Condition: PE as usual versus PE-STEREOBES) ANOVA. Condition will be considered as an inter-group factor and evaluation times as an intra-group factor. We will then carry out multivariate analyses of variance for repeated measures to examine the relationships that might exist between the different outcomes evaluated.

We will also analyze the potential mediating/moderating effects of psychosocial variables (self-esteem, body image, sense of coherence, stress and perceived social support) using repeated-measures MANOVAs. First, we will examine the effect of WBI on the relationship between following a TPE program and perceived benefits in terms of quality of life and sense of self-efficacy. The WBI should have a significant impact on the perceived benefits of following the program. We will then analyze the influence of other psychosocial variables (self-esteem, body image, sense of coherence and stress, perceived social support) on the mediating/moderating effect of WBI. If the results allow, a mediation model will be tested.

Conclusion

Individuals living with obesity are subjected to pervasive stigmatization in Western society, which adversely affects their quality of life and agency. While reducing the stigmatization of people with obesity in our society is challenging, it is nonetheless possible and relevant to implement interventions aimed at mitigating this threat and its harmful consequences. Although this psychosocial aspect has received little attention in previous studies, it appears to be a significant factor in obesity management. The STEREOBES project presents an innovative approach by integrating psychosocial engineering into the development of a physical exercise program within a healthcare setting. Its goal is to encourage patients to shift their attitudes and behaviors, enabling them to adopt more effective and sustainable strategies for managing their situations. This approach aims to enhance factors that reduce vulnerability and bolster resilience in the face of stigmatizing contexts.

However, a notable limitation expected during the inclusion phase is the challenge of distance and availability due to patients' active lifestyles. The Poitiers University Hospital, a renowned facility for the treatment of obesity, attracts patients from across the Nouvelle Aquitaine region, which may present logistical challenges. Evaluating this program will provide new, evidence-based insights that are both valuable and applicable to the field of obesity management. The STEREOBES project is focused on developing techniques to reduce the harmful effects of weight stigma, ultimately enabling patients to adopt more effective coping strategies.

Informed consent is required before inclusion. Persons with direct access to the data must respect their confidentiality. All data collected during the study will be anonymized, and only the inclusion number will appear in the database. A clinical research associate appointed by the sponsor visits the investigating center on a regular basis to control the data collection. Undesirable events / undesirable effects / incidents must be reported to the various health monitoring circuits. An audit may be carried out at any time by persons appointed by the sponsor and independent of those conducting the clinical study. Any substantial modification is the subject of a written amendment submitted to the promoter.
