Protocol for robust gene knockout and reliable validation in human cell lines using quad-guide RNA vectors
Authors: Michael Tobin, Jie Dai, Li-Chuan Chan, Weiwei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCRISPR-Cas9 is a powerful tool for editing genomic loci, however achieving high knockout efficiency at certain targets remains challenging. Here, we present a protocol for gene knockout using an all-in-one, quad-guide RNA-expressing vector. We describe steps for plasmid construction, virus preparation, transduction, and subsequent gene editing and functional validation within DLD-1 colorectal adenocarcinoma cells. This strategy provides an efficient workflow for gene knockout that is rapidly confirmed through PCR amplification of mRNA derived from the targeted gene loci.Subject areas: Genetics, CRISPR, Biotechnology and bioengineering

Before you begin

This protocol describes detailed steps for the design and application of a quadruple guide RNA (qgRNA) vector that enhances in vitro gene knockout of both intracellular and extracellular protein targets. Gene editing is confirmed following PCR amplification of extracted mRNA and gene-edited cells are subsequently confirmed for knockout and used for an antibody dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)-based assay. The protocol is divided into 6 steps: Vector design and synthesis, cell fluorescent marker expression (optional), lentiviral encapsulation, cell transduction and selection, verification of gene knockout, and functional assay application. While this paper describes the use and confirmation of the system in the DLD-1 colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line, the same approach can be applied to various human and mouse cell lines. This makes it useful for both studying gene functions and target validation in the early drug development process.

Innovation

Application of this workflow offers several benefits over existing approaches. First, delivering four single guide RNAs (sgRNA) simultaneously that target unique genomic sites, rather than a single region, enhances the probability of high on-target CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing that is essential for rapid, arrayed CRISPR screening. In this method, all guides are contained within an established, all-in-one vector backbone which reduces unnecessary genomic perturbations that can be introduced via Cas9 pre-integration (or co-sgRNA transduction) and functions across diverse cell types. Previous work has harnessed a similar multi-guide cassette for genome-wide screening; however, the cassette is limited to use within Cas9+ cells.1 For diverse human cell models, obtaining this Cas9+ population adds additional time and introduces genomic alterations into cells which may directly impact physiological relevance within knockout screens. S, this workflow directly quantifies coding sequence (CDS) to validate gene perturbance. Measuring CDS offers a simple and direct readout of how genomic alterations affect gene expression, given its upstream position in protein production. While other studies confirm sgRNA efficacy by analyzing genomic changes, it becomes cumbersome to validate targets across multiple, distant exons. An exon-based validation approach condenses the various regions of interest and enables simpler editing validation. Finally, this methodology is streamlined to support rapid and robust gene editing and validation using simple molecular biology techniques. PCR amplification and gel electrophoresis of CDS provide rapid and cost-effective approaches to qualitatively confirm gene editing, such as fragment deletions. Preliminary identification of genetic editing is particularly useful for targets without well-validated antibodies.

QgRNA cassette layout and targeting strategy

(A) Four unique constitutive promoters are placed upstream of four sgRNA-tracrRNA sequences to independently drive guide RNA expression.

(B) Multiple guide RNAs are selected to target distinct genomic DNA sites to drive gene knockout. Certain criteria enhance probability of knockout success, including targeting sites with high on-target score predictions, targeting early exons within the gene, and spacing guides far enough apart to prevent steric hindrance.

Schematic of the quad-gRNA expression vector

The pLentiCRISPR v2 plasmid was selected for downstream transduction. The delivery of Cas9, antibiotic gene, constitutive promoters, and the four gRNAs can be accomplished simultaneously using a single lentiviral integration.

PCR primer design

Timing: 2–6 days

4.Design primers for use in downstream PCR amplification and sequencing validation.

Design primers for use in downstream PCR amplification and sequencing validation.

Note: Primers can be designed using online tools. Here, a commercially available primer design site, Primer3Plus, identified primer sets that encompassed all sgRNA targeting sites. Primers should be designed using gene cDNA, which contains all four target sites within a condensed sequence and informs whether all sites can be efficiently analyzed using a single-shot PCR (reliable for total sequences <1000 bp). Programs for primer design should meet standard specifications for optimal performance, including 40%–60% C-G content and low self-dimerization. When it is possible to use PCR primers as sequencing primers, both forward and reverse primers should preferably be >15 bp away from the start site of any sgRNA editing site for proper validation (Troubleshooting 1).

Step-by-step method details

GFP transduction into target DLD-1 cells

Timing: 14 days

This step provides constitutive green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression in cells for identification of cell viability during selection processes as well as for tumor cell identification in downstream functional assays (This section is optional).

CRITICAL: Ensure all subsequent cell culture work takes place in a sterile, laminar flow hood using aseptic technique.

Impact of gene knockout through a flow-based phagocytosis assay

Timing: 9 days

Functional validation following gene knockout is essential to confirm cells retain their expected baseline behavior while also directly attributing any observed phenotypic effects to targeted gene disruption. An example of an ADCP functional validation assay is detailed here; however, the user can adapt this section to perform functional assays that are most relevant to their study.

It is well established that CD47 functions as a “don’t-eat-me” signal and inhibits phagocytic uptake of tumor cells (Kamber et al, 20212). Therefore, a phagocytosis uptake assay confirms the functional impact of gene knockout. The assay is adapted from Barkal et al, 2018.3

Expected outcomes

The quad-guide RNA vector system is designed to enhance the likelihood of gene knockout through delivery of multiple sgRNAs as well as to enable quick, visual confirmation of gene editing to the exonic sequence. The qgRNA vectors successfully edited multiple target genes, demonstrating proof of principle for use within larger scale screening assays. Notably, CRISPR editing, and subsequent gene knockout, arose from different DNA repair mechanisms, highlighting the versatility of this system.

Quad-guide RNA target sites selected for gene knockout were all located within the first three exons of the gene and primer-designed amplicons spanned total exonic lengths of approximately 550–650 base pairs. PCR amplicons derived from cell mRNA were first visualized using gel electrophoresis to discern perturbations leading to band displacement or disturbance. For all genes, cDNA was altered relative to parental and non-targeting controls (Figures 4A and 4B; Figure S2A). Visible alterations manifested in various forms, with ARID1A qgRNA-transduced cells displaying a band shift whereas CD47 qgRNA-transduced cells showed significantly decreased complete mRNA expression. Confirmation of similar mRNA extraction across all conditions was provided by assessing the overall transcript levels of the control gene GAPDH (Figure S3). The occurrence of each perturbation is driven in large part by the genomic distance between the cut site locations. The proximity of target sequences within the ARID1A gene increased the potential for fragment deletions to occur during repair, as all four targeted regions are spaced hundreds of base pairs apart. At such short distances between CRISPR target sites, fragment deletions are known to readily occur and indeed a ∼250 bp fragment deletion is easily identified within the ARID1A transduced population. In contrast, all amplicon bands identifying CD47 gene editing remained clustered at ∼600 base pairs, suggesting that any exon edits do not include large fragment deletions. Instead, individual nucleotide substitutions and small indels at cut sites would be expected— as the selected sgRNA targets for CD47 were predominantly spaced >10kb apart in the genomic sequence, a distance too far for excising cut fragments. Nevertheless, the diminished quantity of CD47 amplicon fragments compared to controls is apparent and correspond well to decreased mRNA levels that can arise from cell surveillance pathways, such as nonsense-mediated mRNA decay.4 The mRNA instability or decay observed ranges from moderate to extreme across qgRNA transduced cell lines and can be further verified using additional primer pairs targeting locations within the amplicon interior (Figure S2B). Overall, preliminary observations of gene editing are effectively illustrated using visualization of cDNA amplicons via gel electrophoresis.

While visualization of amplicon length and quantity can provide initial indications of gene editing, sequencing was needed for an in-depth understanding of genomic edits and how they may influence downstream protein expression. Thus, all cell populations underwent Sanger sequencing to assess gene integrity via amplified cDNA. Sequencing results validated preliminary electrophoresis findings and provided confirmation of edits occurring around one or more guide target sites for all genes (Figures 4C and 4D; Figure S2C). The ARID1A-edited population was confirmed to have two independent fragment deletions located at qgRNA target sites, demonstrating that four sgRNAs can all successfully edit the target gene. Combined, the deletions and resulting missense sequence between them altered 50+ amino acids of the ARID1A protein. Within the CD47-edited population, sequencing revealed that various nucleotide substitutions occurred primarily at the 5′ end of the consensus sequence transcript. These substitutions were primarily clustered at the first guide locus, with additional subpopulations showing lower efficacy alterations at other sgRNA target sites (inset). In-depth analysis of this population revealed that among the various nucleotide substitutions that occurred at the first target site is a nucleotide substitution within the start codon. A simple substitution at this site would be predicted to highly disrupt protein expression, emphasizing that even small genomic modifications from qgRNAs can have detrimental impacts on protein expression. Depending on sgRNA on-target cutting efficiency, modifications to the cDNA consensus sequence can range from minimal to expansive.

Effective gene knockout using the qgRNAs was subsequently confirmed at the protein level. Across all gene-edited cells, protein levels were highly reduced or eliminated (Figures 4E and 4F). Evaluation of ARID1A expression was conducted using immunocytochemistry. Complete loss of ARID1A protein (marked by asterisks) was observed within the cell population. Notably, the gene knockout was not homogenous, as other cells within the bulk population did exhibit low to normal levels of protein expression compared to relevant controls. For CD47 transduced cells, gene edits to the start codon and other exons contributed to ∼97% protein knockout when assessed by flow cytometric staining of surface CD47. Importantly, results using qgRNAs seem robust, as a s, independent CD47 qgRNA transduction with the same vector again shows a similarly effective result in delivering high efficiency protein knockout (∼95%), despite a non-identical editing outcome (Figure S4). Overall, the qgRNAs efficiently edited genomic loci to induce high levels of protein knockout.

After obtaining qgRNA vector-mediated CD47 knockout cells, the population was further tested to validate expected behavior and confirm suitability of transduced cells for subsequent downstream functional assays. CD47 is a well-established anti-phagocytic gene whose protein acts as a known “marker of self” on the body’s cells. The expression of CD47 and its subsequent interaction with its receptor Sirpα on macrophages significantly halts macrophage uptake of cells. Therefore, confirmation of CD47 knockout can be investigated functionally using assays measuring cellular uptake via antibody dependent cellular phagocytosis. As expected, anti-EGFR antibody opsonization of CD47 KO DLD-1 cells led to increased macrophage phagocytosis relative to NTC cells, consistent with well-documented CD47 biology (Figure 5). This trend remained consistent across repeated experiments utilizing macrophages differentiated from different PBMC donors.

In summary, employing qgRNA transduction to facilitate robust gene knockout, together with monitoring gene editing via active mRNA transcripts, introduces an efficient approach for rapidly executing and verifying knockouts in cell lines. Additionally, this platform provides a reliable method for knockout validation that is not restricted by the absence of target gene antibodies, offering enhanced quality control prior to array screening.

Limitations

Despite the proven reproducibility of qgRNA-mediated gene editing, several technical considerations remain critical for effective implementation.

First, while the inclusion of multiple guide RNAs within this system increases the likelihood of gene knockout, there is also a higher cumulative risk of off-target activity. The combined use of four guides, as opposed to one or two, heightens the probability for unintended editing events across the genome, even when all individual guides predict low off-target editing. While many of these off-target sites may not affect a cell’s behavior, some could introduce confounding phenotypes into the cell population. Consequently, the gRNAs used within the qgRNA vector require careful design which balances predicted on-target efficiency and minimizing consequential off-target potential.

Next, achieving gene alterations which can be visually identified via gel electrophoresis relies on the presence of well-designed, proximal sgRNA targets. The qgRNA cassette enables multiple, simultaneous gene editing events which may be devised to encourage DNA fragment deletions. However, fragment deletions occur more readily at target sites separated by only a few hundred base pairs, as seen in the above ARID1A genomic sequence. The fragment deletions that are easily identified through CDS amplification decreases sharply with guides targeting distant loci. In the above, sgRNAs targeting CD47 perform efficient, but independent, editing that drives subtle composition changes to the genome, such as nucleotide substitutions, that are not easily identified by size separation. In general, user selection of optimal crRNA sites for every gene can differ widely based on a unique combination of on-target cutting efficiency, off-target recognition, and protospacer locations. For genes containing limited sgRNA target sites with strong predicted activity as well as for genes with well-predicted crRNA targets spread widely across the CDS, opportunities for PCR verification of gene editing may be insufficient. Downstream validation is essential in such cases, with only sequencing providing insight into genomic editing.

Furthermore, the inherent stochasticity of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated repair mechanisms introduces uncertainty regarding the functional knockout, as even the successful deletion of exonic fragments may result in the retention of truncated but functional protein products. For instance, in a subpopulation of ARID1A knockout cells, proximal sgRNAs targeting exon 1 induced independent deletions that unintentionally restored the reading frame. Although the gRNAs were designed in silico for a disrupted open reading frame (ORF), an additional, unpredicted base pair deletion led to sequential +2 and +1 frameshifts to the transcript’s reading frame, which culminated in a return to the native ORF. Consequently, despite a 200-bp internal deletion and a localized nonsense sequence, the downstream exons—including the critical C-terminal functional domain—remained in-frame. This resulted in the translation of a partially truncated but potentially functional protein isoform. Such unpredictable repair outcomes underscore the necessity of additional experimental safeguards, such as targeting sequences in close proximity to essential functional domains or distributing fragment deletion sites across multiple critical exons.

Finally, the efficiency of viral transduction and sgRNA targeting is highly contingent upon cell-type-specific characteristics and physiological profiles. The DLD-1 colorectal adenocarcinoma line employed in this study is well-characterized and highly permissive to lentiviral transduction, achieving robust expression at a low multiplicity of infection. However, extending this protocol to more recalcitrant primary or differentiated cell types, such as lymphocytes or post-mitotic neurons will likely necessitate further optimization. Factors such as viral titer concentration, auxiliary transduction enhancers, and cell-cycle-dependent integration efficiency must be carefully calibrated to ensure high editing frequencies in these challenging models.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Spatial constraints on primer design

The proximity of high-activity gRNA targets to the translation initiation site (TIS) frequently constrains the available flanking sequence for primer design. When targets are located at the extreme 5′ end of the coding sequence, the lack of viable upstream mRNA regions restricts the selection of optimal primer pairs for both PCR-based screening and subsequent sequencing validation (related to PCR Primer Design).

Potential solution: Extend primer range into the exon untranslated regions

Targeting early exons is prioritized to maximize knockout probability, yet it necessitates adapting to the spatial constraints of these loci. When 5′-proximal targets limit exonic primer design, the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) provide essential flanking templates. As UTRs are retained in processed mRNA but absent from the final protein, they offer a viable substrate for the PCR amplification and sequence validation.

Problem 2: Virus transduces a low number of cells

After GFP+ lentiviral introduction to cells, an extremely low percentage of cells express fluorescence (related to Step 4).

Potential solution: Perform cell selection to enhance the population

If the number of cells expressing GFP is low after lentiviral transduction, multiple routes can be applied to enhance the ratio of cells containing desired protein expression. First, a procedural step that introduces antibiotic selection on the transduced population can help to eliminate non-transduced cells. For the above EGFP viral vector, supplementing media with zeocin would be recommended to positively select for transduced cells containing both the bleomycin resistance gene and EGFP. Alternatively, the cell population can be transduced a s time at a higher lentiviral MOI. In hard to transduce cells, increasing the MOI of the lentivirus delivered to a cell population can enable more efficient integration and protein expression. Estimated optimal MOIs for various cell types can be found in literature.

Problem 3: Modest lentiviral titer for quad-guide vectors

Following co-transfection of qgRNA and packaging plasmids, the quantity of virus particles produced is determined to be low. This lower packaging efficiency is often attributable to the increased size and structural complexity of the qgRNA expression cassette (related to Step 9).

Potential solution: Modify the lentiviral backbone to reduce virus size

The first approach for an insufficient viral titer would be to scale up the viral production to one or several 10 cm dishes. However, if reagents are limited or increased scaling does not suffice, several options remain to circumvent the packaging constraints inherent to the all-in-one system. First, the plentiCRISPR v2 backbone can be modified before production to switch to strong constitutive promoters, such as use of a RSV or CMV promoter upstream of the 5′ LTR, or to add a WPRE sequence between the transgene and the 3′ LTR. Alternatively, if this proves insufficient, the Cas9 component can be removed from the vector and delivered separately beforehand using transient transfection from ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes or AAV. This modification enhances viral titers and facilitates the high multiplicity of infection (MOI) necessary for efficient editing in recalcitrant cell lines.

Problem 4: Gel electrophoresis shows no DNA band across all conditions

No amplicon is present after PCR amplification (related to Step 17).

Potential solution: Optimize PCR parameters

Various PCR primers have unique parameters including CG content, melting temperature, and sary structure formation. If a chosen primer set is specific for the gene but shows little or no amplification of cell cDNA across all conditions, optimization of PCR parameters may be required. This includes adjusting settings such as PCR extension time and annealing temperature.

Problem 5: PCR target amplicon is larger than ∼1000 base pairs

The size of the gene or spacing between optimal sgRNA target sites necessitates a large amplicon for exon amplification and sequencing verification (related to Step 17).

Potential solution: Design interior primers that encompass separate but overlapping portions of the exonic sequence

For many applications, including Sanger sequencing analysis, typical fragment read lengths span from 700 bp–1 kb. However, in large genes with spread sgRNA sites, designing inner primers which split the total mRNA length sequence which needs to be amplified and/or sequenced enables reliable confirmation of the entirety of the exon sequence. Alternatively, if the size is no issue for PCR amplification but the size is too large for reliable Sanger sequencing, an internal oligo can be designed to provide high quality reads throughout the region.
