Abstract
Besides the classical offline setup of machine learning, stream learning constitutes a well-established setup where data arrives over time in potentially non-stationary environments. Concept drift, the phenomenon that the underlying distribution changes over time poses a significant challenge. Yet, despite high practical relevance, there is little to no foundational theory for learning in the drifting setup comparable to classical statistical learning theory in the offline setting. This can be attributed to the lack of an underlying object comparable to a probability distribution as in the classical setup. While there exist approaches to transfer ideas to the streaming setup, these start from a data perspective rather than an algorithmic one. In this work, we suggest a new model of data over time that is aimed at the algorithm’s perspective. Instead of defining the setup using time points, we utilize a window-based approach that resembles the inner workings of most stream learning algorithms. We compare our framework to others from the literature on a theoretical basis, showing that in many cases both model the same situation. Furthermore, we perform a numerical evaluation and showcase an application in the domain of critical infrastructure. Keywords: Streaming Data ⋅ \cdot Concept Drift ⋅ \cdot Stream Learning ⋅ \cdot Theoretical Foundations of Stream Learning ⋅ \cdot Theory of Concept Drift.

Introduction

Most works on machine learning consider the batch setup where data is drawn i.i.d. from a single distribution. However, in many real-world applications, data is generated over time and possibly subject to various changes which are commonly referred to as
concept drift

[
2
,
6
,
1
]
or drift for shorthand. Drift can arise from a variety of sources as, for instance, seasonal patterns, shifting user demands, sensor aging, and environmental factors.

These frequently unforeseen distributional changes can yield substantial drops in the performance of machine learning models that often require retraining or even the redesign of entire pipelines. Thus, a considerable body of work focuses on the
stream learning
setup
[
6
,
1
]
and designs models that are adaptable to distributional changes in the data arriving as an infinite sequence known as a
data stream
. A common strategy in this field is to use sliding window-based approaches: By updating the model using only the most recent samples while discarding older ones, the model adapts to the current distribution
[
1
]
.

In contrast to practical approaches, theory in the stream learning setup is still limited. As standard frameworks, for example, statistical learning theory
[
7
]
, impose the assumption that all considered data is drawn i.i.d. from a single distribution, drift complicates theoretical analysis
[
4
]
. Besides, the established way of modeling data over time and defining drift are not suitable for analyzing stream machine learning on a theoretical level
[
3
,
2
,
4
]
. Common definitions focus on a time-point-wise perspective
[
3
,
1
]
, assigning a distribution per time point rather than over a period. Yet, as described before, most algorithms employ time-window-based approaches, looking at data over time spans
[
1
]
. This raises questions about the validity of point-wise drift definitions for practical considerations.

To address these challenges, we propose a new framework to model data over time. It is centered around the idea of time windows rather than time points. Thus, our framework aligns directly with the algorithmic perspective and provides a more practical basis for defining drift. We compare our approach to traditional time point definition and investigate the mathematical relationships between the two. Interestingly, despite their distinct starting points, both frameworks capture similar information. Thus, our analysis not only offers deeper theoretical insights but also justifies previously used approaches. In particular, it supports the
time-as-a-feature
paradigm
[
3
]
that has led to the development of several recent algorithms
[
2
]
.

This paper is structured as follows: First (
Section

2
) we recall the basics of stream learning and concept drift. In the second part (
Section

3
) we introduce our new formal framework (
Section

3.1
), and compare it to the existing definitions of drift theoretically (
Section

3.2
) and algorithmically (
Section

4
). Afterward, we perform two experiments (
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
), showcasing both the found connection between the window-based and time-point-based setup as well as a practical relevant use case of our ideas in the domain of critical infrastructure, before we conclude the paper (
Section

6
).

Preliminary Notes: Stream Learning and Concept Drift

In the classical batch setup of machine learning one considers a dataset
S=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}
are i.i.d.
[
7
]
. In
stream learning
this setup is extended to an infinite sequence of observations
S=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
with the single data points
X_{i}
arriving over time. To deal with this, most stream learning algorithms are based on
sliding windows
which typically contain the last
n
data points, i.e.,
S_{i-n:i}=(X_{i-n},\dots,X_{i})

[
6
]
. In this case, data analysis and model training are performed based on
S_{i-n:i}
replacing the dataset in the batch setup. In particular,
S_{i-n:i}
is updated once the next data point
X_{i+1}
arrives
[
1
,
6
]
.

As the data is collected over longer periods of time, the underlying distribution might change – a phenomenon known as
concept drift

[
1
,
6
,
2
]
. There are different approaches to model concept drift.
The most common way is to allow each data point to follow a (potentially different) distribution
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
. Drift is then defined as a change of the data-point-wise distribution, i.e.,
\exists i,j:\mathcal{D}_{i}\neq\mathcal{D}_{j}

[
1
,
6
]
. Thus, finite streams without drift correspond to the classical i.i.d. setup.

However, as pointed out by
[
3
,
4
]
this setup is not well suited to formulating learning theory results due to its lack of an underlying reference object like a distribution in classical statistics. To solve this issue
[
4
,
3
]
suggested explicitly considering time by introducing a new random variable
T_{i}
marking the arrival time of
X_{i}
. In this fully statistical setup, the data point’s distribution is determined by the arrival time, i.e.,
X_{i}\mid T_{i}=t\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
. Drift is defined as the probability of observing two different distributions being larger than zero. This leads to:

Definition 1

(Distribution Process, Time Window, Holistic Distribution
[

]

Let

Due to the fully statistical nature of this setup, it is possible to draw new data points from a distribution process.
Furthermore, as pointed out by
[
2

4

, a crucial advantage of this model is that it allows us to derive a window-wise distribution in a formally sound way. Since windows play a fundamental role in stream learning algorithms
[
6
]
, an approach corresponding to windows is better suited to transfer theoretical ideas like statistical learning theory to the streaming setup
[
4
]
.

In this case one considers the time-window-based samples, i.e., for a stream
S=((X_{1},T_{1}),(X_{2},T_{2}),\dots)
and
W\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
we have the
window sample

\displaystyle S_{W}=(X_{i})_{i:T_{i}\in W}.

As the data distribution depends on time, taking the limiting distribution relates to taking the sampling frequency to infinite
[
2
]
so that for increasing frequencies
n
the size of
S_{W}^{(n)}
extracted from the stream
S^{(n)}
tends to infinity.

If
S
was initially obtained as an i.i.d. sample of the holistic distribution
\mathcal{D}
then
S_{W}
is an i.i.d. sample of
\mathcal{D}_{W}

[
3
]
. However, in practice, different ways of obtaining samples can be used. This raises the question of whether an algorithm-centered point of view should be favored over a time-point-centered one.

In the following, we will provide a new way to model data over time that primarily targets the distribution on windows and thus a more algorithmic point of view. We will then compare the resulting formal model to the time-point-wise point of view discussed above.

3
A Stream Learning Inspired Framework

As discussed in the last section, basically all algorithmic approaches to stream learning are based on the idea of sliding windows. On the other hand, most formal models used to describe data over time consider the data for each time point separately. As pointed out by
[
3
]
transitioning from the time-point-wise point of view to the window-based point of view is not only crucial for understanding stream learning but also poses a challenge as it is in general not clear how to compute these window distributions in the different formal models.

To bridge this gap we will now introduce a new formal model that, in contrast to most formal models used to describe stream setups, is not based on the time-point-based point of view but the more algorithmic window-based point of view.

3.1
A Formal Model of Window-based Distributions

To construct a window-based formal model for stream learning we need to specify two things: 1) the windows suited for analysis, and 2) the distributions thereon. For simplicity, we will assume that a time window is a set of time points which is not necessarily an interval. The main modeling thus focuses on the question of which sets of time points are reasonable windows and how the distributions placed on those need to relate to each other. We will address the concept of windows in
Definition

2
and the concept of the distributions on those windows in
Definition

3
.

To model the
window system (WS)
we will not focus on a specific algorithmic implementation but the more general question of which windows constitute a reasonable choice in the sense that they are “large enough” to admit estimates when we take the sampling frequency to infinity. For independent
X_{i}
this is the case if
|S_{W}^{(n)}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
We obtain the following definition, which to formally capture this idea also requires considering
null windows
that are not suited for estimates:

Definition 2

(Window System (WS))
.

Let
(\mathcal{T},\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}})
be a measure space. A
window system on
\mathcal{T}

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
is a set system
\mathcal{W}\subset\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
of

\sigma

of

1.

\mathcal{W}
is closed under finite disjoint unions

2.

\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{N}
are disjoint and
\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}
is a semi-ring

3.

\mathcal{W}

\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}

(\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}})_{|W}\subset\sigma(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})
for all
W\in\mathcal{W}

4.

\mathcal{W}
covers
\mathcal{T}
, i.e., for
S\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
if

for all
W\in\mathcal{W}
then
S\in\mathcal{N}
.

If there is a
W\in\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{T}\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
we say that the window system has a
finite horizon
.

We shortly explain the axioms: the overarching idea is that
\mathcal{W}
captures sets which allow a reasonable statistical estimate of the properties of the underlying process.
Axiom 1 captures that if we have two samples which are large enough for that, then so is their union.
Axiom 2 models the fact that there are sets in which there are no observations which can be obtained e.g. by intersecting larger samples.
Axioms 3 and 4 assure that the windows encode just as much information as the
\sigma
-algebra.

The term
finite horizon
relates to the idea that we can, in theory, keep the entire stream in memory. It is therefore a way to limit our considerations to streams of finite time spans.

Based on this concept define
windowed distributions systems (WDS)
that capture the observed windows underlying probabilities and their consistency. For each window, we define the limiting distribution from which the sample
S_{W}
is drawn. The concept of null windows is included by requiring that two windows that only differ by a null window have the same limiting distribution as they produce the essentially same samples, resulting in:

Definition 3

(Windowed Distribution System (WDS), Constant WDS, Extension)
.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
be a window system and
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}})
a measure space. A

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D}_{W})
is a collection of probability measures
(\mathcal{D}_{W})_{W\in\mathcal{W}}
on
\mathcal{X}
such that

1.

for

with

\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}}=\mathcal{D}_{W_{2}}
,

2.

W_{1}\subset W_{2}\subset\dots\nearrow W

W,W_{1},W_{2},\dots\in\mathcal{W}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{W_{n}}=\mathcal{D}_{W}

3.

for pairwise disjoint windows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{W_{I}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{W_{i}}}{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}}\qquad\text{where}\qquad W_{I}:=\bigcup_{i\in I}W_{i}.

The axioms capture that the null windows do not impact the probabilities (axiom 1), probabilities are compatible with sampling (axiom 2) and compatible with unions (axiom 3).
Axioms 1 and 3 are motivated by finite samples: if two estimates are made based on the same samples, then the estimated value is the same (axiom 1), if we combine two samples then the combined estimate is a convex combination of the single estimates (axiom 3).

In the next section, we will show that the concept of WDSs is compatible with the time-point-wise model of a distribution process but more general.

Finally, we consider a more practical problem. Water distribution networks (WDNs) are a crucial part of the critical infrastructure supplying private and commercial customers reliably with clean and safe drinking water. To ensure the functionality of the system, planning and control based on water demand estimates are mandatory

In this case study we assume that instead of having information on the actual water consumption at every time point as visualized in

\mathcal{D}_{t}
.
To approach the problem, we model the consumption of a single household using an inhomogeneous compound Poisson process, i.e., we assume that at random times a random amount of water is consumed. As a consequence, we can model communal consumption as a normal distribution whose parameters can be derived from the Poisson processes. This allows us to simplify
Algorithm

1

\mathbf{D}

[

]

6
Concluding Remarks and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a novel framework for handling data over time and concept drift by focusing on a time-window-based approach rather than the traditional point-in-time perspective. Our framework aligns more closely with practical, algorithmic needs, making it a useful tool for real-world applications where data distributions evolve. We compared our framework to the existing ones on a theoretical level and found that both model the same situation despite their different starting points, providing yet another justification for the ’time-as-a-feature’ paradigm. We numerically evaluated our approach through experiments showing that our window-based setup can effectively reconstruct the information captured by the point-wise setup, thereby validating the equivalence numerically.
To illustrate the practical impact of our framework, we applied it to a case study in critical infrastructure, specifically within water distribution networks. This application highlights that our consideration can provide meaningful insights for considering dynamic environments, showing the relevance of our ideas beyond purely theoretical insights.

Unlike the framework proposed by
[
3
]
, which is restricted to finite horizons, we conjecture that our framework has the potential to handle infinite temporal horizons. Such considerations offer a foundation to statistically model infinite data streams which we will address in future work.

\displaystyle=P(A)\sum_{u\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\underbrace{P(\mathbf{U}_{n}^{u}\mid A)}_{=\mathbf{1}[\mathbf{U}_{n}^{u}\subset A]}\underbrace{\sum_{v\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}Q_{\mathbf{U}_{n}^{u}}(B\cap\mathbf{V}_{n}^{v})}_{=Q_{\mathbf{U}_{n}^{u}}(B)}

\displaystyle=P(A)Q_{A}(B)

where the last equality is condition 2. Similarly

\displaystyle K_{n+1}(A\times B\mid(x,y)\;)=\mathbf{1}[x\in A,y\in B]

from which we can conclude that for all
m\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)^{m}\times(A\times B)^{C}\times Z^{\infty})

(4)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\quad G(Z^{n-1+m-1}\times(A\times B)\times(A\times B)^{C}\times Z^{\infty})=0.

And by adding this in, we get

\displaystyle G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)\times Z^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)\times(A\times B)\times Z^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\underbrace{G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)\times(A\times B)^{C}\times Z^{\infty})}_{=0}

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)^{2}\times Z^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad\>\>\vdots

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)^{m}\times Z^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)\times(A\times B)^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\underbrace{G(Z^{n-1}\times(A\times B)^{C}\times(A\times B)^{\infty})}_{=0}

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n}\times(A\times B)^{\infty})

\displaystyle\qquad\>\>\vdots

\displaystyle\qquad=\quad G(Z^{n-1+m-1}\times(A\times B)^{\infty})

where the forth equality follows from the fact that
G
is a measure and the sequence of sets is monotonously decreasing. So in particular, we have

\displaystyle G(Z^{n-1+m-1}\times(A\times B)^{\infty})=P(A)Q_{A}(B).

Therefore, the marginal distribution of
G
at infinity is indeed the measure
H
. We now need to extract the measure from the sequence. To do so, we will show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}G(Z^{n}\times C^{\infty})
is a pre-measure and then extend it.

Define
G_{n}(C)=G(Z^{n-1}\times C^{\infty}),\;G_{\infty}(C)=\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(C)
which exists as the limit of an increasing bounded sequence and for
A\in\mathcal{U}_{n},B\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
we have
G_{\infty}(A\times B)=G_{n}(A\times B)=P(A)Q_{A}(B)
. We have to show that
G_{\infty}
is a measure.

Denote by
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}
the ring over
\mathcal{U}_{n}*\mathcal{V}_{n}
or
\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{V}
, respectively. Every set
C\in\mathcal{R}
is a finite disjoint union
C=C_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup C_{n}
with
C_{i}\in\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{V}
. Because
\mathcal{U}_{n}
and
\mathcal{V}_{n}
are monotonous, for every
A\times B\in\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{V}=\{A\times B\mid A\in\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{U}_{n},B\in\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{n}\}
there exists an
n
such that
A\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
and
B\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
and therefore
A\times B\in\mathcal{U}_{n}*\mathcal{V}_{n}
.
Hence, there is an
n
such that
C_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}*\mathcal{V}_{n}
for all
i
and thus
C\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

For any disjoint sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
set
C=C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{k}
then we have

\displaystyle(C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{k})^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}C_{j}^{(i)}

where

\displaystyle C_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}C_{j}^{\infty}&\text{ if }i=0\\
C_{j}^{i}\times(C\setminus C_{j})\times C^{\infty}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

As
C_{j}^{(i)}\subset C_{j}^{i}\times(C\setminus C_{j})\times Z^{\infty}
for all
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}*\mathcal{V}_{n}
we have

\displaystyle G(Z^{n-1}\times C_{j}^{(i)})\leq G(Z^{n-1}\times C_{j}^{i}\times(C\setminus C_{j})\times Z^{\infty})=0

by
Eq.

4
. As we can write all
C_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
as
C_{i1}\cup\dots\cup C_{it},\;C_{ij}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}*\mathcal{V}_{n}
disjoint the statement extends to all
C_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle G_{n}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=G(Z^{n-1}\times(C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{k})^{\infty})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\underbrace{G(Z^{n-1}\times C_{j}^{(0)})}_{=G_{n}(C_{j})}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{G(Z^{n-1}\times C_{j}^{(i)})}_{=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}G_{n}(C_{j})

Thus,
G_{n}
and hence also
G_{\infty}
are finitely additive.

Thus
\sigma
-additivity is equivalent to show continuity from below: for
C_{1}\subset C_{2}\subset\dots\nearrow C,\;C,C_{1},C_{2},\dots\in\mathcal{R}
consider

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}G_{\infty}(C_{m})

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(C_{m})

\displaystyle\overset{!^{1}}{=}\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}G_{n}(C_{m})

\displaystyle\overset{!^{2}}{=}\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(C)

\displaystyle\overset{!^{3}}{=}G_{\infty}(C),

where

!^{1}
holds because
Z^{n}\times C_{m}^{\infty}\subset Z^{n+1}\times C_{m}^{\infty}
so the double sequence
G_{n}(C_{m})
is increasing in both arguments
m
and
n
and therefore the limits commute,

!^{2}
holds because
Z^{n}\times C_{1}^{\infty}\subset Z^{n}\times C_{2}^{\infty}\subset\dots\nearrow Z^{n}\times C^{\infty}
for each
n
,
and

!^{3}
holds since as above
C
is contained in some
\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

Hence, as
G_{n}(\emptyset)=0
for all
n
we see that
G_{\infty}
is a pre-measure, indeed.
By Carathéodory’s extension theorem, we have that
G_{\infty}
extends uniquely to a measure on
\sigma(\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{V})=\Sigma_{X}\otimes\Sigma_{Y}
.
∎

Furthermore, we have the following quite helpful lemma:

Lemma 1

(Carathéodory’s extension theorem for WS with finite horizon)
.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
a WS on
(\mathcal{T},\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}})
with finite horizon (and
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}})
a measurable space). Then
\sigma(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})=\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
.

In particular, for every finite pre-measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}
(
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}*(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})
) the outer measure
\mu^{*}
is a measure on
\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
(
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
). This extension is unique, i.e., for every measure
\nu
on
\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
(
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
) with
\nu_{|\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}}=\mu
(
\nu_{|\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}*(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})}=\nu
) it holds
\mu^{*}(S)=\nu(S)
for all
S\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
(
S\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
).

Furthermore, if
\mu(N)=0
(
\mu(\mathcal{X}\times N)=0
) for all
N\in\mathcal{N}
then the total value is determined on the finite horizon, i.e., for
W\in\mathcal{W}
with
\mathcal{T}\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
we have
\mu(W)=\mu^{*}(\mathcal{T})
(
\mu(\mathcal{X}\times W)=\mu^{*}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T})
).

Proof.

If
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
has a finite horizon, then there is a
W\in\mathcal{W}
with
\mathcal{T}\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
. Thus, for
S\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
we have
S=(S\cap W)\cup(S\setminus W)
and
S\cap W\in\sigma(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})
and
S\setminus W\subset\mathcal{T}\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
so
S\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
. Hence
S\in\sigma(\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N})
.

The extension statement is just Carathéodory’s extension theorem.

For the last part take
\mu(W)=\mu^{*}(W)+\mu^{*}(\mathcal{T}\setminus W)=\mu^{*}(\mathcal{T})
as
\mathcal{T}\setminus W\in\mathcal{N}
and thus
\mu^{*}(\mathcal{T}\setminus W)=0
by assumption. The same holds true when taking the product with
\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Clearly, this lemma can be extended to WS with non-finite horizons. However, as already discussed in the paper, this is beyond the scope of this paper and will not be covered here.

.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D}_{W})

Let

\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}

\mathcal{R}
.
For

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}P_{T}(S)\mathcal{D}_{S}(A)&\text{ if }S\in\mathcal{W}\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

We claim that
\mu
is a pre-measure on
\mathcal{R}
.

Obviously,

as
\emptyset\in\mathcal{N}

2
to

Y=(\mathcal{X},\sigma(\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots\}))

(as a
\sigma
-algebra on
W
),

, and with

where the latter is well-defined as

and

are invariant under changes on null windows. Thus, there is a measure

such that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\times W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\times W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}H\left(A_{i}\times W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(A_{i}\times W_{i}\right),

is
\sigma

As

has a finite horizon and

1
, the outer measure

is the unique measure that extends

and is a probability measure.
Therefore,

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{X}\times W)=P_{T}(W)
and
\mathcal{D}(A\times W\mid\mathcal{X}\times W)=\mathcal{D}_{W}(A)
.

Now let
(\bar{\mathcal{W}},\bar{\mathcal{N}},\bar{\mathcal{D}})=i(\mathcal{D})
. For
S\in\mathcal{W}
we have
P_{T}(S)>0
and conversely
S\in\bar{\mathcal{W}}
if and only if

\mathcal{W}\subset\bar{\mathcal{W}}
. Analogous, we see

. Furthermore,
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{W}(A)=\mathcal{D}(A\times W\mid\mathcal{X}\times W)=\mathcal{D}_{W}(A)

∎

.

The proof of

Lemma 3
.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D}_{W})
be a non-constant WDS with

. Fixate
W_{*}\in\mathcal{W}
. Then there exists a unique measure

on
\mathcal{T}
that is compatible with
\mathcal{D}_{W}
and
P_{T}(W_{*})=1

If
(\tilde{\mathcal{W}},\tilde{\mathcal{N}},\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{W})
is another non-constant WDS on a measure space
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S})
with fewer measurable sets, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}\subset\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
, with
W_{*}\in\tilde{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathcal{W}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathcal{N}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{W}=\mathcal{D}_{W}
for all
W\in\tilde{\mathcal{W}}
, then the obtained
\tilde{P}_{T}
is the restriction of
P_{T}
to
\mathcal{S}

\tilde{P}_{T}=(P_{T})_{|\mathcal{S}}

Proof.

Observe that there exists a finite set of pairwise disjoint

such that
\mathcal{N}\cup\{W_{1},\dots,W_{n}\}
is a semi-ring
\mathcal{R}
with
\sigma(\mathcal{R})=\sigma(\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{W})

W\setminus(W_{1}\cup\dots\cup W_{n})\in\mathcal{N}
for all

we have that
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
has a finite horizon and thus
\sigma(\mathcal{R})=\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
by
Lemma

1
.

By definition there are
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
such that for every
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{W_{I}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{D}_{W_{i}}}{\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}}

where
W_{I}=\cup_{i\in I}W_{i}
.

As
\mathcal{D}_{W}
is not constant w.l.o.g. w.m.a.
\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{W_{2}}
.
Thus, there exists a unique
\lambda
such that
\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}\cup W_{2}}=\lambda\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}}+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{D}_{W_{2}}
and as
\lambda_{2}/(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})=1-\lambda
every choice of
\lambda_{1}
uniquely determines
\lambda_{2}
.

Furthermore, for every
i
we have
\mathcal{D}_{W_{i}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{W_{1}}
or
\mathcal{D}_{W_{i}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{W_{2}}
then by the same argument as before we see that
\lambda_{i}/(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{i})
or
\lambda_{i}/(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{i})
is uniquely determined and therefore
\lambda_{i}
is uniquely determined by
\lambda_{1}
. Hence,
\lambda_{1}
determines the value of
\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}
uniquely.

Now, set

P_{T}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{1}[W_{i}\setminus S\in\mathcal{N}]
.
Obviously,
P_{T}(S)=P_{T}(S\cup N)
for all
N\in\mathcal{N}
and therefore
P_{T}(S_{1})=P_{T}(S_{2})
if

. Hence, as every
W\in\mathcal{W}
is up to a set in
\mathcal{N}
a disjoint union of

we see that

is finitely additive, and as

is finite it is thus a pre-measure.
Therefore, by
Lemma

1
, it extends uniquely to a measure on
\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Normalizing

to assure

For the the extension property use that we may choose the same
\lambda_{i}
as before. As those are uniquely determined up to scaling the statement follows.

Lemma 4
.

Let
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D}_{W})
be a non-constant WDS with finite horizon. Any finite content
P_{T}
on
\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}
that is compatible with
\mathcal{D}_{W}
, i.e., fulfills conditions 1.-3. of
Definition

5
, extends uniquely to a compatible measure.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1
, it suffices to show that
P_{T}
is
\sigma
-additive.
Let
W_{1},W_{2},\dots\in\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}
with
\cup_{i}W_{i}\in\mathcal{W}\cup\mathcal{N}
. Set
N=\{i\mid W_{i}\in\mathcal{N}\}
then it holds

\displaystyle P_{T}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=P_{T}\left(\left(\bigcup_{i\in N}W_{i}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{i\not\in N}W_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=P_{T}\left(\bigcup_{i\in N}W_{i}\right)+P_{T}\left(\bigcup_{i\not\in N}W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=P_{T}\left(\bigcup_{i\not\in N}W_{i}\right)

where the last equality follows because
\mathcal{N}

\sigma

. Furthermore, because
\bigcup_{i\not\in N}W_{i}=W\setminus\bigcup_{i\in N}W_{i}

\bigcup_{i\not\in N}W_{i}\in\mathcal{W}
.

Thus, w.l.o.g. w.m.a.
W_{1},W_{2},\dots\in\mathcal{W}
. Alter the sequence at finitely many places to obtain a second sequence

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}W_{i}}(A)\neq\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}V_{i}}(A)

for some
A
.

To see that this can be done proceed as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{W}
is not constant there are

\mathcal{T}

is additive. Now, extend (if necessary)
W_{i}
to
\tilde{W}_{i}
so that we can write
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
as disjoint unions of sets in
\tilde{W}_{i}
. Hence, as
W\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{W}
is continuous, there are
j,k
such that
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{W}_{j}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{W}_{k}}
and w.l.o.g.
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{W}_{j}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\tilde{W}_{i}}
and therefore
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{W}_{j}}\neq\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\neq j}\tilde{W}_{i}}
as the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
in

3
are strictly larger 0. Hence,
\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}W_{i}}
must either differ from
\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\tilde{W}_{i}}

As
V_{i}

\displaystyle P_{T}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}V_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P_{T}(V_{i})\qquad\Leftrightarrow

the limit
\lambda_{\infty}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{k}

\mu_{k},\mu_{\infty}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{k}W_{i}}(A)+(1-\lambda_{k})\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=k+1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A).

(5)

By condition 3. of
Definition

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{k}W_{i}}(A)=\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)
and thus

\displaystyle a_{k}:=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)-\lambda_{k}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{k}W_{i}}(A)}{1-\lambda_{k}}=\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=k+1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

is well-defined and the limit

exists. Rewriting

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\lambda_{k}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{k}W_{i}}(A)+(1-\lambda_{k})a_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)+(1-\lambda_{\infty})\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{k}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(1-\lambda_{\infty})\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda_{\infty})\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{k}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{k}

Performing the same argument based on

we see

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=k+1}^{\infty}W_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=k+1}^{\infty}V_{i}}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}V_{i}}(A)

where
!
holds because
W_{i}
and
V_{i}
