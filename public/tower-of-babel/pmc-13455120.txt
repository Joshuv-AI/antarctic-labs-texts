Evaluation of Stathmin Expression in Different Oral Squamous Lesions: A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Razieh Zare, Pegah Esmailizadeh, Fatemeh Amirmoezi, Hannah Wesley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesStathmin is a phosphoprotein involved in the functions of proliferation, differentiation, and cell migration through microtubules, and its increased expression has been reported in some tumors. The purpose of this study was to compare the level of expression of stathmin in different oral lesions.MethodsThe present investigation aimed to characterize the expression profile of stathmin across a clinicopathological spectrum of 98 oral mucosal samples (between 2011 and 2018), ranging from benign hyperkeratosis (HK) to malignant squamous cell carcinoma available in the archive of pathology department of Shiraz Dental School.ResultsThe comparisons showed that the level of stathmin expression in the central/suprabasal epithelial layer in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) was higher than lichen planus (LP) (p‐value = 0.044), in epithelial dysplasia (ED) was higher than HK and LP without dysplasia (p‐value = 0.002), and in LP with dysplasia (LPD) was higher than HK and LP without dysplasia (p‐value < 0.001).ConclusionPhosphoprotein stathmin is more expressed in OSCC, ED, and LPD than in other lesions that have less dysplastic features. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate stathmin expression in oral LP (OLP) with and without dysplasia and oral HK, expanding its potential role as a marker of dysplasia in oral squamous lesions. Identifying the signal transmission system of this molecule and its molecular inhibitors can be promising in inhibiting tumor growth and therapeutic targets.Keywords: epithelial dysplasia, hyperkeratosis, lichen planus, oral squamous cell carcinoma, stathmin

1. Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a subtype of head and neck carcinoma that develops within the squamous layer of the oral cavity. It accounts for about 90% of oral malignant tumors. Alcohol, tobacco, and human papillomavirus (HPV) are the most common leading causes of OSCC [1].

Oral precancerous lesions are morphologically abnormal mucosa that may be altered to oral cancer. Epithelial dysplasia (ED), carcinoma in situ (CIS), and specific regions with hyperkeratosis (HK) are premalignant lesions of the oral cavity. ED is a histopathological entity based on cytomorphologic features of the epithelium, such as nuclear atypia, increased mitotic figures, pleomorphism, and loss of polarity or differentiation that is graded as mild, moderate, or severe [2, 3]. This classification is useful for managing such lesions.

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic T cell–mediated autoimmune disorder in which cytotoxic CD8+ T cells target oral epithelial cells and induce apoptosis [4]. Malignant transformation in OLP is controversial, and it is better to consider OLP as a premalignant lesion with periodic follow‐ups [5].

HK is defined as an increase in keratin layer production that, in areas with nonkeratinized squamous epithelium of the oral cavity, may be a sign of premalignancy.

Stathmin is a highly conserved 17 kDa phosphoprotein in mammals that is necessary for the regulation of cell cytoskeleton. The cytoskeleton is the framework for many cellular processes, such as cell organization, cell division, and cell motility [6]. Stathmin overexpression was first identified as an intracellular phosphoprotein in leukemia, and its association with squamous cell carcinoma of the larynx [7], breast cancer [8], small cell lung cancer [9], and ovarian cancer [10] has been reported. It has been shown that a high stathmin level in patients with lung cancer [11], esophageal cancer [12], hepatocellular carcinoma [13], gastric cancer [14], pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm [15], nasopharyngeal carcinoma [16], cervical intraepithelial neoplasia [17], colorectal cancer [18], and glioblastoma [19] is associated with a poor prognosis. Stathmin plays an important role in the regulation of the cell cycle and is referred to as oncoprotein 18. When stathmin is mutated and functionally defective, it can cause uncontrolled cell proliferation. If stathmin cannot bind to tubulin, microtubules polymerize without any control and continuously form the mitotic spindle. Without regulation of the mitotic spindle, the cell cycle continues in an uncontrolled manner, and cell proliferation causes cancer [20]. The results of several studies reveal that the histochemical examination of stathmin can be used as a biomarker for therapeutic purposes in cancer. In the present study, we hypothesized that stathmin expression would be higher in OSCC, ED, and lichen planus with dysplasia (LPD) compared to OLP without dysplasia and HK. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate and compare the stathmin expression among different oral squamous lesions.

2. Methods and Materials

2.1. Patients and Tissue Selection

In this retrospective cross‐sectional investigation, 98 patients were selected based on the clinically archived tissue and their medical records in the Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology Department of Shiraz Dental School, Shiraz, Iran, during 2011–2018. Twenty‐four cases out of these were diagnosed with OSCC, 17 cases with ED, 27 cases with oral HK, 15 cases as OLP without dysplasia, and 15 of them as LPD. All samples were confirmed on a definitive diagnosis, and related hematoxylin‐eosin (H&E) slides were reevaluated to confirm the diagnosis. Erosive and ulcerative OLP samples and specimens with no definitive diagnosis and insufficient epithelial components were all excluded. Medical records of patients with an OLP diagnosis were evaluated, and if the clinical and histopathologic diagnosis was OLP, we would use their slides. Moreover, poorly stained samples and small‐sized ones leading to the wrong diagnosis were excluded. Demographic data, including the patients’ age and gender, as well as the histological characteristics of each lesion, were additionally obtained from the patients’ medical records. Subsequently, histopathologic grading of the dysplastic and tumoral specimens was also performed to assess the probability of malignant transformation.

2.2. Ethical Approval

The study was designed after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committees (IEC) of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.DENTAL.REC.1398.072). The current study was carried out in accordance with all the institutional ethical guidelines.

2.3. Immunohistochemistry

The stathmin expression detection was performed with immunohistochemical staining under standard protocols previously described in our recently published article [21]. Rabbit polyclonal antibody to STMN1 (1:50 dilution; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) was used as the primary antibody. To determine the expression of the stathmin marker, five areas were randomly selected with a magnification of ×400, and 500 cells were counted; then, the cytoplasmic staining rate was reported as a percentage. The stained tissue sections were reviewed separately by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinical parameters.

The immunohistochemical sections were analyzed quantitatively to determine the distribution of stained cells as follows: 0 = less than 10%, 1 = weak (10%–25%), 2 = moderate (26%–50%), 3 = strong (51%–75%), and 4 = very strong (76%–100%). The following criteria were used to determine the intensity of stained cells: 0 = no staining, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, and 3 = strong.

The overall distribution and intensity score of staining was considered as low score if it was less than 6 and high score if it was more than 6.

2.4. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 18 software. To compare the meaning of stathmin expression in the test groups, one‐way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, and post hoc Tukey tests were employed. The final scores of all test groups were assessed and compared with each other using the chi‐square test. In addition, a Mann–Whitney test and Spearman’s correlation were conducted to detect if an association exists between the stathmin expression and its clinical variables. The difference was considered statistically significant with a threshold set at p < 0.05.

3. Results

A total of 98 cases of oral lesions were selected, of which 56 (57.1%) were women and 42 (42.9%) were men. Patients had a mean ± SD age of 51.44 ± 14.80 (range: 15–83) years. There was no significant difference between the age and gender of the patients and the location of the lesion in case groups.

The expression of stathmin was observed in all cases (more than 10% of cells were stained). Table 1 and Figure 1 show the average expression percentage of stathmin in the epithelial and stromal components of all studied groups. In the stroma, staining was limited to endothelial cells, and stromal fibroblasts did not show any staining. In OSCC, the average staining of the central layer and the peripheral layer was 64.81, and the average staining of the stroma was 39.08, which had the highest expression in the stroma between different lesions.

Stathmin immunoreactivity was predominantly observed in the central/suprabasal epithelial layer and stromal compartment, where it differed significantly among diagnostic groups (p‐value < 0.001). In contrast, the peripheral/basal layer did not show a significant intergroup difference (p‐value > 0.05). The two‐by‐two comparison showed that the level of expression of stathmin in the central/suprabasal epithelial layer in OSCC was higher than in lichen planus (LP) (p‐value = 0.044), in ED was higher than in HK and LP (p‐value = 0.002), and in LPD was higher than in HK and LP without dysplasia (p‐value <0.001). The post hoc test comparison showed that the level of expression of stathmin in the stroma of HK was lower than in OSCC (p‐value < 0.001), ED (p‐value = 0.041), LPD (p‐value = 0.003), and LP without dysplasia (p‐value = 0.003).

There was no statistically significant difference between the intensity of stathmin expression in the basal/peripheral and central/suprasal layers in the studied lesions (p‐value = 0.335 and 0.625, respectively) (Table 2). Figure 2 graphically demonstrates the differences in the stathmin expression intensity among the studied lesions, complementing the numerical data presented in Table 2. The intensity of staining in the central layer was weak (staining; 1) in 45.80% and strong (staining; 3) in the peripheral layer in 87.05% of the cases. The final score of stathmin expression among all groups (SCC, ED, LPD, OLP, and HK) was statistically significant (p‐value = 0.001), and this score was high in 41.7% of cases and low in 58.3% (Table 3 and Figure 3).

In ED, the staining of the suprabasal and basal layers was 61.17% and 100%, respectively, which had the highest staining percentage with an overall of 80.58%. The intensity of staining in the suprabasal layer was weak (staining; 1) in 52.9% of cases; however, it was strong (staining; 3) in the basal layer of 94.1% of the cases. The final score was high in 64.7% of cases and low in 35.3% of cases.

LPD had the highest expression of stathmin in the suprabasal layer (54.20%) after ED. It was expressed in the basal layer of all cases, as well as having high expression in the stroma with a percentage of 31.40%. The intensity of expression in the suprabasal layer was weak (staining; 1) in 46.7% and moderate (staining; 2) in 46.7% of cases.

Samples from LP patients had the lowest staining in the suprabasal layer (8.66%), unlike the basal layer, which showed 100%. The intensity of staining in the suprabasal layer was weak (staining; 1) and moderate (staining; 2) in 40% of the cases. All the LP cases showed strong staining in the basal layer. Altogether, the final score was low. The inflammatory cells under the epithelial layer had positive staining.

HK also had 29.61% expression in the suprabasal layer, unlike the basal layer, which showed an incidence of 95.92%. The intensity of staining in this lesion in the suprabasal and basal layers was weak (staining; 1) and strong (staining; 3), respectively. The final score was high in 15.4% of cases and low in 84.6%. Surface keratins, including orthokeratin and parakeratin, had negative staining. Figure 4 shows immunohistochemical staining of stathmin in different studied groups.

In all samples, the expression of stathmin in the surface layer of the epithelium was negative. On the other hand, the highest staining percentage in the suprabasal/central layer was related to ED, and the lowest staining percentage was related to HK. Our data showed a progressive increase in the percentage and intensity of stathmin immunoreactivity within the suprabasal epithelial layer, which aligned with the histological progression from dysplasia to OSCC.

4. Discussion

The stathmin phosphoprotein plays an important role in cell proliferation, differentiation, development, and morphogenesis. The role of these phosphoproteins in the pathogenesis of cancer and other diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and multiple sclerosis has been demonstrated [22]. Increased stathmin levels correlate with cell motility and subsequent metastasis [6]. Stathmin is added from its N‐terminus to the end of the polymerization region of microtubules, and with a structural change, it causes some cellular processes, which is called catastrophe promotion, while the C‐terminus of stathmin prevents the polymerization of microtubules [20]. Several studies have investigated the genetic and cytogenetic changes in SCC; however, the details of the mechanisms of oncogenesis, cell differentiation, invasion, and progression of SCC remain ambiguous [23, 24]. Moreover, as our center is a referral one for evaluating oral lesions, several other biomarkers in different databases were driven to investigate the role of these mechanisms using similar methodologies [21, 25].

We indicated that stathmin expression increased in HK, dysplasia, and SCC. The expression of stathmin was higher in neoplastic and dysplastic tissues than in nondysplastic lesions. The higher expression of stathmin in neoplastic lesions compared to nonneoplastic ones emphasizes its role in the carcinogenesis of OSCC. Our findings were in line with the studies of Nakashima et al. [26], Akhtar et al. [27], and Ke et al. [14].

Moreover, we showed that with the increase in the grade of dysplasia, the level of stathmin expression also increases, which is in accordance with Harada et al.’s study. They showed that in 49 patients with OSCC, the level of stathmin expression in primary OSCC was significantly higher than in normal epithelium, and as the stage of the disease worsened, a higher expression of stathmin was observed [28].

The results of Yan et al.’s study, which was conducted on the serum of 364 patients with esophageal SCC, indicated that the average concentration of serum stathmin increased with advancing disease stage and metastasis. These findings confirmed our outcomes [12].

The overexpression of stathmin in OSCC was confirmed by Ma et al. in 2017. By means of 2D electrophoresis and chromatography, they revealed that the expression of stathmin in OSCC was much higher than in normal mucosa. At higher stages, more stathmin expression was observed, and inhibiting it results in reduced proliferation, colony formation, and migration. Stathmin acts as an oncogene that is regulated by p53, and it can be a potential target for anticancer treatment in OSCC [29].

In the study by Shu‐Fang Jin et al., the relationship between stathmin expression and clinical characteristics was investigated in 109 OSCC patients, and an increase in stathmin expression was noted in these lesions. In contradiction to our findings, a correlation between stathmin expression and gender was confirmed. The higher expression of stathmin had a direct relationship with tumor grade and lymph node involvement and an inverse relationship with the survival rate [30].

Wang et al. [31] reported in their study that the expression of stathmin increases significantly in patients with gallbladder adenocarcinoma. Further research indicated that inhibiting stathmin‐1 causes cell cycle inhibition in vitro. Additionally, inhibiting stathmin induces apoptosis and delays the G2/M phase of the cell cycle [31].

Zhang et al. [32] showed that stathmin mRNA and protein expression levels were significantly higher in colon cancer patients than in healthy individuals. Furthermore, the level of stathmin expression was related to lymph node metastasis and TNM stages [32].

LP is considered a premalignant lesion and has a malignant rate of up to 55%. Although the expression of stathmin expression in LP has not been investigated in any study, our study found that stathmin expression in LP was lower than in SCC, ED, and LPD. These results can indicate the role of stathmin in the pathogenesis of OLP. Although our findings do not support the role of stathmin in the malignant transformation of LP, low expression of stathmin could confirm that LP has less malignant potential than ED.

A major strength of the study is the inclusion of OLP with and without dysplasia and oral HK, lesions for which stathmin expression has not been previously reported. The layer‐specific distribution of stathmin observed in our study provides a more refined view of its role in oral ED and malignant transformation. This broader lesion spectrum allows better insight into the potential role of stathmin in early dysplastic changes. In our study, all dysplastic lesions were analyzed as a single group, rather than being classified separately as mild, moderate, or severe dysplasia. Nevertheless, the findings revealed higher stathmin expression in dysplastic lesions compared with nondysplastic lesions, suggesting a possible association between stathmin expression and epithelial dysplastic changes.

One limitation of our study is the use of semiquantitative visual assessment rather than software‐based digital image analysis. However, given the retrospective nature of the study and the variability of archived slides, standardized digital analysis was not feasible. Future prospective studies incorporating digital image analysis are recommended. Recent studies have focused on identifying novel molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets for OSCC, including noncoding RNAs, as well as signaling molecules such as STAT3 [33–35]. These molecules have been implicated in tumor progression. Together with our findings regarding the overexpression of stathmin in dysplastic lesions and OSCC, these data underscore the complex molecular profile of oral carcinogenesis. Integrating stathmin with emerging RNA‐based therapeutic strategies may offer a more comprehensive framework for early diagnostic screening and future personalized therapeutic interventions in oral malignancy.

5. Conclusion

Phosphoprotein stathmin is expressed more in oral dysplastic changes and OSCC, suggesting its potential utility as a reliable diagnostic biomarker. Identifying the signaling transmission system of this molecule and its molecular inhibitors can be promising for inhibiting tumor growth and therapeutic targets.
