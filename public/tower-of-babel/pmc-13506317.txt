Added value of this study

The Asia–Pacific region is vast and heterogenous, with countries at varying levels of epidemiological and economic transitions, diverse health systems, cultural and political landscapes. Our study presents a concerted analysis of a broad spectrum of metabolic, behavioural and environmental/occupational cardiovascular risk factors across individual Asia–Pacific countries, geographical regions, sociodemographic index, and demographic subgroups. The present study uncovers distinct risk patterns informing tailored, “best-buy” policies for the region, facilitating prioritisation of resources toward risk factor-specific targets and interventions.

Implications of all the available evidence

There have been improvements in the age-standardised cardiovascular burden attributable to metabolic (31.2% decrease), behavioural (38.8% decrease), and environmental/occupational risk factors (40.6% decrease) from 1990 to 2023, reflective of the advances in CVD prevention strategies across the Asia–Pacific region. However, the effect from preventive efforts was offset by larger increases in risk factor attributed to population growth and population ageing (by 45.3% and 87.3% respectively). With more than two-thirds of the countries in the Asia–Pacific observing excess cardiovascular risk factor-related morbidity relative to the global average, there is a growing need for bolstering regional efforts in delivering effective primordial and primary CVD prevention to the communities. The metabolic-centric pattern in risk factors within the Asia–Pacific demands for immediate actions in tackling the region's “best-buys” (i.e. high systolic blood pressure, high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and kidney dysfunction), while addressing other emerging drivers of CVD such as dietary risks and air pollution.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) constitute a growing public health challenge, contributing to 19.1 million global deaths and 436 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally in 2023.1 Despite remarkable strides in CVD medical advancements, the global CVD prevalence is anticipated to rise by 1.9-fold from 2025 to 2050, with improvements in CVD-related morbidity and mortality set to plateau.2 Mirroring global trends, the Asia–Pacific region is faced with the unprecedented challenge of tackling a rising CVD burden across a vast and heterogeneous landscape, with countries at varying stages of the demographic and economic transitions, compounded by the diverse range of cultures, political stability, healthcare systems, insurance and financing systems3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Tailoring region-specific preventive strategies in addressing upstream behavioural, metabolic and environmental/occupational risk factors of CVD will be integral in the concerted efforts in tackling CVD in the Asia–Pacific.

Epidemiological evidence has highlighted that modifiable cardiovascular risk factors contribute to 57.2% and 52.6% of the global CVD burden in female and male populations respectively, with hypertension and obesity set to contribute to the largest rise in cardiovascular morbidity in the next 2 decades worldwide.8,9 While prior global studies have provided broad cardiovascular risk factor-attributable burden across world regions,10, 11, 12 a granular and comprehensive characterisation of the full spectrum of behavioural, metabolic, and environmental/occupational cardiovascular risk factors specific to the Asia–Pacific remains absent. By leveraging the updated Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2023, the study addresses this gap by 1) providing de novo aggregation of cardiovascular risk factor-related morbidity and mortality data across 39 Asia–Pacific countries from 1990 to 2023; 2) performing decomposition analysis to identify independent contributions of population growth, population ageing, and risk exposure contributing to the regional cardiovascular risk factor-attributable burden; 3) the SDI-stratified characterisation of intra-regional disparities across countries at varying stages of socioeconomic spectrum; and 4) the age-sex-disaggregated analysis of cardiovascular risk factor burden, elucidating demographic gradients and identifying vulnerable subpopulations not captured in existing global analyses. As part of The Lancet Commission in tackling CVD in the Asia-Pacific,3 this investigation identifies unique shifts in cardiovascular risk factor trends and country-specific risk factor targets for CVD prevention, providing actionable insights for prioritising resources toward high-priority cardiovascular risk factor targets across the Asia–Pacific.

Methods

Overview and definitions

This study complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement (Supplementary Methods 1).13 We utilised data from the GBD 2023 study, an international epidemiological collaboration coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation spanning 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational locations.14 Geospatial and temporal comparisons of disease burden are made possible by the annual estimates for causes, risk factors, mortality and DALYs provided across 375 diseases and injuries. The lack of a consensus agreement on the boundaries of the Asia–Pacific region often means that the countries regarded as member states differ depending on the classification context and institutional framework employed. For the purposes of this investigation, countries and territories classified under South-East Asia Region (SEAR) and the Western Pacific Region (WPR) by the World Health Organization (WHO) are considered constituents of the Asia-Pacific.15 Of these, 39 countries and territories were represented as individual locations in the GBD 2023 study (Supplementary Material 1).

Data sources

GBD 2023 compiled and modelled mortality and DALY data for each cause, stratified by age, sex and country, from vital registration systems, verbal autopsies, open-source databases as well as alternative surveillance systems.16 Gender identity, race and ethnicity data were not available in the country-level GBD estimates and therefore were not included in the present study. Data availability and quality vary substantially across the Asia–Pacific countries in this study. Only 13 of the 39 Asia–Pacific countries with assessed data achieved a cause-of-death data quality rating of four or five stars in the most recent 2010–2023 window, indicating robust and reliable vital statistics. Conversely, 16 countries had a rating of two stars or lower, reflecting limited data infrastructure, while a further 11 had no cause-of-death input data meeting GBD inclusion thresholds: including the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, Bhutan, Timor-Leste, and eight Pacific Island states.17 Vital registration was the primary source for 17 countries, concentrated in Australasia and high-income East Asia; estimates for the remaining countries rested on a constellation of verbal autopsy, sample registration, health and demographic surveillance, survey data, and covariate-driven modelling.17 Risk-factor exposure inputs follow a comparable pattern, with country-specific World Health Organization STEPwise Approach to NCD Risk Factor Surveillance (STEPS), Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), national-survey, and environmental-monitoring data used where available, and covariate-driven modelling applied elsewhere. A country-level summary of data sources and quality indicators across five-year windows from 1990 to 2023 is provided in Supplementary Material 2. The historical estimates from 1990 to 2023 were retrieved from the Global Health Data Exchange website.16

Risk factors

Risk factors in GBD 2023 are organised in a nested hierarchical structure. Level 1 risks comprise metabolic, behavioural, and environmental/occupational domains. Each domain contains multiple level 2 risks, which are further disaggregated into 42 level 3 risks.10 Metabolic risks (level 1) comprise high systolic blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, high fasting plasma glucose, high BMI, and kidney dysfunction (level 2). Behavioural risks (level 1) consist of dietary risks, tobacco use, high alcohol use, and low physical activity (level 2). Environmental/occupational risks (level 1) include air pollution, non-optimal temperature, and other environmental risks (level 2). Estimates of crude and age-standardised mortality and DALYs with their 95% uncertainty intervals were extracted for all level 1–3 risk factors using standard GBD methodology.

CVD burden attributable to risk factors was estimated using the GBD comparative risk assessment framework, which requires epidemiological evidence supporting a biologically plausible association between exposure and disease outcomes. The strength and shape of each risk–outcome relationship were quantified using the Burden of Proof methodology, which synthesises evidence from systematic reviews and applies meta-regression techniques to generate robust dose–response functions while accounting for between-study heterogeneity and potential sources of bias.11

Risk-attributable burden was calculated using population attributable fractions (PAFs; derived from population exposure distributions), relative risk estimates, and theoretical minimum risk exposure levels (TMRELs; defined as the exposure level associated with the lowest disease risk). PAFs were applied to cause-specific DALYs to estimate the proportion of CVD burden attributable to each risk, representing disease burden that would not have occurred if exposure had been reduced to the TMREL.11 Summary exposure values (SEVs) were used to facilitate comparison of exposure levels across risks. SEVs integrate both the prevalence of exposure and the magnitude of associated risk, scaled from 0 (no exposure) to 100 (maximum exposure), and were age-standardised for all analyses (Supplementary Methods 2).11 To avoid double-counting when estimating attributable burden across multiple co-occurring risk factors, the GBD framework addresses this by computing a joint PAF across all risk factors, accounting for the correlation structure between exposures, so that the combined attributable burden does not exceed the total observed burden. When aggregated attributable burden across cardiovascular risk factors, risk exposure effects for individual risk factors were scaled to sum to all-risk exposure effect by location, age, sex, and cause, allowing for consistency across level 1–3 risk aggregations. However, the GBD framework does not explicitly model synergistic interactions between co-occurring risks, hence estimates may underestimate the true burden within populations where clustering of multiple high-risk exposures is prevalent.10,11,18 The cardiovascular risk factor definitions used in this study can be found in Supplementary Material 3.

Mortality

CVD-attributed mortality was ascertained through vital registration data, together with verbal autopsy studies based on household mortality surveys. To account for deaths ascribed to non-fatal, undefined, or intermediate causes, redistribution was conducted using either statistical regression methods or fixed proportions. In regions lacking adequate primary mortality data, global ratios stratified by age were employed.11 The estimation of underlying mortality rate was enhanced by applying a Bayesian noise reduction algorithm.11

The GBD Cause of Death Ensemble model was used to produce cause-specific mortality estimates stratified by location, age, sex, year, and cause, as outlined in earlier studies.14 Briefly, distinct submodels were produced using biological, sociodemographic, and environmental variables to provide estimates regardless of the underlying data availability. Out-of-sample predictive validity testing was used to assign weights to each submodel, following which a weighted average or all submodels were used to generate final mortality estimates.19 Cause-specific mortality estimates were then adjusted such that their sum matched all-cause mortality estimates.11

Disability-adjusted life years

DALY estimates in GBD 2023 serve as indicators of the total disease burden, incorporating both fatal and non-fatal elements. Years of Life Lost (YLLs), calculated as the product of deaths at each age and the remaining life expectancy at that specific age, account for premature mortality, thereby quantifying the fatal component of diseases.11 Standard life expectancies were calculated from the lowest age-specific mortality rates observed across countries. In contrast, Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) capture the non-fatal aspects of disease burden by illustrating the severity of health loss associated with illness or injury. YLDs were derived from multiplying the prevalence of each health condition by its corresponding disability weights, which were derived from surveys of the general population to reflect the impact of population health states. YLLs and YLDs across location, age, sex, year, and cause were then summed to calculate DALYs as a comprehensive assessment of overall disease burden.14

Statistical analysis

Decomposition analysis was conducted using the Das Gupta method to identify drivers of temporal changes in CVD risk-attributable DALYs from 1990 to 2023.20,21 This decomposition analysis has been described previously.1,10 Briefly, attributable burden was modelled as a product of three analytically distinct components: age-specific population size, risk-deleted DALY rate, and risk exposure effect. Therefore, the PAF-related exposure effects remained analytically separate from demographic drivers. The population component was further decomposed into population growth and population ageing. The detailed methodology can be found in Supplementary Methods 4.

Sociodemographic index (SDI) is an aggregate measure incorporating mean educational qualification, overall fertility rate, and income as a reflection the influence of socioeconomic factors on health outcomes in each location. These measures are represented on a 0–100 scale, with combination of these inputs producing an estimate of the joint effect of these factors. SDI quintile assignments were based on GBD's global quintile boundaries applied to each country's SDI value, rather than quintiles formed de novo among the 39 Asia–Pacific countries. The SDI quintiles comprised of five groups: 1) low; 2) low-middle; 3) middle; 4) middle–high; and 5) high SDI (Supplementary Methods 3).14,22 Age-specific burden estimates for each risk factor were obtained from GBD 2023, restricted to both sexes combined. Population data by age group were merged with burden estimates to calculate age-specific rates. Total percent change estimates were derived from GBD aggregate data.

To evaluate the temporal stability and cross-national consistency of cardiovascular risk factor rankings, two rank-based analyses were performed. Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was used to evaluate the stability of cardiovascular risk factor rankings between 1990 and 2023, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) was subsequently employed to quantify the extent of cross-national agreement in risk factor rankings across 39 Asia–Pacific countries in 2023, with W ranging from 0 (no concordance) to 1 (perfect concordance), and significance assessed via chi-squared approximation. Both analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.3.

Ethics

This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board review as it uses publicly available data that did not contain any confidential or identifiable patient information.

Role of the funding source

The funding sources played no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

Regional estimates and trends

In 2023, cardiovascular risk factors contributed to 197,078,095 (183,712,936–210,443,254) crude DALYs, and 4463.7 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 4225.4–4702.0) age-standardised DALY rates in the Asia–Pacific, which was 16.4% higher than the global age-standardised DALY rate. More than two thirds (72%) of all Asia–Pacific countries observed excess age-standardised DALYs relative to the global average (Supplementary Tables S1–S3, Supplementary Figure S1).

From 1990 to 2023, cardiovascular risk factor-related crude DALYs increased by 61.5% (46.9–76.1) in the Asia–Pacific region, recording a more rapid rise than the global increase (39.0% [25.1–52.8]) (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Decomposition analysis revealed that the increase in overall crude morbidity was a result of counteracting drivers of change, with larger increases in burden attributed to population growth (45.3%) and population ageing (87.3%), offsetting any improvements in overall risk exposure (−71.1%) (Fig. 1). In the same timeframe, there was slower improvement in age-standardised DALYs (−34.9% [−42.9 to −26.9]) in the Asia–Pacific compared to that of global trends (−40.6% [−50.0 to −31.2]) (Supplementary Table S6).Fig. 1Percentage change in the number of crude DALYs in the Asia–Pacific region attributable to each risk factor from 1990 to 2023, due to population growth, population ageing and risk exposures. DALY, disability-adjusted life years; LDL, low density lipoprotein.

Risk factor-specific

In 2023, the leading risk factors contributing to CVD-related age-standardised DALY rates in the Asia–Pacific were high systolic blood pressure (2879.8 [2606.7–3153.0]), followed by dietary risks (1823.2 [1383.7–2262.6]), air pollution (1357.5 [1212.3–1502.6]) and high LDL cholesterol (1086.5 [920.5–1252.5]) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S3). Although the top three risk factors in 2023 remained the same as in 1990, high LDL cholesterol overtook tobacco to rank as the fourth leading risk factor in 2023 (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8, Supplementary Figure S2).Fig. 2Trends in crude and age-standardised DALY rates (per 100,000 population) in the Asia–Pacific region from 1990 to 2023, attributable to (A) metabolic risks, (B) behavioural risks and (C) environmental/occupational risks. Trends in crude and age-standardised DALY rates (per 100,000 population) in the Asia–Pacific region from 1990 to 2023, attributable to (A) metabolic risks, (B) behavioural risks and (C) environmental/occupational risks. DALY, disability-adjusted life years; LDL, low density lipoprotein.

From 1990 to 2023, metabolic risk factors saw the most rapid rise in crude CVD-related DALYs by 74.3% (53.2–95.4) (Supplementary Figure S3). Decomposition analysis revealed that this net increase was primarily driven by population growth (66.2%) and population ageing (72.6%), which offset improvements in risk exposure (−64.5%). While all metabolic risk factors observed improvements in risk exposure, high body mass index (BMI) was the only risk factor that demonstrated an increased risk exposure of 64.7%. Following metabolic risk factors, behavioural risk factors recorded an increase in crude CVD-related DALYs of 49.5% (20.3–78.7) from 1990 to 2023, with contributions from population growth (55.7%) and population ageing (66.5%), offsetting improvements in risk exposure (−72.7%). Environmental/occupational risk factors had the smallest increase of 44.6% (24.2–64.9) in crude DALYs, driven by population ageing (83.3%) and population growth (42.7%), outweighing improvements in risk exposure (−81.5%) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S3).

On the other hand, the largest decline in age-standardised DALY rates were related to environmental/occupational risk factors (−40.6% [−52.3 to −28.9]), followed by behavioural (−38.8% [−55.1 to −22.4]) and metabolic risk factors (−31.2% [−42.3 to −20.2]) (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S3). Risk factor-related crude and age-standardised mortality can be found in Supplementary Tables S9–S14.

From 1990 to 2023, metabolic risk factor-specific disaggregated analysis demonstrated the largest improvements in age-standardised DALY rates for high systolic blood pressure (−32.4%), followed by kidney dysfunction (−29.2%), high LDL cholesterol (−24.0%) and high fasting plasma glucose (−18.2%). In contrast, high BMI (30.5%) was the only risk factor to observe a rise in age-standardised DALY rates within the same timeframe (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S4). In terms of behavioural risk factors, the largest improvements in age-standardised DALY rates were contributed by reductions in alcohol use (−51.5%), followed by tobacco (−45.0%), dietary risks (−37.5%) and low physical activity (−32.1%) from 1990 to 2023 (Fig. 2). Specific to dietary risk factors, the most rapid improvements in age-standardised DALY rates were contributed by reductions in seafood omega-3 fatty acids (−53.1%), followed by diets high in sodium (−52.9%) and diet low in fibre (−51.5%). Notably, age-standardised DALY rates attributable to diets high in red meat (81.3%) and diets high in sugar-sweetened beverages (48.9%) increased sharply during this period (Supplementary Table S6). Regarding environmental/occupational risk factors, the largest improvements in age-standardised DALY rates were attributed to reductions in air pollution (−48.5%), followed by non-optimal temperature (−44.7%) and other environmental risks (−20.5%). More granular analysis into the specific environmental risk factors revealed that household air pollution from solid fuels (−75.2%), followed by low temperature (−55.9%), and particulate matter pollution (−48.5%) contributed to the largest improvements in age-standardised DALY rates; whereas ambient particulate matter pollution (45.2%) and high temperature (38.4%) were the key contributors to increases in age-standardised DALY rates (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S6).

Geographic regions

In 2023, the highest cardiovascular risk factor-related age-standardised DALY rates were observed in Oceania (8348.9 [7137.2–9560.7] per 100,000 population), followed by Central Asia (5698.9 [5106.3–6291.6]) and Southeast Asia (5535.2 [5134.6–5935.8]). On the other hand, Australasia (1228.8 [1118.7–1339.0]) recorded the lowest cardiovascular risk factor-attributable age-standardised DALY rates, followed by East Asia (3262.3 [2897.6–3627.0]) and South Asia (5068.0 [4437.5–5698.4]) (Supplementary Table S3).

Between 1990 and 2023, a reduction in age-standardised DALY rates was recorded across all the regions, with the steepest decline in Australasia (−71.8%), followed by East Asia (−56.6%) and Central Asia (−45.3%). The smallest decline in age-standardised DALY was observed in Oceania (−1.2%), followed by South Asia (−11.9%) and Southeast Asia (−17.2%) (Supplementary Table S6). Similar trends were seen for disaggregated age-standardised DALY rates attributable to metabolic, behavioural, or environmental/occupational risk factors (Supplementary Figure S5).

Sociodemographic index

From 1990 to 2023, improving (i.e. more negative) annualised rates of change in age-standardised DALY rates attributable to metabolic, behavioural, and environmental/occupational risk factors were observed in countries with increasing SDI (Supplementary Figure S6). Only 28% of the Asia–Pacific countries (majority were high SDI countries) had larger improvements in annualised rate of change in age-standardised DALY rates than the global average for behavioural and metabolic risk factors; on the other hand, 44% of Asia–Pacific countries had larger improvements in annualised rate of change in age-standardised DALY rate than the global average for environmental/occupational risk factors (Supplementary Figure S6).

In 2023, age-standardised DALY rates attributable to metabolic risk factors were highest in middle SDI (4568.5 [4069.1–5067.9] per 100,000 population), followed by low-middle (4277.0 [3680.4–4873.5] per 100,000 population) and low SDI (3939.0 [3376.7–4501.2] per 100,000 population). The lowest age-standardised DALY rates attributable to metabolic risk factors were observed in high SDI regions (1047.6 [957.8–1137.5] per 100,000 population) and high-middle SDI (2939.7 [2449.9–3429.5] per 100,000 population) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S15). From 1990 to 2023, the most rapid improvements in metabolic risk-related age-standardised DALY rates in the Asia–Pacific were recorded in high SDI regions (−67.0%), followed by high-middle SDI (−52.7%) and middle SDI (−11.6%) regions. Country-specific

In 2023, among Asia–Pacific countries with directly-observed cause-of-death input data (≥3-star), the highest age-standardised DALY rate contributed by cardiovascular risk factors was reported in the Solomon Islands (14,852.5 [12,680.3–17,115.4] per 100,000 population), followed by Myanmar (9234.1 per 100,000 population) and the Federated States of Micronesia (8988.3 per 100,000 population). Among countries that had limited data infrastructure (≤2-star), Nauru reported the highest risk factor-related age-standardised DALY rate (17,441.4 [14,100.4–20,753.5] per 100,000 population), followed by the Marshall Islands (13,764.8 [11,531.1–16,021.2] per 100,000 population) and Tuvalu (13,704.5 [11,518.3–15,991.6] per 100,000 population) (Supplementary Table S3). Age and sex-specific

From 1990 to 2023, male individuals bore a disproportionate burden of cardiovascular risk factor-related age-standardised DALY (5413.0 [5088.5–5737.5]) compared to female individuals (3665.8 [3405.0–3926.6]), with the excess morbidity burden largely driven by male-dominant behavioural risk factors (65.9% borne by males), followed by environmental/occupational risk factors (60.4%), and metabolic risk factors (58.5%) (Supplementary Table S21). The most pronounced sex-specific disparity in age-standardised DALYs were observed in Japan, Singapore and Taiwan, with cardiovascular risk factor burden in male individuals more than double that of females.

From 1990 to 2023, age-standardised DALY rate attributable to all cardiovascular risk factors observed larger improvements in female individuals (−37.4%) compared to male counterparts (−33.0%). These sex-specific disparate trends were observed across metabolic, behavioural, and environmental/occupational risk factors in majority of the Asia–Pacific countries (Supplementary Tables S22 and S23). However, there were some exceptions to the general trend, with increasing age-standardised DALY rates borne by female individuals in Tonga (25.2% increase), Nauru (19.9% increase), and Vanuatu (18.7% increase), with decreasing age-standardised DALY rates observed in male individuals. Similar sex-specific trends were observed with age-standardised mortality (Supplementary Tables S24–S26).

In 2023, age-specific crude DALY burden across all risk factors, metabolic risks, and environmental/occupational risks peaked earlier in males (65–69 years) compared to females (70–74 years), whereas behavioural risks peaked at 65–69 years in both sexes. Discussion

The study adds to the existing literature with an updated country-level analysis of cardiovascular risk factor burden across the Asia–Pacific, with insights into patterns of risk factor burden across age-sex-location-socioeconomic status. There are several principal study findings. First, there have been improvements in the age-standardised cardiovascular burden (34.9% decrease overall) attributable to metabolic (31.2% decrease), behavioural (38.8% decrease), and environmental/occupational risk factors (40.6% decrease) from 1990 to 2023, reflective of the advances in CVD prevention strategies across the Asia–Pacific region. However, the effect from preventive efforts was offset by larger increases in risk factor attributed to population growth and population ageing (by 45.3% and 87.3% respectively). With more than two-thirds of the countries in the Asia–Pacific observing excess cardiovascular risk factor-related morbidity relative to the global average, there is a growing need for bolstering regional efforts in delivering effective primordial and primary CVD prevention to the communities. Second, the high-priority cardiovascular risk factor in the Asia–Pacific is tackling metabolic risk factors that account for the majority of the region's CVD-related morbidity, followed by behavioural, and environmental/occupational risk factors. The leading cardiovascular risk factors in the Asia–Pacific are high systolic blood pressure, followed by dietary risks (particularly sugar-sweetened beverages and diet high in processed meat), air pollution (particularly ambient particulate matter pollution), and high LDL cholesterol. From 1990 to 2023, the slowest improvements in risk factor-related age-standardised morbidity were observed with high fasting plasma glucose and high LDL cholesterol, compounded by worsening morbidity trends attributable to high BMI. Third, low to middle income Asia–Pacific countries suffered higher risk factor-related morbidity burden relative to the regional and global mean estimates in 2023, with these vulnerable countries also observing slower improvements in risk factor-related morbidity rates compared to the regional and global trends. This disparity was most pronounced with metabolic and environmental/occupational risk factors. Fourth, metabolic and behavioural risk factor-related morbidity burden peaked at least 5 years earlier for male individuals (peak at 65–69 years) compared to female counterparts (peak at 70–74 years). Male individuals had more rapid accumulation of risk factors at a younger age, and bearing the region's excess risk factor-related morbidity burden, while female individuals observed a more gradual rise in risk factor-related morbidity burden with age.

Metabolic risk factors emerged as the dominant contributors to the cardiovascular morbidity burden in the Asia–Pacific, providing strategic insights into the implementation of effective CVD preventive policies tailored to the region's high-priority metabolic risk factor targets—high systolic blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, and kidney dysfunction.23, 24, 25 There has been moderate success with CVD prevention efforts in the Asia–Pacific region, evidenced by the 65% reduction in the age-specific attributable rate for metabolic risks over three decades. However, demographic forces have offset these gains, with population growth and population ageing contributing to 66.2% and 72.6% increases in crude DALYs respectively, resulting in a net rise in absolute metabolic risk burden. Although age-standardised rates have improved, owing to advancements in individual-level risk factor control, the absolute metabolic risk factor burden continues rising with increasing life expectancies.7 This demographic-epidemiological tension in the Asia–Pacific emphasises a critical challenge that effective prevention strategies alone may not be sufficient to reduce absolute risk factor burden in rapidly ageing populations, without proportional healthcare infrastructure expansion and intensified primordial prevention targeting the younger population. These findings reveal the need for CVD preventive policies that pre-emptively expand cardiovascular healthcare services to accommodate the rising metabolic disease public health challenge, promote sustainable healthy ageing through targeted surveillance and interventions, and control escalating healthcare expenditure via effective primordial and primary prevention.7 Sustaining progress in the Asia–Pacific warrants the accelerated upscaling of current preventive efforts to outpace demographic forces driving crude disease burden upward. It is important to note, however, that despite primary preventive efforts, high BMI was the only cardiovascular risk factor that had increasing age-standardised attributable DALY rate. This is driven by the region's increased exposure to maternal malnutrition and childhood obesity,26, 27, 28 exacerbated by rapid urbanisation, nutritional transitions especially in low-to-middle income countries.7,25,29 Rising exposure to high BMI portends the development of high systolic blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, and high FPG, even in adolescents and young adults,30 threatening to undermine existing preventative efforts and herald greater CVD burden later in life. Furthermore, the complex “double burden” of undernutrition and obesity, particularly in urbanising and rural areas, must be confronted and addressed by broad food systems, fiscal, regulatory and healthcare reforms, at a more aggressive pace than current measures.31, 32, 33, 34 While the surge in new and scalable anti-obesity pharmacotherapies, such as incretin-based therapies, adds impetus in countering the obesity epidemic in the region,2,35 its overall impact is dependent on treatment accessibility, affordability and clinical inertia, especially in low-to-middle income countries where the “treatment-risk paradox” is most prevalent.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 The key tenet in addressing metabolic risk factors in the Asia–Pacific is tackling entrenched structural and social determinants of health, ensuring equitable primordial and primary CVD prevention for all, and as early in the individual's life-course.9,41,42

Behavioural and environmental/occupational risk factors cannot be deprioritised within the holistic CVD preventive framework, as these exposures are often amplifiers of metabolic risk factors on CVD outcomes. For example, dietary risks and a lack of physical activity often manifest through obesity, dysglycemia, and dyslipidemia.43, 44, 45, 46, 47 Notably, dietary risks remain among the leading contributors to CVD morbidity, reflecting challenges with diets high in sodium intake, sugar-sweetened beverages and processed meat in the Asia–Pacific. Moreover, the present study highlighted that despite the smaller contribution to CVD burden by environmental/occupational risk factors, relative to metabolic or behavioural risks, these regional estimates demonstrated the greatest divergence from global averages. In many Asia–Pacific countries, particularly in South and Southeast Asian countries, PM2.5 levels consistently exceeded WHO guideline thresholds.48 This persistent particulate matter pollution can interact synergistically with metabolic vulnerabilities to accelerate vascular damage.49 Importantly, behavioural and environmental risks are highly amenable to cost-effective, population-wide interventions50—policy levers such as mandatory salt reduction,51 front-of-pack labelling,52 and taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages53 have been adopted sporadically to measurable success. Coordinated emissions regulations and clean-air initiatives have gained traction in several East Asian economies, demonstrating the feasibility of environmental risk reduction when strong political commitment exists.54 Moving forward, progress in CVD prevention will require sustained, cross-border coordination and political will, particularly as many environmental hazards extend beyond national boundaries. An example is the ASEAN Coordinating Centre for Transboundary Haze Pollution Control, which provides a model for regional cooperation in mitigating shared environmental risks.55 Strengthening and expanding such multi-country frameworks, while embedding metabolic, behavioural, and environmental interventions into unified prevention agendas, will be essential for achieving meaningful reductions in the cardiovascular risk across the Asia-Pacific.4,56

The key study finding is the widening regional disparities in the improvements in risk factor-related age-standardised morbidity trends across countries positioned at different stages of the socioeconomic spectrum in the Asia-Pacific.5,12,57,58 While low and low-middle SDI countries displayed slowed progress in cardiovascular risk factor reduction, high SDI countries are reaping the benefits from decades of enhanced education, childhood nutrition, access to healthy foods, and increased habitual exercise, manifesting as rapid and consistent reductions across all risk factors.2 To address this intra-regional variability, useful lessons can be learnt from successful CVD preventive models adopted by high-middle income countries in the region that have seen tremendous strides in cardiovascular risk prevention and control. Our study reveals that in the 1990s, these exemplar countries bore the greatest attributable morbidity across all risk categories, yet they have since achieved the most rapid and sustained improvements, now outperforming global averages for metabolic and behavioural risks. Many of these countries invested heavily into expanding primary care networks, and integrated cardiometabolic management into community-based services, such as China's National Public Health Service Program59 and Thailand's Universal Coverage Scheme.60 Complementing these initiatives were concurrent implementation of population-level interventions, such as Thailand's escalation of tobacco excise taxes and comprehensive advertising bans,61 as well as China's regulatory actions on basic nutrition labelling requirements and voluntary salt reduction initiatives, and public health campaigns promoting healthy eating and physical activity.53 These successful multisectoral Health in All models offer practical and scalable lessons for low-to-middle income countries.62 Moreover, given the wide variations in risk factor burden within each geographical subregion in the Asia–Pacific, our study recommends that the SDI quintile of each country should be taken into consideration when tailoring CVD preventive interventions to the country's socioeconomic means.2

Age-sex disaggregated analyses revealed demographic gradients in cardiovascular risk factor burden across the Asia–Pacific region. Male individuals experienced an earlier peak in cardiovascular risk factor burden, with DALY rates accelerating from the fourth decade and peaking between ages 65 and 69 years, approximately five years earlier than the female counterparts. This pattern reflects higher cumulative exposure to deleterious gendered behavioural risks across the life-course in the Asia–Pacific male population, including tobacco and alcohol use, with earlier onset of metabolic dysfunction. In contrast, females displayed a more gradual accumulation of cardiovascular risk in early and mid-adulthood, with a sharper rise in later life, recording the highest risk factor burden between ages 70 and 79 years. This is underpinned by the postmenopausal metabolic transitions, including loss of oestrogen-mediated vascular protection, accelerated increases in blood pressure and LDL cholesterol, higher propensity to cardiometabolic multimorbidity and longer life expectancy.63,64 The behavioural risk burden in the female population was characterised by higher levels of physical inactivity in later life that may be reflective of greater patterns of frailty, deconditioning, and sarcopenia accentuated in the post-menopausal state.64, 65, 66 Environmental exposures showed a modest male predominance in working ages likely due to occupational factors,67 that converged in later life possibly attributed to the cumulative effects of shared environmental risk factors such as air pollution and non-optimal temperature. Moreover, the paradoxical rise in age-standardised morbidity trends among females in Tonga, Nauru, and Vanuatu represents a distinct female-dominant metabolic health challenge in the region. Prior studies have described that early-onset obesity is more prevalent in females in Pacific Island populations.68 The higher metabolic burden has been attributed to sex-specific disparate drivers, including higher rates of food insecurity69 and undiagnosed metabolic diseases,70,71 lower rates of physical activity and metabolic pharmacotherapy uptake,69,71 and disparate gaps in socioeconomic status and traditional gender roles.72,73 Moreover high rates of polycystic ovarian syndrome and gestational diabetes, coupled with postmenopausal metabolic changes,74,75 contribute to accelerated cardiovascular risks among women, particularly in later adulthood.

This contextualised remapping of cardiovascular risk factor burden in the Asia–Pacific provides insights into the imminent wave of metabolic-centric risk that healthcare systems in the region are facing now, and in the future. The impact of the demographic transition in the most populous, albeit heterogeneous, region, coupled with persistent age-sex-location-socioeconomic disparity and the ever-rising obesity epidemic, may overwhelm underprepared healthcare systems in the future. Strengthening primary care infrastructure and capacity,76,77 and expanding multisectoral CVD preventive policies on metabolic diseases, dietary risks, air pollution, and tobacco use, while ensuring equitable access to preventive services in vulnerable populations will be integral in offsetting the counteractive forces imposed by the enlarging and ageing population. This paper contributes to The Lancet Commission's broader agenda3 in articulating a coherent, region-specific strategy for reducing cardiovascular risk factor burden across diverse health system contexts. Realising this vision will require sustained political commitment, strengthened regional cooperation, and deliberate prioritisation of equity to ensure durable improvements in CVD prevention across the Asia-Pacific.3

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the precision of our estimates is inherently dependent on the availability and quality of primary data across the Asia–Pacific region. Substantial variation exists in the completeness of vital registration systems, population risk factor surveys, and environmental exposure monitoring, particularly in regions with less-developed health monitoring infrastructure.19 For regions and time periods with missing or incomplete primary data, estimates are derived from spatiotemporal modelling of interpolated exposure and outcome estimates.19 Although this allows for greater inter-regional comparability and robust estimates, such approaches are associated with increased uncertainty and may not fully capture local epidemiological realities.19 Second, risk factor-attributable burden calculations depend on relative risk estimates drawn largely from epidemiological studies conducted in high-income or well-characterised populations. While the GBD framework incorporates available Asian cohort data, evidence from Asia–Pacific low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) particularly Oceanian and Southeast Asian countries, remains limited. Consequently, relative risk estimates applied to these populations may not fully reflect the heterogeneity of genetic, behavioural, sociocultural, and environmental contexts in the Asia–Pacific, limiting their generalisability across this diverse region. This introduces residual uncertainty to estimates beyond what is conveyed by the 95% uncertainty intervals and should be considered when interpreting country-level trends in data-sparse areas.1 Third, mortality estimates are derived from cause-of-death data that vary in quality across the region. In settings with incomplete registration systems or inconsistent use of ICD coding, misclassification of cardiovascular deaths and limited ability to distinguish among competing causes limit the accuracy of estimates.4 Fourth, the GBD framework evaluates risk factors independently and does not explicitly model synergistic or interactive effects between co-occurring exposures–for example, the amplified cardiovascular risk observed with combined hypertension and smoking, or the interaction between metabolic dysfunction and air pollution. As such, the estimates presented may underestimate the true burden in populations where clustering of multiple high-risk exposures is common.12 Fifth, our Das Gupta decomposition of attributable burden was performed at the level of age-specific attributable rates rather than at the level of individual components of the population attributable fraction. The ‘epidemiological rate change’ component therefore bundles changes in exposure prevalence, relative risks, TMRELs, and background disease rates, and should be interpreted as a combined epidemiological-improvement quantity rather than as a pure risk-exposure effect. Sixth, this analysis adopts the TMRELs released by the GBD 2023 Comparative Risk Assessment without regional re-estimation. The GBD study does not have Asia-Pacific-specific TMRELs since re-derivation would require individual-participant data from the upstream dose–response meta-analyses. We note, however, that these meta-analyses include substantial Asia–Pacific cohort contributions,78,79 and that emerging data suggest cardiovascular risk in several Asia–Pacific populations may manifest at exposure levels below conventional clinical thresholds,18,78, 79, 80, 81, 82 alluding that the global TMRELs may conservatively approximate, rather than overestimate, attributable risk in the Asia–Pacific. Finally, as race or ethnicity data were not available in the GBD estimates, the present study was not able to comment on subpopulation trends, particularly across heterogenous, culturally distinct, or indigenous populations, such as in the Pacific Island countries, and this is of interest in future research.

Conclusions

Advancements in CVD prevention in the Asia–Pacific have led to a 34.9% reduction in metabolic, behavioural and environmental/occupational risk factor age-standardised morbidity burden from 1990 to 2023. However, the study warns that this progress is likely offset by population ageing and population growth in the region, calling for the urgent need in pre-emptively expanding multisectoral CVD preventive strategies to tackle the ever-rising crude cardiovascular morbidity burden. The metabolic-centric pattern in risk factors within the Asia–Pacific demands immediate action in tackling the region's high-priority risk factors (i.e. high systolic blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, and kidney dysfunction), while addressing other emerging drivers of CVD such as dietary risks and air pollution. With two-thirds of Asia–Pacific countries living with excess cardiovascular risk factor morbidity relative to the global average, this warrants for equitable and accessible health care for all, and prioritising resources to address the age-sex-socioeconomic divide in Asia–Pacific cardiovascular health.
