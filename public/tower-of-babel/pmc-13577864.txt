Symmetrical basal ganglia involvement in pediatric neuropsychiatric lupus: MRI findings in a rare presentation
Authors: Abdur Rehman, Zahra Saeed Ahmed, Kumail Khandwala, Kiran Hilal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBilateral basal ganglia involvement is an uncommon but treatable manifestation of neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) that is rarely described in pediatric patients and is frequently omitted from the standard differential diagnosis for bilateral basal ganglia lesions in childhood. We report an adolescent girl with a recent diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus who presented with acute neuropsychiatric deterioration, including altered mental status and seizures. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated symmetrical T2-weighted/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2/FLAIR) hyperintensity of the bilateral anterior basal ganglia without diffusion restriction, consistent with vasogenic edema. Concomitant findings included a left cerebellar subacute cortical infarct and diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement, a constellation postulated to reflect diffuse systemic lupus erythematosus-related endothelial dysfunction and blood–brain barrier disruption. Infectious and metabolic etiologies were excluded. The patient was managed with pulse methylprednisolone and azathioprine without significant improvement. Subsequent initiation of plasmapheresis resulted in clinical improvement, supporting a humorally mediated mechanism of injury. This case highlights the importance of including NPSLE in the differential diagnosis of symmetrical basal ganglia lesions in children, particularly when systemic autoimmune features are present, to ensure timely escalation of therapy.Keywords: Neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus, Basal ganglia, Vasogenic edema, Pediatric, Brain MRI, NPSLE

Introduction

Neuropsychiatric involvement affects a significant proportion of pediatric patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) commonly demonstrating nonspecific or even unremarkable findings despite being a cornerstone of the diagnostic workup [1,2]. The radiological spectrum of pediatric neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) is typically dominated by focal and multifocal T2-weighted/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2/FLAIR) white-matter hyperintensities, small ischemic infarcts, cerebral atrophy and less commonly, intracranial hemorrhage or cerebral venous sinus thrombosis [1,3]. Cerebrovascular events in these patients are consistently associated with the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies, which remains the strongest serological risk factor for radiologically apparent cerebrovascular disease [3,4].

Bilateral basal ganglia involvement, however, remains a rare finding and is infrequently described in the pediatric population [[5], [6], [7]]. Symmetrical basal ganglia lesions in children usually prompt a differential diagnosis focused on metabolic, toxic, or infectious etiologies, making autoimmune causes such as SLE diagnostically challenging to recognize on neuroimaging. The basal ganglia are conventionally considered spared in lupus; however, emerging quantitative imaging data and small number of dedicated case reports suggest this assumption may require revision [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].

We report an adolescent girl with newly diagnosed SLE who developed acute neuropsychiatric deterioration with MRI demonstrating symmetrical bilateral basal ganglia vasogenic edema. This case highlights an unusual neuroimaging manifestation of pediatric NPSLE. The aim of this report is to contribute to the limited pediatric evidence base for basal ganglia disease as a recognizable, if uncommon, manifestation of NPSLE, and to underscore its potential inclusion in the differential diagnosis when systemic and serological features of lupus coexist with symmetric basal ganglia signal change on neuroimaging.

Case presentation

Clinical history and initial presentation

A previously healthy 13-year-old girl was referred to our emergency department with a 1-week history of progressive headache, 2 days of drowsiness with altered mental status, and 2 witnessed generalized tonic–clonic seizures on the day of admission. Two weeks prior to this presentation, she experienced a febrile illness accompanied by a malar rash and symmetrical small-joint arthralgia. At that time, she was evaluated at an outside institution where skin biopsy, in conjunction with the clinical phenotype, supported a new diagnosis of SLE. Hydroxychloroquine and oral prednisolone therapy were initiated, resulting in gradual improvement of the fever, rash, and joint symptoms over the following days. There was no previous relevant medical or surgical history, and no family history of similar illness.

Neuroimaging at our institution

Following the onset of altered mentation and seizure, brain MRI was performed which demonstrated symmetrical abnormal signal intensities involving the bilateral basal ganglia—including the caudate and lentiform nuclei—which appeared hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences without corresponding diffusion restriction or post contrast enhancement, a pattern consistent with vasogenic edema (Fig. 1A-D). A focal T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesion was additionally noted in the left cerebellar hemisphere, demonstrating restricted diffusion and laminar necrosis, indicating subacute cortical infarction (Fig. 1E-I).

Laboratory and ancillary investigations

Subsequent electroencephalography (EEG) revealed intermittent generalized delta bursts. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed elevated protein levels, low glucose, and lymphocytic predominance. A comprehensive Infectious workup, including meningitis panel and mycobacterial culture, was negative. Antiphospholipid antibody evaluation revealed an activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) below the normal lower limit, with no other significant coagulopathy. Full blood count demonstrated pancytopenia, and alkaline phosphatase was reduced: liver function tests were otherwise within normal limits. Renal evaluation for lupus nephritis and echocardiography was unremarkable.

Treatment and clinical course

Given the diagnosis of NPSLE, the patient was started on intravenous pulse methylprednisolone therapy (1 g daily) for 5 days followed by oral prednisolone. However, no significant clinical improvement was observed. Azathioprine was subsequently added to the treatment regimen. Due to persistent clinical deterioration, rituximab administration was considered but was deferred following a decline in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score requiring intensive care management. Therapeutic plasmapheresis was initiated thereafter via jugular venous catheter access. Following the first cycle of plasmapheresis, the patient demonstrated improvement in GCS and overall neurological status, and she was stepped down to a special care unit. A second cycle of plasmapheresis was performed, and the patient remained clinically stable, afebrile, and free of seizure activity.

The patient was later transferred to another institution for continuation of care due to personal reasons. Follow-up imaging was therefore unavailable. However, telephone follow-up obtained 3 months later revealed she was in good health, with no recurrent seizures and significant overall clinical improvement.

Discussion

Bilateral basal ganglia involvement represents an atypical neuroimaging manifestation of pediatric NPSLE. The spectrum of abnormalities most described in pediatric NPSLE include nonspecific white matter hyperintensities, ischemic infarcts, cerebral atrophy, and hemorrhagic complications [1,3]. In contrast, the MRI findings in this case—symmetrical T2/FLAIR hyperintensity of the bilateral anterior basal ganglia without diffusion restriction, consistent with edema—are rarely described and may pose a diagnostic challenge because of the broad list of differentials associated with this imaging pattern. A comparative review of previously reported pediatric cases suggests that isolated or predominant basal ganglia involvement in pediatric neuropsychiatric SLE remains exceptionally rare, with our case demonstrating additional imaging features of cerebellar and leptomeningeal involvement not consistently described in prior literature [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. The pathophysiologic basis of basal ganglia involvement in NPSLE has not been fully elucidated. Sato et al. [8] reporting one of the few dedicated pediatric cases, described 3 children with NPSLE and reversible basal ganglia lesions in whom elevated CSF interleukin-6, anti-NMDA-receptor and anti-ribosomal-P antibodies suggested an autoantibody- and cytokine-mediated mechanism rather than a primary ischemic etiology [8]. Tsubouchi and colleagues described a young adult with symmetric basal ganglia lesions exhibiting a mixed pattern of cytotoxic edema core surrounded by vasogenic edema, supporting the notion that both immune-mediated vasculopathy and direct neuronal injury may contribute [9]. These mechanisms are further supported by quantitative susceptibility mapping studies demonstrating subclinical basal ganglia signal abnormalities in adult NPSLE patients even when conventional MRI appears unremarkable [14,15], and by the identification of enlarged perivascular spaces within the basal ganglia as a potential marker of glymphatic dysfunction in this population [16]. Collectively, these observations argue against the assumption that basal ganglia structures are spared in lupus and provide a plausible biological context for the overt vasogenic edema observed in our patient.

There is also evidence in literature to suggest the constellation of findings in our case can be a result of concurrent vascular and inflammatory neuropsychiatric SLE, instead of 2 distinct processes. In NPSLE, the role of vasculopathy and autoimmune-inflammatory mechanisms are now recognized as being complementary, with blood–brain barrier disruption as a major link between the systemic immune dysfunction and CNS injury [17,18]. In this context, the coexistence of cerebellar ischemia, basal ganglia vasogenic edema, and leptomeningeal enhancement is consistent with a diffuse endothelial dysfunction and small-vessel vasculopathy, compromising microvascular perfusion and increasing blood–brain barrier permeability to circulating autoantibodies, cytokines, immune complexes, and complement [19,20]. Within this framework, the cerebellar ischemia and the inflammatory blood–brain barrier failure underlying the basal ganglia edema and leptomeningeal enhancement potentially represent complementary injuries along a single pathophysiologic continuum [17,18,20].

This mechanism is further supported by the patient's rapid improvement following plasmapheresis. Plasma exchange removes circulating autoantibodies and immune complexes and reduces complement components, cytokines, and other inflammatory mediators implicated in endothelial activation and CNS injury [21,22]. A brisk clinical response to such therapy can therefore be potentially biologically consistent with the humorally mediated process proposed above [17,19,20]. The evidence for plasma exchange in SLE remains predominantly observational, largely as rescue therapy in severe refractory disease; the response observed here is accordingly presented as indirect mechanistic support rather than proof of efficacy [21,22].

The differential diagnosis for bilateral symmetrical basal ganglia signal change is extensive and includes metabolic disorders, mitochondrial disease, toxic exposure, infectious encephalitis, autoimmune basal ganglia encephalitis, and atypical posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) [[5], [6], [7],23]. A summary is presented in Table 3. Principal alternate diagnoses were considered systematically. Due to the vasogenic edema pattern, PRES was considered in this patient since it is a recognized complication of juvenile SLE [23]. However, the absence of hypertension in our patient, coupled with the lesion distribution which was predominantly anterior rather than parieto-occipital, made this a less convincing unifying diagnosis. Infectious causes, including tuberculous meningoencephalitis, were excluded by negative CSF microbiology including mycobacterial culture. Post-infectious and primary autoimmune basal-ganglia encephalitides, such as Sydenham chorea and pediatric acute-onset neuropsychiatric syndrome (PANDAS) were not supported by the clinical phenotype, which lacked chorea, tics or obsessive-compulsive features; these entities are furthermore typically associated with normal or only subtly abnormal conventional MRI rather than overt vasogenic edema [5]. Inherited metabolic disorders were also considered unlikely given the acute presentation, absence of developmental regression, and established systemic lupus phenotype [6,7] (Fig. 1).

Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis was also less convincing as a differential since malignant disease more frequently demonstrates thick nodular or mixed enhancement, involvement of the cranial nerves, hydrocephalus or association with malignancy [24]. The other inflammatory leptomeningeal processes, including neurosarcoidosis and autoimmune meningitis, were radiologic mimics, but these usually required supportive systemic, serologic, CSF analysis, follow-up, or tissue evidence because there was significant imaging overlap [25].

Recognition of this atypical imaging pattern is clinically important because early diagnosis of NPSLE may facilitate timely escalation of immunosuppressive therapeutic measures and potentially improve neurological outcomes. This case has several limitations that should be considered. Follow-up neuroimaging was not performed at our institution following treatment, precluding radiological confirmation of lesion reversibility. Additionally, the initial MRI performed at an outside institution although reportedly normal, was unavailable for our review, limiting our ability to characterize the temporal evolution of imaging findings. Despite these limitations, this case contributes to the limited pediatric literature describing bilateral basal ganglia abnormalities as a manifestation of NPSLE. It also highlights the need to consider autoimmune etiologies in the differential diagnosis of symmetrical basal ganglia abnormalities in children and adolescents.

Conclusion

Bilateral basal ganglia involvement is a rare but important manifestation of pediatric NPSLE. When symmetric basal ganglia caudate and lentiform signal abnormalities are encountered in a child or adolescent with systemic or serological features suggestive of autoimmune disease, consideration of NPSLE alongside the broader pediatric differential diagnoses of bilateral basal ganglia disease is warranted. Recognition of this atypical imaging pattern is critical, as it may prompt earlier diagnosis and facilitate timely initiation or escalation of immunosuppressive therapy. The present case adds to the limited pediatric literature describing this presentation and reinforces the importance of considering NPSLE in the appropriate clinical and radiologic context.
