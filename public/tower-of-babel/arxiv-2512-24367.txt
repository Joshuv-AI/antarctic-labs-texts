Abstract
. In this paper, we prove that the Euclidean distance between two independent random vectors uniformly distributed on l p n l_{p}^{n} -balls ( 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞ 1\leq p\leq\infty ) or on its boundary satisfies a central limit theorem as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Also, we give a compact proof of the case of the sphere, which was proved by Hammersley in [ 5 ] . Furthermore, we complement our central limit theorem by providing large deviation principles for the cases p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 .



1.
Introduction, notation and results.

Recent developments in Asymptotic Geometric Analysis have significantly deepened our understanding of high-dimensional spaces. This interdisciplinary field, which merges techniques from analysis, geometry, and probability, has unveiled striking regularities and patterns in geometric structures as the dimension grows. In particular, the behavior of random objects in high dimensions has been a central focus, revealing connections to classical probabilistic phenomena.

One prominent area of study concerns central limit theorems (CLT) in high-dimensional geometry. Early works by Poincaré and Borel (see
[
2
]
or
[
16
]
for modern expositions) demonstrated that as the dimension of Euclidean spaces increases, the distribution of random points in high-dimensional spheres or balls tends to converge to a Gaussian distribution. This convergence has since been extended to more general convex bodies, notably through Klartag’s CLT
[
6
,
7
]
, which establishes that projections of random vectors on high-dimensional convex sets exhibit Gaussian-like behavior.

Further advances in the field have focused on understanding the probabilistic structure of high-dimensional bodies, such as cubes and
l_{p}^{n}
-balls. The work of Paouris, Pivovarov, and Zinn
[
10
]
has contributed important results in this direction, providing a central limit theorem for the volume of
k
-dimensional random projections (
k
fixed) of the
n
-dimensional cube as the space dimension
n
tends to infinity. Prochno, Thäle and Tuchel
[
12
]
generalized and complemented this considerably by establishing a central limit theorem and moderate and large deviation principles for the volume of random projections and sections of
l_{p}^{n}
-balls. Moreover, there is a central limit theorem for the volume of convex hulls of Gaussian random vectors obtained by Bárány and Vu
[
1
]
or Reitzner’s central limit theorems for the volume and the number of
i
-dimensional faces of random polytopes in smooth convex bodies
[
13
]
.

Beyond these normal fluctuations, there has been growing interest in understanding large deviations in high-dimensional spaces. These deviations capture rare events where the behavior of random vectors significantly differs from typical Gaussian outcomes, highlighting non-universal features of different convex bodies. In particular, the study of large deviation principles (LDPs) has provided critical insights into these rare events, offering a more complete picture of the probabilistic behavior of random vectors in convex bodies; we refer the interested reader to
[
11
]
and the references cited therein.

The aim of this paper is to investigate CLTs and LDPs related to the Euclidean distance between random vectors in
l_{p}^{n}
-balls, to uncover deeper connections between geometric properties and probabilistic phenomena in high-dimensional settings. This problem was first researched by Hammersley in
[
5
]
, where he proved an initial CLT for the case of the hypersphere. Further, it was extended to spherical distributions by Lord in
[
8
]
. In this paper, we extend those results to
l_{p}^{n}
-balls and their respective boundaries. Furthermore, we obtain LDPs for the regime
2\leq p\leq\infty
.

We start with the central limit theorems and then present the corresponding large deviations counterparts. For
p\in[1,\infty]
and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recall the definition of the
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-norm as

\|x\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&\quad:p<\infty\\
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|&\quad:p=\infty.\\
\end{cases}

We denote the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-norm by
B_{p}^{n}
, that is,

\displaystyle B_{p}^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\|x\|_{p}\leq 1\},

and we write
\partial B_{p}^{n}
for its boundary. We write
X\sim\text{Unif}(B_{p}^{n})
or
X\sim\text{Unif}(\partial B_{p}^{n})
to denote that
X
is a random vector uniformly distributed on
B_{p}^{n}
or on
\partial B_{p}^{n}
, with respect to the cone measure (see Section
2.2
), respectively.

1.1.
Central limit theorems

A cornerstone in probability theory is the central limit theorem. A sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

of random variables is said to satisfy the central limit theorem if

\displaystyle\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}X_{n}}{\sqrt{\text{Var}X_{n}}}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N(0,1),

where
\overset{d}{\rightarrow}
denotes convergence in distribution as
n\to\infty
. That is, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}X_{n}}{\sqrt{\text{Var}X_{n}}}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)=0,

where
\Phi(t)
is the distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
More generally, we say that a sequence of random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a CLT if
Y_{n}\overset{d}{\to}N(0,\sigma^{2})
, for some
\sigma>0
.

Let us introduce the followings quantities

\displaystyle M_{p}(\alpha):=\frac{\Gamma\left((\alpha+1)/p\right)}{\Gamma(1/p)},

where
\alpha>0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.

In this paper, we are going to prove the following theorems. This first theorem establishes a central limit theorem for the distance between
X^{(n)}
and
Y^{(n)}
, which are random vectors uniformly distributed in
l_{p}^{n}
-balls or on their boundary.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
, and let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(\partial B_{p}^{n})
or
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(B_{p}^{n})
be independent random vectors. Then,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(n^{1/p-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}-\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(3/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)}}p^{1/p}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{p}^{2}),

where
\sigma_{p}^{2}>0
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{2}=\frac{p^{2/p}\left(M_{p}(4)+((M_{p}(2))^{2}\right)}{4M_{p}(2)}.

Note that, due to the nature of central limit theorems, the same CLT holds when we consider
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
to be uniformly distributed on
l_{p}^{n}
-balls or on their boundary. Independently, we obtain a similar result for the
n
-dimensional cube and its boundary. Moreover, taking the limit as
p\to\infty
in Theorem
1.1
, it recovers the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(\partial B_{\infty}^{n})
or
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(B_{\infty}^{n})
. Then,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\left(0,\frac{7}{30}\right).

1.2.
Large deviation principles

Large deviation principles describe the asymptotic behavior of the probability of rare events for a sequence of random variables by characterizing their exponential decay rate in terms of the speed and a rate function (see section
2.4
for precise definitions).

We now present the main theorems on the scale of large deviations.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p\geq 2
, and let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(\partial B_{p}^{n})
be independent random vectors. Then, the sequence
\left(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left(n^{1/p-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function

I_{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\geq 0,y>0\\
x^{1/2}y^{-1/p}=z\end{subarray}}\Lambda^{*}(x,y)}&\quad\text{: }z\geq 0\\
+\infty&\quad\text{: }z<0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\Lambda^{*}
is the Legendre-Fenchel transform of the function

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{t_{1}|x-y|^{2}+t_{2}|y|^{p}-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-\frac{|y|^{p}}{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dxdy.

Remark 1.1
.

Let us point out that
\text{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes a normalised distance rather than the standard Euclidean distance. Nonetheless, they coincide if
p=2
.

In contrast to the CLT, large deviation principles exhibit a more sensitive dependence on the underlying distribution. As shown in the following theorems, the LDPs for points uniformly distributed on the boundary and those in
l_{p}^{n}
-balls lead to different behaviors, reflecting the focus of large deviations on tail events rather than fluctuations around the mean.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
p\geq 2
, and let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(B_{p}^{n})
be independent. Then, the sequence
\left(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left(n^{1/p-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function

I_{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\geq 0,z_{2}\geq 0\\
z=z_{1}z_{2}\end{subarray}}I_{V}(z_{1},z_{2})}&\quad\text{: }z\geq 0\\
+\infty&\quad\text{: }z<0,\\
\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle I_{V}(z_{1},z_{2}):=I_{U}(z_{1})+I_{W}(z_{2}),\quad(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

being

I_{U}(z)=\begin{cases}\log(z)&\quad\text{: }z\in(0,1]\\
+\infty&\quad\text{: otherwise }\\
\end{cases}

and

I_{W}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\geq 0,y>0\\
x^{1/2}y^{-1/p}=z\end{subarray}}\Lambda^{*}(x,y)}&\quad\text{: }z\geq 0\\
+\infty&\quad\text{: }z<0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\Lambda^{*}
is the Legendre-Fenchel transform of the function

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{t_{1}|x-y|^{2}+t_{2}|y|^{p}-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-\frac{|y|^{p}}{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dxdy.

We now turn to the case of the
n
-dimensional cube, where we establish a large deviations principle for points uniformly distributed in the cube.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(B_{\infty}^{n})
be independent random vectors. Then, the sequence
\left(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=\left(n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function

I_{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\geq 0\\
z=\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}\Lambda^{*}(x)}&\quad\text{: }z\geq 0\\
+\infty&\quad\text{: }z<0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\Lambda^{*}
is the Legendre-Fenchel transform of the function

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=\log\left[2\int_{0}^{2}e^{tx^{2}}\left(1-\frac{x}{2}\right)dx\right].

2.
Notation and preliminaries

2.1.
General notation

Let
A\subset\mathbb{X}
be a subset of some topological space
\mathbb{X}
, we denote its interior as
A^{\circ}
and its closure as
\overline{A}
. Given a Borel probability measure
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we indicate by
X\sim\tau
that the random vector
X
has distribution
\tau
. In particular, we denote as
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
that the random variable
X
has a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. In dimension
n\geq 2
, we write
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
to indicate the multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Given
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
\overset{d}{\to}
,
\overset{p}{\to}
and
\overset{a.s.}{\to}
to indicate convergence in distribution, probability and almost sure, respectively. Moreover, given two random elements
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, we indicate equality in distribution as
X_{1}\overset{d}{=}X_{2}
. Given two sequences of random vectors
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we denote asymptotic equivalence in distribution, i.e.,
\|X_{n}-Y_{n}\|_{2}\overset{p}{\to}0
, as
X_{n}\overset{d}{\sim}X_{n}
. We write
\mathbb{E}
and
Var
for the expected value and variance, respectively.

2.2.
Probabilistic aspects of
l_{p}^{n}
-balls

Recall that for
p\in[1,\infty]
and
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\|x\|_{p}
for the
p
-norm of
x
. We denote the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-norm by
B_{p}^{n}
, that is

\displaystyle B_{p}^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\|x\|_{p}\leq 1\},

and we write
\partial B_{p}^{n}
for its boundary. Additionally, we will write
S^{n-1}:=\partial B_{2}^{n}
to denote the Euclidean sphere.

Let
\sigma_{p}^{n}
be the surface measure (Hausdorff measure) on
\partial B_{p}^{n}
,
p\geq 1
, and denote by
\mu_{p}^{n}
the cone probability measure on
\partial B_{p}^{n}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{p}^{n}(A)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(A)}\mathrm{Vol}\left(\{ta\in\mathbb{R}^{n};a\in A,0\leq t\leq 1\}\right),\;\;\;\;A\subset\partial B_{p}^{n},

where
\mathrm{Vol}
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

Recall that a random variable is said to be
p-
generalized Gaussian (
1\leq p<\infty
) if its density function
f_{p}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{p}(x)=C_{p}^{-1}e^{-|x|^{p}/p},\;\;\;x\in\mathbb{R},

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, where the normalization constant
C_{p}
is given by
C_{p}:=2p^{1/p}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)
. For the case
p=\infty
, we define the
\infty
-generalized Gaussian as a random variable uniformly distributed on
[-1,1]
. Notice that, if
g
is a
p
-generalized Gaussian for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, then for every
\alpha\geq 0
,
\mathbb{E}|g|^{\alpha}=M_{p}(\alpha)
.

We recall from
[
15
]
the following probabilistic representation for a uniformly distributed random vector in
B_{p}^{n}
and in
\partial B_{p}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.1

(Schechtman and Zinn)
.

Fix
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
Y=(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})
be a random vector with independent
p
-generalized Gaussian coordinates
Y_{1},...,Y_{n}
, and let
U
be a random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, independent of
Y
. Then,

•

The random vector
\displaystyle{\frac{Y}{\|Y\|_{p}}}
and the random varianble
\|Y\|_{p}
are independent.

•

\displaystyle{\frac{Y}{\|Y\|_{p}}}
is uniformly distributed on
\partial B_{p}^{n}
according to the cone measure
\mu_{p}^{n}
.

•

\displaystyle{U^{1/n}\frac{Y}{\|Y\|_{p}}}
is uniformly distributed in
B_{p}^{n}
.

2.3.
Delta Method

We will use the following technical lemma, which characterizes the asymptotic distribution of a differentiable function of a random vector which is asymptotically Gaussian (see
[
9
]
).

Lemma 2.2

(Delta method)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
k-
dimensional random vectors such that
\sqrt{n}(X_{n}-\mu)
converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be continuously differentiable at
\mu
with Jacobian matrix
J_{g}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(g(X_{n})-g(\mu))\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\left(0,J_{g}\Sigma J_{g}^{T}\right)

provided
J_{g}\Sigma J_{g}^{T}
is positive definite.

2.4.
Large deviations

Let
X:=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random elements taking values in some Hausdorff topological space
\mathbb{X}
. Then, we say that
X
satisfies a large deviation principle (LDP) with speed
s(n)
and rate function
I_{X}
if
s:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
,
I_{X}:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
is lower semi-continuous, and if

\displaystyle-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}I_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s(n)}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s(n)}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A)\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{A}}I_{X}(x)

for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{X}
.

The rate function
I_{X}
is said to be a good rate function if it has compact level sets
\{x\in\mathbb{X};I_{X}(x)\leq\alpha\}
, for every
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Then, the Legendre-Fenchel transform of
\Lambda
is denoted as
\Lambda^{*}
and defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\langle x,u\rangle-\Lambda(u)\right],\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, we define the (effective) domain of
\Lambda
to be the set
D_{\Lambda}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\Lambda(u)<\infty\}
.

We now present some fundamental results from large deviations theory. First, we recall what is known as Cramér’s theorem, which provides an LDP for sequences of independent and identically distributed random vectors (Theorem 2.2.3. in
[
3
]
).

Lemma 2.3

(Cramer’s theorem)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},...
be independent and identically distributed random vectors taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Assume that the origin is an interior point of
D_{\Lambda}
, where
\Lambda(u)=\log\mathbb{E}e^{\langle u,X\rangle}
. Then, the sequence of partial sums
\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with speed
n
and good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
.

Next result, which will be used in the following sections, states that if
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of random variables that satisfies an LDP with speed
s(n)
and rate function
I_{X}
and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of random variables that is ”close” to
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in some sense, then the sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
will also satisfy an LDP under some conditions (Theorem 4.2.13. in
[
3
]
).

Lemma 2.4

(Exponential equivalence)
.

Let
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
Y=(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences of random variables and assume that
X
satisfies an LDP with speed
s(n)
and rate function
I_{X}
. Further, assume that
X
and
Y
are exponentially equivalent, i.e.,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s(n)}\log\mathbb{P}(|X_{n}-Y_{n}|>\delta)=-\infty

for any
\delta>0
. Then,
Y
satisfies an LDP with the same speed and the same rate function as
X
.

Lastly, the contraction principle allows to transform a given LDP to another by a continuous function (Theorem 4.2.1. in
[
3
]
).

Lemma 2.5

(Contraction principle)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
be two Hausdorff topological spaces and
F:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
be a continuous function. Further, let
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathbb{X}
-valued random elements that satisfies an LDP with speed
s(n)
and good rate function
I_{X}
. Then, the sequence
Y:=(F(X_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}
with the same speed and with good rate function
I_{Y}=I_{X}\circ F^{-1}
, i.e.,
I_{Y}(y):=\inf\{I_{X}(x);F(x)=y\}
,
y\in\mathbb{Y}
with the convention that
I_{Y}(y)=+\infty
if
F^{-1}(\{y\})=\emptyset
.

3.
Previous results and comparison

In this section, we will provide a short and compact proof of Hammersley’s CLT in the case of the
n
-dimensional Euclidean sphere (see
[
5
]
).

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
be independent random vectors uniformly distributed on the Euclidean
n
-dimensional sphere,
S^{n-1}
. In
[
5
]
, the author approached the problem by studying the distribution function of the distance between
X^{(n)}
and
Y^{(n)}
, which was shown to be close to an incomplete beta-function ratio, and therefore, tends asymptotically to a Gaussian, as the dimension grows.

Following
[
5
]
, in
[
8
]
, the author proved the result by extending it to spherical distributions, whose density function depends only on the modulus of the random vector.

The underlying idea of the proofs relies on the rotational invariance of the sphere, which implies that
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
depends only on a single random variable. To see this, fix a point
x_{0}\in S^{n-1}
on the sphere. For any
x\in S^{n-1}
,

\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{dist}(x,y)\}}d\sigma(y)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{dist}(x_{0},y)\}}d\sigma(y),

where
\sigma
is the normalized cone measure on
S^{n-1}
, i.e.,
\sigma(S^{n-1})=1
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})\leq t)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}(\text{dist}(x,Y^{(n)})\leq t\mid X^{(n)}=x)\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{dist}(x,y)\leq t\}}\,d\sigma(y)\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{dist}(x_{0},y)\leq t\}}\,d\sigma(y)\int_{S^{n-1}}d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{dist}(x_{0},y)\leq t\}}\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})\leq t).

Therefore, the problem is reduced to studying the asymptotics of the random variable
\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})
. For the general case, i.e., when
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
are uniformly distributed on
B_{p}^{n}
or
\partial B_{p}^{n}
, for
p\in[1,\infty]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we develop suitable techniques in sections
4
and
5
.

Let us prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{n-1})
be independent random vectors. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]}{\sqrt{Var[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]}}\overset{d}{\to}N(0,1).

In particular,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2n}\left(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}\right)\overset{d}{\to}N(0,1).

To prove this result, let us first compute the distribution function of the distance.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
be independent random vectors uniformly distributed on
S^{n-1}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for every
t\in[0,2]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})\leq t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{1-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{(n-3)/2}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{0}^{s}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta,

where
s=arccos(1-t^{2}/2)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we fix a point on the sphere
x_{0}=e_{1}
. Let
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
be the angle between the line joining
x_{0}
and the origin, with the line joining the origin and a point
y_{\theta}
on the sphere.

First, note that the lenght of the chord joining
x_{0}
and
y_{\theta}
is
2\sin(\theta/2)
, and so it follows that
\cos(\theta)=1-\text{dist}(x_{0},y_{\theta})^{2}/2
.

Given an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, the
(n-2)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the points at distance
2\sin(\theta/2)
of
x_{0}
is

\frac{2\pi^{\frac{(n-1)}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}.

Notice that, given
t\in[0,2]
, the event
\{\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})\leq t\}
has the same pobability as the event
\{2\sin(\theta/2)\leq t\}
, where
\theta
is again the angle between the line joining
x_{0}
and the origin, with the line joining the origin and
Y^{(n)}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}([\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]\leq t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}}{\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{0}^{s}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta,

where
t=2\sin(s/2)
or
t=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1-\cos(s)}
, i.e.,
s=2\arcsin(t/2)
or
s=\arccos(1-t^{2}/2)
. Thus, we can also write is as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}([\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]\leq t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{1-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{(n-3)/2}dx.

∎

Now, we are ready to compute the expected value of the distance.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{n-1})
independent random vectors. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]=\frac{2^{n-1}\left(\Gamma(n/2)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-1/2)}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\sqrt{2}.

Proof.

By differentiating the distribution function of
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
, we obtain its density function given by

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\left(\sin\left(2\arcsin\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\right)\right)^{n-2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{t^{2}}{4}}}.

Applying the change of variables
\theta=2\arcsin(t/2)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{0}^{\pi}2\sin{(\theta/2)}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta.

Using the identity
\sin(x)=2\sin(x/2)\cos(x/2)
and the change of variables
x=\theta/2
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}2\sin{(\theta/2)}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}2\sin{(\theta/2)}(2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2))^{n-2}d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}(2\sin{(\theta/2)})^{n-1}(\cos(\theta/2))^{n-2}d\theta

\displaystyle=2^{n}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\sin(x))^{n-1}(\cos(x))^{n-2}dx

\displaystyle=2^{n-1}B\left(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}\right),

where
B
is the Beta function. Thus, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}2^{n-1}B\left(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}2^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{2n-1}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-1}\left(\Gamma(n/2)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-1/2)}.

Furthermore, using the second order Stirling’s formula,

\displaystyle\Gamma(1+n)=\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{12n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right),

and applying it to
\Gamma(n/2)
and
\Gamma(n-1/2)
, a straightforward computation yields

\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]=\sqrt{2}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}n}+o\left(1/n\right).

∎

In a similar way, we can compute the variance of the distance and its asymptotics.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(S^{n-1})
independent random vectors. Then,

Var[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]=2-\left(\frac{2^{n-1}\left(\Gamma(n/2)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-1/2)}\right)^{2},

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}2nVar[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]=1.

Proof.

Let us compute

Var[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]=\mathbb{E}[(\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)}))^{2}]-\left(\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]\right)^{2}.

Since for every
\theta\in[0,2\pi]

\sin(\theta/2)=\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}},

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{0}^{\pi}(2\sin((\theta/2))^{2}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\left[\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta-\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(\theta)(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta\right]

On the one hand, using the Beta function,

\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta=B\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(n/2)}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(\theta)(\sin(\theta))^{n-2}d\theta=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle Var[\text{dist}(x_{0},Y^{(n)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\left[\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}{\Gamma(n/2)}\right]-\left(\frac{2^{n-1}\left(\Gamma(n/2)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-1/2)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2-\left(\frac{2^{n-1}\left(\Gamma(n/2)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-1/2)}\right)^{2}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
3.2
taking the limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain that

\lim_{n\to\infty}Var[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]=0

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}2nVar[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]=1.

∎

Before proving Theorem
3.1
, we need the following technical result (see
[
14
]
, Theorem).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of densities on
\mathbb{R}
such that,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}(x)=p(x)

for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
p
is a density, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{S}p_{n}(x)dx=\int_{S}p(x)dx

uniformly for all Borel sets
S
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We write
\mu_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]
and
\sigma^{2}_{n}:=Var[\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})]
, whose values are provided by Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
. Then, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}=\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}\sigma_{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}.

By Slutsky’s Theorem, to prove Theorem
3.1
, it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\overset{d}{\to}N(0,1),

since by Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
,
we have that

\left|\frac{\sqrt{2}-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}n\sigma_{n}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right|

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}\sqrt{2n}=1.

Note that the random variable
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
takes values on the interval
[0,2]
. Thus, the normalized random variable
\sqrt{2n}\left(\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}\right)
takes values of the interval
[-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{2n}-2\sqrt{n}]
.

By Lemma
3.1
we have that for any
t\in[-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{2n}-2\sqrt{n}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4n}-\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{(n-3)/2}dx.

Let
t\in[-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{2n}-2\sqrt{n}]
. We use the change of variables
\sqrt{n}x=y
, and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}dy.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
n_{t}:=\inf\{n;t\in[-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{2n}-2\sqrt{n}]\}
. Then, note that for any
n\geq n_{t}
, since the integrand is non-negative and bounded above by
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy+\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{-t}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy+\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{-t}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy+\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}\Bigg).

We notice that

\displaystyle f_{n}(y):=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)

is a probability density for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and that
f_{n}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}/2}
pointwise, since taking limits
n\to\infty
, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}}

and

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}=e^{\frac{-y^{2}}{2}}}.

Then, by Theorem
3.2
, we have that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy+\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}dy.

For the lower bound, note that if
n\geq n_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\sqrt{n}}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}dy+\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{-t}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}dy\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\Bigg(\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy+\int_{-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sqrt{n}}-t}^{-t}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}dy\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy.

Since we already proved that the latter expression converges to
\int_{-t}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}dy
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})\sqrt{n}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{n}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathds{1}_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}dy.

Thus, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})-\sqrt{2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-t}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx=\mathbb{P}\left(g\leq t\right),

where
g\sim N(0,1)
.
∎

4.
Central Limit Theorems

In this section, we are going to prove the Central Limit Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. We divide the section into two subsections. The first one is devoted to random vectors uniformly distributed on
l_{p}^{n}
-balls
(1\leq p<\infty)
or on their boundaries according to the cone measure, in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, and the second one is devoted to the case of the cube (Theorem
1.2
).

4.1.
CLT for
l_{p}^{n}-
balls

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
be independent random vectors uniformly distributed on the boundary of
B_{p}^{n}
. By the Schechtman-Zinn representation (Lemma
2.1
),

\displaystyle X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}\frac{G}{\|G\|_{p}}\;\text{ and }\;Y^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}\frac{G^{\prime}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}},

where
G=(g_{1},...,g_{n})
and
G^{\prime}=(g^{\prime}_{1},...,g^{\prime}_{n})
are independent
p
-generalized Gaussian vectors on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is, their components are
p
-generalized Gaussian random variables on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then, we can see the distance between random vectors uniformly distributed on the boundary of
B_{p}^{n}
, normalized by
n^{1/p-1/2}
, as

\displaystyle\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})

\displaystyle=n^{1/p-1/2}\left\|\frac{G}{\|G\|_{p}}-\frac{G^{\prime}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}\right\|_{2}=n^{1/p-1/2}\left\|\frac{(g_{1},...,g_{n})}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}-\frac{(g^{\prime}_{1},...,g^{\prime}_{n})}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left\|(g_{1},...,g_{n})-\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}(g^{\prime}_{1},...,g^{\prime}_{n})}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left\|\left(g_{i}-\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}g_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\right\|_{2}.

Note that by the Strong law of large numbers (SLLN),

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}1.

Thus, the idea of the proof will be to first prove that
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
and

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left\|\left(g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\right\|_{2},

have the same asymptotic distribution, and then prove a CLT for (
4.1
). This will imply that
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
satisfies the same CLT.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and let
g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime}
with
i=1,...,n
, be independent
p-
generalized Gaussian random variables. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left\|\left(g_{i}-\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}g_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\right\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left\|\left(g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

Let
G=(g_{1},...,g_{n})
and
G^{\prime}=(g^{\prime}_{1},...,g^{\prime}_{n})
be independent
p
-generalized Gaussian vectors on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Take
A_{n}:=\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}
and
f(x):=\|G-xG^{\prime}\|_{2}
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
. By the mean value theorem, for almost every value of
G
and
G^{\prime}
there exists some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle f(1)-f(A_{n})=f^{\prime}(\xi)(1-A_{n}).

Note that
f^{\prime}
is differentiable for all
x
except possibly when
G=xG^{\prime}
. This event has probability
0
. Therefore, with probability
1
,
f
is differentiable everywhere, and the mean value theorem applies.
Then,

\displaystyle\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}-\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}=\left\langle\frac{G-\xi G^{\prime}}{\|G-\xi G^{\prime}\|},(1-A_{n})G^{\prime}\right\rangle,

for some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}-\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}\right|\leq|1-A_{n}|\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\left|\;n^{-1/2}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}-n^{-1/2}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|\leq n^{-1/2}|1-A_{n}|\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Let us bound this last expression. For the first factor, by the SLLN,

\displaystyle A_{n}=\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}1,

therefore,

\displaystyle|1-A_{n}|\overset{a.s.}{\to}0.

For the second factor, again by the SLLN, there exists come constant
C:=\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}>0
such that

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}C.

Then, it is clear that

\displaystyle\left|\;n^{-1/2}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}-n^{-1/2}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|\overset{a.s.}{\to}0.

It follows that

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{-1/2}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

Therefore, since
n^{1/p}\|G\|_{p}^{-1}\overset{a.s.}{\to}M_{p}(p)^{-1/p}>0
, by Slutsky Theorem we obtain that

\displaystyle n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{1}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{1}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

∎

Let us prove Thorem
1.1
in the case that
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(\partial B_{p}^{n})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(boundary case).

Let us define the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle f(x,y)=x^{1/2}\frac{1}{y^{1/p}}.

First, note that

\displaystyle n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{1}{\|G\|_{p}}\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}=f\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2},|g_{i}|^{p}\right)\right).

Take
S_{1,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}
and
S_{2,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.1
we have that

\displaystyle n^{1/p-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}f\left((S_{1,n},S_{2,n})\right).

By the classical CLT,
\left(\sqrt{n}(S_{1,n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{1,n}]),\sqrt{n}(S_{2,n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{2,n}])\right)
converges in distribution to
N(0,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of
(S_{1,n},S_{2,n})
. Thus, applying the Delta method (Lemma
2.2
) to
X_{n}=(S_{1,n},S_{2,n})
and the function
f
, we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(f(X_{n})-f\left(\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\right)\right)\overset{d}{\to}N\left(0,J_{f}\Sigma J_{f}^{T}\right),

where
J_{f}
is the Jacobian matrix of
f
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=(2M_{p}(2),M_{p}(p))
.

Let us compute the covariance matrix

\Sigma=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{Var}[|g_{1}-g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}]&\text{Cov}[|g_{1}-g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2},|g_{1}|^{p}]\\
\text{Cov}[|g_{1}-g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2},|g_{1}|^{p}]&\text{Var}[|g_{1}|^{p}]\\
\end{array}}\right].

For the first term,

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left[|g_{1}|^{2}+|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-2g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(|g_{1}|^{2}+|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-2g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{2}+|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-2g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{4}+|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{4}+6|g_{1}|^{2}|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-4|g_{1}|^{2}g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}-4|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}\right]-\left(2\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{4}\right]+6E\left[|g_{1}|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}\right]-\left(2\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{4}\right]+2\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2M_{p}(4)+2(M_{p}(2))^{2}.

For the fourth term,

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left[\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{2p}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{1}|^{p}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2p+1}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}-\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p+1}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}=M_{p}(p).

For the remaining terms, note that

\displaystyle\text{Cov}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2},\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}]-\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}].

Let us compute both summands. For the second one,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}]=2\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{1}\right|^{2}]=2\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}.

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}]=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p+1}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}=\frac{1}{p}.

For the first one,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{p+2}+|g_{1}|^{p}|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-2g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}|g_{1}|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{p+2}]+\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}|^{p}]\mathbb{E}[|g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p+3}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}+\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p+1}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}=\frac{4\Gamma\left(\frac{p+3}{p}\right)}{3\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{Cov}[\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2},\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{4\Gamma\left(\frac{p+3}{p}\right)}{3\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}-2\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma\left(\frac{p+3}{p}\right)}{3\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}=\frac{2}{3}M_{p}(p+2).

Thus, we obtain the covariance matrix

\Sigma=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}2M_{p}(4)+2(M_{p}(2))^{2}&\frac{2}{3}M_{p}(p+2)\\
\frac{2}{3}M_{p}(p+2)&M_{p}(p)\\
\end{array}}\right].

For the last step, let us compute the Jacobian of
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle f(x,y)=x^{1/2}\frac{1}{y^{1/p}}.

It is clear that

J_{f}(x,y)=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{y^{1/p}}&\frac{-\sqrt{2}}{p}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{y^{1+1/p}}\\
\end{array}}\right].

Thus, the Jacobian matrix of
f
evaluated at
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=\left(2M_{p}(2),1/p\right)
is

J_{g}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{p^{1/p}}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}}&-\sqrt{2}p^{1/p}\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}\\
\end{array}}\right].

Then, applying elementary computations, one can check that the vaue of
\sigma_{p}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{2}=J_{g}\Sigma J_{g}^{T}=\frac{p^{2/p}\left(M_{p}(4)+(M_{p}(2))^{2}\right)}{4M_{p}(2)}.

∎

Now, let us work on the case where
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
are random vectors uniformly distributed on
B_{p}^{n}
. By the Schechtman-Zinn representation (Lemma
2.1
),

\displaystyle X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}U^{1/n}\frac{G}{\|G\|_{p}}\;\text{ and }\;Y^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}U^{\prime 1/n}\frac{G^{\prime}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}},

where
G=(g_{1},...,g_{n})
and
G^{\prime}=(g^{\prime}_{1},...,g^{\prime}_{n})
are independent
p
-generalized Gaussian vectors on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
U,U^{\prime}
are uniformly distributed in the interval
[0,1]
and independent of
G
and
G^{\prime}
.
Then, similarly to the previous case, we can see the distance between random vectors uniformly distributed on
B_{p}^{n}
, normalized by
n^{1/p-1/2}
, as

\displaystyle n^{1/p-1/2}\|X-Y\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|U^{1/n}\frac{G}{\|G\|_{p}}-U^{\prime 1/n}\frac{G^{\prime}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-\frac{U^{\prime 1/n}}{U^{1/n}}\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2},

where there is an extra
U^{1/n}
factor.
Note that since
U^{1/n},U^{\prime 1/n}\overset{a.s.}{\to}1
and
U^{1/n},U^{\prime 1/n}>0
almost surely, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{U^{\prime 1/n}}{U^{1/n}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}1.

Thus, again we will first prove that
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
and

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}U^{1/n}\left\|\left(g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\right\|_{2},

have the same asymptotic distribution, and then prove a CLT for (
4.2
). This will imply that
\text{dist}(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)})
satisfies the same CLT.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and let
g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime}
with
i=1,...,n
, be independent
p-
generalized Gaussian random variables and
U,U^{\prime}
be independent uniform random variables on the interval
[0,1]
. Then,

\displaystyle n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-\frac{U^{\prime 1/n}}{U^{1/n}}\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

Proof.

The argument is the same as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, replacing

A_{n}=\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}\quad\text{by}\quad A_{n}=\frac{U^{\prime 1/n}}{U^{1/n}}\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}.

In particular, defining
f(x)=\|G-xG^{\prime}\|_{2}
, the same mean value theorem argument yields

\big|\,n^{-1/2}\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}-n^{-1/2}\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\big|\leq|1-A_{n}|\,n^{-1/2}\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}\qquad\text{a.s.}

By the SLLN,
\|G\|_{p}/\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}\to 1
a.s. and
n^{-1/2}\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}\to\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}
a.s.
Moreover,
U^{1/n}\to 1
and
U^{\prime 1/n}\to 1
a.s., hence
A_{n}\to 1
a.s., and therefore
|1-A_{n}|\to 0
a.s..
Consequently, the right-hand side converges to
0
a.s., which implies that

n^{-1/2}\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{-1/2}\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Finally, since
n^{1/p}\|G\|_{p}^{-1}\to M_{p}(p)^{-1/p}>0
a.s., an application of Slutsky’s theorem gives

n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}.

∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(
B_{p}^{n}
-case).

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}\sim\text{Unif}(B_{p}^{n})
be independent. By Lemma
4.2
, it is enough to prove a CLT for (
4.2
).
In particular, notice that

\displaystyle U^{1/n}=e^{\frac{1}{n}\log(U)}\overset{d}{=}e^{-\frac{E}{n}},

where
E
is an exponential random variable with mean
1
. Therefore, using the series expansion of the exponential,

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{E}{n}}=1-\frac{E}{n}+R_{n},

where
R_{n}
denotes the Taylor reminder term, and
|R_{n}|\leq E^{2}/2n^{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{E}{n}}=1-\frac{E}{n}+O_{E}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

where the Landau symbol
O_{E}
depends on the realization of the random variable
E
. Since
E^{2}<\infty
almost surely, the bound above also implies that

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{E}{n}}=1-\frac{E}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

almost surely. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{U^{1/n}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\frac{\left(1-\frac{E}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

This last expression coincides with the case where
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
are uniformly distributed on the boundary of
B_{p}^{n}
. Thus, the Theorem follows.
∎

4.2.
CLT for the cube

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
be independent random vectors uniformly distributed on
B_{\infty}^{n}
. Then
X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}(u_{1},...,u_{n})
and
Y^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}(u^{\prime}_{1},...,u^{\prime}_{n})
where
u_{i},u^{\prime}_{i}
are independent random variables uniformly distributed on the interval
[-1,1]
. Thus, the proof of the CLT for this case is a straightforward computation.

Let us first prove that the case where
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
are uniformly distributed on the cube, is asymptotically distributed as the case where
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
are uniformly distributed on the boundary of the cube.

proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
X^{(n)},Y^{(n)}
be random vectors uniformly distributed on
B_{\infty}^{n}
. Then
X^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}(u_{1},...,u_{n})
and
Y^{(n)}\overset{d}{=}(u^{\prime}_{1},...,u^{\prime}_{n})
where
u_{i},u^{\prime}_{i}
are independent random variables uniformly distributed on the interval
[-1,1]
.

Note that

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(u_{1},...,u_{n})-(u_{1}^{\prime},...,u_{n}^{\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u_{i}-u_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we take

(4.3)

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u_{i}-u_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}.

By the classical CLT, it is clear that
\sqrt{n}\left(S_{n}-\mu\right)\to N(0,\sigma^{2})
, where

\displaystyle\mu:=\mathbb{E}[|u_{1}-u_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}]=\frac{2}{3}

and

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}:=\text{Var}\left[|u_{1}-u_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{1}-u_{1}^{\prime}|^{4}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{1}-u_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}=\frac{2^{4}}{15}-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2}=\frac{28}{45}.

Thus, taking the function
f(x)=\sqrt{x}
, we have that

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=f\left(S_{n}\right).

Applying the Delta method (
2.2
), we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}-f(\mu)\right)\overset{d}{\to}N\left(0,\sigma^{2}(f^{\prime}(\mu))^{2}\right)=N\left(0,\frac{7}{30}\right).

Now, let
\tilde{X}^{(n)},\tilde{Y}^{(n)}
be random vectors uniformly distributed on
\partial B_{\infty}^{n}
. Note that any point on the boundary of the hypercube has one coordinate either
1
or
-1
, and the rest of coordinates are uniform random variables on the interval
[-1,1]
. Therefore, we take
X^{(n)}=(u_{1},...,u_{n})
and
Y^{(n)}=(u^{\prime}_{1},...,u^{\prime}_{n})
as before, and we define
\tilde{X}^{(n)}=(\tilde{u}_{1},...,\tilde{u}_{n})
as

\tilde{u}_{i}=\begin{cases}u_{i},&\text{if }i\neq K_{X}\\
S_{X}&\text{if }i=K_{X}\\
\end{cases}

where
K_{X}\sim\text{Unif}(\{1,...,n\})
and
S_{X}\sim\text{Unif}(\{-1,1\})
, and
\tilde{Y}^{(n)}=(\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{1},...,\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{n})
as

\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{i}=\begin{cases}u^{\prime}_{i},&\text{if }i\neq K_{Y}\\
S_{Y}&\text{if }i=K_{Y}\\
\end{cases}

where
K_{Y}\sim\text{Unif}(\{1,...,n\})
and
S_{Y}\sim\text{Unif}(\{-1,1\})
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we take

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i}|^{2},\text{ and }\tilde{S}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\tilde{u}_{i}-\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{i}|^{2}.

Note that
S_{n}
differs from
\tilde{S_{n}}
in at most two summands, for which

\displaystyle\left|(u_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i})^{2}-(\tilde{u}_{i}-\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{i})^{2}\right|\leq 8.

since
|u_{i}|,|u^{\prime}_{i}|,|\tilde{u}_{i}|,|\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{i}|\leq 1
, for any
i\in\{1,...,n\}
. Then, it follows that

(4.4)

\displaystyle|S_{n}-\tilde{S}_{n}|=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((u_{i}-u^{\prime}_{i})^{2}-(\tilde{u}_{i}-\tilde{u^{\prime}}_{i})^{2}\right)\right|\leq 2\frac{8}{n}=\frac{16}{n}

almost surely. Note that,

\displaystyle\left|n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}-n^{-1/2}\|\tilde{X}^{(n)}-\tilde{Y}^{(n)}\|_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{S_{n}}-\sqrt{\tilde{S}_{n}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|S_{n}-\tilde{S}_{n}\right|}{\sqrt{S_{n}}+\sqrt{\tilde{S}_{n}}}

almost surely, since
S_{n},\tilde{S}_{n}>0
with probability
1
.
Therefore, since
S_{n}\overset{a.s.}{\to}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]>0
and
\tilde{S_{n}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]
, there exists some
c>0
such that
\sqrt{S_{n}}+\sqrt{\tilde{S}_{n}}>c
almost surely, and using (
4.4
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}-n^{-1/2}\|\tilde{X}^{(n)}-\tilde{Y}^{(n)}\|_{2}\right|\leq\frac{16/n}{c}\overset{a.s.}{\to}0

which implies that

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}\overset{d}{\sim}n^{-1/2}\|\tilde{X}^{(n)}-\tilde{Y}^{(n)}\|_{2}.

Therefore,
n^{-1/2}\|\tilde{X}^{(n)}-\tilde{Y}^{(n)}\|_{2}
satisfies the same CLT as
n^{-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}
.
∎

5.
Large Deviation Principles

In this section, we are going to prove the Large Deviation Principles results. We divide the section into two subsections. The first one is devoted to random vectors uniformly distributed on
l_{p}^{n}
-balls
(2\leq p<\infty)
in order to prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, and the second one is devoted to the case of the cube (Theorem
1.5
).

5.1.
LDP for
l_{p}^{n}-
balls,
p\geq 2

As in the previous section, instead of proving that an LDP is satisfied by
n^{1/p-1/2}\|X^{(n)}-Y^{(n)}\|_{2}
, we show that it is satisfied by

\displaystyle W_{n}:=n^{1/p-1/2}\frac{1}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
(g^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are independent
p
-generalized Gaussian random variables.
For that, we need to first prove that these two expressions are exponentially equivalent. In that case, by Theorem
2.4
, both will satisfy the same LDP with speed
n
and the same rate function.

Let us first see that the two expressions are exponentially equivalent.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p\geq 2
, and let
G=(g_{1},...,g_{n}),G^{\prime}=(g_{1}^{\prime},...,g_{n}^{\prime})
be independent generalized
p-
gaussian vectors, and let
U,U^{\prime}
be independent uniform random variables on the interval
[0,1]
, independent of
G
and
G^{\prime}
. Let

\displaystyle A_{n}=\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}\;\text{ or }\;A_{n}=\frac{U^{1/n}}{U^{\prime 1/n}}\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

is exponentially equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}.

Equivalently, for every
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}-\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|>\delta\right]=-\infty.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\|G-xG^{\prime}\|_{2}
. Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}-\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\|_{2}\right|\leq|1-A_{n}|\|G^{\prime}\|_{2}

almost surely.
Now, note that for any
\delta>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}-\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|>\delta\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{n^{1/p-1/2}}{\|G\|_{p}}\left|\left\|G-A_{n}G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}-\left\|G-G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right|>\delta\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{n^{1/p}}{\|G\|_{p}}|1-A_{n}|\frac{\left\|G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}}{n^{1/2}}\right|>\delta\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{n^{1/p}}{\|G\|_{p}}>\alpha\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\left|1-A_{n}\right|>\beta\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\left\|G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}}{n^{1/2}}\geq\gamma\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\|G\|_{p}^{p}}{n}<\frac{1}{\alpha^{p}}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\left|1-A_{n}\right|>\beta\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\left\|G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}\geq\gamma^{2}\right].

where
\alpha=1/M_{p}(p)^{1/p}+\epsilon
,
\gamma=(M_{p}(2)+\epsilon)^{1/2}
and
\beta=\delta/(\alpha\gamma)
. In particular, noticing how
\alpha\gamma
increases with
\epsilon
, we can take
\epsilon>0
big enough such that
\beta\in(0,1/2]
.

Let us bound these three terms. For the first term, by Cramer’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\|G\|_{p}^{p}}{n}\right|<\frac{1}{\alpha^{p}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}<\frac{M_{p}(p)}{\left(1+\epsilon M_{p}(p)^{1/p}\right)^{p}}\right]\leq e^{-c_{1}n}

for some
c_{1}>0
.

For the third term, by Cramer’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\left\|G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}\right|\geq\gamma^{2}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{\left\|G^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}\right|\geq M_{p}(2)+\epsilon\right]\leq e^{-c_{2}n}

for some
c_{2}>0
.

For the second term, we differenciate two cases of
A_{n}
. If
A_{n}=\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}
, we have that for every
\delta>0
, and taking a suitable
\epsilon>0
such that
\beta\in(0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}-1\right|>\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}<1-\beta\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}>1+\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}<(1-\beta)^{p/2}M_{p}(p)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}>(1-\beta)^{-p/2}M_{p}(p)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}>(1+\beta)^{p/2}M_{p}(p)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}<(1+\beta)^{-p/2}M_{p}(p)\right).

By Cramer’s theorem, the four terms decay exponentially with speed
n
.

In the second case, if
A_{n}=\frac{U^{1/n}}{U^{\prime 1/n}}\frac{\|G\|_{p}}{\|G^{\prime}\|_{p}}
, note that for every
\delta>0
, and taking a suitable
\epsilon>0
such that
\beta\in(0,1/2]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{U^{1/n}}{U^{\prime 1/n}}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}-1\right|>\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{U^{1/n}}{U^{\prime 1/n}}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}<1-\beta\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{U^{1/n}}{U^{\prime 1/n}}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}}>1+\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}<(1-\beta)^{p/4}M_{p}(p)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}>(1-\beta)^{-p/4}M_{p}(p)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}>(1+\beta)^{p/4}M_{p}(p)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{p}<(1+\beta)^{-p/4}M_{p}(p)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(U^{1/n}<(1-\beta)^{p/4}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(U^{\prime 1/n}>(1-\beta)^{-p/4}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(U^{1/n}>(1+\beta)^{p/4}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(U^{\prime 1/n}<(1+\beta)^{-p/4}\right).

Applying Cramer’s theorem as before, the first four terms decay exponentially with speed
n
. Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(U^{1/n}<x\right)=e^{-n|\log(x)|},\text{ if }x\in(0,1)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(U^{1/n}>x\right)=0,\text{ if }x\geq 1,

the remaining four terms either decay exponentially with speed
n
or their value is
0
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove both LDP’s.

Proof of Theorem

As before, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recall the random vector

\displaystyle S_{n}=\left(S_{1,n},S_{2,n}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\prime}\right|^{2},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{i}\right|^{p}\right).

Then, for

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\langle t,(|g_{1}-g_{1}^{\prime}|^{2},|g_{1}|^{p})\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{t_{1}|x-y|^{2}+t_{2}|y|^{p}-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-\frac{|y|^{p}}{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dxdy.

Note that if
t_{1}\geq 0
, using the inequality
|x-y|^{2}\leq 2(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{t_{1}|x-y|^{2}+t_{2}|y|^{p}-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-\frac{|y|^{p}}{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2t_{1}|x|^{2}+2t_{1}|y|^{2}+t_{2}|y|^{p}-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}-\frac{|y|^{p}}{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dxdy

\displaystyle=\log\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\left(t_{2}-\frac{1}{p}\right)|y|^{p}}\frac{1}{4p^{2/p}\left(\Gamma(1+1/p)\right)^{2}}dy.

Notice that if
