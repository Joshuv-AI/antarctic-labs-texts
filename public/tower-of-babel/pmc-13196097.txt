A modeling study on the critical sensitivity of epidemic peaks and final size to marginal changes in non-pharmaceutical intervention efficacy
Authors: Jingwei Xuan, Sha He, Sanyi Tang, Yanni Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During the COVID-19 pandemic in China, non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) have been changed from the containment to the dynamic zero-case policy (DZCP) to achieve the goal of zero COVID in approximately 40 days, and then to reopening. Epidemic characteristic metrics are similar for multiple outbreaks but with markedly different peak values, timings and final sizes. In order to quantify the sensitivity of epidemic dynamics to the effectiveness of NPIs, and to guide intervention strategies for future outbreaks the data-driven modeling approach is adapted. In particular, to examine to which process or which parameter is sensitive, we used analytic techniques to identify and analyze major changes in epidemiological indices caused by small changes in characteristic metrics. By comparing basic statistics for 80 outbreaks induced by three different strains, we show that the controlled epidemic trajectories of COVID-19 epidemics depend entirely on the efficacy of NPIs: the infectivity of a strain has little relevance, even for the most infectious strains. Thus, minor changes in the strength of NPIs will lead to huge differences in epidemiological indicators such as outbreak peak value and outbreak clearing time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27349-6.

Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, prevention and control strategies have been changed from the containment in the early stage to the dynamic zero-case policy (DZCP), and then to reopening in China. Note that strict control of more than 136 waves of epidemics induced by different strains achieved zero-COVID at the community level (defined as the absence of unlinked community cases) in about 40 days. Regardless of the epidemics induced by any strain, their characteristic metrics, such as the early exponential growth rate, the late decline rate and the clearing time, are basically the same, but the epidemic indices such as the peak values, the peak times and the final sizes do differ significantly. Shaping the epidemic characters under the DZCP is essential for the control of emerging infectious diseases, but remain unclear, posing questions such as: are the above huge differences in epidemic outcomes caused by differences in virus strain infectivity or in the efficiencies of NPIs? How to determine the reporting lag of each outbreak and what is its impact on the peak value and final size of each outbreak? How did late discovery and delayed peak time affect the DZCP? Our main purpose is to explore if there are universal characteristic metrics that shape the epidemic curves of multiple outbreaks and to quantify impacts of these characteristic metrics on outbreak outcomes.

The efficacy of NPIs against COVID-19 has been extensively studied using various modeling approaches [1, 2]. However, most existing research has focused on countries adopting various mitigation strategies, such as broad-scale lockdowns and gradual relaxation policies, aiming at mitigating the transmission while protecting healthcare capacity [3, 4]. Under such policy contexts, numerous compartment models have been developed to assess the efficacy of NPIs [5–7]. Given DZCP in China multiple small-scale outbreak waves instead of large-scale nationwide outbreaks were observed. Although the existing modeling studies have examined the specific aspects of China’s COVID-19 response, systematic analyses of the efficacy of NPIs and sensitivity across multiple waves under this policy framework remain limited. The present study addresses the gap by using data-driven modeling approach to quantify the efficacy of NPIs and examine the influence of efficacy of NPIs on outbreak trajectories across 136 epidemic waves under China’s DZCP.

Materials and methods

On the basis of the classic transmission dynamics model with close contact tracing, we obtained the iterative relationship on the number of consecutively daily reported cases. Through parameter estimation and cluster analysis, we examined the characteristic metrics of each outbreak, determining the epidemic indices, and analyzed the variation in epidemic indices with intensities and timings of NPIs with aim of revealing the butterfly effect of China’s dynamic zero-COVID policy. In this study, the term ‘butterfly effect’ refers to the phenomenon whereby small perturbations in key parameters can lead to disproportionately large variations in the epidemic outcomes.

Data

We obtain the case data from the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China. The data were released and analyzed anonymously. Matlab (R2021b) and R (3.5.3) were used for the data analysis. Codes are available on GitHub at https://github.com/jwxuan/marginal-changes-critical-sensitivity-covid19.

By 31 August 2022, the original/Alpha strain and the Delta and Omicron mutant strains had caused 136 COVID-19 outbreaks in China, defined as occurrences of cases other than sporadic importations that last at least 10 days with at least 10 cases at the peak (Fig. 1a). We define that an outbreak was ended by the DZCP if the number of newly reported cases remains 0 for three consecutive days [8–12]. The DZCP aims to reduce case numbers to zero in a short period by an integrative/comprehensive package of NPIs [8–13]. Among the 136 outbreaks, 39 were caused by the original/Alpha strain, 19 by the Delta strain and 78 by the Omicron strain (Fig. 1a). In order to determine common characteristic metrics of these multiple outbreaks and to reveal the dynamic relationship between the infectivity of COVID-19 variants and NPIs, we excluded: (1) outbreaks with huge fluctuations such as the Hubei outbreak (Omicron) that started on 27 March 2022 and the Shanghai outbreak that started on 24 February 2022; (2) ongoing (Omicron) outbreaks as of 31 August 2022 (such as the Xinjiang outbreak that started on 30 July 2022, and a Xizang outbreak that started on 8 August 2022); (3) outbreaks with multiple peaks and/or waves without a clearly defined exponential growth phase. So, 80 outbreaks induced by three different strains were included: 24 outbreaks caused by the original/Alpha strain, 14 of the Delta strain and 42 of the Omicron strain (Fig. 1b, c, d).

Model formulation

We employed a classical Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered (SIR)-type epidemiological model with contact tracing developed by Keeling & Rohani [17]. We divided the population into the following groups of individuals: susceptible (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S$$\end{document}), infected (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I$$\end{document}), quarantined susceptible (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{q}$$\end{document}), quarantined infected (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{q}$$\end{document}) and recovered/confirmed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R$$\end{document}) [18–20]. Model fitting

We use the least squares method to fit model (5) (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{y}={a}_{1}t+{b}_{1}$$\end{document}) and model (6) (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{y}={a}_{2}t+{b}_{2}$$\end{document}) to the growth and decline phases of the 80 outbreaks; see Fig. 2 for four examples; all others are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. The estimations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1},{a}_{2},{b}_{1},{b}_{2}$$\end{document} are listed in EXCEL file of the Supplementary Information. We then estimated the aforementioned key metric indices (see EXCEL file in the Supplementary Information). We then validated our fitting results by projecting the durations of each outbreak, the results of which compare well with the actual durations (Supplementary Figure S2). Compared to classical models, our approach achieves fitting accuracy comparable to the SEIR model while requiring fewer estimated and predefined parameters (see Supplementary Table S1). By utilizing linear fitting techniques, our model achieves significantly faster computational speeds without compromising performance.

We defined the control reproduction numbers (CRN, or control efficacy number) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{c}^{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{c}^{2}\:$$\end{document}for the exponential growth and decline phases. Based on this we can get the relationships, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{a}_{1}=\gamma}_{1}({R}_{c}^{1}-1)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{a}_{2}={\upgamma}}_{2}\left({R}_{c}^{2}-1\right)$$\end{document}, and consequently we need the “actual” transmission period of infected cases (the average time from infectiousness to isolation) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{1/\gamma\:}_{1}\:\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}\:{1/\gamma\:}_{2}$$\end{document} when the DZCP was implemented to shorten these actual transmission periods by mass testing followed by rapid close-contact tracing. We allowed the period to vary among 6, 5 and 4 days for the exponential growth phase, and 3, 2 and 1 days during the exponential decline phase. Under the strongest NPIs implemented in DZCP, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{2}$$\end{document} was cut to one day (confirmed by the negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{2}$$\end{document} and positivity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{c}^{2}$$\end{document} for each outbreak, see the EXCEL file in the Supplementary Information). Accordingly, three key parameters govern the model dynamics: the control reproduction number (CRN), the recovery rate, and the change point of the CRN. To quantify these parameters, we employed Bayesian estimation and polynomial fitting to estimate the change points, exponential growth rates, and exponential decline rates from the reported case data.

Results

Characteristic metrics of outbreaks

There was a consistent geometric pattern for all the 80 outbreaks regardless of whether they were caused by the Alpha, Delta, or Omicron variants. Each outbreak consisted of an exponential growth phase followed by an exponential decline phase with a relatively constant exponential growth/decline rate, see the triangle patterns, termed “standard outbreak curves” (SOCs), in the Shaanxi, Sichuan, and Gansu outbreaks and the trapezoid patterns in the Jilin outbreak (Fig. 2).

A statistical analysis of the key indices in these geometric patterns are shown in Fig. 3 and Table 1. The means of the exponential growth rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1}$$\end{document}) of outbreaks caused by the original (Alpha)/Delta/Omicron strains were 0.41, 0.46 and 0.45, and the mean exponential decline rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{2}$$\end{document}) were − 0.21, -0.3 and − 0.29, respectively. The SOCs caused by the Delta strain not only had the fastest growth rates in the exponential growth phase, but also the fastest decline rates in the exponential decline phase. The means of the reporting delays were 4.7, 3.1 and 4.1 days, respectively. Similarly, on average, the exponential growth periods (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{1}$$\end{document}) of outbreaks induced by the original/Alpha strain were longer (9.1 days) than those induced by the other two strains (7.4 and 8.8, respectively). The average plateau periods (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{2}$$\end{document}) were 2.8, 2.9 and 8.8 days, and the average durations (T) were 32, 24 and 34 days, respectively. Comparing all these metric indices for the SOCs shown in Fig. 3 and Table 1, we conclude that the 14 outbreaks induced by the Delta strain were the most successfully controlled ones under the China’s DZCP. During the period dominated by the Delta variant in China, significant experience gained from the control of Original(Alpha)-induced outbreaks and public health sectors at all levels helps us to effectively implement the policy of “early detection, early report, early quarantine, and early treatment” [8, 21, 22], resulting in shorter reporting delays. Despite the significant variation of infectivity of the three different strains, the characteristic metrics of all outbreaks remained similar. These almost identical characteristic metrics for all outbreaks strongly support the conclusion that the characteristic metrics measure the efficiency of prevention and control and are predominately determined by the strength of NPI strategies, not the strain-specific infectivity.

Further comparisons, between the characteristic metrics between the Omicron-outbreaks and Delta-outbreaks, illustrate that the fluctuations of exponential growth rate (a1), reporting delay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{(t}_{d})$$\end{document}, exponential growth duration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{(t}_{1})$$\end{document}, plateau period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{(t}_{2})$$\end{document} and epidemic duration (T) during Omicron-outbreaks were greater than those during Delta-outbreaks. In particular, the outbreaks induced by the Omicron strain had longer exponential growth durations, longer plateau periods and longer dynamic zeroing times (81 days) (Fig. 3d-f). This confirms that the more infective the virus is, the more difficult to control the outbreaks and the less precise the control measures [6, 23–25]. The difficulty in achieving precision in implementing NPIs and achieving the DZCP goal increases with the infectivity of the strain. Therefore, minor changes in NPI intensity, even when each outbreak’s epidemic curve has similar characteristic metrics, will lead to significant differences in the epidemiological indicators. This butterfly effect, by which small changes in the initial conditions and NPIs can lead to large scale and unpredictable outbreak outcomes, will be further addressed below.

Figure 3(g-i) summarizes our correlation analyses of six epidemic indices for three different strains, revealing that the exponential growth rates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({a}_{1}$$\end{document}) and the durations of the exponential growth phases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({t}_{1}\right)$$\end{document} for all of the three strains were significantly negatively correlated; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{2}$$\end{document} (the exponential decline rate) for the original(Alpha) strain were significantly negatively correlated; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1}$$\end{document} for the Delta and Omicron strains were significantly negatively correlated with the duration of each outbreak (T). Therefore, with a higher exponential growth rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1}$$\end{document}, the duration of the exponential growth (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{1}$$\end{document}) and the outbreak (T) was shortened. This is associated with the increasing speed and scale of mass testing, the first principle of the DZCP, resulting in early detection and shortened outbreak duration.

Note also that the duration of the exponential growth phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{1}$$\end{document}) is significantly positively correlated to the total outbreak duration (T) for all outbreaks and for all different strains. Note also that the plateau period (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{2}$$\end{document}) and T were significantly and positively correlated only for outbreaks when Omicron was the circulating strain, suggesting that a quick response to the initial exponential growth is essential to shorten the overall duration for a strain with high transmissibility. The duration of the exponential growth phase for Omicron stains was also significantly and positively correlated to the peak and final size. Although DZCP can be used to achieve the intended goal of controlling cases down to zero, the high transmissibility of a strain could enhance the butterfly effects, making the intended goal much more challenging and with higher costs [26–32] .

In the average sense, the above three characteristic indicators have similar effects on the key epidemiological indicators.

Discussion

During the almost three years of the COVID-19 pandemic, China’s response has been successful in controlling the more than 136 outbreaks by adapting the DZCP. Shaping the epidemic characters under the DZCP is essential for the control of emerging infectious diseases. Data from the 80 outbreaks with SOCs that we have analyzed revealed common characteristic metrics and showed their tremendous impact on epidemiological indices. Key conclusions revealed by analyzing the effects of NPIs and DZCP are that the durations, peak values and final sizes of outbreaks in China mainly depended on the rate and duration of the initial exponential growth, which reflects the efficiency of NPIs instead of the transmissibility of different strains. The exponential decrease rate will affect the duration of an outbreak, while it has little effect on the peak and final size.

In conclusion, without the DZCP, there would have been no remarkable prevention and control situation of 363 days of zero newly reported cases within three years in China [10–25], which provides the invaluable time guarantee for the protection of people’s lives and health and the dynamic adjustment of prevention and control strategies. If the DZCP had not been implemented so resolutely and decisively, even if its implementation failed to cut off the exponential growth rapidly, such that each outbreak continued to spread for an additional week, then the total number of reported cases in the 80 outbreaks would have increased by more than 100 times [18, 33, 35]. If the 136 outbreaks before 31 August 2022 are considered together, the total number of reported cases would continue to expand to a surprising value when two of the three key geometric indicators \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({a}_{1}$$\end{document} and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{t}_{1}$$\end{document}) increase and the third (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{2})$$\end{document} decrease simultaneously. Therefore, for China, a populous country, the consequences of COVID-19 would have been catastrophic if it had not adopted strong NPIs and implemented a DZCP in the early days.

The analyses of 80 outbreaks induced by different strains revealed that effective implementation of the DZCP requires rapid identification of infected individuals within one or two days since their exposure, and controlling the CRN at the exponential decline stage to a low level between 0.5 and 0.7. This was feasible thanks to decisive NPI measures at the cost of substantial reduction of social economic activities. The relaxation of some of these NPI measures since the beginning of September of 2022, such as shortening the quarantine duration for close contacts and inbound travelers by two days, has resulted in increasing frequency of outbreaks and increasing number of infections at the outbreak time. This is due to the butterfly effect we reported here, and it indicates the substantial challenge in maintaining the DZCP and calls for reconsideration of the concept and practice of dynamic zero given its past success and the current/future challenge to reopen the social and economic activities by increasing the immunity of the population through enhanced vaccine utilization.

This study has several limitations. First, our model assumes infection scale remains small, approximating the susceptible population as the total population size. While reasonable for the small outbreaks under China’s DZCP, this assumption limits the model’s applicability to large-scale epidemics where susceptible depletion becomes significant. Second, we used changes in the reproduction number to capture the impact of NPIs, which may integrate multiple factors, including transmission dynamics, behavioral/psychological changes and etc. However, it cannot tease apart what each factor contributed in each wave. Third, vaccination is not explicitly modeled. Recent studies show that NPIs and vaccination exhibit dynamic complementarity, with their combined effect exceeding individual contributions, and that NPIs can compensate for vaccine delays by suppressing early transmission [36–38]. Future work will extend our framework to incorporate vaccination, considering viral evolution, immune escape, and behavioral feedback, to identify optimal integrated strategies for diverse “Disease X” scenarios.
