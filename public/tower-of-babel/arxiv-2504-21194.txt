Geolocating Earth Imagery from ISS: Integrating Machine Learning with Astronaut Photography for Enhanced Geographic Mapping
Vedika Srivastava ID, Hemant Kumar Singh ID, and Jaisal Singh

Abstract
This paper presents a novel approach to geolocating images captured from the International Space Station (ISS) using advanced machine learning algorithms. Despite having precise ISS coordinates, the specific Earth locations depicted in astronaut-taken photographs often remain unidentified. Our research addresses this gap by employing three distinct image processing pipelines: a Neural Network based approach, a SIFT based method, and GPT-4 model. Each pipeline is tailored to process high-resolution ISS imagery, identifying both natural and man-made geographical features. Through extensive evaluation on a diverse dataset of over 140 ISS images, our methods demonstrate significant promise in automated geolocation with varied levels of success. The NN approach showed a high success rate in accurately matching geographical features, while the SIFT pipeline excelled in processing zoomed-in images. GPT-4 model provided enriched geographical descriptions alongside location predictions. This research contributes to the fields of remote sensing and Earth observation by enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of geolocating space-based imagery, thereby aiding environmental monitoring and global mapping efforts.



I
Introduction

IN the era of advanced space exploration and Earth observation, high-resolution imagery captured from the International Space Station (ISS) presents a unique opportunity for geographical and environmental studies. However, a significant challenge lies in pinpointing the exact Earth locations depicted in these images. Accurate geolocation of ISS imagery is pivotal for a range of applications, from climate monitoring to disaster response. Current methodologies often struggle with the dynamic and complex nature of space-based imagery due to atmospheric interference and angle variations. Our approach directly addresses these challenges by integrating multiple robust machine learning models. This paper introduces an innovative approach to address this challenge by harnessing advance machine learning techniques.

The primary objective of our research is to develop and evaluate algorithms capable of automatically identifying the geographical locations captured in astronaut-taken photographs from the ISS. Despite the availability of precise ISS orbital data, the specific terrestrial regions in these images often remain uncharted due to the lack of direct georeferencing. To tackle this, we implement three distinct image processing pipelines: a Neural Network (NN) based method utilizing the VGG16 model, a Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) based approach, and TerraByte, an advanced AI system integrating GPT-4 with vision capabilities.

Our NN-based approach exploits the robust feature extraction capabilities of the VGG16 model, followed by a cross-correlation technique for geolocation prediction. The SIFT methodology, renowned for its effectiveness in feature detection and matching, is adapted for the unique challenges presented by ISS imagery. Finally, TerraByte leverages the extensive knowledge base and image recognition prowess of GPT-4, providing detailed geographical insights alongside geolocation predictions.

The contribution of this paper lies in its comprehensive approach to solving a complex problem in the field of remote sensing and Earth observation. By exploring and evaluating different methodologies, we aim to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of geolocating space-based imagery, thereby enriching the data’s applicability for scientific and environmental purposes. Our findings have implications not only for the enhancement of geographical data accuracy but also for the broader scope of environmental monitoring and global mapping.

III-A

Pipeline 1: Neural Network-Based Geolocation (NN-Geo)

The NN-Geo pipeline manifests a novel convergence of neural networks with geospatial analysis, tailored to geolocate images captured by the International Space Station (ISS). At its core, the pipeline integrates the VGG16 neural network, a deep convolutional model pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, repurposed here for extracting detailed features from ISS imagery. These features form the cornerstone of the geolocation process, which is further enhanced by the pipeline’s innovative application of geospatial data extraction and transformation techniques.

Upon the acquisition of a query image, the NN-Geo pipeline initiates the metadata extraction process. Utilizing the EXIF data embedded within the image, critical information such as the camera model, focal length, and GPS coordinates are retrieved. The pipeline utilizes a zoom level of 9.5 for fetching Area of Interest (AOI) images using the Mapbox API. This zoom level is strategically chosen to provide a balance between detail and coverage area, enabling the effective identification and analysis of features within the query image against a sufficiently detailed yet manageable AOI.

III-B

Pipeline 2: Scale-Invariant Feature Transform-Based Matching (SIFT-Match)

SIFT-Match revolves around the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) algorithm, a well-established technique in computer vision for its exceptional capability in feature detection and matching. The choice of SIFT is strategic; its invariance to image scale, rotation, and illumination changes aligns perfectly with the unique challenges posed by ISS imagery, which often encompasses varying angles and distances. The pipeline employs a sliding window technique, where the SIFT algorithm matches features of the ISS image against a set of subsampled images derived from high-resolution Area of Interest (AOI) images. These AOI images, spanning a large geographic area, are generated using an image stitching method that aggregates multiple Google Maps tiles.

Metadata Extraction: Critical to the pipeline’s initial stage is the extraction of metadata from ISS images’ EXIF data, encompassing GPS coordinates, altitude, camera model, and focal length. These parameters are essential for accurately determining the ISS’s location during image capture and estimating the photographed ground area’s extent.

AOI Image Generation: Central to the pipeline is the creation of a high-resolution Area of Interest (AOI) image, spanning 1200km x 1200km and centered on the ISS’s coordinates at the time of capture. This 30,000 x 30,000-pixel image is constructed by stitching together multiple Google Maps tiles, ensuring comprehensive geographical coverage for subsequent matching processes.
[We used open source git repo for this: ”https://github.com/alduinien/GoogleMapDownloader”]

Matching Process: The pipeline generates subsampled images from the AOI, tailored to correlate with the ISS image’s visible area. Employing the SIFT algorithm, it performs a detailed comparison for each subsample against the query image, selecting the subsample with the highest matching score as the best geographical match. This process ensures the identification of at least one subsample accurately representing the query image’s featured location.

Enhancements for Accuracy and Efficiency: The pipeline incorporates a preclusion criterion to exclude predominantly aquatic images without significant landmass, aligning the process with the dataset’s characteristics. Further, it stores the GPS coordinates of the center pixels of the top two best-matched subsample images, enhancing the accuracy of location identification. This nuanced approach addresses cases where the second-best match more accurately represents the query image’s location. One identified scenario is when our query image was manhattan image , and in the subsampled image the best match was a similar outline of manhattan but it was in desert, here the second best match was manhattan. An illustrative example of this approach’s efficacy is seen with a Manhattan query image, where the pipeline’s nuanced method corrected an initial misidentification of a desert outline similar to Manhattan city, by accurately recognizing the true Manhattan location as the second-best match, thereby ensuring precise geolocation.

Data Management and Validation: By utilizing a CSV file format for result management, the pipeline enhances data accessibility and manipulation, especially in varied computing environments such as Docker containers. Validation is conducted using images with known coordinates to verify the accuracy of predicted locations against actual labeled locations.

The SIFT-Match pipeline, through its meticulous metadata extraction, AOI image generation, and refined matching process, substantially advances the field of remote sensing and Earth observation. By addressing the unique challenges of ISS imagery and incorporating strategic enhancements, this pipeline achieves high precision in geolocating Earth imagery, contributing significantly to the enhancement of geospatial analysis and mapping capabilities.

III-C

Pipeline 3: TerraByte - AI-Enhanced Geospatial Analysis

TerraByte stands at the forefront of AI-enhanced geospatial analysis, integrating the sophisticated capabilities of the GPT-4 model with enhanced vision functionalities. This state-of-the-art pipeline is designed to analyze ISS images using GPT-4’s advanced natural language processing and image recognition features, enabling it not only to pinpoint geolocations but also to interpret and elucidate the content within these images. TerraByte’s dual functionality facilitates a comprehensive understanding of both natural and man-made features, drawing from a vast knowledge base for complex reasoning and analysis of space imagery’s abstract patterns and visuals. Its ability to offer detailed descriptions of observed Earth features enhances its utility in applications requiring both precise geolocation and contextual insight. However, TerraByte’s dependency on the subscription-based GPT-4 model might limit its accessibility, posing a challenge in its widespread deployment, particularly in scenarios where the ISS’s location significantly differs from the imaged area.

Collectively, these pipelines embody a concerted effort to push the boundaries of current geolocation technologies. NN-Geo’s fusion of CNNs with cross-correlation, SIFT-Match’s precision in feature-based matching, and TerraByte’s AI-driven contextual analysis illustrate a comprehensive approach to deciphering the complexities of Earth’s landscapes as captured from space. The development and refinement of these pipelines contribute significantly to the fields of remote sensing and geographic information systems, offering promising tools for enhancing global environmental monitoring and mapping. V
Conclusion

In this paper, we explored three distinct geolocation pipelines, each employing advanced technologies to address the complex challenge of pinpointing the Earth locations depicted in images captured by the International Space Station (ISS). As summarized in Table 1, while each pipeline has its strengths, they also present unique challenges that future research will aim to address. The Neural Network-based approach (NN), utilizing the VGG16 model, adeptly analyzed complex features in ISS imagery, highlighting deep learning’s potential in space imagery analysis. The Scale-Invariant Feature Transform-based method (SIFT) excelled in detailed feature matching, especially effective in well-defined terrestrial regions. In contrast, TerraByte, leveraging GPT-4’s vision capabilities, set a new benchmark by not only geolocating ISS images but also enriching the analysis with comprehensive contextual insights. These pipelines collectively signify a substantial advancement in remote sensing and geographic information systems, addressing unique ISS photography challenges like image scale, orientation, and atmospheric conditions, and thereby enhancing global environmental monitoring and mapping capabilities.

However, our research also shed light on limitations such as computational intensity and the dependency on subscription-based models, underscoring areas for future improvement. As we refine these technologies, we anticipate a more profound impact on our capacity to monitor and understand Earth from space. The development and enhancement of the NN, SIFT, and TerraByte pipelines thus represent a crucial step forward in interpreting the wealth of data from the ISS, not only augmenting our current Earth observation capabilities but also laying a foundation for future innovations in the field. This work contributes significantly to our ongoing efforts to comprehend and preserve our planet, showcasing the potential of advanced technology in unveiling the mysteries of Earth as seen from the cosmos.

Future research will explore the integration of real-time processing capabilities and the adaptation of these algorithms for other satellite platforms, potentially broadening the scope of our geolocation techniques.

CasMTR Experimentation

The CasMTR (Cascade Transformer) approach was also explored for its potential in capturing spatially informative keypoints. Despite its promise, this method faced significant computational resource allocation challenges, primarily due to its heavy GPU requirements. The key issues were:

1.

Infeasibility in a resource-constrained environment, limiting its practical applicability for large-scale ISS image analysis.

2.

Challenges in training the model effectively, owing to the limited availability and diversity of training data representative of ISS imagery.

A-C

Trial with Spherical Transform for Geo-Localization

An attempt was made to employ a differentiable spherical transform for aligning ISS images with Earth-based references. This method, although innovative, did not perform as expected due to:

1.

Complexities involved in accurately representing the curved surface of the Earth in ISS images.

2.

Issues with aligning images that have vastly different perspectives and scales, a common characteristic of ISS photography.

A-D

Use of Simulated Imagery for Reference Generation

We experimented with generating simulated reference images using virtual Earth simulators, like Cesium. However, this approach faced challenges in rendering speed and realism, particularly:

1.

Slow rendering speeds, making it impractical for real-time or large-scale processing.

2.

Difficulty in achieving a high level of realism necessary to match the quality and detail of ISS images.

A-E

Implementation of Automated Pattern Recognition Techniques

Efforts were also directed toward automated pattern recognition techniques for identifying geographical features in ISS images. The limitations encountered in this area were:

1.

Inconsistent recognition accuracy, particularly in images with cloud cover or lacking distinct geographical markers.

2.

Challenges in differentiating between natural and man-made structures from the ISS perspective.

In summary, while these methods provided valuable learning experiences and contributed to the overall understanding of the complexities involved in ISS image geolocation, they did not meet the criteria for effective and efficient application in this context. The documentation of these experimentations serves as a crucial part of the research process, offering a comprehensive view of the methodologies considered and providing a foundation for future studies to build upon.
