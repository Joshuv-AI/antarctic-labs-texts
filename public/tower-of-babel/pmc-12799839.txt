METTL4 regulates synaptic UCP2 N6-adenosine methylation to mediate pain hypersensitivity in female mice
Authors: Yanqiong Wu, Yifan Luo, Qin Xiao, Xueqin Xu, Wenjiao Jin, Longhui Li, Cheng Liu, Zhigang He, Zhixiao Li, Juan Li, Xuesong Yang, Fan Jiang, Zeyong Yang, Daqing Ma, Changbin Ke, Hongbing Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpitranscriptomics modifications play an important role in sex-dependent biological phenomena. N6-adenosine methylation (m6A), the most prevalent epitranscriptomics modification in eukaryotic mRNA, participates in regulating various sex-specific physiological processes. Here, we generated METTL4 knockout mice lacking methyltransferase-like 4, which mediates m6A. Behavioral analyses revealed that only female METTL4−/− mice exhibited pain hypersensitivity, with subsequent experiments showing the involvement of METTL4-mediated m6A in this sex-differentiated biological phenotype. Further exploration demonstrated that this sex-specific pain hypersensitivity is closely associated with sex-dependent expression of uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) in synapses. Specifically, elevated UCP2 expression in METTL4−/− female mice enhances the efficiency of synaptic transmission by modulating mitochondrial energy metabolism at synapses. Collectively, this study identifies a distinct pathway mediated by METTL4-driven m6A modification, providing critical insights into the molecular basis of sex-specific differences in pain transmission. These findings also highlight the potential of targeting METTL4 for sex-differentiated pain management strategies in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06010-2.Keywords: METTL4, Sexual dimorphism, M6A, Pain hypersensitivity, UCP2, Mitochondrial metabolism, Synaptic transmission

Introduction

Men and women respond differently to pain [1, 2]; clinically, women reportedly show higher pain sensitivity [3, 4] and are more prone to chronic pain disorders, such as neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia syndrome, and migraine [5, 6]. Sexual dimorphism is increasingly recognized as a key factor in pain, with both quantitative and qualitative differences effectively linked to genetic factors, neuroimmune, and neural circuit mechanisms [6]. Epitranscriptomics plays a well-established role in different pain conditions [7, 8] by regulating gene expression through methylation, histone modification, chromosome remodeling, and non-coding RNAs, which influence transcription, translation, or downstream function. However, the mechanisms by which epitranscriptomics contribute to sexual dimorphism in chronic pain remain unclear.

N6-adenosine methylation (m6A) is the most prevalent modification of the epitranscriptome, found on at least 25% of vertebrate mRNAs [9]. This modification broadly affects mRNA processing, including regulation of splicing, translation, degradation, and stability. As a reversible epitranscriptomic modification, m6A is dynamically regulated by methyltransferases (writers), including methyltransferase-like (METTL)3, METTL4, METTL14, METTL16 [10, 11], Wilms tumor 1 associated protein (WTAP) and RBM15 [12], and can be removed by m6A demethylases (erasers) comprising alkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) and obesity-associated protein (FTO) [13, 14]. A third class of regulatory proteins, namely m6A readers, recognize and selectively bind to m6A sites to affect RNA stability or function, and mainly include YTH domain-containing family proteins (YTHDF)1/2/3 [15–17]. The m6A modification is enriched in the nervous system [18], with recent evidence suggesting that m6A on mRNA affects neuronal activity and synaptic transmission efficiency by regulating the translation of local synaptic proteins [19], and participates in the formation of inflammatory and neuropathological pain [20, 21] Additionally, m6A modulates neuronal functions and sex determination in Drosophila [19, 22], and the transcriptional regulation of m6A mediates sex-dimorphic metabolic traits [23]. Although several studies have shown that m6A is critical in a variety of sex-dependent biological processes [19, 24], its biological functions and the mechanistic links among the m6A writers, readers, and targets in the sexual dimorphism of chronic pain have not been directly investigated.

METTL4 shares homology with the catalytic MT-70 A subunit of the MT-A70 methyltransferase family, but diverges from the METTL3 and METTL14 subclades [25]. Studies have shown that METTL4 mediates m6A of mRNA [26, 27], small nuclear RNA (snRNA) [28], and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [29] in various eukaryotic organisms. In Drosophila, METTL4 mediates m6A of U2 snRNA in a sex-dependent manner [30]. In the present study, we generated a full knockout of METTL4 in mice. Behavioral analysis indicated that the METTL4 knockout resulted in heightened sensitivity of pain transmission, but only in female mice. Further experiments clarified that METTL4 mediated the m6A modification involved in the sex-differentiated biological phenotype. Finally, we demonstrated that sex-differentiated pain hypersensitivity was closely related to the sex-dependent expression of uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) in the synapse, which in turn enhanced the efficiency of synaptic transmission in female mice by regulating local mitochondrial energy metabolism. These data identify a new pathway through which METTL4 mediates the m6A modification, establish a molecular basis for the existence of sex-specific differences in pain development, and suggest a potential target for sex-differentiated clinical pain management.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experimental procedures and protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hubei University of Medicine. METTL4−/− mice were supplied from Biocytogen Inc. (www.biocytogen.comcyagen.cn) and C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice were provided by the Animal Center of Hubei University of Medicine. Pups were housed with their dams until weaning at age 21 days, then grouped by sex, at four to five mice per cage. Genomic DNA was extracted from tail tip tissue to determine the genotype of each mouse. Animals were housed at 24 ± 0.5 °C, with 35% ± 10% relative humidity, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were used for the following experiments at 10–15 g body weight and age 7–8 weeks. The experimental timeline is presented in Figure S1.

Behavioral testing

Mice were placed within the test environment to habituate for approximately 3 days, after which mechanical sensitivity of the hind paw was evaluated using von Frey testing with an automated dynamic plantar esthesiometer (Ugo Basile, Stoelting, IL, USA). Prior to testing, mice were acclimated for 1 h in an acrylic enclosure on an elevated wire grid. A straight metal filament (0.5 mm diameter) was oriented beneath the hind paw pad and upward force was applied until it touched the plantar surface. The paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was defined as three or more positive responses over five trials, spaced 5 min apart, starting with lower force filaments.

Microinjection of adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors into the spinal cord

Microinjections of AAV vectors into the spinal cord were performed in a manner similar to that in our previous report [31]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the target lumbar vertebra (L4–L5) was identified and cut to expose the spinal cord. A microsyringe was used to inject AAV into the spinal cord parenchyma with a target injection capacity of 300 nL and a flow rate of 100 nL/min. Post injection, the needle was left in place for another 10 min before it was removed. The surgical incision was sutured and iodine disinfectant was applied.

Cell cultures

Primary spinal cord cultures were plated and cultivated in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The 3-to 4-day-old mice were anesthetized and decapitated, laminectomy was performed, and the L4-L6 segment of the dorsal spinal cord was removed. Tissues were enzymatically digested with trypsin/DNase I, centrifuged, and filtered to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were plated directly on plastic culture dishes and grown in an incubator in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) for 2 h, then cultured in Neurobasal (Gibco). Neurons were treated with a gene-specific interfering RNA (RNAi) lentivirus (RNAi-LV) or control lentivirus (Con-LV) vector on day 5 and examined on day 10.

Methylated RNA Immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) sequencing (MeRIP-seq)

After behavioral testing on day 14, mice were sacrificed under 3% isoflurane anesthesia to harvest the L4–L6 spinal cord segment. Total RNA extracted from spinal cord was treated with RNA Fragmentation Reagents (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), yielding fragments of approximately 100 nucleotides, and precipitated with ethanol. Magnetic beads conjugated with Protein A/G (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) were washed with immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (0.5% NP40,10 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl) and then incubated with anti-m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, San Jose, CA, USA) at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were resuspended in IP buffer, fragmented RNA was added, and the mixture was rotated at 4 °C for 4 h. The supernatant containing m6A-enriched RNA was eluted, collected, and purified. Reverse transcription and library construction of both IP and input samples were performed using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-seq Kit v2-Pico Input Mammalian (TAKARA, Osaka, JP) following the manufacturer’s user manual. AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) were used for fragment size selection prior to sequencing and differential gene expression analysis.

Real time-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) of MeRIP samples

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen). Following RNase treatment, RNA was fragmented for 3 min in fragmentation buffer at 94 °C. MeRIP was performed as described above, and RT-qPCR was used to measure the relative abundance of mRNAs selected using anti-m6A antibodies.

RT-qPCR of spinal cord tissues and cultured cells

To detect changes in gene expression levels, total RNA was extracted from L4–L6 spinal cord tissue or cultured cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Invitrogen). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA to perform qRT-PCR assays using Gapdh as an internal reference. All reactions were performed in triplicate. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2−△△CT method.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized and fixed by intracardiac perfusion in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The L4–L6 spinal cord was immediately removed, post-fixed in PFA for 1 h at 4 °C, immersed for 48 h in 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C, and sectioned into 30-µm transverse frozen slices using a CM 1900 freezing microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Cultured cells were fixed in 4% PFA at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, spinal cord sections or cells were incubated with blocking solution (0.3% Triton X-100 and 3% normal goat serum in phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]) for 10 min at room temperature (22℃~25℃) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. For immunofluorescence detection, the sections were washed three times (10 min each) and then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies, diluted in antibody solution (0.1% Tween 20 and 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were scanned using an SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Germany), and images were analyzed with ImageJ software to quantify puncta.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from L4–L6 spinal cord tissues by homogenization in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Wuhan Kerui Biotechnology Co., Wuhan, CHN). Samples were heated for 10 min at 95 °C. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), which were saturated in blocking solution (5% non-fat dry milk and 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After a 60-min incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature, the PVDF membranes were washed in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. Chemiluminescent signals were detected with the ECL Plus kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, CHN). Grayscale values of the signals were quantified using Image Lab software and normalized to housekeeping genes.

Electrophysiology

Spinal cords were removed from mice and embedded in low-melting agarose to obtain 300-µm segments. L4–L6 segments were then incubated in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 10 D-glucose; pH 7.3, 300 mOsm. The solution was saturated with 95% O2/5% CO2 and maintained at ∼32 °C for 15 min. After a 60-min recovery period at room temperature, an individual slice was transferred to a submerged recording chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF (3.0 mL/min). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed at room temperature. Glass recording pipettes (resistance: 5–8 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 135 K-gluconate, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, 5 MgATP, and 0.3 Na2-GTP; pH 7.3, osmolarity 290 mOsm. Recordings were performed using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Foster City, CA, USA). All data were filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz with a Digidata 1550 A and Clampex 10.5 software (Molecular Devices).

To elicit action potentials, increasing amounts of depolarizing currents (0 pA to 220 pA with 20-pA increments) were used. Parameters from the first evoked action potential were used for analysis. Evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) were recorded by holding the membrane potential at − 70 mV and 0 mV, respectively.

Calcium imaging of spinal cord segments

Mice were perfused with 20 mL precooled PBS through the left ventricle, and spinal cords were rapidly isolated, placed in separation solution, and ventilated with a 95% O2/5% CO2 mixture. Spinal cords microinjected with an AAV expressing the calcium fluorescence indicator GCaMP6s (BrainVTA, Wuhan, CHN), AAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s, were embedded in agarose, mounted on a Leica VT1000 S microtome knife stage (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and sectioned into 300-µm thick slices. The specimens were then placed in separation solution at 35 °C for 15 min to recover. L4–L6 spinal cord segments were then placed in ACSF (126 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 11 mM glucose, 26 mM NaHCO3; pH 7.4, 320 mOsm). Calcium imaging of dorsal horn neurons was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 laser confocal microscope in time-lapse mode (512 × 512 pixels) at 30 frames per s for a duration of 500 s per imaging block.

Transmission electron microscopy and image analysis

Injured spinal cord tissue samples (1 mm3) were selected for transmission electron microscopy imaging. Briefly, thin Sections (40 nm) were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and then examined using a FEI Tecnai G212 transmission electron microscope (FEI Company, Eindhoven, the Netherlands). Digital electron micrographs were recorded with a Mega View charge-coupled device using iTEM-SIS software (FEI Company). Synapse density was estimated using the dissector method by comparing 10–15 paired electron micrographs per sample to create reference planes. Micrographs from the same exact regions were collected from adjacent sections to create look-up planes. Synapses visible in the reference plane but absent in the corresponding look-up plane were counted to quantify synapses within the intersectional volume defined by the reference plane, the look-up plane, and the distance between them.

Mitochondrial function evaluation

ATP and reactive oxygen species (ROS) concentrations in synapses isolated from mouse spinal cord tissues were determined using ATP and ROS assay kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, the contents of each well were thoroughly mixed and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C protected from light. The plate was read with a fluorescence microplate reader equipped for excitation in the 530–570 nm range and for emission in the 590–600 nm range. Each sample and the standards were assayed in duplicate or triplicate. To examine the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of mitochondria in synapses, mice were anesthetized, perfused with ice-cold PBS, and spinal cords were collected. The spinal cords were sectioned using a Vibration microtome (Leica), fixed, and stored at 4 °C prior to staining with a JC-1 kit (Beyotime). Fluorescence was examined under confocal microscopy, and MMP was determined by calculating the ratio of red to green fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple comparisons among groups. The normal distribution of the variables was determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was calculated using the Student’s t-test. All data analysis was performed using SPSS ver. 20.0, and a p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

METTL4 knockout induces pain hypersensitivity in female mice

METTL4 exhibits obvious sex differences in epitranscriptomic regulation [30] through the m6A modification on RNA molecules [26, 27]. To investigate whether METTL4 also contributes to sex differences in pain formation, we used the CRISPR/CAS9 system to construct METTL4 knockout mice. To generate mice with a complete METTL4 knockout, we designed two CRISPR/CAS9 cut sites flanking exons 1 and 9 (Fig. 1A), resulting in a ~ 24-kb deletion encompassing all nine exons. The target and guide-RNA sequences are shown on the right in Fig. 1B.

METTL4 protein expression in the spinal cord was examined using immunofluorescence, revealing widespread neuronal expression in WT mice and no detectable expression in METTL4−/− mice of both sexes (Fig. 1C). These results were confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 1D). To investigate the possible involvement of METTL4 in sex-specific differences in nociceptive responses of mice, we assessed the mechanical pain thresholds of WT and METTL4−/− mice. The results demonstrated that female METTL4−/− mice, but not male METTL4−/− mice, exhibited hypersensitivity to mechanical stimulus (Fig. 1E). This finding suggested that METTL4 regulates nociceptive responses in female mice.

METTL4 induces distinct and extensive m6A profiles in male and female mice

As an epitranscriptomic modification, m6A is involved in regulating various physiological processes that exhibit sex differences [19, 32]. METTL4 is necessary to maintain normal m6A modification levels and distribution patterns [33]. To analyze the effect of METTL4 on the m6A expression profile of mouse spinal cord, we performed MeRIP-seq on WT and METTL4−/− mouse spinal cord tissues. In both male and female METTL4−/− mice, the majority (~ 92%) of m6A peaks were located within intragenic regions, and the dominant sequence motif was the consensus RRACH (Fig. 2A). These m6A peaks were enriched in coding sequences (CDSs), 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs), and near stop codons (Fig. 2B and C).

(B) and (C) m6A peak enrichment distribution. (D) Differences in m6A peaks between male and female of WT or METTL4−/− mice. (E) Pathway enrichment analysis of male and female METTL4−/− mice. (F)-(M) Quantitative analysis of the expression of METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, FTO, ALKBH5, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3 by western blot respectively

The m6A peaks in both female and male METTL4−/− mice showed substantial overlap with those in WT mice overall, with 13,157 and 13,412 shared peaks in females and males respectively. And there were 2383 peaks and 2406 changed peaks in females and males, respectively (Fig. 2D). Gene pathway enrichment analysis revealed that m6A-upregulated genes were enriched in glutamate synapse, mitochondrial organization, and metabolic pathway in female mice, whereas in male mice, the enrichment pathways included mTOR signaling and Rab1signaling (Fig. 2E).

Under physiological conditions, m6A is dynamically regulated by methyltransferases (writers), demethylases (erasers) and m6A-binding proteins (readers) [18]. To assess the involvement of these m6A regulators in pain sensitization in female mice, we used western blotting to examine their expression levels in the spinal cords of WT and METTL4−/− mice. Compared with WT mice, METTL4 knockout did not alter the expression of METTL14, METTL3, WTAP, FTO, ALKBH5, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, or YTHDF3 in either sex (Fig. 2F and M). These results suggested the specific involvement of METTL4-mediated m6A modification in spinal cord neurons in the formation of pain sensitization in female mice.

METTL4 expressed in synapse without sex different

The propagation of pain signals is intricately linked to synaptic remodeling and the efficacy of synaptic transmission. METTL4 knockout resulted in pain hypersensitivity in female mice, highlighting its potential role in modulating synaptic function. To address this possibility, we first sought to investigate whether METTL4 localizes to the neuronal synapses in the spinal cord by performing immunofluorescence double labeling using antibodies against METTL4 and the synapse marker synapsin I. The results showed wide neuronal expression of METTL4 in the mouse spinal cord, with high-magnification imaging revealing clear colocalization with synapsin I, indicating synaptic localization (Fig. 3A). To further characterize this observation, immunofluorescence triple labeling was performed on cultured neuron with antibodies against METTL4, synapsin I, and MAP2, a neuronal marker. The merged image clearly showed close colocalization of METTL4 and synapsin I at synapses along neuronal axons and dendrites (Fig. 3B). As a methyltransferase, METTL4 modulates epitranscriptomic regulatory processes to influence gene expression. To assess whether METTL4 knockout alters synapsin I expression to disrupt synaptogenesis in a sex-specific manner, we quantitatively analyzed synapsin I levels in the spinal cords of male and female WT and METTL4−/− mice (Fig. 3C). Synapsin I expression was not affected by METTL4 knockout in either sex. These results were confirmed by quantitative analysis of synapsin I and METTL4 puncta, as well as their colocalized puncta along neuronal axons and dendrites of cultured spinal cord neurons (Fig. 3D and F). These results indicated that METTL4 is widely expressed at synapses without sex-specific differences, and that its absence in METTL4−/− mice does not affect synapsin I expression, synaptogenesis, or synapse number.

METTL4 is mainly localized at glutamatergic synapses

To further elucidate whether METTL4-mediated pain hypersensitivity in female mice is associated with sex-dependent localization in glutamatergic or GABAergic synapses, we performed immunofluorescence double labeling of spinal cord sections using antibodies against METTL4, the GABAergic synaptic marker VGAT, and the glutamatergic synaptic marker Vglut2 in both male and female mice (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis of the colocalization puncta revealed that ~ 80% of METTL4 molecules were localized at glutamatergic synapses in both male and female mice, compared with ~ 20% at GABAergic synapses (Fig. 4C). Consistently, immunofluorescence triple labeling of cultured neurons using antibodies against METTL4, MAP2, and VGAT or Vglut2 showed that METTL4 was predominantly localized at glutamatergic synapses, but not at GABAergic synapses, along neuronal axons and dendrites. This finding was confirmed by quantitative analysis of the colocalization of METTL4 puncta with VGAT and Vglut2 puncta (Fig. 4B and D). These results suggested that METTL4 is mainly expressed in glutamatergic neurons and synapses, where it may regulate excitatory synaptic function and thereby contribute to pain hypersensitivity in female mice.

METTL4 knockout enhances excitatory signaling in the spinal dorsal horn of female mice

To explore whether METTL4 can regulate synaptic function, we performed patch-clamp experiments on spinal cord segments from WT and METTL4−/− mice, targeting neurons in laminae I–II of the dorsal horn. Neuronal excitability analysis revealed that METTL4 knockout decreased the minimum current required to evoke action potentials and increased the firing frequency in female mice, but not in male mice (Fig. 5A). These results indicated that METTL4 knockout enhances neuronal excitability specifically in female mice.

Next, we recorded spontaneous EPSCs (sEPSCs) and spontaneous IPSCs (sIPSCs). METTL4 knockout enhanced the amplitude and frequency of sEPSCs in female mice, but not in male mice (Fig. 5B), and had no effect on sIPSCs in either sex (Fig. 5C). Consistent with the predominant localization of METTL4 at glutamatergic synapses, these results indicated that METTL4 knockout specifically enhances excitatory synaptic transmission in female mice, but does not affect inhibitory synaptic transmission in either sex. Furthermore, these findings suggested that METTL4 sex-dependently regulates neuronal excitability and excitatory synaptic transmission in female mice, thereby contributing to their pain hypersensitivity.

Next, we expressed the genetically encoded calcium fluorescence indicator GCaMP6s in the spinal dorsal horn of mice via injection of AAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s in vivo. Calcium imaging analysis of the resulting spinal cord segments showed that METTL4 knockout increased the firing rate and frequency of spinal dorsal horn neurons in female mice, but not in male mice (Figure S2A–S2C).

METTL4 modulates synaptic UCP2 expression via sex-dependent m6A modification

The efficiency of synaptic transmission is closely related to energy metabolism at the synapse. UCP2 is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it regulates neurotransmitter release at presynaptic terminals by modulating mitochondrial energy metabolism [34, 35]. To investigate whether METTL4 knockout enhances excitatory synaptic transmission by upregulating UCP2 expression to modulate mitochondrial energy metabolism, we examined the synaptic expression of UCP2. Immunofluorescence double staining using antibodies against UCP2 and synapsin I in mouse spinal cord sections (Fig. 6A) and cultured neurons (Fig. 6B) showed wide synaptic expression of UCP2. Next, we used high-resolution imaging to analyze the colocalization of synapsin I and UCP2 puncta in spinal cord sections from WT and METTL4−/− male and female mice (Fig. 6C). Quantitative analysis of the colocalized puncta of synapsin I and UCP2 revealed that METTL4 knockout significantly increased the synaptic expression of UCP2, but did not affect the synaptic expression or localization of synapsin I, in female mice (Fig. 6D and F). These results indicated that METTL4 knockout selectively regulates the synaptic expression and localization of UCP2 in a sex-specific manner.

Given the sex-dependent regulation of UCP2 expression in METTL4−/− mice, we hypothesized that METTL4 may modulate synaptic UCP2 levels by mediating m6A of Ucp2 in a sex-specific manner. To test this hypothesis, we examined m6A of Ucp2 in synapses using MeRIP–qPCR, which revealed that METTL4 knockout significantly decreased the m6A level of Ucp2 transcripts in female mice (Fig. 6G). Because m6A is crucial for regulating RNA stability, splicing, degradation, and translation, we further investigated whether the downregulation of Ucp2 m6A could lead to sex-dependent upregulation of synaptic UCP2 expression. Using RT-PCR and western blotting, we found that METTL4 knockout significantly increased synaptic UCP2 expression in female mice at both the mRNA and protein levels, respectively (Fig. 6H and I). To reinstate METTL4 expression specifically within the dorsal horn, METTL4-overexpressing AAV was micro-injected stereotaxically into the L4–L6 segments of METTL4−/− female mice (Figure S3A and S3B). METTL4 overexpression in the dorsal horn significantly reversed nociceptive thresholds in these METTL4−/− females (Figure S3C), concomitantly elevated m6A-UCP2, and suppressed UCP2 abundance (Figure S3D and S3E). These results indicated that METTL4 selectively regulates UCP2 expression in female mice through sex-dependent m6A modification of Ucp2 in synapses.

m6A readers promote mRNA degradation by recognizing m6A and recruiting the mRNA decay machinery. Notably, the m6A reader YTHDF2 is essential for oocyte competence, with mutations linked to female infertility [36]. Although METTL4 knockout did not affect the expression of other m6A cofactors (Fig. 2), its effect on Ucp2 expression likely depends on m6A reader function. To test this hypothesis, cultured spinal cord neurons were transfected with LVs carrying small interfering RNA sequences targeting the m6A reader genes Ythdf1, Ythdf2, and Ythdf3. Immunofluorescence images revealed that all three m6A readers were expressed in synapses along neuronal dendrites and axons. Knockdown of Ythdf2, but not of Ythdf1 or Ythdf3, increased synaptic UCP2 expression and its localization along dendrites and axons (Figures S4A, S5A, and S6A). RT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed that Ythdf2 knockdown increased UCP2 expression (Figures S4B–S4C, S5B–S5C, and S6B–S6C). Quantitative analysis showed that Ythdf2 knockdown increased Ucp2 m6A levels and UCP2 puncta in neuronal spines (Figure S4D and S4E). These results suggested that, while METTL4-mediated synaptic UCP2 expression and localization depend on Ythdf2, METTL4 does not regulate the expression of the m6A reader.

METTL4 knockout enhances mitochondrial fission

Synaptic activity demands substantial energy supplied by mitochondrial ATP production, and UCP2 has been shown to promote mitochondrial fission and increase ATP production [34]. Given that METTL4 knockout enhances excitatory transmission in female mice via m6A-dependent regulation of Ucp2 expression, we hypothesized that this sex-dependent increase in synaptic transmission is likely mediated through sex-dependent changes in mitochondrial dynamics. To determine whether METTL4 knockout sex-dependently alters mitochondrial fission and mitochondrial number, we examined synaptic mitochondria in the spinal cord using transmission electron microscopy. The presynaptic membrane was identified by the presence of neurotransmitter vesicles and the synaptic cleft (Fig. 7A and B). Quantitative analysis of mitochondrial microscopy images showed that METTL4 knockout increased the mitochondrial number exclusively in female mice (Fig. 7E). To further clarify the role of METTL4 on synaptic mitochondrial fission, we performed immunofluorescence double staining using antibodies against synapsin I and the mitochondrial marker COX in WT and METTL4−/− mice of both sexes. High-resolution immunofluorescence images revealed that COX closely colocalized with synapsin I in across all four experimental groups (Fig. 7C and D), with the METTL4 knockout increasing mitochondrial puncta and colocalized COX and synapsin I puncta only in female mice (Fig. 7F and G). These findings demonstrated that METTL4 knockout enhances mitochondrial fission and augments mitochondrial number in the spinal synapses of female mice.

To test causal sufficiency, we stereotaxically delivered AAV of shUcp2 into the dorsal horn of METTL4−/− female mice, achieving robust spinal knockdown of UCP2 (Figure S7A and S7B) that fully reversed their mechanical hypersensitivity (Figure S7C). UCP2 silencing reduced synaptic mitochondrial density (Figure S7D and S7E) and ATP production (Figure S7F–S7H), and selectively diminished sEPSCs amplitude and frequency while leaving sIPSCs unchanged (Figure S7I and S7J). Collectively, these data establish a spinal, female-specific METTL4–UCP2 axis that gates nociceptive transmission by tuning synaptic mitochondrial bioenergetics.

METTL4 knock out boosts synaptic mitochondrial ATP production in female mice

Mitochondrial dynamics determine the fate and function of cells. Mitochondria are highly dynamic in morphology, and maintaining their normal shape is crucial in the process of mitochondrial ATP production. To identify whether the METTL4 induced processes of mitochondrial fission would affect the mitochondrial morphology and function, the morphological analysis of the scanning electron microscope mitochondrial images was performed and the mitochondrial length and area were measured. The results indicated that METTL4 knock out did not change the mitochondrial size and morphology both in male and female mice (Fig. 8A and D). Then the mitochondrial DNA was examined by RT-PCR. The results showed that, only in female mice, METTL4 knock out results in increase mitochondrial DNA copies indicating more generation of mitochondrial, (Fig. 8E). To evaluate the mitochondrial function, the synaptic mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), ROS and ATP were examined using assay Kits according the experiment protocols. The results METTL4 knock out did not affect the MMP and production of ROS in both of male and female mice, (Fig. 8E and G), but promote production of ATP significantly in female mice. (Fig. 8H). These results suggested that MEETTL4 knock out promoted synaptic mitochondrial fission and increased the number of mitochondrial and therefor increased the production of ATP in the synapse of female mice.

Discussion

In the present study, we generated complete METTL4 knockout mice using the CRISPR-Cas9 system and demonstrated that METTL4 deficiency induces pain hypersensitivity exclusively in female mice. Further investigations revealed that METTL4 knockout increases the excitability of excitatory neurons by upregulating UCP2 expression in an m6A-dependent manner. This synaptic upregulation of UCP2 promotes mitochondrial fission and increases mitochondrial number to enhance synaptic ATP production, which in turn strengthens excitatory synaptic transmission. Collectively, our findings highlight the pronounced sex differences in METTL4-mediated regulation of pain transmission and provide a potential sex-differentiated animal model for pain hypersensitivity.

Previous research indicates that METTL4 exhibits sex-specific differences in epitranscriptomic regulation [37]. For instance, in Drosophila, METTL4 specifically regulates the m6A of U2 snRNA, with significantly higher modification levels in females than in males [28]. In the current study, we found no significant differences in the physiological processes of pain transmission between male and female WT mice. However, female mice with METTL4 knockout exhibited greater sensitivity to noxious stimuli than did their male METTL4−/− counterparts. Thus, our findings have clarified that METTL4 differentially regulates pain transmission in a sex-dependent manner, highlighting the potential of this methyltransferase as a therapeutic target for pain management in females.

METTL4 is essential for maintaining normal m6A modification levels and distribution patterns [28]. Through MeRIP-seq experiments and bioinformatics analysis, we demonstrated that METTL4 knockout results in distinct m6A modification profiles and physiological regulation in male and female mice, further supporting the sex-specific role of METTL4 in epitranscriptomic regulation [19, 38–40].

The efficiency of synaptic transmission relies on the synaptic energy supply, which primarily originates from local mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Thus, the capacity of synaptic mitochondria to synthesize ATP is critical for maintaining synaptic energy supply and functional status.

UCP2 is a member of the mitochondrial uncoupling protein family [40]. Unlike other proteins in this family, UCP2 primarily maintains mitochondrial homeostasis and ATP synthesis in the nervous system and plays a crucial role in synaptic remodeling [35, 41]. In the current study, we demonstrated that UCP2 is localized at synapses and displays no difference in expression between male and female WT mice. However, METTL4 knockout was found to increase synaptic UCP2 levels exclusively in female mice. In previous studies, UCP2 exhibited significant sex differences in regulating neuronal activity and animal behavior. Thus, METTL4 appears to differentially regulate pain transmission in a sex-dependent manner by modulating synaptic Ucp2 expression through m6A modification.

m6A modifications in mRNA are reversible, allowing for dynamic regulation. RNA methylation levels are finely balanced through the interplay among m6A writers, erasers, and readers. In the current study, we confirmed that METTL4 functions as a writer in spinal cord synapses, and that its m6A of synaptic Ucp2 mRNA is YTHDF2-dependent, suggesting that YTHDF2 acts as a reader in these METTL4-mediated epitranscriptomic modifications.

UCP2 is essential for energy production and synaptic plasticity [35], decoupling oxidative phosphorylation from ATP synthesis by facilitating controlled proton re-entry into the mitochondrial matrix. In neurons, sustained UCP2-mediated uncoupling promotes mitochondrial proliferation and ATP production, enhancing adaptability to dynamic activity fluctuations [35, 41]. Thus, sex-differentiated synaptic UCP2 expression drives mitochondrial fission, increases metabolic load, and amplifies pain transmission in females. These findings suggest that mitochondrial-dependent changes in synaptic energy metabolism may underlie sex differences in pain.

Previous studies showed that mitochondrial fission is tightly regulated to generate ATP via oxidative phosphorylation [42]. Chronic stress promotes mitochondrial fission in the medial prefrontal cortex in a sex-dependent manner, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction [42, 43]. Emerging evidence points to two types of fission: peripheral division, which clears smaller mitochondria through mitophagy, and midzone division, which is associated with mitochondrial proliferation. Inheritance of complete mtDNA is crucial for maintaining the normal function of new mitochondria produced through fission. Thus, increase of synaptic mtDNA always indicated the midzone division of mitochondrial which would directly lead to an increase in the number of mitochondria. In the current study, we demonstrated that METTL4 knockout increased mtDNA levels and mitochondrial number in the synapses of female mice, without altering mitochondrial size in either sex. Together with our quantitative analysis of mitochondria in electron microscopy experiments, our results suggest that METTL4 knockout promotes mitochondrial proliferation via fission, enhancing ATP production in the synapses of female mice.

In summary, our study demonstrates that METTL4 differentially regulates synaptic UCP2 expression in a sex-dependent and m6A-dependent manner, thereby promoting mitochondrial fission and proliferation in synapses. This leads to increased energy production and supply, enhanced synaptic transmission, and heightened pain sensitivity in female mice. Our findings establish a sex-differentiated animal model of pain hypersensitivity and highlight METTL4 as a potential target for female-specific pain management in clinical settings.
