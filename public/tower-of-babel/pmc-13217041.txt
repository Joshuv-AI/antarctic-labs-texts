Fifteen-minute consultation: Modern diagnostic approach to the child with recurrent fractures
Authors: Alexandra Blaschitz, Wolfgang Högler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren and young people presenting with recurrent fractures pose a diagnostic challenge for clinicians. Such patients require early referral to a tertiary paediatric centre with bone specialists and a multidisciplinary team taking a comprehensive, modern approach to unravel the underlying causes. Using a guiding diagnostic algorithm, incorporating a thorough medical history, informed clinical examinations, targeted laboratory tests, state-of-the-art imaging and molecular genetic analyses, specialists can expedite the identification of underlying causes of bone fragility in children.The modern diagnostic approach integrates the latest evidence-based guidelines and technological advancements, thus enabling a swift and effective management strategy. This fifteen-minute consultation equips healthcare professionals with the necessary information to optimise history taking and basic case evaluation and provides insights into procedures at tertiary bone centres. Good collaboration among general paediatricians, orthopaedic surgeons and paediatric bone specialists ultimately enhances the care and outcomes for children with recurrent fractures.Keywords: Paediatrics, Adolescent Health, Endocrinology, Genetics, Growth

Introduction

This article provides an overview of modern diagnostics for bone fragility in children. We first guide general paediatricians through the process prior to referral, and then focus on specialised diagnostic approaches conducted by bone experts.

Case

When Marc first visited our metabolic bone clinic at the age of 4, he already had a long history of hospital visits. At 1 year of age, his parents had sought help from a general paediatrician as they recognised a pronounced laxity of all joints and muscular hypotonia. Given these signs, he was referred on to a neuromuscular specialist. When he started to walk at 2 years of age, he also started to fall, which led to multiple long bone fractures. This led the neuromuscular specialist to suspect bone fragility and prompted a referral to our metabolic bone clinic.

Bone fragility in children

Bone fragility in children is a serious clinical concern, describing a condition in which a child’s bones break more often and easily compared with their peers. This condition is associated with chronic pain, deformities, reduced mobility and consequently with impaired quality of life.1 As there are many causes of bone fragility, both primary (genetic) and secondary (acquired), the road to accurate diagnosis can be long. Primary osteoporosis usually manifests in the first years of life and is caused by genetic conditions which lead to faulty collagen type 1 synthesis, bone formation or bone resorption.2 3 Secondary osteoporosis is caused by underlying medical conditions that alter bone metabolism such as acute or chronic disease, immobility, poor nutrition or bone-toxic medications.1 4

Timely referral to a paediatric bone specialist (usually paediatric endocrinologist) is crucial for rare disease diagnosis, state-of-the-art multidisciplinary therapy and to help meet the patient’s complex care needs.

Basic evaluation

Children with bone fragility can present with multiple low-trauma fractures, bone deformities, back or joint pain but also with muscular hypotonia, short stature or dental manifestations.

Non-accidental injury

Non-accidental injury is important to consider in any child with low-trauma fractures. The physician might notice a delay in seeking medical attention, unexplained bruising and a reported mechanism of injury that does not correlate with the fracture type. In addition, fractures before the age of 2 years, certain types of fractures and multiple fractures in different states of healing can hint towards non-accidental injury, although these findings also apply to primary osteoporosis.6

If non-accidental injury is suspected, local safeguarding policy and protocols should be followed and the Royal College of Paediatric and Child Health Safeguarding Portal can be consulted for further advice.7

A referral to a metabolic bone clinic should be initiated no later than this stage of the diagnostic pathway.

Case

During the first contact with our metabolic bone team, we noticed some conspicuous findings. Marc had a history of bilateral inguinal hernia and had already sustained eight low-trauma fractures by the age of 5 years.

At clinical examination, he demonstrated grey-tinged sclera, white teeth, joint laxity and muscular hypotonia, as well as weight and height under the third percentile.

The basic lab and urine analysis showed normal results. Because of his fracture history and his typical clinical presentation, osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) was suspected.

Bone and spine imaging

The next step is to add imaging to the puzzle. Systemic bone fragility affects the whole skeleton and vertebral bones in particular. Therefore, the main imaging techniques are not just dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) but also lateral spine imaging, as well as a skeletal survey. A skeletal survey is indicated if, for example, non-accidental injury is suspected or when there is clinical concern for multiple occult fractures.8 Modern DXA machines can obtain lateral spine views to allow vertebral fracture assessment (VFA). VFA by DXA is preferred over VFA from lateral spine radiographs due to lower radiation exposure yet comparable detection rates of vertebral fractures.9 10 However, because of the lower imaging quality compared with conventional radiographs, additional X-rays or MRI of the spine may be necessary nonetheless.

If scoliosis and/or deformities are present, X-rays of the affected areas are always indicated.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)

DXA is the gold standard method to evaluate bone mass in children, from age 5 years. Imaging in younger children is restricted to radiographs due to the lack of normative data for DXA. Performing and interpreting DXA in children requires a lot of expertise and should not be conducted outside paediatric specialist centres.11

The preferred scanning sites in children for assessment of bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD) are the posterior–anterior spine (L1–4) and the total body less head (TBLH). Given DXA’s two-dimensionality, size adjustments are required. Therefore, bone mineral apparent density and height-adjusted Z-scores are used for the spine scans, and height-adjusted or lean mass-adjusted Z-scores for TBLH scans.9

A BMD Z-score lower than −2 SD is considered ‘low bone mass’. The diagnosis ‘osteoporosis’ can be made if one of the following criteria is fulfilled:12

It is also important to know that a normal BMD does not exclude childhood osteoporosis. In addition, even one fracture of a long bone, considering the child’s clinical history, radiographic features, as well as the location of the fracture, can lead to the diagnosis of osteoporosis. This makes this diagnosis challenging and hence requires the expertise of a specialist.6

Conventional peripheral quantitative CT (pQCT) and high-resolution pQCT (HR-pQCT)

Both pQCT and HR-pQCT have been used in research and clinical practice as an adjuvant diagnostic tool for many years. Due to limitations in spatial resolution and repositioning, pQCT has not achieved widespread routine clinical use. The newer HR-pQCT can assess compartment-specific bone geometry, density, structure and microarchitecture of long tubular bones, using low radiation doses (see figure 1). Up until now, this technique has been mostly used for research; nevertheless, it is a promising modality to gain a better understanding of bone and how different conditions affect bone.13

Case

Marc’s X-rays showed one compressed lumbar vertebra at L1. DXA revealed substantially reduced BMD and BMC in the total body as well as in the lumbar spine with Z-scores ranging from −4.5 to −3.7. He therefore fulfilled the criteria for childhood osteoporosis and we initiated further diagnostic steps followed by treatment with zoledronic acid.

Molecular genetic diagnostics (assessing DNA)

If a patient fulfils the definition of childhood osteoporosis and clinical information does not suggest secondary osteoporosis, testing for genetic causes is indicated (figure 2). Whole exome sequencing (WES) in the form of targeted gene panels is the standard of care. However, targeted gene panels are not always up to date and coverage of genes may be incomplete.14 Additionally, variants of unknown significance (VUS), whose pathogenicity is uncertain, might be detected.15 Identifying the responsible gene defects is very important because it enables the bone specialist to understand the underlying mechanism, consider the use of targeted therapies and provide adequate genetic counselling.

Bone biopsy

In cases where all diagnostic steps do not lead to a definite diagnosis or the genetic testing reveals a VUS, the specialist will consider taking a transiliac bone biopsy (figure 2). This has to happen before initiating any pharmacological therapy that affects bone. By bone biopsy, detailed information on the dynamic rate of bone formation and resorption, degree of mineralisation, bone structure and material properties can be unveiled.16

RNA-sequencing

If no disease-causing mutations in the genomic DNA are detected, taking a skin biopsy and/or urine should be considered. From that, fibroblasts/urothelial cells can be cultured and their RNA can be sequenced and the proteome analysed in research labs. This can confirm whether a VUS actually causes changes in protein expression, or unravel the effect of deep intronic variants that alter the mRNA sequences, which would not be detected by genomic DNA sequencing.17

Case

Marc’s WES results came back normal. After 2 years, we repeated genetic testing in a different laboratory, which showed a heterozygous VUS in the COL1A2 gene. That this variant was pathogenic seemed unlikely at that time. Before starting zoledronic acid, we carried out a bone and skin biopsy. The bone biopsy was suggestive of OI (eg, increased mineralisation), although some features (eg, increased osteoblast number) were not typical.

Only the RNA-sequencing from cultured skin fibroblasts finally gave us clarity as it revealed that the previously detected VUS led to a new splice-acceptor site. This variant therefore leads to an altered collagen-I α2 chain consistent with a diagnosis of OI.

Summary

There are a lot of unmet needs of people living with rare bone disease. Lack of medical knowledge leads to late referrals and diagnostic delay and therefore also to a delay in appropriate treatment. Such delays lead to significant long-term consequences for affected patients and their families.

Following the state-of-the-art diagnostic process, a comprehensive evaluation of potential underlying causes of secondary osteoporosis should be undertaken, while simultaneously considering the specific features of the primary bone fragility condition.

After referral to tertiary care, imaging extends beyond conventional radiography to incorporate further imaging techniques as DXA and HR-pQCT. In addition, molecular genetic analyses (DNA/RNA sequencing) and, where indicated, transiliac bone biopsy are critical components in establishing an accurate diagnosis.

Together, these strategies enable multidisciplinary care, expert knowledge of all diagnostic options and targeted treatment approaches, in order to support patients to lead their lives with as few limitations and pain as possible. Bone specialists also work in international clinical expert networks and liaise with rare disease support groups, thus connecting people and science.
