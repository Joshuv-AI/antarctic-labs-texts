Comparative feasibility of reasoning and non-reasoning large language models for gynecologic cancer emergency care
Authors: Junhwan Kim, Uisuk Kim, Jae Kyung Bae, Misong Kim, Ji Young Ham, Juwon Lim, Ji Hyun Kim, Sang-Soo Seo, Sokbom Kang, Sang-Yoon Park, Myong Cheol Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have led to increasing exploration of their clinical applications, particularly in enhancing decision-making and patient management. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of LLMs, specifically the non-reasoning Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT)-4o and reasoning o3-mini-high, in supporting emergency care of patients with gynecologic cancer.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center study, 15 real-world emergency cases in gynecologic oncology were selected. Two gynecologic oncology fellows, two obstetrics and gynecology residents, GPT-4o, and o3-mini-high assessed each case through four steps: providing differential diagnoses and suggesting relevant examinations; interpreting findings, establishing diagnoses, and proposing treatment; prescribing medical orders; and generating patient education materials (LLMs only). Responses were scored for relevance and speed. Paired tests with bootstrapping were used to estimate mean differences (MDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsBoth LLMs completed the tasks significantly faster than physicians, with an average time reduction of approximately 300 s per model (P < 0.001). GPT-4o achieved higher total scores than physicians (MD, 3.55; 95% CI, 2.98–4.10; P < 0.001) and maintained superiority when speed metrics were excluded (MD, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.80–1.79; P < 0.001). o3-mini-high also outperformed physicians in total scores (MD, 3.05; 95% CI, 1.98–3.88; P < 0.001), but not with speed metrics. Satisfaction scores for LLM-generated management were 1.9/2.0 for GPT-4o and 1.8/2.0 for o3-mini-high.ConclusionsBoth GPT-4o and o3-mini-high are feasible tools for emergency care of patients with gynecologic cancer. GPT-4o may provide advantages, reflecting the pattern-based structure of emergency care in this domain. LLM selection should be based on the domain-specific medical knowledge required rather than on the reasoning status or model version. Further prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm our findings as well as clinical effectiveness of LLMs in gynecologic cancer emergency care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13668-8.Keywords: Large language model, GPT-4o, o3-mini-high, Emergency, Diagnosis, Gynecologic oncology

Introduction

Emergency healthcare systems encounter significant challenges due to resource constraints or personnel shortages. In South Korea, healthcare policy changes in February 2024 triggered mass resignations of residents and physicians, creating critical gaps in emergency care and reducing access to specialty support [1]. This situation presented substantial difficulties for the remaining physicians treating patients with cancer in emergency departments, as these patients often present with problems beyond the physicians’ primary expertise [2, 3].

Managing patients with cancer in emergency settings is a complex procedure. These patients have higher risks of hospitalization due to advanced disease and treatment side effects, and their management is complicated by multiple comorbidities, with presentations ranging from infection and pain to respiratory distress and gastrointestinal symptoms [4, 5]. In particular, patients with gynecologic cancer present with various symptoms related to both oncologic and non-oncologic complaints in emergency departments [6, 7]. Physicians who care for these patients and work in settings where specialists are unavailable must navigate complex decision-making processes to provide appropriate care while avoiding potential harm and poor outcomes from misdiagnosis or delayed treatment [8, 9]. This scenario presents an opportunity to explore innovative technological approaches that assist physicians in clinical decision-making and enhance patient care in resource-constrained environments.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in large language models (LLMs), such as Generative Pretrained Transformer 4 (GPT-4, OpenAI), show promise in addressing these issues and have demonstrated diagnostic and management skills, outperforming resident physicians in board examinations [10, 11]. OpenAI’s LLMs continue to evolve with the release of the reasoning model o3-mini-high in February 2025, while the non-reasoning model GPT-4o, launched in May 2024, still provides more economical and sufficient responses. Reasoning LLMs employ multi-step logical processes such as chain of thought reasoning to systematically decompose complex problems, whereas non-reasoning models often generate outputs based on simpler associative strategies found in data. The potential use of these LLMs in emergency care for patients with gynecologic cancer may signify a new frontier in healthcare innovation requiring thorough investigation, particularly as healthcare systems seek solutions to enhance clinical decision-making in scenarios where specialized expertise may be lacking.

We aimed to evaluate the feasibility of using GPT-4o and o3-mini-high to support clinical decision-making in emergency care for patients with gynecologic cancer. Specifically, we investigated the differences between the capabilities of a non-reasoning model and a reasoning model during the process of generating clinical assistance.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective, single-institution, case-based study. The medical records of patients diagnosed with gynecologic cancer who visited the emergency department at the National Cancer Center in South Korea, between January 1, 2005, and September 30, 2024, were retrospectively reviewed. From this dataset, 15 cases covering a range of emergency conditions in gynecologic oncology were selected, representing common critical scenarios that frequently present at our institution or are considered high-risk: (1) functional ileus, (2) adhesive ileus, (3) radiation-induced enterocolitis, (4) bowel perforation, (5) hematemesis, (6) vesicovaginal and rectovaginal fistula, (7) complicated lymphocele, (8) brain metastasis (including leptomeningeal seeding), (9) septic shock, (10) neutropenic fever, 11) postoperative bleeding, 12) pulmonary thromboembolism, 13) epilepsy, 14) drug-induced anaphylaxis, and 15) pneumonia. These cases were then assessed by two board-certified obstetrics and gynecology specialists (gynecologic oncology clinical fellows; Physician 1 [P1] and Physician 2 [P2]); two obstetrics and gynecology residents (post-graduate year [PGY]-3 and PGY-1; Physician 3 [P3] and Physician 4 [P4]), GPT-4o (model name: gpt-4o-2024-11-20), and o3-mini-high (model name: o3-mini-2025-01-31).

After a brief presentation of each case history, participants performed a structured evaluation and provided responses through a stepwise approach (Step 1 to Step 4), as demonstrated in Fig. 1. The same cases were evaluated using GPT-4o and o3-mini-high through the structured prompts that were uniquely developed and optimized for tasks (Supplementary Table S1). For Steps 1–3, GPT-4o, o1-mini-high, and physician participants were each provided with a generalized researcher-curated summary at each step, including the patient’s presenting symptoms and relevant history, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and imaging findings extracted from the medical records, since direct LLM access to the electronic medical record was not permitted at our institution. Responses from the participants, GPT-4o, and o3-mini-high were analyzed to assess diagnostic accuracy, completeness of examination selection, appropriateness of treatment plans, and time efficiency. We evaluated the participating physicians’ satisfaction with the responses generated by GPT-4o and o3-mini-high, as well as the relevance and quality of the patient education materials produced.

Scoring system

Responses from both human participants and GPT-4o and o3-mini-high across Steps 1–4 were scored based on a predefined scoring system (Supplementary Table S2). In Step 1, we assessed relevance of differential diagnosis, relevance of suggested necessary examinations, and speed in suggesting differential diagnoses and necessary examinations. In Step 2, we evaluated relevance of examination interpretations, relevance of the final diagnosis, relevance of treatment plans, and speed in interpretations of examinations and suggestions for the final diagnosis and treatment plans. In Step 3, we scored relevance of prescribed orders and speed in prescribing orders. Each factor was rated on a 2-point scale (0–2). The total score for Steps 1–3 was determined by summing all components, with a maximum possible score of 18 points. For Step 4, we assessed relevance of generated patient education materials and speed of generating patient education materials, both scored on a 2-point scale (0–2). Additionally, physicians’ satisfaction with the generated answers and patient education materials were evaluated using the same 2-point scale. In each case, GPT-4o and o3-mini-high were instructed to generate responses three times for Steps 1–4. Average scores of these models were calculated, as well as time taken for each Step. Two physician evaluators independently scored all responses and resolved any discrepancies through discussion to reach a consensus for each item.

Statistical analysis

Estimated scores were summarized using descriptive statistical analysis. The normality of paired score differences between groups was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the assumption of normality was met, paired t-tests were used to compare mean differences (MDs); when normality was violated, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. To estimate MDs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) across all pairwise comparisons, a non-parametric bootstrapping procedure with 10,000 resampled iterations was performed. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution were used to derive the CIs. Bootstrapping with 10,000 resampled iterations was employed to estimate the MDs and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) across all pairwise comparisons, including model-to-model and model-to-physician comparisons. The 95% CIs were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrapped distributions. An MD was considered significant if the corresponding CI did not include zero. A two-sided P < 0.05 was regarded as significant for all analyses. Data were analyzed using Python (version 3.13.2; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) with NumPy and Matplotlib packages for bootstrapping and visualization, and R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for additional statistical testing. Because the pairwise comparisons between models and physicians were exploratory rather than confirmatory, no formal multiplicity correction was applied; instead, exact bootstrap CIs were presented for each comparison.

Comparison of performance between GPT-4o, o3-mini-high, and the physicians

Detailed physician performance data are available in Supplementary Table S5, with comparisons between the LLMs and physicians documented in Supplementary Table S4. Figure 2 illustrates the mean total scores of GPT-4o, o3-mini-high, and the physicians. GPT-4o (MD, 3.55; 95% CI, 2.98–4.10; P < 0.001) and o3-mini-high (MD, 3.05; 95% CI, 1.98–3.88; P < 0.001) achieved significantly higher scores than the physicians. Pooled scores of both models and the physicians were also investigated across Steps 1–3, with each median score with 95% CIs presented in Fig. 3, visualizing that scores of GPT-4o and o3-mini-high were higher than those of the physicians; we assumed that the weight of every question and every attempt was equal. In addition, both models completed tasks significantly faster than physicians, with time reductions of approximately 302.85 s for GPT-4o (95% CI, − 343.1 to − 263.6, P < 0.001) and 301.30 s for o3-mini-high (95% CI, − 341.4 to − 261.5, P < 0.001; Fig. 4). When speed metrics were excluded from the analysis, only GPT-4o outperformed physicians (MD, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.80–1.79; P < 0.001; Fig. 5). Both models offered detailed and correct doses and administration intervals of drugs, including antibiotics, in actual order prescriptions. In the case of vesicovaginal fistula with rectovaginal fistula, o3-mini-high correctly read vesicovaginal fistula with rectovaginal fistula on computer tomography (CT) reading but persistently prioritized and maintained only vesicovaginal fistula as the final diagnosis based on the patient’s symptom of increased vaginal discharge, which resulted in incorrect treatment plans and patient education materials. No definite hallucinations were observed in the responses of both models.

Comparison of the step-by-step performance between GPT-4o, o3-mini-high, and the physicians

We evaluated the mean total scores and time taken from Steps 1–4 for LLMs and the physicians (Supplementary Table S7). GPT-4o and o3-mini-high showed no statistical difference in score and time taken from Steps 1–3 (all P > 0.05). However, both models demonstrated significantly higher scores and shorter times in every Step when compared to the mean score and time of the physicians (all P < 0.05). GPT-4o scored 1.7 points higher in MD while finishing tasks 111.1 s faster than the physicians at the maximum in Step 2; o3-mini-high scored 1.0 points higher in MD in Steps 1 and 2 while it finished tasks 109.6 s faster at the maximum in Step 2. When comparing scores and time taken between GPT-4o and each physician in Steps 1–3, GPT-4o revealed significantly higher scores at the maximum of 1.8 points in Step 2 and shorter time at the maximum of 189.1 s in Step 2 (all P < 0.05). Meanwhile, o3-mini-high significantly scored better and finished tasks faster than physicians, except when compared with gynecologic oncology clinical fellows P2, where no significant difference was found in the mean total scores for Step 1 and Step 2.

Physicians’ satisfaction with management recommendations and patient education materials generated by the GPT-4o and o3-mini-high

The mean value for physicians’ satisfaction on management from GPT-4o was 1.9 (range, 1.7–2.0) and 1.8 (range, 0.7–2.0) for o3-mini-high (Table 1), where no significant difference was observed (MD, 0.05; 95% CI, − 0.21 to 0.33; P = 0.83). Meanwhile, the mean value for satisfaction for patient education materials was 1.8 (range, 1.7–2.0) for GPT-4o and 1.7 (range, 1.0–2.0) for o3-mini-high, also revealing no significant difference (MD, 0.16; 95% CI, − 0.03 to 0.37; P = 0.14).

Discussion

We evaluated the comprehensive feasibility of GPT-4o and o3-mini-high in emergency care of gynecologic oncology using real-world cases with a stepwise approach. Both LLMs demonstrated significantly better time efficiency than the physicians, while only GPT-4o showed higher mean total scores in suggesting examinations, final diagnoses, treatment plans, and actual order prescriptions than the physicians. The participating physicians evaluated the clinical recommendations and patient education materials generated by GPT-4o and o3-mini-high as nearly very helpful and very satisfactory.

It is notable that both foundation LLMs provided relevant information to assist clinical decisions for non-refined real-world cases by stepwise optimized prompts, irrespective of reasoning capability, with a significantly shorter time than the physicians. Studies have evaluated the capabilities of LLMs in medicine to suggest differential diagnoses and treatment plans for clinical cases in the United States Medical Licensing Exam and board examinations [12–14]. However, the cases used in those studies were mostly refined and summarized to avoid controversy compared with those in our study. There were also studies examined the accuracy or reliability of LLMs in generating adequate answers to medical questions from general medicine to expert specialties, but those questions were primarily focused on confirming the level of relevant knowledge of LLMs with multiple sentences [15–17]. Using real-world cases, our findings suggest that GPT-4o and o3-mini-high comprehend the non-refined medical texts and successfully convert them to refined context to generate relevant responses quickly, which is critical in emergency care, using proper prompts.

Here, the task resolution speed of GPT-4o and o3-mini-high showed the most significant differences in less experienced residents (P3 and P4) compared to relatively experienced board-certified specialists (P1 and P2) across all steps. This is likely because the less experienced residents needed more time for activities such as searching through various references to compensate for their knowledge gaps; reducing this time is essential in emergency care, where fast decision-making is crucial. Therefore, utilizing GPT-4o and o3-mini-high in the emergency department would especially benefit physicians with relatively less medical knowledge and experience. This would also be helpful for primary care physicians who treat patients in suburban areas with limited professional healthcare resources or in low-income countries.

Interestingly, we found that GPT-4o was superior through Steps 1–3 compared to the physicians, while o3-mini-high was not in this study. Considering that o3-mini-high is a more recent and developed reasoning model, this finding evokes a critical discussion point. On the contrary, our research demonstrated the opposite. We think that the “dual-process theory” of human cognition could explain this phenomenon by providing a framework for understanding the operational mechanisms of current LLMs: System 1 is fast, intuitive, and optimized for pattern-based cognition, while System 2 is slow and optimized for complex logical analysis [18–20]. We suggest that non-reasoning LLMs may operate similarly to System 1, while reasoning LLMs function more like System 2 with “chain-of-thought”. Therefore, GPT-4o could be inherently efficient through direct pattern matching. Moreover, these typical clinical scenarios are likely to be widely represented in its pretraining data, further enhancing the effectiveness of pattern matching. Meanwhile, o3-mini-high might be superior in tasks that require complex logic, such as molecular or multidisciplinary tumor boards for rare clinical cases, but they could face limitations in standardized pattern-based tasks due to higher computational cost, latency, risk of overthinking, and potential accuracy drops in simple tasks from flawed reasoning chains. Recent works have also suggested that “chain-of-thought” can elicit System 2–like behavior in LLMs, generally improving performance on complex tasks while also revealing reasoning–accuracy trade-offs depending on task structure and complexity, and have claimed to adaptively select between two systems according to the task type [21–23]. The case of vesicovaginal and rectovaginal fistula illustrated the risk of overthinking associated with o3-mini-high, which consistently prioritized and selected the most probable final diagnosis from multiple CT findings based on symptoms, even though all findings should be regarded as the final diagnosis. Therefore, the selection of LLMs for clinical assistance should not default to the latest versions or better reasoning capabilities. Instead, it should be based on the specific characteristics of the medical knowledge domain and practice where the assigned LLMs are to be used; the optimal LLM should be chosen based on whether the domain primarily relies on well-established knowledge pattern recognition or complex logical reasoning. Google’s recent non-reasoning MedGemma models (4B text-only and 27B multimodal) are specialized for medical domains, achieving strong performance on medical Question Answering benchmarks despite their relatively small size [24]. This supports our finding that compact, pattern-oriented LLMs can effectively handle well-defined clinical tasks, complementing larger reasoning-focused models.

In addition, our study confirmed that both foundation models, in their current trained states, required no additional specialized training or reinforcement for knowledge in emergency care of gynecologic cancer patients, demonstrating performance comparable to physicians. Nonetheless, the quality of responses from both models can be enhanced by retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and improving usability. Currently, LLMs can be combined with “Agents” that utilize vector databases of specific interests, application programming interfaces such as PubMed, or model context protocol for particular interests to implement RAG [25–28]. Increasing usability of LLMs can be achieved by adopting vision-related multimodal features, such as analyzing real-time computer screen that shows examination results and history of patients. Developing optimized prompts is also necessary for consistent and relevant responses from GPT-4o.

Physicians found the LLMs helpful as they eliminated the need to manage patients from scratch and reduced the likelihood of missing important examination results. In addition, physicians often face difficulties explaining information related to the current state and further plans to the level of patients’ knowledge because of communication-related problems in clinical practice [29]. However, the patient education materials generated by the LLMs tailored to the patients’ knowledge level and relevant to each case were satisfactory to physicians. Therefore, these tailored patient education materials generated by LLMs can further help patients understand their current condition and subsequent treatment plans in real-world setting.

Recent advances highlight how artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded across gynecologic oncology, from diagnosis to surgical workflows, underscoring a broader implementation of AI in clinical practice [30, 31]. Within this evolving landscape, our study provides early evidence on the feasibility of deploying LLMs in gynecologic cancer emergency care, representing a novel extension of AI integration into acute health service settings and aligning with evidence that LLMs could support less experienced residents in time-pressured clinical scenarios [32]. However, further research is needed to establish practical pathways for their safe use. LLMs have shown promise in enhancing clinical decision support and making triage for general populations of patients visiting emergency department more efficient and equitable; however, these models can occasionally produce hallucinations if used without proper physician-in-the-loop safeguards, underscoring the need for robust safeguards [33, 34]. Accordingly, we carefully suggest that deploying LLMs should be used as assistive systems with active human supervision and clear guidelines, so that clinicians verify all LLM-driven suggestions in real time to ensure patient safety. Emerging techniques such as conformal risk control can further support human-in-the-loop deployment by statistically routing outputs to ensure safety constraints at the Application Programming Interface level, offering a practical pathway for auditable control without model retraining [35, 36]. In addition, careful attention should be paid to the potential biases embedded in training data, particularly in the field of gynecologic cancer, where racial, cultural, and socioeconomic underrepresentation is common [37–40]. Such biases can exacerbate existing health disparities and raise ethical and governance challenges, underscoring the need for rigorous pre-deployment validation, physician-in-the-loop safeguards, and clear legal, ethical, and policy frameworks.

Our study has some limitations. It utilized bootstrapping in the analyses, but relied on the assumption that the observed sample is representative of the population. Given the relatively small number of case-level observations in our study, the resampled distributions may not fully capture the true variance, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of the 95% CIs. Moreover, the limited number and non-random selection of test cases reduce the power of statistical comparisons and restrict the generalizability of the results to broader clinical scenarios. Another limitation of the study is the relatively small number of participating physicians, particularly the lack of attending physicians, which may not fully represent the general level of gynecologic cancer emergency care in real-world practice. In addition, the lack of formal validation of the scoring system and the absence of formal inter-rater reliability analysis represent methodological limitations of this study. We also could not assess patients’ satisfaction with education materials due to the retrospective study design. Lastly, we only evaluated GPT-4o and o3-mini-high as representatives of non-reasoning models and reasoning models, respectively; these models may not reflect performance variation of other contemporary or future LLMs. Nonetheless, our study is novel as we evaluated the feasibility of non-reasoning and reasoning LLMs through stepwise optimized prompts with raw examination results and gynecologic cancer emergency case descriptions in real-world. Moreover, we demonstrated that the selection of LLMs for clinical assistance should be based on the characteristics of the required knowledge used in the process. Further prospective multicenter studies should be conducted to confirm our findings as well as clinical effectiveness of LLMs in gynecologic cancer emergency care.

Conclusions

Both GPT-4o and o1-mini-high demonstrated feasibility for emergency care of gynecologic oncology patients, with GPT-4o outperforming physicians in suggesting examinations, treatment plans, and prescribing orders while completing tasks significantly faster. Selection of LLMs for clinical assistance should prioritize domain-specific knowledge requirements over model version or reasoning capability. Further validation through prospective multicenter trials is warranted to confirm our findings and clinical effectiveness.
