Pneumococcal meningitis among hospitalised children after introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine in India: a sentinel hospital surveillance (2019–2022)
Authors: Girish Kumar Chethrapilly Purushothaman, Prabu Rajkumar, Boopathi Kangusamy, Swati Gupta, Leyanna Susan George, Rosemol Varghese, Poovazhagi Varadarajan, Balasubramanian Sundaram, Veeraraja Basavanthappa Sathenahalli, Priya Sreenivasan, Vijay Kumar Guduru, Shikha Malik, Bhadresh R Vyas, Dilip Turbadkar, Milap Sharma, Ghanshyam Saini, Nirbhay Mehta, Bhagat Baghel, Anil Kumar Chaudhary, Nowneet Kumar Bhat, Narender Pal Singh, Kuldeep Singh, Aparna Parmar, Vishal Guglani, Rakesh Sharma, Anubha Srivastava, Amit Garg, Anil Kumar Tiwari, Adil Raza, Ashok Kumar, Reema Nath, Ajanta Sharma, Purbasha Ghosh, Kalpana Datta, Sandip Samanta, Arunabha Sarkar, Devasena Radhakrishnan, Sulochana Putli Bai, Bhavana Jagannatha, Jyothi Rajahamsan

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn 2017 India introduced the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13; Prevenar 13®) in select high-burden sites and in 2021, transitioned to nationwide introduction of 10-valent PCV (PCV10-SII; PNEUMOSIL®). We examined data from a large sentinel paediatric meningitis surveillance network to assess early changes in pneumococcal meningitis among hospitalised children after introduction of pneumococcal vaccine.MethodsChildren (1–59 months) who were hospitalised with suspected meningitis at 32 hospitals across 19 states of India were enrolled in this hospital-based surveillance from 2019 to 2022. Clinical and demographic data were collected. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were tested for Streptococcus pneumoniae using real-time PCR. Serotypes were identified using real-time PCR in TaqMan Array Cards. Cases were categorised based on PCV implementation in their district of residence on their admission date as pre-PCV (before rollout of PCV10-SII/PCV13), early-PCV (<1 year since rollout), or post-PCV (≥1 year since rollout) group.FindingsAmong 12,971 children enrolled, 303 (2.3%) had pneumococcal meningitis; 65.3% were between 1 and 11 months of age, and 60.1% were males. Among 260 children with outcome data, 93.1% recovered, while 6.9% died during hospitalisation. The proportion of children with pneumococcal meningitis decreased from 3.5% pre-PCV to 1.8% during both early- and post-PCV periods. Among serotyped pneumococcal meningitis, the proportion of PCV10-SII serotypes was 41.4% in pre-PCV, 33.3% early-PCV, and 25.9% post-PCV periods. The most common PCV10-SII serotypes were 5, 14, and 6B whereas non-PCV10-SII serotypes were 18C/18B, 8, and 10F.InterpretationDecline in the proportion of PCV10-SII serotype among paediatric pneumococcal meningitis patients represents preliminary signals of PCV impact in India. These findings provide programmatically relevant evidence to inform ongoing evaluation of PCV implementation. Continued surveillance is essential to monitor serotype distribution and pneumococcal meningitis patterns over time.FundingGates Foundation (OPP1188408) and the United Nations Development Programme (00101970).Keywords: Pneumococcal meningitis, Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, Serotypes, PCV10, India

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for evidence of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) impact on meningitis in India using the following terms in combination “pneumococcal vaccines” OR “pneumococcal conjugate vaccine” OR “PCV” AND “meningitis” OR “invasive pneumococcal disease” OR “IPD” AND “impact” OR “effectiveness” OR “PCV effectiveness” AND “child, preschool” OR “Infant” on July 28, 2025, with no date restrictions. We screened 131 titles and excluded publications unrelated to pneumococcal or PCV and included forty studies for screening. The pneumococcal serotypes responsible for invasive diseases differ across geographic locations and time periods. The evidence shows that globally PCV introductions led to decline in pneumococcal disease burden caused by serotypes represented in the introduced PCV formulation, as well as increases in non-vaccine-type disease, also referred to as “replacement disease.” The shifting serotype landscape has driven the development of newer, higher-valent PCVs to ensure broader and more effective coverage against diverse serotypes. However, the search did not find any published study specifically evaluating the impact of PCV introduction on pneumococcal meningitis in India.

Added value of this study

This study provides assessment on the early changes in hospitalised meningitis cases among children following PCV introduction in India, using a sentinel paediatric meningitis surveillance network. The data from this study reflect both earlier targeted use of PCV13 (Prevenar 13®) and national use of PCV10-SII (PNEUMOSIL®) in India. The proportion of children with pneumococcal meningitis decreased from 3.5% pre-PCV to 1.8% during both early- and post-PCV periods. Among serotyped pneumococcal meningitis, the proportion of PCV10-SII serotypes was 41.4% in pre-PCV, 33.3% early-PCV, and 25.9% post-PCV periods. The decrease in the proportion of pneumococcal meningitis due to serotypes covered by the PCV formulation rolled out nationwide are suggestive of an early impact of the PCV program in India.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with global evidence, our findings support the sustained use of PCV in the national immunisation programme in India and globally. The results are particularly relevant for countries that have introduced or are planning to introduce PCV10-SII. Continued surveillance is warranted to monitor the effect of increasing PCV10-SII coverage on serotypes causing pneumococcal meningitis and contribute to the development of future PCVs with optimal serotype coverage.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is a leading cause of bacterial pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis in children.1 Meningitis is the most severe manifestation of pneumococcal disease, with a pooled case fatality ratio of 33.4% among children from low- and middle income countries.2 In 2019, among 1.28 million estimated meningitis globally in children less than five years, 13.3% of the disease and 17.3% of deaths were attributable to S. pneumoniae.3 Data from multi-centric surveillance networks in India also confirm S. pneumoniae as the leading cause of bacterial meningitis among children less than five years.4, 5, 6

Vaccination against S. pneumoniae is a critical and effective intervention to reduce the burden of bacterial meningitis in children.7,8 Pneumococcus has more than 100 capsular serotypes with different invasive potential; the serotypes responsible for the most invasive disease differ across geographic location and time period.9,10 Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) contain combinations of serotype antigens representing predominant serotypes.11 PCV introduction in several countries has shown a marked reduction in pneumococcal disease caused by serotypes represented in the introduced PCV formulation.11, 12, 13 PCV impact arises from both direct effects among vaccinated children and from indirect effects, which help protect the unvaccinated, mediated through decreased carriage and transmission of vaccine serotypes.14 Increases in non-vaccine-type carriage following PCV introduction can result in increases in non-vaccine-type disease, known as “replacement disease”.15 The shifting serotype landscape has driven the development of newer, higher-valent PCVs to ensure broader and more effective coverage against diverse serotypes,15 and highlights the importance of monitoring pneumococcal disease following PCV implementation.16

The inclusion of PCV in India's National Immunisation Program (NIP) marked an important step towards combating pneumococcal diseases among children.17 Starting in 2017, Prevenar 13® (PCV13) was introduced using a three-dose schedule (6 and 14 weeks; booster at 9 months) in select districts in high burden states, with gradual expansion in those areas through 2020.17 In 2021, India achieved nationwide PCV coverage with the introduction of PNEUMOSIL® (PCV10-SII), a WHO-prequalified, indigenously developed, affordable vaccine into the NIP, administered at 6 weeks, 14 weeks and 9 months of age.18 States previously using PCV13 transitioned to PCV10-SII in 2021. Considering the need to document changes in pneumococcal disease following PCV introduction across India, the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) established a meningitis surveillance network comprising 32 tertiary care hospitals and 6 laboratories. The primary objective of the surveillance was to monitor changes in S. pneumoniae serotype distribution in pneumococcal meningitis among children younger than five years of age following the PCV rollout; a secondary objective was to describe the proportion of all suspected meningitis with a pneumococcal aetiology. In this study, we describe the findings from the surveillance network for the period January 2019 to December 2022.

Methods

Hospital-based surveillance began with 24 tertiary care hospitals (designated as sites) and gradually expanded to 32 sites across 19 states in India (Supplementary Figure S1). The timeline of surveillance initiation and expansion along with PCV rollout is summarised in Supplementary Figure S2. Based on the geographic locations, the sites were grouped into four regions (North, South, West, and East) of the country (Supplementary Table S1). Four sites served as regional reference laboratories (RRLs) for PCR testing of specimens: Sawai Man Singh Medical College, Jaipur; King George's Medical University, Lucknow; ICMR National Institute for Research in Bacterial Infections, Kolkata; and ICMR National Institute of Epidemiology, Chennai. The Christian Medical College, Vellore and the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences, Bengaluru, served as the national reference laboratories (NRLs) in the network for confirmation and molecular serotyping of pneumococcal meningitis (Supplementary Figure S1). The Streptococcus Laboratory from the Division of Bacterial Diseases, US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), a global reference laboratory, provided technical support for molecular detection and pneumococcal serotyping at NRLs. All the sites and laboratories in the network followed a common surveillance protocol for case recruitment, data collection, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing, molecular detection and pneumococcal serotyping. Clinical management of cases followed the protocols of the participating hospitals. The Communicable Diseases Division of ICMR served as the administrative and technical coordinator of the surveillance network. ICMR National Institute of Epidemiology, Chennai, coordinated the data management.

Children between 1 and 59 months of age with suspected meningitis admitted in the paediatric wards, intensive care units, or emergency rooms of the participating hospitals were eligible for enrolment. Suspected meningitis was defined as fever of <7 days of duration and presenting with one or more symptoms of meningitis (neck stiffness, bulging fontanel, altered or reduced level of consciousness, prostration, lethargy, convulsion) or with a strong clinical suspicion of meningitis.19 Children with post-operative or post lumbar-puncture meningitis and children with a documented seizure disorder were excluded. Study staff recruited participants within 24 h of admission. Carbonless paper-based Case Report Forms (CRFs) were used to collect clinical and laboratory details. Clinical data included demographic details, symptoms, clinical history, vaccination details (from immunisation records), antibiotic administration details, and outcome. Where available, vaccination cards were reviewed and photocopies attached to study records. Laboratory data comprised details of specimen collection, storage, transportation, and routine hospital laboratory findings, such as culture, leucocyte count, protein, and glucose estimations, if done. To ensure data quality, data were checked for completeness and internal consistency at the site level and during centralised data review. Discrepancies were resolved through source document verification where feasible.

Procedures

CSF specimens were aseptically collected per routine clinical management practice. Each specimen was linked to the corresponding case with a unique identification number. One aliquot (∼200 μL–1 mL) of CSF was prepared for surveillance testing, stored at ≤ −20 °C and shipped to RRLs on dry ice for triplex real-time PCR detection of bacterial meningitis pathogens. At the RRLs, CSF samples were subjected to column-based DNA extraction (Qiagen) and screened for S. pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis using real-time PCR targeting lytA, hpd, and sodC genes, respectively.20

All positive (Ct ≤ 35.0), equivocal (Ct > 35.0 and < 40.0) and 10% of negative CSF specimens were sent to the NRLs for confirmation. CSF with Ct ≤ 37.0 for S. pneumoniae detection were subjected to molecular serotyping using real-time PCR in TaqMan Array Card (TAC) format. The TAC included 75 assays targeting 94 pneumococcal serotypes as individual serotypes or small serogroups, 2 targets to identify pneumococcus (lytA and piaB), and human DNA marker (RNaseP).21,22 The CSF specimens positive for serogroup 18 in the TAC were further discriminated into 18C/18B using the method described by Downs et al.23 S. pneumoniae positive CSF specimens with no serotype identified by TAC were considered to be non-typeable. CSF culture was also performed for all eligible cases as per site laboratory protocols whenever adequate specimen volume was available.

Confirmed bacterial meningitis cases were those testing positive for S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae or N. meningitidis, and pneumococcal meningitis cases were those positive for S. pneumoniae. Suspected cases with data on CSF cell count, sugar, and protein levels were further classified as probable bacterial meningitis if the CSF was turbid, or CSF leucocyte count was >100 cells/mm3, or CSF leucocyte count was 10–100 cells/mm3 accompanied by either an elevated protein level (>100 mg/dl) or a decreased glucose level (<40 mg/dl).19 Suspected and pneumococcal meningitis was described by age group, sex, presenting signs/symptoms, geographic region, educational profile of caregivers, antibiotic exposure, and outcomes. Nutritional status was assessed based on WHO standards and reported as wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age) and underweight (low weight-for-age) using R package “anthro”.24 Severe malnutrition was defined as a weight-for-height z score < -3 using World Health Organisation standards.25

Cases were classified based on the status of PCV implementation in their district of residence on their date of admission: pre-PCV was before rollout of PCV10-SII or PCV13, early-PCV was within 1 year of rollout of either product, and post-PCV was at least 1 year since rollout of either product. The distribution of pre/early/post-PCV cases across the sites is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Cases from early-PCV and post-PCV periods were further classified based on the PCV products used in their district of residence as of their date of admission: districts using PCV13 at the time of admission, districts using PCV10-SII after a period of PCV13 use, and districts using PCV10-SII with no prior PCV13 use.

Serotype coverage, stratified by PCV implementation period, was calculated for PCV10-SII (serotypes 1, 5, 6A, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 19A, 19F, 23F) and for various other PCV formulations, including Synflorix™ (PCV10-GSK; serotypes 1, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F, and 23F), PCV13 (PCV10-GSK serotypes plus 3, 6A, and 19A), Pneubevax 14™ (PCV14; serotypes 1, 3, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 9V, 14, 18C, 19A, 19F, 22F, 23F, and 33F), Vaxneuvance™ (PCV15; PCV13 serotypes plus 22F and 33F), and Prevnar 20® (PCV20; PCV15 serotypes plus 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, and 15B). Since serotypes 7A, 9A, and 18B are not commonly reported from India, unresolved serotypes 7F/7A, 9V/9A, and 18C/18B were assumed to be 7F, 9V, and 18C, respectively.26 Cases with multiple serotypes detected were considered covered by a PCV formulation if any of the serotypes identified were included in that formulation. Cases with missing serotype information (TAC not performed) were excluded from estimations of serotype coverage of PCV formulations.

Among all enrolled meningitis cases older than or equal to 6 weeks with card/record-confirmed vaccination data, the frequency of receipt of at least 1 dose of any PCV product was examined as a crude indicator of PCV uptake in study areas. A child was considered fully vaccinated if the number of doses received is appropriate to his/her age or partially vaccinated if at least one dose was received without completing the schedule.

Statistical analysis

We summarised demographic characteristics, anthropometric indices, vaccination details, clinical characteristics, pre-admission details, outcome, PCV period, and serotypes as frequencies with percentages, and duration of hospitalisation as median with interquartile range (IQR). We compared the proportions of pneumococcal meningitis among all enrolled suspected meningitis cases and PCV10-SII serotypes among all pneumococcal meningitis cases by PCV periods using the Chi-square test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using STATA release 17 and IBM SPSS Statistics 27.

Overall, 303 (2.3%) tested positive for S. pneumoniae; other bacterial aetiologies included H. influenzae (n = 84, 0.6%) and N. meningitidis (n = 6, 0.05%). Categorised by regional zones, the proportion of cases attributable to S. pneumoniae was: 3.8% (110/2924) in the south zone, 3.1% (57/1837) in the east, 2.2% (72/3293) in the west, and 1.3% (64/4917) in the north. Among the 303 pneumococcal meningitis patients, 65.3% were between 1 and 11 months, and 60.1% were male. The most common clinical manifestation was convulsions (observed in hospital or parent-reported; 83.8%), followed by lethargy (63.7%). Among 296 pneumococcal meningitis patients with data on CSF characteristics, 31.4% (n = 93) met criteria for probable bacterial meningitis (Table 1). Of the 285 that underwent CSF culture, 4.9% (n = 14) were pneumococcal culture positive.

Prior antibiotic exposure data were available for 224 (73.9%) pneumococcal meningitis patients; of these, 21.0% (47/224) had received antibiotics within 48 h before admission. Data on in-hospital antibiotic administration were available for 91.4% (277/303) of pneumococcal meningitis cases; among these, 48.0% (133/277) received it prior to CSF collection. Outcome information was available for 260 (85.8%) children with pneumococcal meningitis; 93.1% (242/260) recovered, whereas 6.9% (18/260) died during hospitalisation. Among 43 children (14.2%) with missing outcome data, 93.0% left against medical advice and 7.0% were referred to other hospitals. The median hospitalisation duration was 4.0 days for children who died in-hospital, 11.0 days for those who recovered, 12.0 days for those who left against medical advice, and 29.0 days for those who were referred (Table 1).

By PCV period, the proportion of suspected meningitis with pneumococcal aetiology was 3.5% (141/4083) pre-PCV, 1.8% (58/3235) early-PCV, and 1.8% (104/5653) post-PCV (p-value <0.0001; post-PCV vs pre-PCV). Among 141 pre-PCV cases, 7.1% (n = 10) were from districts that later used PCV13 (followed by PCV10-SII), while 92.9% (n = 131) were from districts that used PCV10-SII only. Among 58 early-PCV cases, 13.8% (n = 8) were from districts currently using PCV13 (during 2019–2021) and 86.2% (n = 50) were from districts using PCV10-SII only. Among 104 post-PCV cases, 34.6% (n = 36) were from PCV13-using districts, 26.9% (n = 28) were from districts using PCV10-SII after PCV13, and 38.5% (n = 40) were from districts using PCV10-SII only (Table 2).

Serotype data was available for 247 (81.5%) children with pneumococcal meningitis, which included nine children with multiple serotypes (Table 3). Serotype information was not available for the remaining 56 (18.5%) patients, as TAC was not performed due to insufficient sample. The proportion of cases due to serotypes covered by PCV10-SII was 41.4% (46/111) in pre-, 33.3% (17/51) in early-, and 25.9% (22/85) in post-PCV cases (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S3) (p-value 0.023; post-PCV vs pre-PCV). The most common PCV10-SII serotypes were 5 (n = 16), 14 (n = 15), and 6B (n = 15), comprising 54.1% of the PCV10-SII serotypes. The major non-PCV10-SII serotypes were 18C/18B (n = 36), 8 (n = 10), and 10F (n = 8), comprising 33.3% (54/162) of the non-PCV10-SII serotypes (Table 3). The serotype coverage of other PCV products was highest for PCV20 (64.0% [71/111] pre-, 56.9% [29/51] early, and 51.8% [44/85] post-PCV). Within each PCV implementation period, PCV13, PCV14, and PCV15 had similar serotype coverage (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S3).

Eight (15.1%) PCV10-SII serotype cases had received one or more PCV doses, including 5 cases with one PCV dose (3 in districts using PCV10-SII [one serotype 6B, one serotype 9V/A, and one multiple serotype 5, 24F/24B] and 2 in districts using PCV13 [one serotype 5 and one serotype 23F]), 1 case with two doses (in a district using PCV10-SII [serotype 6A]), and 2 cases with three doses (in districts using PCV10-SII [one serotype 5 and one serotype 19F]).

Discussion

Data from a national hospital-based meningitis surveillance platform employing TAC-based pneumococcal serotyping showed a reduction in the proportion of suspected meningitis cases with a pneumococcal aetiology, as well as in the proportion of PCV10-SII serotypes among pneumococcal meningitis cases. At this stage, post-PCV data likely reflect the effects of PCV13 implementation in select areas prior to the national-level rollout of PCV10-SII as well as potential early impact of the national use of PCV10-SII, which covers all PCV13 serotypes except 3, 4, and 18C. As PCV10-SII coverage increases, it will be important to continue monitoring serotypes causing pneumococcal meningitis and to provide additional data on PCV10-SII performance in real-world setting.

Globally, most countries have incorporated PCVs into their routine infant immunisation programs, with PCV10-GSK and PCV13 being the most widely used.27 Studies evaluating PCV10-GSK and PCV13 across various countries have consistently demonstrated a robust decline in meningitis due to vaccine-type S. pneumoniae.16,28,29 This study adds to the growing evidence of PCV benefits, showing a reduction in PCV10-SII-type in the post-PCV period in India. This is consistent with findings from studies that reported on the proportion of pneumococcal meningitis cases due to vaccine serotypes before and after PCV introduction such as from Cameroon (PCV13; Pfizer; 62.0%–35.8%),30 Ghana (PCV13; Pfizer; 81.3%–40.0%),31 The Gambia (PCV13; Pfizer; 44.4%–0.0%),32 Mozambique (PCV10-GSK; 84.2%–0.0%).33 We also observed an increase in the proportion of pneumococcal meningitis caused by non-vaccine types, with non-PCV10-SII serotypes rising from 58.6% in the pre-PCV period to 74.1% in the post-PCV period, driven primarily by increases in the relative frequency of serotypes 18C/18B, 8, 19B, and 31. It is important to note that this increase in percent non-vaccine types among cases does not necessarily reflect a rise in the burden of non-vaccine type disease; as vaccine-type disease is prevented, an increasing proportion of the remaining burden will naturally be due to non-vaccine types. Nonetheless, serotype replacement is a well-documented phenomenon following PCV introduction,15 and these findings emphasise the importance of ongoing surveillance to monitor evolving serotype dynamics and consideration of higher valent vaccines with broader serotype coverage. Although the current sentinel surveillance network does not cater to measuring pneumococcal meningitis burden, it provides essential insight into predominant serotypes and early changes following PCV introduction in India.

Despite its important benefits, PCV introduction poses a significant economic burden, particularly for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).17 PCV10-SII, developed by Serum Institute of India in collaboration with global health partners, was designed to be an affordable and accessible pneumococcal vaccine.18,34 The serotypes included were chosen for optimal balance of cost and serotype, coverage relevant to LMICs.10,18,34,35 Although the data from this study reflect both targeted use of PCV13 and national use of PCV10-SII in India, they provide important real-world evidence of the performance of PCV10-SII in the context of a routine immunisation program.

In our study, the coverage of pneumococcal serotypes by PCV10-SII was lower compared to the other WHO prequalified PCVs, including PCV10-GSK and PCV13 as well as 14-valent, 15-valent, and 20-valent PCVs. The increased coverage seen with PCV10-GSK and PCV13 was primarily due to serotype 18C, which is not included in PCV10-SII. Beyond PCV13, the incremental coverage with higher valent PCVs was marginal owing to the lower prevalence of serotypes 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15B, 22F, 33F that are unique to these PCVs. Although India implemented PCV13 in targeted, high-burden districts before the national roll-out of PCV10-SII, serotype 18C remains an important cause of pneumococcal meningitis, and continued surveillance is essential to determine the need for PCV10-SII reformulation or use of vaccines that include other serotypes.

Pneumococcal meningitis, the most severe manifestation of pneumococcal disease, is associated with high mortality and serious long-term complications such as hearing loss, neurological damage, or developmental delays in survivors.36 Though it only represents the tip of the iceberg of overall burden of pneumococcal infections, it is important to prevent pneumococcal meningitis in order to achieve reductions in morbidity and mortality in young children. The clinical outcomes of pneumococcal meningitis in children can be improved by early recognition and referral, strengthening laboratory capacity, adherence to evidence-based antimicrobial treatment guidelines, access to intensive care support, and long-term follow-up for neurological sequelae. The 5.9% case fatality we observed among children who were hospitalised in the surveillance hospitals highlights the severity of pneumococcal meningitis. Of note, only 31.4% (93/296) of confirmed S. pneumoniae meningitis met criteria for probable bacterial meningitis based on CSF appearance, cell count, glucose and protein levels, and only 4.9% (14/285) of the PCR-positive grew S. pneumoniae in culture. Although culture was performed at some of the sites, this information was not consistently available. Surveillance testing results are not available in real-time to guide patient management. Therefore, these data highlight the limitations of standard CSF-based criteria for identifying probable bacterial meningitis in the clinical setting, since pneumococcal meningitis may be missed and considered not likely bacterial, which could lead to sub-optimal treatment and increase the risk for poor outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Cases may have been missed due to various reasons, including lack of access to healthcare, delayed care-seeking, failure to collect CSF from suspect cases, and low volume of CSF collected. Antibiotic exposure before CSF collection might have influenced the S. pneumoniae detection, however DNA-based real-time PCR assays used in this study are more sensitive than culture and less affected by prior antibiotic exposure. The surveillance period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, and during peak COVID-19 waves, temporary reductions in hospital attendance and elective admissions were observed at several sentinel sites, which could have influenced enrolment of suspected meningitis. The surveillance was sentinel, without a defined catchment population, and some enrolment sites were added or dropped over time. Therefore, these data cannot be used to estimate the burden of pneumococcal meningitis in India. Furthermore, the proportion of suspect meningitis due to S. pneumoniae could be impacted by various factors beyond PCV, including increases or decreases in the burden of other meningitis-causing pathogens or variability in threshold for hospital admission for children with signs or symptoms of meningitis. The fraction of cases that left against medical advice may have resulted in underestimation of the case fatality proportion, particularly if those were more severe or had poorer prognosis. The case fatality proportion also did not account for deaths among children with meningitis who died without seeking care. Although TAC was able to detect serotypes in 198 specimens, a relatively high proportion (49/247, 19.8%) were non-typeable by TAC. This non-typeability could be due to method failure to detect low bacterial DNA or genetic variations among existing serotypes, especially changes in the primer and probe binding regions or the presence of serotypes not included in the TAC, which limited the study's ability to provide a comprehensive overview of serotype distribution. For estimating the serotype coverage of PCV formulations, cases with unresolved serogroup 18C/18B were assumed to be 18C based on the relative infrequency of 18B in India; this assumption could have biased towards a higher serotype coverage of PCVs that cover 18C (PCV10-GSK, PCV13, PCV14, PCV15 and PCV20). The post-PCV period primarily reflects the impact of PCV13 in select areas and only a limited number of cases were from districts where PCV10-SII had been in use for over a year. The non-availability of vaccination records, particularly among pneumococcal cases, limited the ability to assess the PCV coverage among the enrolled children. Even among the subset of children for whom PCV vaccination status was available, PCV type (PCV13/PCV10-SII) information was not consistently available to ascertain breakthrough infections.

The findings from this study have important implications for shaping public health strategies against paediatric pneumococcal meningitis in India and other similar LMICs. The decrease in the proportion of PCV10-SII serotypes among paediatric pneumococcal meningitis is suggestive of the early impact of the national PCV program in India. Continued surveillance is required to monitor the effect of increasing PCV10-SII coverage on serotypes causing pneumococcal meningitis. This evidence could inform public health policy and the development of future PCVs with optimal serotype coverage for the Indian context.
