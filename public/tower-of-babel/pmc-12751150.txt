Prevalence and associated risk factors of urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasites infections in communities around the Soum dam agropole, Nanoro, Burkina Faso
Authors: Sountong-Noma Faizatou Sorgho, Berenger Kabore, Marc Christian Tahita, Dramane Zongo, Toussaint Rouamba, Hamidou Ilboudo, Helle Hansson, Hermann Sorgho, Pascal Magnussen, Halidou Tinto, Léon Gueswende Blaise Savadogo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary schistosomiasis and intestinal helminth infections remain a public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa. In Burkina Faso, prevalence varies by region. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal helminth infections and the associated risk factors for transmission.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from October to December 2023 among 807 individuals, aged 6 to 92 years, from five villages around the Soum Dam in the Nanoro area. Transmission-related associated risk factors were precise using a structured questionnaire. Stool samples were analyzed to detect intestinal parasites using the Kato-Katz technique and Formol-Ether/Ethyl Acetate Concentration Technique (FECT). Urine samples were examined for Schistosoma haematobium through urine filtration and microhematuria detection using Haemastix reagent strips.ResultsOut of the 807 individuals enrolled in the study, 84 (10.4%) were found to be infected with at least one parasitic species (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 8.5%–12.7%). Nematode infections were the most prevalent, affecting 47 (5.8%) of participants (95% CI: 4.4%–7.7%), and included Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, Ancylostoma spp., and Enterobius vermicularis. Intestinal protozoa (Entamoeba histolytica/dispar and Entamoeba coli) were detected in 15 (1.9%) of individuals (95% CI: 1.0%–3.3%). Platyhelminth infections were observed in participants, including 19 (2.4%) (95% CI: 1.4%–4.0%) due to cestodes (Hymenolepis nana) and 4 (0.05%) (95% CI: 0.1%–1.4%) due to trematodes (S. haematobium). Overall, people aged 15 years and older were the most infected population. The greatest proportion of infections was due to nematodes, which are transmitted by food and soil (soil-transmitted helminths). The bivariate analysis revealed several important trends, particularly with regard to geographical location, living in Séguédin (Prevalence Ratio (PR) = 0.4; 95% CI: 0.2%–0.9%) and Soala (PR = 0.3; 95% CI: 0.1%–0.9%) was associated with a lower prevalence of parasitic infections.ConclusionThis study showed that the prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasitosis are low among populations living around Soum dam agropole. However, soil-transmitted helminths were the most frequent parasites highlighting the need to improve water, sanitation, and hygiene practices in this area.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12245-y.Keywords: Prevalence, Soil-transmitted helminths, Schistosomiasis, Intestinal protozoa, Agropole, Soum, Burkina Faso

Introduction

Helminths (schistosomiasis, soil-transmitted helminths, intestinal protozoa) infections remain a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries [1]. These infectious diseases are primarily caused by intestinal protozoa (e.g. amoebiasis and giardiasis) or parasitic worms (e.g. schistosomiasis, soil- transmitted helminthiasis) infect more than 1.5 billion people worldwide, leading to 450 million severely ill and 155,000 deaths annually [2]. Lack of access to clean water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), coupled with overcrowding and poverty have been identified as the main factors favouring transmission of these infections [3].

In Burkina Faso, the government’s strategy to establish agropoles around large dams to boost agriculture and food security creates major environmental changes. These altered conditions directly impact human health by shifting patterns of waterborne and vector-borne diseases.

While studies from other hydro-agricultural complexes in Burkina Faso report alarmingly high parasite prevalence rates ranging from 5.3% to 70% for schistosomiasis in the Sourou Valley [4, 5] and exceeding 46% to 80% near the Ziga and Bagré dams, respectively [6]. Reliable epidemiological data specific to the communities surrounding the Soum Dam agropole are scarce, having not been updated for two decades.

This study aims to fill the knowledge gap about urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasitic infections in the communities surrounding the Soum Dam agropole in central-western Burkina Faso, since two decades. The Soum Dam region was specifically selected due to its longstanding history of agricultural activity and the absence of recent surveillance data (since two decades), which prevents effective public health planning. We hypothesize that the long-term environmental modifications created by the dam, coupled with sustained poor WASH infrastructure, have led to high and persistent prevalence of urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasitic infections in the surrounding communities.

Specifically, we aim to determine the current prevalence of these infections and identify the demographic, behavioural, and environmental factors associated with their transmission. Understanding these dynamics is essential for informing public health planning and developing targeted intervention strategies in dam-related agricultural zones.

Methods

Study site

The study was carried out around the Soum agropole within the Nanoro Health District catchment area, located in the Sudan savannah zone in the Centre-West region of Burkina Faso, approximately 85 km from Ouagadougou (the capital city of Burkina Faso) (Fig. 1). The total population of the Nanoro Health District was estimated at 160,000 inhabitants in 2022 living over five rural communes [6, 7]. Health infrastructures of the district comprise twenty-five peripheral health centre (Centre de Santé et de Promotion Sociale), four Medical Centres (CM) and the hospital of district (Centre Médical avec Antenne Chirurgicale Saint Camille de Nanoro). Of particular relevance to this study is the Soum hydro-agricultural development project initiated in 2003 and completed in 2020. This project aims to reduce poverty by boosting food security through advanced farming techniques and better handling of water resources.

Study area map, Nanoro health district, Burkina Faso HDSS/Nanoro/2025

Five villages were included in the study based on their proximity to the dam nearer (Soum and Gouloure), midway (Nazoanga) and far away (Seguedin and Soala) from the dam (Fig. 1).

Socio-economic characteristics of the study area

The main economic activities in the study catchment area are agriculture, in particular food crops (maize, rice) and market gardening (onions, tomatoes, cabbage, eggplants, etc.), as well as fishing, especially around the Soum dam [8]. Agricultural and market garden produce from the Soum dam is sold on a number of local, provincial and national markets. The markets of Nanoro, Soaw, Kordié and Boussé serve as sales outlets for these products. In terms of school facilities, there are several schools in each village, including pre-schools, secondary schools (post-primary) and secondary/technical schools.

Study design and sample size

The study is an ancillary study embedded in the project: Mitigate the effects of climate change on health in Burkina Faso; Acronym CLIMSA, which aims to improve the health of rural populations in Burkina Faso in the context of climate change. A cross-sectional survey was conducted between October to December 2023. The sample size was calculated using the Schwartz formula [9] based on a finite population of approximately 1,700 households. The sampling unit was the household (simple random sampling). Assuming an estimated prevalence of 36% waterborne diseases related to microbial contamination [10], with a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of ± 8.5% and an anticipated non-response rate of 5%, the required sample size was estimated at 122 households per month.

Over three months (October to December 2023), a total of 366 distinct households were surveyed using simple random sampling. In each selected household, a complete listing of all eligible adults and all eligible school-age children was provided by the Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) [11]. One adult and one school-aged child of both sexes were selected using Simple Random Sampling (SRS). This was achieved by assigning a unique number to each eligible member in the respective lists and using a random number generator to select the final participants. Therefore, the sample size was estimated to be at least 366 children and 366 adults, for a total of 732 participants.

Demographic data and samples collection

Prior to sample collection, demographic and relevant health information were obtained using an electronic case report form (eCRF, Additionnal File 1). Each participant received clear instructions regarding the procedures for stool and urine sample collection. During scheduled household visits, each selected individual was asked to provide a fresh stool sample and a mid-morning urine sample. Both samples were collected early in the day to optimize parasite detection. The research team provided pre-labelled, sterile, wide-mouthed plastic containers for sample collection the preceding day of the sample collection. On the collection, a field worker arrived early in the morning to get samples from each participant. Upon collection, the samples were placed in clean, labeled containers, stored in cool boxes with ice packs, and immediately transported to the Clinical Research Unit of Nanoro (CRUN) Laboratory for parasitological analysis.

Laboratory procedures

Stool samples were analysed for the detection of Schistosoma mansoni, soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) and intestinal protozoa. Following WHO guidelines, a single thick smear was prepared using the Kato-Katz technique with a standard 41.7 mg template [12, 13]. Each smear was examined microscopically to identify and quantify helminth eggs. The stool samples were also analysed using the Formol-Ether/Ethyl Acetate Concentration Technique (FECT) [14, 15] to detect protozoa with high sensitivity. This method involves emulsifying stool in formalin, adding ether or ethyl acetate, and centrifuging the mixture. The principle relies on differential sedimentation, where heavier parasitic elements concentrate at the bottom, allowing for clearer microscopic identification in order to obtain the comprehensive results observed in our data.

Urine samples were first screened for S. haematobium using Hemastix reagent strips (Siemens, Healthineers, Germany), a rapid and indirect method for detecting microhematuria [16]. All samples that tested positive for microhematuria were then subjected to confirmation using the standard urine filtration technique [17]. Specifically, 10 mL of urine was filtered through a polycarbonate membrane with a pore size of 12 μm, and the retained material was examined microscopically (Lugol’s coloration was applied) to identify and count S. haematobium eggs.

This integrated diagnostic strategy, which combined the use of the established Kato-Katz technique for intestinal helminths, urine filtration for S. haematobium, and the Formol-Ether Concentration Technique for intestinal protozoa, allowed for the specific detection and quantification of all targeted parasites. While the sensitivity for light-intensity helminth infections may be limited by the use of a single Kato-Katz smear, the combined approach provided a comprehensive assessment of parasite distribution [18].

Statistical analysis

STATA 16.1 software was used for statistical analysis. An initial descriptive analysis was used to characterize the study population, distributed across five villages located around the Soum dam in the commune of Nanoro. Sociodemographic variables were summarized as absolute and relative frequencies (n, %).

The prevalence of parasitic infections was calculated for the entire population, as well as for the main species identified. Results were expressed as percentages, along with 95% confidence intervals (95% Cis). The distribution of infections was analysed by age group (6–15 years,16–45 years, 46–55 years, and ≥ 56 years). A bivariate analysis (Chi-square test) was then conducted to examine the association between individual characteristics and the presence of a parasitic infection. Prevalence ratios (PRs) with their 95% Cis were estimated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The mean age was 30.9 years (standard deviation (SD) = 0.8 years). Participants were recruited from five villages, with the highest number enrolled in Soum. Across all sites, 6–15 years old age group was the most represented. The sociodemographic characteristics of the study population are presented in the Table 1.

Prevalence of S. haematobium infection

S. haematobium eggs responsible for urinary schistosomiasis, were identified in four individuals, corresponding to a prevalence of 0.5% (CI 95%: 0.2%–1.3%) (Table 2) exclusively among people living in the village of Nazoanga and Soala.

Prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections

Among all individuals analysed (N = 807), the overall prevalence of infection by parasite group was 5.8% (95% CI: 4.4%–7.8%) for nematodes, 2.4% (95% CI: 1.5%–3.7%) for cestodes, and 1.9% (95% CI: 1.1%–3.0%) for protozoa. Overall seven species of parasites were identified: Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, Ancylostoma sp, Enterobius vermicularis, Hymenolepis nana, Entamoeba histolytica/dispar, and Entamoeba coli (Table 2). Ascaris lumbricoides was the most frequently detected followed by Hymenolepis nana and Ancylostoma sp. In terms of age distribution, the highest prevalence of parasitosis was observed among people aged ≥ 56 years (12.0% (95% CI: 7.8%-18.1%)) followed by participants aged 46–55 years (11.8% (95% CI: 6.7%-20.1%)). While most parasite species do not show a statistically significant difference in prevalence across age groups (p > 0.05), the prevalence of Ancylostoma sp is significantly different by age (p < 0.05), with older individuals showing a higher rate of infection. Furthermore, just one individual was found to be co-infected with more than one parasitic species, indicating a single case of mixed parasitic infection in the study population.

Bivariate analyses (Table 3) showed no statistically significant associations between parasitic infections and gender or age group. However, individuals aged 16–45 showed trend toward lower risk (PR = 0.7; 95% IC: 0.4–1.2) compare to 06–15 years (Reference). Discussion

This cross-sectional study, carried out in populations living around the Soum dam agropoles in central-western, Burkina Faso, found an overall prevalence of urinary shistosomiasis and intestinal parasitic infections of 10.4% among individuals aged 6 to 92 years. Ascaris lumbricoides was the most prevalent species. This result is significantly lower than the historically high rates (up to 30% to 80%) previously reported in hydro-agricultural complexes in the Sourou, Ziga, and Bagré valleys in Burkina Faso [4–6, 19, 20].

These variations suggest a complex epidemiological scenario. The ecosystem surrounding the Soum dam may differ in terms of vector density (e.g., snails, mosquitoes) [4, 21, 22], water usage patterns, and community awareness, compared to areas like the Sourou Valley where endemicity remains high [23]. Additionally, the low overall prevalence may reflect the cumulative impact of long-standing mass drug administration (MDA) programmes targeting school-age children in Burkina Faso [24, 25]. Chemotherapy is known to reduce parasite load and egg excretion, thereby lowering prevalence. Seasonal factors may also have influenced the results, as data collection occurred during the transition to the dry season (October–December), a period when transmission and water contact may be reduced compared to the peak rainy season. Despite the low overall prevalence, the persistence of species such as A. lumbricoides and Hymenolepis nana indicates that fecal–oral transmission cycles remain active within these communities.

Analysis of associated factors revealed several trends, although the absence of a multivariate model limits the ability to identify independent risk factors conclusively. The significantly lower prevalence observed in Séguédin and Soala compared to Goulouré aligns with previous findings that schistosomiasis prevalence varies considerably by micro-location and proximity to water bodies or infected snail hosts [4, 22, 26]. This underscores the importance of highly localized intervention strategies based on community-specific transmission mapping. An important observation was that more than half of infections (51.2%) occurred in individuals over the age of 15, particularly those aged ≥ 56 years. Current treatment strategies primarily target school-age children, yet these findings suggest that adolescents and adults may play a significant and potentially overlooked role in maintaining the environmental reservoir of infection. Future control programmes should consider expanding treatment campaigns to include adult populations to effectively interrupt transmission.

Interestingly, no significant association was found between the absence of toilets and parasitic infections, which contrasts with established literature linking open defecation to increased risk of intestinal helminth infections [27, 28]. Similarly, while well water use was associated with a higher prevalence rate consistent with contamination risks in sub-Saharan Africa [29, 30] this association was not statistically significant. The lack of a significant link between water contact and infection also contradicts current conclusions on schistosomiasis transmission.

These non-significant findings may suggest that exposure to risk factors such as open defecation, well water use, and water contact is uniformly high across the study population. More likely, they reflect limited statistical power due to the low overall prevalence. With a small number of infected individuals, detecting subtle differences in exposure becomes challenging.

In summary, the low overall prevalence suggests that control efforts have been effective. However, the persistence of infection in specific geographic areas and among older age groups indicates that chemotherapy alone is insufficient for disease elimination. To sustain progress and move toward transmission interruption, complementary strategies are urgently needed [31]. These should include improvements in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), localized surveillance, and expanded MDA targeting adult reservoirs of infection particularly in high-risk environments shaped by the Soum dam.

Limitations

Due to constraints for research and funding, we were unable to recruit a large number of people in the study area. Moreover, this study represents only short-term data, so other studies conducted over a longer period should cover a larger number of participants and other potential factors associated with urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasitic infections in populations living in and around agropoles in different parts of the country. Therefore, the sample size and the duration of the study may have also influenced the reported prevalence. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships. And also the diagnostic approach (use of a single Kato-Katz smear,) which has reduced sensitivity for light infections.

Conclusion

This study provides new data on urinary schistosomiasis and intestinal parasitic infection in population living around the Soum agropole in Nanoro (Burkina Faso). The overall prevalence was relatively low, with soil-transmitted helminthiasis being the most common. These findings support the continuation of mass drug administration but also point to the need for complementary public health strategies, particularly WASH interventions, to sustain progress and move toward elimination of these infections in rural Burkina Faso.
