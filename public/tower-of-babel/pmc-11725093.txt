Introduction to the special issue: A festschrift for Janice Kiecolt-Glaser – At the origin of stress and immunity
Authors: Christopher Fagundes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis special issue of Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology pays tribute to Janice Kiecolt-Glaser's groundbreaking career and marks her well-earned retirement. As a leader in human psychoneuroimmunology (PNI), Kiecolt-Glaser transformed the domain by showing that everyday stressors, along with significant life events, can significantly affect the immune system. Her groundbreaking research on informal dementia caregivers revealed the detrimental effects of chronic psychological stress, including delayed wound healing, virus reactivation, heightened proinflammatory cytokine levels, and weakened vaccine responses. This issue features contributions from her former mentees and collaborators, reflecting the enduring influence of her mentorship and research. Few psychologists in PNI have matched her mentorship legacy, and the included works showcase the ways her guidance has shaped their scientific careers. These articles explore new frameworks for integrating affective science with immune health, interventions designed for couples managing chronic illness, and studies examining the immune consequences of relational dynamics. Personal reflections from her trainees and collaborators highlight Kiecolt-Glaser's unique ability to balance rigorous expectations with unwavering support, fostering their professional growth. Collectively, this special issue illustrates how Kiecolt-Glaser's scholarship continues to advance the field, pushing the boundaries of interdisciplinary inquiry. Her influence on the next generation of scientists ensures that her legacy will remain central to PNI, reinforcing the critical role of relationships in health and disease.Keywords: Festschrift, Psychoneuroimmunology, Health psychology, Mentorship, Legacy

It is with deep gratitude and admiration that I introduce this special issue of Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology, which I have had the privilege to guest-edit in honor of Dr. Janice Kiecolt-Glaser. Dr. Kiecolt-Glaser's pioneering research and exceptional mentorship have shaped the field of psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) and had a profound influence on the careers of her trainees, including my own. Her work revealed how stress—particularly in the context of close relationships—affects immune responses to vaccines, reactivates latent herpesviruses, impairs wound healing, and increases systemic inflammation [[1], [2], [3],3,4]. Her groundbreaking studies on caregiving and marital conflict demonstrated the far-reaching impact of psychological stress on immune function, helping to define the field of PNI and transforming our understanding of the intersection between relationships, stress, and health[3,4].

Collaboration has been a defining element of Jan's career. Her partnership with her late husband, immunologist Dr. Ronald Glaser, gave rise to one of the most influential and productive collaborations between social and biological scientists in modern scientific history. Together, they bridged the fields of psychology and immunology, pioneering the field of human psychoneuroimmunology [5]. When they began their work, the field was predominantly focused on animal research, with the limited human studies emphasizing the impact of extreme life events on immune function [6]. Their groundbreaking research demonstrated that everyday stressors—such as academic exams and marital conflicts—can also have significant effects on immune health. Through meticulous studies across diverse populations, including couples and medical students, Kiecolt-Glaser and Glaser shifted the field's focus from extraordinary stress to the physiological toll of routine challenges [7,8]. Their findings revealed the profound impact of relationship quality on health, insights that continue to shape public health strategies and clinical practice to this day.

Jan's mentorship has been transformative in my own career. She was known for her speed and precision in returning feedback, often providing detailed audiotaped responses that were both direct and constructive. Her feedback was invaluable for my development, helping me grow as both a writer and an independent researcher. Jan's guidance extended beyond research mechanics—she pulled her post-docs into conversations about lab management, budgeting, and navigating the realities of academia. These candid discussions gave me the tools I needed to launch my own lab, and I continue to emulate her balance of high expectations and unwavering support in my mentorship today. One of her most enduring pieces of advice—“Speak English, not psychology”—remains a guiding principle in my own writing and teaching, reminding me of the importance of clarity in communication. The research I conduct today builds directly on Jan's foundational work.

This special issue honors the profound contributions of Janice Kiecolt-Glaser, whose research has redefined the field of psychoneuroimmunology (PNI). The studies featured here not only reflect the core themes of her pioneering work but also push forward new paradigms in stress, immunity, and relational health. Across diverse domains, these articles demonstrate how Kiecolt-Glaser's influence extends through generations of research, driving inquiry into how psychological factors can shape immune function.

One major contribution of Kiecolt-Glaser's legacy is the focus on psychological distress and its physiological consequences, which is extended by Renna and Shrout in “You Can't Spell Distress Without Stress: The Impact of Psychological Distress on Immune Function in Cancer Survivors.” This work continues the investigation into how chronic stress affects the immune system, particularly in vulnerable populations, emphasizing the long-term health risks posed by emotional distress [9]. Their findings resonate with Kiecolt-Glaser's early work, underscoring how the body's immune responses are deeply intertwined with psychological experiences.

Building on the emotional aspects of PNI, Graham-Engeland offers new conceptual frameworks in “Moving Toward Affective Immunology: Investigating Emotions, the Immune System, and Health.” This article proposes novel ways to understand how emotional states regulate immune function, expanding PNI beyond its traditional focus to integrate affective science [10]. Such interdisciplinary approaches reflect the evolution of PNI, showing how Kiecolt-Glaser's work paved the way for more nuanced models that capture the dynamic interplay between mind and body.

The emphasis on relationships, a hallmark of Kiecolt-Glaser's career, is further explored by Robles in “Interpersonal Relationships, PNI, and Health: Seeds in the 1980s, Fruiting Trees Today.” Robles traces the trajectory of Kiecolt-Glaser's early work on marital conflict and immune health, demonstrating how this research continues to inform both theoretical models and practical interventions [11]. His article underscores the importance of social relationships in health, reflecting the sustained relevance of Kiecolt-Glaser's research in contemporary public health strategies.

The enduring impact of Kiecolt-Glaser's work on relationship science is also evident in Gouin and Dymarski's “Couples-Based Health Behavior Change Interventions: A Relationship Science Perspective on the Unique Opportunities and Challenges to Improve Dyadic Health.” Their article builds on her foundational insights into how close relationships influence health behaviors, providing new directions for leveraging relational dynamics to promote health[12]. This focus on dyadic health interventions illustrates how Kiecolt-Glaser's research not only transformed PNI but also continues to shape health behavior change strategies.

Furthering Kiecolt-Glaser's exploration of intimate relationships, Shrout, Renna, Leonard, Friedman, and Miller examine how relationship quality impacts cancer survivorship in “Couples in Breast Cancer Survivorship: Daily Associations in Relationship Satisfaction, Stress, and Health.” Their research builds on Kiecolt-Glaser's work by showing how relationship dynamics affect both psychological well-being and immune function in cancer survivors, emphasizing the importance of relational health in chronic disease management [13].

The profound impact of Janice Kiecolt-Glaser's mentorship is illuminated through personal reflections from those she guided, showcasing how her influence extended far beyond research contributions to shape the careers and personal growth of her mentees. Personal reflections on Kiecolt-Glaser's mentorship and scientific contributions are provided by Christian in “Turning Stress into Success: A Festschrift in Honor of Janice Kiecolt-Glaser.” Christian highlights how Kiecolt-Glaser's guidance shaped her career, offering insights into the balance of high expectations and unwavering support that defined her mentorship style [14]. Heather Derry also offers a reflective piece titled "Writing Tips for Psychoneuroimmunology Trainees: Lessons Learned from Dr. Kiecolt-Glaser." Derry draws from her experiences as a trainee in Kiecolt-Glaser's Stress and Health Lab, sharing insights into the mentorship that shaped her career [15]. She emphasizes the critical importance of developing strong writing habits, a skill Kiecolt-Glaser prioritized alongside scientific rigor. Derry details specific strategies, such as daily writing routines, the use of audiotaped feedback, and the art of concise, clear communication—techniques that remain invaluable for trainees navigating academic research. These reflections serve as a reminder of the importance of mentorship in scientific careers, reinforcing the idea that Kiecolt-Glaser's influence extends beyond her published work to the personal and professional development of her mentees.

The impact of Kiecolt-Glaser's mentorship also manifests in the next generation of research, as seen in Madison's “A Social Science: Using Psychoneuroimmunology Principles to Promote Career Longevity, Productivity, and Meaning.” [16]. Madison's work reflects the values instilled by Kiecolt-Glaser—balancing rigorous scientific inquiry with attention to personal well-being and career sustainability. This focus on translating scientific principles into practical strategies for professional growth exemplifies how Kiecolt-Glaser's legacy extends beyond research findings to shape the lives of those she mentored.

Malarkey's “Psychoneuroimmunology and the Research of Janice Kiecolt-Glaser: It Informs Self-Care and the Practice of Medicine” reflects on their long-standing collaboration and the clinical significance of Kiecolt-Glaser's work [17]. His article provides a personal perspective on how her research influenced clinical practice, underscoring the translational impact of her findings on self-care and healthcare delivery. This piece highlights the practical implications of PNI research, demonstrating how Kiecolt-Glaser's insights continue to shape both research and practice.

Bennett's “The Post-Cartesian Dilemma: Reuniting the Mind and Body Through Psychoneuroimmunology” reflects on the integration of psychological and biological perspectives that has defined Kiecolt-Glaser's work [18]. Bennett's article serves as a call to further bridge the mind-body divide in scientific research, echoing Kiecolt-Glaser's commitment to holistic approaches in understanding health.

Wilson's “Is Age More Than a Number? Accounting for Adult Development and Aging in the Study of Psychoneuroimmunology, Stress, and Health” examines how age-related changes interact with stress and immune function, inspired by Kiecolt-Glaser's research on aging [19]. This article highlights the importance of considering developmental changes in PNI research, continuing Kiecolt-Glaser's tradition of studying nuanced factors that influence health outcomes across the lifespan.

Expanding on Kiecolt-Glaser's focus on caregiving, Heffner's “Caring About the Family Caregiver: The Impact of Caregiving Stress on Immune Health” emphasizes the unique health challenges faced by family caregivers [20]. This article continues Kiecolt-Glaser's line of inquiry by highlighting how the chronic stress of caregiving compromises immune function, contributing to the growing literature on the health risks associated with caregiving roles. Heffner's work reinforces the need for interventions that address both the physical and emotional needs of caregivers, a theme that was central to Kiecolt-Glaser's research.

Finally, research from my lab, led by Vincent Lai, explores how social support buffers the negative effects of marital distress on caregivers. Our study, titled “Perceived Partner Responsiveness Alters the Association Between Marital Distress and Well-Being in Dementia Spousal Caregivers,” extends Kiecolt-Glaser's work on caregiving by investigating how relational dynamics influence health outcomes among caregivers of individuals with dementia [21]. This contribution reflects the continued relevance of Kiecolt-Glaser's insights in addressing the complexities of caregiving stress and well-being.

Through these contributions, this special issue highlights how Jan's work has inspired new generations of researchers to push the boundaries of PNI. Her commitment to integrating trainees into every aspect of research—from study design to lab management—has prepared many of us to lead our own labs and mentor future generations. This special issue is more than a celebration of Jan's extraordinary career; it is a testament to the enduring impact of her research and mentorship. Her legacy will continue to shape the field of PNI for years to come, carried forward by those of us privileged to have learned from her.

I am honored to have had the opportunity to edit this special issue, which reflects the depth and breadth of Jan's influence. Her research, mentorship, and collaborative spirit will continue to inspire my own work and the work of so many others in the years ahead.
