Bayesian Networks and Proof-Nets: the proof-theory of Bayesian Inference
Rémi Di Guardia, Thomas Ehrhard, Jérôme Evrard, Claudia Faggian

Abstract
We study the correspondence between Bayesian Networks and graphical representation of proofs in linear logic. The goal of this paper is threefold: to develop a proof-theoretical account of Bayesian inference (in the spirit of the Curry-Howard correspondence between proofs and programs), to provide compositional graphical methods, and to take into account computational efficiency. We exploit the fact that the decomposition of a graph is more flexible than that of a proof-tree, or of a type-derivation, even if compositionality becomes more challenging.



1
Introduction

Bayesian Networks
[
24
]
are a prominent tool for probabilistic reasoning, as they are able to express
large probability distributions in a way which is compact (
factorized representation
) and which allows for efficient inference (
factorized computation
).

In this paper we propose a proof-theoretical account of Bayesian Networks and of factorized inference, via Linear Logic
[
12
,
11
]
, namely its
graph syntax—proof-nets—which embeds
cut-elimination
(and its dual, cut-expansion) as a graph-rewriting process.
The benefits of an approach bringing together Bayesian Networks and the rich toolbox of a proof-theory which is intrinsically resources-aware, is to accommodate both compositional, graphical reasoning, and efficient computations.

In the setting of categorical probability theory, a rich body of work pioneered by
[
1
,
17
,
18
]
has already disclosed a deep connection between Bayesian Networks and logic, bringing a compositional and structured approach into probabilistic reasoning (an in-depth treatment is in
[
19
]
). A key ingredient here is the graphical calculus
of string diagrams, which provides a
uniform and expressive language for formal diagrammatic reasoning, interpreted into monoidal categories.

One aspect which is generally missing in the categorical approach is the space and time consumption of probabilistic reasoning, which however is a strong motivation behind the introduction
[
23
]
and development of Bayesian Networks.
Recent work rooted in Linear Logic
[
5
,
6
,
8
]
is bringing a cost-aware perspective into semantics. In particular,
[
8
]
introduces a semantical framework that integrates the efficiency of Bayesian networks with the compositional nature of type systems, by equipping (a linear logic variant of)
\lambda
-terms with a semantics based on
factors
, the very same mathematical structures underlying BNs and their inference algorithms.

With the proof-nets formalism, we aim at unlocking the best of both approaches: efficiency and graphical reasoning. Along the way, we gain a
foundational understanding, where the cornerstone of proof-theory, namely
cut-elimination
(and its dual, cut-expansion), has a prominent role.

Factorized representation and factorized computation.

A joint distribution is a
global
function involving many variables.
A common way to deal with a complex function is to factorize it as a product of
local
functions, each of which depends on a subset of the variables. In the setting of Bayesian Networks, factorization involves both the representation and the algorithms for (exact) inference.

In proof-theory, the natural way to
factorize a proof
in smaller components is to factorize it in sub-proofs which are then
composed via cut
. We follow exactly this way.

In section
8.1
, we
factorize
a proof-net in the composition of smaller nets, whose interpretation has a smaller cost (
Section

8.2
).

Graphical reasoning.

In
Section

7
we demonstrate graphical reasoning by providing
an extremely simple (diagrammatic) proof of the soundness of d-separation, a well-known graphical criterion to establish conditional independence among random variables.

Graph Decomposition and Graph Compositionality

Both to achieve an efficient factorization and in graphical reasoning, we exploit the fact that the decomposition of a graph is much more flexible than that of a proof-tree (or of a type-derivation). The challenge we face is that (efficient) compositionality is less immediate (
Theorem

5.2
), and associating a proof-tree to the graph decomposition is also non trivial (
Section

8.3
).

Cost.

In general, the cost of
actually computing
the semantics explodes when taking a categorical approach, because the product
\otimes
behaves like the tensor product of matrices (see
[
8
]
for examples);
computing the semantics of
n
binary random variables easily leads to intermediate
computations whose size is much larger than
2^{n}
, the size of the
full
joint distribution. The efficiency of a factors-based semantics (as in Bayesian Networks, and as in
[
8
]
) lies in a definition of product (the
factors product
) which
is not
behaving like the tensor product of matrices.

Related work.

We have already mentioned above the most relevant literature related to our work.
Here we briefly comment on the papers which are more technically related.

The syntax of proof-net with boxes is introduced in
[
5
]
, however while the syntax allows the encoding of Bayesian Networks, it does not characterize them (a proof-net does not necessarily correspond to a Bayesian Network). We are inspired by that paper, which advocates cost-awareness in the computation of the semantics. The approach in
[
5
]
is to use inference algorithms to efficiently compute the denotation of any proof.
In this paper, we follow a different directions: we focus on the proof-nets, and on inference-as-interpretation where the data structure which supports the computations is the proof-net itself.

We adopt the factor-based semantics introduced in
[
8
]
; while we inherit most of their results, our notions of “component” and “compositionality” are stronger (how we explain in
Section

5.2
), yielding to
Theorem

5.2
.

2.2
The language of Bayesian Reasoning

Let us briefly revise the language of Bayesian modeling that we use.
For more details, we refer to
[
3
]
for a concise presentation, and to standard texts for an exhaustive treatment
[
24
,
4
,
22
]
.

Bayesian methods provide a formalism for reasoning about partial beliefs under
conditions of uncertainty. Since we cannot determine for
certain the state of some features of interest, we settle for determining how
likely
it is that a particular feature is in a particular state.
Random variables (r.v.s) represent features of the system being modeled.
A r.v. can be seen as a
name
for an atomic proposition (
e.g.
“Wet”) which assumes values from a set of states (
e.g.

\{\mathtt{t},\mathtt{f}\}
). The system is modeled as
a joint probability distribution
on all possible values of the variables of interest – each instantiation representing a possible state of the system.

Random Variables.

We adopt the standard convention of capital letters (
e.g.

X,Y
) denoting random variables, while lowercase letters (
e.g.

\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y}
) are particular fixed
values
of those variables,
i.e.

\mathtt{x}
an instantiation of the r.v.
X
. As standard,
\Pr(x)
stands for
\Pr(X=x)
.
For simplicity, random variables are here taken to be binary, with

\mathsf{Val}(X)=\{\mathtt{t},\mathtt{f}\}
– the generalization to any discrete r.v. is straightforward.

A finite set of r.v.s
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
defines a “compound” r.v. whose value set
\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{X})
is the Cartesian product

\mathsf{Val}(X_{1})\times\dots\times\mathsf{Val}(X_{n})
. A tuple in the cartesian product is an instantiation of
\mathbf{X}
, denoted by
{\mathbf{x}}
.

Names.

Given a countable set
Names
, we associate to each name
X\in\textsf{Names}

a
finite
set of values, denoted by
\mathsf{Val}(X)

(typically
\mathsf{Val}(X)=\{\mathtt{t},\mathtt{f}\}
). From now on, we silently identify a name
X
with the pair
(X,\mathsf{Val}(X))
,
which effectively defines a
random variable
.

Queries to a probabilistic model.

Given a probabilistic model
\Pr(\mathbf{X})
, and some variable of interest
\mathbf{Y}\subset\mathbf{X}
, a typical query is the marginal probability
\Pr(\mathbf{Y})
or
\Pr(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}={\mathbf{z}})
, the probability of
\mathbf{Y}
, given evidence
{\mathbf{z}}
for
\mathbf{Z}
.
The former is obtained by
summing out
the r.v.s which are not relevant; the latter
via
Bayes’s rule
, which leads us to compute two
marginals
,
\Pr(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
and
{\Pr(\mathbf{Z})}
, because
\Pr(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z})=\frac{\Pr(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})}{\Pr(\mathbf{Z})}
.

2.3
Bayesian Networks, formally

The challenge of Bayesian reasoning is that
a joint probability distribution is usually too large to be feasibly represented explicitly. For example, a joint probability distribution over
64
binary random variables, corresponds to
2^{64}
entries.

Bayesian Networks represents a joint probability distribution in a compact way via a
factorized representation
, obtained by associating with each node in the DAG a
conditional probability table (CPT). The semantics of BNs is revised in
Section

5.1
.

A
Bayesian Network

\mathcal{B}
over the set of r.v.s
\mathbf{X}
is a pair
(\mathcal{G},\Phi)
where:

•

\mathcal{G}
is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over the set of nodes
\mathbf{X}
.

•

\Phi
assigns, to each variable
X\in\mathbf{X}
a
conditional probability table (a CPT)
for
X
given its parents.

3.4
Bayesian Networks and Bayesian Proof-Nets

We prove that every Bayesian Network is associated to a positive Bayesian proof-net and vice versa.
To formalize this result, we need the notion of polarized order.

The polarized order.

Let

We write

(Polarized correctness

Assume that

Then

5.1
Factors, and the semantics of Bayesian Networks

Inference algorithms rely on basic operations on a class of functions
known as
factors
, which generalize the notions of probability distribution and of conditional distribution. Factors will be the key ingredients also in our semantics.

Definition 5
(Factor)

A
factor

\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi}
over a set of r.v.s
\mathbf{X}

is a function
\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi}:\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{X})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

mapping each tuple
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbf{X}
to a non-negative real.

Letters
\phi,\psi
range over factors.
When
\mathbf{X}
is clear from the context, we simply write
\phi
(omitting the superscript
\mathbf{X}
); then
\mathsf{Nm}(\phi)
denotes
\mathbf{X}
.
In the literature about BNs,
\phi({\mathbf{x}})
is often written
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}
. We adopt this convenient notation in explicit calculations.

Factors come with two important operations: sum (out) and product. Product of factors is defined in such a way that only “compatible“ instantiations are multiplied.

Given a subset
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{X}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{x}}|_{\mathbf{Y}}
the restriction of
{\mathbf{x}}
to
{\mathbf{Y}}
(so,
{\mathbf{x}}|_{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{Y})
).
Given two sets of names
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, the instantiations
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{X})
and
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{Y})

are compatible
(
{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{y}}
for short) whenever
{\mathbf{x}}|_{\mathbf{X}\cap\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{y}}|_{\mathbf{X}\cap\mathbf{Y}}
,
i.e.
they agree on the common names.

•

The
sum out
of
\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\mathbf{X}
from
\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi}
is a factor
\sum_{\mathbf{Z}}\phi
over
\mathbf{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Z}
, defined as:

\left(\sum_{\mathbf{Z}}\phi\right)({\mathbf{y}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{Z})}\phi({\mathbf{z}}\,{\mathbf{y}})

•

The
product
of
\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi_{1}}
and
\overset{\mathbf{Y}}{\phi_{2}}
is a factor
\phi_{1}\odot\phi_{2}
over
\mathbf{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{X}\cup\mathbf{Y}
, defined as:

(\phi_{1}\odot\phi_{2})({\mathbf{z}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\phi_{1}({\mathbf{x}})\phi_{2}({\mathbf{y}})\quad\mbox{where ${\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbf{X}}$ and ${\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbf{Y}}$.}

We denote
n
-ary products by
\bigodot_{n}\phi_{n}
. We denote by
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{Y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\overset{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{1}}
the factor over the set of names
\mathbf{Y}
, sending every tuple of
\mathsf{Val}(\mathbf{Y})
to
1
. Observe that
\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi}\odot\overset{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{1}}=\overset{\mathbf{X}}{\phi}
if
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{X}
.
Factors over an empty set of variables are allowed, and called trivial. In particular, we write
\mathbf{1}_{\emptyset}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\overset{\emptyset}{\mathbf{1}}
for
the trivial factor assigning
1
to the empty tuple.
Product and summation are both commutative, product is associative, and—crucially—they distribute
under suitable conditions
:

\text{If }\mathbf{Z}\cap\mathsf{Nm}(\phi_{1})=\emptyset\text{ then }\sum_{\mathbf{Z}}(\phi_{1}\odot\phi_{2})=\phi_{1}\odot\left(\sum_{\mathbf{Z}}\phi_{2}\right)

(1)

This distributivity is the key property on which exact inference algorithms rely.
CPT’s being factors, they admit sum and product operations. Please notice that the result of such operations is not necessarily a CPT, but, of course, it is a factor.

Remark 3
(Cost of Operations on Factors)

Summing out any number of variables from a factor
\phi
demands
\mathbf{O}\left(2^{w}\right)
time and space, with
w
the number of variables over which
\phi
is defined.
Multiplying
k

\mathbf{O}\left(k\cdot 2^{w}\right)

time and space, where

is the number of variables in the resulting factor.

5.2
Semantics of Bayesian proof-nets

The semantics of a
bpn

\mathcal{R}:\Delta
is given in a similar way to that of a Bayesian Network.

Semantics of boxes.

We have seen that
to each (name of an) atom we associate a boolean r.v., and to each box
\mathtt{b}^{X}:X^{+},Y^{-}_{1},\dots Y^{-}_{k}
we associate

\Pr(X|Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})

\llparenthesis{\mathtt{b}^{X}}\rrparenthesis\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\phi^{X}

Semantics of a

A positive proof-net
\mathcal{R}:\Delta
defines a marginal distribution over
\mathsf{Nm}(\Delta)
.
The product of the CPT’s associated to the boxes is the joint probability distribution of the underlying model;
finally, the sum out all the names not appearing in the conclusion provides the desired marginal distribution.

Definition 6
(Semantics of
bpn
’s)

Let
\mathcal{R}:\Delta
be a
bpn

\llparenthesis{\mathcal{R}}\rrparenthesis\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\mathbf{X}-\mathsf{Nm}(\Delta)}\left(\bigodot_{\mathtt{b}\in\mathsf{Boxes}(\pi)}\llparenthesis{\mathtt{b}}\rrparenthesis\right)

for
\mathbf{X}=\bigcup_{\mathtt{b}\in\mathsf{Boxes}(\pi)}\mathsf{Nm}(\mathtt{b})

Remark 4

If
\mathcal{R}
is a proof-net such that
\mathsf{Boxes}(\pi)=\emptyset
, then
\llparenthesis{\pi}\rrparenthesis={\mathbf{1}}_{\emptyset}
.

5.3
Invariance and compositionality

Such a definition of the semantics has two clear advantages:

•

the correspondence with Bayesian Networks semantics is immediate, yielding an efficient representation of the probabilistic model;

•

the invariance of the semantics via reduction is immediate, because both the boxes and the conclusions are invariant.

The crucial question however is
compositionality
.
It is common in denotational semantics to define the interpretation inductively; compositionality is then intrinsic, while the difficulty is to prove that the semantics is invariant via reduction and expansion.
By adopting a definition which is global, invariance is for free, but compositionality needs to be proved.

Is such a semantics compatible with a modular definition, in terms of components?
The answer is positive, and in a stronger sense then in previous work
[
8
]
, where compositionality follows the inductive, tree-like definition of the type derivation.

By

Composition is obtained by

Conditional independence is a fundamental property in probability theory.
