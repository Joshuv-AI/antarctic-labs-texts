Prospects for relic neutrino detection using nuclear spin experiments Preprint: CPPC-2025-06
Yeray Garcia del Castillo, Giovanni Pierobon, Dipan Sengupta, Yvonne Y. Y. Wong

Abstract
Direct detection of the cosmic neutrino background (C ν \nu B) remains one of the most formidable experimental challenges in modern physics. In this work, we extend recent studies of C ν \nu B-induced coherent transitions in polarised nuclear spin ensembles. Adopting an open quantum system framework, we model coherent neutrino effects in large spin ensembles using a Lindblad master equation that also incorporates realistic experimental imperfections such as local dephasing and imperfect polarisation. We solve the Lindblad equation numerically by way of a fast and computationally inexpensive method that can be extended to an arbitrarily large number of spins. Using our numerical solutions, we forecast the sensitivities of future experiments such as CASPEr to the local C ν \nu B overdensity parameter δ ν \delta_{\nu} . Our findings indicate that a CASPEr-like experiment, though primarily aimed at axion dark matter search, could also constrain the C ν \nu B overdensity to δ ν ∼ 10 9 − 10 11 \delta_{\nu}\sim 10^{9}-10^{11} in configurations achievable by currently planned experimental efforts, and down to δ ν ∼ 10 7 \delta_{\nu}\sim 10^{7} in the most optimised scenario. While C ν \nu B detection remains out of reach in the foreseeable future, our results highlight the potential of using quantum sensing to probe fundamental physics.

Consider a spin-
1/2

We assume coherence over nuclear distances, allowing us to treat the fermion as a point-like particle and neglect its internal structure. II.1
Coherent regime

An inspection of the form factor (
11

R

q

Since the incoming neutrinos follow a momentum distribution
N_{i}(p^{0})
, the momentum transfer
\vec{q}=\vec{p}_{i\pm}(\vec{p})-\vec{p}
is also dictated by the distribution. The coherence properties must therefore be understood in terms of the typical momentum transfer
{}\vec{q}_{\rm typ}
extracted from this distribution.
When the condition
q_{\rm typ}R\ll 1
is satisfied, we see in Eq. (
11

j_{1}(q_{\rm typ}R)\approx q_{\rm typ}R/3
, leading to
F_{\pm}\sim{\cal O}(N^{2})
.
This condition can always be fulfilled if the typical neutrino momentum
p_{\nu}
itself is small compared with the inverse system size, i.e.,
p_{\nu}R\ll 1
. For relic neutrinos with a typical incoming momentum of
p_{\nu}\sim 3.15\,T_{\nu,0}\simeq 5.3\times 10^{-4}~\mathrm{eV}\sim 27~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}
, the condition
p_{\nu}R\ll 1
therefore requires a very small
R\ll 0.04~\mathrm{cm}
. However, even for the larger-size spin samples to be considered in this work, e.g.,
R\sim{\cal O}(1)
cm, where formally
p_{\nu}R\gg 1
, it is still possible to fulfil the broader condition
q_{\rm typ}R\ll 1
and hence maintain coherence across the sample in some limits. Specifically, the typical C
\nu
B kinetic energy is much larger than the energy splittings
\omega_{0}\sim 10^{-8}
eV considered in this work. In this limit, the typical momentum transfer is
q_{\rm typ}\approx 2p_{\nu}\sin(\theta/2)
, where
\theta
is the angle between the incoming and outgoing neutrino. Thus, we see that coherence can be maintained for those scattering angles satisfying
\theta\ll(p_{\nu}R)^{-1}
.

In terms of the total excitation and de-excitation rates (
9
), the
p_{\nu}R\gg 1
limit is effectively equivalent to demanding that only
outgoing momentum directions falling in the range
\cos((p_{\nu}R)^{-1})<\mu<1
contribute to the
N^{2}
enhancement. Thus, evaluating the
\mu
-integral immediately yields

\Gamma_{\pm}\approx\frac{N^{2}}{16p^{2}_{\nu}R^{2}}\,\gamma_{\pm},

(12)

which we note is suppressed by a factor
\sim(2p_{\nu}R)^{-2}
relative to the naïve coherent rate. The typical momentum transfer in this regime is
q_{\rm typ}\sim\omega_{0}m_{\nu}/p_{\nu}
. This will be the case of interest in our study and was first introduced in Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
as an analogy to the Rayleigh-Gans regime
1925AnP…381…29G
. See Appendix
B
for details.

As a concrete example, consider a spin ensemble composed of liquid
129
Xe characterised by
(A,Z)=(129,54)
and a spin density of
n_{s}\sim N/R^{3}\sim 10^{22}~\text{cm}^{-3}
. In this case, assuming an energy splitting
\omega_{0}=10^{-8}~\text{eV}

and a standard C
\nu
B with three normally-ordered masses summing to
\sum m_{\nu}=0.15~\text{eV}
,
the total excitation rate of the spin ensemble evaluates to

\Gamma_{\pm}^{\rm SM}\simeq 6\times 10^{-4}~\text{Hz}\left(\frac{R}{1~\text{cm}}\right)^{4},

(13)

where the
R^{4}
scaling represents the aforementioned forward limit of the

enhancement

.
The dependence of the total interaction rate (

) on the system parameters, including
\omega_{0}
and the sample size

, is illustrated in the left panel of Fig.

III
An open quantum system

Although our full system comprises a spin ensemble
S
and a relic neutrino bath
B
that interact with each other, in practice we are interested only in the dynamics of the former. To this end, we adopt the framework of open quantum systems, which assumes unitary evolution of the density matrix of the full system,
\rho=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}
, under a total Hamiltonian

H=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{\rm int}\,,

(14)

where
H_{S}
and
H_{B}
denote, respectively, the free Hamiltonian of the spin ensemble and of the bath, and
H_{\rm int}
describes their interaction. Tracing out the bath degrees of freedom, the evolution of the reduced density matrix
\rho_{S}
is described by the Lindblad master equation
Lindblad:1975ef
;
Gorini:1975nb
;
Manzano:2020yyw
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}[H_{S}+H_{LS},\rho_{S}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}{\cal D}_{{\cal O}_{k}}[\rho_{S}(t)]\,,

(15)

where the so-called Lamb-shift Hamiltonian
H_{LS}
is a small hermitian correction to
H_{S}
arising from
H_{\rm int}
, and

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\cal O}[\rho_{S}(t)]

\displaystyle={\cal O}\rho_{S}{\cal O}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{{\cal O}^{\dagger}{\cal O},\rho_{S}\}

(16)

is the Lindblad super-operator, which acts on the reduced density matrix and describes the
dissipative
effects of the system-bath interaction. Dissipation can occur through multiple channels, each specified by its own operator
{\cal O}_{k}
and the associated interaction rate
\gamma_{k}
.

The Lindblad equation (
15
) serves well under the condition of weak coupling of the system to a large bath with no memory and when the Larmor frequency
\omega_{0}
is much larger than the interaction rates. These conditions enter the derivation of Eq. (
15
) at various stages via the so-called Born, Markovian, and rotating-wave/secular approximation, which are discussed in more detail in Appendix
C
. See also, e.g., Ref.
Manzano:2020yyw
for a general introduction to the Lindblad formalism.

III.1
The master equation

In the case of a single spin-
1/2
interacting with a neutrino bath, the Hamiltonians of the spin system and its interaction with the neutrinos,
H_{S}^{(1/2)}
and
H_{I}^{(1/2)}
, are given in Eq. (
4
). The corresponding Lindblad equation for the spin density matrix in the interaction picture takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{(1/2)}_{S}=

\displaystyle-{\rm i}\Delta[J_{z},\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}]

(17)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{-}(J_{-}\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}J_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{+}J_{-},\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}\})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{+}(J_{+}\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}J_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{-}J_{+},\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}\})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{z}(J_{z}\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}J_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{z}^{2},\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}\})\,,

where
\cdot\equiv{\rm d}/{\rm d}t
, and we have highlighted
\rho^{(1/2)}_{S}
as the reduced density matrix for a single two-level system. We refer the reader to Appendix
C
for an
ab initio
derivation of this equation.

Inspecting Eq. (
17
), we see firstly an energy shift due to the
\Delta
, which is a combination of the
{\cal O}(G_{F})
, Stodolsky effect that is present only when the neutrino-antineutrino asymmetry is non-zero, and
{\cal O}(G^{2}_{F})
terms from the Hermitian part of the Lindblad equation. The
J_{\pm}
terms and the corresponding coefficients
\gamma_{\pm}

represent the non-Hermitian interaction of the spin with the neutrinos, causing the spin to either excite or de-excite via inelastic scattering, analogously to absorption and emission in quantum optics.
7
7

7

While we draw parallels between our system and quantum optics and introduced terminology commonly used in the latter subject, we always use “excitation” and “de-excitation” to refer to the effects of the C
\nu
B.

The
J_{z}
/
\gamma_{z}
term is, on the other hand, energy conserving and represents the effect of dephasing.
The computations of the coefficients
\gamma_{\pm,z}
can be found in Appendix
D
. Their general forms including both neutrino scattering and pair emission/absorption processes can be found in Eqs. (
122
), (
123
), and (
124
), and, for
\gamma_{\pm}
, match the heuristic single-spin excitation and de-excitation rates (
6
) under the same settings.

The single-spin equation (
17
) can be generalised to
N
spins, where the Hilbert space of the spin ensemble is now
\rho_{S}=\otimes^{N}\rho^{(1/2)}
and Eq. (
8
) gives the corresponding Hamiltonian. As we show in Appendix
E
, omitting the Hermitian part, the Lindblad equation for the
N
two-level systems reads

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{-}(J^{\alpha}_{-}\rho_{S}J^{\beta}_{+}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{J^{\alpha}_{+}J^{\beta}_{-},\rho_{S}\})

(18)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha\beta}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{+}(J^{\alpha}_{+}\rho_{S}J^{\beta}_{-}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{J^{\alpha}_{-}J^{\beta}_{+},\rho_{S}\})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha\beta}\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{z}(J^{\alpha}_{z}\rho_{S}J^{\beta}_{z}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{J^{\alpha}_{z}J^{\beta}_{z},\rho_{S}\})\,,

where
\alpha
and
\beta
run over all the spins of the system, and the angular operators
J_{\pm,z}^{\alpha}
act only on the spin
\alpha
. The coefficients
\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\pm,z}

again encode information of the spin-bath interaction, but now correlate the interaction at spatial position
\vec{x}_{\alpha}
to that at
\vec{x}_{\beta}
. For instance, assuming only inelastic neutrino-spin scattering, the
\gamma_{\pm}^{\alpha\beta}
coefficients read

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}^{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle 2\,G_{F}^{2}\pi g_{A}^{2}\,\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;e^{-{\rm i}\Delta\vec{x}_{\alpha\beta}\cdot(\vec{p}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime})}

(19)

\displaystyle\times\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}\pm p^{\prime 0}\mp p^{0})

\displaystyle\times\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]\,.

Depending on the extent to which correlations can be maintained across spatial distances
\Delta\vec{x}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\vec{x}_{\alpha}-\vec{x}_{\beta}
of the spin ensemble, qualitatively very different dynamics can arise. Two contrasting limits are the fully-coherent and the fully-incoherent regimes.

Fully-coherent regime

Full coherence is achieved when
e^{-{\rm i}\Delta\vec{x}_{\alpha\beta}.(\vec{p}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime})}\approx 1
across the whole spin ensemble, or, equivalently, when the condition
qR\ll 1
is satisfied for momentum transfer
q
and a sample size
R
. In this limit, the interaction coefficients are effectively the same for all spin pairs. The Lindblad equation then reduces to

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}=

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}({\cal J}_{-}\rho_{S}{\cal J}_{+}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{{\cal J}_{+}{\cal J}_{-},\rho_{S}\})

(20)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{+}({\cal J}_{+}\rho_{S}{\cal J}_{-}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{{\cal J}_{-}{\cal J}_{+},\rho_{S}\})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{z}({\cal J}_{z}\rho_{S}{\cal J}_{z}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{{\cal J}_{z}^{2},\rho_{S}\})\,,

where we have introduced the
collective
spin operators
{\cal J}_{\pm,z}=\sum_{\alpha}J_{\pm,z}^{\alpha}
. This collective Lindblad equation is structurally identical to that for a single spin and the coefficients
\gamma_{\pm,z}
are the same single-spin interaction rates of Eqs. (
122
), (
123
), and (
124
).
In the language of quantum optics, these terms describe, in the order of appearance, collective emission, collective absorption, and collective dephasing (see, e.g., Ref.
PhysRevA.98.063815
). Since the system Hamiltonian is
H_{S}=\omega_{0}{\cal J}_{z}
, the collective dephasing term commutes with
H_{S}
and has no dynamical effect on the observables of interest; we will therefore neglect it in the following.

Incoherent regime

In the opposite,
qR\gg 1
limit, the phase differences across the system are large, leading to a complete loss of coherence. This defines the incoherent regime, in which neutrino-spin interactions are fully local. The master equation in this case factorises into
N
independent Lindblad equations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{-}(J^{\alpha}_{-}\rho_{S}J^{\alpha}_{+}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{J^{\alpha}_{+}J^{\alpha}_{-},\rho_{S}\})

(21)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{+}(J^{\alpha}_{+}\rho_{S}J^{\alpha}_{-}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{J^{\alpha}_{-}J^{\alpha}_{+},\rho_{S}\})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{z}(J^{\alpha}_{z}\rho_{S}J^{\alpha}_{z}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{(J^{\alpha}_{z})^{2},\rho_{S}\})\,.

Here, each spin evolves independently under local interactions with the bath, and the rates
\gamma_{\pm,z}
are again the single-spin rates. This local Lindblad form is general for any uncorrelated ensemble-bath interaction and applies to a wide class of physical systems for which the Lindblad approximation holds. In Sec.
V
, we will use Eq. (
21
) to model other (non-neutrino) environmental effects that eventually drive the spin ensemble towards thermal equilibrium in a realistic NMR experiment.

III.2
Dicke basis and initial states

Suppose all
N
spins are identically prepared and indistinguishable, and experience the same interactions with the environment. It is then convenient to use the framework of Dicke states
PhysRev.93.99
, which are eigenstates of the collective operators
{\cal J}^{2}
and
{\cal J}_{z}
, and form a basis for the permutationally invariant subspace of the full Hilbert space. As permutational invariance is preserved by the dynamics of the scenarios of interest, adoption of the Dicke basis allows us to drastically reduce the computational complexity of the problem, both analytically and numerically: instead of working in the full
2^{N}
-dimensional Hilbert space, we can restrict the dynamics to a much smaller subspace,
spanned by the Dicke states labelled by the total spin and its projection,
|j,m\rangle
, where
j=N/2,N/2-1,\ldots,j_{\min}
, with
j_{\min}=0
if
N
even or
j_{\min}=1/2
if
N
odd, and
|m|\leq j
. The full density matrix therefore contains

n_{D}=\sum_{j_{\rm min}}^{N/2}(2j+1)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(N^{3})

(22)

elements. In the presence of purely-coherent interactions, where the dynamics are confined to the fully symmetric subspace (
j=N/2
), this reduces further to
n_{D}=N+1
.

The left panel of Fig.
2
illustrates the intuition provided by the Dicke representation—commonly visualised as the Dicke triangle—which organises the Hilbert space according to total spin
j
, including both the symmetric subspace (
j=N/2
) and the non-symmetric sectors (
j<N/2
) that do not exhibit cooperative or superradiant behaviours. When the coherence condition is satisfied, the neutrino-spin interaction is represented by collective, “vertical” transitions along the Dicke ladder in steps of
|N/2,m\rangle\to|N/2,m\pm 1\rangle
. This behavior contrasts with that of incoherent local processes such as dephasing, which induce only “horizontal” motion along the
j
-axis of the Dicke triangle. For more details on the Dicke basis and the effects of local interactions, we refer the reader to Ref.
PhysRevA.98.063815
and references therein. See also Ref.
Gross:1982dkt
;
Masson:2021eyk
for an exposition on Dicke superradiance.

We are interested in three types of permutation-symmetric initial states, whose density matrices in the symmetric Dicke basis and Bloch sphere visualisations are shown in the centre and right panels of Fig.
2
.

•

The ground state
|G\rangle\equiv|\downarrow\rangle^{\otimes N}=|N/2,-N/2\rangle
represents the case in which all
N
spins are in the low-energy state of the splitting (pointing down in our notation), such that the polarisation is unity in the Bloch sphere. From an experimental perspective, such a state is called hyperpolarised, as the polarisation far exceeds the thermal equilibrium expectation (see Sec.
V
). Nonetheless, hyperpolarisation can be achieved experimentally for, e.g.,
129
Xe-enriched ensembles, via spin exchange optical pumping, as planned in the upcoming stages of the CASPEr experiment
Walter:2025ldb
.

•

The fully excited state
|E\rangle\equiv|\uparrow\rangle^{\otimes N}=|N/2,N/2\rangle
has all
N
spins in the high-energy state of the splitting (pointing up). Starting from a hyperpolarised state like
|G\rangle
, the
|E\rangle
state can be produced by applying a
\pi
pulse, i.e., applying a weak magnetic field oscillating at the Larmor frequency, until the polarisation is inverted.

•

The equatorial coherent spin state (CSS), or “product state”, is defined by
|P\rangle\equiv|\theta=\pi/2,\varphi=0\rangle_{\rm CSS}
, where

\displaystyle\qquad|\theta,\varphi\rangle_{\rm CSS}

\displaystyle=\otimes^{N}\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|\uparrow\rangle+e^{{\rm i}\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|\downarrow\rangle\right)\,.

(23)

In the Dicke basis it can be written as
|P\rangle=\sum_{m}c_{m}|j,m\rangle
, with

c_{m}=2^{-j}\binom{2j}{j+m}
.
This state serves as the standard benchmark in quantum sensing because of its resemblance to the superradiant state
|S\rangle\equiv|N/2,0\rangle

PhysRev.93.99
. It can be prepared by applying a
\pi/2
pulse to the ground state.

Lastly, we remark that achieving a close-to-unity polarisation may not always be possible in a realistic experimental setting. Therefore, in our numerical solutions of the Linblad equation in Sec.
VI.1
, we will introduce an effective polarisation parameter
p=\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle/{\cal J}
, in order to connect our results with the level of polarisation achievable in current and future experimental setups.

IV
Coherent observables

Given an initial state, the Lindblad equation (
18
) can be solved to find the evolution of the spin ensemble. Several observables are of interest. The most common first-order observable is the collective magnetisation, whose expectation value can be constructed from
\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle={\rm tr}(\rho_{S}{\cal J}_{z})
, and is related to the ensemble’s energy. At second order, relevant observables include
\langle{\cal J}^{2}_{z}\rangle
and
\langle{\cal J}^{2}_{x}\rangle
, where the latter represents the squared transverse spin component.

Exact analytical solutions for these observables generally do not exist. Numerical solutions, on the other hand, can become prohibitive for large values of
N
. We therefore consider first approximate analytical solutions in different limits and under different settings in order to map out the limiting behaviours of the system, before attempting a numerical treatment. We discuss first in this section approximate analytical solutions in the presence of coherent neutrino effects only. Section
V
considers the effects of non-neutrino “noise” from local interactions.
We present numerical solutions including both neutrino-induced coherent and local noise effects in Sec.
VI
.

Before we delve into the solutions, we first define two timescales that characterise the evolution of the spin ensemble in the presence of coherent neutrino interactions.

•

The superradiant timescale,

t_{\rm SR}\equiv\frac{1}{N^{2}\gamma_{\pm}}\sim 10^{-2}~{\rm s}~\left(\frac{10^{25}}{N}\right)^{2},

(24)

characterises the timescale for a single collective spin-flip, i.e., the transition
|j,m\rangle\to|j,m\pm 1\rangle
. The numerical estimate follows from setting
\gamma_{\pm}=\gamma_{\pm}^{\rm SM}
, where
\gamma_{\pm}^{\rm SM}
is given in Eq. (
7
).

•

The dissipative timescale,

t_{\rm diss}\equiv\frac{1}{N\gamma_{\pm}}\sim 10^{23}~{\rm s}~\left(\frac{10^{25}}{N}\right),

(25)

is the dynamical timescale of the system, defined as the duration over which an
{\cal O}(1)
change in a collective observable such as
\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle
becomes apparent. This timescale also marks the end of the coherent superradiant regime, and limits the validity of the perturbative solution discussed below. The numerical estimate assumes again
\gamma_{\pm}=\gamma_{\pm}^{\rm SM}
from Eq. (
7
).

IV.1
Perturbative solution

Considering the weakness of the neutrino-spin interactions, Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
proposed a perturbative/iterative solution to Eq. (
20
) around the initial state. Assuming only coherent neutrino interactions are present and to linear order in

, the solution reads

\rho_{S}(t)\approx\rho_{S}(0)+t\gamma_{-}{\cal D}_{{\cal J}_{-}}[\rho_{S}(0)]+t\gamma_{+}{\cal D}_{{\cal J}_{+}}[\rho_{S}(0)]\,,

(26)

recalling the notation of
{\cal D}_{\cal O}[\rho]
from Eq. (
16
), and we have again omitted the collective dephasing term.

In this perturbative limit, the evolution of
\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle
for an ensemble initially prepared in the
|P\rangle

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle_{|P\rangle}

\displaystyle\approx-\left(\frac{N}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{N}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\gamma_{\rm net}t

(27)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{N^{2}}{4}\gamma_{\rm net}t\,,

with
\gamma_{\rm net}\equiv\gamma_{-}-\gamma_{+}
. As already noted in Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq

In a similar vein, the second-order observables evolve in the perturbative limit according to

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{2}_{z}\rangle_{|P\rangle}

(28)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{2}_{x}\rangle_{|G\rangle}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N}{4}+2\gamma_{+}N(N+1)~t\,,

(29)

\gamma_{\rm tot}=\gamma_{-}+\gamma_{+}

\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle_{|P\rangle}

(for the

) but
without the need to rotate the spins to the equatorial plane.

8

It is also a quantity directly accessed by experiments like CASPEr that measure the transverse magnetisation.

Inspecting the solutions (

V
Local noise effects

Our analysis so far has focused exclusively on the coherent interaction between the spin ensemble and the C
\nu
B. Realistic experimental setups, however, are inevitably subject to other, non-neutrino environmental interactions that relax the system, i.e., drive it to a state of thermal equilibrium, by locally exciting or de-exciting the spins, as well as locally dephasing the spin precessions.
Amongst these, local dephasing plays a particularly important role.

This form of noise originates from random, uncorrelated fluctuations acting independently on each spin, due to, e.g., spin-spin interactions and magnetic field fluctuations, and, phenomenologically, can be described by the operator
\sqrt{\gamma_{\phi}^{\rm loc}}J_{z}^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
labels the individual spin. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the key feature of local dephasing is that it acts incoherently and forces the system to move into lower-
j
sectors where collective effects such as superradiance are suppressed or entirely lost. The relevant timescale is
t_{\phi}\sim 1/\gamma_{\phi}^{\rm loc}
, which is easily much shorter than the dissipative timescale
t_{\rm diss}
from Eq. (
25
). Consequently, local dephasing can wash out coherent signatures induced by the neutrino-spin interaction, effectively setting a limit on the observable impact of the C
\nu
B.

In a purely phenomenological approach, let us assume that relaxation is dominated by local (single-particle) effects, i.e., we do not model spin-spin interactions. This simplification allows us to “repurpose” the Lindblad equation (
21
) in the incoherent regime to model the relaxation characteristics of a realistic experimental sample. Identifying
\gamma_{z}
in Eq. (
21

\gamma^{\rm loc}_{\phi}
and the rates
\gamma_{\pm}

\gamma^{\rm loc}_{\pm}

to match the experimental longitudinal and transverse relaxation times,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}

Explicitly, starting from Eq. (

(33)

\displaystyle+N\gamma^{\rm loc}_{+}~\text{tr}[J_{i}J_{+}\rho_{S}^{(1/2)}J_{-}-\frac{1}{2}J_{i}\{J_{-}J_{+},\rho_{S}^{(1/2)}\}]

\displaystyle+N\gamma_{\phi}^{\rm loc}~\text{tr}[J_{i}J_{z}\rho_{S}^{(1/2)}J_{z}-\frac{1}{2}J_{i}\{J_{z}J_{z},\rho_{S}^{(1/2)}\}]\,,

Numerical solutions

VI.1
Second-order approximation

While it is possible to numerically solve the Lindblad master equation (
42

N

N
larger than about 100 (see Appendix
F

Rather than dealing with the full density matrix, then, we shift the focus to tracking just a handful physically meaningful quantities.

Specifically, as was done in Ref.
Shammah_2017
for the case of Dicke superradiance and also touched upon in Sec.
V

42

\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle
,
\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle
, and
\langle{\cal J}_{x}^{2}\rangle

However, differential equations thus constructed generally do not form a closed system, in the sense that the equation of motion for, e.g.,
\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle
depends on a higher moment
\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle
, whose own equation of motion depends in turn on
\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{3}\rangle

To solve the system, therefore, one needs to truncate the hierarchy at a chosen order.

Here, we opt to truncate the hierarchy at second-order. For the observables
\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle
and
\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle

\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{3}\rangle\approx\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle+\gamma_{+}\left(\langle{\cal J}^{2}\rangle-\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle-\langle{\cal J}_{z}\rangle\right)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\langle{\cal J}^{2}\rangle=-

\displaystyle\gamma_{\phi}\left(\langle{\cal J}^{2}\rangle-\langle{\cal J}_{z}^{2}\rangle-\frac{N}{2}\right)\,,

To estimate the sensitivity to the C

VIII
Conclusions

We have investigated in this work the dynamics of large spin ensembles interacting with the cosmic neutrino background, with a particular focus on identifying the conditions under which coherent collective effects—scaling as
N^{2}
, where
N
is the number of spins—can amplify the signal from an otherwise extremely weak neutrino-spin coupling. Such collective neutrino-spin interactions are expected to contribute to dissipating a spin ensemble and can potentially be exploited as a competitive way to set limits on the local C
\nu
B overdensity, as first put forward in Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
.

After briefly reviewing the interaction rates of the C
\nu
B with a system of
N
two-level systems (e.g., spin-
1/2
particles) and the Lindblad master equation in the open quantum system framework,
we reproduced the perturbative solutions presented in Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
, valid when the spin ensemble has not deviated too much from its initial state. We then extend the analysis by considering steady-state analytical solutions as well as numerical simulations of the Lindblad master equation
for different initial spin configurations. Notably, our numerical simulations account for realistic noise sources that can degrade the collective C
\nu
B signal, such as local dephasing as well as imperfect initial spin polarisation. We make use of the Dicke basis—illustrated in Fig.
2
—to facilitate this analysis, which provides an intuitive representation of how collective and local interactions influence the spin ensemble’s evolution under the Lindblad master equation.

We have furthermore presented a fast and computationally inexpensive method to derive approximate numerical solution from the Lindblad equation. Specifically, the method solves directly for observables of interest while capturing both coherent neutrino effects and local interactions, and
is able to reproduce the full density matrix solution to
{\cal O}(0.01)\%
accuracy. This fast method enables us to use statistical inference methods to forecast the sensitivity of future NMR experiments to the C
\nu
B overdensity, while incorporating the complete quantum evolution of the spin ensemble.

Considering the projected experimental specifications of the forthcoming CASPEr experiment
Budker:2013hfa
;
JacksonKimball:2017elr
;
Walter:2025ldb
and nuclear-spin-based experiments more generally, we compute future experimental sensitivities to the C
\nu
B overdensity parameter
\delta_{\nu}
using a
\chi^{2}
analysis.
We find that achievable near-future setups along the lines of CASPEr could constrain the C
\nu
B overdensity parameter at the level of
\delta_{\nu}\sim 10^{9}-10^{11}
, compatible at face value with current and projected
\delta_{\nu}
limits from the KATRIN collaboration
KATRIN:2022kkv
.
Notably, in addition to the scaling with the sample volume and the obvious dependence on instrumental noise, we find that the initial polarisation
p
of the spin ensemble plays a prominent role in our projected sensitivities: scaling as
\delta_{\nu}\propto p^{-2}
, a competitive constraint requires at least
p\sim 0.25
. This requirement also means that thermal polarisation levels achievable at typical experimental conditions—
p\sim 10^{-3}
for
129
Xe at
T\sim 4
K and
B\sim 10
T—are not suited to C
\nu
B detection. Advanced polarisition techniques are needed to reach the desired
p\gtrsim 0.25
.

In optimistic experimental scenarios—limited only by spin projection noise, a large sample volume up to
R=10
cm, and 100% polarisation—constraints as good as
\delta_{\nu}\lesssim 10^{7}
may even be possible. This is nominally a much stronger sensitivity than the initial estimate of Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
derived under similar assumptions, and is primarily a consequence of two key differences in our analysis: (i) Our forecast assumes a spin ensemble made up of liquid
129
Xe, i.e., proton-neutron asymmetric nuclei with a large weak-coupling form factor, which alone enhances the interaction rate by several orders of magnitude. (ii) Our
\chi^{2}
analysis integrates the observable over its entire time evolution up to the transverse coherence time
T_{2}\sim 1000
s. This effectively allows us to pick up another factor of
T_{2}^{-1/2}
in the projected
\delta_{\nu}
sensitivity, compared with the signal-to-noise ratio estimate of Ref.
Arvanitaki:2024taq
based upon evaluating the observable at a single time point
t\approx T_{2}
alone.

Our results indicate that C
\nu
B direct detection is likely beyond the reach of current NMR technology, even in the most optimistic scenario. Indeed, even meeting the “realistic” projected
\delta_{\nu}
constraints represent significant experimental challenges. We emphasise however much effort is currently being devoted to overcoming these challenges—in the case of CASPEr, in the context of dark matter axion searches. Should the desired experimental configurations materialise, we have shown that, by means of a pulsed-NMR measurement such as performed recently in
Walter:2025ldb
,
the potential exists for such axion searches to simultaneously set competitive constraints on the C
\nu
B for free. Constraining the C
\nu
B with nuclear spin experiments therefore remains an attractive possibility.

Low energy non-relativistic spin Hamiltonian

We outline the derivation of the non-relativistic Hamiltonian for the neutrino-fermion spin interaction. We begin with the Lagrangian for a fermion
\Psi
in the presence of an external electromagnetic 4-potential
A_{\mu}=(A_{0},\vec{A})
and a 4-fermi interaction with a neutrino
\nu_{i}
in the mass basis,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\bar{\Psi}({\rm i}\not{\partial}-m)\Psi-q\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi A_{\mu}-\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}\bar{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}(g^{i,j}_{V}-g^{i,j}_{A}\gamma^{5})\Psi\,\bar{\nu}_{i}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma^{5})\nu_{j}\,,

(60)

where
i,j
label the neutrino mass eigenstate, and
q
is the electric charge of the fermion.
The corresponding equation of motion is

\left({\rm i}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m-q\gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}-\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}\gamma^{\mu}(g^{i,j}_{V}-g^{i,j}_{A}\gamma^{5})\bar{\nu}_{i}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma^{5})\nu_{j}\right)\Psi=0\,,

(61)

or, equivalently,

\Bigg((p^{0}\gamma_{0}-qA^{0}\gamma_{0})-\vec{\gamma}.(\vec{p}-q\vec{A})-\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(g_{V}^{i,j}-g_{A}^{i,j}\right)\gamma^{0}\Sigma_{0}^{i,j}+\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(g_{V}^{i,j}-g_{A}^{i,j}\right)\vec{\gamma}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}^{i,j}\Bigg)\Psi=0\,,

(62)

where we have used
\vec{p}=-{\rm i}\vec{\nabla}
, and
p^{0}={\rm i}\partial_{t}
, and
defined
\Sigma^{i,j}_{\mu}\equiv\bar{\nu}_{i}\gamma_{\mu}(1-\gamma^{5})\nu_{j}
.

We write the fermion spinor in 2-component notation,

\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{0}\\
\chi_{0}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(63)

such that in the non-relativistic limit, we can make the
ansatz

\Psi(\vec{x},t)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{0}\\
\chi_{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}e^{-{\rm i}mt},

(64)

which yields the solution
\chi_{0}\approx[\vec{\sigma}\cdot(\vec{p}-q\vec{A})/(2m)]\phi_{0}
, where
m
is the mass of the fermion.
Substituting this solution back into the equation of motion (
62
) in 2-component form, we find a Schrödinger-Pauli-like equation with additional neutrino-fermion interaction terms,

\left(\frac{(\vec{p}-q\vec{A})^{2}}{2m}-\frac{q}{2m}\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\sigma}+qA_{0}+\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}g^{i,j}_{V}\Sigma^{i,j}_{0}+\sum_{ij}\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}g^{i,j}_{A}\vec{\Sigma}^{i,j}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\phi_{0}=E\phi_{0}\,,

(65)

where
\vec{B}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{A}
is the external magnetic field, and
p^{0}=E
is the energy.

In the form (
65
), the total Hamiltonian can be immediately identified with the terms in parentheses on the left-hand side of the equation. We concentrate exclusively on the spin interaction of the fermion with the C
\nu
B. Assuming the fermion (spin) is at rest, this interaction is described by the spin Hamiltonian,

H=-\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B}+\sqrt{2}G_{F}\sum_{ij}g^{i,j}_{A}\vec{J}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}^{i,j}\,,

(66)

where we have used
\vec{J}=\vec{\sigma}/{2}
, and the magnetic moment is defined as
\vec{\mu}=(q/m)\vec{J}
. Although this result has been obtained assuming a charged fermion, an analogous spin-magnetic field interaction is also present for neutral particles like the neutron or nuclei. Here, the interaction of a magnetic field with the magnetic moment due to the particle’s internal structure can be described by an effective Lagrangian of the form
\bar{\psi}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\psi F_{\mu\nu}
. In the non-relativistic limit, this interaction term also reduces to a Hamiltonian of the form
H=-\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B}
. For nuclei, the convention is to define
\vec{\mu}=g\mu_{N}\vec{J}
, where
g
is the
g
-factor of the nucleus under consideration, and
\mu_{N}=e/2m_{p}
is the nuclear magneton.

Supposing without any loss of generality that the magnetic field points along the negative
z
-axis, i.e.,
\vec{B}=-B\hat{z}
,
the spin Hamiltonian can be recast into a more suggestive form,

H=\omega_{0}J_{z}+\Sigma_{z}J_{z}+\Sigma_{-}J_{-}+\Sigma_{+}J_{+}\,,

(67)

where the Larmor frequency
\omega_{0}=\gamma B
is also the energy splitting between two adjacent spin states, with
\gamma
the gyromagnetic ratio, and we have defined
J_{\pm}\equiv J_{1}\pm{\rm i}J_{2}
, and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}G_{F}\sum_{ij}g^{i,j}_{A}\bar{\nu}_{i}\gamma^{3}(1-\gamma^{5})\nu_{j}\,,

(68)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\pm}=

\displaystyle\frac{G_{F}}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{ij}g^{i,j}_{A}\bar{\nu}_{i}(\gamma^{1}\mp{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})\nu_{j}\,.

Then, splitting the Hamiltonian into a spin ensemble and an interaction term as

H=H_{S}+H_{I}\,,

(69)

we identify
H_{S}\equiv\omega_{0}J_{z}
,
H_{I}\equiv\Sigma_{z}J_{z}+\Sigma_{-}J_{-}+\Sigma_{+}J_{+}
, and

\displaystyle H^{I}_{I}(t)=

\displaystyle e^{{\rm i}H_{s}t}H_{I}e^{-{\rm i}H_{s}t}

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}J_{z}+e^{-{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}\Sigma_{-}J_{-}+e^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}\Sigma_{+}J_{+}

gives the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture.

In the case of a system with
N
spins, the Hamiltonians of the spin ensemble and the interaction can be straightforwardly generalised from Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
) to

\displaystyle H_{S}=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\sum_{\alpha}J^{\alpha}_{z}\,,

(71)

\displaystyle H^{I}_{I}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\alpha}_{z}J^{\alpha}_{z}+e^{-{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}\sum_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\alpha}_{-}J^{\alpha}_{-}+e^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}\sum_{\alpha}\Sigma^{\alpha}_{+}J^{\alpha}_{+}\,,

where the operators
J^{\alpha}_{\pm,z}
act only on the spin
\alpha
at the spatial position
\vec{x}_{\alpha}
, and
\Sigma^{\alpha}_{\pm,z}
are defined as per Eq. (
68
) but with the understanding that the neutrino wavefunctions must now be specified as
\nu_{i}(t,\vec{x}_{\alpha})
.

Appendix B
Excitation and de-excitation rates

B.1
Single spin

Consider first a single spin immersed in the cosmic neutrino bath. Following Fermi’s golden rule the excitation and de-excitation rates are given by

\gamma_{\pm}=|T^{\pm}_{\rm fi}|^{2}2\pi\delta^{(1)}(E_{\rm f}-E_{\rm i})\,\rho_{\pm}(E_{\rm f})\,,

(72)

where the superscripts “
+
” and “
-
” refer respectively to excitation and de-excitation,
|T^{+}_{\rm fi}|^{2}
is the transition probability from the initial state
|{\rm i}\rangle=|\downarrow\rangle\otimes|{\rm i}_{\nu}\rangle
to the final state
|{\rm f}\rangle=|\uparrow\rangle\otimes|{\rm f}_{\nu}\rangle
, while
|T^{-}_{\rm fi}|^{2}
is its counterpart for the transition
|{\rm i}\rangle=|\uparrow\rangle\otimes|{\rm i}_{\nu}\rangle
to
|{\rm f}\rangle=|\downarrow\rangle\otimes|{\rm f}_{\nu}\rangle
. The quantity
\rho(E_{\rm f})
is the density of states at the final energy
E_{\rm f}
, which in our case can be written as,

\rho_{\pm}(E_{\rm f})=\sum_{jk}\sum_{a,c}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{n}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}\mp\vec{n})\,P_{{\rm i}_{\nu}}\,P_{{\rm f}_{\nu}}\,,

(73)

where
\vec{p}
and
\vec{p}\,^{\prime}
are the 3-momenta of the incoming and outgoing neutrinos respectively,
\vec{n}
is the momentum of the final-state fermion (assuming it is initially stationary),
P_{{\rm i}_{\nu}}
and
P_{{\rm f}_{\nu}}
represent respectively the thermal weight of the initial-state neutrino and any Pauli blocking factor associated with the final state,
j
and
k
are mass indices of the initial- and final-state neutrinos, while
a
and
c
index their helicities.
Since the recoil of the fermion is expected to be tiny, the angle of the outgoing neutrino is essentially unrestricted. We therefore proceed to use the Dirac delta in Eq. (
73
) to eliminate the integral over the nuclear momentum, to arrive at

\rho_{\pm}(E_{\rm f})=\sum_{jk}\sum_{a,c}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}P_{{\rm i}_{\nu}}\,P_{{\rm f}_{\nu}}\,,

(74)

where it is now understood that the relation between
|\vec{p}|
and
|\vec{p}\,^{\prime}|
is determined by energy conservation alone.

Figure 11:
Feynman diagrams of the processes that contribute to the excitation of a spin in a 4-fermi effective interaction.

At first order in perturbation theory (i.e., the Born approximation), the transition amplitude is given by

T^{\pm}_{\rm fi}=\langle{\rm f}|\Sigma_{\pm}J_{\pm}|{\rm i}\rangle\,.

(75)

Focussing on excitation, consider an initial-state neutrino specified by a wavefunction

\nu_{j}(t,\vec{x})=u_{j,a}(\vec{p})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}\,,

(76)

where
a
is a helicity index. Suppose it scatters off a spin inelastically, thereby transferring energy
\omega_{0}
to it, and then exits the interaction in a final-state plane wave given by
\nu_{k}(t,\vec{x})=u_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{\prime 0}t-\vec{p}^{\prime}\cdot\vec{x})}
. This process is represented by the leftmost diagram in Fig.
11
, and its transition probability can be written as

\displaystyle|T^{+}_{\rm fi}|^{2}=

\displaystyle|\langle\uparrow|J_{+}|\downarrow\rangle|^{2}\,|\langle{\rm f}_{\nu}|\Sigma_{+}|{\rm i}_{\nu}\rangle|^{2}

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{G_{F}^{2}}{2}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\frac{1}{2p^{0}2p^{\prime 0}}\left|\bar{u}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{j,a}(\vec{p})\right|^{2}\,,

where we have used
|\langle\uparrow|J_{+}|\downarrow\rangle|^{2}=1
at the second equality, and the factors of
1/2p^{0}
originates from the normalisation of the
u
-spinors to
2p^{0}
.
The thermal weight factors in Eq. (
73
) are then
P_{{\rm i}_{\nu}}=N_{j,a}(p^{0})
and
P_{{\rm f}_{\nu}}=1-N_{k,a}(p^{\prime 0})
, where
N_{i,a}(p^{0})
is the neutrino occupation number, while the Dirac delta
\delta^{(1)}(E_{\rm f}-E_{\rm i})
becomes
\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}+p^{\prime 0}-p^{0})
,
enforcing the change in energy between the initial- and final-state neutrinos to be equal to
\omega_{0}
, the energy splitting due to the external magnetic field. A similar calculation can also be performed in the case of an antineutrino exciting a spin, as represented by the middle diagram in Fig.
11
.

Putting it altogether, we then find the excitation rate of one spin to be

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}=

\displaystyle G_{F}^{2}\pi\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\sum_{ac}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}^{\prime}\;\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}+p^{\prime 0}-p^{0})

(78)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[(1-N_{k,c}(p^{\prime 0}))\,N_{j,a}(p^{0})\,\bar{u}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{j,a}(\vec{p})\bar{u}_{j,a}(\vec{p})(\gamma^{1}+{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})

\displaystyle+(1-\bar{N}_{k,c}(p^{\prime 0}))\,\bar{N}_{j,a}(p^{0})\bar{v}_{j,a}(\vec{p})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})v_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})\bar{v}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}+{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})v_{j,a}(\vec{p})\Bigg]\,,

where we have defined the phase space measure
{\rm d}\tilde{p}\equiv{\rm d}^{3}\vec{p}/((2\pi)^{3}2p^{0})
. Ignoring the relative motion between the spin and the C
\nu
B, and assuming the neutrino occupation numbers to be independent of helicity, the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}=

\displaystyle 8\,G_{F}^{2}\pi\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}^{\prime}\;\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}+p^{\prime 0}-p^{0})\,p^{0}p^{\prime 0}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]\,,

(79)

which, using the Dirac delta, can be further reduced to

\gamma_{+}=\frac{4G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\int_{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}+m_{k})^{2}-m_{j}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\,p_{k+}\,\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k+}}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{0}_{k+}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{0}_{k+}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]\,,

(80)

with
p_{k+}=[(\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m_{j}^{2}}-\omega_{0})^{2}-m_{k}^{2}]^{1/2}
, and
p^{0}_{k+}=\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k+}}
.

Computation of the de-excitation rate from inelastic neutrino-spin scattering proceeds in a similar fashion, and yields

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}=

\displaystyle G_{F}^{2}\pi\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\sum_{ac}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}^{\prime}\;\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}+p^{0}-p^{\prime 0})

(81)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[(1-N_{k,c}(p^{\prime 0}))\,N_{j,a}(p^{0})\,\bar{u}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}+{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{j,a}(\vec{p})\bar{u}_{j,a}(\vec{p})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})

\displaystyle+(1-\bar{N}_{k,c}(p^{\prime 0}))\,\bar{N}_{j,a}(p^{0})\bar{v}_{j,a}(\vec{p})(\gamma^{1}+{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})v_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})\bar{v}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})v_{j,a}(\vec{p})\Bigg]\,.

Again, ignoring any neutrino-spin relative motion and assuming helicity-independent neutrino occupation numbers, we find

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}=

\displaystyle 8\,G_{F}^{2}\pi\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\sum_{ac}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}^{\prime}\;\delta^{(1)}(\omega_{0}+p^{0}-p^{\prime 0})\,p^{0}p^{\prime 0}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{\prime 0}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\int_{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-m_{k})^{2}-m_{j}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\,p_{k-}\,\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k-}}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{0}_{k-}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{0}_{k-}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]\,,

with
p_{k-}=[(\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m_{j}^{2}}+\omega_{0})^{2}-m_{k}^{2}]^{1/2}
, and
p^{0}_{k-}=\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k-}}
. The lower integration holds if it evaluates to a real number. Otherwise, it needs to be set to zero.

Suppose for simplicity
m_{j}=m_{k}=m
and
\omega_{0}\ll m
. The lower integration limits of the
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
integrals, Eqs. (
80
) and (
82
), are
\sqrt{(\omega_{0}+m)^{2}-m^{2}}\approx\sqrt{2m\omega_{0}}
and zero, respectively. We can take the former to zero too if
\omega_{0}\ll|\vec{p}|^{2}/(2m)\ll m
is satisfied by the bulk of the neutrino distribution. The same condition also allows us to approximate
p_{k\pm}\approx\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}\mp 2m\omega_{0}}\approx|\vec{p}|\mp m\omega_{0}/|\vec{p}|
. Then, to leading order, we find

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}\approx\gamma_{-}

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\;p_{k+}\sqrt{m^{2}+p^{2}_{k+}}\left[N(p^{0})+\bar{N}(p^{0})\right]

(83)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\;m\,|\vec{p}|\left[N(p^{0})+\bar{N}(p^{0})\right]\,,

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm net}\equiv\gamma_{-}-\gamma_{+}

\displaystyle\propto\int^{\infty}_{0}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\;\left[p_{k-}\,\sqrt{m^{2}+p^{2}_{k-}}-p_{k+}\,\sqrt{m^{2}+p^{2}_{k+}}\right]\left[N(p^{0})+\bar{N}(p^{0})\right]

(84)

\displaystyle\approx\int^{\infty}_{0}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\;\frac{2m^{2}\omega_{0}}{|\vec{p}|}\;\left[N(p^{0})+\bar{N}(p^{0})\right]\,,

where we have omitted for brevity the Pauli-blocking terms in both expressions. Thus, we see immediately that
\gamma_{+}<\gamma_{-}
, and
\gamma_{\rm net}
is suppressed by a factor
\sim 2m\omega_{0}/|\vec{p}|^{2}
relative to
\gamma_{\pm}
.

B.2
N
spins

Consider now a system of
N
spins described by the interaction Hamiltonian (
71
). We are interested in the excitation and de-excitation rates of the whole spin ensemble, which can be defined via

\Gamma_{\pm}=\sum_{{\rm f}_{\rm spin}}|T_{\rm fi}^{\pm}|^{2}2\pi\delta^{(1)}(E_{\rm f}-E_{\rm i})\,\rho_{\pm}(E_{\rm f})\,,

(85)

where

\displaystyle T^{\pm}_{\rm fi}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\langle{\rm f}_{\rm spin}|J_{\pm}^{\alpha}|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle\langle{\rm f}_{\nu}|\Sigma_{\pm}^{\alpha}|{\rm i}_{\nu}\rangle

(86)

are the excitation and de-excitation amplitudes of the
N
-spin ensemble from an initial state
|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle
to an unspecified final state
|{\rm f}_{\rm spin}\rangle
. The summation over
{\rm f}_{\rm spin}
encompasses a complete basis of final states; the ladder operators will project out the final states that have one unit of energy
\omega_{0}
more (
+
) or less (
-
) than the initial state.

Again, focussing first on spin excitation due to inelastic scattering of a plane-wave neutrino from
\nu_{j}(t,\vec{x}_{\alpha})=u_{j,a}(\vec{p})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}_{\alpha})}
to
\nu_{k}(t,\vec{x}_{\beta})=u_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{\prime 0}t-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}\cdot\vec{x}_{\beta})}
, we find

\displaystyle|T^{+}_{\rm fi}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle{\rm f}_{\rm spin}|J_{+}^{\alpha}|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle\langle{\rm i}_{\rm spin}|J_{-}^{\beta}|{\rm f}_{\rm spin}\rangle

(87)

\displaystyle\times\frac{G_{F}^{2}}{2}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\frac{1}{2p^{0}2p^{\prime 0}}\left|\bar{u}_{k,c}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})(\gamma^{1}-{\rm i}\gamma^{2})(1-\gamma^{5})u_{j,a}(\vec{p})\right|^{2}\,e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot(\vec{x}_{\alpha}-\vec{x}_{\beta})}\,.

A similar expression can be found for
|T^{-}_{\rm fi}|^{2}
.
Then, following the same procedure as for a single spin, it is straightforward to arrive at the collective excitation and de-excitation rates

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}=

\displaystyle\frac{2G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\int_{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}+m_{k})^{2}-m_{j}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\,p_{k+}\,\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k+}}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{0}_{k+}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{0}_{k+}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]

(88)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-1}^{1}{\rm d}\mu\;F_{+}(\vec{p}_{k+}-\vec{p})\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}=

\displaystyle\frac{2G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{jk}g_{A}^{j,k}g_{A}^{k,j}\int_{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-m_{k})^{2}-m_{j}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\,p_{k-}\,\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{k-}}\,\left[(1-N_{k}(p^{0}_{k-}))\,N_{j}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{0}_{k-}))\,\bar{N}_{j}(p^{0})\right]

\displaystyle\times\int_{-1}^{1}{\rm d}\mu\;F_{-}(\vec{p}_{k-}-\vec{p})\,,

where
\mu\equiv\hat{p}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{p}=\cos\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}
, with
\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}
the angle between the incoming and outgoing momenta, and

F_{\pm}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\equiv\sum_{\rm f_{spin}}\Big|\langle{\rm f}_{\rm spin}|\sum_{\alpha}J_{\pm}^{\alpha}e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\alpha}}|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle\Big|^{2}

(89)

are form factors that depend on the initial state of the whole
N
spin ensemble.
Since the operator
\sum_{\alpha}J_{\pm}^{\alpha}
already projects the initial state into the subspace of final states that has the correct energy requirements, we can use the completeness relation of all possible final states
\sum_{\rm f^{\prime}_{spin}}|{\rm f^{\prime}}_{\rm spin}\rangle\langle{\rm f^{\prime}}_{\rm spin}|=1
to simplify the form factors to

F_{\pm}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})=\langle{\rm i}_{\rm spin}|\sum_{\beta}J_{\mp}^{\beta}e^{+{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha}J_{\pm}^{\alpha}e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\alpha}}|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle\,,

(90)

where it is understood that
F_{\pm}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})
are to be evaluated for
\vec{p}\,^{\prime}=\vec{p}_{k+}
or
\vec{p}\,^{\prime}=\vec{p}_{k-}
.

Suppose the spin ensemble is initially in the ground state, i.e.,
|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle=|\downarrow_{1},\downarrow_{2},\cdots,\downarrow_{\gamma},\cdots,\downarrow_{N}\rangle
. We see immediately that the de-excitation form factor must always yield
F^{G}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})=0
, while the excitation form factor evaluates to

F^{G}_{+}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})=\langle\downarrow_{1},\downarrow_{2},\cdots,\downarrow_{N}|\sum_{\beta}J_{-}^{\beta}e^{+{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha}J_{+}^{\alpha}e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\alpha}}|\downarrow_{1},\downarrow_{2},\cdots,\downarrow_{N}\rangle=N\,,

(91)

irrespective of the momentum transfer. Thus, in this case there is no coherent effect from the neutrino-spin interaction. If, on the other hand, the spin ensemble is initially in the product state
|{\rm i}_{\rm spin}\rangle=|\rightarrow_{1},\rightarrow_{2},\cdots,\rightarrow_{\gamma},\cdots,\rightarrow_{N}\rangle
, where
|\rightarrow\rangle\equiv(|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, then the excitation and de-excitation form factors evaluate to

\displaystyle F^{P}_{\pm}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})

\displaystyle=\langle\rightarrow_{1},\rightarrow_{2},\cdots,\rightarrow_{N}|\sum_{\beta}J_{\mp}^{\beta}e^{+{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha}J_{\pm}^{\alpha}e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}_{\alpha}}|\rightarrow_{1},\rightarrow_{2},\cdots,\rightarrow_{N}\rangle

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\neq\alpha}e^{-{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot(\vec{x}_{\alpha}-\vec{x}_{\beta})}\,,

which features possible coherent effects in the second term. When the spatial separation between spins
|\vec{x}_{\alpha}-\vec{x}_{\beta}|
is much smaller than
V^{1/3}
, where
V
is the volume of the sample, we can take the continuum limit and, for a constant spin density
n_{s}=N/V
, approximate the last expression as

\displaystyle F^{P}_{\pm}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}+\frac{N^{2}}{4V^{2}}\left|\int{\rm d}^{3}x\;e^{{\rm i}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\cdot\vec{x}}\right|^{2}

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}+\frac{N^{2}}{4}\frac{9}{|\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p}|^{2}R^{2}}\left|j_{1}\left(|\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p}|R\right)\right|^{2}\,,

where
j_{1}(x)
is the spherical Bessel function of order one, and we have assumed at the second equality the spin sample to be a sphere of radius
R
. Clearly, the second,
N^{2}
term dominates if
|\vec{p}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}|R\ll 1
, with
|j_{1}(x)|^{2}/x^{2}\to 1/9
as
x\to 0
.
In this limit, the system exhibits coherent behaviours.

Given the typical momentum of the neutrino bath
\vec{p}_{\nu}
, in order for the coherence condition to hold over the whole length
R
of the spin sample, we must demand
|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|R\ll 1
. Assuming for simplicity
m_{i}=m_{j}=m
, the typical momentum transfers during excitation and de-excitation are

|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|^{2}_{\pm}=p_{\nu}^{2}+(\sqrt{p_{\nu}^{2}+m^{2}}\mp\omega_{0})^{2}-m^{2}-2p_{\nu}\left[(\sqrt{p_{\nu}^{2}+m^{2}}\mp\omega_{0})^{2}-m^{2}\right]^{1/2}\cos{\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}}\,,

(94)

with
p_{\nu}\equiv|\vec{p}_{\nu}|
.
If we want coherence over the whole object, we must demand that
|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|^{2}\ll 1/R^{2}
even for
\cos\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}=-1
.
For non-relativistic neutrinos, where
\sqrt{p_{\nu}^{2}+m^{2}}\approx m+p_{\nu}^{2}/(2m)
,
this yields the condition

p_{\nu}^{2}+(m+\epsilon_{\pm})^{2}-m^{2}+2p_{\nu}\sqrt{(m+\epsilon_{\pm})^{2}-m^{2}}\ll 1/R^{2}\,,

(95)

or, equivalently,

p_{\nu}+\sqrt{(m+\epsilon_{\pm})^{2}-m^{2}}\ll 1/R\,,

(96)

with
\epsilon_{\pm}\equiv p_{\nu}^{2}/(2m)\mp\omega_{0}
. This in turn leads to

p_{\nu}\left[1+\sqrt{1\mp\omega_{0}\frac{2m}{p_{\nu}^{2}}}\right]\ll 1/R\,,

(97)

when expanded to linear order in
\epsilon_{\pm}
.

From Eq. (
97
), we see that excitation can only happen if
\omega_{0}<p_{\nu}^{2}/(2m)
. But, if excitation happens at all, the coherence condition is also automatically fulfilled for any incoming momentum satisfying
p_{\nu}R\ll 1
.
On the other hand, while de-excitation has no kinematic threshold, coherence also does not come for free. Rather, in order to have coherent de-excitation, the condition
\omega_{0}\ll p_{\nu}^{2}/(2m)
must be satisfied if
p_{\nu}R\ll 1
. Physically, this means that the energy splitting must be much smaller than the incoming neutrino kinetic energy. If this is not the case, the momentum transfer of the neutrino is large and coherence is lost. For a typical C
\nu
B momentum of
p_{\nu}\sim 5.3\times 10^{-4}~{\rm eV}\sim 27~{\rm cm}^{-1}
and a neutrino mass of
m=0.05
eV, this condition amounts to demanding that
\omega_{0}\ll 3\times 10^{-6}
eV.

For typical spin samples of size
R\sim 1
cm, the condition
p_{\nu}R\ll 1
cannot be realised by the C
\nu
B across the whole sample volume. However, even in the case of
p_{\nu}R\gg 1
, coherence is still possible under limited conditions. Specifically, assuming
\omega_{0}\ll p_{\nu}^{2}/(2m)
, Eq. (
94
) evaluates to

|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|^{2}\approx 4p_{\nu}^{2}\sin^{2}{\frac{\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}}{2}}\,.

(98)

Demanding
|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|^{2}R^{2}\ll 1
, we see that
coherence can be achieved even in the case
p_{\nu}R\gg 1
for small angles
\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}\ll 1/(p_{\nu}R)
between the incoming and outgoing neutrino momenta. Under this condition, we can approximate the coherent part of the form factors as

\int_{-1}^{1}{\rm d}\mu\;F_{\pm}^{P}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{p})\approx\frac{N^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{(p_{\nu}R)^{-1}}{\rm d}\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}\,\sin\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}\approx\frac{N^{2}}{4}\frac{1}{2(p_{\nu}R)^{2}}\,.

(99)

In other words, the collective excitation and de-excitation rates
\Gamma_{\pm}
in the
p_{\nu}R\gg 1
regime are suppressed by a factor
(2p_{\nu}R)^{-2}
relative to the fully coherent rates. Evaluating Eq. (
94
) for the momentum transfer squared
q^{2}\equiv|\vec{p}_{\nu}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\nu})|^{2}
in the forward direction (i.e.,
\cos{\theta_{\vec{p}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}}}=1
) and expanding in
\epsilon_{\pm}
as above, it is straightforward to show that the momentum transfer in this regime is
q\sim\omega_{0}m/p_{\nu}
.

Appendix C
Derivation of the Lindblad equation

The Lindblad formalism assumes unitary dynamics of the total neutrino-spin system described by a Hamiltonian that can be decomposed into three parts,

H=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{I}\,.

(100)

Here,
H_{S}
is the free Hamiltonian of the spin ensemble,
H_{B}
the free Hamiltonian of the neutrino bath, and
H_{I}
specifies the interaction between the spin ensemble and the neutrino bath. The density matrix
\rho
acts on the Hilbert space of the whole neutrino-spin system, and we assume it can be split into a direct product of the spin-system density matrix
\rho_{S}
and the bath density matrix
\rho_{B}
, i.e.,

\rho=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\,.

(101)

Such a separation can be made in the limit of weak system-bath interactions and large baths (relative to the spin ensemble), where no significant correlations develop between the spin ensemble and the bath. This is the so-called Born approximation.

The dynamics of the total neutrino-spin system is governed by the Liouville-von-Neumann equation,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\rm i}\,[H,\rho]\,,

(102)

where the density matrix
\rho
is understood to be in the Schrödinger picture.
In the interaction picture this equation can be equivalently written as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho^{I}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\rm i}[H^{I}_{I},\rho^{I}]\,,

(103)

where
H^{I}_{I}
is given by Eq. (
70
), and has the formal solution

\rho^{I}(t)=\rho^{I}(0)-{\rm i}\int_{0}^{t}[H^{I}_{I}(s),\rho^{I}(s)]\,{\rm d}s\,.

(104)

Plugging (
104
) into (
103
) and doing a partial trace over the bath, we arrive at an equation of motion for the spin ensemble alone that reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\rm i}\,\text{tr}_{B}\{[H_{I}(t),\rho(0)]\}-\int_{0}^{t}\text{tr}_{B}\{[H_{I}(t),[H_{I}(s),\rho(s)]]\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

(105)

where we have omitted the interaction label (superscript “
I
”) for notational simplicity.

Observe that, implicit in the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{I}
, the first term is formally
\mathcal{O}(G_{F})
, while the second term is
\mathcal{O}(G_{F}^{2})
, and repeatedly plugging Eq. (
104
) into (
105
) would yield in principle a perturbative series in
G_{F}
. Considering however that the interactions of interest are very weak, it suffices to iterate only once to capture the leading-order effects. Physically, the first,
\mathcal{O}(G_{F})
term in Eq. (
105
) corresponds to the so-called Stodolsky effect
Stodolsky:1974aq
, which can be most easily seen in the Schrödinger picture where it manifests as a shift in the energy levels of the spin ensemble,
provided
that a non-zero neutrino-antineutrino asymmetry is present. We shall therefore not be concerned with this shift and will henceforth drop the
\mathcal{O}(G_{F})
term.

To discern the role of the second,
\mathcal{O}(G_{F}^{2})
term in Eq. (
105
), we apply the Markovian approximation, which is valid under two assumptions: (i) The bath’s correlation time is much shorter than the spin ensemble’s relaxation time. This ensures the bath quickly loses memory of the interaction, thereby preventing information from flowing back to the spin ensemble; and (ii) the bath does not change much over time. This is the case for the relatively weak interactions under consideration. Implementing the Markovian approximation consists formally in changing
\rho(s)\rightarrow\rho(t)
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
105
), i.e.,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{0}^{t}\text{tr}_{B}\{[H_{I}(t),[H_{I}(s),\rho(t)]]\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

(106)

such that any step taken by the spin ensemble at any time
t
depends only on the state of the system at that time. Equation (
106
) is the so-called Redfield equation.
Next, we define a new integration variable
s^{\prime}=t-s
and take the integration limit to
\infty
(or, equivalently in terms of the original integration variable, we take the lower limit to
s\to-\infty
). This approximation leads us to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{tr}_{B}\{[H_{I}(t),[H_{I}(t-s),\rho(t)]]\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

(107)

and corresponds to assuming that the correlations decay fast enough that the dynamics of the system remains the same irrespective of what happened at earlier times.

At this point we can simply evaluate Eq. (
107
) for the Hamiltonian (
70
). A brute-force evaluation will yield an intractably long expression. However, because the neutrino-spin interaction rate is expected to be much smaller than the Larmor frequency
\omega_{0}=g\mu_{N}B
(i.e., many oscillations can occur over
a time scale dictated by the interaction rate), we may adopt the so-called rotating-wave/secular approximation, which amounts to neglecting fast-oscillation terms of the form
e^{\pm{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}
or
e^{\pm 2{\rm i}\omega_{0}t}
. Doing so yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle(J_{-}\rho_{S}J_{+}-J_{+}J_{-}\rho_{S})\Gamma_{+-}^{(2)}(-\omega_{0})+(J_{+}\rho_{S}J_{-}-\rho_{S}J_{-}J_{+})\Gamma_{-+}^{(1)}(-\omega_{0})+(J_{+}\rho_{S}J_{-}-J_{-}J_{+}\rho_{S})\Gamma_{-+}^{(2)}(\omega_{0})

(108)

\displaystyle+(J_{-}\rho_{S}J_{+}-\rho_{S}J_{+}J_{-})\Gamma_{+-}^{(1)}(\omega_{0})+(J_{z}\rho_{S}J_{z}-J_{z}J_{z}\rho_{S})\Gamma_{zz}^{(2)}(0)+(J_{z}\rho_{S}J_{z}-\rho_{S}J_{z}J_{z})\Gamma_{zz}^{(1)}(0)\,,

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{(1)}(\omega_{0})\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{\rm i}\omega_{0}s}\,\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma_{\alpha}(t-s)\Sigma_{\beta}(t)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

(109)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{(2)}(\omega_{0})\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{\rm i}\omega_{0}s}\,\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma_{\alpha}(t)\Sigma_{\beta}(t-s)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s\,.

Note that
\Sigma_{\alpha}
is an operator in the interaction picture and hence depends on time.

Rearranging Eq. (
108
) to separate the hermitian and non-hermitian terms yields the Lindblad equation for our system:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle-{\rm i}\,[G_{-}J_{+}J_{-}+G_{+}J_{-}J_{+}+G_{z}J_{z}J_{z},\rho_{S}]

(110)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{-}(J_{-}\rho_{S}J_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{+}J_{-},\rho_{S}\})+\gamma_{+}(J_{+}\rho_{S}J_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{-}J_{+},\rho_{S}\})+\gamma_{z}(J_{z}\rho_{S}J_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{z}J_{z},\rho_{S}\})\,,

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}\equiv

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+-}^{(1)}(\omega_{0})+\Gamma_{+-}^{2}(-\omega_{0})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}s}\,\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma_{+}(0)\Sigma_{-}(s)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

(111)

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}\equiv

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-+}^{(2)}(\omega_{0})+\Gamma_{-+}^{(1)}(-\omega_{0})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}s}\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma_{-}(s)\Sigma_{+}(0)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{z}\equiv

\displaystyle\Gamma_{zz}^{(1)}(0)+\Gamma_{zz}^{(2)}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma_{z}(0)\Sigma_{z}(s)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s,\,

and

\displaystyle G_{-}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{+-}^{(2)}(-\omega_{0})-\Gamma_{+-}^{(1)}(\omega_{0})}{2{\rm i}}\,,

(112)

\displaystyle G_{+}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{-+}^{(2)}(\omega_{0})-\Gamma_{-+}^{(1)}(-\omega_{0})}{2{\rm i}}\,,

\displaystyle G_{z}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{zz}^{(2)}(0)-\Gamma_{zz}^{(1)}(0)}{2{\rm i}}\,,

and we have assumed that the bath is constant in time. Observe that the first, Hermitian term in Eq. (
110
) commutes with
H_{S}
. Its form therefore remains unchanged in Schrödinger picture and represents an energy shift in the free Hamiltonian that is suppressed by
G_{F}^{2}
through the bath-dependent coefficients
G_{\pm,z}
. The remaining terms are non-hermitian and characterise dissipative effects in the spin ensemble due to the bath via the
\gamma_{\pm,z}
coefficients.
Note that if the
g
-factor was negative, e.g., in the case of electrons, the Larmor frequency
\omega_{0}
would be positive, which would effectively exchange
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
in the Lindblad equation.

Appendix D
Computation of
\gamma_{\pm},\gamma_{z}

Our objective now is to compute
\gamma_{+}
,
\gamma_{-}
and
\gamma_{z}
, which requires that we calculate correlators of
\Sigma_{\alpha}
that depend on the neutrino fields. To this end, we assume that neutrinos are Dirac fermions, whose expansion in modes is given by

\nu_{i}(t,\vec{x})=\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\;\left(a^{s}_{{\rm i},\vec{p}}u_{i,s}(\vec{p})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}+b^{s{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}v_{i,s}(\vec{p})e^{{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}\right)\,,

(113)

where

,
u_{s}(\vec{p})
and
v_{s}(\vec{p})
are the spinor solutions of the Dirac equation, and the creation/annihilation operators obey the anticommutation relations

\{a^{s}_{i,\vec{p}},a^{r{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{q}}\}=\{b^{s}_{i,\vec{p}},b^{r{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{q}}\}=(2\pi)^{3}2p^{0}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}-\vec{q})\delta_{rs}\delta_{ij}\,.

(114)

All other anticommutators of
a^{s}_{i,\vec{p}},a^{r{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{q}},b^{s}_{i,\vec{p}},b^{r{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{q}}
are zero.

We start with
\gamma_{+}
given in Eq. (
111
), which evaluates using (
68
) to

(115)

where have introduced the notations
P_{L}\equiv(1-\gamma^{5})/2
and
\Upsilon_{\pm}\equiv\gamma^{1}\pm{\rm i}\gamma^{2}

Substituting Eq. (
113

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}=

\displaystyle+e^{{\rm i}[(-q^{0}-q^{\prime 0})s-(\vec{p}+\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-\vec{q}-\vec{q}\,)\cdot\vec{x}]}\,\bar{u}_{i,a}(\vec{p})\;\Upsilon_{-}\;P_{L}v_{j,b}(\vec{p}\,^{\prime})\bar{v}_{k,c}(\vec{q})\;\Upsilon_{+}\;P_{L}u_{l,d}(\vec{q}\,^{\prime})\,\text{tr}_{B}\left\{a^{a{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}b^{b{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{p}^{\prime}}b^{c}_{k,\vec{q}}a^{d}_{l,\vec{q}^{\prime}}\rho_{B}\right\}

To compute the thermal traces of the bath, we assume that each neutrino species assumes a thermal distribution, such that the background density matrix is given by

\rho_{B}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{B}}}{\text{tr}_{B}\left\{e^{-\beta H_{B}}\right\}}\,,

(117)

where

H_{B}=\sum_{i}\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\;p^{0}\,(a^{s{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}a^{s}_{i,\vec{p}}+b^{s{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}b^{s}_{i,\vec{p}})\,.

Using this information and the anticommutation relations (
114

\displaystyle\text{tr}_{B}\left\{a^{a{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}a^{b}_{j,\vec{p}^{\prime}}a^{c{\dagger}}_{k,\vec{q}}a^{d}_{l,\vec{q}^{\prime}}\rho_{B}\right\}=\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}N_{i,a}(p^{0})N_{k,c}(q^{0})+\delta_{q^{\prime}p}\delta_{p^{\prime}q}(1-N_{k,c}(q^{0}))N_{i,a}(p^{0})\,,

\displaystyle\text{tr}_{B}\left\{a^{a{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}a^{b}_{j,\vec{p}^{\prime}}b^{c}_{k,\vec{q}}b^{d{\dagger}}_{l,\vec{q}^{\prime}}\rho_{B}\right\}=\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}N_{i,a}(p^{0})(1-\bar{N}_{k,c}(q^{0}))\,,

\displaystyle\text{tr}_{B}\left\{b^{a}_{i,\vec{p}}b^{b{\dagger}}_{j,\vec{p}^{\prime}}a^{c{\dagger}}_{k,\vec{q}}a^{d}_{l,\vec{q}^{\prime}}\rho_{B}\right\}=\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\delta_{qq^{\prime}}N_{k,c}(q^{0})(1-\bar{N}_{i,a}(p^{0}))\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}

\displaystyle=G_{F}^{2}\pi\sum_{ik}g_{A}^{i,k}g_{A}^{k,i}\sum_{ac}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\int{\rm d}\tilde{q}

\displaystyle\frac{4G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{ik}g_{A}^{i,k}g_{A}^{k,i}\Bigg[\int_{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-m_{k})^{2}-m_{i}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}q_{k-}\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+q_{k-}^{2}}\;\left((1-N_{k}(q_{k-}^{0}))N_{i}(p^{0})+(1-\bar{N}_{k}(q_{k-}^{0}))\bar{N}_{i}(p^{0})\right)

(123)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-m_{k})^{2}-m_{i}^{2}}}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}q_{k^{\prime}}\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+q_{k^{\prime}}^{2}}\,\left(1-\bar{N}_{i}(p^{0})\right)\left(1-\bar{N}_{k}(q_{k^{\prime}}^{0})\right)\Bigg]\,,

with
q_{k-}=[(\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m_{i}^{2}}+\omega_{0})^{2}-m_{k}^{2}]^{1/2}
,
q_{k-}^{0}=\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+q_{-}^{2}}
,
q_{k^{\prime}}=[(\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m_{i}^{2}}-\omega_{0})^{2}-m_{k}^{2}]^{1/2}
, and
q_{k}^{\prime 0}=\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}+q_{k}^{\prime 2}}
. Again, this expression can be contrasted with the heuristic rate (
82
), which does not contain the second integral that contributes only if
m_{k}+m_{i}<\omega_{0}
.

For
\gamma_{z}
we find

\gamma_{z}=\frac{16G_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{ik}g_{A}^{i,k}g_{A}^{k,i}\int_{\sqrt{m_{k}^{2}-m_{i}^{2}}}^{\infty}{\rm d}|\vec{p}|\;|\vec{p}|^{2}\sqrt{|\vec{p}|^{2}+m_{i}^{2}-m_{k}^{2}}\sqrt{m_{i}^{2}+|\vec{p}|^{2}}\,\left(N_{i}(p^{0})(1-N_{k}(p^{0}))+\bar{N}_{i}(p^{0})(1-\bar{N}_{k}(p^{0}))\right)\,.

(124)

Contrary to
\gamma_{\pm}
, this term does not describe energy exchange processes. Rather, it describes the collective dephasing of the spin ensemble due to elastic scattering.

In the case of Majorana neutrinos, the main difference in the computation of
\gamma_{\pm,z}
is that the expansion in modes of the neutrino fields is given by

\nu_{i}(t,\vec{x})=\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\;\left(a^{s}_{{\rm i},\vec{p}}u_{i,s}(\vec{p})e^{-{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}+a^{s{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}v_{i,s}(\vec{p})e^{{\rm i}(p^{0}t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x})}\right)\,,

(125)

and the corresponding bath Hamiltonian is

H_{B}=\sum_{i}\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}\tilde{p}\;p^{0}\,a^{s{\dagger}}_{i,\vec{p}}a^{s}_{i,\vec{p}}\,.

(126)

If we neglect pair absorption and emission (which, as discussed above, are normally not kinematically allowed in NMR experiments except for close-to-zero neutrino masses), the expressions for
\gamma_{\pm,z}
—Eqs. (
122
), (
123
), and (
124
)—are exactly identical for Dirac and Majorana neutrinos. The only difference between the two cases occurs in the value of the occupation number
N_{i,a}(p^{0})
. In the Dirac case, when ultra-relativistic neutrinos decouple from the early universe plasma, they are always in a left-helicity state. However, as the universe cools down and the neutrinos become non-relativistic, we would generically expect them to relax via gravitational interactions into an approximately equal number of left- and right-helicity states with an average occupation number (assuming no neutrino-antineutrino asymmetry) given by

N_{i,a}(p^{0})=\bar{N}_{i,a}(p^{0})\approx n_{F}(|\vec{p}|)/2
, where

n_{F}(|\vec{p}|)=\frac{1}{e^{|\vec{p}|/T+1}}

(127)

is the relativistic Fermi-Dirac distribution.
On the other hand, Majorana neutrinos are their own antiparticles. Thus, relaxation into left- and right-helicity states merely turn an neutrino into an antineutrino and vice versa,
leading to an occupation number of
N_{i,a}(p^{0})=\bar{N}_{i,a}(p^{0})\approx n_{F}(|\vec{p}|)
.

Consider now a system comprising

\displaystyle\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{z}=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{tr}_{B}\{\Sigma^{\alpha}_{3}(0)\Sigma^{\beta}_{3}(s)\rho_{B}\}\,{\rm d}s\,,

Appendix F
Full numerical solutions

We demonstrate here how we find a full numerical solution of the Lindblad master equation (
42
) for the spin ensemble’s density matrix
\rho_{S}
. We show first the case of collective neutrino interactions only. This effectively restricts the dynamics to the fully symmetric subspace of total angular momentum
j=N/2
, where numerical solutions can scale with
N^{3}
(see Ref.
PhysRevA.98.063815
for a detailed discussion) and are hence tractable for relatively large values of
N
. We then extend the numerical treatment to include local interactions, where the dynamics can no longer be restricted to the
j=N/2
manifold.

In the presence of coherent interactions alone, we set
\gamma_{\phi}^{\rm loc}=0
, and solve

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}{\cal D}_{{\cal J}_{-}}[\rho_{S}]+\gamma_{+}{\cal D}_{{\cal J}_{+}}[\rho_{S}]

(134)

\displaystyle=L_{D}[\rho_{S}]\,,

where we have introduced the Liouvillian super-operator at the second equality, expressed in the Dicke basis. We work in Liouville space by vectorising (i.e., column-stacking) the density matrix
\rho_{S}\to|\rho_{S}\rangle
and using the Liouvillian super-operator

L_{D}=\sum_{\pm}\gamma_{\pm}\left({\cal J}_{\mp}^{\top}\otimes{\cal J}_{\pm}-\frac{1}{2}I\otimes{\cal J}_{\mp}{\cal J}_{\pm}-\frac{1}{2}({\cal J}_{\mp}{\cal J}_{\pm})^{\top}\otimes I\right)\,,

(135)

where the action of the operators on
\rho_{S}
from the left and the right becomes a Kronecker product, namely,
X\rho_{S}Y\to(Y^{\top}\otimes X)|\rho_{S}\rangle
.

To numerically integrate the master equation (
134
), we implement standard time-integration schemes such as the Euler method, as well as higher-order solvers such as the fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) algorithm. To ensure numerical stability, especially for such explicit integration schemes,
we compute the spectral radius of the Liouvillian, i.e., the largest eigenvalue of
L_{D}
,
\lambda_{\rm max}
, and set the time step
\Delta t
such that
\Delta t\,\lambda_{\rm max}\ll 1
. This criterion ensures convergence and bounds the error at each step, particularly for stiff systems or rapidly decaying modes. In practice, we choose
\Delta t=h/\lambda_{\rm max}
with
h\sim 0.1
depending on the desired accuracy. We have additionally tested implicit integration methods and found no substantial improvement in convergence compared to explicit methods, provided the timestep factor
h
is sufficiently small. Given their significantly lower computational cost, explicit schemes remain the optimal choice for efficiency in this context.
