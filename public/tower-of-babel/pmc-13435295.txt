Endoscopic Technique for Iliotibial Band and Gluteus Maximus Tendon Lengthening in the Treatment of External Snapping Hip Syndrome

ABSTRACT

External snapping hip (ESH) affects approximately 5% to 10% of the population. 1 ESH is caused by the iliotibial band (ITB) snapping over the greater trochanter, typically due to thickening of the posterior ITB and a thigh attachment of the gluteus maximus to the femur anchoring the ITB closer to the greater trochanter. 2 , 3 Although often asymptomatic in its early stages, repetitive snapping can lead to irritation of the trochanteric bursa, resulting in inflammation and lateral hip pain. 4 , 5

Conservative management is typically the first‐line treatment for ESH and includes physical therapy, stretching, and targeted corticosteroid injections. 3 , 4 Traditionally, surgical interventions such as endoscopic or open release of the ITB are reserved for recalcitrant cases. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Both approaches have shown favorable patient‐reported outcomes, with low complication and recurrence rates. 5 , 10 , 11 , 12

We present a step‐by‐step guide to the endoscopic treatment of recalcitrant ESH. This approach can be performed in isolation to address ESH or in conjunction with procedures for intra‐articular pathology, such as femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) and labral tears. It involves releasing the ITB and addressing the gluteus maximus tendon at its femoral attachment, aiming to minimize recurrence and provide sustained pain relief.

Step‐by‐Step Surgical Technique

The patient is positioned supine on a traction table; however, traction is not required for this technique. The operative leg is mobilized into 20°‐30° of abduction.

If performed in isolation, the visualization portal is established under fluoroscopic guidance, located approximately 2 cm anterior and 2 cm distal to the greater trochanter. A second (working) portal is then created under direct visualization, positioned 3 cm lateral and 3 cm posterior to the visualization portal (Figure 1A).

(A) Portal placement depicted in left hip for isolated cases of ESH. (B) Portal placement depicted in left hip for ESH cases concomitant with hip arthroscopy. (AL, anterolateral portal; DALA, distal anterolateral portal; ESH, external snapping hip; GT, greater trochanter.)

If the procedure is performed following hip arthroscopy, the distal anterolateral accessory portal may be used for visualization, and the anterolateral portal as the working portal (Figure 1B). Fluoroscopic guidance is recommended to ensure accurate orientation (Figure 2).

Intraoperative anteroposterior (AP) fluoroscopic image of the left hip demonstrating cannula placement (solid black arrow) through the distal anterolateral accessory (DALA) portal. The cannula is advanced just inferior to the greater trochanter, providing safe entry into the peritrochanteric space.

Once in the peritrochanteric space, a partial trochanteric bursectomy is performed to improve visualization and facilitate identification of the femoral insertion of the gluteus maximus tendon, as well as the anterior and posterior borders of the ITB (Figures 3, 4).

Intraoperative view of the left hip through distal anterolateral accessory (DALA) portal, viewing the anterior border of the iliotibial band (white arrow) and posterior border of the iliotibial band (black arrow).

Intraoperative view of the left hip through distal anterolateral accessory (DALA) portal, viewing gluteus maximus tendon insertion.

The release is then performed using a radiofrequency wand at the level of the vastus ridge, following an “L”‐shaped pattern (Figure 5). The release begins with a transverse incision from the anterior border of the ITB extending posteriorly. Upon reaching the posterior border, the incision is turned distally to create a longitudinal cut, continuing until the gluteus maximus tendon insertion is fully released (Figures 6, 7).

Intraoperative view of the left hip through distal anterolateral accessory (DALA) portal, viewing iliotibial band (black arrow) release starting from the anterior border towards the posterior border.

DISCUSSION

Surgical treatment for ESH in the hip preservation literature has shown favorable outcomes, both when performed in isolation and when combined with hip arthroscopy. 5 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 A recent trend has emerged favoring minimally invasive techniques due to their improved results. For example, Pierce et al. conducted a systematic review of patients with isolated, painful ESH who failed conservative management and subsequently underwent surgical intervention. 7 The review included 7 studies with a mean follow‐up of 39 months, comprising 46 patients treated endoscopically and 67 treated with open surgery. The authors reported excellent outcomes, with a recurrence rate of 7% (8 patients: 6 open, 2 endoscopic), and only 1 patient requiring revision surgery to further address the gluteus maximus tendon attachment.

Furthermore, Randelli et al. recently reviewed the literature comparing surgical options for ESH, including open and endoscopic techniques. 10 They found that endoscopic approaches were associated with favorable clinical outcomes, lower recurrence rates, and fewer complications compared to open procedures.

In cases of painful ESH concomitant with FAI, Zhang et al. examined 23 hips treated with ITB release as part of comprehensive arthroscopic management for both FAI and ESH. 16 Their technique involved a transverse ITB release without addressing the gluteus maximus tendon attachment. These patients were propensity matched to a control group of FAI patients without ESH. Both groups achieved significant and comparable improvements in the modified Harris Hip Score, International Hip Outcome Tool, and Visual Analog Scale scores, with no significant differences in abduction strength, complication rates, or revision rates at 24‐month follow‐up.

The present study describes a stepwise endoscopic approach for treating ESH, involving release of both the ITB and the gluteus maximus tendon attachment to the femur using an “L”‐shaped configuration. This technique is designed to minimize recurrence and provide sustained pain relief and can be effectively performed either in isolation or in combination with hip arthroscopy for addressing concomitant intra‐articular pathology.
