Effective field theory for weakly bound two-neutron halo nuclei: corrections from neutron-neutron effective range
Davi B. Costa, Masaru Hongo, Dam Thanh Son

Abstract
Using an effective field-theoretical approach, we investigate the properties of weakly bound two-neutron halo nuclei (also known as Borromean nuclei) that do not support a low-energy s s -wave core-neutron resonance. Extending the recently formulated effective field theory for weakly bound Borromean nuclei, we incorporate corrections arising from the effective range of neutron-neutron scattering and evaluate their impact on the mean-square radii and electromagnetic response. In particular, we compute the ratio of the matter and charge radii, the shape of the E ​ 1 E1 dipole strength function, and the electric polarizability. Our results indicate that these corrections remain numerically small when the two-neutron separation energy of the Borromean nucleus is much less than 1 MeV.

II.1
Effective Lagrangian

k=(0,{\bm{k}}),\quad p=\biggl(-B+\frac{{\bm{k}}^{2}}{8m_{h}},\bm{0}\biggr),

(29)

where the “photon” is assumed to couple to either the electric charge or dineutron densities, as we now explain.

In what follows, we consider two distinct “photons”: one is the usual photon
A_{\mu}^{\mathrm{em}}
that couples to the electric charge, and the other is a hypothetical one
A_{\mu}^{\mathrm{nn}}
—referred to as the “dineutron photon”—which minimally couples to the neutrons in the halo but not to those in the core.
The dineutron photon can be theoretically introduced as a U(1) gauge boson coupled to
B-\frac{A}{Z}Q
, where
B
is the baryon number,
Q
is the electric charge, and
Z
and
A
are the atomic and mass numbers of the core, respectively.
Recalling the charges assigned to each field, we introduce terms responsible for the two distinct photon couplings by replacing the partial derivatives of each field in the effective Lagrangian (
1
)–(
2
) with the following covariant derivatives:

\displaystyle D_{\mu}h

\displaystyle\equiv(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}ZA_{\mu}^{\mathrm{em}}-2\mathrm{i}A_{\mu}^{\mathrm{nn}})h\,,

(30a)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}\phi

\displaystyle\equiv(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}ZA_{\mu}^{\mathrm{em}})\phi\,,

(30b)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}n

\displaystyle\equiv(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}A_{\mu}^{\mathrm{nn}})n\,,

(30c)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}d

\displaystyle\equiv(\partial_{\mu}-2\mathrm{i}A_{\mu}^{\mathrm{nn}})d\,,

(30d)

where
\mu
runs over the spacetime indices
t,x,y,z
.
In this framework, the charge (or dineutron) form factor can be computed by assigning these “photon” in Eq. (
28
) to be either the usual photon or the dineutron photon.

In addition to the two distinct photons, one can introduce a mass “photon” to calculate the matter radius
\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle
.
However, the matter radius can also be derived from the charge and dineutron radii, as they are interrelated.
To demonstrate this, recall that our EFT models the core and neutrons as point-like particles.
Let
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}
represent the core’s coordinates, and
\bm{r}_{1}
and
\bm{r}_{2}
represent the coordinates of two neutrons.
For convenience, we define the Jacobi coordinates as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{r}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2},

(31)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{A}{A+2}}(\bm{r}_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}-2\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}),

(32)

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{\text{cm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A+2}(\bm{r}_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}+A\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}).

(33)

Here, the normalization of
\bm{\rho}
is chosen so that the kinetic part of the first-quantized Hamiltonian becomes symmetric under rotations in the six-dimensional space
(\bm{r},\bm{\rho})
.

-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{r}_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{r}_{2}^{2}}+\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}^{2}}\right)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{r}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\rho}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2(A+2)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{R}_{\text{cm}}^{2}}\,,

(34)

We note that the ground state of the halo nucleus is assumed to be symmetric under the exchange of the two neutrons
\bm{r}_{1}\leftrightarrow\bm{r}_{2}
, which implies
\langle\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\rho}\rangle=0
.
The charge, dineutron, and matter radii are then expressed as

\displaystyle\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle=\frac{\langle\bm{\rho}^{2}\rangle}{A(A+2)}\,,

(35a)

\displaystyle\langle r_{n}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle(\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle+\langle(\bm{r}_{2}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle\Big]=\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{A}{A+2}\langle\bm{\rho}^{2}\rangle+\langle\bm{r}^{2}\rangle\right]\,,

(35b)

\displaystyle\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A+2}\Big[\langle(\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle+\langle(\bm{r}_{2}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle+A\langle(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}-\bm{R}_{\text{cm}})^{2}\rangle\Big]=\frac{\langle\bm{\rho}^{2}\rangle+\langle\bm{r}^{2}\rangle}{2(A+2)}\,.

(35c)

As a result, the matter radius can be expressed in terms of the dineutron and charge radii as

\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle=\frac{2}{A+2}\langle r_{n}^{2}\rangle+\frac{A}{A+2}\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle\,.

(36)

Moreover, the ratio of the matter radius to the charge radius is directly related to the ratio of the mean squares of the two Jacobi coordinates:

\frac{\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle}{\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{A}{2}\left(1+\frac{\langle\bm{r}^{2}\rangle}{\langle\bm{\rho}^{2}\rangle}\right).

(37)

\frac{\delta\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle}{\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle_{0}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{3\pi}{16}\sqrt{\frac{B}{\epsilon_{0}}}}\,,&B\gg\epsilon_{n},\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}\frac{B}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}\epsilon_{0}}}}\,,&B\ll\epsilon_{n}.\end{cases}

(63)

Figure

shows this correction as a function of
B
.
From this, we observe that the correction is numerically not so large: it does not exceed approximately 25% for
B<1
MeV.
The largeness of the relative correction to
\langle\bm{r}^{2}\rangle
is compensated by the fact that this Jacobi coordinate contributes only a small fraction (
1/4
for
B\gg\epsilon_{n}
) to the mean square matter radius.

Finally, we discuss the magnitude of the effective-range correction to the ratio of the matter radius to the charge radius.
Substituting Eqs. (

) and (

) into Eq. (

\frac{\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle}{\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{A}{2}\left[1+\frac{f_{n}(\beta)+\tilde{r}_{0}f_{n}^{(1)}(\beta))}{f_{c}(\beta)+\tilde{r}_{0}f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)}\right].

(64)

In Fig.

As indicated by the range on the vertical axis, the

IV
Electromagnetic response

In this section, we investigate the electromagnetic response of halo nuclei.
After analyzing the effective-range correction to the
E1
dipole strength function in Sec.
IV.1
, we present our results on electric polarizability in Sec.
IV.2
.

IV.1
E1
dipole strength function

We here compute the effective-range correction to the
E1
dipole strength function, defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(\omega)\equiv\sum_{n}|\langle n|\bm{\mathcal{M}}|0\rangle|^{2}\delta(E_{n}-E_{0}-\omega),

(65)

where the dipole operator is defined as
\bm{\mathcal{M}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}Ze(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}-\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cm}})
, with
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}
and
\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cm}}
denoting the coordinates of the core and the center of mass, respectively.
We also introduce a sum over all excited states of the halo,
|n\rangle
, with eigenvalues
E_{n}
(
n=0
corresponding to the ground state).
The
E1
dipole strength function measures the spectral weight of the excited states induced by the insertion of the dipole operator, characterizing how the halo nucleus responds to external electromagnetic fields.

As shown in Ref.
[
34
]
, by noting that
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\bm{J}
, where
\bm{J}
is the total electric current operator carried by the halo, we can express the
E1
dipole strength function as

\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(\omega)=-\frac{3}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\pi\omega^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}G_{JJ}(\omega),

(66)

where the two-point correlation function of the total electric current is defined as

\mathrm{i}G_{JJ}(\omega)\equiv\int\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\langle 0|T\bm{J}(t)\cdot\bm{J}(0)|0\rangle,

(67)

Since the insertion of the total current operator corresponds to an external photon with vanishing momentum, one finds a diagrammatic representation of
G_{JJ}(\omega)
in Fig.
10
, where we use the simplified notation
\omega=(\omega,\bm{0})
.
Moreover, we assume that the initial state of the halo nucleus is a static on-shell ground state, and therefore set
p=(-B,\bm{0})
.

Figure 10:
The Feynman diagram determining the
E1
dipole strength function.

By collecting another diagram generated by reversing the direction of momentum flow
\omega=(\omega,\bm{0})
on the two photon lines in Fig.
10
, we obtain
\mathrm{i}G_{JJ}(\omega)
as

\begin{split}\mathrm{i}G_{JJ}(\omega)&=(\mathrm{i}g)^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\mathrm{i}G_{\phi}(q)\frac{Ze{\bm{q}}}{m_{\phi}}\mathrm{i}G_{\phi}(q+\omega)\frac{Ze{\bm{q}}}{m_{\phi}}\mathrm{i}G_{\phi}(q)\mathrm{i}D(p-q)\\
&=\mathrm{i}(Ze)^{2}\frac{g^{2}}{m_{\phi}^{2}\omega^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{q}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}q^{2}D\left(\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2m_{\phi}},-{\bm{q}}\right)+(\omega\to-\omega),\end{split}

(68)

where we performed the
q_{0}
-integral by completing the contour in the lower-half plane.
Taking the imaginary part and setting
\omega>0
, we find that the second term vanishes, leading to

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}G_{JJ}(\omega)&=(Ze)^{2}\frac{g^{2}}{m_{\phi}^{2}\omega^{2}}\!\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{q}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,{\bm{q}}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}D\left(\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2m_{\phi}}\,,-{\bm{q}}\right),\\
&=-(Ze)^{2}\frac{4\pi g^{2}}{m_{\phi}^{2}\omega^{2}}\!\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{q}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,{\bm{q}}^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2\mu}}}{\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2\mu}+\left[\frac{1}{a}-\frac{r_{0}}{2}\left(\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2\mu}\right)\right]^{2}}\theta\left(\omega-B-\frac{{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2\mu}\right).\end{split}

(69)

which generalizes the result of Ref.
[
34
]
by incorporating the dimer propagator with the effective-range correction.

Performing the remaining
{\bm{q}}
-integral, we obtain the analytic formula for the
E1
dipole strength with the first-order effective-range correction (see Appendix
B.3
for details):

\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=\frac{3}{4\pi}(Ze)^{2}\frac{12g^{2}}{\pi}\frac{A^{1/2}}{(A+2)^{5/2}}\theta(\omega-B)\frac{(\omega-B)^{2}}{\omega^{4}}\left[f_{E1}\left(\frac{1}{-a\sqrt{\omega-B}}\right)+\frac{r_{0}}{a}f^{(1)}_{E1}\left(\frac{1}{-a\sqrt{\omega-B}}\right)\right],

(70)

where we defined two dimensionless functions

\displaystyle f_{E1}(x)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{8}{3}x(1+x^{2})^{3/2}+4x^{2}\Big(1+\frac{2}{3}x^{2}\Big),

(71)

\displaystyle f_{E1}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=1-4x(1+2x^{2})\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+8x^{2}(1+x^{2}).

(72)

The asymptotic behavior of the dimensionless functions
f_{E1}(x)
and
f_{E1}^{(1)}(x)
in the limits of small and large
x
are

f_{E1}(x)\to\begin{cases}\displaystyle{1-\frac{8}{3}x+O(x^{2})},&x\ll 1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{6x^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{x^{4}}\right)},&x\gg 1,\end{cases}\qquad f_{E1}^{(1)}(x)\to\begin{cases}1-4x+O(x^{2}),&x\ll 1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{16x^{4}}+O\left(\frac{1}{x^{6}}\right)},&x\gg 1.\end{cases}

(73)

In particular, in the unitarity limit of the neutron-neutron interaction (
a=\infty
,
r_{0}=0
), both dimensionless functions reduce to unity, resulting in a particularly simple form for the
E1
dipole strength:

\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\bigg|_{\text{unitarity}}=\frac{3}{4\pi}(Ze)^{2}\frac{12g^{2}}{\pi}\frac{A^{1/2}}{(A+2)^{5/2}}\theta(\omega-B)\frac{(\omega-B)^{2}}{\omega^{4}}\,.

(74)

It is worth noting that our result, including the effective-range correction, satisfies the sum rule that relates the
E1
dipole strength function to the charge radius
[
34
]
.
This sum rule follows from the definition of the
E1
dipole strength in Eq. (
65
) as

\int\limits_{B}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=\langle 0|\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{2}|0\rangle=\frac{3}{4\pi}(Ze)^{2}\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle,

(75)

and serves as a useful self-consistency check of our calculations.
In our calculation, integrating the
E1
dipole strength over frequency directly confirms that

\int\limits_{B}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=\frac{3}{4\pi}(Ze)^{2}\frac{4g^{2}}{\pi{B}}\frac{A^{1/2}}{(A+2)^{5/2}}\left[f_{c}(\beta)+\tilde{r}_{0}f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)\right]=\frac{3}{4\pi}(Ze)^{2}\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle,

(76)

where we used the following integrals:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{y^{4}}f_{E1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{y-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}f_{c}(\beta),

(77)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{y^{4}}f^{(1)}_{E1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{y-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3\beta}f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta).

(78)

with
f_{c}(\beta)
and
f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)
defined in Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
).

Let us now evaluate the magnitude of the effective-range correction to the
E1
dipole strength function.
It is important to recall that our calculation does not determine the overall normalization of the
E1
dipole strength due to the presence of the running coupling
g
.
We thus focus on the shape of the
E1
dipole function by normalizing our result (
70
) with Eq. (
76
) and
B
as

\frac{1}{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega}=3\theta(\omega-B)\frac{(\omega/B-1)^{2}}{(\omega/B)^{4}}\frac{f_{E1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\omega/B-1}}\right)+\frac{r_{0}}{a}f^{(1)}_{E1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\omega/B-1}}\right)}{f_{c}(\beta)+\tilde{r}_{0}f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)},

(79)

Figure
12
compares the normalized
E1
dipole functions (
79
) with and without the effective-range correction.
As evident from Fig.
12
, incorporating the effective-range correction does not significantly alter the shape of the
E1
dipole function.

For a more quantitative evaluation of the effective-range correction, we compute the relative correction to the
E1
dipole strength, following from Eq. (
70
), as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(\mathrm{d}B(E1)/\mathrm{d}\omega)}{(\mathrm{d}B(E1)/\mathrm{d}\omega)_{0}}=\frac{r_{0}}{a}\frac{f_{E1}^{(1)}\Big(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\omega/B-1}}\Big)}{f_{E1}\Big(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\omega/B-1}}\Big)}\,.

(80)

The asymptotic behavior of this correction is given by

\frac{\delta(\mathrm{d}B(E1)/\mathrm{d}\omega)}{(\mathrm{d}B(E1)/\mathrm{d}\omega)_{0}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{3}{8}r_{0}a(\omega-B)},&0<\omega-B\ll 1/a^{2},\\
\displaystyle{\frac{r_{0}}{a}}\,,&\omega-B\gg 1/a^{2}.\end{cases}

(81)

As shown in Fig.
12
, the magnitude of the relative correction is numerically small, remaining below
r_{0}/|a|\approx 0.15
at all values considered.

IV.2
Electric polarizability

As another indicator of the electromagnetic response, we compute the electric polarizability
\alpha_{D}
—a measure of how easily the halo nucleus can be deformed by external electric fields.

We first define the electric polarizability by considering the interaction energy between the dipole operator
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
and an applied weak electric field
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
, given by

H_{\mathrm{dipole}}=-\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\bm{\mathcal{M}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{E}}=-Ze(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}-\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cm}})\cdot\bm{\mathcal{E}}.

(82)

Let
|0^{\prime}\rangle
denote the perturbed halo ground state under the applied electric field.
The electric polarizability
\alpha_{D}
is then defined as the linear coefficient of the induced dipole moment:

\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\langle 0^{\prime}|\bm{\mathcal{M}}|0^{\prime}\rangle=\alpha_{D}\bm{\mathcal{E}}.

(83)

Using the standard formula for the perturbed ground state,

|0^{\prime}\rangle=|0\rangle+\sum_{n\neq 0}c_{n}|n\rangle\quad\mathrm{with}\quad c_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\frac{\langle n|\bm{\mathcal{M}}|0\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{0}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{E}},

(84)

we obtain an expression for the electric polarizability:

\alpha_{D}={\frac{1}{3}}\frac{4\pi}{3}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{2|\langle n|\bm{\mathcal{M}}|0\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}-E_{0}}.

(85)

Furthermore, recalling Eq. (
65
), we derive another sum rule that relates the electric polarizability to the
E1
dipole strength function:

\alpha_{D}=\frac{2}{{3}}\frac{4\pi}{3}\int\limits_{B}^{\infty}\frac{\!\mathrm{d}\omega}{\omega}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}B(E1)}{\mathrm{d}\omega},

(86)

Substituting Eq. (
70
) and performing the above integral, we obtain the electric polarizability with the first-order effective-range correction as (see Appendix
B.4
for details)

\alpha_{\text{D}}=(Ze)^{2}\frac{2g^{2}}{3\pi}\frac{A^{1/2}}{(A+2)^{5/2}}\frac{1}{B^{2}}\left[f_{\text{pol}}(\beta)-\tilde{r}_{0}\beta f_{\text{pol}}^{(1)}(\beta)\right],

(87)

where we have introduced two dimensionless functions:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{pol}}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\beta^{2}+2}{2(1-\beta^{2})^{2}}-\frac{3\beta\arccos\beta}{2(1-\beta^{2})^{5/2}}},&\beta<1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{5}}\,,&\beta=1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\beta^{2}+2}{2(\beta^{2}-1)^{2}}-\frac{3\beta\arccosh\beta}{2(\beta^{2}-1)^{5/2}}},&\beta>1,\end{cases}

(88)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{pol}}^{(1)}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{11\beta^{2}+4}{4(1-\beta^{2})^{3}}-\frac{3\beta(2\beta^{2}+3)\arccos\beta}{4(1-\beta^{2})^{7/2}}},&\beta<1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{3}{35}}\,,&\beta=1,\\
\displaystyle{-\frac{11\beta^{2}+4}{4(\beta^{2}-1)^{3}}+\frac{3\beta(2\beta^{2}+3)\arccosh\beta}{4(\beta^{2}-1)^{7/2}}},&\beta>1.\end{cases}

(89)

Note that the functions
f_{\textrm{pol}}(\beta)
and
f_{\textrm{pol}}^{(1)}(\beta)
are continuous across
\beta=1
.
As in the previous sections, we provide alternative expressions for these functions in the regions
\beta<1
and
\beta>1
, explicitly demonstrating that they remain real when their arguments are real. The asymptotic behavior of these functions at large and small
\beta
is given by

f_{\text{pol}}(\beta)\to\begin{cases}1+O(\beta),&\beta\ll 1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\ln\beta}{\beta^{4}}\right)},&\beta\gg 1,\end{cases}\qquad f_{\text{pol}}^{(1)}(\beta)\to\begin{cases}1+O(\beta),&\beta\ll 1,\\
\displaystyle{\frac{6\ln(2\beta)-11}{4\beta^{4}}+O\left(\frac{\ln\beta}{\beta^{6}}\right)},&\beta\gg 1.\end{cases}

(90)

The effective-range correction is suppressed by at least a factor of
\tilde{r}_{0}\beta=r_{0}/|a|\approx 0.15
.

As the rms radii and the
E1
dipole strength function, the electric polarizability is proportional to the running coupling
g^{2}
and its absolute magnitude cannot be predicted in our EFT.
On the other hand, one can construct a ratio involving the electric polarizability
\alpha_{D}
, charge radius,
\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle
and binding energy
B
,

\frac{B\alpha_{\text{D}}}{\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{1}{6}(Ze)^{2}\frac{f_{\text{pol}}(\beta)-\tilde{r}_{0}\beta f_{\text{pol}}^{(1)}(\beta)}{f_{c}(\beta)+\tilde{r}_{0}f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)},

(91)

which we can can predict.
This ratio has a particularly simple form in the unitarity regime of neutron-neutron interaction (
a=\infty
,
r_{0}=0
, or
\beta\to 0,\tilde{r}_{0}=0
):

\frac{B\alpha_{\text{D}}}{\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle}\bigg|_{\text{unitarity}}=\frac{1}{6}(Ze)^{2}.

(92)

At the physical scattering length the value of the ratio is less than the unitarity value.
The correction due to the effective range is rather small, as seen in Fig.
13
.

V
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we evaluated the corrections from the neutron-neutron effective range to various physical quantities of two-neutron halo (Borromean) nuclei.
We computed the effective-range correction to the mean-square radii, demonstrating its contributions to the ratio of the matter and charge radii, as well as the magnitude of the relative corrections to the zero-range result.
Additionally, we evaluated the effective-range corrections to the electromagnetic response, including the
E1
dipole strength function and the electric polarizability.
We found that for many physical quantities, corrections are suppressed by at least a factor of
r_{0}/|a|\approx 0.15
.
The only quantity where the correction is relatively large is the inter-neutron distance
\langle\bm{r}^{2}\rangle
(see Fig.
7
).
For this observable, the correction is already of order 1 when the binding energy of the halo
B
is around
1
MeV.
This suggests that a resummation of the effective-range corrections may be necessary for this particular quantity.

There are several potential directions worth pursuing based on the present work.

One direction is to compute corrections arising from other sources.
As mentioned in Sec.
II.1
, two leading corrections—aside from the neutron-neutron effective range considered in this paper—are accessible within the framework of effective field theory.
The first source of correction involves incorporating the leading irrelevant operator responsible for the core-neutron scattering length, which contributes to Feynman diagrams with three-loop corrections.
The second source arises in cases where a core-neutron
p
-wave resonance is present, whose effects can be treated perturbatively following the method of Ref.
[
22
]
.

Another interesting direction is to explore the correspondence with other formulations, such as the first-quantized approach.
For instance, one may expect that the effective-range correction to the ratio
\langle r_{m}^{2}\rangle/\langle r_{c}^{2}\rangle
can be obtained using the alternative method of Ref.
[
36
]
, where the three-body wave function remains dominated by a single Faddeev component.
On the other hand, an EFT-based computation of the wave function should also be possible.
A direct comparison between our EFT approach and first-quantized methods (such as those based on the Faddeev equation) would be a valuable subject for further investigation.
We defer these interesting directions to future work.

Appendix A
A simple model of a two-neutron halo nucleus without core-halo resonance

In this Appendix, using a simple quantum mechanical model, we demonstrate that a bound state of a core and two neutrons can exist even when only the neutron-neutron interaction is resonant, while the core-neutron interaction remains far from resonance.

Our model consists of a system of two particles (“neutrons”) and a third, infinitely massive particle (“core”).
In contrast to the EFT approach in the main text, the dynamics of the heavy core particle is assumed to be suppressed, serving instead as a fixed source of the neutron-core interaction at its position,
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{core}}=0
.
Modeling this non-resonant neutron-core interaction as an attractive Gaussian potential, we consider the Hamiltonian given by

H=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{r}_{1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{r}_{2}}^{2}-V_{0}\left(e^{-r_{1}^{2}/2}+e^{-r_{2}^{2}/2}\right),

(93)

where
\bm{r}_{1}
and
\bm{r}_{2}
denote the positions of the two neutrons.

On the other hand, the two neutrons interact with each other through a resonant point-like interaction.
This interaction is implemented by imposing the Bethe-Peierls boundary condition on the wave function
[
40
]
.
To express this condition, we introduce the Jacobi coordinates [corresponding to Eqs. (
31
)-(
32
) with
A\to\infty
]:

\bm{\rho}=\bm{r}_{1}+\bm{r}_{2}\,,\qquad\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}.

(94)

and require that the
s
-wave component of the wave function be a singular function of
\bm{r}
as the inter-neutron distance approaches zero (
\bm{r}\to 0
):

\psi(\bm{\rho},\bm{r})\sim\frac{C(\bm{\rho})}{r}+O(r)+(\text{higher partial waves in $\bm{r}$}).

(95)

We now show that this model could support a three-body bound state despite the non-resonant core-neutron interaction.
To this end, we first note that the two-particle subsystem of the core and one neutron forms a bound state when the attraction is sufficiently strong.
Indeed, solving the Schrödinger equation numerically shows that such a bound state appears when
V_{0}
exceeds the critical value
V_{0}^{cn}\approx 0.671
.
We then define
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}
as the minimum value required for the formation of a three-body bound state.
Below, using the variational method, we demonstrate that
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}<V_{0}^{cn}\approx 0.671
.
In other words, we establish the existence of a window,
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}<V_{0}<V_{0}^{cn}\approx 0.671
, in which the attractive potential is too weak to form a two-body bound state but strong enough to support a three-body bound state.

To estimate
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}
, we recall that the energy expectation value for any variational wave function is always an upper bound on the true ground state energy.
In particular, a three-body bound state appears when the variational energy becomes negative for
V_{0}<V_{0}^{cn}(\approx 0.671)
.
We determine the minimum
V_{0}
at which the variational energy reaches zero using two different variational wave functions, thereby confirming that
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}<V_{0}^{cn}(\approx 0.671)
.

As the first simple variational wave function, we take

\psi(\bm{\rho},\bm{r})=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi^{3/2}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})/4}}{r}=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi^{3/2}}e^{-\alpha\rho^{2}/8}\,\frac{e^{-\alpha r^{2}/8}}{r}\,,

(96)

which corresponds to the ground-state wave function of two resonantly interacting particles in a harmonic potential with an appropriately chosen oscillator frequency. This wave function explicitly satisfies the Bethe-Peierls boundary condition.
Using this variational wave function, the energy expectation value is given by

E(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{4V_{0}\alpha^{3/2}}{(1+2\alpha)\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\,.

(97)

Minimizing
E(\alpha)
for a given
V_{0}
, we obtain an upper bound on the ground-state energy of the three-body system. This upper bound reaches zero at
V_{0}=\frac{1}{8}\phi(2\phi+1)^{1/2}\approx 0.416
, where
\phi
is the golden ratio [At this value of
V_{0}
, the minimum of
E(\alpha)
occurs at
\alpha=(\phi-1)/2
)].
Our variational calculation thus establishes that a bound state must exist for
V_{0}>0.416
, providing an upper bound on the critical potential depth:
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}<0.416
.
Notably, this upper bound is less than
2/3
of
V_{0}^{cn}
, the critical value at which the two-body bound state appears.

With an alternative variational approach, we can further refine our estimate.
First, we express the Hamiltonian in terms of the Jacobi coordinates:

H=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{r}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\rho}^{2}}-V_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{(\bm{r}+\bm{\rho})^{2}}{8}\right)-V_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{(\bm{r}-\bm{\rho})^{2}}{8}\right).

(98)

Next, changing to the coordinates
R
,
\alpha
,
\hat{\bm{r}}
, and
\hat{\bm{\rho}}
where
\hat{r}
and
\hat{\bm{\rho}}
denote the directions of
\bm{r}
and
\bm{\rho}
, respectively, and

\displaystyle r=R\sin\alpha,\quad\rho=R\cos\alpha,

(99)

we rewrite the Hamiltonian as

\begin{split}H=&-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}-\frac{5}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}-\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tan\alpha}-\tan\alpha\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\\
&-\frac{1}{R^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\bm{r}}^{2}}-\frac{1}{R^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\bm{\rho}}^{2}}-2V_{0}e^{-R^{2}/8}\cosh\left(\frac{R^{2}}{8}\sin 2\alpha\,\hat{\bm{r}}\cdot\hat{\bm{\rho}}\right).\end{split}

(100)

To construct a variational wave function, we restrict our consideration to isotropic wave functions in
\hat{\bm{r}}
and
\hat{\bm{\rho}}
.
Within this class, the last term in the Hamiltonian can be replaced by its angular average, leading to the effective form

H=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}-\frac{5}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}-\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tan\alpha}-\tan\alpha\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}-2V_{0}e^{-R^{2}/8}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{R^{2}}{8}\sin 2\alpha\right)}{\frac{R^{2}}{8}\sin 2\alpha}\,.

(101)

We further assume a separable ansatz for the wave function:

\psi(R,\alpha)=\frac{\psi(R)}{\sin\alpha}\,.

(102)

which remains consistent with the Bethe-Peierls boundary condition (
95
).
Applying this ansatz simplifies the Hamiltonian to

H=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}-\frac{5}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}-\frac{3}{R^{2}}-2V_{0}e^{-R^{2}/8}\left\langle\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{R^{2}}{8}\sin 2\alpha\right)}{\frac{R^{2}}{8}\sin 2\alpha}\right\rangle_{\alpha},

(103)

where
\langle\rangle_{\alpha}
denotes the average over
\alpha
:

\langle f(\alpha)\rangle_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{4}{\pi}\!\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!d\alpha\,\cos^{2}\alpha\,f(\alpha).

(104)

To compute the variational energy, we expand the wave function in a Taylor series in
\alpha
, average each term, and then resum the series. This procedure yields the effective Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}-\frac{5}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}-\frac{3}{R^{2}}-2V_{0}e^{-R^{2}/8}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\frac{1}{2};1,\frac{3}{2};\frac{R^{4}}{256}\right).

(105)

Solving the Schrödinger equation numerically, we find that this Hamiltonian develops a bound state at
V_{0}\approx 0.3285
, providing an improved estimate for
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}
:
V_{0}^{\text{3bdy}}<0.3285
.
Thus, for a Gaussian-shaped potential, the critical depth required to bind two resonantly interacting particles is less than half the critical depth needed to bind a single particle.

Another way to quantify how far the core-neutron interaction is from resonance is to compute the scattering length and effective range for low-energy neutron scattering on a Gaussian potential with depth
V_{0}=0.3285
.
At this depth, we find the scattering length
a\approx-1.570
and effective range
r_{0}\approx 3.324
.
Clearly, the hierarchy
|a|\gg r_{0}
which one would expect if the neutron-core interaction were near resonance, is not satisfied.

\displaystyle{-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}-1}}+\displaystyle{\frac{\beta\arccosh\beta}{(\beta^{2}-1)^{3/2}}}\,,&\beta>1,\end{cases}

(109)

\displaystyle f_{c}^{(1)}(\beta)=-\frac{1}{2}\!\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{\partial_{y}[yf^{2}(y;-1/\beta)]}{\sqrt{y-1}}

B.3
E1
dipole strength function

We here we present the detailed computation of the
E1

The

We first perform the angular part of the integral and use the Heaviside theta function to rewrite it as
