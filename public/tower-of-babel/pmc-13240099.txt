Recommendations for genetic counseling for individuals at risk of autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease in Latin America

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD) represents a small but impactful subset of Alzheimer's cases. Asymptomatic individuals at genetic risk face substantial personal and family implications when considering predictive testing for known familial variants. Genetic counseling and testing (GCT) frameworks remain limited in Latin America (LatAm). Recommendations for GCT in LatAm were developed through an iterative, multidisciplinary consensus process. Evidence inputs included a structured literature review, site‐level recommendations from participating LatAm centers, and a qualitative synthesis of focus groups with experienced investigators. The resulting model includes pre‐test evaluation, sample collection, result disclosure, and structured follow‐up. Core elements comprise mental health assessment, psychoeducation, exploration of expectations and decision‐making needs, guided disclosure with emotional support, and a suggested 3‐month post‐disclosure reassessment using validated psychological measures. Our framework provides structured guidance for the safe and ethical delivery of GCT for ADAD through a multidisciplinary, culturally informed, and patient‐centered approach in LatAm.Keywords: autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease, cultural adaptation, genetic counseling, Latin America, psychological impact

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia worldwide and represents one of the major public health challenges of our time. 1 The vast majority of AD cases are considered sporadic and result from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, aging, environmental and lifestyle risk factors. In approximately 20% of cases, AD aggregates within familes beyond what would be expected by chance, reflecting a stronger genetic contributions from combinations of common and rare susceptibility variants. Importantly, a small proportion (less than 1%) of AD dementia is caused by germline pathogenic variants in the amyloid beta (Aβ) precursor protein (APP), presenilin 1 (PSEN1), or presenilin 2 (PSEN2) gene, with almost 100% penetrance, generally with an age at onset of symptoms before age 65. 2 , 3 This form of AD is known as autosomal dominant AD (ADAD). ADAD shares an underlying pathologic process similar to sporadic AD and has played a central role in understanding AD biology and the development of disease‐modifying and prevention strategies. 4

Despite their rarity, pathogenic variants in these genes have a disproportionate impact on affected families and health systems, particularly when appearing in multiple family members at a young age. The relatively predictable age at onset and almost complete penetrance allow individuals to be identified at very early or pre‐symptomatic stages. 5 This poses a profound challenge during pivotal life phases, including balancing familial, professional, and financial responsibilities and the loss of several decades of life expectancy. In addition, witnessing the disease in other family members, coupled with awareness of potentially carrying the same risk, can have significant personal implications and profoundly influence an individual's behavior. 6

Genetic testing of familial AD is gaining interest, with more at‐risk individuals seeking information and the availability of new treatments as well as innovative clinical trials for prevention. 7 , 8 Knowledge of genetic status (GS) may support proactive health care planning, reproductive decision‐making, and personal decisions. At the same time, genetic counseling and testing (GCT) in ADAD raise complex ethical, social, legal, and psychological considerations for patients and families that need to be addressed. For all participating family members, it provides a structured process of open communication designed to facilitate understanding of medical and genetic information, its implications, and the significance of each available option, thereby promoting optimal adaptation to the disease or risk condition.

In 2022, researchers from Latin America (LatAm) established the PRograma de Asesoramiento Genético para América Latina (PRAGA) working group to advance GCT services through a standardized and safe protocol tailored to ADAD families in LatAm. Building on accumulated clinical and research experience with ADAD families across the region, the group developed these GCT guidelines, with the objective of providing practical, ethically grounded, and culturally responsive recommendations for the evaluation, counseling, and psychological support of individuals from ADAD families, regardless of whether they choose to pursue or decline disclosure of their genetic testing results.

METHODS

Study design and recommendations development approach

These recommendations were developed using a multidisciplinary, consensus‐based methodology within the PRAGA working group, a network of clinicians and researchers from Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Cuba, México, and Perú. The primary objective was to generate practical, ethically grounded guidance for the delivery of GCT in ADAD, informed by existing evidence and expert clinical experience across Latin American settings.

Evidence sources informing recommendations

To support these recommendations, we employed a mixed‐methods approach combining evidence synthesis and expert consensus. A scoping review of the literature on GCT was conducted to identify existing models, ethical considerations, and reported outcomes. These findings were complemented by multidisciplinary expert input from PRAGA investigators and integrated through an iterative consensus process to develop unified, context‐specific guidance.

Scoping review

We performed a scoping review of the existing literature on GCT for ADAD and other adult‐onset neurodegenerative diseases. This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines. Scoping review findings were synthesized using descriptive thematic analysis to map existing recommendations and identify areas relevant to ADAD genetic counselling. Fully reproducible search strategies for each database and the list of articles selected for the final synthesis can be found in the Supplemental Materials 1 and 2.

Expert input and consensus process

The development of the recommendations was informed by a multidisciplinary panel of experts (n = 24) with recognized experience in the clinical management of ADAD and/or genetic counseling, including specialists in neurology (n = 8), psychiatry (n = 5), psychology (n = 2), clinical genetics/genetic counseling (n = 7), and bioethics (n = 2). The panel included members of the PRAGA working group and experts selected based on predefined criteria, including a minimum of 5 years of relevant clinical or research experience, peer‐reviewed publications in the field, and active involvement in patient care or policy development. They provided written feedback on preliminary drafts and participated in five virtual meetings to discuss key aspects of the recommendations. Final approval of the recommendations was obtained after unanimous agreement by all panel members.

Scope and target population

The scope of the present guidelines is limited to the autosomal dominantly inherited form of AD. Specifically, we address the delivery of genetic counseling for adult relatives of individuals with molecularly confirmed ADAD. The prototypical scenario involves young adults who have usually grown up seeing one of their parents, siblings, and/or other relatives living with AD. 9 Although genetic counselling can be requested at any age, these guidelines are intended for individuals 18 years of age or older and focus on predictive GCT rather than diagnostic evaluation. The working group does not recommend genetic testing before 18 years.

Of note, our recommendations presuppose the existence of a molecularly confirmed ADAD and therefore do not address the upstream diagnostic process required to establish the genetic etiology in families with suspected hereditary AD. This initial step typically involves a comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological evaluation of a symptomatic individual, together with the systematic collection of detailed multigenerational family history to assess inheritance patterns consistent with autosomal dominance, which in turn justifies the indication of genetic testing strategies such as whole‐exome sequencing or targeted gene panels. 10

Rationale and conceptual framework

Genetic counseling is defined as a clinical process that assists individuals and families in understanding and adapting to the medical, psychological, and familial implications of genetic contributions to disease. 11 It involves education, risk assessment, and emotional support to promote informed decisions regarding health and family planning. In the case of ADAD, the high penetrance of pathogenic variants in PSEN1, PSEN2, and APP; the younger age at onset; and the increasing relevance of preclinical therapeutic strategies create ethical and psychosocial challenges that extend beyond those encountered in many other adult‐onset genetic disorders. To date, the number of ADAD families in LatAm may be underestimated due to high testing costs and limited access to genetic counseling. 12 , 13 There are no standardized guidelines for familial AD counseling 14 and most practices follow those for other dominantly inherited diseases, like Huntington's disease. In addition, although several national strategies for AD have been developed in LatAm, most do not address ADAD and the potential legal and financial consequences of genetic testing. 15 Despite these challenges, LatAm countries are expanding specialized centers for ADAD patients and families, promoting equity in access to GCT. 13 Therefore, there is currently a need for a thoughtful approach, grounded in ethics, to implement genetic services and family counseling programs in LatAm, so that the unique needs of these populations are recognized and diagnosed. 16

RESULTS

Program requirements and team preparation

The genetic counseling programs should adopt a person‐centered approach addressing the psychological, familial, and other social needs of at‐risk individuals and ADAD families beyond clinical diagnosis. A context‐adapted genetic counseling program should integrate standardized participant assessment protocols, a supportive counseling framework, and complementary resources.

Implementation of such programs requires a clearly defined multidisciplinary team including professionals with expertise in neurology, genetics or genetic counseling, psychology, and psychiatry, with clearly delineated roles and responsibilities across counseling phases. All team members involved in counseling and disclosure should have specific training in predictive genetic testing for adult‐onset neurodegenerative diseases and in the management of psychological distress associated with risk disclosure. Similarly, it is important to adopt common definitions for key generic terms, as outlined in the glossary (see Box 1). In the ideal scenario, the team should include trained mental health professionals to formally assess the emotional impact of both the counseling process and potential disclosure of genetic results. 19 All procedures related to participant confidentiality must be continuously reviewed, and the program should outline and define pathways for referral to psychological support networks available to individuals and their families. Recommended counseling structure

The PRAGA guidelines recommend a structured, phase‐based genetic counseling model grounded in established principles for adult‐onset autosomal dominant disorders established by the American College of Medical Genetics and the National Society of Genetic Counselors, including elements from the guidelines of the Huntington's Disease Society of America. 11 , 20

The counseling process should be organized as a multistep process that can be delivered over a variable number of sessions, depending on local resources, team availability, and individual participant needs. For ADAD we recommend a phase‐based matrix model to ensure consistency, safety, and reproducibility across sites while allowing flexibility for local implementation. Within this framework, four phases are proposed: (1) pre‐test genetic counseling, (2) sample collection for genetic testing, (3)disclosure of results with post‐test counseling, and (4)optional follow‐up that may be implemented when clinically indicated, feasible, or requested by the participant. Each phase has defined objectives, minimum procedural requirements, and decision checkpoints to support informed choice and psychological safety. Phase‐specific recommendation

In this section, we outline core recommendations for delivering each GCT phase. Nevertheless, patient vulnerabilities (e.g., socioeconomic deprivation, geographical isolation, etc.), should be identified and considered when setting up the GCT protocol. Accordingly, centers may streamline the pathway by combining or abbreviating selected components, provided that informed decision‐making, confidentiality, and participant safety are preserved. For example, this could lead to combining pre‐test counseling with mental health evaluation and sample collection in one visit. Similarly, we recommend to consider telehealth as an option for delivering the program, since it has shown to be non‐inferior or comparable to in‐person genetic counseling with regard to patient satisfaction, knowledge and psychosocial outcomes in genetic counselling for AD and other adult‐onset neurodegenerative disorders. 21 , 22 , 23

Phase 1: Pre‐test genetic counseling

The pre‐test counseling phase aims to support informed and voluntary decision‐making by educating participants about ADAD, the clinical indications and appropriateness in their individual family, and the implications of genetic testing, as well as assessing mental health, coping mechanisms, family dynamics, and sociocultural context. Discussions address the benefits, limitations, and risks of genetic testing, as well as the potential emotional consequences of learning one's GS. Counseling should be individualized according to participants’ educational and cultural backgrounds in order to foster emotional preparedness and support systems.

Predictive testing in asymptomatic individuals raises distinct ethical and psychological considerations compared with diagnostic testing in affected individuals; therefore, the first encounter should primarily be an informational and supportive conversation. At this stage, the team should consider factors that may facilitate or warrant the postponement of predictive testing, including psychological vulnerability, coping capacity, and available social support. 7 , 9 , 24 Table 2 summarizes general factors that may support progression to testing or warrant caution and further assessment prior to disclosure.

Baseline mental health assessment

The purpose of this session is to identify active psychological vulnerabilities that could interfere with decision‐making or increase emotional distress during counseling and result disclosure. This assessment should be proportionate to the participant's clinical presentation and local resources and can include a focused psychological screening. Assessing personal traits and individual coping mechanisms is also essential for informing the care plan, as is reviewing the patient's available support networks including family and friends. This will also help to evaluate the socio‐familial and cultural background, a cornerstone of an effective counseling process. Baseline evaluation should include screening for cognitive status and relevant medical comorbidities to ensure capacity for informed consent.

The session should be led by a trained mental health professional (i.e., clinical psychologist, neurologist, other), with referral for evaluation by psychiatry specialist when clinically indicated. Baseline psychological assessment should include the use of standardized instruments appropriate for evaluating emotional distress, coping capacity, and psychological readiness for GCT in individuals at risk for ADAD. The specific instruments currently used across Latin American sites participating in the PRAGA network have been described elsewhere. 19 However, other scales could be used depending on local validation and practice.

Psychoeducation session

This session, conducted by a health care professional trained in genetic counseling, focuses on establishing a therapeutic alliance and providing psychoeducation regarding the genetics of ADAD, phenotype variability, penetrance, and age at onset, and what types of genetic tests are available (including which information each test may or may not yield), among other important information for decision making. Key elements include a discussion of the different causes of dementia, risk (including variability) for ADAD in the patient's family based on the specific mutation identified in a blood relative, clinical features and disease progression of ADAD, testing procedures, accuracy, and limitations; exploration of participant expectations, beliefs, and misconceptions; emotional support to process potential anxiety, uncertainty, or guilt; and documentation of the participant's intention regarding whether to learn test results.

It is crucial to meet with the individual/participant in a quiet, comfortable, and private setting that fosters a therapeutic relationship and enables the counselor to address questions and concerns. Participants receive information about the disease affecting their family and the socio‐familial, cultural, and legal implications of predictive testing. Emotional validation and support can be decisive, as it can give the patient an opportunity to acknowledge and express emotions related to the uncertainty of carrying a pathogenic variant for AD.

The counselor must always consider a person‐centered approach for the effective communication of complex medical information, taking into account the educational background, cultural attitudes towards the disease, and previous beliefs about genetic testing. It is beneficial to explore the individual's motivations for testing and how they will use the information. In addition, it is important to discuss the impact of the results on family planning and age‐appropriate support in sharing results with children. The use of visual aids is highly recommended for all people to ensure the understanding of highly complex information. A balanced discussion of the potential benefits and drawbacks of receiving an early diagnosis is key to ensure informed decision‐making.

Phase 2: Sample collection for genetic testing

This phase starts with verifying that all procedures recommended during the pre‐test genetic counseling (Phase 1) have been carried out appropriately. Discussion in a multidisciplinary team meeting can help determine the best course of action regarding whether to proceed with or pause testing in each case. Prior to blood or saliva sample collection for genetic testing, participants will state their intention to receive or decline the disclosure of their results. This decision will be documented and subsequently reconfirmed during the disclosure session. It's advisable to have a waiting period between Phase 1 and Phase 2 to allow participants time to reflect and decide whether to proceed with genetic testing. It is also important to communicate to the patient that they have the right to refuse test results disclosure if they change their mind after sample collection.

Phase 3: Post‐test genetic counseling

Post‐test genetic counseling phase starts immediately after sample collection for genetic testing. Existing resources should be offered from this point prior to the GS disclosure. The disclosure of results should guarantee the patient's understanding and provide space to raise questions or concerns regarding the impact of the findings on daily life and on the potential risk of developing a major neurocognitive disorder.

Disclosure of genetic status (GS)

When results become available (typically within several weeks), willingness to proceed with disclosure should be confirmed and options to delay or decline disclosure offered. If a participant delays or declines disclosure, they should be informed that any future request to receive the results will require a new mental health assessment prior to disclosure. Participants may choose to be accompanied by a support person, provided that confidentiality and autonomy are respected. In our experience, disclosure of GS in the presence of a support person may enhance emotional processing and adjustment, especially for individuals considered more prone to adverse emotional responses.

Results must be provided both verbally and in written form, and the team must make sure there is a clear understanding of the meaning of the report and the implications of a positive, negative, or inconclusive result. The team should also provide support to the patient and their family as they express their emotional responses to the genetic information. Psychological support should be offered to anyone including non‐carriers who may experience guilt or other psychological responses associated with not carrying a familial pathogenic variant. 25

It is important to contract during pre‐testing genetic counseling on the planned method of results delivery. A thorough discussion of logistics to ensure that the patient has privacy and distractions minimized is encouraged. This can include topics such as time of appointment, day of the week, will they have to work after the appointment, and which individuals are best suited for support. Disclosure may be conducted in person or remotely, depending on participant preference, clinical judgment, and local feasibility.

Support network involvement and future planning

A key message for the clinical team to convey is that an ADAD diagnosis should serve as a catalyst for activating family and social support networks. This is relevant not only for emotional coping but also for planning related to health care, employment, and legal decisions. Having a preclinical diagnosis of ADAD could be seen as an opportunity to anticipate future decisions relating to social and health needs, since significant problems in decision‐making have been documented in mild cognitive impairment and very mild dementia. 26 Future planning will require guidance and support on navigating complex social and health services that are not necessarily prepared for dealing with the particular needs of at‐risk individuals.

Involvement in support groups and research

There is relevant evidence on the benefit of involvement in support groups for rare forms of dementia including familial AD. 27 Counseling should also include discussion of ongoing research opportunities, with careful distinction between clinical care and research participation. Peer support interventions for people living with or caring for someone with a rare form of dementia are still scarce in LatAm, although some of the first initiatives have been developed in Colombia and Argentina. Recent approval of disease‐modifying therapies for AD has boosted research looking for a greater impact of amyloid removal on cognitive and functional outcomes. In that context, there is a growing interest on the ability of monoclonal antibody therapies to prevent or delay disease onset in at‐risk individuals, but further research is needed to clarify the extent to which complete amyloid removal can delay, slow, or prevent AD dementia in ADAD mutation carriers. 28

Phase 4: follow‐up assessment

We recommend follow‐up assessment ≈3 months after result disclosure using the same scales used for baseline evaluation of mental health status, and including scales for assessing decision regret and psychological impact of genetic testing and revealing of genetic status. Evidence supports the safety and benefit of structured follow‐up in mitigating adverse psychological outcomes. 19 , 29 , 30

Special considerations and implementation

ADAD‐specific ethical and psychosocial considerations

Within the context of predictive genetic testing for individuals at high risk of ADAD, several ethical dilemmas arise; genetic counseling must address a distinct set of ethical and psychosocial challenges arising from deterministic inheritance, early age at onset, and the frequent overlap between clinical care and research participation. For some groups, these challenges are also coupled with unequal access to information and a lack of support for people carrying a pathogenic variant. The PRAGA framework is focused on safe access to genetic information about the disease and informed decision‐making regarding disclosure of GS. The GCT process should aim to prevent unwanted consequences (non‐maleficence), empower individuals to take ownership of their information and future (autonomy), promote psychological well‐being and access to appropriate support (beneficence), and ensure equitable access to genetic information and counseling services (justice).

Our experience suggests that it is crucial to conduct thorough psychological assessments prior to disclosing GS, ensuring that individuals are prepared both cognitively and emotionally to receive potentially life‐altering information. In addition, there are key elements for success in the process, including facilitating informed decision‐making, enabling individuals to understand their condition, anticipating future needs, and engaging meaningfully with treatment options and research studies. Ultimately, the genetic counseling protocol seeks not only to inform but also to equip individuals with the necessary tools to proactively manage their health outcomes, ensuring that they are supported in integrating this knowledge into their lives in a healthy and constructive manner. Predictive testing should be deferred when active psychiatric conditions, impaired decision‐making capacity, or insufficient psychosocial support may compromise participant safety.

Deterministic inheritance and predictable onset

Individuals at risk of ADAD are often medically healthy at the time of genetic counseling, frequently encountering testing decisions during early or mid‐adulthood. Another distinctive feature is the relatively consistent age at onset observed within affected family members across generations, reflecting the near‐complete penetrance of pathogenic variants in PSEN1, PSEN2, and APP. Together, these characteristics confer a high degree of certainty regarding lifetime disease risk and an approximate temporal window for symptom onset, which is uncommon among adult‐onset neurodegenerative disorders. Awareness of a high likelihood of developing AD at a relatively defined early stage of life may generate anticipatory psychological burden, influence life planning, and intensify concerns related to family responsibilities, career trajectories, and reproductive decisions. These factors underscore the importance of counseling approaches that explicitly address anticipatory distress, temporal perceptions of risk, and intergenerational implications of genetic information.

Cultural and social considerations in LatAm

Genetic counseling should be responsive to variations in population‐level of knowledge of genetic diseases, medical genetics, and genetic counseling in each country. However, decisions about testing can be driven less by careful consideration of factual information and more by rapid, intuitive judgments as well as the influence of relatives’ opinions. 31 In that context, it is important to consider that cultural beliefs in some Latin American communities, such as magical and religious thinking, may influence perceptions of genetic risk, attitudes toward testing, monitoring of the psychosocial effects of ADAD, and ethical consultations in preclinical stages. For example, in a sample of ADAD families of Mexican origin, 86.67% of participants expressed feeling embarrassed about having a family member with the disease, whereas others believed that only God could change or cure someone with AD (22.97%) or viewed it as a curse (18.92%). 32 Of note LatAm countries are characterized by cultural, socioeconomic, and educational heterogeneity, which may introduce context‐specific challenges during GCT. Differences in educational attainment, health‐system engagement, and prevailing belief systems may affect understanding of genetic information, trust in medical recommendations, and willingness to engage in predictive testing or research participation. 33 , 34

Overall, these factors underscore the importance of culturally sensitive counseling strategies that assess individual explanatory models of illness, address stigma explicitly, adapt educational materials to local contexts, and reinforce biomedical information without dismissing personal belief systems. Such adaptations are essential to support informed decision‐making, reduce distress, and promote engagement with genetic counseling services in diverse Latin American settings.

Implementation in LatAm and workforce limitations

Implementation of GCT in LatAm is constrained by multiple structural and workforce‐related barriers. Key barriers include high costs of genetic testing, limited availability of trained genetic providers, and limited clinician familiarity with testing. 35 Across LatAm, access to genetic counseling and clinical genetics services remains limited and uneven, with structural, academic, and cultural factors contributing to significant regional disparities. Access to genetic services remains limited, especially for the population living outside capital cities. For example, Bucio et al. reported 248 medical geneticists across Mexico, concentrated mainly in Mexico City, whereas in states like Jalisco, Nuevo León, Querétaro, Hidalgo, and Yucatán, there is an average of one geneticist for every 500,000 people. 36 This rate is similar to that reported in countries such as Chile and remains far from the World Health Organization's recommended target of one medical geneticist per 100,000 individuals. 14

In most countries, genetic counseling is still emerging as a distinct professional discipline and post‐graduate training programs for counselors remain scarce. The availability of genetic services is even worse for adult‐onset conditions because clinical genetics services have traditionally focused on pediatric and congenital conditions, resulting in particularly limited expertise and infrastructure for adult‐onset neurogenetic disorders. 14 , 37 Standardized counseling programs for adult‐onset neurogenetic disorders are particularly underdeveloped, with the only exception of programs for genetic testing in Huntington disease, 38 , 39 whereas comparable programs for ADAD remain largely absent. In this context, the PRAGA framework is intended to provide a practical blueprint and guiding principles for ADAD genetic counseling, while explicitly recognizing that local adaptation will be necessary to accommodate resource constraints, workforce capacity, and health‐system variability. For this reason, the recommendations should be interpreted as clinical principles rather than fixed operational requirements, and they may be implemented through simplified, staged, or hybrid models designed to reduce financial, geographic, and time burdens on participants.

DISCUSSION

These recommendations represent the first effort to summarize lessons from the development and implementation of the PRAGA genetic counseling framework, a structured and culturally adapted protocol designed to guide GCT for families affected by ADAD in LatAm. Building on prior empirical work demonstrating the feasibility and psychological safety of structured GCT, 19 , 30 , 40 these recommendations provide consensus‐based guidance to support the delivery of high‐quality GCT by integrating evidence‐informed practices with a multidisciplinary, person‐centered approach. The recommendations aim to address gaps in genetic counseling and testing for at‐risk family members by providing a safe, culturally responsive framework adapted to the heterogeneous and resource‐constrained health systems of LatAm.

A key contribution of this approach is recognition of mental health screening as a core safeguard in pre‐test counseling. Active psychiatric conditions, limited coping capacity, and insufficient social support are well‐recognized risk factors for distress during predictive testing for adult‐onset neurodegenerative diseases. 41 , 42 The structured use of validated instruments to inform clinical evaluation ensures the early identification of individuals who may require additional support or for whom testing and disclosure may be inappropriate. This stepwise assessment is consistent with international recommendations developed for Huntington disease, but PRAGA expands upon these models by embedding sociocultural considerations, including beliefs surrounding illness, caregiving roles, and stigma that are unique to Latin American families.

Another central feature of the recommendations is the emphasis on a person‐centered approach to ensure an informed decision‐making process. In settings where disease awareness is limited, educational materials and counseling strategies are adapted to participants’ literacy levels, prior knowledge, and cultural frameworks to enhance comprehension of complex medical and ethical issues. This is critical given the persistence of misconceptions about inheritance, the influence of religious or magical thinking, and variable familiarity with genetic concepts in the region. The use of visual aids, explicit exploration of expectations, and reinforcement of autonomy are essential for supporting informed decision‐making in contexts where individuals may have limited prior exposure to genetic information. We also highlight the importance of family and social networks in the counseling process, as well as the benefits of early psychosocial support in mitigating isolation and facilitating adaptive coping. 27

These recommendations are based on expert consensus informed by available empirical evidence, clinical experience, and ethical principles; where high‐quality empirical evidence was limited or unavailable, recommendations reflect agreement among experienced clinicians and counselors involved in the care of families with ADAD. The recommendations should be interpreted in the context of evolving scientific evidence and local regulatory frameworks. In addition, patients and family members were not directly involved in the guideline development process. However, recommendations were informed by extensive longitudinal clinical experience with individuals and families affected by ADAD across PRAGA‐affiliated centers, incorporating commonly reported concerns, preferences, and psychosocial challenges observed in clinical practice. To date, program implementation has been concentrated in specialized centers with experience in familial AD research, which may limit immediate generalizability to routine clinical settings. Moreover, given the rapidly evolving landscape of genetic testing technologies and disease‐modifying and preventive interventions for AD, these recommendations will require periodic reassessment and updating as new evidence emerges and an increasing number of at‐risk individuals seek genetic testing.

Despite the above‐mentioned limitations, the PRAGA framework represents an important step toward equitable access to high‐quality genetic counseling for ADAD in LatAm and a coordinated response to the need for standardized GCT protocols that balance clinical, ethical, and psychosocial considerations. The framework proposes models grounded in international guidelines but adapted to the sociocultural and structural realities of the region. Moreover, it provides a practical roadmap for health systems seeking to expand genetic services. As interest in precision medicine and predictive genetic testing grows, the guidelines offer valuable insights into how to implement safe, ethical, and person‐centered GCT across diverse global contexts.

CONCLUSIONS

Genetic counseling for ADAD requires a multidimensional and culturally sensitive approach. In that context, researchers and clinicians involved in the care of at‐risk individuals for known familial variants must ensure that GCT are delivered through structured practices that prioritize informed decision‐making, psychological safety, and longitudinal support, empowering individuals and families to face genetic risk with disease awareness, effective coping strategies, and psychological resilience. Persistent inequities in access to specialized genetic services across LatAm underscore the need for context‐specific guidance. The PRAGA framework provides consensus‐based recommendations intended to support the development and harmonization of ADAD genetic counseling services and to inform future efforts to expand equitable access to high‐quality GCT across the region.
