An L 2 L^{2} -quantitative global approximation for the Stokes initial-boundary value problem
Mitsuo Higaki

Abstract
We establish the first quantitative Runge approximation theorem, with explicit L 2 L^{2} -estimates, for the 3d nonstationary Stokes system on a bounded spatial domain. This result addresses the two primary limitations of the qualitative result [ 18 ] obtained in collaboration with Franck Sueur: first, it bypasses the non-constructive Hahn-Banach theorem used in [ 18 ] , precluding quantitative estimates; and second, it extends the scope of the theory from interior approximations to the physically important initial-boundary value problem. Our proof is founded on the modern quantitative framework of Rüland-Salo [ 28 ] , which we adapt to the Stokes system by combining semigroup theory with a quantitative approximation for the associated resolvent problem.

Introduction

The global approximation (or Runge-type) theorem is a foundational concept in partial differential equation (PDE) theory. It typically states that any local solution of an equation in a compact set
K
can, under topological connectedness of the complement of
K
, be approximated by a global solution defined in the full space. This field has historically evolved in two distinct directions.
The first is the qualitative direction, focused on proving the existence of such approximations. This theory, originating from Runge
[
31
]
and Mergelyan
[
27
]
in complex analysis of one variable, is extended to elliptic PDEs by Lax
[
23
]
, Malgrange
[
26
]
, and Browder
[
6
]
, and later generalized to non-elliptic equations such as the parabolic equations by Jones
[
20
]
and Enciso–García-Ferrero–Peralta-Salas
[
12
]
; see also Dipierro-Savin-Valdinoci
[
9
]
treating the fractional heat equations and Donaldson
[
11
]
the instanton equations. Furthermore, the Runge approximation property continues to play a crucial role in inverse problems, such as the Calderón problem
[
22
]
.

The second, more recent direction is the quantitative problem, which seeks to derive explicit estimates linking the approximation error to the given data; this is often called determining the cost of approximation
[
8
]
. This modern methodology was initiated by Rüland-Salo
[
28
]
, updating the classical works
[
23
,
26
]
in a quantitative manner. Indeed, quantitative approximation results have been established for various other operators, including non-elliptic ones by
[
29
,
30
,
8
]
, notably for the Schrödinger operator by Enciso–Peralta-Salas
[
14
]
. The proof in this paper also builds upon these ideas of
[
28
]
.

The global approximation theorem for the 3d Stokes system is proved in
[
18
]
. The main result
[
18
, Theorem A]
states that the velocity part of any local solution
(v,q)
of the Stokes system in
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
, a compact set satisfying the usual conditions, can be approximated by the velocity part of a solution
(u,p)
of the Stokes system in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
. In general, these global solutions are unbounded in space
[
18
, Theorem B]
. This behavior stands in sharp contrast to the heat equation
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
, where approximations with decay at infinity are possible. These qualitative approximation theorems highlight a fundamental property of the underlying linear equations:
rigidity
. This property, often manifested as a unique continuation principle, dictates that a local solution can, at best, only be approximated by a global one, excluding the possibility of a perfect match (i.e.,
\varepsilon=0
) unless the local solution happens to be the restriction of a global one. A very different scenario arises with the concept of
flexibility
, a property observed in certain nonlinear systems, such as the 3d Euler system. For these “flexible” equations, an entirely different theory applies in the context of weak solutions; see e.g.
[
7
,
24
]
applying convex integration. Surprisingly, this framework allows for the construction of global in space-time weak solutions that exactly coincide with a given local smooth solution; see the recent remarkable progress
[
13
]
for the 3d Euler system. The reader is also referred to
[
9
]
for a “flexible” Runge-type approximation to linear nonlocal equations and
[
30
]
for the cost of approximation.

The results in
[
18
]
are inherently non-quantitative, meaning that the global approximations cannot be estimated by the given local data. This limitation is a direct consequence of the proof strategy employed. That proof consisted of two main stages: first, constructing an intermediate approximation
v_{1}
, under forcing with poles far from
K
, using the “sweeping of poles and discretization” method
[
6
,
12
]
; and second, extending
v_{1}|_{K}
to a global solution
u
using an ordinary differential equation (ODE) technique. The bottleneck, however, lies in the first stage. The “sweeping” method, while effective for proving existence, relies fundamentally on a non-constructive Hahn-Banach theorem. It is this reliance that makes it impossible to derive quantitative estimates. This non-constructive barrier is the specific drawback that our present paper aims to bypass using a completely different approach.

Furthermore, the results in
[
18
]
are restricted to interior approximations, meaning that they do not cover the practically important case of initial-boundary value problems on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This is a significant limitation, as many applications of the Stokes system involve fluid motion constrained by solid boundaries. While the interior framework of
[
18
]
is general enough to encompass pathological solutions, so-called parasitic solutions or Serrin’s examples
[
32
]
, it leaves open the problem of global approximation for solutions defined on a bounded spatial domain with prescribed boundary conditions.

This paper addresses these two limitations. We shift the focus from interior approximations of
[
18
]
to more physically relevant initial-boundary value problems on a bounded domain
D
. Our primary objective is to establish the first quantitative approximation theorems in this setting, specifically by deriving explicit
L^{2}
-estimates that link the global approximations directly to the initial data; see Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
below.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Set

\begin{split}C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(D)&=\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D)^{3}~|~\operatorname{div}\varphi=0\},\\
L^{2}_{\sigma}(D)&=\big(\text{Closure of $C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(D)$ in $L^{2}(D)^{3}$}\big).\end{split}

Consider the 3d nonstationary Stokes system on
D
under the no-slip condition:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v-\Delta v+\nabla q=0&\mbox{in}\ D\times(0,\infty),\\
\operatorname{div}v=0&\mbox{in}\ D\times[0,\infty),\\
v=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial D\times(0,\infty),\\
v=v_{0}\in L^{2}_{\sigma}(D)&\mbox{on}\ D\times\{0\}.\end{array}\right.

(S)

The solution of (
S
) can be represented by the Stokes semigroup, which we now recall. Let
{\mathbb{P}}:L^{2}(D)^{3}\to L^{2}_{\sigma}(D)
denote the orthogonal projection, called the Helmholtz projection, satisfying
{\mathbb{P}}\nabla p=0
for
p\in H^{1}(D)
. Then we define

{\mathbb{A}}=-{\mathbb{P}}\Delta,\qquad D({\mathbb{A}})=L^{2}_{\sigma}(D)\cap H^{1}_{0}(D)^{3}\cap H^{2}(D)^{3}.

The operator
{\mathbb{A}}
is called the Stokes operator; see
[
33
, Section 2.1, III]
. It is well known (see e.g.
[
21
, Chapter 2]
) that
{\mathbb{A}}
is nonnegative and self-adjoint in
L^{2}_{\sigma}(D)
and that
-{\mathbb{A}}
generates the
C_{0}
-analytic semigroup
\{e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}}\}_{t\geq 0}
, called the Stokes semigroup. Moreover,
{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}
is completely continuous, and the spectrum
\sigma({\mathbb{A}})
is discrete. The elements
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
are positive and converge to
\infty
, and each element has finite multiplicity.

Our global approximation theorems for the Stokes system (
S
) are stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain whose complement
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D
is connected. Then, there exist positive constants
C,\nu
for which the following statement holds: let
v_{0}\in D({\mathbb{A}})
and set
v(t)=e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}}v_{0}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a smooth solution
(u,p)
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty),\\
\operatorname{div}u=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,\infty)\end{array}\right.

such that

\|v(t)-u(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\varepsilon e^{t}\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)},\quad t\geq 0,

(1.1)

and that

\begin{split}|u(x,t)|&\leq\exp\big(\exp(C\varepsilon^{-\nu})\big)\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\\
&\quad\times\max\big\{\langle x\rangle^{\exp(C\varepsilon^{-\nu})},\,\exp(C\varepsilon^{-1/2}|x|)\big\}\exp(t),\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}.\end{split}

(1.2)

Theorem 1.2

D
be given as in Theorem

. Then, there exist positive constants

and set

\varepsilon>0
and

\displaystyle|u_{{\rm S}}(x,t)|

\displaystyle|u_{0}(x)|

(1.5)

Remark 1.3

(i)

One of the key motivations for this work was to improve the spatial behavior of the global approximations found in the previous qualitative work
[
18
]
. Indeed, the global approximations in
[
18
]
, constructed via a non-constructive Hahn-Banach theorem, exhibit exponential growth in space. It is worth noting that this spatial exponential growth in
[
18
]
could have been mitigated. In fact, the proof strategy in this paper, notably the decomposition of the resolvent solution into a Stokes part
u_{1}
and a heat part
u_{2}
in Proposition
2.7
, is conceptually separable from the quantitative idea. Had this decomposition been applied within the qualitative framework of
[
18
]
, it would have yielded a global approximation with only polynomial growth in space, akin to a qualitative counterpart of Theorem
1.2
.

The true novelty of the present paper, therefore, lies not just in achieving polynomial spatial growth in Theorem
1.2
, but in doing estimation quantitatively both in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. By replacing the abstract Hahn-Banach argument with a constructive proof based on the Rüland-Salo framework and resolvent estimates, we establish explicit pointwise bounds for global approximations. This quantitative approach, however, introduces a trade-off in the temporal behavior. While the global approximations in
[
18
]
decay as
t\to\infty
(see
[
18
, Section 3.6]
), our current constructive proof via the Dunford integral necessarily results in approximations that grow exponentially in time as
\exp(t)
, as seen in (
1.2
) and (
1.4
).

(ii)

Contrary to the global-in-time approximation (
1.1
) in Theorem
1.1
, the approximation (
1.3
) in Theorem
1.2
is restricted to finite time intervals
[0,T]
. This restriction is due to the particular structure of the approximation, which decomposes the global solution into the Stokes part
u_{{\rm S}}
and the heat semigroup part
u_{{\rm H}}
. Although the heat component
u_{{\rm H}}
, generated by the compactly supported initial data
u_{0}
, decays as
t\to\infty
, the Stokes component
u_{{\rm S}}
still exhibits temporal growth, similar to the behavior of the full approximation
u
in Theorem
1.1
. Hence Theorem
1.2
isolates a decaying component, but at the cost of restricting the approximation to finite time. This restriction is necessary as our proof does not guarantee the long-time behavior of this decomposition.

(iii)

The results of this paper are not restricted to the no-slip boundary condition. Our proof relies on two main aspects: the representation of the local solution
v(t)
via the Dunford integral for the analytic semigroup
\{e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}}\}_{t\geq 0}
in Section
3
and the quantitative approximation for resolvent problems in Section
2
. The Dunford integral representation is valid for any sectorial operator
-\mathcal{A}
whose resolvent set contains a sector with angle greater than
\pi/2
, generating the analytic semigroup
\{e^{-t\mathcal{A}}\}_{t\geq 0}
. The core of this work, the quantitative approximation for the resolvent, is based on spectral properties and logarithmic stability in Lemma
2.3
, which are not exclusive to the no-slip case. Consequently, any set of boundary conditions, such as the Navier-slip condition with favorable sign or periodic boundary conditions, that yields a Stokes operator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying such abstract properties can be treated by the same quantitative methodology. The structure of the quantitative estimates in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
should remain, though the constants
C
involved in the estimates would depend on the given geometric and operator-theoretic properties of the chosen boundary condition. A detailed analysis of the cases where the Stokes operator
\mathcal{A}
might possess a non-trivial kernel (e.g., under certain geometric symmetries or different types of slip conditions) is beyond the scope of this paper and will be addressed in future work.

(iv)

It would be of great interest to extend the
L^{2}
-quantitative global approximation results in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
to an
L^{p}
-setting
[
34
,
17
,
1
]
. However, such an extension presents significant challenges, as the Rüland-Salo framework utilized in Section
2
depends heavily on the underlying Hilbert space structure.

The key to our proof lies in establishing a quantitative approximation
u
for the homogeneous Stokes resolvent problem; see Proposition
2.1
. A crucial step in this process, adapting the quantitative Runge approximation framework of Rüland-Salo
[
28
]
, is the decomposition of the global approximation
u
into two distinct parts (see Proposition
2.7
):

•

A solution
u_{1}
to the Stokes resolvent problem, exhibiting polynomial growth in space

•

A solution
u_{2}
to the heat resolvent problem, exhibiting exponential growth in space

This decomposition allows us to derive precise, quantitative estimates for the growth of each component in terms of the approximation error
\varepsilon
; see Propositions
2.8
and
2.9
, which are essential for estimating the constructed global approximations in Section
3
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the global approximation theorem for the homogeneous Stokes resolvent problem. Section
3
is devoted to the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, which are based on the results in Section
2
.

2
Global approximation for the Stokes resolvent problem

The goal of this section is to prove the following proposition for global approximations to the local resolvent problem. The proof will be provided at the end of this section.

Proposition 2.1

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain whose complement
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus D
is connected. For
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\pi-\delta}\cap\{|\lambda|\geq 1\}
with
\delta\in(0,\pi/2)
, let
v\in H^{2}(D)^{3}
and
q\in H^{1}(D)
satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda v-\Delta v+\nabla q=0&\mbox{in}\ D,\\
\operatorname{div}v=0&\mbox{in}\ D.\end{array}\right.

Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
(u,p)
solving

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda u-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\operatorname{div}u=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\end{array}\right.

such that
u
approximates
v
in
D
as

\|v-u\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\varepsilon\Big(\|v\|_{H^{1}(D)}+\|q\|_{H^{1}(D)}\Big).

Moreover,
u
can be decomposed into
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
, where
u_{1}
is a solution to the Stokes resolvent problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with polynomial growth, and
u_{2}
is a solution to the heat resolvent problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with exponential growth; see Proposition
2.7
. Their quantitative estimates are provided in Propositions
2.8
and
2.9
, respectively.

We begin by stating a few lemmas. Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
. Recall that the function

E_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}|x|}}{4\pi|x|}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}

satisfies the distributional equation
\lambda u-\Delta u=\delta_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Set

\vec{E}_{\lambda}=(E_{\lambda},E_{\lambda},E_{\lambda}).

For given
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
, we define

u=\vec{E}_{\lambda}\ast{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f,\qquad\nabla p=({\rm Id}-{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})f.

Then
(u,p)
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda u-\Delta u+\nabla p=f&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\operatorname{div}u=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{array}\right.

The following lemma is well-known, and thus we state it without proof.

Lemma 2.2

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain and
f\in L^{2}(D)
. Set
w=E_{\lambda}\ast f
. Then, for any bounded domain

, we have

The implicit constant is independent of
\lambda
.

A key lemma in the proof of Proposition
2.1
is the following.

Lemma 2.3

(logarithmic stability)

Let
(v,q)
be given as in Proposition
2.1
. Let
D^{\prime}
be a subdomain of

satisfying
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD^{\prime}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\subset D

(2.1)

Proof.

Set

Then
(w,s)

By the 3-cylinder inequality for this system in

]

\begin{split}&\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2}\int_{B_{R_{2}}}|w(x,t)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t\\

\begin{split}\langle\vec{E}_{\lambda}\ast{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathtt{e}_{Y}f),\mathtt{e}_{D}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}&=\langle\vec{E}_{\lambda}\ast{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathtt{e}_{Y}f),{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathtt{e}_{D}v)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\

&=\langle f,\vec{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}\ast{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathtt{e}_{D}v)|_{Y}\rangle_{L^{2}(Y)},\end{split}

which verifies the claim of (

Proof.

Since

, one can expand

by

in Lemma

(

For any

Then we see that

(2.3)

Moreover,

approximates

Notice that

(2.4)

To improve this bound, we consider

(2.5)

The estimate (
2.4

\begin{split}\|\mathsf{E}\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}&=\langle v-AF,\mathsf{E}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}=\langle v,\mathsf{E}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}\\

\begin{split}&\langle\mathtt{e}_{D}v,\bar{\lambda}w-\Delta w+\nabla s\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\

where

Proposition 2.6

that solves

for some smooth

in

Proof.

Recall that

is chosen so that

with ball

satisfying
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\subset B_{R}

Let

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda v_{1}-\Delta v_{1}=-\nabla q_{1}&\mbox{in}\ B_{\rho},\\
\operatorname{div}v_{1}=0&\mbox{in}\ B_{\rho}.\end{array}\right.

Our goal is to construct a global solution
u
to the homogeneous Stokes resolvent problem that approximates
v_{1}
in
D
. The strategy involves extending the restriction
v_{1}|_{B_{\rho}}
to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This is achieved by using an explicit representation for solutions of the vector Helmholtz equation, obtained through an expansion into vector spherical harmonics
[
5
,
35
]
, and by truncating the series for

at a sufficiently large frequency

. Since the explicit representation is detailed in
[
18

In the ball

, we expand

in a series of (scalar) spherical harmonics

\begin{split}q_{1}(x,\lambda)&=q_{1}(r,\theta,\phi,\lambda)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}b_{lm}(r,\lambda)Y_{lm}\end{split}

in a series of vector spherical harmonics

(2.11)

Here we took

without loss of generality, and

\begin{split}c^{r}_{lm}&=\langle v_{1},\bm{Y}_{lm}\rangle_{S},\\
c^{(1)}_{lm}&=\frac{1}{\mu_{l}}\langle v_{1},\bm{\Psi}_{lm}\rangle_{S},\quad l\geq 1,\\

Let

be regarded as functions of one variable

in the following. From the calculations in

is given by

(2.12)

and

where

is defined by

C^{r}_{lm}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{l+\frac{1}{2},-1}(\sqrt{\lambda})}\int_{0}^{\rho}(c^{r}_{lm}(r)-\Lambda_{lm}(r))r^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muI_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}r)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mur^{2}\,{\rm d}r

and

&\quad+\frac{1}{\mu_{l}}\Big(2\Lambda_{lm}(r)+r\frac{\,{\rm d}\Lambda_{lm}}{\,{\rm d}r}(r)\Big)\end{split}

(2.18)

with

\begin{split}\frac{\,{\rm d}\Lambda_{lm}}{\,{\rm d}r}(r)&=-lB_{lm}\bigg(\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}r}\big(r^{-\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}r)\big)\int_{0}^{r}s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}s)\,{\rm d}s\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}r}\big(r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}r)\big)\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}s)\,{\rm d}s\bigg)\\
&=\sqrt{\lambda}lB_{lm}\int_{0}^{r}r^{-\frac{3}{2}}K_{l-\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}s)\,{\rm d}s\\
&\quad-\sqrt{\lambda}lB_{lm}\int_{r}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l-\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\lambda}s)\,{\rm d}s-\frac{l+2}{r}\Lambda_{lm}(r).\end{split}

(2.19)

Here the following well-known formulas are used:

where

(2.21)

We emphasize that all of
b_{lm},c^{r}_{lm},c^{(1)}_{lm},c^{(2)}_{lm}
above are defined for

.

Next we construct the global approximation
u
starting from (
2.11
). Denote

\|f\|=\bigg(\int_{0}^{\rho}|f(r)|^{2}r^{2}\,{\rm d}r\bigg)^{1/2}.

1.1
:

By density argument, it suffices to prove the statement when
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(D)
.
Assume that
0<\varepsilon<1
without loss of generality. The proof relies on the representation of
\{e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}}\}_{t\geq 0}
by the Dunford integral
[
15
, Section
\mathrm{I}\hskip-1.2pt\mathrm{I}
.4.a]

v(t)=e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}}v_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}e^{t\lambda}(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}\,{\rm d}\lambda,\quad t>0,

where
\gamma
denotes an oriented (counterclockwise) curve in
\mathbb{C}
:

\gamma=\Big\{|\operatorname{arg}z|=\frac{3}{4}\pi,\mkern 9.0mu|z|\geq 1\Big\}\cup\Big\{|\operatorname{arg}z|\leq\frac{3}{4}\pi,\mkern 9.0mu|z|=1\Big\}.

Using the property
\mathtt{e}_{D}v_{0}=v_{0}
, we set

u_{1}(t)=e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}v_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}e^{t\lambda}(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}\,{\rm d}\lambda,\quad t>0

(3.1)

and decompose the right-hand side of

v(t)-u_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}e^{t\lambda}\Big((\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}-(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}\Big)\,{\rm d}\lambda

(3.2)

into
w_{1}(t)+w_{2}(t)
by setting

\begin{split}w_{1}(t)&:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}e^{t\lambda}\Big((\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}-(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}\Big)\,{\rm d}\lambda,\\
w_{2}(t)&:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}e^{t\lambda}\Big((\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}-(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}\Big)\,{\rm d}\lambda,\end{split}

(3.3)

with the constant
L>1
that will be determined later depending on
\varepsilon
.

The first term
w_{1}(t)
in (
3.3
) is estimated as follows. From

\begin{split}w_{1}(t)&=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}e^{t\lambda}\bigg((\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg)v_{0}\,{\rm d}\lambda\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}e^{t\lambda}\bigg((\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg)v_{0}\,{\rm d}\lambda\end{split}

combined with the resolvent identities

\begin{split}&\lambda(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}-v_{0}=(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}v_{0},\\
&\lambda(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}-v_{0}=(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{0},\\
\end{split}

one can bound
w_{1}(t)
in
D
as, using standard estimates,

\begin{split}\|w_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}&\lesssim\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}\frac{e^{t\Re\lambda}}{|\lambda|}\|(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\,{\rm d}|\lambda|\\
&\quad+\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}\frac{e^{t\Re\lambda}}{|\lambda|}\|(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\,{\rm d}|\lambda|\\
&\lesssim\big(\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\|{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\big)\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|>L\}}\frac{e^{t\Re\lambda}}{|\lambda|^{2}}\,{\rm d}|\lambda|\\
&\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\frac{1}{L}\exp\Big(-\frac{tL}{\sqrt{2}}\Big),\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

Now choose
L=\varepsilon^{-1}
. Then we have

\|w_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\lesssim\varepsilon\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\exp\Big(-\frac{t}{\varepsilon\sqrt{2}}\Big),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.4)

Next we consider the second term
w_{2}(t)
in (
3.3
). Fix
\lambda\in\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}
. We apply the results in Section
2
for the Stokes resolvent problem. Define
V
in
D
by

V=(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}-(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}.

(3.5)

Since
V_{1}:=(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}v_{0}
solves, for some
Q_{1}\in H^{1}(D)
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda V_{1}-\Delta V_{1}+\nabla Q_{1}=v_{0}&\mbox{in}\ D,\\
\operatorname{div}V_{1}=0&\mbox{in}\ D,\\
V_{1}=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial D\end{array}\right.

and
V_{2}:=(\lambda+{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}v_{0}
solves, for some
Q_{2}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda V_{2}-\Delta V_{2}+\nabla Q_{2}=v_{0}&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\operatorname{div}V_{2}=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\end{array}\right.

we see that
V
solves, for some
Q\in H^{1}(D)
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda V-\Delta V+\nabla Q=0&\mbox{in}\ D,\\
\operatorname{div}V=0&\mbox{in}\ D.\end{array}\right.

Hence one can apply Proposition
2.1
to
(V,Q)
with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\varepsilon^{3}
. Then, there exists a smooth global approximation
U
solving, with some smooth
P
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda U-\Delta U+\nabla P=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\operatorname{div}U=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3},\end{array}\right.

that approximates
V
in
D
as

\|V-U\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\varepsilon^{3}\Big(\|V\|_{H^{1}(D)}+\|Q\|_{H^{1}(D)}\Big).

Observe from
\lambda\in\gamma
that
\|V\|_{H^{1}(D)}+\|\nabla Q\|_{L^{2}(D)}\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}
and

\begin{split}\|Q\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\|\nabla Q\|_{H^{-1}(D)}&=\|\lambda V-\Delta V\|_{H^{-1}(D)}\\
&\lesssim\langle\lambda\rangle\|V\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\|V\|_{H^{1}(D)},\end{split}

where
\int_{D}Q=0
is assumed without loss of generality. Thus we have

\|V-U\|_{L^{2}(D)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{3}\langle\lambda\rangle\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

Using the global approximation
U
, we define

u_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}e^{t\lambda}U(\lambda)\,{\rm d}\lambda,\quad t\geq 0.

(3.6)

Since
L=\varepsilon^{-1}
, we have

\begin{split}\|w_{2}(t)-u_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}&\lesssim\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}e^{t\Re\lambda}\|V-U\|_{L^{2}(D)}\,{\rm d}|\lambda|\\
&\lesssim\varepsilon^{3}\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}\langle\lambda\rangle e^{t\Re\lambda}\,{\rm d}|\lambda|\\
&\lesssim\varepsilon\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}e^{t},\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.7)

Now we define a globally defined velocity field
u
by

u(x,t)=u_{1}(x,t)+u_{2}(x,t),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.8)

with
u_{1}
defined in (
3.1
) and
u_{2}
in (
3.6
). By definition,
u
is a smooth solution of the nonstationary Stokes system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,\infty)
with some associated smooth pressure
p
. Moreover, we see from (
3.2
), (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.7
) that
u
approximates
v
in
D
as

\begin{split}\|v(t)-u(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}&\leq\|w_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\|w_{2}(t)-u_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\\
&\lesssim\varepsilon\exp\Big(-\frac{t}{\varepsilon\sqrt{2}}\Big)\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\varepsilon e^{t}\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\\
&\lesssim\varepsilon e^{t}\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)},\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

Hence the desired approximation (
1.1
) holds true.

It remains to provide the quantitative estimate (
1.2
) of
u
. Firstly we have

\begin{split}\|u_{1}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}&=\|e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}v_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\
&\lesssim\|e^{-t{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\\
&\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(D)}\\
&\lesssim\|{\mathbb{A}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)},\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.9)

By Proposition
2.7
, the global approximation
U(\lambda)
in (
3.6
) is decomposed into the sum of
U_{1}(\lambda)
and
U_{2}(\lambda)
, where
U_{1}(\lambda)
solves the Stokes resolvent problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
U_{2}(\lambda)
the heat resolvent problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. According to this decomposition, we define

\begin{split}u_{2,i}(x,t)&:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}e^{t\lambda}U_{i}(x,\lambda)\,{\rm d}\lambda,\quad i=1,2.\end{split}

(3.10)

Then
u_{2,1}(t)
is a solution of the nonstationary Stokes system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Moreover, by the quantitative estimate of
U_{1}
in Proposition
2.8
, using the following estimates

\begin{split}N_{\varepsilon,\lambda}\leq C\varepsilon^{-8/\mu},\qquad\|V\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\end{split}

valid for
\lambda\in\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}
, one can estimate
u_{2,1}(t)
as

\begin{split}|u_{2,1}(x,t)|\leq\exp\big(\exp(C\varepsilon^{-8/\mu})\big)\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}\langle x\rangle^{\exp(C\varepsilon^{-8/\mu})}e^{t},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}.\end{split}

(3.11)

In the last line, we have taken
C
in (
2.30
) to be sufficiently large.

In addition,
u_{2,2}(t)
is a solution of the heat equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In a similar manner as above, by the estimate of
U_{2}
in Proposition
2.9
, one can estimate
u_{2,2}(t)
as

\begin{split}|u_{2,2}(x,t)|\leq\exp\big(\exp(C\varepsilon^{-8/\mu})\big)\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}e^{C\varepsilon^{-1/2}|x|}e^{t},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}.\end{split}

(3.12)

Now we conclude (
1.2
) by (
3.8
)–(
3.12
) and replacing
8/\mu
by
\nu
. The proof is complete.

\Box

Proof of Theorem
1.2
:

This proof uses the notation in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. By density argument, it suffices to prove the statement when
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\sigma}(D)
. Set

u_{{\rm S}}(x,t)=u_{1}(x,t)+u_{2,1}(x,t).

(3.13)

Then the estimate (
1.4
) follows by the proof of Theorem
1.1
above.

Next we aim to construct the initial data
u_{0}
in Theorem
1.2
from
u_{2,2}(x,t)
. Firstly, observe that
u_{2,2}(x,t)
satisfies the following inequality

|u_{2,2}(x,t)|\leq Me^{a|x|^{2}},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,T),

for some
M,a>0
allowed to depend on each fixed
0<T<\infty
. Hence, thanks to the uniqueness result
[
19
, Section 7.1 (b)]
, by taking the initial trace
f(x):=u_{2,2}(x,0)
and using the Gauss kernel
G_{t}
, we see that
u_{2,2}
can be uniquely represented as

u_{2,2}(x,t)=(G_{t}\ast f)(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G_{t}(x-y)f(y)\,{\rm d}y,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}.

Taking cut-off
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
being equal to
1
on
B_{M}
, we consider

f_{M}(x):=\chi(x)f(x)=\chi(x)u_{2,2}(x,0),

which is smooth and compactly supported by definition. Since
f
can be written as

\begin{split}f(x)=u_{2,2}(x,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}U_{2}(x,\lambda)\,{\rm d}\lambda,\end{split}

and
U_{2}
satisfies (
2.42
), we have

\begin{split}&|u_{2,2}(x,t)-e^{t\Delta}f_{M}|\\
&=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G_{t}(x-y)\big(f(y)-f_{M}(y)\big)\,{\rm d}y\bigg|\\
&=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G_{t}(x-y)\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}U_{2}(y,\lambda)\big(1-\chi(y)\big)\bigg)\,{\rm d}y\bigg|\\
&\leq\exp\big(\exp(C\varepsilon^{-8/\mu})\big)\|V\|_{L^{2}(D)}\int_{|y|\geq M}G_{t}(x-y)e^{C\varepsilon^{-1/2}|y|}\,{\rm d}y.\end{split}

(3.14)

For given
a>1
and
0<T<\infty
, one can estimate the integral

I_{a}(x,t):=\int_{|y|\geq M}G_{t}(x-y)e^{a|y|}\,{\rm d}y,\quad(x,t)\in D\times[0,T]

by choosing
M
sufficiently large but independently of
a
as follows. Let
R>1
satisfy
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muD\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\subset B_{R}
. Then, by taking
M
large enough depending on
R,T
, we can obtain

G_{t}(x-y)\leq G_{T}(x-y)\leq\frac{1}{(4\pi T)^{3/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(|y|-R)^{2}}{4T}\Big),\quad|y|\geq M.

Thus, in the spherical coordinates,

\begin{split}I_{a}(x,t)&\leq\frac{1}{(4\pi T)^{3/2}}\int_{|y|\geq M}\exp\Big(-\frac{(|y|-R)^{2}}{4T}+a|y|\Big)\,{\rm d}y\\
&\leq C_{T}\int_{M}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{(r-R)^{2}}{4T}+ar\Big)r^{2}\,{\rm d}r,\end{split}

for some
C_{T}
depending on
T
. From

\begin{split}-\frac{(r-R)^{2}}{4T}+ar&=-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{4T}+aR+a^{2}T,\qquad r_{0}:=R+2aT,\end{split}

we see that

I_{a}(x,t)\leq C_{T}\exp\Big(a^{2}(R+T)\Big)\int_{M}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{4T}\Big)r^{2}\,{\rm d}r.

(3.15)

By change of variables and the Young inequality,

\begin{split}&\int_{M}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{4T}\Big)r^{2}\,{\rm d}r\\
&=\int_{M-r_{0}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{4T}\Big)(u+r_{0})^{2}\,{\rm d}u\\
&\leq 2\int_{M-r_{0}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{4T}\Big)(u^{2}+r_{0}^{2})\,{\rm d}u.\end{split}

Moreover, since

\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{4T}\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{(M-r_{0})^{2}}{8T}\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{8T}\Big),\quad u\geq M-r_{0},

we have

\begin{split}&\int_{M}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{4T}\Big)r^{2}\,{\rm d}r\\
&\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{(M-r_{0})^{2}}{8T}\Big)\int_{M-r_{0}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{u^{2}}{8T}\Big)(u^{2}+r_{0}^{2})\,{\rm d}u\\
&\leq C_{T}a^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{(M-r_{0})^{2}}{8T}\Big).\end{split}

Substituting this estimate into (
3.15
), we obtain

I_{a}(x,t)\leq C_{T}a^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{(M-r_{0})^{2}}{8T}+a^{2}(R+T)\Big).

Hence, by choosing
M
so that

M=-C_{0}\big(\log(a^{-4})\big)a\exp\big(C_{0}a^{8/\mu}\big)(R+T)^{1/2}T^{1/2}+r_{0},

(3.16)

and by taking
C_{0}
sufficiently large depending on
R,T
, then we conclude that

I_{a}(x,t)\leq C_{1}a^{-2}\exp\big(-C_{1}\exp(a^{8/\mu})\big),\quad(x,t)\in D\times[0,T]

(3.17)

for some
C_{1}
independent of
a
and depending on
T,R
.

With the estimate (
3.17
) with
a=C\varepsilon^{-1/2}
, we go back to (
3.14
) to see that

\begin{split}|u_{2,2}(x,t)-e^{t\Delta}f_{M}|\leq\varepsilon\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)},\quad(x,t)\in D\times[0,T],\end{split}

(3.18)

by taking
C_{0}
in (
3.16
) to be sufficiently large again if needed. Fixing such
M
, we set

u_{0}(x)=f_{M}(x)=\chi(x)u_{2,2}(x,0)=\chi(x)\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma\,\cap\{|\lambda|\leq L\}}U_{2}(x,\lambda)\,{\rm d}\lambda\bigg).

(3.19)

Then the desired approximation (
1.3
) follows from the definitions of
u_{{\rm S}}
in (
3.13
) and of
u_{{\rm H}}
by
u_{{\rm H}}:=e^{t\Delta}u_{0}
, the estimates in the proof of Theorem
1.1
above, and (
3.18
). Moreover, by setting
\nu=\max\{8/\mu,1/2\}
, we see that the estimate (
1.5
) for the initial data
u_{0}
is a consequence of (
3.12
), (
3.16
) and (
3.19
). This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

\Box
