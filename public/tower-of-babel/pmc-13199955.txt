Behavioral health and drunk driving prevention: The history and policy implications of the Washington Regional Alcohol Program (WRAP)

Introduction

In the winter of 2000–01, the Washington Regional Alcohol Program (WRAP) kicked off the tenth year of its widely-acclaimed SoberRide® service with a flyer inviting residents of the Washington, D.C.–Maryland–Virginia (DMV) area to request a free taxi home from their holiday festivities. WRAP committed to pay for rides of up to $50 between 8:00 p.m. and 4:00 a.m. from December 13 to January 2 as part of its mission to prevent injuries and deaths associated with drunk driving. The flyer did not merely inform the public about the SoberRide service but cast drinking and driving itself as a personal choice with serious consequences. The poster warned, “YOU make the CALL,” and pictured a police car on one side and a yellow cab on the other. The correct options for those who decided to “celebrate the holidays with alcohol,” WRAP insisted, were to “designate a driver” or to call SoberRide. The alternative was to risk being arrested for drunk driving or causing a crash [1].

Since its establishment in 1983, WRAP has worked to decrease the heavy costs of drunk driving and underage drinking through a range of interventions. WRAP provides resources and holds public events to teach DMV residents about responsible alcohol use; offers SoberRide on holidays like the Fourth of July; partners with private stadiums, bars, and restaurants to restrict access to alcohol; and advocates for strict drinking-and-driving policies. These include sobriety checks, minimum drinking age laws, open container laws, low blood alcohol concentration (BAC) levels for automatic DWI (driving while intoxicated) offenses, and mandatory sentences for DWI convictions [2]. These efforts seem to have had some impact. From 1988 to 2023, annual fatalities involving drunk driving across the DMV fell 50% from 160 to 79. The number of alcohol-related crashes likewise declined 33% from almost 4800 to 3200 [3,4]. Although national data on nonfatal collisions are scarce, regionwide mortality trends compare favorably with the statistics available from the Fatality Analysis Reporting System [5],1.

According to WRAP cofounders Bill Bronrott and John V. Moulden, these strategies were complementary parts of the WRAP model, a public health approach to preventing drunk driving and underage drinking based on the principles of general deterrence. Moulden, who started his career at the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and later became president of the National Commission Against Drunk Driving (NCADD), concluded that the most effective way to keep individuals from drinking and driving was to increase the real and perceived risks of doing so [6,7]. Along these lines, WRAP worked to change the culture around drunk driving, turning what was once regarded as a trivial mistake into the “most common violent crime.” [8,9] WRAP not only applied education, statistics, and stories about deadly crashes to scare and to shame would-be drunk drivers but also stiffened the penalties for violations. WRAP and its supporters tended to interpret drunk driving as a problem of unsafe behavior with several points of intervention: the choice to drink or not; the choice to drink more or less alcohol on one occasion; and the choice to drive or not to drive after drinking [10]. For decades, WRAP has tried to steer the public toward safer decisions at each of these steps.2

WRAP built on the precedent of earlier initiatives to control drunk driving following the repeal of Prohibition in 1933. Despite fueling violent crime and corruption, Prohibition sharply decreased the quantity of drinks consumed per capita and contemporary mortality rates from alcoholism and cirrhosis [11,12]. These trends reversed from the mid-1930s onward, contributing to the rise of crashes, injuries, and deaths involving intoxicated drivers and new efforts to prevent drunk driving. Until the 1970s, however, the success of the alcoholic beverage and advertising industries at placing the responsibility for safe drinking on consumers; the cultural distinctions between “social,” “responsible,” “excessive,” and “problem” drinkers; and the focus of experts on education and alcoholism constrained these projects [9,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Drunk driving was dismissed as nothing more than a “bad habit” and became a fact of life [18].

Historians and public health researchers have explained how advocacy groups including Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD) disrupted this status quo, redefining drunk driving as a public health crisis resulting from selfish personal choices [9,19,20]. Under Cindi Lamb and Candace Lightener, MADD publicized the dangers of drunk driving, backed heavy and consistent penalties for DWIs, and labeled drunk driving “a socially acceptable form of homicide.” [21] MADD drew on traumatic experiences from the casualties of drunk driving to prompt Americans to imagine one day filling the roles of the penitent drunk driver or the innocent victim [22]. Overall, MADD took a zero tolerance approach to drunk driving and considered punishment necessary for deterrence and to discipline those who put others at risk.

Drawing on written records from the WRAP archives and interviews with its cofounders, this article investigates the history, methods, and theoretical bases of the Washington Regional Alcohol Program to make three contributions to the historical and public health scholarship on drunk driving. First, most accounts of late-twentieth-century efforts to curb drunk driving focus on grassroots, “victim-led” groups including Remove Intoxicated Drivers (RID), Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD), and Students Against Drunk Driving (SADD) [9,11,15,20]. WRAP collaborated with these groups but had a distinctive structure, forming public-private partnerships between safety experts, law enforcement, elected officials, businesses, and the media. WRAP brings out another perspective on the movement against drunk driving.

Second, WRAP exemplifies ways to extend the practice of health behavior from molding personal knowledge and beliefs to reconfiguring the sociomaterial environments where people make choices. Transportation safety experts have recently proposed a Safe Systems Pyramid to schematize the level of individual effort and public health impact associated with specific injury countermeasures. This model clarifies how WRAP compares with conventional programs based on driver accountability. The pyramid has five tiers, placing the most personally labor-intensive and least broadly effective interventions at the top and the least laborious and most sweeping interventions at the base. From top to bottom, these tiers consist of education, “active” safety measures (e.g. seatbelts), “latent” or passive safety measures (e.g. airbags), reengineering the built environment (e.g. speed bumps), and socioeconomic factors [23]. Whereas MADD originally focused on education and the active measure of enforcing DWI laws, WRAP has experimented with a wider range of socio-ecological interventions including subsidizing Lyft rides on holidays and equipping new cars with ignition interlocks [24,25]. Although WRAP has generally worked with its corporate partners to implement these programs on a voluntary basis, comparable solutions could be expanded and codified into law.

Finally, WRAP raises questions about the broader public health shift from deterrence to harm reduction. Despite the benefits of decriminalizing, destigmatizing, and administering new services to improve the safety of risky behaviors like illicit drug use and unprotected sex, WRAP has consistently worked to make drunk driving more socially unacceptable [26]. Philip J. Cook has divided prevailing strategies to control drunk driving into three categories: discouraging driving after drinking using education and enforcement; redesigning cars, roadways, and public transit systems to minimize the damage resulting from drunk driving crashes; and restricting access to alcohol with serving limits, minimum drinking age laws, and excise taxes [11]. WRAP combines all three approaches based on the principles of health behavior, unequivocally condemning drunk driving, incentivizing safer alternatives, and occasionally constraining the choices of those who might otherwise choose to drink and drive [27]. The movement to combat drunk driving can and should combine general deterrence with policies meant to prevent the misuse of alcohol from leading to fatal collisions.

Methods

This article uses historical methods (1) to analyze how WRAP tried to counteract drunk driving in the DMV and (2) to reflect on the policy implications of its approach. WRAP not only intensified the risks of punishment and social disapproval to convince drinkers not to drive but also shifted control over this decision to family, friends, and coworkers; retailers; systems; and technology. On one hand, WRAP – like MADD – characterized drunk driving as an unjustifiable moral lapse to call for stronger deterrence measures. On the other, WRAP tried to make these penalties incidental using environmental interventions to reduce alcohol consumption outside the home and to deliver alternative transportation.

This project relies on three sets of primary sources: four boxes of archival records from WRAP consisting of about 2500 pages of flyers, educational resources, press releases, reports, news clippings, and institutional newsletters; 4 hour-long interviews with WRAP cofounders Bill Bronrott, John Moulden, and Jerry Sachs and with Kurt Erickson, its current president; and the material available on the WRAP website. The authors of this article consulted every source from these repositories before moving forward with textual analysis. These sources were then interpreted using close-reading, synthesis, and historical contextualization. This procedure can be compared with abductive reasoning: the coauthors intensively reviewed material to identify key themes and used these themes to reanalyze the archival records and interview transcripts with a focus on behavioral health. In the methodological terms of the historical profession, this article offers a social and institutional account of WRAP and interrogates how its members and supporters have conceptualized “behavior” from the 1980s to the present.

Across these phases, the authors compiled literature on the history and control of drunk driving, traffic safety, and health behavior using deep iterative searches of the library catalogue, academic databases, and the reference lists of previous studies. The resulting set of primary and secondary evidence was arranged thematically and chronologically and then turned into a draft for feedback from WRAP and the Johns Hopkins University Center for Injury Research and Policy (CIRP). Two methodological qualifications are worth underlining here. First, this article does not attempt to measure the success of WRAP and mostly provides long-term data on drunk driving for context. Second, the four cited interviews with the leaders of WRAP have been interpreted using the principles of oral history, which delves into the experiences of the interviewee rather than seeking to extract objective information about certain events [28]. This humanistic approach complements the quantitative and qualitative strategies commonly used to study public health practice, opening new lines of cross-disciplinary inquiry.

Results

In December of 1982, officials from Washington D.C., Montgomery County, Arlington County, Fairfax County, and Prince George's County signed a resolution calling on “government, private sector businesses, media, and citizen groups” to convince “all citizens to avoid the use of alcohol while driving in order to eliminate death and injury during this holiday season.” [29] This campaign took up the playbook that WRAP had commissioned from the public relations firm Earle Palmer Brown and set the stage for the incorporation of WRAP a few months later. Whereas MADD relied on grassroots activists to apply political pressure and to conduct outreach, WRAP tapped into the business community, law enforcement, the media, and regional governments [30]. This coalition communicated the dangers of drinking and driving with newspapers, radio, television, posters, pocket guides, publicity events, and trainings for high-school students and employees [8]. WRAP also allocated resources to crack down on drunk driving and to reconfigure the contexts where drinkers chose to drive through restrictive serving policies.

For the cofounders of WRAP, drinking and driving usually resulted from a series of risky, immediate, and context-dependent decisions. As John Moulden explained, a choice at the first points of this process did not necessarily determine the choices downstream: “You think, okay, well, we're gonna have a lot of fun. And you know, we're probably going to get a bit loaded. We don't have to drive. We can make some other arrangement. Okay, nothing wrong with that […] The last thing we are is anti-drinking.” [6] Those who went out for a drink could always plan ahead, designating a driver or finding another mode of transportation. Short-term thinking, conversely, could lead responsible community members to make the “wrong” choices, and WRAP aimed to extricate decisions about drinking from the act of drinking itself.

WRAP instructed DMV residents to drink responsibly and to consider the costs of a crash or DWI before choosing to drink. An early WRAP poster invited its audience to “Join the Team to Stop Drunk Driving” with a photograph of four local athletes, declaring “Know your own limits,” “Don't let a friend drive drunk,” and “Report drunk drivers to the police.” [31] WRAP repeated this messaging in its guidebook for employers. The 2000 edition, which featured a table-of-contents designed to look like a tombstone, described how to safely host office parties with alcohol and contained a chart to help drinkers estimate their BACs after various numbers of drinks [32]. WRAP also printed recipes for mocktails and explained how to perform sobriety checks on intoxicated friends and coworkers. Overall, WRAP took a pragmatic approach to adult drinkers: “By doing a few simple things, we all can play a role in saving lives and preventing injuries.” Students under the age of 21, conversely, were told, “Think. Don't Drink.” [33,34].

At the same time, WRAP strove to influence the behavior of drinkers who failed to plan ahead with a set of structural solutions. WRAP not only worked with law enforcement to deter would-be drunk drivers with sobriety checkpoints and mandatory minimum penalties for DWIs but also asked how the availability of alcohol led some community members to drive drunk. In the winter of 1982, Moulden went on the radio to warn the public about the impacts of drunk driving. The broadcast attracted the attention of businessman Jerry Sachs, the president of the Capital Centre in Washington, who went on to cofound WRAP with Moulden and Bill Bronrott, the press secretary for U.S. Congressman Michael Barnes and future member of the Maryland House of Delegates [34]. According to Sachs, the radio program helped him realize that the Capital Centre was basically “the largest open-air bar” in the DMV. He soon implemented new policies to stop serving alcoholic beverages at events after halftime, to card potential buyers, to cap the number of drinks per patron, and to sell smaller beers. Sachs persuaded other businessowners who sold alcohol for on-site consumption to follow suit. As retailers decreased how much their customers drank, these spaces became more family-friendly and profitable [35]. Preventing drunk driving, WRAP reasoned, had to involve industry as well as consumers.

These supply-side countermeasures also included the WRAP SoberRide program, which turned from drinking to driving. Since 1991, SoberRide has paid for 90,769 rides home over the winter holiday season, New Year's Eve, St. Patrick's Day, Cinco de Mayo, Independence Day, and Halloween [36]. Whereas most other WRAP initiatives targeted what people decided to do before and during drinking, SoberRide acted as a last resort for DMV residents who drank too much to drive but might take the risk because of the cost of a cab or unreliable access to regional public transit. Due to its small budget and persistent anxieties about the moral hazard of incentivizing excessive drinking, WRAP only runs the service on specific holidays and categorically bars riders under the age of 21 [24]. Despite these qualifications, SoberRide has become a helpful safety valve at the end of the causal chain from the initial, personal choice to drink to the sometimes-deadly choice to drink and drive.

Discussion

Against the backdrop of the 1980s backlash against government regulation and welfare programs, WRAP committed to the model of public-private partnerships to counteract drinking and driving. The Presidential Commission on Drunk Driving (1982–1983), which coincided with the formation of WRAP, called on grassroots activists and industry leaders to educate the public about the risks of drunk driving and to push for stricter enforcement [37]. Speaking to the positive results of this approach with WRAP, Bill Bronrott confirmed, “These community coalitions have shown remarkable strength in changing public attitudes, behaviors, and policies in communities where drunk driving was once tolerated or ignored.” Bronrott attributed these accomplishments to the way WRAP was able to pool expertise, raise money, collect in-kind donations, coordinate outreach, and reach different audiences through its network of contacts [8]. WRAP has capitalized on this structure to shape road safety across the DMV.

The long-term reliance of WRAP on volunteers, philanthropy, and corporations, however, has multiple downsides. Due to its increasing ridership and the constraints of the WRAP budget, SoberRide eventually capped the covered amount for a free ride home at $50 in 2000 and then lowered the maximum fare to $30 in 2011 and $15 in 2017 3. These changes make the program less attractive to drivers worried about costly fares and may specifically discourage participation from lower-income, rural, and exurban residents of the DMV. Although WRAP benefits from the support of businesses and trade groups including GEICO, the Washington Area New Automobile Dealers Association, Anheuser-Busch, the Beer Institute, and Giant Food, WRAP has often been willing to settle for small-scale efforts with uncontroversial goals. WRAP has broadly focused on deterring drinkers from driving and offering guidelines for retailers, not raising taxes on alcohol or tightly regulating the market 4. As Moulden stated, “The last thing [most companies] want is suppressed alcohol sales.” Even if coalitions like WRAP can improve traffic safety, public-private partnerships allow businesses and nonprofits to set the agenda for this work based on “vested interests” as well as the common good [6].

WRAP also demonstrates the extent to which the environment influences behavior and raises questions about whose choices matter to prevent drinking and driving. Arising alongside the socio-ecological theories of experts including the psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner, WRAP located personal choices within a matrix of overlapping systems [38]. WRAP not only targeted the actions of would-be drunk drivers but also tried to mold the opinions of community members who already drank safely or abstained entirely, intensifying the social pressure to drink less per session and to avoid driving home [39,40]. Other WRAP programs trained staff and supervisors to monitor and, if necessary, to cut off their customers for safety reasons. Police and prosecutors were similarly instructed to test the BACs of suspected drunk drivers and to penalize offenders with license suspensions, fines, and jail time [41]. Extending responsibility for drinking and driving beyond consumers of alcoholic beverages, WRAP applied the principles of behavioral health to radically change contemporary decision environments.

Perhaps most crucially, WRAP invites us to reflect on the tradeoffs of using stigma as a tool for public health. The Black Lives Matter movement has underlined the risks of relying on law enforcement to keep marginalized communities safe, and WRAP programs may expose the Black and Latine residents of the DMV to more traffic stops and arrests [7,24]. Although automatic social and legal consequences for DWIs improve their effectiveness as deterrents, this solution may impose disproportionate costs on overpoliced populations and permanently damage the employment options and reputations of convicted defendants [9,[42], [43], [44]]. WRAP has correspondingly turned to devices like ignition interlocks to keep drunk drivers off the road without reinforcing existing racial, ethnic, and class biases.

Despite the expanding application of harm reduction to illicit drug use, sex, and crime, WRAP reveals the benefits of stigmatizing and criminalizing some risky behaviors. According to Moulden, WRAP combined education, policing, and the SoberRide program to delegitimize the historical “excuses” for drunk driving, making the decision to do so morally indefensible [6,9]. This strategy may have reduced the probability of drinkers choosing to drive and allowed WRAP and its allies to strengthen the penalties for drinking and driving. These punishments reinforced the social stigma attached to DWI convictions and added to their practical costs, forming a negative feedback loop between the cultural perception and legal status of drunk driving. This feedback loop was crucial to the WRAP model and still informs deterrence today [45].

Along these lines, WRAP offers valuable insights for future public health approaches to gun violence and traumatic brain injuries (TBIs). Strategies for mitigating injuries from firearms (e.g. locking safes, waiting periods, and background checks) and contact sports (e.g. concussion protocols, helmets, and rule changes) have often fallen short. Many Americans interpret these injuries as tragic but inescapable outcomes of the freedoms to own weapons and to play risky sports [[46], [47], [48]]. WRAP overcame similar pushback regarding drunk driving, and safety practitioners are working to communicate the risks of these decisions and to implement promising ecological solutions. These possibilities include repealing stand-your-ground laws; raising excise taxes on firearms; systematizing licensing for gun owners; holding schools, leagues, teams, and coaches liable for the safety of their athletes; promoting non-contact sports; and forcing sports leagues and gun manufacturers – like tobacco companies – to pay for the high healthcare costs of their products. Following WRAP, these measures seek to influence the behavior of people who might use firearms or play risky sports and to change the decision environments where these choices can lead to harm.
