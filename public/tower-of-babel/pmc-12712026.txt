Effects of diaphragmatic breathing training on alarm fatigue and patient safety culture among ICU nurses: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Sirous Pourkhajoei, Fatemeh Najarzadeh Riseh, Neda Asadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aim Alarms in ICUs are a major factor in causing fatigue in nurses. This can lead to a decrease in the quality of nursing care and patient safety culture. This study aimed to investigate the effect of diaphragmatic breathing training on Alarm fatigue and patient safety culture from the perspective of intensive care unit nurses. Methods This quasi-experimental study was conducted on ICU nurses in southeastern Iran, Kerman. Eligible nurses were selected through purposive sampling and allocated to either the intervention group (n = 25) or the control group (n = 25). The intervention group received diaphragmatic breathing training for four weeks. Data were collected using the Alarm Fatigue Symptoms Scale, and Patient safety culture questionnaire at pre-intervention and immediately post-intervention. Results The findings showed that the mean score of Alarm fatigue in the intervention group decreased from 67.62 before the intervention to 51.07 after the intervention (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the mean Patient safety culture score in the intervention group increased from 129.37 before the intervention to 154.82 after the intervention (P < 0.001). Between-group comparisons indicated that the Alarm fatigue score and Patient safety culture score significantly improve in the intervention group compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Conclusion The results of this study showed that diaphragmatic breathing training can reduce alarm fatigue and increase patient safety culture in ICU nurses. Diaphragmatic breathing training is recommended as a simple, practical, and non-invasive method to maintain the mental and physical health of nurses and increase patient safety culture.

Introduction

The intensive care unit (ICU) is a vital part of hospitals where critically ill patients are admitted1. One of the important equipment in ICU care is alarm systems to closely monitor the patient’s condition2. However, due to the large number of medical devices and continuous monitoring, they often generate excessive alarms that may not always indicate significant changes in the patient’s clinical condition3,4. An increase in the number of alarms can lead to alarm fatigue, a condition in which nurses become less sensitive to alarms and may not respond appropriately5. This can have negative psychological effects on nurses, causing them to experience high levels of fatigue6. This fatigue not only affects the quality of professional life, but can also compromise patient safety culture7. Despite the use of technology in patient management, monitoring devices can have unintended negative consequences for patients. This issue is considered one of the top 10 technology related threats to patient health and between 2012 and 20148. The emergency care research institute ranked it as the first technology threat to patients9.

Given the high occupational sensitivity, mental health is of particular importance in this group. Several pharmacological and non-pharmacological methods are proposed to address this problem, and nowadays non-pharmacological treatments are preferred due to their low side effects. One of these methods is short-term breathing exercises such as diaphragmatic breathing. Researchers have studied this issue on patients and have shown positive effects on anxiety relief7. In a study by Perciavalle et al., the positive effects of deep breathing on mood improvement and stress reduction in students were proven10. In another study, students were able to reduce their anxiety during the ASCII exam with the deep breathing technique11.

Continuous monitoring of alarm fatigue, nurse stress, and patient safety culture indicators is recommended to guide adjustments in training frequency and timing. Using these data to refine intervention protocols can support sustainable improvements in nurse well-being and safety culture.Breathing-based interventions should complement system-level alarm management measures such as parameter optimization, alarm escalation pathways, and technical filtering. Integrating physiological stress-reduction with engineering and workflow solutions may help reduce overall alarm burden while maintaining patient safety culture10. Incorporating a concise practical module on diaphragmatic breathing into nurse orientation and continuing3 professional development could ensure that staff learn correct technique, timing, and self-practice strategies. Embedding this skill within education programs may promote sustained use during high-stress clinical tasks3.

Despite these findings, the application of diaphragmatic breathing in ICU nurses particularly its impact on alarm fatigue and patient safety culture remains underexplored12. Considering the multifactorial nature of alarm fatigue, combining individual-level interventions such as breathing techniques with system-level strategies (e.g., optimizing alarm parameters and escalation protocols) may offer a more comprehensive solution13. However, no study was found that examined the effect of diaphragmatic breathing technique in ICU nurses and its effect on alarm fatigue and patient safety culture. Since one study showed that 70% of nurses reported that frequent alarms caused them fatigue and stress, this fatigue can lead to a decrease in the quality of care and an increase in risks for patients14. The researchers conducted the present study.

Methods

Study design and participants

This quasi-experimental before-and-after study was conducted from May to July 2025 to evaluate the effect of diaphragmatic breathing training on alarm fatigue and patient safety culture among ICU nurses in southeastern Iran. Participants were selected by convenience sampling based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. After enrollment, participants were pseudorandom allocated to either the intervention or control group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured through sealed opaque envelopes. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician, and participant enrollment was conducted by the research coordinator. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and instructors was not feasible. However, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation to minimize detection and analysis bias. The intervention protocol was referenced and approved by faculty members specialized in respiratory therapy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included, at least an BSc’s degree, six months of work experience, no lung problems and not attending yoga classes, no hearing disorder, Any nurse who did not meet these criteria was not included in the study.

Exclusion criteria included: reluctance to continue participating in the study, starting to use sedatives and nerve medications during the study, and failure to perform interventions more than one day a week.

Study measurements

In this study, data were collected using a three-part questionnaire, including, (1) demographic information, (2) The Alarm Fatigue Symptoms Scale, and (3) Patient safety culture questionnaire.

Demographic information questionnaire

This questionnaire included age, sex, marital status, level of education, Sleep disorder, Shift Type, Second job, Relevant training course, Work experience ICU, Number of night shifts per month.

Alarm fatigue symptoms scale

This scale was developed and validated by the researchers of the present study. We used comparative method, meaning that items were prepared by reviewing the texts with related keywords. The scale consists of 17 items. The scores were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from never = 1 to always = 5(total score 17–85). content validity was performed with a group of experts. I-CVI between 0.8 and 1 and S-CVI 0.98, Content validity ratio (CVR) 0.78. were obtained. The results of the exploratory factor analysis confirmed two dimensions of the accepted interpretation. Internal consistency of the AFSS was good (α = 0.815). Cronbach’s alpha for Scale and its subscales ranged from 0.85 to 0.875.

Patient safety culture questionnaire

known as HSOPSC, was designed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality in the United States by Joann Sorra et al. in 2004.This questionnaire has 42 questions that measure 12 different dimensions of patient safety culture. The Likert response scale is 5-point ranging from strongly agree16 to strongly disagree1. The score obtained from the patient safety culture scale ranges from 42 to 210. Joann Sorra et al. reported in 2004 that the construct validity of the components of the Patient safety Culture Questionnaire ranged from 0.23 to 0.60. The reliability of the questionnaire dimensions was also calculated separately, with Cronbach’s alpha for each dimension ranging from 0.63 to 0.8617. In the current study, the content validity index was determined to be 0.83, and the internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.89.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.KMU.REC.1403.587), and all procedures were conducted under relevant national and international ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. They were given ample time to consider participation and could ask questions freely. Voluntariness was emphasized, and no form of pressure or persuasion was applied. Only those who provided written informed consent were included in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study process, and all collected data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes.

Intervention

The diaphragmatic breathing training protocol used in this study was adapted from previously validated programs developed by Perciavalle et al. (2017)10 and Jerath et al. (2006)18, which describe stepwise training for abdominal and full breathing aimed at promoting relaxation and autonomic regulation.

Training sessions were coordinated according to each participant’s work schedule. Nurses in the intervention group received the same educational content and daily practice schedule, but they performed the exercises individually during their own rest periods to avoid interference with clinical duties.

In coordination with the participants, time was allocated for staff training. The relevant questionnaires were completed. At the beginning of the training session, explanations were given about respiratory skills and their effects on physical and mental health. Then, abdominal breathing exercises and full breathing (three parts) were taught with the help of educational videos. The trainings have been approved by researchers along with professors specializing in this field. In the first week, abdominal breathing in the supine position, in the second week, abdominal breathing in the sitting position, and in the third and fourth weeks, complete breathing, were considered as interventions. These exercises were performed once a day at the beginning of the day and once at night, 12 times in each turn, and one breath was added to this number every day. Training was delivered by a nurse educator in a single ~ 20-minute on-site orientation using a standardized script and a brief video; participants then practiced twice daily (start of day and at night). Each session began at 12 breaths and increased by + 1 breath/day as tolerated; each breath used a slow nasal inhale (~ 4–5 s), brief pause, and pursed-lip exhale (~ 5–6 s) without strain.

Adherence was tracked with a simple daily log (AM/PM completion noted); Fidelity was promoted with a 5-item instructor checklist at orientation (posture, hand placement, abdominal expansion, pacing, pursed-lip exhalation) and brief spot observations (~ 10% of participants/week) with immediate corrective feedback.

At the beginning of the new week, this number started again from m 12 breaths in each turn. at the end of the fourth week, the relevant questionnaires were completed again.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22. The primary outcome was alarm fatigue score; the secondary outcome was patient safety culture. Chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, and independent t-test were used to compare baseline characteristics and outcome scores between groups. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals were reported for primary comparisons. To simplify the tables, data were expressed as: F (%), mean (SD). The word “comparison” was included in the table titles. It was considered more appropriate to separate the mean comparison table from the demographic table.

Discussion

Recent findings demonstrate that diaphragmatic breathing training, as a simple, practical, and non-invasive intervention, can significantly alleviate alarm fatigue and enhance patient safety culture among ICU nurses. Multiple studies in health psychology and clinical therapy have confirmed that diaphragmatic breathing is an effective relaxation technique with beneficial effects on both physical and mental health19.

The beneficial effects observed in this study are plausibly explained by the autonomic and cognitive mechanisms activated through diaphragmatic breathing. This technique reduces sympathetic arousal and enhances parasympathetic activity, promoting physiological relaxation and emotional stability. Such regulation can lower state anxiety and mental fatigue, thereby improving attentional recovery and the ability to respond effectively to clinically relevant alarms. Through these processes, brief deep-breathing practice may restore alertness and responsiveness, mitigating alarm desensitization and promoting safer, more attentive performance in the ICU environment. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that slow, controlled breathing modulates the autonomic nervous system and improves attentional regulation18.

The observed reduction in alarm fatigue symptoms in the intervention group highlights the efficacy of this technique in improving nurses’ psychological resilience against frequent alarm exposure.

The results of the present study are consistent with the findings of previous studies. Several studies have shown that training in relaxation techniques, including deep breathing, meditation, and mindfulness, can significantly reduce anxiety and stress levels among healthcare workers. For example, a study by Liu et al. (2021) on intensive care unit nurses showed that performing deep breathing exercises for two weeks significantly reduced anxiety, mental fatigue, and work errors20. Also, Askar et al. (2024) reported that implementing regular relaxation programs in the workplace reduced the severity of fatigue in intensive care unit nurses and did not have any unwanted side effects21.

Therefore, training in relaxation techniques such as deep breathing appears essential and beneficial in mitigating anxiety among nurses a key psychological factor exacerbated by alarm overload. Relaxation techniques have been widely used in anxiety management, both individually and within group therapy settings, yielding favorable outcomes. Thus, implementing deep breathing strategies may serve as a viable approach to reducing alarm induced fatigue.

The improvement in patient safety culture among nurses further underscores the positive impact of diaphragmatic breathing training on fostering a safety-oriented culture within ICU settings. The improvement in patient safety culture perceptions among the intervention group suggests that diaphragmatic breathing not only benefits individual well-being but also contributes to a stronger safety culture in the ICU. Enhanced focus, emotional stability, and reduced stress likely play a role in fostering safer nursing practices and more effective responses to clinical alarms. This result is consistent with Liu et al. (2022), emphasized the role of mindfulness meditation significantly improved the patient safety culture. The researchers stated, this technique as a simple and effective intervention, plays a positive role in improving patient safety22.

Moreover, Sammer et al. (2010) demonstrated a direct link between clinical staff’s mental health and patient safety culture outcomes23. Supporting this, There is evidence that training in mindfulness-based techniques can improve conscious thinking in care situations and provide a good foundation for safe behavior24. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that this type of intervention improves job satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, and reduces emotional burden, which are also important predictors of a patient safety culture25.

Compared to previous studies, the present research introduces innovation in terms of intervention type, duration, and assessment tools. Unlike studies that employed behavioral training or alarm management technologies26, this study utilized diaphragmatic breathing as a straightforward and feasible method within clinical environments. The structured breathing protocol provided sufficient time for stabilization of psychological and physiological effects. The four-week intervention period provided sufficient time for the stabilization of both psychological and physiological effects27. Jerath et al. (2006) explained the physiological mechanism of deep breathing in regulating the autonomic nervous system and reducing sympathetic arousal18, which may account for the observed outcomes.

These outcomes are plausibly explained by reduced sympathetic arousal and improved attentional recovery from brief deep breathing practice, which can lower state anxiety and cognitive load and thereby restore alertness and responsiveness to clinically relevant alarms. The results extend existing evidence on breathing-based relaxation by applying it to occupational alarm burden in critical care; unlike technology-focused alarm management, this low-cost, portable intervention can be implemented immediately as a complement to system-level solutions and may improve both individual well-being and unit-level safety performance28,29.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

Despite these promising results, Conducting the research an experimental study with a control group is conducted systematically, it will have better generalizability. Additionally, reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce bias. Studies such as Hravnak et al. (2018) and Salyers et al. (2017) have also highlighted similar challenges in measuring fatigue and safety30,31. The absence of physiological indicators such as heart rate variability (HRV) or electroencephalography (EEG) to assess fatigue represents a methodological limitation that future research should address. Future trials should incorporate physiological outcomes (e.g., HRV), objective alarm response metrics, longer follow-up, multicenter replication, and comparisons with combined interventions (mindfulness, workflow changes) to confirm durability, mechanism, and real-world impact on adverse events14.

Moreover, future research could compare diaphragmatic breathing with or integrate it into combined interventions for instance, mindfulness-based stress reduction or workflow modification programs to elucidate mechanisms and confirm its real-world impact on adverse event rates and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed that diaphragmatic breathing training can reduce alarm fatigue and increase patient safety culture in ICU nurses. Diaphragmatic breathing training is recommended as a simple, practical, and non-invasive method to maintain the mental and physical health of nurses and increase patient safety culture.
