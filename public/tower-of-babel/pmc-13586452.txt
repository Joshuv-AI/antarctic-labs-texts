Divergent associations of estimated cardiorespiratory fitness with cognitive trajectories in Chinese and U.S. middle‐aged and older adults: A descriptive cross‐national comparison from CHARLS and HRS
Authors: Jiangping Ma, Nan Chen, Linghao Xu, Jingxuan Huang, Huifang Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe association between cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and cognition may differ across populations, but cross‐cultural evidence remains limited. We explored whether estimated CRF (eCRF) shows divergent associations with cognitive trajectories in Chinese and U.S. middle‐aged and older adults.METHODSWe included 3458 participants from CHARLS and 6905 from HRS. Cognitive trajectories were identified using latent class growth modelling. eCRF was estimated using sex‐specific non‐exercise algorithms. The unit of eCRF is metabolic equivalents of task (MET). Multinomial logistic regression examined the association between eCRF (per 1‐MET increase) and trajectory membership, with the persistently high trajectory as the reference.RESULTSThree cognitive trajectories—persistently high, moderate, and low—were identified in both cohorts. In CHARLS, higher eCRF was unexpectedly associated with increased odds of the low (odds ratio [OR] = 1.28, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16–1.43) and moderate (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.18) trajectories. In HRS, higher eCRF was associated with lower odds of the moderate trajectory (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98), with no significant association for the low trajectory.DISCUSSIONThis descriptive observation reveals divergent associations in the eCRF‐cognition relationship between Chinese and U.S. adults, but should be interpreted cautiously. The finding persisted across multiple sensitivity analyses. However, given the lack of direct CRF measurement and objective assessment of activity type, we cannot distinguish whether this divergence reflects measurement differences and residual confounding, or true biological heterogeneity. Context‐specific validation of eCRF equations and objective activity‐specific measures are needed to clarify eCRF's role in cognitive aging.

BACKGROUND

The accelerating aging of the global population has elevated cognitive decline to a major public health challenge, severely compromising quality of life for individuals and imposing immense burdens on families and societies. With the prevalence of dementia projected to surpass 150 million worldwide by 2050 and in the absence of disease‐modifying treatments, 1 the identification of modifiable protective factors is critically important for delaying cognitive aging.

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)—the integrated ability of the circulatory, respiratory, and muscular systems to supply and utilize oxygen during sustained activity—represents a key modifiable factor. 2 A robust body of evidence associates higher CRF with superior cognitive performance, a slower rate of cognitive decline, and a reduced risk of dementia. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Longitudinal studies corroborate that individuals with higher baseline CRF experience a more favorable cognitive trajectory over time. 7 , 8 , 9 Furthermore, CRF is linked to a lower risk of progression in key Alzheimer's disease biomarkers, suggesting it may confer protection by influencing underlying neuropathology. 10 However, the association is not universal; some studies report null findings for specific cognitive domains, indicating the relationship may be moderated by population characteristics, cultural context, and measurement approaches. 11 Significantly, the prevailing evidence base originates predominantly from Western, high‐income countries, creating a gap in understanding whether the CRF–cognition relationship generalizes across diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts.

In recent years, estimated CRF (eCRF) algorithms based on non‐exercise parameters have been developed and validated. 12 , 13 These algorithms demonstrate good agreement with measured CRF and are suitable for large‐scale cohort studies. 14 However, cross‐national comparisons of the eCRF‐cognition association face several methodological challenges. First, the eCRF equations were originally developed in predominantly White, Western populations and may have limited cross‐cultural validity in non‐Western cohorts. 12 , 15 Second, and more critically, the physical activity component of eCRF, that is, moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA), does not distinguish between occupational labor and leisure‐time exercise. 16 In China, a substantial proportion of MVPA may derive from obligatory physical work, whereas in the United States, MVPA more typically reflects voluntary leisure activity. 17 , 18 This distinction is clinically meaningful, as occupational and leisure‐time physical activities may have divergent, or even opposing, associations with cognitive health. 19

China, as the nation with the world's largest elderly population, may demonstrate cognitive aging trajectories distinct from those observed in Western populations. 20 Investigation into the link between CRF and cognition among Chinese older adults remains relatively scarce, with most studies limited by cross‐sectional designs and a lack of systematic assessment of long‐term cognitive change. 21 , 22 Furthermore, measuring cardiorespiratory fitness typically requires maximal exercise testing, which is difficult to implement in large‐scale epidemiological studies.

Although previous studies have examined the association between CRF and cognition, most have relied on a single cognitive measure or short‐term follow‐up, making it difficult to capture the dynamic trajectory of cognitive function over time. To date, no studies have directly compared the relationship between eCRF and long‐term cognitive trajectories in China and the United States. Given the significant differences between the two countries in terms of lifestyle, physical activity patterns, the spectrum of chronic diseases, and social environments, 17 , 18 , 23 the association between eCRF and cognitive trajectories may exhibit cross‐national heterogeneity. Identifying this heterogeneity and its potential moderating factors is of great significance for guiding the development of cognitive health promotion strategies tailored to different populations.

Therefore, leveraging two large, nationally representative, and methodologically comparable longitudinal studies—the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS)—this study aims to: (1) identify distinct long‐term cognitive trajectories among middle‐aged and older adults in China and the United States, respectively, and (2) examine and directly compare the association between eCRF and membership in these cognitive trajectories. The findings will provide preliminary descriptive evidence to inform the tailoring of fitness‐based interventions for cognitive health promotion across different cultural and national contexts.

METHODS

Study design and population

This longitudinal study utilized data from two nationally representative cohort studies: the CHARLS and the HRS. Both studies are part of the Gateway to Global Aging Data harmonization initiative, enabling cross‑national comparisons. 24 For CHARLS, we used data from waves 1 to 5 (2011–2020). 25 For HRS, we used data from waves 11 to 15 (2012–2021). 26 Participants were included if they were aged ≥45 years or older at baseline and had complete data on eCRF components. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prevalent dementia or missing dementia data at baseline; (2) missing data on eCRF; and (3) fewer than two waves of cognitive assessments. After exclusions, the final analytic sample comprised 3,458 participants from CHARLS and 6,905 participants from HRS. A detailed flowchart is provided in Figure S1. All participants provided written informed consent, and the respective institutional review boards approved each study. 25 , 26

Assessment of eCRF

Baseline eCRF was estimated using sex‑specific non‑exercise algorithms previously validated in middle‑aged and older populations. 12 , 14 , 16 The algorithms incorporate age, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), resting heart rate (rHR), MVPA, and smoking status. For men, the formula was: eCRF (METs) = 21.2870 + (age × 0.1654) − (age2 × 0.0023) − (BMI × 0.2318) − (WC × 0.0337) − (rHR × 0.0390) + (MVPA × 0.6351) − (current smoking × 0.4263). For women, the formula was: eCRF (METs) = 14.7873 + (age × 0.1159) − (age2 × 0.0017) − (BMI × 0.1534) − (WC × 0.0085) − (rHR × 0.0364) + (MVPA × 0.5987) − (current smoking × 0.2994). 12 , 16 MVPA was defined as self‑reported engagement in vigorous or moderate physical activity more than once per week (coded 1 = yes, 0 = no). The original questions and harmonization rules for physical activity measures in CHARLS and HRS are provided in Table S1. Current smoking was coded 1 for current smokers and 0 otherwise. Resting heart rate was measured by trained staff after a 5‑min seated rest. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. WC was measured at the umbilical level. In sensitivity analyses, we also categorized eCRF into sex‑ and age‑adjusted tertiles (low = Q1, moderate = Q2, high = Q3) following prior studies.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed the literature using PubMed and other traditional sources. Previous research has consistently linked higher cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) to better cognitive outcomes and reduced dementia risk in Western populations. However, evidence from non‑Western populations, particularly China, remained sparse and was largely limited to cross‑sectional designs. No prior study had directly compared the longitudinal association between estimated CRF (eCRF) and long‑term cognitive trajectories across China and the United States. Interpretation: Our findings reveal a marked cross‑national divergence: higher eCRF was associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories in US older adults but with poorer trajectories in their Chinese counterparts. This opposing pattern, corroborated by multiple sensitivity analyses, challenges the prevailing assumption that CRF universally protects cognitive health. Our findings suggest that the cognitive implications of cardiorespiratory fitness may be modulated by contextual factors such as the source and nature of physical activity, chronic disease burden, and sociocultural environment. However, given the observational and measurement‐driven nature of this study, these findings remain hypothesis‐generating and require further validation to distinguish measurement artifacts from true biological differences. Future directions: Future research should: (a) validate and calibrate eCRF equations in diverse non‑Western populations to ensure cross‑cultural measurement equivalence; (b) employ objective measures of physical activity to disentangle the differential cognitive effects of leisure‑time aerobic exercise versus occupational physical labor; and (c) apply causal inference approaches to clarify the directionality of the eCRF–cognition relationship across different cultural contexts.

The eCRF algorithm was originally developed and validated in the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (ACLS), a predominantly White, community‐based US cohort, using directly measured CRF from treadmill exercise tests as the dependent variable. The unit of eCRF is metabolic equivalents of task (METs). Although the algorithm was derived from a Western population, it has been increasingly applied in non‐Western cohorts, including CHARLS, with demonstrated predictive utility for associations with cardiovascular disease, hypertension and all‐cause mortality, incident diabetes, circadian syndrome, incident frailty, and arthritis. 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the cross‐cultural validity of this algorithm in Chinese older adults has not been formally established against gold‐standard cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and systematic estimation bias cannot be excluded. Therefore, eCRF in this study should be strictly interpreted as an algorithmic health index rather than a definitive measure of physiological cardiorespiratory fitness.

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed at each wave using study‑specific batteries, which were harmonized to enable cross‑cohort comparison. In CHARLS, global cognition was measured using a Chinese adaptation of the Mini‑Mental State Examination (MMSE). 33 Two domains were assessed: episodic memory (immediate and delayed recall of 10 unrelated words, score range 0–20, sum of both trials) and mental intactness (serial subtraction of 7 from 100 up to 5 trials, orientation to date, day of the week, season, and an intersecting pentagon copying test, score range 0–11). The total global cognitive score was the sum of episodic memory and mental intactness (range 0–31). In HRS, cognitive function was assessed using the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS) adapted for in‑person interviews. 34 The HRS cognitive battery included episodic memory (immediate and delayed recall of 10 common words, score range 0–20, sum of both trials) and mental intactness (the serial seven test and the counting‐backward test). The total global cognitive score was the sum of these components (range 0–27). To make scores comparable across CHARLS and HRS, we standardized global cognitive scores within each cohort at baseline (z‑score, mean 0, SD 1) and applied the same baseline mean and SD to standardize follow‑up scores, following the harmonization approach used in multi‑cohort studies. 16 , 23

Outcome‐cognitive trajectories

The primary outcome was the longitudinal trajectory of global cognitive function. We used latent class growth modeling (LCGM) to identify distinct cognitive trajectory patterns separately for CHARLS and HRS. 33 , 35 Age was used as the time scale. Models with 2 to 5 trajectory classes were tested. The optimal number of trajectories was selected based on the following criteria: (1) lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) or sample‑size adjusted BIC (SABIC); (2) average posterior probability (AvePP) of assignment > 0.7 for each class; and (3) each class comprising at least 5% of the sample. The identified trajectory group membership was used as the categorical outcome in subsequent regression analyses. Importantly, the identified trajectories represent cohort‐specific relative cognitive performance. The labels “persistently high,” “moderate,” and “low” reflect relative ordering of cognitive function within each cohort and do not imply equivalence of absolute cognitive levels across cohorts.

Covariates

All covariates were measured at baseline. Harmonized definitions were applied across CHARLS and HRS to ensure comparability. 16 , 23 Demographic characteristics included age, sex (male/female), marital status (married/other), educational level (below primary school/secondary to vocational school/college or above), and residence (urban/rural). Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status (Ever/Never), drinking status (Yes/No), and social activity (Yes/No). Social activity was defined as self‑reported participation at least once per month in activities such as voluntary work, educational or training courses, sports or social clubs, or community‑related organization. 23 Chronic conditions were based on self‐reported physician diagnosis of hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and heart disease (including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure); dyslipidemia was defined by self‑report in CHARLS and by biomarker criteria in HRS. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‑D): the 10‑item version (CESD‑10) in CHARLS (cutoff ≥10) and the 8‑item version (CESD‑8) in HRS (cutoff ≥3). Functional and sensory status included activities of daily living (ADL) disability (defined as difficulty in any of five to six basic ADL items: bathing, dressing, eating, transferring, toileting, and walking across a room), 23 visual impairment (self‑reported yes/no), and hearing impairment (self‑reported yes/no). A comprehensive table of all covariates with original variable names and coding in each cohort is provided in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized as mean (standard deviation [SD]) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables, stratified by cohort (CHARLS vs. HRS). Differences between the two cohorts were compared using independent‑samples t‑tests for continuous variables and chi‑square tests for categorical variables.

Identification of cognitive trajectories

Longitudinal cognitive trajectories were identified using LCGM with age as the time scale. We tested models with two to five trajectory classes, employing linear functions. The optimal number of trajectories was selected based on: (1) the lowest BIC; (2) an AvePP of assignment > 0.7 for each class; and (3) each class comprising at least 5% of the sample. 35

Association of eCRF with cognitive trajectories

We used multinomial logistic regression to examine the association between baseline eCRF and cognitive trajectory membership, with the “persistently high” trajectory as the reference category. eCRF was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per 1 increase) and as a categorical variable (tertiles: low, moderate, high, with the lowest tertile as reference). Models were adjusted for all covariates listed in the Covariates section. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To formally test whether the association between eCRF and cognitive trajectories differed between cohorts, we performed a pooled analysis with an eCRF × country interaction term using multinomial logistic regression, adjusting for all covariates. A significant interaction (p < 0.05) would indicate cross‐national heterogeneity.

To explore potential non‑linear dose‑response relationships, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots (placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of eCRF) within a multinomial logistic regression framework using the VGAM package. The RCS curves were plotted for the comparisons of “persistently low vs. high” and “persistently moderate vs. high” trajectories, with the OR referenced to the median eCRF value. Non‑linearity was formally tested by comparing a model containing only the linear term of eCRF with a model including the RCS terms using a likelihood ratio test; a significant test (p < 0.05) indicated a non‑linear relationship.

Complementary analysis: eCRF and continuous cognitive change

To complement the primary trajectory analysis, we fitted linear mixed‐effects (LME) models to directly examine whether baseline eCRF predicts the subsequent rate of cognitive change. Crucially, to avoid the conflation of within‐person and between‐person effects, we used time since baseline as the within‐person time scale, and adjusted separately for baseline age (centered) as a between‐person covariate. The models included fixed effects for Time, eCRF (continuous), their two‐way interaction (eCRF × Time), baseline age (centered), and all baseline covariates. Random intercepts and random slopes for Time were included at the individual level to account for within‐subject correlation and inter‐individual heterogeneity in cognitive change. The model was specified as: Cognitionij=β0+β1Timeij+β2eCRFi+β3Timeij×eCRFi+γXi+u0i+u1iTimeij+εij where u0i and u1i are random intercept and random slope for Time, respectively, and εij is the residual error.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

We performed subgroup analyses to examine whether the association between eCRF (continuous) and the “persistently low vs. High” cognitive trajectory differed across strata of the following baseline variables: age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex, marital status, educational level, residence, drinking status, smoking status, social activity, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, stroke, heart disease, depression, visual impairment, hearing impairment, ADL disability, and physical activity level (MVPA vs. others). For each subgroup, the same multinomial logistic regression model was refitted, and the OR for eCRF was extracted. To formally test for effect modification, multiplicative interaction terms (eCRF × each stratifying variable) were introduced into the fully adjusted model, and the significance was assessed using likelihood ratio tests. These analyses are exploratory in nature and were conducted to generate hypotheses regarding potential effect modifiers, rather than to provide confirmatory evidence. Given the exploratory purpose, we did not apply formal multiple testing correction; however, all interaction p‐values are reported, and findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Sensitivity analyses

To comprehensively assess the robustness of our findings and to directly address methodological concerns, we conducted a series of post hoc sensitivity analyses. Addressing potential bias and confounding: We first investigated reverse causation by regressing eCRF at wave 2 on baseline cognitive score. We also excluded participants with low baseline cognitive scores (1.5 SD below the age‐stratified mean), excluded the first wave of assessments, and excluded participants with rapid early cognitive decline (> 1.5 SD between waves 1 and 2). In these three exclusion analyses, we re‐ran the latent class growth models and multinomial logistic regressions to re‐estimate the associations. To address residual confounding, we additionally adjusted for household income and retirement status, repeated the models with adjustment for baseline cognitive score, and restricted both cohorts to the most recent common wave (Wave 4, 2018). To specifically account for selective attrition and survival bias, we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) to the multinomial logistic regression models.Addressing exposure and outcome measurement: We examined the non‐comparability of the physical activity component by modeling MVPA as a standalone predictor, applying strict (> 1 time/week) and lenient (≥1 time/week) thresholds, excluding agricultural workers in CHARLS, and reconstructing eCRF without the physical activity component using the Sloan equation. 36 We then exploratorily decomposed eCRF into its individual components (age, BMI, waist circumference, resting heart rate, MVPA, and smoking) entered simultaneously into the models. Given the inherent collinearity and structural overlap among components, this exploratory component‐level analysis does not constitute a formal decomposition of the composite eCRF effect. To address measurement differences, we re‐identified trajectories using a memory domain‐specific score and a harmonized core cognitive score identical across cohorts, and repeated the primary analyses using raw cognitive scores. We also calculated standardized odds ratios per 1‐SD increase in eCRF (CHARLS: 1.99 METs; HRS: 2.05 METs).Addressing statistical modeling robustness: To account for potential bias from classification uncertainty, we first used a three‐step approach that incorporates classification error and re‐identified trajectories using two‐class and four‐class solutions. Furthermore, to evaluate whether our continuous cognitive change findings were dependent on the choice of time scale, we re‐fitted the LME models described in the main text using chronological age instead of time since baseline. This alternative specification tested the eCRF × age interaction and included random intercepts and random slopes for age, alongside identical covariate adjustments. To further address model specification concerns for this model, we additionally adjusted for birth year and baseline cognition, applied IPW for informative dropout and survival bias, and visualized predicted scores using three‐dimensional surface plots.

All statistical analyses were conducted separately for CHARLS and HRS, given the cross‑national design. A two‑sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) with packages including LCMM, NNET, VGAM, RMS, LME4, and ggplot2.

Although CHARLS and HRS employ complex survey designs, baseline survey sampling weights were not applied in the primary analyses. Because our objective was to evaluate analytical associations rather than descriptive population parameters, we utilized unweighted multivariable models adjusted for the key socio‐demographic variables that inform the survey design (e.g., age, sex, education, and residence), which effectively accounts for sampling design bias. Furthermore, rather than using cross‐sectional baseline weights, we applied IPW to specifically account for longitudinal attrition and survival biases over the follow‐up period.

Estimated cognitive aging trajectories

We identified distinct cognitive trajectories using latent class growth modelling separately for CHARLS and HRS. Based on the BIC, AvePP, and a minimum class proportion of 5%, the three‑class solution was selected as optimal for both cohorts (CHARLS: BIC = 58,531.7, AvePP range 0.87–0.94; HRS: BIC = 155,467.1, AvePP range 0.78–0.92; detailed fit statistics are shown in Tables S7 and S8). The three trajectories were labeled as “persistently high”, “persistently moderate”, and “persistently low” cognitive function (Figure 1). In CHARLS, the proportions of participants in the persistently high, moderate, and low trajectories were 42.3% (n = 1,466), 44.0% (n = 1,520), and 13.7% (n = 472), respectively. The corresponding proportions in HRS were 33.8% (n = 2,331), 48.3% (n = 3,332), and 17.9% (n = 1,242) (Table S9). As shown in Figure 1, the three trajectories remained consistently ordered across age, with the persistently high group maintaining the highest cognitive scores and the persistently low group the lowest throughout the follow‑up period.

Associations between eCRF and cognitive trajectories

Figure 2 presents the associations between baseline eCRF and cognitive trajectory membership from multinomial logistic regression models, with the “persistently high” trajectory as the reference. In CHARLS, each 1‐MET increase in eCRF was associated with higher odds of belonging to the “persistently low” trajectory (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.16–1.43) and the “moderate” trajectory (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.18) after full adjustment (Model 3). Compared with the lowest eCRF tertile, the highest tertile was associated with significantly increased odds of both the low trajectory (OR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.45–4.01) and the moderate trajectory (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.04–1.94) in CHARLS. In contrast, HRS showed a protective pattern: each 1‐MET increase in eCRF was associated with lower odds of the moderate trajectory (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98), while the association with the low trajectory was attenuated to non‑significance after full adjustment (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.94–1.05). The highest eCRF tertile in HRS was associated with reduced odds of the moderate trajectory (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.95) but not significantly for the low trajectory (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.71–1.20). To formally test cross‐national heterogeneity, we included an eCRF × country interaction term in a pooled multinomial logistic regression model. The interaction was significant for the low vs. high trajectory comparison (OR = 0.925, 95% CI: 0.861–0.993, p = 0.031), confirming that the detrimental association in CHARLS differed significantly from that in HRS. For the moderate vs. high trajectory, the interaction was not significant (OR = 0.968, 95% CI: 0.921–1.018, p = 0.203).

Complementary analysis: eCRF and rate of cognitive change

As a supplementary analysis, we fitted LME models to directly test whether baseline eCRF predicts the subsequent rate of cognitive change (Figure 4). In CHARLS, the eCRF × Time interaction was negative and significant (β = −0.0064, p < 0.001), indicating that higher baseline eCRF was associated with a faster rate of cognitive decline over time. In contrast, in HRS, the eCRF × Time interaction was positive and significant (β = 0.0049, p < 0.001), indicating that higher baseline eCRF attenuated the rate of cognitive decline. These results were consistent with the primary LCGM trajectory findings.

In HRS, significant effect modifications were observed for physical activity and smoking status in the “low vs. High” cognitive trajectory comparison (both p for interaction < 0.05). Among physically inactive participants (“Others”), higher eCRF was unexpectedly associated with an increased risk of the low cognitive trajectory (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.05–1.39), whereas no association was found among those engaging in MVPA (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.92–1.05). For smoking status, smokers showed a non‑significant trend toward higher risk (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.94–1.23), while non‑smokers had a neutral association (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.05). No other interaction reached statistical significance in HRS.

In CHARLS, education and residence significantly modified the eCRF‑cognition association. For the “low vs. High” trajectory, the interaction p values were 0.02 for education and 0.0009 for residence. The detrimental effect of higher eCRF was most pronounced among participants with high school education (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.24–2.73) and among urban residents (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.27–1.86). DISCUSSION

This study reveals a directional divergence in the association between eCRF and cognitive trajectories among Chinese and U.S. older adults. In the HRS cohort, higher eCRF was associated with lower odds of the moderate cognitive trajectory, consistent with the protective pattern in Western literature. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 37 In the CHARLS cohort, higher eCRF was unexpectedly associated with increased odds of both the low and moderate trajectories. This descriptive observation should be interpreted cautiously.

Reverse causation

Preclinical cognitive decline may reduce physical activity and alter metabolic parameters, thereby lowering eCRF. The “neuroselection” hypothesis suggests that associations between CRF and midlife cognition may originate from cognitive differences already present in childhood. 37 We conducted direct empirical tests of the reverse pathway, that is, regressing baseline cognition on subsequent eCRF, which yielded non‐significant results in both cohorts. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with low baseline cognition, excluding the first cognitive wave, and excluding those with rapid early decline consistently reproduced the primary association. These analyses do not provide strong support for reverse causation as the predominant explanation, although we cannot fully exclude residual confounding or health selection. However, we acknowledge that the alternative temporal sequence—whereby pre‐existing cognitive status or general health influences the components of the eCRF score—remains a theoretically plausible pathway in a subset of older adults reflecting a “health selection” effect rather than a protective effect of fitness on the brain.

Exposure non‐comparability

The eCRF algorithm was originally developed in the ACLS, a predominantly White, well‐educated, high‐socioeconomic‐status U.S. population, using CRF assessed from maximal treadmill exercise tests. 12 , 14 In contrast, CHARLS represents a Chinese population with substantially different anthropometric characteristics: lower BMI but higher body fat at the same BMI level, different resting heart rate distributions, and different physical activity patterns.

While these algorithms have demonstrated good agreement with measured CRF in their derivation cohorts 14 and have been increasingly applied in non‐Western cohorts, 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 their cross‐cultural validity in Chinese older adults has not been validated against gold‐standard cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Systematic bias in absolute eCRF estimation cannot be excluded; if the algorithm systematically over‐ or underestimates true CRF, spurious associations could arise. Nevertheless, multiple independent studies using the identical algorithm in CHARLS have demonstrated consistent, dose–response associations with many health outcomes. 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 We acknowledge, however, that this reflects predictive utility rather than construct validity as a measure of CRF.

Cognitive measurement differences

CHARLS and HRS use different cognitive assessment instruments: a Chinese adaptation of the MMSE in CHARLS (range 0–31) 25 and a modified TICS in HRS (range 0–27). 26 To address scale range differences, we applied within‐cohort Z‐score standardization. A more fundamental concern is whether the two instruments measure the same underlying cognitive construct. Both batteries assess episodic memory and executive function—the two cognitive domains most closely tied to hippocampal and prefrontal structural integrity, and the strongest cognitive predictors of dementia progression. 38 , 39 , 40 Sensitivity analyses using cognitive scores identical across both cohorts yielded results consistent with the primary findings, showing that the cross‐national divergence may be not an artifact of different cognitive measurement instruments.

Selection bias and survival bias

In longitudinal aging studies, selection bias and survival bias are important considerations. Excluded participants in both cohorts were older, had lower eCRF, lower education, and greater functional limitations, indicating selective attrition. The exclusion of deceased participants may introduce survival bias because individuals with higher CRF tend to live longer and, therefore, have more opportunity to experience cognitive decline, potentially attenuating protective effects. In sensitivity analysis, the CHARLS reverse association remained significant, while the HRS protective association remained robust, indicating that selection bias did not materially alter our results.

The critical role of physical activity purpose: Occupational versus leisure activity

The most critical factor in understanding the CHARLS paradox lies in the physical activity component of the eCRF formula. MVPA is measured identically in both cohorts but may capture fundamentally different behaviors. In HRS, MVPA predominantly reflects voluntary, structured leisure‐time exercise, typically performed at moderate intensity with adequate recovery. 41 , 42 In CHARLS, however, MVPA conflates leisure activity with obligatory physical labor. 17 , 18 , 43

This distinction is not merely semantic. Recent evidence from the CHARLS cohort demonstrates that the health effects of physical activity depend fundamentally on its purpose. 19 Zhao found that job‐related vigorous physical activity was significantly associated with cognitive decline, whereas leisure‐related moderate physical activity was linked to reduced cognitive decline. 19 These opposing associations suggest that the neurobiological effects of physical activity are conditional on the context in which it occurs.

Several mechanisms may explain why obligatory physical labor fails to confer the same cognitive benefits as leisure‐time exercise. Occupational physical activity is often performed under time pressure with low autonomy, activating stress pathways—elevated cortisol, sympathetic overactivity, and chronic inflammation—that counteract neuroprotection. 19 , 44 , 45 Moreover, repetitive high‐intensity movements without adequate recovery lead to cumulative fatigue, musculoskeletal strain, and oxidative stress that may impair brain health. 46 , 47 , 48 In rural Chinese populations, occupational activity often occurs in environments with high air pollution exposure, independently linked to cognitive decline. 49 , 50 , 51 Finally, the socioeconomic patterning of occupational activity—lower education, lower income, limited healthcare access—may amplify these negative effects through synergistic pathways. 20

Component‐level analysis suggested that although BMI was protective, its negative coefficient may mathematically contribute to the paradoxical composite association. Conversely, MVPA's positive coefficient may inject the unfavorable trend of obligatory occupational labor into the eCRF score. Given inherent collinearity, this interpretation remains hypothesis‐generating.

Socioeconomic and contextual factors

Beyond the MVPA measurement issue, socioeconomic factors may further amplify the observed divergence. In CHARLS sample, 64.3% of participants had primary school education or less, a powerful risk factor for cognitive decline that may mask protective effects of high eCRF. 20 Subgroup analyses revealed that education significantly modified the eCRF‐cognition association, with the detrimental effect more pronounced among higher‐educated participants. This finding likely reflects that higher‐educated individuals in China are more likely to engage in sedentary mental work, with their “high eCRF” derived from commuting‐related walking rather than health‐promoting leisure exercise. Urban residence similarly modified the association, with urban residents experiencing greater harm from high eCRF, possibly because urban physical activity in China is often limited to non‐leisure activities such as transportation. 52 , 53

These findings resonate with a broader pattern documented by Wu, who found that cardiometabolic risk was associated with cognition in the U.S. but not in China. 54 The cardiovascular health–brain health pathway itself appears shaped by social, economic, and medical contexts that differ between the two countries. In China, rapid urbanization and economic development have created a unique pattern where higher socioeconomic status does not necessarily translate to better cardiovascular health and may even be associated with higher risk due to dietary transitions, reduced physical activity, and environmental exposures. 54 , 55

Comparison with prior literature

Our HRS findings align with the predominant Western literature. The HUNT study reported that each 1‐MET increase in eCRF was associated with a 16% reduction in incident dementia risk. 4 The BLSA found that higher baseline VO2max was associated with less accelerated memory decline, 7 and the IGNITE study reported positive associations between CRF and performance across multiple cognitive domains. 5 These studies, conducted in populations where MVPA predominantly reflects leisure‐time activity, support the neuroprotective role of voluntary exercise.

The CHARLS finding is not without precedent. He et al. recently reported a finding using the same CHARLS cohort: higher eCRF was protective against depressive symptoms in HRS and ELSA, but increased risk in CHARLS. 16 The convergence of this “reversed” association across two distinct health outcomes in the same cohort may suggest that the unexpected directionality reflects population‐specific characteristics of the CHARLS sample rather than statistical anomaly. Pinto Pereira similarly found that associations between CRF and cognition disappeared after adjustment for confounders, highlighting the importance of socioeconomic and behavioral factors. 56

Clinical and public health implications

The implications of our findings must be tempered by the descriptive nature of this observation. While the U.S. data reinforce the established public health message of promoting leisure‐time exercise for cognitive health, the Chinese data do not suggest that improving CRF is harmful. Rather, they suggest that further investigation is needed to determine whether shifting focus from merely increasing physical activity quantity to improving its quality—distinguishing obligatory physical labor from voluntary, structured exercise—may be warranted in public health recommendations. In China, where many older adults remain in physically demanding occupations, public health strategies could prioritize promoting leisure‐time physical activity and reducing occupational physical burdens where feasible.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the eCRF algorithm was developed in Western populations and has not been formally validated against gold‐standard cardiopulmonary exercise testing in Chinese older adults. Second, the MVPA measure conflates occupational and leisure‐time activity, limiting our ability to disentangle their distinct effects. Third, despite extensive sensitivity analyses, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (diet, sleep, genetics, air pollution) cannot be excluded. Fourth, the exclusion of deceased participants may introduce survival bias. Fifth, while sensitivity analyses do not provide strong evidence for reverse causation as the primary driver, the “neuroselection” hypothesis suggests that childhood cognitive ability may predict adult fitness, a form of selection bias that our adult‐only data cannot fully address.

CONCLUSION

This descriptive cross‐national study provides robust evidence of a directional divergence in the association between eCRF and long‐term cognitive trajectories. Higher eCRF was associated with better cognitive outcomes in U.S. older adults, whereas it was unexpectedly linked to poorer cognitive trajectories in their Chinese counterparts. These findings remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses and were supported by a statistically significant formal test for cross‐national heterogeneity.

While the divergent directions of these associations were consistently observed within our analytical framework, the underlying mechanisms remain undetermined. Given the lack of direct CRF measurement and objective differentiation of activity types, the current data cannot definitively distinguish whether this divergence reflects measurement bias and residual confounding (particularly the conflation of obligatory physical labor with voluntary leisure‐time exercise) or true biological heterogeneity. Therefore, rather than asserting a true biological harm of eCRF, our results highlight a critical descriptive observation requiring future validation using objective physical activity measures and population‐specific eCRF calibration.
