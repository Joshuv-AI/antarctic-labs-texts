Associations between perioperative lung ultrasound score and postoperative pulmonary complications in patients with skull base tumor resection: a retrospective single-center cohort study
Authors: Haojie Yu, Jie Wang, Wenjie Huang, Liyong Zhang, Min Zeng, Zhen Wu, Yuming Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are common after major surgery and may be particularly frequent after skull base neurosurgery. This study aimed to investigate the incidence of PPCs following skull base tumor resection and the relationship between lung ultrasound scores (LUS) and PPCs in these patients.MethodsThis is a retrospective, single-center, cohort study. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected from the Hospital Information System. Perioperative lung ultrasound was routinely performed in patients undergoing skull base tumor resection. Only those with complete preoperative and postoperative lung ultrasound data were included. The primary outcome was the incidence of the composite endpoints of PPCs, including pneumonia, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax and bronchospasm. Associations were reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsBetween March 2022 and June 2024, a total of 140 consecutive patients who underwent elective skull base tumor resection were screened, and 129 were ultimately included in this study. Among them, 40 (31%) patients experienced PPCs within 7 days after surgery. We found that postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 (adjusted OR 2.59, 95% CI: 1.14–5.89) and delayed extubation (adjusted OR 4.68, 95% CI: 1.76–12.46) were identified as independent risk factors for PPCs with an area under the curve of 0.727 (95% confidence interval: 0.635 to 0.819).ConclusionThe incidence of pulmonary complications after skull base tumor resection in adults is as high as 31%. We found that postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 and delayed extubation were identified as independent risk factors for PPCs after skull base tumor resection. These findings are hypothesis-generating and exploratory, requiring prospective validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03938-1.

Introduction

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are the most common complications and lead to increased mortality and morbidity, seriously affecting the prognosis of patients [1]. The incidence of PPCs varies significantly among studies, depends on the clinical status of the patient, the type of surgery and its complexity [2]. Studies have shown that the incidence of PPCs in patients after neurosurgery is about 25%−40% [3, 4]. The pathophysiological alterations associated with neurosurgery may lead to a high incidence of pulmonary complications [5].

Early identification and effective intervention is important for improving postoperative outcomes and reducing the related complications. Unlike static imaging modalities such as chest X-ray or CT, LUS provides dynamic real-time assessment, which is essential for modern intensive care monitoring. As highlighted in a recent comprehensive review by Vetrugno et al. [6], LUS has evolved over the past 30 years to become a cornerstone of critical care, effectively rivaling the supine chest X-ray for acute respiratory failure assessment.

A recent study demonstrated the potential of LUS in detecting a variety of pulmonary complications such as atelectasis, pleural effusion, and pneumonia, which were common after neurosurgical procedures [7]. However, the predictive ability of LUS in patients with high risk for pulmonary complications such as skull base neurosurgery has not been reported. The unique pathophysiological challenges in this specific population may affect the typical patterns and predictive significance of LUS results when compared to other surgical cohorts. Therefore, this study aims to explore the role of LUS in predicting PPCs specifically in patients undergoing skull base tumor resection.

Materials and methods

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study and was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB). Adult patients who underwent skull base tumor resection and had complete LUS data were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with pulmonary space-occupying lesions or thoracic deformities, (2) patients with cardiac insufficiency or pulmonary embolism during the perioperative period, (3) history of previous cranial neurosurgery or other neurological diseases (vascular malformation, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and primary epilepsy), (4) patients discharged or underwent secondary surgery during follow-up, (5) patients who refused to participate, and (6) patients with incomplete clinical data. All procedures involving human participants in this study were performed in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Anesthesia protocol

Anesthesia induction and tracheal intubation were conducted using propofol, sufentanil, and a neuromuscular blocking agent (rocuronium or cisatracurium). An arterial catheter was placed after the induction of general anesthesia. Other aspects of anesthetic induction and maintenance were managed at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist. All patients received mechanical ventilation. Volume-controlled ventilation was employed with the following parameters: Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) was set at 50%, a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight, a respiratory rate of 12 breaths/min (adjusted to maintain an end-tidal carbon dioxide level between 30 and 35 mmHg), and an I:E ratio of 1:2. The attending anesthesiologist was permitted to adjust ventilator settings as needed.

Exposure parameters

The main exposure in this retrospective cohort study was the LUS scores assessed at 20 min after anesthesia induction and at the end of the surgery. In the original study design, the analysis plan prespecified the evaluation of regional lung ultrasound (LUS) scores, with a primary focus on the inferior lung zones, based on an a priori anatomical hypothesis regarding the susceptibility of dependent lung regions to postoperative pathology. Lung ultrasonography was performed before and after surgery using a linear probe. The chest was divided into two hemithoraces by the parasternal line. Each chest side was divided into 6 areas: the area from the paracsternal line to the anterior axillary line is the anterior chest area, the area from the anterior axillary line to the posterior axillary line is the lateral chest area, and the area posterior to the posterior axillary line is the posterior chest area [8, 9]. Each area is divided into two halves, totaling 12 areas (Fig. 1). Ultrasound was performed in each area. According to ultrasonographic appearance, a score (the worst grade observed in each region) was assigned to each area as follows: 0 points for A-lines or ≤ 2 isolated B-lines, 1 point for ≥ 3 well-spaced B-lines, 2 points for coalescent B-lines (Fig. 2), 3 points for coalescent B-lines with subpleural consolidation. The final LUS score was calculated as the sum of scores (range: 0–36 points).

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of a composite endpoint of PPCs occurring within 7 days after skull base tumor resection. PPCs were considered to have occurred if at least one postoperative pulmonary event was observed, such as pneumonia, pleural effusion, respiratory failure, pneumothorax, atelectasis of the lung, and bronchospasm, according to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome criteria (EPCO) [10, 11]. Respiratory physicians diagnose pulmonary complications based on imaging results, clinical manifestations, and physical and laboratory examinations. All patients were charted 7 days after surgery to confirm the occurrence of PPCs.

The secondary outcome was the severity of the PPCs according to the Clavien-Dindo classification [12], the onset time of the PPCs, length of hospital stay and socioeconomic costs.

Data collection and processing

Baseline data were collected, including demographics, diagnoses, preoperative comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, and hydrothorax) and medication, drinking and smoking history, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, the Charlson comorbidity index and information on tumor characteristics. Intraoperative data included blood pressure upon entering and leaving the operating room, operation type, intraoperative medication, duration of surgery and anesthesia, fluid balance and blood transfusion, perioperative arterial blood gases, and LUS score. An independent anesthesiologist comprehensively determined the occurrence of PPCs within 7 days after surgery by integrating relevant medical record information.

Sample size estimation and statistical analysis

Our primary aim was to generate hypotheses and provide preliminary data on the association between LUS and PPCs in this specific surgical population. Therefore, we did not perform a formal a priori sample size calculation. Instead, all consecutive eligible patients during the study period were retrospectively included to ensure a representative sample. A post hoc power analysis was subsequently conducted using G. Power software, which indicated that our final sample size provided adequate power (0.87, effect size = 0.3, α = 0.05, df = 2) to detect significant differences between groups.

Clinical characteristics were compared between participants with and without PPCs using χ2, Fisher exact or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Variables with statistical significance in the univariate binary logistic regression were incorporated into the multivariate regression analysis. The results are presented as odds ratios (OR) or adjusted OR with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The correlation between the LUS and the severity and the onset time of PPCs were analyzed using Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Collinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and variables with VIF > 3 were excluded. The ROC analysis was performed and derived diagnostic accuracy parameters were calculated with their 95% CIs. To ensure a more robust variable selection process, we have incorporated LASSO logistic regression with tenfold cross-validation to identify the most relevant predictors while minimizing the risk of overfitting. Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 25 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and R 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025), and a 2-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 129 adult patients who underwent elective skull base tumor resection between March 2022 to June 2024 in Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University were included in the study (Fig. 3). The incidence of PPCs after elective skull base tumor resection was 31%. Establishment of the regression model

The variables with statistical differences in univariate regression analysis were included in multivariate regression analysis. The results showed that postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 (adjusted OR = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.14–5.89, p = 0.023) and retaining tracheal intubation at the end of surgery (adjusted OR = 4.68, 95% CI: 1.76 −12.46, p = 0.002) were risk factors for PPCs (Table 4). To address the risk of overfitting and improve model stability, we employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression for variable selection. Based on tenfold cross-validation, two independent predictors were identified for the final multivariate model. The results showed that a postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 (adjusted OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.03–5.56, p = 0.045) and delayed extubation (adjusted OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 1.71–12.75, p = 0.003) were risk factors for PPCs (Table S4).The model composed of the above two risk factors can predict PPCs occurred within 7 days after skull base tumor resection with the area under the curve (AUC) of 0.727 and the 95% confidence interval of 0.635 to 0.819 (Fig. 4). Pneumonia and respiratory failure, which are components of the composite endpoint of pulmonary complications, were analyzed separately. Postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 was risk factor for pneumonia (OR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.06–6.76, p = 0.037), rather than for respiratory failure (OR = 2.58, 95% CI: 0.84–7.91, p = 0.097) (Table S5). Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between postoperative LUS scores and the severity of PPCs using the Clavien-Dindo classification. Discussion

Studies have shown that the incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing intracranial surgery is approximately 25% to 40% [3, 13]. The skull base region has a complex structure. The brainstem serves as the respiratory center, and cranial nerves critical to airway and lung function traverse this region. Injuries to the medulla oblongata, the lower cranial nerves (LCN), or the phrenic nerve, as well as postoperative brain tissue edema may aggravate the already fragile airway and lung conditions. Respiration is regulated through close coordination between the respiratory system and the nervous system [14]. Thus patients undergoing brainstem surgery are most commonly affected by PPCs [15]. In addition, prolonged operation, anesthesia, and mechanical ventilation times contribute to the high incidence of PPCs in neurosurgery. PPCs can prolong hospital stays and increase morbidity and mortality rates, particularly in patients undergoing brainstem surgery or posterior cranial fossa surgery [5].

Given the high incidence and clinical impact of PPCs, early detection is crucial. In this context, lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as a promising bedside tool [16]. A prospective study demonstrated that LUS could detect pleural effusions with a sensitivity of 92.9% and specificity of 96.0%, which is superior to traditional chest X‑rays [17, 18]. Previous studies showed that LUS is useful as a predictor of oxygen saturation during the immediate postoperative period and perioperative LUS is recommended as a useful tool to determine the oxygenation level during the immediate postoperative period [19].

This exploratory, hypothesis-generating study demonstrates the association between regional perioperative lung ultrasound findings and PPCs in patients undergoing skull base tumor resection. In contrast to studies that used total LUS scores, our study found that only the a postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 was an independent risk factor for PPCs, whereas the total LUS score did not show a significant correlation. This finding suggests that the pathophysiological changes in the dependent lung regions (such as dorsal or inferior areas due to surgical positioning or blood flow redistribution) play an important role in the development of PPCs after skull base surgery. This is consistent with previous physiological studies, which indicate that patient positioning during surgery leads to blood flow redistribution toward dependent lung regions, resulting in V/Q mismatch [20, 21]. Position changes are helpful for improving the V/Q imbalance. Our results refine these general findings by pinpointing that monitoring specifically the inferior lung area may be more efficient than whole-lung scanning in this neurosurgical population.

This finding provides a critical nuance to the recent debate on LUS utility. Vetrugno et al. recently argued that the association between high LUS and PPCs might be confounded by pleural effusions, which elevate the score without necessarily indicating severe respiratory compromise [22]. However, our results refine this perspective by demonstrating that not all LUS elevations are equal. Since gravity-dependent abnormalities, such as effusions or atelectasis, primarily accumulate in the inferior zones, focusing on this specific region helps distinguish clinically significant findings from benign ones. Thus, monitoring the inferior lung area may offer superior specificity compared to whole-lung scanning, effectively addressing the concern that total LUS merely reflects baseline lung conditions.

Our results further indicate that while a postoperative LUS score > 0 in the inferior lung area is a sensitive indicator for the presence of PPCs, it does not necessarily correlate with the clinical severity grade of these complications. This dissociation suggests that the mere presence of sonographic abnormalities reflects a general predisposition to pulmonary pathology but does not quantify the degree of physiological impairment. This finding aligns with the pathophysiological understanding that structural changes detected by ultrasound may precede or exist independently of the functional deterioration that defines severe clinical outcomes.

Higher LUS scores may reflect abnormal fluid accumulation or impaired ventilation in the lungs during or after surgery, potentially leading to PPCs. Other contributing factors include neurosurgical procedures involving significant changes in intracranial pressure and nerve reflexes, which can alter lung function due to limited respiratory capacity or lung tissue compression.

It is important to note that LUS was performed immediately after surgery in this study. While this timing allows for the early identification of PPCs, the observed ultrasound patterns may reflect transient, acute intraoperative physiological changes, such as resorption atelectasis or fluid overload, rather than indicating a definitive predictive signal for subsequent PPCs. Although these immediate postoperative findings may serve as precursors to clinical deterioration, they could also resolve spontaneously with standard postoperative care. Therefore, while early LUS provides valuable real-time information, its role as a standalone predictive tool may be confounded by surgical and anesthetic effects.

Beyond LUS findings, we also identified delayed extubation as an independent factor associated with PPCs. However, delayed extubation should be interpreted as a surrogate marker of baseline neurological severity, clinical stability, and perioperative decision-making, rather than a direct causal factor for PPCs. Patients requiring prolonged ventilation commonly exhibit more severe preoperative impairment, compromised airway protective reflexes, or greater neurosurgical risk, all of which independently predispose to PPCs. This finding aligns with the pathophysiological consequences of skull base surgery, particularly the inherent risk of LCN injury. Impairment of the glossopharyngeal (CN IX) and vagus (CN X) nerves impairs the swallowing reflex and airway protection, predisposing patients to occult or overt aspiration. Consequently, clinicians must weigh the necessity of postoperative mechanical ventilation against the heightened risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia and the potential for aspiration due to impaired bulbar function. The retained endotracheal tube may serve as a nidus for bacterial colonization and biofilm formation, leading to lower airway infection. Therefore, our data strongly support the implementation of rigorous perioperative airway management protocols for patients requiring intubation to mitigate the incidence of complications.

While the multivariate model achieved statistical significance, its moderate discriminative ability (AUC = 0.727) limits its utility for individual risk stratification and requires further refinement to improve predictive accuracy. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design and lack of a priori sample size calculation constrain the statistical power. Second, LUS assessments were restricted to two time points, potentially failing to capture dynamic pulmonary changes. Furthermore, we did not formally assess inter- or intra-observer variability, introducing potential measurement bias despite the use of trained operators. Third, the use of a composite PPC endpoint may obscure specific risk factors for distinct subtypes and introduce outcome heterogeneity, given that pneumonia, atelectasis, pleural effusion, and respiratory failure differ in pathophysiology and severity. Fourth, key intraoperative ventilatory variables, including PEEP, driving pressure, and recruitment maneuvers, were not systematically recorded. These well-established confounders for PPCs could not be adjusted for in our analyses. This omission is therefore an important methodological limitation that may affect the interpretation of the independent contribution of LUS findings. Although our hypothesis regarding regional vulnerability was anatomically guided, the risk of Type I error due to multiple regional comparisons cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the single‑center design limits the generalizability of our findings. Thus, future multicenter studies will be necessary to validate these results.

In summary, 31% of patients undergoing skull base tumor resections developed PPCs. A postoperative inferior lung zone LUS score > 0 may serve as an early screening indicator for identifying patients at high risk of PPCs, and individualized pulmonary management for those with delayed extubation could help reduce the incidence of these complications. These findings may provide preliminary insights into optimizing perioperative care strategies.
