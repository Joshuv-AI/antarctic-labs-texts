Cryptic exon activation caused by a novel deep‐intronic splice‐altering variant in Becker muscular dystrophy
Authors: Zhiying Xie, Yunlong Lu, Chang Liu, Chengyue Sun, Jiaxi Yu, Chen Ling, Xinghua Luan, Wei Wang, Li Wang, Yanhong Liang, Qianbo Luo, Lingchao Meng, Zhaoxia Wang, Yun Yuan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn accurate genetic diagnosis of Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) can be sometimes challenging due to deep intronic DMD variants. Here, we report on the genetic diagnosis of a BMD patient with a novel deep‐intronic splice‐altering variant in DMD.MethodsThe index case was a 3.8‐year‐old boy who was suspected of having a diagnosis of BMD based on his clinical, muscle imaging, and pathological features. Routine genomic detection approaches did not detect any disease‐causing variants in him. Muscle‐derived DMD mRNA studies, followed by genomic Sanger sequencing and in silico bioinformatic analyses, were performed in the patient.Results DMD mRNA studies detected a cryptic exon‐containing transcript and normally spliced DMD transcript in the patient. The cryptic exon‐containing transcript encoded a frameshift and premature termination codon (NP_003997.1:p.[=,Asp2740Valfs*52]). Further genomic Sanger sequencing and bioinformatic analysis identified a novel deep‐intronic splice‐altering variant in DMD (c.8217 + 23338A > G). The novel variant strengthened a cryptic donor splice site and activated a cryptic acceptor splice site in the deep‐intronic region of DMD intron 55, resulting in the activation of a new dystrophin cryptic exon found in the patient.ConclusionOur case report expands the genetic spectrum of BMD and highlights the essential role of deep‐intronic cryptic exon‐activating variants in genetically unsolved BMD patients.

INTRODUCTION

Dystrophinopathies, among the most common inherited muscle diseases, are caused by pathogenic loss‐of‐function variants in the DMD gene and include Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD; OMIM #300376), Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD; OMIM #310200), and the relatively rare allelic form X‐linked dilated cardiomyopathy (OMIM # 302045). 1 The DMD gene, as the largest gene annotated in the human genome, consists of 79 canonical exons and spans over 2.5‐Mb. 1 Approximately 99% of DMD is non‐coding intronic sequences, making the occurrence of deep‐intronic splice‐altering variants more prevalent than other monogenic disease genes. 2 Unlike the typical pathogenic DMD variants which mainly occur in DMD coding and/or flanking non‐coding regions and can be detected by routine genomic detection approaches for dystrophinopathies, atypical deep‐intronic DMD variants cannot be covered by the routine exome analysis of genomic DNA. 3 The accurate detection and genetic interpretation of deep‐intronic DMD variants requires the application of RNA‐ and DNA‐based sequencing techniques and computational analyses. 4 Due to recent improvements in DNA and RNA sequencing techniques and bioinformatic analyses, some previously unrevealed deep‐intronic DMD variants have been increasingly reported as a significant cause of DMD and BMD. 5

Here, we recruited a 3.8‐year‐old boy because he was suspected of having a molecular diagnosis of BMD based on his clinical, muscle imaging, and pathological findings. We performed routine genomic detection approaches consisting of multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA)‐based duplication/deletion analysis of DMD and a next‐generation sequencing panel for inherited neuromuscular diseases, 3 , 4 which failed to identify typical pathogenic variants in him. Therefore, we performed muscle‐derived DMD mRNA analysis of the patient and detected a cryptic exon‐containing transcript and normally spliced DMD transcript. Further genomic Sanger sequencing and bioinformatic analyses detected a novel deep‐intronic cryptic exon‐activating variant in the DMD gene, confirming the genetic diagnosis of BMD in the enrolled patient.

METHODS

Patient and muscle biopsy

A 3.8‐year‐old boy with an asymptomatic hyper‐creatine kinase‐emia phenotype was included and analyzed in this case report. His clinical features were ascertained by review of his medical records and a detailed neurological examination. Muscle MRI examination at the pelvis and thigh level was performed in the boy according to a standard protocol. 3 Muscle biopsy was obtained from the biceps brachii muscle in a normal control and the enrolled boy. Routine histological, histochemical, and immunohistochemical staining were performed as previously described. 4

Routine genomic detection approaches

MLPA‐analysis of DMD was performed in the enrolled boy to detect possible exonic copy number variants. 3 , 4 To reveal pathogenic single‐nucleotide variants, small deletions, small insertions, or small deletion–insertions, we performed a next‐generation sequencing panel 3 , 4 for inherited neuromuscular diseases in the enrolled patient.

Muscle‐derived DMD mRNA studies and genomic DMD sequencing

Total muscle RNA was extracted from the muscle biopsy sample and subsequently retrotranscribed to cDNA through reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) approach. 4 Whole‐length cDNA of DMD (GenBank accession number NM_004006.2) was amplified and Sanger sequenced in 22 overlapping fragments using a set of specific primers according to the previous protocols. 4 TA cloning 6 was performed when the direct Sanger sequencing of abnormal RT‐PCR products failed to identify various transcripts. Genomic Sanger sequencing around the sites from which abnormal DMD transcripts were produced was performed to identify possible genomic DMD variants.

In silico bioinformatic analysis

The genomic DMD variants, mRNA variants, and protein variants detected in the enrolled boy were described according to the Human Genome Variation Society nomenclature. 7 The genome similarity sequence search (genome build GRCh37/hg19) was performed using the Human BLAT Search tool. In silico analyses of variant and reference sequences of DMD were performed to detect potential splicing alterations using various algorithms including Human Splicing Finder (HSF), 8 Maximum Entropy Scan (MaxEntScan), 9 and SpliceAI. 10 Pathogenicity of the detected genomic DMD variants was determined according to the standard guidelines. 11 , 12

RESULTS

Clinical, muscle imaging, and pathological findings

The enrolled 3.8‐year‐old boy is the only son of the non‐consanguineous parents (Figure 1A). His serum creatine kinase level was markedly elevated in every test (range 4766–6463 IU/L; normal 25–195 IU/ L). No one else in his family had a hyper‐creatine kinase‐emia. He had neither exercise‐induced muscle cramps nor delayed motor milestones. The neurological examination confirmed that he had neither obvious muscle weakness nor calf hypertrophy. His muscle MRI examinations of the pelvis and thigh muscles revealed only a slight muscle fatty infiltration of the gluteus maximus muscle (Figure 1B,C). His muscle biopsy showed a muscular dystrophic pattern (Figure 1D). The immunohistochemical staining of dystrophin protein revealed a partial reduction of dystrophin‐N and dystrophin‐C and a slight reduction of dystrophin‐R with a few negative expression fibers (Figure 1E–G), suggesting a suspected molecular diagnosis of BMD in the enrolled patient.

Routine genomic detection approaches

We performed routine genomic detection approaches for dystrophinopathies 3 , 4 on the basis of his suspected diagnosis of BMD. However, the MLPA‐based duplication/deletion analysis of DMD did not find any exonic copy number variants, that is, duplications and/or deletions in DMD‐coding regions. Moreover, the next‐generation sequencing panel did not detect any causal variants in the enrolled patient.

Muscle‐derived DMD mRNA studies and genomic DMD sequencing

As no genetic diagnosis of the patient could be confirmed by the routine genomic detection approaches, muscle‐derived DMD mRNA analysis was conducted to detect potential abnormal transcripts of DMD. Gel electrophoresis analysis of the amplified 22 overlapping cDNA fragments of DMD indicated abnormal splicing transcripts in the eleventh cDNA fragment. Two different bands were shown in the further RT‐PCR analysis (Figure 2A): the upper band was longer than the expected band, while the lower faint band was almost the same size as the expected band (normally spliced DMD transcript). Direct Sanger sequencing of the abnormal RT‐PCR products could not recognize the overlapping sequences (Figure 2B). TA cloning of the abnormal RT‐PCR products revealed the normal splicing of DMD exons 55 to 56 (Figure 2C) in addition to an abnormal 80‐bp insertion sequence between DMD exons 55 and 56 (Figure 2D).

The genome similarity sequence search revealed that the abnormal 80‐bp insertion transcript was originating from the intronic sequence of DMD intron 55. Hence, the abnormal 80‐bp insertion transcript was genetically interpreted as a cryptic exon activation (NM_004006.2:r.[=,8217_8218ins8217 + 23258_8217 + 23337]). Real‐time quantitative PCR analysis of cryptic exon‐containing and normal DMD transcripts was performed (Appendix S1). The cryptic exon‐containing DMD transcript presented at levels of 65% of the amount of normal DMD transcript found in a normal control muscle (Figure S1). A significant level of normal DMD transcript was present as well (51% of that found in a normal control muscle). The abnormal 80‐bp insertion transcript was targeted for degradation by nonsense‐mediated decay, as it encoded a frameshift and premature termination codon occurring 52 codons downstream of exon 55 (NP_003997.1:p.[=,Asp2740Valfs*52]; Figure 2F). The residual level of normally spliced DMD transcript, concomitantly with the abnormal 80‐bp insertion transcript, resulted in the reduction of dystrophin expression observed in the enrolled boy (Figure 1E–G).

Genomic Sanger sequencing of the DMD sequence around the cryptic exon identified a novel single‐nucleotide variant in the deep‐intronic region of DMD intron 55, that is, the NM_004006.2:c.8217 + 23338A > G (NC_000023.10:g.31622452 T > C) variant, which was inherited from his unaffected mother (Figure 2E). This novel deep‐intronic variant c.8217 + 23338A > G has not been reported in the published literature, which is also absent from population and disease‐specific databases, including Genome Aggregation Database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), Leiden Open Variation Database (https://databases.lovd.nl/shared/variants/DMD/unique), and ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). The deep‐intronic variant c.8217 + 23338A > G strengthened a cryptic donor splice site (HSF score, reference sequence: 67.34, variant sequence: 94.48; MaxEnt score, reference sequence: 1.04, variant sequence: 9.22) in intron 55 of DMD (Figure 2F). In addition, the c.8217 + 23338A > G variant also activated a cryptic acceptor splice site (CTTCTCTTGAAG|GT with an HSF score of 87.33 and a MaxEnt score of 9.82) in the deep‐intronic region of DMD intron 55 (Figure 2F). SpliceAI also predicted Acceptor Gain for the cryptic acceptor splice site (80‐bp; 0.70) and Donor Gain for the strengthened cryptic donor splice site (1‐bp; 0.83) in the deep‐intronic region of DMD intron 55 caused by the c.8217 + 23338A > G variant. The enhancement of a cryptic donor splice site and activation of a cryptic acceptor splice site caused by the c.8217 + 23338A > G variant resulted in the activation of a new dystrophin cryptic exon found in our BMD patient. According to the standard guidelines for interpretation of sequence variants, 11 , 12 the novel deep‐intronic splice‐altering variant c.8217 + 23338A > G in DMD is classified as a pathogenic variant, which confirmed the genetic diagnosis of BMD in our patient.

DISCUSSION

Unidentified genetic diagnosis in patients with a suspected hereditary disease may lead to improper disease treatment and family genetic counseling. Although genome sequencing can detect deep‐intronic variants in DMD, the interpretation of their pathogenicity remains complicated. Our case study illustrates the challenge in diagnosing BMD patients without a pathogenic DMD variant after routine genomic detection approaches.

The DMD gene, located in the Xp21.2 locus and over 2.5‐Mb in size, is the largest one identified to date in the human genome, which is also one of the most complex human genes yet identified. The DMD gene consists of 79 canonical exons, 78 very long introns, eight tissue‐specific promoters, and two polyadenylation sites. 13 In addition, retrotransposons, DNA transposons, and common fragile sites that can cause large‐scale genomic rearrangements are relatively common in the vast non‐coding regions of DMD. 14 Thus, the DMD gene has a complex and wide mutation spectrum, and nearly all variant types could occur. Gross genomic rearrangements, including duplications, deletions, and large‐scale structural variants, account for the majority of pathogenic DMD variants (approximately 80%). The remaining pathogenic DMD variants are represented by small variants, including nonsense, missense, small insertion, small deletion, small deletion–insertion, canonical splicing, and deep‐intronic splicing variants. 15 Throughout the years, it has appeared that MLPA‐based analysis of DMD followed by Sanger or short‐read sequencing of DMD exons and flanking intron sequences becomes the routine diagnostic approach for dystrophinopathies, 3 , 4 as it can detect most DMD variants, that is, typical pathogenic DMD variants. However, the routine diagnostic approach cannot identify or interpret atypical pathogenic DMD variants, such as missense or synonymous variants that cause aberrant DMD splicing, 16 , 17 deep‐intronic splicing variants, 3 , 4 , 15 and complex structural variants. 18 , 19

The interpretation and detection of atypical pathogenic DMD variants demands mRNA‐ and DNA‐based genetic testing techniques. Hence, we performed muscle‐derived DMD mRNA studies in a boy with a suspected diagnosis of dystrophinopathy, as the routine diagnostic approach did not identify disease‐causing variants in him. We successfully detected a non‐canonical splicing transcript in DMD, a cryptic exon‐containing transcript, and then revealed a novel deep‐intronic splice‐altering variant c.8217 + 23338A > G in DMD intron 55. Reduced expression of dystrophin protein was also observed in our patient due to the existence of residual normally spliced DMD transcript. The novel c.8217 + 23338A > G variant is interpreted as a pathogenic splice‐altering variant according to the standard guidelines for interpretation of sequence variants, 11 , 12 as it met the criteria for very strong evidence (PVS1), moderate evidence (PM2), and supporting evidence (PP4). Therefore, the enrolled boy was genetically diagnosed with a dystrophinopathy. Unlike coding variants in DMD that can directly alter amino acid sequences of dystrophin protein, deep‐intronic splicing variants cause alterations in dystrophin protein sequences via aberrant splicing of DMD pre‐mRNA. Deep‐intronic DMD variants that cause alterations in splicing regulatory elements or essential splicing signals can result in aberrant DMD splicing, that is, activation of cryptic exons, which are increasingly identified and reported in DMD and BMD patients. 3 As in our patient, the novel deep‐intronic DMD variant (c.8217 + 23338A > G) strengthened a cryptic donor splice site (an essential splicing signal) and thus caused the activation of a new cryptic exon in DMD.

With recent advances in genome sequencing and in silico bioinformatics, clinical geneticists and physicians often turn to in silico predictions when encountering genomic variants with uncertain significance, such as synonymous, missense, and deep‐intronic variants. The in silico predictive algorithms, like the SpliceAI, 10 MaxEntScan, 9 and HSF 8 algorithms used in this case report, may be used as computational tools to predict splicing alterations in cis‐acting splicing elements (splice sites, branch point, and splicing regulatory elements) caused by possible splice‐altering variants. However, in silico algorithms cannot accurately predict the precise sequences of abnormally spliced transcripts introduced by possible splice‐altering variants. 8 , 9 As in our study, the SpliceAI, MaxEntScan, and HSF algorithms only confirmed the alterations in splice sites caused by the c.8217 + 23338A > G variant, and the precise sequence of the cryptic exon‐containing transcript was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of dystrophin cDNA. Hence, pathogenicity interpretation of variants with uncertain significance cannot rely on in silico predictions alone, but requires functional mRNA and protein studies.

In conclusion, our study highlights that muscle‐derived dystrophin mRNA and protein analysis plays an essential role in the genetic diagnosis of BMD patients with deep‐intronic splice‐altering variants. Identification of pathogenic deep‐intronic DMD variants has potential implications for gene therapies targeting full‐length dystrophin expression.
