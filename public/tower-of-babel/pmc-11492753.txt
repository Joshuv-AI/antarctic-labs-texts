Factors associated with positive human papillomavirus (HPV) test results in cervical precancer screening: a cross-sectional study at Souro Sanou National Teaching Hospital (SSNTH) in Bobo Dioulasso, Burkina Faso
Authors: Ziemle Clement Meda, Herve Hien, Tiandiogo Isidore Traore, Cheick Ahmed Ouattara, Zakaria Ramde, Bernard Ilboudo, Der Adolphe Some, Souleymane Ouattara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHuman papilloma virus testing is a new method of screening for precancerous cervical lesions. Here we identified factors associated to the positive Human papilloma virus-testing in the context of cervical precancer screening at the Souro Sanou National Teaching Hospital in Burkina Faso.Materials and methodConducted from June 2021-May 2022, this was a cross-sectional study, including patients aged between 25 and 55 years-old and screened for precancerous lesions and received HPV-testing at the Department of Gynecology, Obstetrics and Reproductive Medicine (DGORM) of the SSNTH. The proportion of positive HPV-test was calculated, and we identified factors associated to positive HPV-test using logistic regression.ResultsOf the 759 patients came for precancerous lesions screening, 559 patients were included. Their mean-age was 38.8 ± 7.9 years-old, 94.3% were from urban area and 50.3% identified as housewives. Regarding the past medical history, it noted: number of gestures (3.2 ± 2.0), parity (2.8 ± 1.9), number of living children (2.8 ± 1.9), having abortion experience (24.0%), age of first-sexual-intercourse(18.6 ± 2.3 years), alcohol (9.1%) and tobacco (1.0%) consumption, sexually-transmitted-infection (27.0%), Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)infection (5.0%); none had been vaccinated against HPV. Biologically, 16.6% [95% CI: 13.6–20] of the women had a positive HPV-test. The factors significantly associated with positive HPV-test were: occupation in the private sector [OR: 0.06(0.0–0.5); p < 0.001], having a sexually-transmitted-infection [OR: 3.9(2.0–7.7); p < 0.001], age of first-sexual-intercourse [OR: 0.7(0.6–0.9); p < 0.001], sexual-multiple-partnership [OR: 17.5(8.1–39.6); p < 0.001], and HIV status [OR: 13.2(4.4–40.5); p < 0.001].ConclusionThese results call for health actions through the reinforcement for behavioral change mainly about sexually-transmitted-infections, and for the raising awareness of the population for the screening related to the precancerous lesions and HIV.Keywords: Screening, Sexually transmitted infection, HIV, Africa

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is a tissue neoformation destroying the cervix with local and distant invasion [1], and the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, mainly in developing countries [2]. Indeed, its incidence worldwide was estimated to 661,021 cases with 348,189 deaths in 2022, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia [3], and caused of 1,230 cases and 838 deaths in 2010 in Burkina Faso [4].

CC is of infectious origin, due to Human papillomavirus (HPV) as the most common sexually-transmitted-infection (STI) worldwide [5], which is associated to 95% of malignant lesions [2].

Fighting against CC include vaccination against the HPV and the HPV-Test. The World Health Organization (WHO) advises vaccination by the age of 15 and recommends HPV testing for women between the ages of 30 and 60.

New HPV infections acquired at any age are almost always benign. However, persistent infections with one of approximately 12 carcinogenic HPV types (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59) are responsible for nearly all cases of cervical cancer [6].

Hence the value of the HPV test, which involves gently sampling cervical cells for analysis to determine whether they contain the DNA or mRNA of a high-risk HPV type. It an effective method for identifying individuals at risk of developing cervical cancer, allowing for appropriate follow-up monitoring, further testing, or treatment of abnormal cells [7].

Identifying the most affected populations and understanding the factors associated with HPV infection.

can help in formulating targeted interventions to reduce the incidence of cervical cancer such as prioritizing high-risk areas for addressing HPV tests or vaccine distribution strategies.

The present study identified the factors associated with positive HPV-testing in the context of cervical precancer screening (CPCS) at the Souro Sanou National Teaching Hospital (SSNTH) in Burkina Faso.

Material and methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by Burkina Faso's National Ethics and Health Research Committee Deliberation N°2020–10-27. Before the study commenced, authorizations were obtained from the Director General of the SSNTH (N°2019–001/MS/SG/CHUSS/DG/DRH on June 25, 2019). To ensure confidentiality, only the serial number assigned to each anonymous participant was reported, and the data were coded. These data were collected as part of the Scale Up Cervical Cancer Elimination with Secondary Prevention Strategy (SUCCESS) project, which was authorized by the French National Committee for Ethics and Health Research.

Study setting

This study was set-up at the Department of Gynecology, Obstetrics, and Reproductive Medicine (DGORM) and the laboratory of the SSNTH. Alone in the West part of the country, the SSNTH is the third level for care of the national health system in Burkina Faso.

Type and period of the study

This was a cross sectional conducted from March 1, 2021, to May 30, 2022, at the DGORM.

Study population and criteria

All patients aged between 25 and 55 years-old, self-referred or referred for cervical precancer screening, and who gave verbal informed consent were included in this study.

Sample collection and HPV testing

This was an exhaustive sampling of all 759 women screened for HPV in the DGORM with samples results from the laboratory of the SSNTH.

Each participant underwent self-sampling with a swab or brush guided by trained midwife. Subsequently, these samples were cold-conditioned and sent to the laboratory for HPV testing.

The endo-cervical cell samples self-collected by women were analyzed by real polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using GeneXpert® HPV technique. When the HPV test yielded a positive result, the woman underwent a gynecological examination. If the squamocolumnar junction was visible, a VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid) was performed. If the VIA result was negative, thermocoagulation was administered; if positive, diathermic loop resection was conducted. In cases where the squamocolumnar junction was not visible or if there was suspicion of cancer, a cervical smear or biopsy was performed, and the results determined further management.

Data collection

Beforehand, the patients were subjected, following their informed consent, to a pre-established questionnaire completed by telephone call, describing variables relating to sociodemographic data (age, place of residence, profession, level of education), gynecological and obstetrical history (number of gestures, parity, number of live children, notion of sexually transmitted infection, notion of abortion), medical history (up-to-date vaccination against HPV, up-to-date vaccination against hepatitis B virus, serological status against HBV, serological status against HIV), eating habits and lifestyle (age of first sexual intercourse, sexual multiple partners, smoking, alcohol consumption), and para-clinical parameter (HPV test).

Data analysis

Data entry and analysis were carried out using epi info software version 7 and R Studio Version 1.1.463. The quantitative data were expressed as means (mean ± standard deviation) and qualitative data were rendered as numbers and percentages.

As statistical tests, it has been used the student t test for the comparison of the mean, and the Chi 2 test for the comparison of the proportions with a significant threshold of 5%. Finally, it was identified the factors related to the positive HPV test by logistic regression according to the univariate analysis and then the multivariate one only of the factors of the univariate analysis with their p-value ≤ 0.200 in the model. Multivariate regression analysis control for confounding and all estimates were reported, along with their corresponding precision measures such as 95% confidence intervals. The threshold for significance of the tests was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

During the study period from June 17, 2021, to December 31, 2021, five hundred and fifty-nine (559) patients were included among the seven hundred and fifty-nine (759) patients enrolled in the screening for precancerous cervical lesions through HPV testing, representing a participation rate of 73.7%. The HPV prevalence was 16.6% (Fig. 1).

The proportion of HPV-screened patients included in the present study who self-reported HIV infection was 5.0%. No patients reported having received HPV vaccination. A total of 28.6% of patients reported being up to date with their hepatitis B vaccination, while 0.1% of patients reported a history of hepatitis B; for 33.6% of patients, their hepatitis B status was unknown. Alcohol consumption was reported by 9.1% of women, and 1.1% of the patients reported exposure to passive smoking.

The women tested positive for HPV were 93 giving a relative frequency of 16.6% [95% CI 13.7; 19.8].

The factors significantly associated with positive HPV test were: occupation in the private sector [OR: 0.06(0.0–0.5); p < 0.001], having a sexually transmitted Infection (STI) [OR: 3.9(2.0–7.7); p < 0.001], age of first sexual intercourse [OR: 0.7(0.6–0.9); p < 0.001], sexual multiple partnership [OR: 17.5(8.1–39.6); p < 0.001], and HIV status [OR: 13.2(4.4–40.5); p < 0.001].

Discussion

The objective of this study was to identify factors associated with positive Human Papillomavirus (HPV) testing within the context of cervical precancer screening at the Souro Sanou National Teaching Hospital in Burkina Faso. The primary findings revealed that 16.6% of the women tested positive for HPV. Factors significantly associated with a positive HPV test included employment in the private sector, having a sexually transmitted infection (STI), age at first sexual intercourse, multiple sexual partnerships, and HIV status.

The study had major methodological constraints, namely it is descriptive based one a cross-sectional study, even if is analytic, which does not allow for temporal causal links. Also, the conclusion regarding STIs is based on self-reporting. Since other infections were not measured in the study, it is not possible to conclude a real association, and this limitation makes it difficult to generalize the results.

In the present study, the mean age was comparable to the result found by Ouedraogo et al. (37.37 years-old) in Burkina Faso [8], De Vuyst et al. (35 to 39 years-old) in Kenya [9], but lower than that of Holme et al. (40.8 years-old) in Nicaragua [10]. Moreover, according to the literature, precancerous lesions of the cervix occur at the most in the age range of 26 to 30 years-old and invasive cancer at the latest 15 years [10].

Regarding the level of education in this series, Dominique et al., in Senegal [11] and De Vuyst et al. in Kenya [9] identified the secondary level as the predominant educational attainment, albeit with varying proportions of 31% and 54%, respectively. Krings reported similar results in Ghana [12]. Similarly, Traore et al. in Burkina Faso reported that 36% of their subjects fell into this category [13]. This suggests that women with a secondary level of education may have easier access to information and a more comprehensive understanding of the benefits associated with cervical precancer screening.

According to the findings of this study, most of the women were in monogamous relationships. This result is similar to the 73.3% reported by Ouedraogo et al., in Burkina Faso [14], yet lower than the 84% documented by Traore et al., also in Burkina Faso [13]. Conversely, this percentage was higher than those found by Flores et al., in Mexico, with 60.6% [15], and by Duan et al., in China, with 60.2% [16].

Half of the women in this study identified as housewives. This observation is consistent with findings from Kodio et al., who reported approximately 51.39% in Mali [17], Ouedraogo et al., with 32.8% [8], and Sawadogo et al., with 41.3% [6], all in Burkina Faso. In Burkina Faso, the challenge of women's employability is significant, dominated by a lack of job opportunities and social issues unfavorable to them.

The relative frequency of HPV infection in this study was 16.6%, which aligns with various prevalences observed globally. For instance, a study by Marie et al., in Cameroon reported a similar prevalence of 16.6% [17]. In our study, endocervical cell samples were collected using a brush by medical personnel. Comparatively, Holme et al., in Nicaragua [10] found that approximately 15.2% of women tested positive for HPV. The prevalences were markedly higher in different regions of Burkina Faso, with 38.7%, 43.3%, and 26.2% in the central-eastern, central, and Hauts-Bassins regions respectively, culminating in an overall prevalence of 35.4% as reported by Ouedraogo et al., in a descriptive multicenter cross-sectional study [18]. These variations in prevalence could be attributed to differences in sampling techniques and study sample sizes, potentially affecting the comprehensive representation of all cases.

The persistent HPV (high risk) infection is the necessary, but not sufficient, cause in the genesis and development of cervical cancer [3]. It has been identified co-factors which could increase the risk of viral infections and therefore promote cervical cancer development.

Regarding the STIs, it found that women who reported having STIs were 3.9 times more likely to have HPV infection than those who did not report STIs. Kjver et al., in Denmark [19], Kennedy et al., in Nigeria [20], Maria et al. in Burkina Faso [21], and Berraho et al. in Marocco [22], had observed the same associations between HPV infection and STIs. In addition to Denmark, Svare et al., found the same observations by comparing the risk factors of HPV infection between two populations of women from two different regions [23]. Contrary to findings that highlight a link between sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, a study by Ouedraogo et al., involving a population of adolescent girls in Burkina Faso reported no such association [8]. Similarly, studies conducted by Traore et al., in Bobo-Dioulasso [13] and Zohoncon in Ouagadougou [24], both within Burkina Faso, observed an absence of this association. These differing observations might be attributed to several factors, including the sample size, the data collection techniques used for STIs, the targeted population, and their level of knowledge about these infections. These results underline the need to enhance awareness of STI risk factors among the population. In the present study, an increase in the age of first intercourse by one year reduced the risk of HPV infection by 26.0%. Significant associations have been reported by some authors such as Kennedy et al., in Nigeria [20], Flores et al. in Mexico [15], Walmer et al., in Haiti [25]. In Burkina Faso, Ouedraogo et al., reported that the age of first sexual intercourse before 18 years increases the risk of HPV infection [14]. However, these results were inconsistent with those from Ouedraogo et al., who did not find a significant association with HPV infection and age of first intercourse in their study population [8].

Women who reported having multiple sexual partners were found to be 17.5 times more likely to contract an HPV infection compared to those who reported having a single sexual partner. Comparable findings have been documented in various international studies, including Walmer et al., in Haiti [25], Kjver et al. [19]. and Svare et al. [23] in Denmark, Chaouki in Morocco [26], Flores et al. [15] and Torres-Poveda et al. [27] in Mexico, Kennedy et al., in Nigeria [20], and both Ouédraogo et al. [14]. and Ouedraogo et al. [8] in Burkina Faso. These studies collectively reinforce the significant correlation between engaging in multiple sexual partnerships and the risk of HPV infection.

The women living with HIV were 13.2 times more likely to have HPV infection. For Belglaiaa et al., there aws an association between HIV and HPV in Marocco [28]. Jary et al., in Mali found a highest prevalence of HIV infection among participants with HPV [29]. In Burkina Faso, according to Zohoncon [24], the prevalence of infection with different high-risk HPV genotypes was significantly higher among HIV-positive women, 74.1% of whom had at least three high-risk HPV genotypes in their general study population. These studies were conducted among female sex workers with the mention of significant HPV prevalences among HIV-infected populations. Indeed, Luchters et al., reported a 73.3% of prevalence of HPV among HIV-positive women compared to 45.5% among those without HIV [30].

In the current study, women employed in the private sector exhibited a 94.0% decrease in the risk of contracting Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection compared to those who were housewives. While not observed in this study, other factors have been highlighted in the literature as contributing to the risk of HPV infection, including early age pregnancy in Morocco [26], early age at marriage [26], use of oral contraceptives [26, 31], multiparity [22, 31], low educational attainment [22], low socioeconomic status [22, 31], and poor genital hygiene [31].

Conclusion

From the cohort of the women screened by the HPV test introduced in June 2021 at the SSTNH, 16.6% of the 559 women included in the present study had a positive HPV test. The factors significantly associated with positive HPV test were: occupation in the private sector, having a sexually transmitted infection (STI), age of first sexual intercourse, sexual multiple partnership, and HIV status. That calls for health actions through the reinforcement for behavioral change mainly about sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and for the raising awareness of the population on screening related to the precancerous cervical lesions and HIV.
