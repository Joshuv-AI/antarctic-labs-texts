Geostatistics from Elliptic Boundary-Value Problems: Green Operators, Transmission Conditions, and Schur Complements
J. J. Segura, ORCID: 0009-0004-2742-4353

Abstract
Classical geostatistics encodes spatial dependence by prescribing variograms or covariance kernels on Euclidean domains, whereas the SPDE–GMRF paradigm specifies Gaussian fields through an elliptic precision operator whose inverse is the corresponding Green operator. We develop an operator-based formulation of Gaussian spatial random fields on bounded domains and manifolds with internal interfaces, treating boundary and transmission conditions as explicit components of the statistical model. Starting from coercive quadratic energy functionals, variational theory yields a precise precision–covariance correspondence and shows that variograms are derived quadratic functionals of the Green operator, hence depend on boundary conditions and domain geometry. Conditioning and kriging follow from standard Gaussian update identities in both covariance and precision form, with hard constraints represented equivalently by exact interpolation constraints or by distributional source terms. Interfaces are modelled via surface penalty terms; taking variations produces flux-jump transmission conditions and induces controlled attenuation of cross-interface covariance. Finally, boundary-driven prediction and domain reduction are formulated through Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators and Schur complements, providing an operator language for upscaling, change of support, and subdomain-to-boundary mappings. Throughout, we use tools standard in spatial statistics and elliptic PDE theory to keep boundary and interface effects explicit in covariance modeling and prediction.

Introduction

Spatial random fields are central in geostatistics and spatial statistics, where dependence is commonly described through covariance functions and variograms
(
Matheron 1963
;
Matheron 1971
;
Cressie 1993
;
Chilès and Delfiner 2012
)
.
A complementary operator-centric viewpoint defines Gaussian fields through
precision operators
, often via elliptic SPDE models, so that covariances are Green operators of boundary-value problems
(
Whittle 1954
;
Whittle 1967
;
Lindgren et al. 2011
;
Lindgren et al. 2022
)
.
This perspective is especially informative on bounded or irregular domains and in the presence of internal interfaces, because boundary conditions and transmission conditions enter the Green operator and therefore directly shape covariance, variograms, and kriging predictions.

Related work and positioning.

Three strands are closest to the present article.

(i) SPDE–Matérn and GMRF discretizations
make the operator–kernel duality computationally central by defining Matérn fields as solutions of Whittle-type operators and exploiting sparse precisions
(
Whittle 1954
;
Whittle 1967
;
Lindgren et al. 2011
;
Lindgren et al. 2022
)
.

(ii) Energy-based spatial random fields
(including Sparta/SRF constructions) build covariance models from Boltzmann–Gibbs energy functionals, emphasizing locality, statistical mechanics structure, and Green-function correlations
(
Hristopulos and Elogne 2007
;
Hristopulos 2020
;
Allard et al. 2021
;
Hristopulos 2022
)
.

(iii) Gaussian free fields and QFT free fields
provide the mathematical template: quadratic actions on manifolds, Green operators, and (when needed) distributional kernels
(
Cardy 1996
;
Polyakov 1987
;
Di Francesco et al. 1997
;
Polchinski 1998
)
.
The emphasis here is
operator-first
: we keep boundary conditions and interfaces explicit at the level of the Green operator, and we place (a) surface-defect interfaces and their variational transmission conditions, (b) boundary effective operators (Dirichlet-to-Neumann) and exact domain reduction (Schur complements), and (c) the metric vs. deformation duality for non-stationarity, into a single narrative aligned with spatial statistical modeling and elliptic PDE theory on bounded domains.

The rigorous layer uses only coercive variational theory (Lax–Milgram), Gaussian conditioning identities,
and exact operator reductions (Schur complements/DtN) for elliptic boundary-value problems.

2
Operator Backbone: Actions, Green Operators, and Gaussian Fields

2.1
Energy spaces and coercive forms

Assumption 2.1

(Prototypical elliptic setting)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Let
A:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be measurable, symmetric, and uniformly elliptic: there exist

0<a_{\min}\leq a_{\max}<\infty
such that for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

a_{\min}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert^{2}\leq\xi^{\top}A(x)\xi\leq a_{\max}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert^{2}.

Let
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfy
c(x)\geq c_{0}>0
a.e.

Definition 2.1

(Energy space and bilinear form)
.

Let
V:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(Dirichlet boundary condition, for concreteness).
Define

a(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)^{\top}A(x)\nabla v(x)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}c(x)\,u(x)v(x)\,dx,\qquad u,v\in V.

Lemma 2.1

(Boundedness and coercivity)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exist
M,\alpha>0
such that

|a(u,v)|\leq M\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\qquad a(u,u)\geq\alpha\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Proof.

Cauchy–Schwarz and the bounds on
A
and
c
yield boundedness. Coercivity follows from uniform ellipticity and
c\geq c_{0}
:

a(u,u)\geq a_{\min}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+c_{0}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\min\{a_{\min},c_{0}\}\,\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

∎

Theorem 2.1

(Lax–Milgram)
.

Let
V
be a Hilbert space and
a(\cdot,\cdot)
a bounded coercive bilinear form on
V
.
Then for every
f\in V^{\ast}
there exists a unique
u\in V
such that

a(u,v)=f(v)\qquad\forall v\in V,

and
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{V}\leq\alpha^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{V^{\ast}}
.

Proof.

Define
J:V\to\mathbb{R}
by
J(w):=\frac{1}{2}\,a(w,w)-f(w)
.
Coercivity implies
J
is strictly convex and coercive, hence admits a unique minimizer
u
.
The Euler–Lagrange condition
J^{\prime}(u)[v]=0
for all
v\in V
yields
a(u,v)=f(v)
.
Taking
v=u
gives the stability bound.
∎

2.2
From bilinear form to operator and Green operator

Proposition 2.1

(Operator induced by a coercive form)
.

Let
a(\cdot,\cdot)
be bounded and coercive on a Hilbert space
V
.
Define
\mathcal{L}:V\to V^{\ast}
by

(\mathcal{L}u)(v):=a(u,v),\qquad u,v\in V.

Then
\mathcal{L}
is a bounded isomorphism
V\to V^{\ast}
. Moreover, for each
f\in V^{\ast}
,
the unique
u\in V
solving
a(u,\cdot)=f(\cdot)
satisfies
\mathcal{L}u=f
.

Definition 2.2

(Green operator (variational inverse))
.

Under Theorem
2.1
, define
G:V^{\ast}\to V
by
Gf=u
, where
u
is the unique solution

a(u,\cdot)=f(\cdot)
. Equivalently,
G=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
in the sense of Proposition
2.1
.
We call
G
the
Green operator
associated with
(V,a)
.

Proposition 2.2

(Symmetry/positivity)
.

If
a
is symmetric, then for all
f,g\in V^{\ast}
,

f(Gg)=g(Gf),\qquad f(Gf)=a(Gf,Gf)\geq 0.

2.3
Discrete Gaussian fields and precision–covariance duality

Definition 2.3

(Gaussian vector with precision)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive definite.
A random vector
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
precision

Q
if

p(z)=\frac{\det(Q)^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}Qz\right),

i.e.
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
.

Theorem 2.2

(Precision–covariance identity)
.

If
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
, then
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
and
\Cov(Z)=Q^{-1}
.

2.4
Variograms as derived quadratic functionals

Definition 2.4

(Variogram)
.

Let
Z
be a second-order field on an index set
T
with covariance
C(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[Z(s)Z(t)]
.
The (semi-)variogram is

\gamma(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(Z(s)-Z(t))^{2}\big],

whenever finite.

Proposition 2.3

(Variogram–covariance identity)
.

If
Z
is centered and second order, then

\gamma(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}\big(C(s,s)+C(t,t)-2C(s,t)\big).

If moreover
C(s,s)
is constant, then
\gamma(s,t)=C(s,s)-C(s,t)
.

2.5
Operator inverse vs. integral kernel (U3)

A recurring ambiguity is “
C(x,x^{\prime})
exists” versus “
G=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
exists”. Our convention is:

•

Primary object:
the Green operator
G:V^{\ast}\to V
defined variationally (Definition
2.2
).
This exists under coercivity and does
not
require any pointwise kernel.

•

Kernel representation (secondary):
when additional regularity holds,
G
admits an integral kernel
G(x,x^{\prime})

(or a distributional kernel) such that
(Gf)(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}
for suitable
f
.
This depends on dimension, coefficients, and boundary regularity; see e.g.
(
Evans 2010
;
Gilbarg and Trudinger 1983
)
.

Terminology convention.

We reserve
Green operator
for the variational inverse
G=\mathcal{L}^{-1}:V^{\ast}\to V
.
When an integral representation exists we write
Green’s function
or
Green kernel
for a function (or distribution)

G(\cdot,\cdot)
representing
G
by
(Gf)(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)f(y)\,dy
in the appropriate sense.

Remark 2.1

(When we write
G(x,x^{\prime})
)
.

Whenever we write
G(x,x^{\prime})
(or
C(x,x^{\prime})
), this should be read as:
(i) an honest function kernel when regularity permits,
(ii) otherwise a
distributional kernel
defined by duality.
All kriging/conditioning identities remain exact at the discrete level and meaningful at the operator level without requiring pointwise kernels.

Remark 2.2

(Variograms without diagonal kernels)
.

In intrinsic settings (e.g. massless fields in low dimension),
C(x,x)
may fail pointwise.
Variograms remain meaningful through increments (Definition
2.4
) and/or discrete approximations

(
Matheron 1971
;
Cardy 1996
)
.

3
Polyakov-Type Actions as Variogram Generators

Let
(\Sigma,h)
be a 2D Riemannian manifold (possibly with boundary) with Laplace–Beltrami operator

\Delta_{h}Z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\partial_{a}\!\left(\sqrt{h}\,h^{ab}\partial_{b}Z\right).

(For the Polyakov/free-field viewpoint in physics, see
(
Polyakov 1987
;
Polchinski 1998
;
Di Francesco et al. 1997
)
.)

Definition 3.1

(Polyakov-type quadratic action)
.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}>0
and
m\geq 0
.
Define

S[Z]=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{h}\,h^{ab}\partial_{a}Z\,\partial_{b}Z\,d^{2}\sigma+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{h}\,Z^{2}\,d^{2}\sigma,

with boundary conditions encoded in the energy space
V
(Dirichlet/Neumann/Robin).

Proposition 3.1

(Associated precision operator)
.

Under boundary conditions compatible with integration by parts,

S[Z]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\sqrt{h}\;Z\,(\mathcal{L}Z)\,d^{2}\sigma,\qquad\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\Delta_{h}+m^{2}.

Remark 3.1

(Boundary-condition dependence is structural)
.

Even with identical differential expression
\mathcal{L}
, the Green operator depends on the boundary condition,
so the induced variogram does as well. Equivalently: the differential expression does not determine a unique
Green kernel without boundary/interface data.
Section
4
gives an explicit 1D calculation showing this dependence in closed form.

4
Worked Micro-Examples: Boundaries and Interfaces (U2)

This section provides pocket calculations illustrating:
(i) Green kernels depend on boundary conditions, and
(ii) interface surface terms yield flux-jump conditions and attenuate cross-interface covariance.

4.1
1D interval: Dirichlet vs Neumann Green kernel and variogram

Consider
\Omega=(0,L)
and the 1D operator

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+m^{2},\qquad m>0.

Let
G_{B}(x,y)
denote the Green kernel under boundary condition
B\in\{D,N\}
.

Example 4.1

(Dirichlet Green kernel)
.

For Dirichlet
u(0)=u(L)=0
, one has

G_{D}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m\sinh(mL)}\begin{cases}\sinh(mx)\,\sinh(m(L-y)),&x\leq y,\\[2.0pt]
\sinh(my)\,\sinh(m(L-x)),&x>y.\end{cases}

The induced variogram is
\gamma_{D}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}(G_{D}(x,x)+G_{D}(y,y)-2G_{D}(x,y))
when interpreted pointwise.

Example 4.2

(Neumann Green kernel)
.

For Neumann
u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(L)=0
, the Green kernel is

G_{N}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m\sinh(mL)}\begin{cases}\cosh(mx)\,\cosh(m(L-y)),&x\leq y,\\[2.0pt]
\cosh(my)\,\cosh(m(L-x)),&x>y,\end{cases}

and
\gamma_{N}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}(G_{N}(x,x)+G_{N}(y,y)-2G_{N}(x,y))
.

Remark 4.1

(Same operator, different variograms)
.

G_{D}\neq G_{N}
even though the differential operator is identical; only the boundary condition changes.
Therefore the induced variogram is boundary-condition dependent.

Implication for empirical variography on bounded domains (U4).

Examples
4.1
–
4.2
and the interface model in Section
4.2

show that in an operator-first formulation the covariance
C=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
and variogram
\gamma
are determined by the

boundary-value problem
, not the differential expression alone: the domain
\Omega
, boundary condition
B
on
\partial\Omega
,
and internal defects/transmission terms enter the Green operator. Consequently, experimental variograms computed from data supported
in a bounded region need not match the infinite-domain template associated with the same symbol (e.g. Matérn/Whittle), even when
interior coefficients are constant: boundary screening/reflection and interface transmission modify long-lag behaviour and can shift
apparent sill and range.

A practical recommendation is to treat
(\mathcal{L},B,S)
as the calibrated object: estimate parameters against the domain-dependent
Green operator (e.g. via SPDE/GMRF likelihood), and stratify empirical variogram diagnostics by pair-classes (interior vs near-boundary;
same-side vs cross-interface). A simple diagnostic is to compute empirical variograms separately for points within a buffer of
\partial\Omega

versus deep interior; competing boundary conditions (Dirichlet/Neumann/Robin, or mixed) can be compared by likelihood or predictive score
within the same SPDE discretisation.

4.2
Two-subdomain interface: surface term and derivative jump

Let
\Omega=(-L,L)
with an interface at
S=\{0\}
.
Consider the energy (1D for simplicity)

\mathcal{E}[Z]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{0}\big(|Z^{\prime}|^{2}+m^{2}Z^{2}\big)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\big(|Z^{\prime}|^{2}+m^{2}Z^{2}\big)\,dx+\frac{\alpha}{2}\,Z(0)^{2},\qquad\alpha\geq 0,

with (say) Dirichlet at
\pm L
to avoid endpoint terms.

Trace remark.
In one dimension the point trace
Z(0)
is well-defined for
Z\in H^{1}(-L,L)
; in higher dimensions replace point evaluation by a surface trace on an interface hypersurface
S
(cf. Proposition
7.1
).

Proposition 4.1

(Interface (jump) condition)
.

Stationarity of
\mathcal{E}
implies

(-Z^{\prime\prime}+m^{2}Z)=0\quad\text{on }(-L,0)\cup(0,L),

continuity of
Z
at
0
(for
Z\in H^{1}
), and the derivative jump condition

Z^{\prime}(0^{+})-Z^{\prime}(0^{-})=\alpha\,Z(0).

Remark 4.2

(Explicit Green-kernel update (delta barrier))
.

Let
G_{0}
be the Dirichlet Green kernel on
(-L,L)
for
-d^{2}/dx^{2}+m^{2}
without the interface penalty (
\alpha=0
).
Then

G_{\alpha}(x,y)=G_{0}(x,y)\;-\;\frac{\alpha\,G_{0}(x,0)\,G_{0}(0,y)}{1+\alpha\,G_{0}(0,0)}.

In particular
G_{\alpha}(x,y)\leq G_{0}(x,y)
pointwise, and cross-interface covariance (
x<0<y
) decreases monotonically with
\alpha
.
For the Dirichlet kernel on
(-L,L)
one can evaluate
G_{0}(0,0)=\frac{\tanh(mL)}{2m}
.

S
with defect penalty

5
Product Manifolds and Nested Covariance Structures

Nested/multi-range covariance models are ubiquitous in practice
(
Chilès and Delfiner 2012
)
.
A clean operator mechanism is to place the field on a product
\Sigma\times\mathcal{K}
and decompose into internal modes.

Assumption 5.1

(Compact internal factor)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary.
Let
\{-\Delta_{\mathcal{K}}\phi_{n}=\lambda_{n}\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be an orthonormal eigenbasis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{K})
.

Proposition 5.1

(Mode decomposition on
\Sigma\times\mathcal{K}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\Sigma\times\mathcal{K}
and

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}\otimes I+I\otimes(-\Delta_{\mathcal{K}})+m^{2},

with
\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
positive self-adjoint (boundary conditions included).
If
G_{n}:=(\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}+(m^{2}+\lambda_{n}))^{-1}
exists (variationally) for each
n
, then
for
f(\sigma,y)=\sum_{n}f_{n}(\sigma)\phi_{n}(y)
,

(\mathcal{L}^{-1}f)(\sigma,y)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(G_{n}f_{n})(\sigma)\,\phi_{n}(y).

Remark 5.1
.

Each internal mode shifts the effective mass
m_{n}^{2}=m^{2}+\lambda_{n}
, contributing a component with its own range.
If
\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}=(\kappa^{2}-\Delta_{\Sigma})^{\alpha/2}
(Whittle–Matérn form), then each mode produces

\kappa_{n}^{2}=\kappa^{2}+\lambda_{n}
and an effective range scale on the order of
(\kappa^{2}+\lambda_{n})^{-1/2}
.

6
Conditioning, Kriging, and Source Representations

6.1
Gaussian conditioning in covariance form

Theorem 6.1

(Conditional law of a joint Gaussian vector)
.

Let
(X,Y)
be jointly Gaussian with mean zero and block covariance

\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{XX}&\Sigma_{XY}\\
\Sigma_{YX}&\Sigma_{YY}\end{pmatrix}\right),

where
\Sigma_{XX}
is symmetric positive definite (hence invertible). Then the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X=x
is Gaussian:

Y\,\big|\,X=x\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\ \Sigma_{YX}\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}x,\ \Sigma_{YY}-\Sigma_{YX}\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\ \right).

The conditional covariance is the (positive semidefinite) Schur complement of
\Sigma_{XX}
in the joint covariance matrix.

6.2
Linear-Gaussian update in precision form

Theorem 6.2

(Linear-Gaussian update)
.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
with
Q
SPD and observations

z=RZ+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,N),

with
N
SPD. Then

Z\mid z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{z},Q_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}),\qquad Q_{\mathrm{post}}=Q+R^{\top}N^{-1}R,\qquad\bar{z}=Q_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}R^{\top}N^{-1}z.

6.3
Hard conditioning as delta constraints / point sources

Proposition 6.1

(Delta constraints as Fourier representations)
.

Formally, hard constraints
Z(x_{i})=z_{i}
satisfy

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta(Z(x_{i})-z_{i})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d\lambda_{i}}{2\pi}\,\exp\!\left(i\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}Z(x_{i})-i\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}z_{i}\right).

Remark 6.1
.

In operator language, conditioning inserts linear functionals (rows of
R
). In interpretative field-theory language, the Fourier factors act as point sources.

6.4
Parameter inference and identifiability

Let
\theta
collect hyperparameters in the prior precision operator and defect terms, e.g.

\theta=(m,\alpha,\text{metric/anisotropy parameters},\ldots)
.
With linear observations
z=RZ+\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,N)
, the marginal likelihood is

z\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\;\Sigma_{\theta}\bigr),\qquad\Sigma_{\theta}=R\,\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{-1}R^{\top}+N,

and

\ell(\theta)=-\tfrac{1}{2}z^{\top}\Sigma_{\theta}^{-1}z-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma_{\theta})+\text{const}.

This is the same likelihood optimized in SPDE–GMRF practice; in particular, it applies to defect/interface parameters such as
\alpha

and to metric parameters controlling anisotropy
(
Lindgren et al. 2011
;
Lindgren et al. 2022
)
.

7
Defects and Interfaces via Surface Terms

Proposition 7.1

(Surface penalty induces transmission condition)
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
with smooth interface
S=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
.
Consider

\mathcal{E}[Z]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\big(\nabla Z^{\top}A_{k}\nabla Z+c_{k}Z^{2}\big)\,dx+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{S}(\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z)^{2}\,dS.

Stationarity under variations supported on
S
yields the interface condition

(A_{1}\nabla Z_{1})\cdot n_{1}+(A_{2}\nabla Z_{2})\cdot n_{2}+\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z=0.

Remark 7.1

(Continuity vs. flux jump)
.

In this single-field
H^{1}(\Omega)
setting the trace of
Z
is single-valued across
S
(no jump in
Z
);
the defect term produces a jump only in the normal flux, as made explicit in Remark
7.2
.

Remark 7.2

(Flux jump form and trace regularity)
.

Let
n:=n_{1}=-n_{2}
be a chosen unit normal along
S
(so the outward normals satisfy
n_{2}=-n_{1}
).
Then the interface condition in Proposition
7.1
can be written in the standard jump form

\big[(A\nabla Z)\cdot n\big]_{S}+\alpha\,\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z=0,

where
\big[w\big]_{S}:=w|_{\Omega_{1}}-w|_{\Omega_{2}}
denotes the jump across
S
and
\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z
is the Sobolev trace of
Z
on
S
.
In the
H^{1}
-based setting,
\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z\in H^{1/2}(S)
is well-defined, so the surface penalty

\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{S}(\operatorname{Tr}_{S}Z)^{2}\,dS
is meaningful. If discontinuities of
Z
are intended, one can instead penalize the trace jump,
e.g.
\int_{S}|\operatorname{Tr}_{S}(Z_{1}-Z_{2})|^{2}\,dS
.

8
Non-stationarity: Deformation vs Metric Geometry

Definition 8.1

(Pullback of a random field)
.

Let
W
be a random field on
\Omega^{\prime}
and
f:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
measurable.
Define
Z(x)=W(f(x))
.

Proposition 8.1

(Covariance under pullback)
.

If
W
is second order with covariance
C_{W}
, then
Z
has covariance

C_{Z}(x,x^{\prime})=C_{W}(f(x),f(x^{\prime})).

If
W
is stationary on
\Omega^{\prime}
, then
C_{Z}(x,x^{\prime})=C_{0}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))
, typically non-stationary on
\Omega
.

Remark 8.1
.

In 2D, conformal maps induce conformal metrics and can be used to encode spatial deformation
(
Sampson and Guttorp 1992
;
Chilès and Delfiner 2012
)
.

9
Change of Support and Spectral Identities

Proposition 9.1

(Linear images of Gaussians)
.

If
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
and
Y=AZ
, then
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,ACA^{\top})
.

Proposition 9.2

(Spectral effect of averaging filters)
.

If
Z
is stationary with spectral density
f_{Z}(k)
and
Y=h*Z
, then

f_{Y}(k)=|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}\,f_{Z}(k).

Remark 9.1
.

Coarse supports suppress high-frequency modes; refitting within a parametric family induces a parameter flow.

10
Boundaries, DtN Maps, and Schur Complements

10.1
Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) map

Definition 10.1

(Dirichlet-to-Neumann map)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let

\mathcal{L}u:=-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)+cu

with
A
bounded, measurable and uniformly elliptic, and
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
c\geq 0
.
For
\varphi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
choose any lifting
w_{\varphi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\operatorname{Tr}_{\partial\Omega}w_{\varphi}=\varphi
, and let
v_{\varphi}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the unique weak solution of

\int_{\Omega}\nabla v_{\varphi}^{\top}A\nabla\eta\,dx+\int_{\Omega}c\,v_{\varphi}\,\eta\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla w_{\varphi}^{\top}A\nabla\eta\,dx-\int_{\Omega}c\,w_{\varphi}\,\eta\,dx,\qquad\forall\eta\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Set
u_{\varphi}:=w_{\varphi}+v_{\varphi}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
; then
\mathcal{L}u_{\varphi}=0
in the weak sense and

\operatorname{Tr}_{\partial\Omega}u_{\varphi}=\varphi
.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator is the bounded map
\Lambda:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
defined by

\langle\Lambda\varphi,\psi\rangle_{H^{-1/2},H^{1/2}}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\varphi}^{\top}A\nabla u_{\psi}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}c\,u_{\varphi}\,u_{\psi}\,dx,\qquad\forall\psi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),

where
u_{\psi}
is constructed as above. Equivalently,
\Lambda\varphi
is the conormal flux

(A\nabla u_{\varphi})\cdot n
on
\partial\Omega
understood as an element of
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Remark 10.1

(Notation)
.

When
u_{\varphi}
is regular enough, the duality pairing

\langle\Lambda\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{H^{-1/2},H^{1/2}}
coincides with the boundary integral

\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\,(\Lambda\varphi)\,dS
.

Theorem 10.1

(DtN energy identity)
.

Assuming sufficient regularity (or interpreting the right-hand side as a duality pairing),

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\varphi}^{\top}A\nabla u_{\varphi}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}c\,u_{\varphi}^{2}\,dx=\langle\Lambda\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{H^{-1/2},H^{1/2}}.

10.2
Schur complement reduction

Lemma 10.1

(Schur complement as marginal precision)
.

Let
(U,V)
be jointly Gaussian with block precision

Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{UU}&Q_{UV}\\
Q_{VU}&Q_{VV}\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q\succ 0.

Then the marginal law of
U
has precision

Q_{\mathrm{marg}}=Q_{UU}-Q_{UV}Q_{VV}^{-1}Q_{VU}.

Remark 10.2
.

DtN operators are continuum boundary effective operators; Schur complements are their discrete analogues.

11
Generating Functionals and Conditional Simulation

Theorem 11.1

(Gaussian generating functional)
.

If
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
, then for any
J
,

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{J^{\top}Z}\big]=\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}J^{\top}Q^{-1}J\right).

Lemma 11.1

(Sampling from a precision)
.

Let
Q=LL^{\top}
be a Cholesky factorization and
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.
If
L^{\top}\eta=\xi
, then
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
.

Corollary 11.1

(Posterior conditional simulation)
.

In Theorem
6.2
, a conditional simulation is

Z^{(s)}=\bar{z}+\eta,\qquad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}).

12
Multivariate fields and matrix-valued operators

Multivariate Gaussian fields can be specified by block (matrix-valued) precision operators; after discretisation this yields an SPD block matrix
Q
and cross-covariances from
C=Q^{-1}
.
A simple separable (coregionalization) case is

Q=G^{-1}\otimes Q_{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C=G\otimes Q_{0}^{-1},

recovering a shared spatial kernel with variable-to-variable coupling
(
Chilès and Delfiner 2012
)
.
