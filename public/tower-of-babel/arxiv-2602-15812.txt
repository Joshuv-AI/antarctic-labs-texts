Separable C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras Without the Countable Axiom of Choice Thanks: I.F. is partially supported by NSERC
Bruce Blackadar, Ilijas Farah

Abstract




The goal of this paper is twofold. In addition to the results stated in the next paragraph, we present some classical results on absoluteness relevant to functional analysis that are well known to logicians but not nearly as well advertised as they should be.

We show that the theory of separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras can be developed in ZF (that is, without using any Choice). This includes proving the Gelfand–Naimark representation theorems as well as the Spectral Mapping Theorem for polynomials and developing continuous functional calculus for commuting normal elements.
Some of our proofs are modifications of the standard ones, obtained by avoiding the use of Choice. Some other proofs require new ideas in order to avoid the use of Choice. Yet another batch of proofs proceeds by using the set-theoretic Shoenfield Absoluteness Theorem. This result (well known to logicians but regrettably not as well advertised as it deserves) implies that statements about standard Borel spaces of low quantifier complexity (to be precise,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{2}
statements; see §
5.3
) that are provable in ZFC, or even ZFC together with the Continuum Hypothesis (or Martin’s Axiom,
\OCAT
,…) are provable in ZF.
One of the main objectives of this paper is to present these results in a convenient form that can be utilized by analysts not familiar with set theory.

We also show that in the absence of Choice (more precisely, assuming the existence of a Russell set) there is a concretely representable unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra that is not isomorphic to
C(X)
for any compact Hausdorff space
X
. Finally, from the model-theoretic point of view, while the property of having a tracial state is provably axiomatizable in ZFC, it is not provably axiomatizable in ZF+DC.

1.
C*-Algebras

1.0.1

We take the usual definition of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
[
Bla06
, II.1.1.1]
, with just the caution that “complete” means “
\sigma
-complete.” As in
[
BFKar
, Definition 1.0.1]
, we say that a metric space

(X,\rho)
is
\sigma
-complete
if, whenever
(A_{n})
is a decreasing sequence of
nonempty closed subsets of
X
with
\diam(A_{n})\to 0
, then
\bigcap_{n}A_{n}
is nonempty (it is then necessarily a singleton). In
[
BSB96
]
spaces with this property were called
Cantor complete
. For separable metric spaces
\sigma
-completeness is equivalent to
usual Cauchy completeness
[
Bla25
, XII.15.9.7]
(so “complete” is unambiguous in this case).

1.0.2

The two standard examples of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras are:

(i)

The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
of all bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, or more generally any norm-closed *-subalgebra thereof.
(The fact that
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is
\sigma
-complete and has an adjoint operation making it a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra requires a little proof, see
[
BFKar
, 7.0.1]
,
[
BFKar
, 7.0.3]
.)

(ii)

C_{0}(X)
, the complex-valued continuous functions vanishing at infinity on a locally
compact Hausdorff space
X
(with complex conjugation as adjoint).

The two Gelfand–Naimark theorems, two of the most basic facts in the subject, assert that these
examples are universal in the categories of all
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and all commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, respectively. However, without some Choice these results can fail, and thus we will
have to carefully reestablish all basic properties of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

2.
Banach Space Prerequisites

We define a Banach space to be a normed space that is
\sigma
-complete in the norm metric
(Cauchy completeness suffices in the separable case).
The following result can be proved in ZF; the result is probably well known, but we state it for future reference.

2.0.1

Proposition.

Suppose that
X
is a separable Banach space (real or complex). Then the following results hold.

(1)

Hahn–Banach extension theorem: For every subspace
Y
of
X
and a bounded linear functional
\varphi
on
Y
, there is a linear functional
\psi
on
X
that extends
\varphi
and has the same norm as
\varphi
.

(2)

Banach–Alaoglu theorem: The closed unit ball
\mathbf{B}(X^{*})
of the dual space
X^{*}
is weak*-compact.

(3)

The weak* topology on
\mathbf{B}(X^{*})
is compact, metrizable, and separable.

(4)

Krein–Milman theorem: If
K
is a weak*-compact and convex subset of
X^{*}
, then
K
is the closed convex hull of its extreme boundary,
\partial K
.

(5)

Hahn–Banach separation theorem: If
A
and
B
are disjoint convex subsets of
X
and
A
is open, then there is
\varphi\in X^{*}
such that
Re(\varphi(x))<\inf_{y\in B}Re(\varphi(y))
for all
x\in A
.

(6)

The Open Mapping Theorem, Closed Graph Theorem, and
Uniform Boundedness Theorem hold for maps with domain
X
(and separable codomain for the Closed Graph Theorem).

Proof:
Let
x_{n}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be an enumeration of a dense subset of the unit ball of
X
.

(
1
) It suffices to prove this in the case when
X
is a real Banach space. The standard proof, using Zorn’s Lemma (e.g.,
[
Ped12
, Lemma 2.3.2]
), can be performed using a countable dense set instead, as follows.
Then
Y_{n}=\Span(Y\cup\{x_{j}\mid j\leq n\})
defines an increasing sequence of linear subspaces of
X
with dense union. Let
Y_{0}=Y
and
\psi_{0}=\varphi
. The standard proof of the Hahn–Banach theorem shows that for every
n
and linear functional
\psi_{n}
on
Y_{n}
there is a closed interval
[\alpha,\beta]
in
\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\gamma\in[\alpha,\beta]
,
\psi_{n+1}(y+tx_{n})=\psi_{n}(y)+t\gamma
extends
\psi_{n}
to
Y_{n+1}
and satisfies
\|\psi_{n}\|=\|\psi_{n+1}\|
. Use
\gamma=\alpha
to define
\psi_{n+1}
. This defines
\psi
on
\bigcup_{n}Y_{n}
, and
\psi
extends
to
X
by continuity.

(
2
) For the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, note that the evaluation map from
\mathbf{B}(X^{*})
into
\mathbb{D}^{n}
(where
\mathbb{D}
denotes the closed unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
) defined by
\varphi\mapsto(\varphi(x_{n}))
is, by the standard proof, a homeomorphism onto its range. Since
\mathbb{D}
is homeomorphic to
[0,1]^{2}
, and since
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem (no choice needed, cf.
[
Bla25
, XII.11.6.9]
),
\mathbb{D}^{\mathbb{N}}
is compact. The range of the evaluation map is closed, hence compact.

(
3
) The standard metric on
\mathbf{B}(X^{*})
,

d(\varphi,\psi)=\sum_{n}2^{-n}|\varphi(x_{n})-\psi(x_{n})|

is as required, by the standard proof.
\mathbf{B}(X^{*})
is second countable, hence separable by

[
Bla25
, XII.15.9.8]
.

(
4
) and (
5
): While we don’t know ordinary proofs of these assertions that do not use the Axiom of Choice, we can prove that a proof exists. See
6.1.6
and and
6.1.5
.

(
6
): The Baire Category Theorem for separable complete metric spaces
requires no Choice. The proofs of the Open Mapping Theorem and Uniform Boundedness
Theorem (second proof) as written in
[
Bla25
]
require no additional choice
(in the proof in XVIII.8.2.5 the
x_{n}
can be systematically chosen from a dense sequence),
and the Closed Graph Theorem is a simple corollary of the Open Mapping Theorem.

If
X
is just a separable normed space, then the completion
\bar{X}
is also separable,
and
X^{*}=\bar{X}^{*}
, so (1)–(5) (not (6)!) hold for
X
. But there is a subtlety: in
\bar{X}
is
separable, it is not obvious that
X
is also separable (even if
X
is an inner product space).
In this case, (1)–(5) still hold for
X
.

The proof of Proposition
2.0.1
(
1
) shows the following.

2.0.2

Proposition.

Suppose that
X
is a Banach space with a well-ordered dense subset. Then the Hahn–Banach extension theorem holds for
X
. ∎

Note also the following special case of
[
Bla25
, XII.15.9.8]
:

2.0.3

Proposition.

Let
X
be a separable Banach space. Then every closed subset (e.g. closed subspace) of
X

is separable. In particular, every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is separable.

2.1.
Extensions

2.1.1

If
X
is a Banach space,
Y
a closed subspace, and both
Y
and
X/Y
are separable, is

X
necessarily separable? Without Choice, the answer is probably no in general: a weak
cross-section property is needed (which is automatic under Countable AC):

2.1.2

Proposition.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
Y
a closed subspace of
X
. Then
X
is separable if and only
if
Y
is separable and there is a countable subset
C
of
X
whose image in
X/Y
(with the
quotient norm) is dense.

Proof:

If
X
is separable, then
Y
is separable (
2.0.3
), and a countable dense set
in
X
works for
C
. Conversely, let
C
be such a subset of
X
, and
D
a countable dense
subset of
Y
. Then
C+D
is countable. Let
\pi:X\to X/Y
be the quotient map. Let
x\in X

and
\epsilon>0
. Then there is a
c\in C
with
\|\pi(x)-\pi(c)\|_{X/Y}=\|\pi(x-c)\|_{X/Y}<\epsilon
.
From the definition of the quotient norm and density of
D
in
Y
, there is a
d\in D
with

\|(x-c)-d\|_{X}=\|x-(c+d)\|_{X}<\epsilon

and thus
C+D
is dense in
X
.

2.1.3

Thus for
X
to be separable, one needs (in addition to separability of
Y
) a countable dense subset
of
X/Y
on which there is a cross section in
X
. Note that there will always be such a dense
set if
X/Y
is finite-dimensional (one only needs to take a lift of a basis; recall that finitely
many choices can be made without any form of Choice).

2.1.4

Thus it is unclear (without Countable AC) whether if
A
is a C*-algebra,
J
a closed ideal, and
J
and
A/J
are
separable, then
A
is necessarily separable; it is true if
A/J
is finite-dimensional.

2.2.
Existence of states

2.2.1

Recall that a
state
on a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is a positive linear functional of norm 1. Without the Hahn–Banach Theorem it is not automatic that there are enough states on a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra to determine the norm: for example, if all sets of reals have the Property of Baire (e.g., if the Axiom of Determinacy is assumed or in Solovay’s model in which all sets of reals are Lebesgue measurable,
[
Sol70
]
), neither the commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N})/c_{0}(\mathbb{N})
nor the Calkin algebra have any states at all (see
[
BFKar
, Corollary 8.4.3]
and
[
Far19
, Exercise 12.6.6]
), respectively).

2.2.2

Continuing
2.2.1
, one may object that Solovay’s construction uses an inaccessible cardinal, and moreover that by a çelebrated result of Shelah (
[
She84
, Theorem 5.1]
) a model of ZF in which all sets of reals are Lebesgue measurable (or even a model in which all
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{3}
sets are Lebesgue measurable, see Definition
5.2.1
) cannot be constructed without assuming the existence of an inaccessible cardinal in some inner model. This is a problem because the theory ZFC+‘There exists an inaccessible cardinal
\kappa
’ implies that the rank-initial segment
V_{\kappa}
of the von Neumann universe
V
is a model of ZF and in particular that ZF (as well as ZFC) is consistent. Since by Gödel’s theorem ZFC cannot prove its consistency (unless it is inconsistent, in which case it also proves
0=1
), this theory is strictly stronger than ZFC. This is not a place to go into the (fascinating) subject of large cardinals and consistency strengths of extensions of ZFC. See for example
[
Kan95
]
or
[
Jec03
]
for more information.
Fortunately for us, in
[
She84
, Corollary 7.17]
it was proved that a model of the theory ZF+DC+“every set of reals has the Property of Baire’ can be constructed starting from a model of ZFC. (Here ‘reals’ stand for ‘any Polish space’ and DC is the axiom of Dependent Choices.)

2.2.3

Let us say that a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
has
sufficiently many states
if for every
x\in A
there is a state
\varphi
such that GNS representation associated with
\varphi
satisfies
\|\pi_{\varphi}(x)\|=\|x\|
. The Axiom of Choice implies that every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra has sufficiently many states, but the previous paragraph shows that this is not the case if the Axiom of Choice fails.

2.2.4

Lemma.
Every separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, and more generally every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra with a well-ordered dense subset, has sufficiently many states.

Proof:
Fix a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
. We may assume that
A
is unital. Fix
x\in A
.
We will prove that there is a state
\varphi
on
A
such that
\varphi(x^{*}x)=\|x\|
. In particular,
if
A
is a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and
x\in A
, then
x^{*}x\in A_{+}
(Proposition
2.3.9
), and it follows from functional
calculus that
\|\lambda 1+x^{*}x\|=\lambda+\|x^{*}x\|
for any
\lambda\geq 0
. Thus, if
S
is
the two-dimensional subspace of
A
spanned by 1 and
x^{*}x
, the linear functional
\phi
on
S

defined by
\phi(1)=1
and
\phi(x^{*}x)=\|x^{*}x\|=\|x\|^{2}
has norm 1.

The Hahn–Banach Theorem for separable Banach spaces does not require any Choice (Proposition
2.0.1
); in fact
\phi
has an algorithmically constructed extension to
A
of norm 1 (once a countable dense sequence is fixed). Since a linear functional
\psi
on
A
is a state if and only if it has norm 1 and
\psi(1)=1

[
Bla06
, II.6.2.5]
, we conclude that a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra has sufficiently many states.

2.2.5

In ZF one cannot prove that every complete, totally bounded, metric space is compact (see
[
Fre05
, 561Y (c)]
, also
[
FMMar
]
or
[
Bla25
, XII.15.12.9]
). We therefore include a proof for the space of states
\mathsf{S}(A)
below.

2.2.6

Lemma.

Suppose that
A
is a separable unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. Then the state space
\mathsf{S}(A)
with respect to the weak*-topology is a separable compact metrizable space.
The pure state space
\mathsf{P}(A)
is a Borel (more precisely,
G_{\delta}
, i.e.,
\Pi^{0}_{2}
in modern descriptive-set theoretic notation, see
[
Kec95
]
or
[
Bla25
, XV.3.4.1]
) subset of
\mathsf{S}(A)
.

If
A
is commutative, unital, and separable, then
X=\mathsf{P}(A)
is a compact metric space and
A\cong C(X)
.

Proof:
The state space is nonempty by Lemma
2.2.4
. The standard proof that
\mathsf{S}(A)
is a weak*-closed subset of the unit ball of the Banach space dual of
A
requires no choice. By the Banach–Alaoglu theorem (Proposition
2.0.1
(
2
) and (
3
)), the latter is a separable compact metric space.

Finally, by the Krein–Milman theorem for separable compact convex sets (a proof is provided in
6.1.6
),
\mathsf{S}(A)
is the closed linear span of the space of its extreme points, which is the space of pure states.

That
\mathsf{P}(A)
is a
G_{\delta}
is a standard result, see. e.g.
[
Bla25
, XVIII.9.5.10]
.

The proof in case when
A
is not necessarily separable but has a well-ordered dense subset is analogous and uses Proposition
2.0.2
(although
\mathsf{P}(A)
will not be Borel in general).

Finally, if
A
is unital and commutative then the standard proof that the space of characters is closed in
\mathsf{S}(A)
and that it coincides with
\mathsf{P}(A)
applies.

2.2.7

Proposition.

A separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra has a faithful state.

Proof:

We may assume
A
is unital. Then
\mathsf{S}(A)
is weak-* separable (
2.2.6
).
Let
(\phi_{n})
be a dense sequence in
\mathsf{S}(A)
, and set
\phi=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\phi_{n}
. Then
\phi
is a state on
A
. We claim that
\phi
is faithful. Fix a positive
a\in A
of norm 1.
Then there is an (algorithmically constructed) state
\psi
on
A
with
\psi(a)=1
.
Approximating
\psi
by a
\phi_{n}
, there is an
n
with
\phi_{n}(a)>1/2
, and we have

\phi(a)\geq 2^{-n}\phi_{n}(a)>2^{-n-1}>0
.

2.3.
Spectrum and continuous functional calculus

2.3.1

If
A
is a unital Banach algebra and
x\in A
, the
spectrum
of
x
is

\sigma(x)=\sigma_{A}(x)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:x-\lambda 1\mbox{ is not invertible in }A\}.

Since the invertible elements in
A
form an open set,
\sigma(x)
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{C}
.

If
A
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and
x=x^{*}\in A
, then
\sigma(x)\subseteq\mathbb{R}

[
Bla06
, II.1.6.2]
(this proof requires no Choice; see
[
Goo82
, 4.3]
for a more purely algebraic
proof which transparently requires no Choice).

We have the following theorem; see e.g.
[
Goo82
]
for a proof not requiring any Choice.

2.3.2

Theorem.

Let
A
be a unital complex Banach algebra, and
x\in A
.
Then
\sigma(x)
is nonempty and compact, and

\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}=\inf_{n}\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}\ .

2.3.3

Corollary.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra,
x=x^{*}\in A
. Then
\sigma(x)\subseteq[-\|x\|,\|x\|]
, and at least
one of
\|x\|,-\|x\|
is in
\sigma(x)
. More generally, if
x
is normal, then

\|x\|=\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}
.

Proof:

By the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-axiom,
\|x^{2^{k}}\|^{2^{-k}}=\|x\|
for any
k
.
So we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}
exists and equals
\inf_{n}\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}
, and submultiplicativity of the norm implies
\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}\leq\|x\|
for all
n
, so we have
\|x^{n}\|^{1/n}=\|x\|
for all
n
.
Thus the sequence in the statement of Theorem
2.3.2
is constant.

Combining this with the Spectral Mapping Theorem for polynomials
[
Bla25
, XVI.10.3.15]
, we obtain:

2.3.4

Corollary.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra,
x
a normal element of
A
, and
p
a polynomial with
complex coefficients. Then

\|p(x)\|=\max\{|p(\lambda)|:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}.

See
6.1.4
for a much better result, even for the case
n=1
.

2.3.5

The usual facts about continuous functional calculus of self-adjoint elements of a

-algebra
hold without any Choice.
No Choice is needed to prove the Stone–Weierstrass Theorem (cf.
[
Bla25
, XVIII.10.2.16]

Combining this with the Spectral Mapping Theorem for polynomials, we obtain:

2.3.6

Proposition.

Let
A
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, and

. Then
p\mapsto p(x)
(

extends to an isometric
isomorphism from

generated by

If

2.3.7

As usual, we say an element
x
in a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is
positive
(
x\geq 0
) if
x=x^{*}
and

\sigma(x)\subseteq[0,\infty)
. Denote by
A_{+}
the set of positive elements of

From continuous functional calculus we obtain most of
[
Bla06
, II.3.1.2]
(we
later get all of this result):

2.3.8

Proposition.

Let
A
be a (unital)
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and
x\in A
. Then

(i)

If
x\geq 0
and
-x\geq 0
, then
x=0
.

(ii)

If
x=x^{*}
, then
x^{2}\geq 0
.

(iii)

If
x\geq 0
, then
\|x\|=\max\{\lambda:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}
.

(iv)

If
x=x^{*}

, then
x\geq 0
if and only if
\|1-x\|\leq 1
.

(v)

If
x=x^{*}
and

, then

if and only if
f(t)\geq 0

for all

.

(vi)

If
x=x^{*}
there is a unique decomposition

in

with

and
x_{+}x_{-}=0
. We have

and

, where

and

is a linear combination

.

(vii)

(x^{\alpha})^{\beta}=x^{\alpha\beta}
,

,
and

is also

These parts of
[

[

, II.3.1.3]
work (with no Choice needed):

2.3.9

Proposition.

Let
A
be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. Then

(i)

A_{+}
is a closed cone in

, then

(ii)

If

.

It then follows from

We also have the following technical fact. This is usually proved using the Gelfand Representation

2.3.10

Proposition.

Let
A
be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra,
0\leq x\in A
. Then there is a unique positive

a\in A
with
a^{2}=x
.

Proof:

There is a canonical

with
a^{2}=x
(Proposition
2.3.8
(vii)). It remains to prove uniqueness of the square root in
A
.
Since
a
is a uniform
limit of polynomials in
x
, it commutes with any element of
A
which commutes with
x
.
If
b
is any positive element of

with

, then
b
commutes with

and hence
with

. Thus, replacing

with
C^{*}(a,b)
, we may assume

is commutative. Since

0=a^{2}-b^{2}=(a-b)(a+b)

we have
a-b\perp a+b
, and thus
a-b\perp(a+b)^{1/2^{n}}
for any

. But

and

, hence
a-b
,
are in the hereditary
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of

generated by

(which is positive), and
\{(a+b)^{1/2^{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an
approximate unit for this hereditary

.

2.3.11

We can then get uniqueness (in
A
) of the elements
x^{\alpha}
of Proposition

(vii) for all

\alpha
(i.e. if

there is a unique

with

uniqueness follows from

if

is a dyadic rational, and then for
all
\alpha
by continuity, as follows. If in addition

, if
\beta
is close to 1 and
\alpha\beta

is dyadic rational, then
(c^{\beta})^{1/\alpha\beta}=x=(b^{\beta})^{1/\alpha\beta}
, so

. Letting

, we obtain

.

2.3.12

So if
x\in A
, then Proposition
2.3.8
(vi) implies that

can be canonically written

where

, with

continuous functions of

. Thus, if

is separable and
(x_{n})
is a dense sequence,
write

; then

is dense in

, so

is closed in

, hence separable by

2.0.3
.

2.4.
Continuous functional calculus for
n
-tuples of commuting normal operators

2.4.1

It is not obvious whether functional calculus extends to normal elements. The special thing about a self-adjoint element

is that if
p
is a complex polynomial, then
[p(x)]^{*}=\bar{p}(x)
is another polynomial
in
x
, so the closure in
A
of the polynomials in
x
is a *-algebra, which fails if

is not self-adjoint. Nevertheless, we will use a metamathematical workaround to obtain continuous functional calculus for tuples of commuting normal elements (Proposition
6.1.4
).

2.4.2

Suppose that
n\geq 1
and
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is an
n
-tuple commuting normal elements of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

. The

of

is

\jsigma(\bar{a})=\jsigma_{A}(\bar{a})=\{\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:a_{j}-\lambda_{j}1,\mbox{ for $j\leq n$, generate a proper ideal in }A\}.

The following is the Spectral Mapping Theorem for polynomials, again a theorem of ZF.

2.4.3

Theorem.
Suppose that
n\geq 1
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are commuting normal elements of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
.
Then
\jsigma(A)
is nonempty.
If

is a complex *-polynomial in
2n
variables, then

\sigma(f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{n}^{*}))\\
=\{f(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n},\bar{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\bar{\lambda}_{n})\mid(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\jsigma(\bar{a})\}.

2.4.4

A proof of the case of a single normal operator of the Spectral Mapping Theorem that does not use the Axiom of Choice is given in
[
Whi68
, Lemma 2]
. It can be modified to the case of

commuting normal operators by using induction.
We are indebted to Chris Schafhauser for sharing this proof with us.

Our proof, given in

, uses Shoenfield’s Absoluteness Theorem (Theorem

) combined with a straightforward computation of the complexity of the assertion of Theorem

.

3.
Representability, Gelfand–Naimark theorems for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras

We will show that both Gelfand–Naimark theorems for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras hold in ZF. We will also give an example showing that if a Russell set exists then there exists a unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra that is not isomorphic to
C(X)
for any compact Hausdorff space
X
.
A Russell set gives a particularly strong failure of the Axiom of Choice for countable sets.

3.0.1

Definition.

A
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is
representable
if it is isomorphic to a closed *-algebra of bounded operators
on a Hilbert space.

By the Gelfand–Naimark Theorem, which requires the Hahn–Banach Theorem, every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is representable. In fact, representability of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is equivalent to a weak form of the Hahn–Banach Theorem:

3.0.2

Proposition.

A
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is representable if and only if
A
has enough states to determine its norm, i.e.

\|x\|^{2}=\sup\{\phi(x^{*}x):\phi\in\mathsf{S}(A)\}

for every
x\in A
, where
\mathsf{S}(A)
is the set of states on
A
.

Proof:

If
A
is representable, then vector states determine the norm. Conversely, if states determine the norm,
a direct sum of GNS representations is faithful (isometric).

3.0.3

By
2.0.1
we obtain representability for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, and more, without any Choice.

3.0.4

Theorem.

Let
A
be a separable (unital)
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, and
x\in A
. Then there is a state
\psi
on
A
with

\psi(x^{*}x)=\|x\|^{2}
. In particular, the states on
A
determine the norm, so
A
is representable.

Proof:
By Proposition
3.0.2
it suffices to prove that
A
has sufficiently many states, and this was shown in Lemma
2.2.4
.

In fact, using
2.2.7
we can do better, since the GNS representation from a faithful
state is faithful:

3.0.5

Theorem.

Every separable C*-algebra has a faithful representation as a concrete C*-algebra of operators
on a separable Hilbert space.

3.0.6

For a commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
and
a\in A
let
\hat{a}
denote the evaluation function on
\mathsf{P}(A)
,
\hat{a}(\varphi)=\varphi(a)
. This is the Gelfand transform from
A
into the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(\mathsf{P}(A))
, and the standard proof that it is continuous does not require Choice (see e.g.,
[
Arv02
]
).

3.0.7

Theorem.

If
A
is a separable and commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, then the Gelfand transform

A\ni a\mapsto\hat{a}\in C_{0}(\mathsf{P}(A))

is an isomorphism. In particular, every separable commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is isomorphic to
C_{0}(X)
for some locally compact metrizable space
X
. If
A
is unital, then it is isomorphic to
C(X)
for some compact metrizable space
X
.

Proof:

We first prove the assertion with the additional assumption that
A
is unital. By Lemma
2.2.4
,
A
has sufficiently many states. Since the state space of
A
is compact and metrizable, the Krein–Milman theorem (
6.1.6
) implies that it is equal to the closed convex hull of the space of pure states of
A
. Therefore the Gelfand transformation is isometric. The image of
A
is a *-subalgebra of
C(\mathsf{P}(A))
that contains all scalars and (by definition) separates the points. Since the Stone–Weierstrass theorem can be proved without the Axiom of Choice (
2.3.5
), the map is an isomorphism as required.

If
A
is not unital, consider its unitization
\tilde{A}
. Then
\mathsf{P}(A)
is naturally identified with an open dense subspace of
\mathsf{P}(\tilde{A})
that separates the points in the image of
A
under the Gelfand map.

3.1.
A concretely representable, commutative, unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra that is not isomorphic to
C(X)
for any compact Hausdorff space
X

In
[
FMMar
, Corollary 8.4.3]
it was proved that the unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N})/c_{0}(\mathbb{N})
is, in some models of ZF, not representable on a Hilbert space and therefore not of the form
C(X)
for a compact Hausdorff space
X
. The title of this subsection describes the refinement of this result proved below.
A
Russell set
is a Dedekind–finite set that can be written as a union of countably many disjoint two-element sets (see
[
BFKar
, 5.7]
).

3.1.1

Proposition.
Suppose that there exists a Russell set
X
. Then the following holds.

(1)

Some unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is concretely representable on a Hilbert space but it is not isomorphic to
C(Z)
for any compact Hausdorff space
Z
. This algebra is an inductive limit of a sequence of finite-dimensional
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras (but it is not separable).

(2)

The state space of
A
is not compact, and has no extreme points.

Proof:

(
1
) Let
X=\bigsqcup_{n}X_{n}
be a Russell set, where each
X_{n}
is a two-element set. We first define a universal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
given by generators and relations; the existence of such algebra does not require any Choice. We will then define a state
\tau
on
A
, and the required algebra will be the image of
A
under the GNS representation associated with
\tau
.

Generators of
A
are
p_{x}
, for
x\in X
. The relations require that each
p_{x}
is a projection (that is,
p_{x}=p_{x}^{*}=p_{x}^{2}
), that for every
n
we have
\sum_{x\in X_{n}}p_{x}=1
, and that
[p_{x},p_{y}]=0
for all
x
and
y
.
Thus
p_{x}p_{y}=0
if and only if
x
and
y
are distinct and belong to the same
X_{n}
. The space of linear combinations of products
\prod_{x\in F}p_{x}
, where
F\subset X
intersects each
X_{n}
in at most one point (every such transversal is finite because
X
is a Russell set), is a dense *-subalgebra of
A
.
On such monomials define a state by
\tau(\prod_{x\in F}p_{x})=2^{-|F|}
. This defines a tracial state on
A
, and
\pi_{\tau}[A]
is a concrete commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra.

We need to prove that the pure state space of
A
is empty. Assume
\varphi
is a pure state on
A
. Then for every
n
we would have
\varphi(p_{x})=1
for some
x\in X_{n}
, otherwise
\varphi
would be a convex combination of
\psi_{x}(a)=\varphi(p_{x}a)/\varphi(p_{x})
and
\psi_{y}(a)=\varphi(p_{y}a)/\varphi(p_{y})
, where
X_{n}=\{x,y\}
.
Thus from
\varphi
one could define a subset of
X
,
\{x\in X\mid\varphi(p_{x})=1\}
that intersects each
X_{n}
in exactly one point; contradiction.

This implies that the Gelfand spectrum of
A
is empty, and therefore
A
is not isomorphic to
C(Z)
for a compact Hausdorff space
Z
.

For each
n
, let
Y_{n}=\bigsqcup_{1\leq k\leq n}X_{k}
. Then
Y_{n}
is a finite set with
2n

elements, and
X
is the increasing union of the
Y_{n}
.
Let
A_{n}
be the set of linear combinations of
\prod_{x\in F}p_{x}
, where
F\subset Y_{n}
intersects each
X_{k}
in at most one point. Then
A_{n}
is a unital C*-subalgebra of
A

isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2^{n}}
,

A_{n}\subseteq A_{n+1}
, and
\bigcup_{n}A_{n}
is dense in
A
. In fact, if
B_{n}
is the C*-subalgebra
generated by
\{p_{x}:x\in X\setminus Y_{n}\}
, then
A
is naturally isomorphic to

A_{n}\otimes B_{n}
.

(
2
) Let
\mathsf{S}(A)
be the state space of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
as in (
1
).
\mathsf{S}(A)
can be described as an “inverse limit” of finite-dimensional
simplexes (note that inverse limits are not well defined without Choice): there is an inverse
system

\cdots\to\mathsf{S}(A_{n+1})\to\mathsf{S}(A_{n})\to\cdots\to\mathsf{S}(A_{1})

where the connecting maps are given by restriction.
\mathsf{S}(A_{n})
is a
(2^{n}-1)
-simplex, and
the connecting maps send extreme points to extreme points. The space
\mathsf{S}(A)
can be
naturally put at
-\infty
, and the restriction maps to the
\mathsf{S}(A_{n})
separate points since

\bigcup_{n}A_{n}
is dense in
A
.

To see that
\mathsf{S}(A)
is not compact (in the weak-* topology), let
K_{n}
be the set of states of
A
whose restriction to
A_{n}

is a pure state. Then
K_{n}
is closed since the pure state space of
A_{n}
is closed, and
nonempty since any tensor product of a pure state of
A_{n}
and any state of
B_{n}
is in
K_{n}
.
So
(K_{n})
is a decreasing sequence of nonempty closed subsets of
\mathsf{S}(A)
, and any element of
\bigcap_{n}K_{n}
would define a choice function for
X
. Therefore
\bigcap_{n}K_{n}=\emptyset

and
\mathsf{S}(A)
is not compact.

3.1.2

There is an obvious linear map
\theta
from
A
as in Proposition
3.1.1
to the Banach space of bounded continuous
affine functions from
\mathsf{S}(A)
to
{\mathbb{C}}
with supremum norm. Since
A
is representable,
\theta
is
isometric, but it is not obvious that
\theta
is surjective (the usual proof of surjectivity
for a unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra uses various forms of Choice).

3.1.3

It is not difficult to see that the proof of Proposition
3.1.1
goes through if the assumption that each
X_{n}
is a two-element set is relaxed to asserting that each
X_{n}
is finite. It also works if the set
X
is amorphous and it can be presented as
X=\bigsqcup_{z\in Z}X_{z}
, where
Z
is strongly amorphous, and
X_{z}
are pairwise disjoint finite sets. A set with these properties was used in
[
BFKar
, 6.2.3]
to produce a Hilbert space without an orthonormal basis. We don’t know whether the space
L^{2}(A,\tau)
with
A
and
\tau
as in the proof of Proposition
3.1.1
has an orthonormal basis, or whether it is isomorphic to a subspace of a Hilbert space with an orthonormal basis.

4.
Separability of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

In the usual (Choice) case, there is a well-known theorem:

4.0.1

Theorem.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space. Then
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is norm-separable if and only if
{\mathcal{H}}
is
finite-dimensional.

4.0.2

The usual proof (or at least one standard proof) requires at least the Countable AC. But it turns
out that all use of Choice can be avoided, at the cost of some additional argument. We outline
the standard argument, and then adapt it to the non-choice setting; results of
[
BFKar
]
yield that all the subspaces and projections used
are constructible without any Choice..

One direction is clear: if
{\mathcal{H}}
has finite dimension
n
, then
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
has an
obvious explicit countable dense subset, which requires no Choice. So we need only the converse.

Under the Countable AC, any subspace of a separable metric space is separable. However, this cannot be
proved without the Countable AC. For example, a set of Cohen reals in Cohen’s symmetric model is a nonseparable subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Such set is an example of a Cohen-finite set whose existence is equivalent to some curious statements about Hilbert spaces (see e.g.
[
BFKar
, Proposition 6.0.3]
). However, we need only a special version.
Let
(X,\rho)
be a metric space, and
\epsilon>0
. A subset
\{x_{i}:i\in I\}
of
X
is

\epsilon
-discrete
if
\rho(x_{i},x_{j})\geq\epsilon
for all
i,j\in I
,
i\neq j
.

4.0.3

Every
\epsilon
-discrete
subset of a separable metric space
X
is countable.
To see this,
\{x_{i}:i\in I\}
be an
\epsilon
-discrete subset of
X
, and let
(y_{n})
be a dense sequence
in
X
. For each
i\in I
, let
n_{i}
be the smallest
n
such that
\rho(x_{i},y_{n})<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.
Then
i\mapsto n_{i}
is an injective function from
I
to
\mathbb{N}
, so
I
is countable.

4.0.4

Proposition.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space. If
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
contains a sequence of mutually orthogonal
nonzero projections, then
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is not norm-separable.

Proof:

Let
(P_{n})
be a sequence of mutually orthogonal nonzero projections in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, and let

{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
be the range of
P_{n}
. Then
({\mathcal{H}}_{n})
is a sequence of mutually orthogonal closed
subspaces of
{\mathcal{H}}
. For each
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}
be the closed span of

\{{\mathcal{H}}_{n}:n\in S\}
, and
P_{S}
the projection onto
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}
. Then
\{P_{S}:S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\}

is a 1-discrete subset of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
of cardinality
2^{\aleph_{0}}
. Since
2^{\aleph_{0}}>\aleph_{0}
,

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
cannot be separable by
4.0.3
.

Thus to finish the proof of Theorem
4.0.1
, it suffices to find a sequence of
mutually orthogonal nonzero projections in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
for
{\mathcal{H}}
infinite-dimensional. This is
easily done in the usual (Countable AC) case by taking an orthonormal sequence of vectors.
However, in the absence of the Countable AC, an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space need not
contain an orthonormal sequence, or even an infinite orthonormal set
[
BFKar
, 6.2.3]
;
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

need not even contain any sequence of mutually orthogonal nonzero projections
[
BFKar
, 8.3.2]
.
So to prove
4.0.1
, we proceed indirectly.

4.0.5

Suppose
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is separable. Let
(T_{n})
be a dense sequence
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, and for each
n
let
S_{n}=(T_{n}+T_{n}^{*})/2
be the real part of
T_{n}
. Then
the
S_{n}
are self-adjoint, and they are dense in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})_{sa}
. If
P
is a projection
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
and
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, there is an
S_{n}
with
\|P-S_{n}\|<\epsilon
.
The spectrum of
S_{n}
is contained in
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\cup(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)

and hence
\frac{1}{2}\notin\sigma(S_{n})
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function which is 0 on

\left(-\infty,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and 1 on
\left(\frac{1}{2},+\infty\right)
; then
f
is
continuous on
\sigma(S_{n})
, so we may form
P_{n}=f(S_{n})
(no Choice is needed for this
functional calculus (
2.3.6
)). Then
P_{n}
is a projection, and we have

\|P-P_{n}\|\leq\|P-S_{n}\|+\|S_{n}-P_{n}\|<\epsilon+\epsilon=2\epsilon

and thus the sequence
(P_{n})
(defined only for the
n
for which
\frac{1}{2}\notin\sigma(S_{n})
)
of projections is dense among all projections of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
.

4.0.6

If
\xi
is a nonzero vector in
{\mathcal{H}}
, let
P
be the rank-one projection onto the span of
\xi
.
For any
\epsilon
,
0<\epsilon<1
, there is an
n
for which
\|P-P_{n}\|<\epsilon
, and since

\epsilon<1
,
P_{n}
is also rank one. Thus the rank-one projections in the sequence
(P_{n})

are dense in the rank-one projections of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
.

4.0.7

Reindexing, let
(P_{n})
be a sequence of rank-one projections in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
whose closure contains
all rank-one projections. For each
n
set

Q_{n}=\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}P_{k}\ .

Then
Q_{n}
is a finite-rank projection or rank
\leq n
, and
(Q_{n})
is an increasing sequence.
The supremum of the
Q_{n}
is
I
since every vector in
{\mathcal{H}}
is in the closure of the union of
the ranges of the
P_{n}
. There are two possibilities:

(1)

The
Q_{n}
stabilize, i.e. there is an
n_{0}
such that
Q_{n}=Q_{n_{0}}
for all
n>n_{0}
.
Then
Q_{n_{0}}=I
and
{\mathcal{H}}
is finite-dimensional.

(2)

The
Q_{n}
do not stabilize, i.e. by passing to a subsequence they strictly increase.
In this case, the projections
Q_{n+1}-Q_{n}
form a sequence of mutually orthogonal nonzero
projections in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, contradicting that
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is separable (
4.0.4
).

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.0.1
with no use of Choice.

4.0.8

We actually showed a little more than necessary: the projections
Q_{n+1}-Q_{n}
have rank one,
i.e. if
{\mathcal{H}}
is infinite-dimensional and
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is separable, then it contains a sequence of
mutually orthogonal rank-one projections whose ranges span
{\mathcal{H}}
(which then contradicts
4.0.4
).
This, however, does not directly show that
{\mathcal{H}}
is separable without the Countable AC: to obtain
an orthonormal sequence, one would have to pick a unit vector in the range for each
n
.
In fact, without the Countable AC there is a nonseparable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
with such a
sequence of mutually orthogonal rank-one projections
[
BFKar
, 6.2.3, 6.2.4]
. We do not know how to
directly prove without Choice that if
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is separable, then
{\mathcal{H}}
is separable; of course
this follows indirectly from our proof that
{\mathcal{H}}
must be finite-dimensional.

4.0.9

After the first author proved Theorem
4.0.1
, the second author realised that he had already seen the method for effectively finding a dense set of projections in
[
Kec98
]
and even used it in a proof that the
K_{0}
group of a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is Borel-computable (
[
FTT14
, Lemma 3.13(4)]
).

5.
Absoluteness

5.0.1

In this section we introduce set-theoretic absoluteness, but only after we emphasize that no foul play is involved in the ongoing discussion. The following Lemma provides an algorithm for finding an ‘honest’ proof of any statement proved using absoluteness. As the algorithm is wildly inefficient and yields a formal proof which is likely to be incomprehensibly complicated, we state this fact only for reference.

5.0.2

Lemma.
If there is a proof that a statement
\varphi
is provable in ZF (or any other recursively axiomatizable theory
T
), then there is an algorithm for finding a proof of
\varphi
in ZF (or
T
).

Two standard Borel spaces are Borel-isomorphic if and only if they have the same cardinality, which is either countable or

5.3.3

An exercise?
As an illustration, we state what may appear as an innocent–looking ‘exercise’ in real analysis, but is much more than that.
Consider a subset of

5.4.
ZF and its models

The following discussion is well known to logicians and can be found in all sufficiently advanced books in logic in one form or another.
Every mathematical statement
\varphi
that can be formalized within ZF (to the best of our knowledge, this includes all mathematical statements) either has a proof in ZF or the completeness theorem of the first-order logic implies that there is a model of ZF+
\lnot\varphi
. The latter cannot be taken as an indication that ZF+
\lnot\varphi
is necessarily a reasonable theory.

For example, if
\varphi
is the Gödel sentence that codes the assertion ‘ZF is consistent’, then
\varphi
has a proof in ZF if and only if it is false. Thus, if ZF has a model (i.e., if it is a consistent theory), then it has a model
M
in which
\lnot\varphi
holds. Such
M
contains a nonstandard natural number, a code for a ‘proof’ of inconsistency of ZF.
In particular,
M
is not well-founded (and even its set of natural numbers is not well-founded). Sentences consistent with ZF that hold only in ill-founded models are generally considered to be false, albeit not provably false in ZF; see the last paragraph of
6.3.5
for additional examples.

5.4.1

Most natural models of ZF are the ones that are transitive. A model
M
of ZF is
transtive
if the ‘
\in

M
is interpreted in the natural way and every element of
M
is a subset of
M
.
While models obtained by the Löwenheim–Skolem method are rarely transitive, every well-founded model of ZF is isomorphic to one that is transitive via the
Mostowski collapse
(see e.g.,
[
Far19
, Theorem A.6.2]
).
Every transitive model is correct about the set of natural numbers. Since a proof of the inconsistency of ZF (or of any other statement) can be coded by a natural number, a transitive model cannot satisfy the sentence asserting that ZF is inconsistent.

5.4.2

If
\varphi(x,\bar{a})
is a first-order formula of the language of ZF with parameters
\bar{a}

If

\mathsf{A}^{M}=\{b\in M\mid\varphi(b,\bar{a})^{M}\}.

5.4.3

A convention.

Among set theorists, the reals are commonly identified with the Cantor set (which is identified with
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
) or the Baire space

5.5.1

Lemma

5.5.2

Definition.

If
\mathsf{X}

5.5.3

Lemma.
If
M
is a transitive model of ZFC and

Since

is still an infinite branch in

is well-founded in

is an ordinal of cardinality no greater than

sets can fail even between models of ZFC that contain all ordinals. Absoluteness for all
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{3}
sets, and even all projective sets, can also be achieved, but for a more restrictive class of models and under the assumption of the existence of large cardinals (see
[
Kan08
]
).

5.6.
Coding

5.6.1

If
M\subseteq N
are transitive models of ZF, then a Borel set in
M
is not necessarily a Borel set in
N
. This is for example the case if
M
is the Gödel’s universe
L
and
N
is Cohen’s model in which the Continuum Hypothesis fails. Then
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the set of real numbers in
M
, is an uncountable set of reals in
N
whose cardinality is
\aleph_{1}<2^{\aleph_{0}}
and therefore it (or any of its uncountable Borel subsets) cannot be Borel in
N
.

Coding of Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
or of any other Polish space was developed in
[
Sol70
]
, but we will not need its full power. The Baire space
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
has a natural code, the tree
{\mathbb{N}}^{<\mathbb{N}}
of all finite sequences of natural numbers. Thus the interpretation of
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
in a transitive model
M
of ZF is
({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{M}
, the set of all branches of the tree
{\mathbb{N}}^{<\mathbb{N}}
that belong to
M
.

Separable

6.1.1

We provide an alternative proof of Lemma

Proof:

6.1.2

We can now give a proof of the Spectral Mapping Theorem for polynomials (Theorem

Proof:

By replacing

\textstyle B=\{(\bar{\lambda},\bar{b})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times D^{n}\mid\|\sum_{j\leq n}b_{j}(a_{j}-\lambda_{j}1)-1\|<1\}.

\bar{\lambda}\in\jsigma(\bar{a})

\sum_{j}d_{j}(a_{j}-\lambda_{j}1)=1

It remains to prove that if

\sigma(f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{n}^{*}))\\

6.1.5

We can now give a proof of the Hahn–Banach separation theorem promised in
2.0.1
.

Proof:
Fix disjoint convex subsets
A
and
B
of the dual ball of a separable normed space

such that

is open. By replacing
B
with its closure, we may assume

In the following we write

for the

and

The duality map from

6.1.6

We conclude this section with a proof of the Krein–Milman theorem promised in

.

Proof:

Suppose that

is a compact convex subset of the dual of a separable Banach space
X
.
By the Banach–Alaoglu theorem,

is a compact metrizable space. Fix a compatible metric

The extreme boundary of

\partial K=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{x\mid x=(y+z)/2\text{ implies }d(y,z)<1/n\}.

This is clearly a

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
(it involves a code for

Absoluteness Theorem.

This proves that the assertion ‘Every compact convex subset of the dual space of a separable Banach space has an extreme point’ is absolute between transitive models of ZF that contain all countable ordinals. In order to prove that every separable compact convex set in a locally convex space is the closed convex hull of its extreme points, assume otherwise. Consider the closed convex hull

of

and fix

Other applications

We record some other applications of Shoenfield’s Absoluteness theorem.

6.2.1

We will first need some standard facts about coding separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. A discussion analogous to the following applies to Banach spaces, Banach algebras, and separable metric structures in general.
A Borel space of concrete separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras was defined in
[
Kec98
]
. The space
\hat{\Xi}
of abstract separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras defined in
[
FTT14
, §2.4]
will be more convenient for our purposes.
Its elements are countable
(\mathbb{Q}+i\mathbb{Q})
-algebras equipped with a norm and involution.
As noted in
[
FTT14
, p. 8]
, axioms for being a normed *-algebra and the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-equality correspond to Borel conditions. By induction on quantifier rank one proves an analogous statement for every condition in continuous logic
(
[
BYBHU08
]
, as adapted to
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and other not necessarily bounded metric structures in
[
FHL+21
]
, see also
[
Har23
]
).
Therefore, for every sentence
\varphi
in the language of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, the set of codes in
\hat{\Xi}
that correspond to separable algebras
A
such that
\varphi^{A}=0
is Borel.

This implies the following.

6.2.2

Lemma.

If
{\mathcal{P}}
is a property of separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, and it is provable
in ZF
that
{\mathcal{P}}
is axiomatizable, then the set of codes in
\hat{\Xi}
that correspond to algebras that satisfy
{\mathcal{P}}
is Borel. ∎

The assumption that the axiomatizability is provable in ZF (and not merely in ZFC) is not vacuous; see Proposition
6.3.3
below.

6.2.3

Recall that a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is nuclear if and only if for every
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
the algebraic tensor product
A\odot B
has a unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-norm and that it satisfies CPAP (completely positive approximation property) if the identity map on
A
is nuclear, meaning that for every
\varepsilon>0
and every finite
F\subseteq A
there is
m
and completely positive maps
\varphi:A\to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
\psi:M_{m}(A)\to A
such that
\max_{a\in F}\|\psi\circ\varphi(a)-a\|<\varepsilon
.
Nuclearity and CPAP are equivalent in ZFC (see e.g.,
[
Bla06
, Lemma IV.3.1.6]
), but we don’t know whether they are equivalent in ZF.

6.2.4

Lemma.
For each of the following properties of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, the set of codes of separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras that satisfy it is Borel:
Being finite, stably finite, purely infinite and simple.

The set of codes for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras that have a tracial state is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

The set of codes for separable
nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
.

The set of codes for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras with CPAP is Borel.

Proof:
For finite, stably finite, and purely infinite and simple one only needs to observe that the defining property of the class is already first order; see the proof of
[
FHL+21
, Theorem 2.5.1]
.

Let
A
be a code for a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra.
Since the space
\mathsf{S}(A)
of states of
A
is a compact metrizable space (Lemma
2.2.6
) and the evaluation function is continuous, the space of all tracial states of

is closed and the assertion that it is nonempty is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

The class
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras with CPAP is Borel by a result of Effros (
[
Kec98
, Theorem 1.2]
). This result is stated using the equivalence of nuclearity and CPAP, but from the proof it is clear that CPAP is used. An alternative proof of this, and Borelness of related approximation properties (such as for example small nuclear dimension) can also be extracted from
[
FHL+21

For nuclearity, fix a (code for) a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A

Then
A
is not nuclear if and only if there is a C*-algebra

with
\|\cdot\|_{\max}\neq\|\cdot\|_{\min}
on

with
\|z\|_{\max}>\|z\|_{\min}

\|\cdot\|_{\max}\neq\|\cdot\|_{\min}
on

Therefore, we may assume

is separable. Since
\|\cdot\|_{\min}
is the norm in the GNS-representation by states of the form
\psi\otimes\eta

\psi
and
\eta
are faithful states of

and

(B,x,\varphi)
where

is a code for a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra,

is Borel.
Therefore, the set of codes for non-nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is, as the projection of this set,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}

This implies that the set of codes for nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
as claimed.

6.2.5

Theorem
6.2.6
below implies that the trace problem and the UCT problem are absolute between transitive models of ZFC. It however does not imply that one or both of these statements cannot be independent from ZF, or even ZFC. See the discussion in
6.3.5
.
As pointed out in the paragraph preceding Lemma

, we do not know whether in ZF one can prove that CPAP is equivalent to nuclearity.

6.2.6

Theorem.
Each one of the following statements is equivalent to a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
statement and therefore absolute between transitive models of ZF.

(1)

Every separable, stably finite,
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra has a tracial state.

(2)

Every separable, nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra satisfies the Universal Coefficient Theorem (UCT).

(3)

Every separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra with CPAP satisfies the Universal Coefficient Theorem (UCT).

Proof:

By Lemma
6.2.4
, the set of codes in
\hat{\Xi}
corresponding to separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras that are stably finite and have no tracial state is Borel. Thus the assertion that this set is empty is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
, hence absolute between transitive models of ZF by Theorem
5.5.4
.

The set of codes for separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras with UCT is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
by
[
PST26
, Theorem 3.27]
. By Lemma
6.2.4
, (
3
) asks whether a concrete Borel set is included in a concrete

set and (

) asks whether a concrete
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
set is included in a concrete
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
set. Thus each of these two statements is equivalent to asserting that every code for a separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra belongs to a certain
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
set. Such statement is equivalent to a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
statement by Lemma
5.5.5
, and therefore absolute for transitive models of ZF by Theorem
5.5.4
.

By Lemma
6.2.4
, codes for nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras form a
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
set.
Therefore the set of all Kirchberg algebras with the same K-theory as
{\mathcal{O}}_{2}
is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
, and the assertion that every two
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras in this class are isomorphic is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{2}
.
Hence the assertion ‘all separable nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras belong to the UCT class is
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{1}_{2}
’, and it is absolute for transitive models of ZF that contain all countable ordinals by Shoenfield’s Absoluteness Theorem.

6.3.
Reflection and

6.3.1

In the presence of the Axiom of Choice, many properties of nonseparable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras reflect to separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebras (see
[
Bla06

, and also
[
Far19
, §7.3]
). These facts hinge on the directed system
\Sep(A)
of separable

-subalgebras of

being
\sigma

[

6.3.2

One cannot prove that
\Sep(A)
is
\sigma

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. For example, For the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

constructed from a Russell set in Proposition

(and many examples in
[

is not
\sigma

Compare the following with Lemma
6.2.4
. DC stands for the axiom of Dependent Choices.

6.3.3

Proposition.

While it is provable in ZFC that having a tracial state is axiomatizable, this is not provable in ZF+DC.

Proof:

The property of having a tracial state is axiomatizable in continuous logic by
[
FHL+21
, Theorem 2.5.1]
. This proof uses abstract characterization of axiomatizability via ultraproducts.

We will prove that in some model of ZF there are elementarily equivalent
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras
A
and
B
such that
B
has a tracial state and
A
does not. Once proved, this will imply that having a tracial state is not axiomatizable.
As pointed out in
2.2.1
, (see also
2.2.2
) the commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A=\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N})/c_{0}(\mathbb{N})
has no states (tracial or otherwise) if all sets of reals have the Property of Baire. A model with this property in which DC holds was constructed starting from a model of ZFC in
[
She84
, Corollary 7.17]
.
Since DC implies AC

, in this model

is
\sigma
-complete and therefore Löwenheim–Skolem Theorem (
[
Far19
, Theorem 7.1.9]
) implies that
A
is elementarily equivalent to some of its separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebras. Every commutative, separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
is isomorphic to
C(X)
for a compact metrizable space
X
(Lemma

) and in particular has tracial states.

On the other hand, we have the following.

6.3.4

Theorem.
The statement ‘Every unital stably finite
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra has a tracial state’ is absolute between transitive models of ZFC that contain all countable ordinals.

Proof:
It suffices to prove (using AC) that if there is a stably finite
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra without a tracial state then there is one that is in addition separable.
If
A
is a unital C*-algebra, and every separable unital C*-subalgebra
B
has a tracial state

\tau_{B}
, extend
\tau_{B}
to a state
\phi_{B}
on
A
; then any cluster point of the net
(\phi_{B})

is a tracial state on
A
.

6.3.5

Theorem
6.2.6
and Theorem
6.3.4
imply that the standard set-theoretic methods such as the use of forcing or axioms such as the Continuum Hypothesis, or any other methods used in
[
Far19
]
, cannot obtain independence of the statement from Theorem
6.3.4
. This is because these methods result in transitive models that contain all ordinals.
However, an absolute statement may be independent from ZF or even ZFC. For example, Gödel’s sentence for ZFC is absolute between transitive models of ZF (or even between models of ZF that contain no nonstandard integers), in spite of being independent from ZFC. The point here is that (i) its independence cannot be proved by standard set-theoretic methods such as passing to inner models or to forcing extensions and (ii) unless ZFC is inconsistent, Gödel’s sentence is true.
Since completeness of first-order logic implies that for every statement consistent with ZFC there is a model of ZFC in which this statement holds, this implies that for some statements all such models are ill-founded. Proving that such a concrete statement is independent from ZFC would require methods so novel that such proof would be even more interesting from the point of view of set theory then its resolution would be from the point of view of the theory of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

That said, there are a few examples of absolute, but independent, mathematical statements.
In
[
PH77
]
a finitary consequence of Ramsey’s theorem (and therefore of ZFC) was isolated that is expressible in the language of Peano arithmetic, but not provable in it (see also
[
Fri71
]
).
For statements in analysis with similar properties see
[
For20
]
and
[
BCLPG20
]
.
