Cardiopulmonary bypass elevates the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and reduces lymphocyte counts: a comparison of on-pump versus off-pump CABG
Authors: Xiong-fei Xia, Yun-chen Zhu, Liang Zhang, Rui Bao, Dai Li, Xiao-ming Deng, Jin-jun Bian, Bai-ling Li, Jia-feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effects of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) on peripheral white blood cell counts, neutrophil surface marker expression, and neutrophil function by comparing patients undergoing on-pump CABG (ONCAB) versus off-pump CABG (OPCAB), and to identify CPB-related immunological and inflammatory alterations.MethodsPatients undergoing on-pump CABG (ONCAB) or off-pump CABG (OPCAB) were recruited. Blood samples were collected preoperatively and at 24 h postoperatively. The primary outcome was the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) measured at 24 h after surgery. Secondary outcomes included monocyte Human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR) expression, lymphocyte programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) expression, and neutrophil surface markers (CD11b, CD18, CXCR2, CD35, CD63, CD66b, CD88, and programmed cell death-ligand 1). Neutrophil apoptosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and plasma inflammatory mediators (interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-10) were also measured. Postoperative clinical outcomes and laboratory parameters were recorded.ResultsData from 36 ONCAB patients and 18 OPCAB patients were analyzed. Postoperative NLR was significantly higher in the ONCAB group than in the OPCAB group (16.6 ± 6.1 vs. 13.1 ± 3.9; p = 0.015). This difference was attributable to lower lymphocyte counts in the ONCAB group (0.7 ± 0.2 vs. 0.8 ± 0.2 × 109/L; p = 0.002), whereas neutrophil counts did not differ significantly between groups. In both groups, monocyte HLA‑DR expression decreased and lymphocyte PD-1 expression increased after surgery. CPB did not result in significant alterations in neutrophil adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation markers, ROS production, or apoptosis. ONCAB patients had higher postoperative levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), and procalcitonin (PCT), as well as longer durations of mechanical ventilation and intensive care unit stay.ConclusionsCPB elevates the NLR after CABG primarily, accompanied by reduced level of lymphocyte count, but not neutrophil count or molecular markers of neutrophil functional. These results might reflect a higher risk of short-term complications after on-pump CABG.

Background

Coronary heart disease remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and its prevalence has continued to increase over recent years [1]. Despite advances in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) techniques, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the main revascularization therapy for many patients. Compared with PCI, CABG has been associated with lower incidences of stroke and repeat revascularization [2]. Although fractional flow reserve (FFR)-guided PCI may provide more accurate assessment of coronary hemodynamics, there is little evidence supporting the superiority of PCI over CABG. The five-year follow-up results of the FAME 3 trial demonstrated that CABG was associated with a reduced incidence of myocardial infarction and a lower need for repeat revascularization [3].

CABG can be performed with or without the assistance of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). CPB is a non-physiologic circulatory system that may induce systemic inflammatory responses and inflammatory organ dysfunction. On the other hand, on‑pump CABG (ONCAB) provides a stable surgical field without interference from cardiac motion or blood flow, which may facilitate successful graft anastomosis. A meta-analysis reported that off-pump CABG (OPCAB) might increase mid-term coronary reintervention and long-term mortality [4]. Therefore, ONCAB remains the preferred approach for many surgeons treating multi-vessel coronary artery disease.

CPB-induced inflammatory responses are a major concern in ONCAB. Inflammation is often accompanied by immune suppression due to the depletion of pro-inflammatory mediators and the activation of anti-inflammatory responses [5]. Consequently, due to surgical trauma and CPB‑induced systemic inflammation, which triggers neutrophil demargination from endothelial surfaces via inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‑6, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor [G-CSF] ), most patients exhibit a marked increase in neutrophil counts and a decrease in lymphocyte counts on routine blood tests after cardiac surgery with CPB [6–8]. Immunosuppression after cardiac surgery may be associated with an increased risk of infection. Our previous work demonstrated that neutrophils can induce lymphocyte apoptosis and sepsis-induced immunosuppression via programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) [9, 10]. We therefore hypothesized that CPB might induce functional impairment and PD-L1 upregulation in neutrophils. Although elevated neutrophil counts are common after cardiac surgery and are generally well tolerated, in some patient subsets a persistently high neutrophil‑to‑lymphocyte ratio has been associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, including infection and prolonged ICU stay [11, 12]. Accordingly, this study was designed to: (1) compare the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) between ONCAB and OPCAB patients; (2) determine whether CPB induces alterations in neutrophil functional markers (adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation, ROS production, apoptosis) and immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1); and (3) explore whether any observed changes are associated with early postoperative clinical outcomes. By directly comparing ONCAB (with CPB) and OPCAB (without CPB), we aimed to isolate the specific effects of CPB from those of cardiac surgery itself. Establishing whether CPB independently alters neutrophil function or primarily affects lymphocyte counts will help guide future immunomodulatory therapies—for example, whether to target neutrophil activation or to preserve lymphocyte function—and may ultimately reduce postoperative infections and organ dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Study design and the participants

A prospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University between September 2023 and November 2024. Patients diagnosed with coronary heart disease who were scheduled for elective CABG under general anesthesia were enrolled. All participants provided written informed consent. The trial protocol, case report forms (CRFs), study records, and informed consent forms were jointly developed by the investigators and sponsors in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, and relevant regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee.

Adult patients scheduled for elective ONCAB or OPCAB were included. Exclusion criteria were: CABG combined with any other surgical procedure; emergency surgery; severe left ventricular dysfunction (preoperative ejection fraction < 30%); recent myocardial infarction (< 3 months); reoperation; autoimmune diseases; preoperative severe liver, lung, kidney, or other vital organ failure; malignant tumors; active inflammation; or infection. Patients were also excluded if substantial amounts of data were missing.

Anesthesia and surgery management

Routine preoperative fasting and fluid restriction were implemented. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive and invasive blood pressure monitoring, and pulse oximetry. Peripheral and central venous access were obtained for infusion of lactated Ringer’s solution. All patients received a standardized anesthesia protocol. Anesthesia was induced with midazolam, etomidate, rocuronium, and sufentanil, and was maintained with sevoflurane inhalation combined with continuous infusions of dexmedetomidine, sufentanil, and cisatracurium. Intraoperative monitoring included ECG, pulse oxygen saturation (SpO₂), arterial blood pressure (ABP), central venous pressure (CVP), pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), nasopharyngeal temperature, and rectal temperature. Dose adjustments were made by the attending anesthesiologist based on clinical judgment. After induction of anesthesia, routine disinfection and draping were performed, followed by median sternotomy. The surgical procedure adhered to a standardized protocol. All patients were routinely transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for postoperative monitoring and recovery.

CPB was performed using a non-pulsatile roller pump, a hollow-fiber oxygenator with an integrated heat exchanger, an arterial filter, an open-heart surgical reservoir, and a polyethylene tubing system. Mild hypothermia (34–35 °C) and hemodilution were applied. Systemic heparinization (4 mg/kg) was administered, and CPB was initiated when the activated clotting time reached 480 s. The bypass flow was maintained at 2.4 L/min/m², with a mean arterial pressure of 50–80 mmHg and a hematocrit of 18%–25%. Cardiac arrest and myocardial protection were achieved by antegrade and retrograde perfusion of cold (8–12 °C) Del Nido cardioplegic solution prepared by the hospital pharmacy. The solution consisted of 1000 mL of multiple electrolyte injection (MEI) supplemented with 4 mL of 50% MgSO₄, 16.3 mL of 20% mannitol, 13.3 mL of 15% KCl, 15 mL of 8.4% NaHCO₃, and 6.5 mL of 2% lidocaine. The initial dose was 1000 mL, infused at a rate of 300–400 mL/min and a pressure of 150–180 mmHg. A second dose of 500 mL was administered after 90 min if necessary. No blood was added to the solution.After weaning from CPB, protamine was administered to neutralize heparin. For OPCAB, the activated clotting time was maintained above 180 s. Mechanical stabilization of the coronary artery dissection area was achieved using a suction stabilizer, and a soft plastic coronary artery shunt was inserted to maintain distal blood flow, reduce myocardial ischemia, and improve visualization of the anastomosis site.

Outcome

Demographic data were recorded, including age, sex, weight, height, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, history of hypertension or diabetes, baseline ejection fraction (EF), preoperative complete blood count, and liver and kidney function tests. The primary outcome was the neutrophil‑to‑lymphocyte ratio (NLR) at 24 h after surgery, which was compared to the preoperative NLR. 24 h after surgery was selected as the sampling time based on previous reports that CPB‑induced inflammatory and immune changes typically peak within the first postoperative day, and because this time point is a standard clinical sampling window following cardiac surgery. Secondary outcomes included: postoperative complete blood counts, arterial blood gas parameters, liver and kidney function tests; clinical outcomes such as duration of mechanical ventilation, need for renal replacement therapy, occurrence of infection, reoperation, ICU length of stay (LOS), total hospital LOS, 28-day mortality, and in-hospital mortality; plasma inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-6 [IL-6], tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], and interleukin-10 [IL-10]); immune cell phenotypes, including HLA-DR on monocytes, programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) on lymphocytes, and PD-L1 on neutrophils; and neutrophil functional markers, including CXCR2, CD35, CD63, CD66b, CD88, ROS production, and apoptotic rate.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to detect surface and intracellular molecules on white blood cells. Red blood cells were lysed with lysis buffer (BD, NJ, USA), and the remaining white blood cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD3, CD14, CD15, PD-L1, PD-1, HLA-DR, CXCR2, CD35, CD63, CD66b, and CD88 (all from Biolegend, CA, USA). For ROS and apoptosis assays, neutrophils were purified as previously described. Neutrophils were stimulated with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) for 15 min and then incubated with dihydrorhodamine 123 (Absin, Shanghai, China) to assess ROS production. Apoptosis was evaluated using Annexin V (AV) and propidium iodide (PI) staining (BD, NJ, USA) after in vitro incubation of neutrophils for 21 h.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Plasma levels of TNF‑α and IL‑10 were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using PASS 18.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). Based on published literature, NLR values after ONCAB and OPCAB were reported as 20.7 ± 13.9 and 10.2 ± 4.6, respectively. With α = 0.05, β = 0.2, and a dropout rate of 15%, the required sample size for comparing NLR between ONCAB and OPCAB was 20 patients per group. Because ONCAB was more frequently performed at our center, patients were recruited in a 2:1 ratio, yielding target sample sizes of 40 for ONCAB and 20 for OPCAB.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD); non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons of baseline characteristics were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. For postoperative laboratory parameters, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with the corresponding preoperative value as a covariate to adjust for baseline differences. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

A total of 60 patients were recruited, and none dropped out during the study. However, four patients in the ONCAB group and two patients in the OPCAB group were excluded due to excessive missing data. Thus, 36 patients in the ONCAB group and 18 patients in the OPCAB group were included in the final analysis.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA classification, preoperative EF, preoperative WBC count, red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count, total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes (Table 1).

White blood cell counts and NLR

Postoperative NLR was significantly higher in the ONCAB group than in the OPCAB group (16.6 ± 6.1 vs. 13.1 ± 3.9; p = 0.015). No significant between-group differences were observed for postoperative WBC, neutrophil, or monocyte counts. However, the postoperative lymphocyte count was significantly lower in the ONCAB group (0.7 ± 0.2 × 109/L) than in the OPCAB group (0.8 ± 0.2 × 109/L) (p = 0.002). (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Laboratory and clinical outcomes

Postoperative AST, cardiac troponin I (cTnI), and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were higher in ONCAB patients than in OPCAB patients. ONCAB patients also had a longer duration of mechanical ventilation and a longer ICU stay. Total hospital LOS was comparable between the two groups. No significant differences were observed in postoperative EF or in the incidence of complications such as infection or acute kidney injury (Table 3).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, ONCAB was associated with a significantly higher postoperative NLR than OPCAB, accompanied by reduced lymphocyte counts, but not neutrophil counts or functional markers (adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation, ROS production, apoptosis). The postoperative NLR has been demonstrated to be a marker of inflammatory stress, severity of coronary artery disease, and postoperative complications, such as postoperative atrial fibrillation [13–15]. Thus, the elevation in NLR, a clinically relevant endpoint, might imply a higher risk of complications after on-pump CABG.

The effect of CPB on the inflammatory and immunological status of patients undergoing cardiac surgery has been a long-standing concern for surgeons, anesthesiologists, and intensivists. Pro-inflammatory cytokines have been used as biomarkers for mortality or organ dysfunction in cardiac or aortic surgery [16, 17]. Thus, it seemed reasonable to explore anti-inflammatory measures, such as extracorporeal hemadsorption, to reduce the inflammatory load during cardiac surgery. Unfortunately, recent studies have failed to demonstrate any benefit of hemadsorption in reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines [18, 19]. These negative results suggest that our understanding of the inflammatory and immunological changes induced by cardiac surgery and CPB remains incomplete.

Neutrophils, as the most abundant white blood cells, provide the first line of defense against bacterial pathogens. However, they can also induce multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) under overwhelming inflammatory challenge induced by infection, such as sepsis [20]. The release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), respiratory burst, and degranulation are critical for neutrophil bacterial killing, but they are also key mediators of endothelial injury and tissue damage. CPB has been reported to activate neutrophils and contribute to organ injury after surgery; therefore, clarifying the changes in neutrophils during cardiac surgery with CPB is of potential importance. Gessler et al. reported reduced CD11b/CD18 expression and respiratory burst capacity after CPB in a pediatric population [21]. Similar results were observed by Fung et al., who reported that CPB suppressed the expression of CD16, CD18, and CD11b, as well as the respiratory burst capacity of neutrophils, while increasing CD43 expression [22]. Other studies have reported significantly increased NET levels after CPB, which were associated with postoperative complications and mortality [23, 24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that neutrophil activation after CPB may lead to organ injury, whereas the bactericidal capacity of neutrophils may be compromised due to reduced expression of adhesion molecules and respiratory burst capacity. However, a more recent study by Lesouhaitier et al. [25] reported enhanced neutrophil function and bactericidal activity against Staphylococcus aureus after cardiac surgery with CPB. In that study, neutrophil viability, migration ability, NET release, and ROS production were increased after surgery, whereas CD11b, CD18, CD35, CD63, CD88 and phagocytic capacity were unaffected. The authors concluded that neutrophils were activated after CPB and that CPB did not induce immunosuppression.

Notably, all of the above studies compared postoperative neutrophil function with preoperative baseline values within the same patients, rather than comparing patients who underwent CPB with those who did not. To address this gap, we directly compared neutrophil markers between ONCAB and OPCAB patients. In our cohort, markers of adhesion (CD11b, CD18), chemotaxis (CXCR2), degranulation (CD35, CD63, CD66b), phagocytosis (CD88), respiratory burst (ROS production), and apoptosis were not significantly different between ONCAB and OPCAB patients, although CD88 expression and ROS production were reduced postoperatively in the ONCAB group compared with preoperative values. These findings suggest that CPB does not directly alter the neutrophil functions.

Lymphocyte changes are also well recognized in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. As early as the end of the last century, it was reported that CPB may induce a decreased lymphocyte count and an impaired ability of lymphocytes to generate cytokines such as interferon-γ [26, 27]. Since neutrophilia is common in inflammatory conditions such as sepsis, we have previously shown that increased neutrophils may directly induce lymphocyte apoptosis and immunosuppression via PD-L1 [9, 10]. A similar phenomenon might be expected in CPB-induced inflammation. However, in the present study, we did not observe a significant upregulation of PD-L1 expression on neutrophils, although our data did show that lymphocyte counts were significantly reduced in ONCAB patients. Thus, CPB-induced lymphopenia may be mediated by factors other than neutrophils. Recently, it was reported that monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells can induce lymphocyte dysfunction [7]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the immunological impact of CPB is more pronounced on lymphocytes than on neutrophils. Therefore, lymphocytes may represent a more critical therapeutic target than neutrophils in ONCAB patients. However, modulating lymphocyte function during the perioperative period remains a significant clinical challenge.

Finally, our results showed that ONCAB was associated with higher levels of direct bilirubin (DBIL) and AST, as well as higher levels of creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), cTnI, and PCT. Thus, although on-pump surgery may provide better long-term outcomes for CABG patients, off-pump techniques may be associated with better short-term outcomes. The incidence of infection was not significantly different between ONCAB and OPCAB patients, but the elevation of PCT may parallel the observed reduction in lymphocyte count.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. The experience of surgeons, anesthesiologists, intensivists, and CPB technicians may have influenced the systemic effects of CPB and cardiac surgery. Second, we measured markers of neutrophil function rather than direct functional assays. A gap remains between phenotype and function. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short, and the transient changes in WBC counts may not be clinically important. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study, ONCAB was associated with a significantly higher NLR than OPCAB, and this difference was primarily driven by lymphopenia rather than neutrophilia. Markers of neutrophil adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation, ROS production, and apoptosis were not substantially altered by the use of CPB. These findings suggest that the immunological impact of CPB may be more pronounced on lymphocytes than on neutrophils.
