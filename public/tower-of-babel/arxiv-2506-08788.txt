Hidden geometric invariance in Laplace-governed magnetization problems
Yujun Shi

Abstract
For over a century, the response of magnetic materials to static fields—formulated as transmission problems of the Laplace equation, has been a standard topic in undergraduate electrodynamics jackson_classical_1999 ; Zangwill_2012 ; griffiths2014introduction ; Purcell_Morin_2013 ; vanderlinde2004classical . In the infinite permeability limit, the body becomes nearly equipotential and the internal magnetic field nearly vanishes, giving rise to magnetostatic shielding—a phenomenon in which the magnetic induction is nearly zero throughout the interior free space. While this textbook-level result has long been considered fully understood, here, we identify a previously overlooked geometric invariance that complements the quasi-equipotential picture: in the same limit, the entire external magnetic response of a body is determined solely by its outer surface geometry, regardless of internal structure or deformation, as long as the outer surface remains fixed. This surface-encoded invariance encompasses the external field distribution and all magnetic multipole moments of the body, including the dipole and quadrupole, derived as volume integrals from the internal scalar potential. It provides a theoretical foundation for the lightweight design of certain magnetic devices, such as flux concentrators. Given that analogous Laplace transmission problems arise across physics, including electricity conduction, heat conduction and acoustic scattering, this geometric invariance exhibits cross-disciplinary universality. It merits inclusion in undergraduate electromagnetism curricula, as it and the quasi-equipotential property together form a complete characterization of Laplace transmission problems in the infinite permeability limit.



Introduction

In essence, both physics and mathematics are concerned with identifying properties or quantities that remain invariant under specific classes of transformations. These invariants form the backbone of our understanding: in physics, they appear as conservation laws and symmetries, ensuring that energy, momentum, and electric charge remain constant despite motion or interaction. In mathematics, they underpin entire branches of geometry and topology—whether it be the distance between points preserved under translation or rotation, the Euler characteristic of a topological surface, or the curvature terms in the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. Invariants not only guide the formulation of natural laws and mathematical classification, but also serve as anchors of thought: Transformations let a problem breathe; Invariants reveal the spirit beneath. Motivated by a pursuit of invariants, this brief paper uncovers a remarkably simple yet robust geometric invariance, overlooked in a textbook-level, well-established problem in classical electromagnetism.

Transmission problems of the Laplace equation

The problem concerns the magnetization of a body in an external static magnetic field. Consider a compact (bounded and closed) region
\overline{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}(d=2,3)
, which representing a homogeneous, isotropic body with linear constitutive relation
\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}
, embedded in an unbounded medium with permeability
\mu_{0}
. In the absence of free currents, the magnetic field can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar potential
\phi
, such that
\mathbf{H}=-\nabla\phi
, and the magnetic induction satisfies
\mathbf{B}=-\mu\nabla\phi
. The governing equation is then
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
, which leads to a classical transmission problem for the Laplace equation. That is, the potential
\phi
satisfies

\Delta\phi^{-}=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\Delta\phi^{+}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega},

(1)

with interface conditions on the boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
,

\phi^{-}=\phi^{+}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,\qquad\mu\frac{\partial\phi^{-}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=\mu_{0}\frac{\partial\phi^{+}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

(2)

and an asymptotic condition ensuring that

\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})\to\phi^{\mathrm{bg}}(\mathbf{r})\quad\text{as }|\mathbf{r}|\to\infty,

(3)

where
\phi^{\mathrm{bg}}
is a given harmonic background potential defined in the exterior domain, representing the applied magnetic field. Equations
1
–
3
together describe how a material body perturbs a background potential field. Beyond magnetostatics, they arise naturally in a broad range of physical contexts, including electrostatics, electricity conduction, steady-state heat conduction, and acoustic scattering.

Clues to Geometric Invariance

For a general region
\Omega
with arbitrary geometry, analytical solutions of Equations
1
–
3
are often unattainable, necessitating the use of numerical approaches such as finite element methods. This naturally raises a fundamental question: do there exist geometric invariances or invariants associated with the region
\Omega
? In fact, nontrivial geometric invariances in the context of the Laplace equation are relatively rare. A celebrated example is the conformal invariance of the two-dimensional Laplace equation, which, in principle, allows potential problems in complex geometries to be reformulated in simpler domains. Identifying new invariances under broader classes of geometric transformations remains both challenging and rewarding.

In the infinite permeability limit, the body becomes nearly equipotential and the internal magnetic field
\mathbf{H}
nearly vanishes, giving rise to magnetostatic shielding—a phenomenon in which the magnetic induction
\mathbf{B}
is nearly zero throughout the interior free space. This quasi-equipotential behavior is a standard description found in nearly all classical electrodynamics textbooks addressing magnetization in the high-permeability limit. While undoubtedly correct, this description is incomplete, as it only concerns the potential field inside the region
\Omega
. But what about the potential outside, which represents the body’s external magnetic response? Since the zero point of the scalar potential is arbitrary, we can, without loss of generality, shift the reference potential so that the quasi-equipotential corresponds to a quasi-zero potential. In terms of geometric invariance, this quasi-zero potential exhibits a form of ”super-invariance”, remaining invariant under arbitrary geometric deformations of
\Omega
. Given that the interior and exterior potential fields are tightly coupled via boundary conditions on the outer surface, the exterior potential field
\phi^{+}
may likewise exhibit a form of geometric invariance. To the best of our knowledge, this possibility appears to have been overlooked in both textbooks and prior literature.

Further insight into the geometric invariance of
\phi^{+}
can be gained from the well-understood phenomenon of electrostatic shielding in conductors. The electrostatic response of a conductor to an external field is also governed by the transmission problem for the Laplace equation, albeit with different boundary conditions. Rather than the continuity conditions given by equations
2
, the boundary enforces a strict equipotential constraint:

\phi^{-}=\phi^{+}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,\qquad\phi^{-}=\text{const.}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(4)

which is equivalent to

\phi^{-}=\phi^{+}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,\qquad\frac{\partial\phi^{-}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

(5)

From the mathematical structure alone, it is evident that electrostatic shielding represents the limiting case of magnetostatic shielding. Let the conductor’s potential be zero. This exact zero-potential property constitutes a “super-invariance” under arbitrary geometric deformations of the conductor. Moreover, since the external field cannot penetrate the conductor, any alterations to the interior—such as introducing cavities or inserting structures—leave the induced outer surface charge distribution unchanged. This surface charge distribution fully determines the electrostatic response in the exterior region. Hence, the gradient of the potential
\phi^{+}
, corresponding to the electric field distribution outside, is invariant under all internal geometric transformations that preserve the conductor’s exterior surface. Since the conductor has been assigned zero potential, the potential
\phi^{+}
itself inherits this geometric invariance. However, these invariances are trivial: they are simply a restatement of the classical result that induced surface charge distribution depends solely on the geometry of the conductor’s outer surface. As such, they are often left unstated. Textbook treatments of magnetostatic shielding frequently draw analogies to electrostatic shielding. As illustrated in Fig. 1, discussions of shielding phenomena have traditionally only focused on one side of the coin: an observer inside a hollow shielded region perceives no external field. But what about the other side of the coin—what remains invisible to an external observer?

In electrostatic shielding, one cannot externally detect any internal geometric modifications that preserve the outer surface of the conductor. But in magnetostatic shielding, the situation is more subtle. Unlike the purely outer-surface-confined nature of electrostatic induction in conductors, the magnetic response depends on the volumetric distribution of magnetization within the body and is closely tied to its interior geometry. Even from the perspective of equivalent magnetic charges, nonzero magnetic charge distributions arise on both the inner and outer surfaces. Yet, as we will demonstrate, the final conclusion is parallel: an external observer cannot discern any internal geometric modifications that preserve the body’s outer surface.

The Geometric Invariance

That is, in the limit of infinite permeability, the entire external magnetic response is determined solely by the body’s outer surface geometry, regardless of any internal structure or deformation, as long as the outer surface remains fixed. This surface-encoded invariance encompasses the external field distribution and all magnetic multipole moments of the body, including the dipole and quadrupole, derived as volume integrals from the internal scalar potential.

A complete proof of the above invariance conclusion can be found in the Methods section. The core of the proof can be summarized as follows: in the limit of infinite permeability, let the body approaches a zero-potential. Under any deformation that preserves the outer surface geometry, the solution to the Laplace equation in the exterior region reduces to a Dirichlet exterior problem governed solely by quasi-zero potential prescribed on the fixed outer surface:

\begin{cases}\Delta u(\mathbf{r})=0,&\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
u(\mathbf{r})\to-\phi^{bg},&\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out}\\
u(\mathbf{r})\to 0,&|\mathbf{r}|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(6)

where
u(\mathbf{r})
is the perturbation term, defined as

\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})=u(\mathbf{r})+\phi^{bg}(\mathbf{r}).

The formal solution of the equations
6
can be composed of the integral of Green function on the boundary,

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega^{out}}dS^{\prime}u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

(7)

\displaystyle\to\int_{\partial\Omega^{out}}dS^{\prime}\phi^{bg}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}},

where
G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
is the existing and unique Green function, satisfying

\begin{cases}\Delta G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=-\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to 0,\quad|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|\to\infty,\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.&\end{cases}

(8)

Equation
7
shows that the solution to the boundary value problem depends continuously on the boundary condition. The resulting scalar potential in the exterior region converges to a unique limiting distribution. Consequently, the magnetic field
\mathbf{H}
also approaches a well-defined limiting configuration. On the other hand, the perturbation term
u(\mathbf{r})
can be expanded in terms of magnetic multipole moments associated with the magnetized body. This immediately implies that, although the internal scalar potential may vary under deformation, the corresponding magnetic multipole moments, expressed as volume integrals, remain invariant. Specifically, the dipole vector can be expressed as

\mathbf{m}=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(9)

and the traceless quadrupole tenser is

Q_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\left[M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{j}^{\prime}+M_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{i}^{\prime}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right]\quad\quad(\text{3D}),

(10)

or

Q_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}\left[M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{j}^{\prime}+M_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{i}^{\prime}-\delta_{ij}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right]\quad\quad\quad(\text{2D}).

(11)

And
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})
is the magnetization distribution, obtained from

\mathbf{M}=-(\mu_{r}-1)\nabla\phi^{-},

where
\mu_{r}
is the relative permeability of the body. Accordingly,
u(\mathbf{r})
admits a magnetic multipole expansion of the form:

u(\mathbf{r})=\quad\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[0+\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{r^{3}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{3r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{5}}Q_{ij}+...\right]\quad\quad(\text{3D}),

or

u(\mathbf{r})=\quad\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[0+\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{r^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{2r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{4}}Q_{ij}+...\right]\quad\quad(\text{2D}).

Higher-order multipole terms involve increasingly cumbersome expressions and are not presented here for brevity.

Application to Lightweight Device Design

One of the most immediate applications of the geometric invariance identified in this work lies in enabling lightweight designs of magnetic devices. A representative example is the magnetic flux concentrator (MFC), which is widely employed in magnetic sensors, energy harvesters, and wireless power transfer systems

Conclusion

In summary, we have identified a simple yet robust geometric invariance in transmission problems of the Laplace equation—one governed exclusively by a body’s outer surface, irrespective of any internal geometric variations. This is not because the interior is irrelevant, but because the boundary silently encodes all essential information. In magnetostatics, this invariance subtly challenges, or complements, the prevailing notion that a body’s perturbation of an external magnetic field is determined by its overall shape, including internal geometry. Given the ubiquity of Laplace transmission problems across physics, this geometric invariance exhibits cross-disciplinary universality. It offers a direct theoretical foundation for lightweight device design, and serves as a benchmark for verifying the accuracy of numerical solutions. Together with the quasi-equipotential property, it completes the full characterization of Laplace transmission problems in the infinite permeability limit, and therefore especially merits inclusion in undergraduate electromagnetism curricula.

Methods

We begin by formulating both the internal and external scalar potentials as boundary integrals of Green’s functions. Based on this formulation, we rigorously establish that, in the limit of infinite magnetic permeability, the internal potential approaches a quasi-equipotential state, while the external potential exhibits a geometric invariance governed solely by the boundary geometry. Finally, we derive integral expressions for the magnetic dipole moment and quadrupole tensor directly from the internal scalar potential.

.1
Proof of the quasi-equipotential and geometric invariance

In the limit of infinite permeability within the region
\Omega
, the normal derivative of the scalar potential at the boundary satisfies

\frac{\partial\phi^{-}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\mu}\frac{\partial\phi^{+}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\to 0,

implying that the problem reduces to an
interior Neumann problem
for the potential
\phi^{-}
with quasi-zero normal derivative on the boundary:

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi^{-}(\mathbf{r})=0,&\mathbf{r}\in\Omega\\
\frac{\partial\phi^{-}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=f(\mathbf{r})\to 0,&\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(12)

given the Neumann Green function which satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=-\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),&\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\Omega\\
\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{A}},&\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega,\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

(13)

where A denotes the area of the boundary surface
\partial\Omega
. The potential in
\Omega
is

\displaystyle\phi^{-}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\phi^{-}}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}-\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}\phi^{-}\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

(14)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})|_{f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to 0}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{A}}\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}\phi^{-}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\to\phi_{0},

where

\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{A}}\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}\phi^{-}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

The equations
14
show that the internal potential approaches a constant value in the infinite-permeability limit. Note that,the solution to the Laplace equation with Neumann boundary conditions is determined only up to an additive constant, reflecting a global degree of freedom inherent to the problem. Specifically, if
\phi_{0}
is a solution, then
\phi_{0}+C
, with
C
constant, is equally valid. In the presence of a background potential field
\phi^{\mathrm{bg}}(\mathbf{r})
, however, this indeterminacy propagates to the background potential. To fix the gauge, we set
\phi_{0}=0
. By continuity, the surface potential
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}
likewise tends to zero. The resulting exterior problem is thus a Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation with quasi-zero boundary values on
\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{out}}
:

\begin{cases}\Delta\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})=0,&\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})\to 0,&\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out}\\
\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})\to\phi^{bg}(\mathbf{r}),&\left|\mathbf{r}\right|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(15)

Let
\phi^{+}(\mathbf{r})=u(\mathbf{r})+\phi^{bg}(\mathbf{r})
, the perturbation term
u(\mathbf{r})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta u(\mathbf{r})=0,&\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
u(\mathbf{r})\to-\phi^{bg},&\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out}\\
u(\mathbf{r})\to 0,&|\mathbf{r}|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(16)

Given the Dirichlet Green function which satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=-\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=0,\quad\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\\
G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\to 0,\quad|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|\to\infty,\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega^{out},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.&\end{cases}

(17)

the formal solution of the equations
16
can be composed of the integral of Green function on the boundary,

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega^{out}}dS^{\prime}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\phi^{+}}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}-\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

(18)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega^{out}}dS^{\prime}u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\to\int_{\partial\Omega^{out}}dS^{\prime}\phi^{bg}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}.

Equation
18
shows that the solution to the boundary value problem depends continuously on the boundary condition. Hence, the exterior potential
\phi^{+}
converges to a unique limiting configuration, thereby establishing the geometric invariance discussed in this paper.

.2
Integral expressions for the multipole

On the other hand, the perturbation term
u(\mathbf{r})
can be expanded in terms of magnetic multipole moments associated with the magnetized body:

u(\mathbf{r})=\phi_{mono}+\phi_{dipole}+\phi_{duadrupole}+...

(19)

The perturbation term
u(\mathbf{r})
is entirely determined by the magnetization distribution within the magnetized body. From the perspective of equivalent magnetic charges, and by direct analogy with the electrostatic problem,
u(\mathbf{r})
arises from the combined contributions of volume magnetic charges and surface magnetic charges on the boundary.

.2.1
3D case

In the three-dimensional case,

u(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\frac{\rho_{m}}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right|}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}\frac{\sigma_{m}}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right|}.

(20)

By substituting
\rho_{m}=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})
and
\sigma_{m}=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{M}
,

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\frac{\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}dS^{\prime}\frac{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}.

Using the expansion

\frac{1}{\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right|}=\frac{1}{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}\frac{1}{r}+...,

(22)

we can derive the expressions for the multipole terms in
u(\mathbf{r})
as follows.

\phi_{mono}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\frac{1}{r}=0.

(23)

\displaystyle\phi_{dipole}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\left[-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{r}\right]

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\prime}}\left[-r_{\alpha}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{r_{i}}{r^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{r_{i}}{r^{3}}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{r_{i}}{r^{3}}m_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{r^{3}},

where dipole moment

\mathbf{m}=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad m_{i}=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(25)

\displaystyle\phi_{duadrupole}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}\frac{1}{r}\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\prime}}\left[\frac{1}{2}r_{\alpha}^{\prime}r_{\beta}^{\prime}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{\alpha}\partial r_{\beta}}\frac{1}{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\prime}}\left[\frac{1}{2}r_{\alpha}^{\prime}r_{\beta}^{\prime}\left(\frac{3r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta}r^{2}}{r^{5}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta}r^{2}}{r^{5}}\right)\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\prime}}\left(r_{\alpha}^{\prime}r_{\beta}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\frac{3r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta}r^{2}}{r^{5}}\right)\bar{Q}_{\alpha\beta},

where

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}^{\prime}}\left(r_{\alpha}^{\prime}r_{\beta}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\left[M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{\beta}^{\prime}+M_{\beta}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{\alpha}^{\prime}\right].

The trace of
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
is

\mathrm{Tr}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}})=2\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right].

Removing the trace from
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
to construct the traceless tensor
\mathbf{Q}
:

\displaystyle Q_{ij}

\displaystyle=\bar{Q}_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\mathrm{Tr}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}})

(27)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}dV^{\prime}\left[M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{j}^{\prime}+M_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{i}^{\prime}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right].

Then

\displaystyle\phi_{duadrupole}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\left(\frac{3r_{i}r_{j}-\delta_{ij}r^{2}}{r^{5}}\right)\left(Q_{ij}+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\operatorname{Tr}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{3r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{5}}Q_{ij}.

From the above, we obtain

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\phi_{mono}+\phi_{dipole}+\phi_{duadrupole}+...

(29)

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[0+\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{r^{3}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{3r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{5}}Q_{ij}+...\right].

Higher-order multipole terms involve increasingly cumbersome expressions and are not presented here for brevity.

.2.2
2D case

In the two-dimensional case,

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}\left[-\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right]\ln\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right|-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}dl^{\prime}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\ln\left|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right|

(30)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\left[\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|\right].

Proceeding similarly, we derive

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\phi_{mono}+\phi_{dipole}+\phi_{duadrupole}+...

(31)

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[0+\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m}}{r^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{2r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{4}}Q_{ij}+...\right],

where

\mathbf{m}=\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),m_{i}=\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

and

Q_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}dS^{\prime}\left[M_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{j}^{\prime}+M_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})r_{i}^{\prime}-\delta_{ij}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right].

(32)

Specifically, a symmetric and traceless second-order tensor
\mathbf{Q}
in two dimensions has only two degrees of freedom, and can be written as

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{xx}&Q_{xy}\\
Q_{xy}&-Q_{xx}\end{bmatrix}.
