Tumor microenvironment-responsive cerium oxide nanozyme coated ZIF-8 nanoplatform with ICG encapsulation for precise breast cancer surgery and efficacious synergistic suppression
Authors: Tingting Li, Lin Lin, Xiaofen Li, Xin Wang, Jiao Zhang, Lidan Hu, Jianqing Lin, Yuhang Li, Kaiyan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Strategically designing and utilizing nanomaterials with enhanced therapeutic capabilities offers a solution to mitigate the financial burden of treatment and address the subpar efficacy of existing breast cancer treatments. In this study, we introduce a one-step synthesis of zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) nanoparticles, where indocyanine green (ICG) is encapsulated and CeO2 nanozymes along with polyethylene glycol (PEG) are anchored onto the surface, forming ZIF-Ce&ICGP. This nanosystem presents precise tumor accumulation and responsiveness to the acidic tumor microenvironment, facilitating the intracellular release of ICG, Zn2+, and CeO2 nanoparticles from the nanocarriers. In particular, the engineered ZIF-Ce&ICGP achieves accurate delineation of tumor margins, and tumors can be completely removed under fluorescence imaging guidance. Notably, under laser irradiation, the generation of a photothermally amplified ROS storm via bimetallic catalysis effectively inhibited tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, ZIF-Ce&ICGP demonstrated extended blood circulation times and excellent biocompatibility. Overall, this research presents a promising nanoplatform for oncological therapy, expanding the utility of ZIF-8 in the realm of combinatorial cancer treatments.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be the most prevalent cancer among women, especially in developing nations. Tackling this escalating health issue will require an urgent and cohesive approach that integrates primary prevention, early detection, and superior treatment modalities through collaborative efforts across various sectors [1]. The spectrum of breast cancer treatments encompasses breast-conserving surgery and mastectomy, complemented by chemotherapy or radiotherapy to decrease the risk of recurrence [2,3]. However, the enduring side effects of chemotherapy [[4], [5], [6]] and the inherent toxicity of radiotherapy [7] present considerable challenges. Neoadjuvant therapies, capable of transforming tumors from inoperable to operable, hold the promise of streamlining treatment protocols and improving patient outcomes [7,8]. Such therapies encompass a range of options, including anticancer nanoparticles [9], immunotherapies [10], targeted therapies [11], and antibody‒drug conjugates [12]. Specifically, functional anticancer nanoparticles are known to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the tumor microenvironment, culminating in the death of cancer cells [[13], [14], [15]]. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), such as ZIF-8, offer vast potential to craft multifaceted nanoplatforms for cancer therapy [[16], [17], [18]]. Owing to its minimal systemic toxicity and pH-responsive properties, ZIF-8 has garnered widespread attention in bioimaging and cancer treatment [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Numerous researchers are now focusing on the development of ZIF-8-based nanocomposites for imaging and therapeutic applications. For instance, Zhao et al. engineered a ZIF-8 nanoplatform equipped with two-photon fluorescence and pH-responsive MR imaging capabilities [24], Zhang et al. discussed the combination of ZIF-8 and upconversion nanoparticles [25], and Lv et al. created ZIF-8-assisted nanocomposites for bioimaging [26]. In an application that leverages their pH-sensitive nature, several ZIF-8-based nanocomposites have been deployed for chemodynamic therapy (CDT) in the tumor microenvironment, where CDT can exploit the overabundance of H2O2, H+, and glutathione (GSH) to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH) [27,28]. However, the application of ZIF-8 nanocomposites in cancer treatment is constrained by the limited efficacy of CDT as a singular treatment modality, as CDT is insufficient to effectively curb tumor growth. Synergistic therapies have become a cornerstone of cancer management [29,30]. According to collision theory in thermodynamics, elevating local temperatures can increase the efficacy of Fenton and Fenton-like reactions [31], positioning photothermal therapy (PTT) as an optimal adjunctive treatment [32]. Given its significant absorption in the near-infrared spectrum, indocyanine green (ICG) stands out as a promising agent for bioimaging (beyond 1000 nm) and PTT [33]. In this context, it is valuable to synthesize ZIF-based nanoplatforms integrated with ICG for PTT and CDT; such nanoplatforms could yield synergistic and amplified therapeutic effects with promising prospects in cancer therapy.

Monometallic nanozymes can increase oxidative stress through cascading reactions that increase ROS levels while depleting GSH [34]. However, their catalytic efficiency is often insufficient for clinical use, highlighting the need for innovative catalyst designs to enhance enzymatic performance. In contrast to single-metal nanocatalysts, bimetallic nanoparticles have pronounced benefits [35,36][49]. Recently, bimetallic oxide nanoagents have been crafted to increase catalytic performance at relatively high pH levels, confirming that the synergistic effects between two types of metal ions accelerate the reduction of high-valence ions and initiate peroxidase (POD)-like reactions [37,38]. Cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles, as a nanocatalytic therapeutic, exhibit multienzyme-mimicking capabilities because of their rapid and reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ cycling, notably their ability to generate ROS from H2O2, providing robust oxidative agents in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [39,40]. The POD-like catalytic performance of CeO2 is highly dependent on the surface exposure of Ce ions, which is crucial for its various biological applications [41]. Henych et al. reported that the POD-like activity of 5 nm CeO2 nanoparticles is more than 30 times greater than that of 10 nm nanozymes [42]. Consequently, crafting CeO2 nanoparticles that are approximately 5 nm in diameter is more effective for enzymatic catalysis. Specifically, anchoring CeO2 nanoparticles of this size on the surface of ZIF-8 can intrinsically induce an ROS storm within tumor cells such that synergistic bimetallic catalytic performance can be achieved.

Motivated by these compelling findings, this study introduces a straightforward, one-step methodology to synthesize a multifunctional ZIF-8 nanoframework with encapsulated ICG dyes and surface-integrated CeO2 nanozymes measuring approximately 5 nm. After PEGylation on the MOF surface (denoted as ZIF-Ce&ICGP), this nanoplatform specifically targets breast cancer cells (4T1 cell line) and triggers the release of ICG, Zn, and Ce ions in the acidic TME (pH = 6.5) (Fig. 1). Upon cellular uptake, ZIF-Ce&ICGP effectively induces localized hyperthermia and amplifies the •OH storm from synergistic multi-ion catalysis upon NIR laser irradiation. This combination of PTT and CDT resulted in significant tumor elimination both in vitro and in vivo. More importantly, as an NIR probe, the ZIF-Ce&ICGP-based nanoplatform enables precise ICG delivery to breast carcinoma, allowing successful delineation of tumor contours. When surgery was guided by this innovative NIR-II (1000–1700 nm) fluorescence imaging system, the tumors were completely removed, with a minimal risk of local recurrence or metastasis observed up to 30 days post-surgery. Materials and methods

Synthesis of ZIF-8 and ZIF&ICG

ZIF-8 synthesis: Zinc acetate nitrate (Zn(NO3)2 •2H2O, 1 mmol) was dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water (ddH2O). Separately, 2-methylimidazole (50 mmol) was dissolved in 20 mL of ddH2O. The aqueous solution of zinc acetate was then added slowly to the 2-methylimidazole solution under intense agitation. Since ICG is more soluble in ethanol than in water, after 30 min of reaction, the resulting ZIF-8 nanoparticles were isolated via centrifugation and washed (7000 rpm, 10 min) with ethanol three times. Finally, the precipitate was resuspended in ddH2O for subsequent use. Before characterization, the sample was washed twice with ddH2O.

ZIF&ICG synthesis: To incorporate ICG dyes, Zn(NO3)2•2H2O (1 mmol) was dissolved in 2 mL ddH2O. In a separate vessel, 20 mL ddH2O was used to co-dissolve 2-methylimidazole (50 mmol) and ICG (2 mg). This mixed ligand/ICG solution was slowly added to the zinc acetate precursor under vigorous stirring. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min. The product was harvested by centrifugation and then washed with ethanol more than three times (7000 rpm, 10 min). The final ZIF&ICG nanoparticles were dispersed in ddH2O for further applications.

Preparation of the ZIF-Ce&ICG and ZIF-Ce&ICGP nanoplatforms

ZIF-Ce&ICG Synthesis: ZIF&ICG nanoparticles (100 mg) were mixed with CeCl3•6H2O (600 mg) in ddH2O (60 mL). The suspension was heated to 90 °C and maintained at that temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, hexamethylenetetramine (HMT, 155 mg) was added, and stirring was continued at 90 °C for 4 h. The resulting nanoparticles, comprising CeO2 nanozyme-anchored ZIF&ICG (designated ZIF-Ce&ICG), were retrieved by centrifugation and rinsed thoroughly with ddH2O.

ZIF-Ce&ICGP Fabrication: ZIF-Ce&ICG nanoparticles (100 mg) were combined with PEG2000 (20 mg) in ultrapure water. This mixture underwent gentle agitation at ambient temperature (25 °C) for 12 h. The PEG-functionalized nanoparticles (termed ZIF-Ce&ICGP) were collected by centrifugation, washed (7000 rpm, 10 min), and finally redispersed in ddH2O.

pH-dependent decomposition and release studies

To assess the pH-responsive degradation of ZIF-Ce&ICG, particle suspensions (200 μg/mL) were incubated at distinct pH values (5.5, 6.5, and 7.4) under ambient conditions. Samples were analysed following incubation periods of 1, 3, 6, 12, and 36 h. Changes in the morphology of the precipitates at each time point were characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

In vitro cellular uptake of ZIF-Ce&ICGP

In this work, to avoid the death of mice from intraoperative anaesthesia and postoperative infection, we used BALB/c mice and murine breast cancer cells (4T1) as the animal model and cell source, respectively. 4T1 cells were plated at 1.0–1.5 × 105 cells/mL and cultured for ≥12 h prior to treatment. Cells were exposed to free ICG (10 μg/mL), ZIF&ICGP, or ZIF-Ce&ICGP (100 μg/mL ZIF-equivalent concentration) for 7 h. Then, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (0.4–0.6 mL). Intracellular ICG localization was visualized with a Leica TCS SP5 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), with ICG detected at λex/λem = 633/700 nm and DAPI at λex/λem = 345/455 nm. Quantitative assessment of cellular ICG uptake was performed via flow cytometry.

Intracellular ROS determination

CLSM was used to assess intracellular ROS generation through ZIF-Ce&ICGP-mediated Fenton-like catalysis and Zn/Ce ion activity following cellular internalization. 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with foetal bovine serum and antibiotics. The experimental procedure consisted of ① pretreatment with 100 μM H2O2 for 2 h, ② coincubation with ZIF-Ce&ICGP, and ③ 808 nm laser irradiation (5 min, 0.8 W cm−2) after 9 h of incubation. The control groups included ZIF&ICGP with laser (ZIF&ICGP + L), free ICG with laser (ICG + L), and PBS. The concentration of ICG was 10 μg/mL, and the concentrations of ZIF&ICGP and ZIF-Ce&ICGP were both 100 μg/mL. ROS production was quantified using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCHF-DA). After 30 min of incubation, CLSM analysis was used to determine catalytic ROS levels (detected at λex/λem = 488/550 nm) within cells [[50], [51]].

Murine breast tumor model

Female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animal procedures complied with protocols approved by Fujian Medical University's IACUC (Approval #2022-CAARM035). To establish subcutaneous tumors, 4T1 cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) were injected into the caudal side of the right flank. The tumors reached ∼100 mm3 (∼10 days post-injection, suitable for therapy studies) and 200–300 mm3 (∼15 days post-injection, appropriate for NIR-II fluorescence imaging/PTT analysis).

In vivo tumor targeting

Mice bearing 4T1 tumors received intravenous injections of ZIF-Ce&ICGP, ZIF&ICGP, or free ICG. NIR-II fluorescence imaging (808 nm excitation; 1000 nm LP filter) was used to track the biodistribution at multiple timepoints. Thirty-six hours post-administration, major organs and tumors were excised for ex vivo NIR-II imaging to evaluate targeting specificity.

In vivo photothermal therapy assessment

Mice bearing subcutaneous tumors received intravenous injections of ZIF-Ce&ICGP, ZIF&ICGP, or free ICG. Twelve hours post-injection, the tumor sites were irradiated (808 nm, 1.0 W cm−2, 7 min). Throughout the irradiation period, infrared thermal imaging was performed using a FOTRIC 225s camera to monitor temperature changes.

NIR-II fluorescence-guided tumor surgery

Ten 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomized into two surgical cohorts (n = 5/group): ①In the white light group, tumors were resected under white light 12 h post-ZIF-Ce&ICGP injection (10 mg/kg). ②In the NIR-II group, tumor margins were demarcated via NIR-II imaging (λex = 808 nm, long-pass (LP) 1000 nm) 12 h post-injection, followed by resection under white light. The surgical bed was reimaged to identify and excise residual fluorescent tissue.

All excised tumors were subjected to H&E staining. Adjacent muscle/skin tissues were also histologically analysed. Postoperative tumor recurrence was assessed via bioluminescence imaging on Day 30.

In vivo tumor suppression study

Tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were randomized into four groups: ①PBS control, ②PBS + laser (PBS + L), ③ZIF-Ce&ICGP, and ④ZIF-Ce&ICGP + laser (ZIF-Ce&ICGP + L). Nanoparticles (10 mg/kg) were administered intravenously on Days 0 and 9. Laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W cm−2, 7 min) was applied to the tumor sites 12 h post-injection. Tumor volume (calculated as [length × width2]/2) and body weight were tracked three times weekly for 18 days. Terminal tumors (n = 4/group) were subjected to H&E staining and TUNEL assays. Major organs were harvested for histological evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD from ≥3 biological replicates. Intergroup differences were analysed by a two-tailed Student's t-test using GraphPad Prism 9. Significance levels were indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

ZIF-Ce&ICGP construction and characterization

The fabrication of the ZIF-Ce&ICGP nanoplatform is shown in Fig. 2A. Building upon a reported method [17], ZIF-8 nanoparticles encapsulating ICG, termed ZIF&ICG, were initially synthesized at room temperature. In this process, ionic interactions and weak coordination between Zn2+ ions and the sulfonic acid groups of ICG drove the self-assembly of Zn2+-ICG complexes. The subsequent addition of the 2-methylimidazole linker facilitated the formation of ZIF-8, effectively trapping ICG molecules within its nanostructure [43]. TEM images revealed nearly identical sizes (∼130 nm) and morphologies for both ZIF-8 and ZIF&ICG nanoparticles (Fig. 2B and C), indicating that ICG incorporation did not hinder MOF formation. UV–vis spectroscopy confirmed successful ICG loading, showing that ZIF&ICG exhibited broad absorption analogous to that of free ICG (Fig. 2F). Notably, the ICG absorption maximum shifted from 790 nm to 813 nm upon encapsulation, likely indicating Zn2+-ICG interactions [43]. Quantitative analysis revealed that the ICG loading efficiency of ZIF&ICG was approximately 9.7 wt%. Subsequently, a surface layer of CeO2 nanozymes was anchored onto the ZIF&ICG nanoparticles to form ZIF-Ce&ICG. In a process exploiting the affinity of ZIF-8 for lanthanide ions at a pH > 6 [44], CeCl3•6H2O was introduced into an aqueous reaction system containing ZIF&ICG. Under hydrothermal conditions with weakly alkaline HMT, this lanthanide precursor hydrolysed to form CeO2 nanogranules coating the ZIF&ICG surface (Fig. 2D). This hydrothermal treatment induced partial collapse of the originally smooth MOF surface. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM, Fig. 2E) confirmed that the ZIF-Ce&ICG nanoparticles possessed a rough morphology because of in situ nucleation and growth of CeO2, with their average size increasing slightly to 135 nm. Magnification of a single ZIF-Ce&ICG revealed that these surface-deposited CeO2 nanogranules were under 5 nm in size (Fig. 2G). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum of ZIF-Ce&ICGP exhibited strong diffraction peaks that were similar to those of pure ZIF-8. It also showed typical diffuse peaks, confirming the presence of an amorphous structure from the CeO2 nanozymes (Fig. S1). Finally, to increase colloidal stability, minimize opsonization, and prolong systemic circulation, the ZIF-Ce&ICG nanoparticles were coated with PEG2000, yielding the final ZIF-Ce&ICGP nanoplatform. Dynamic laser scanning (DLS) analysis revealed that surface PEGylation increased the hydrodynamic diameter from 144 nm to 152 nm (Fig. 2H). In addition, both ZIF-Ce&ICG and ZIF-Ce&ICGP were positively charged according to the zeta potential data, facilitating mitochondrial targeting (Fig. S2). Moreover, after 96 h of storage at either room temperature or 4 °C, the size and surface charge of ZIF-Ce&ICGP remained nearly unchanged (Figs. S3 and S4), demonstrating its excellent long-term stability. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging demonstrated the monodispersity and consistent nanostructure of both the ZIF-Ce&ICG and ZIF-Ce&ICGP composites (Fig. 2I and J). HRTEM of ZIF-Ce&ICGP verified the dense coverage of the MOF surface with nanogranules (Fig. 2K). Finally, elemental mapping via high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) (Fig. 2L and M) and energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS) data (Fig. S5) suggested homogeneous distributions of Zn, O, Ce, and N throughout the ZIF-Ce&ICGP nanoplatform. Biodegradation, PTT and PPT enhanced the ROS storm

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to elucidate the oxidation states of elements in ZIF-Ce&ICG. The survey scan (Fig. 3A) clearly revealed distinct Zn 2p and Ce 3d5/2/3d3/2 features. Deconvolution of the Ce 3d region (Fig. 3B) confirms the coexistence of Ce3+ and Ce4+ species with a Ce2O3/CeO2 ratio of 0.22. A higher Ce4+ fraction conferred glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-like activity, promoting GSH consumption and in situ generation of Ce3+ within tumor cells [45]; the resulting increase in cytosolic Ce3+ mediated robust ROS formation through POD-like catalysis. The Zn 2p doublet at 1022 and 1045 eV corresponds to Zn2+ (Fig. 3C). Because ZIF-8 is known to be acid labile, we challenged ZIF-Ce&ICG in buffers mimicking lysosomes (pH 5.5), the tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5), and physiological conditions (pH 7.4). TEM analysis revealed markedly faster degradation under acidic conditions than at neutral pH, where the spherical morphology remained for up to 6 h (Fig. 3D). At pH 5.5, complete disassembly occurred within 12 h. At pH 6.5, structural failure began by 6 h, with the particles evolving to hollow structures by 12 h (Fig. 3B) and fully disintegrating by 36 h, confirming the ultrasensitive acid responsiveness of this MOF nanoplatform. We also employed inductively coupled plasma‒optical emission spectrometry (ICP‒OES) to quantify the two metal ions released from ZIF-Ce&ICGP at pH 6.5. The results revealed that Zn and Ce ions exhibited nearly identical release rates, each reaching a maximal release plateau at approximately 36 h (Fig. S6). Heterogeneous metal hybridization has been regarded as a practical and powerful strategy to efficaciously modulate the structure and properties of nanocatalysts [46]. In this work, the electronegativity and redox mismatches between the released Zn and Ce ions promote cooperative interfacial coupling, markedly increasing electron transport, reactant adsorption, and •OH generation. The performance of the NIR-II probe was then evaluated by fluorescence spectroscopy. Like free ICG, under 808 nm light illumination, the emission of ICG-loaded ZIF-8 extended beyond 1000 nm, and strong off-peak NIR-II luminescence verified the successful construction of ZIF-Ce&ICG (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, photostability testing via a fluorescence imaging system highlighted the advantage of encapsulation: after 6 min of continuous irradiation, ZIF-Ce&ICG retained 89.7 % of its initial NIR-II fluorescence, whereas free ICG lost 77.8 % of its signal (Fig. 3F and G). The photothermal behaviour of the encapsulated ICG was subsequently assessed. As depicted in Fig. 3J, the temperature of ZIF-Ce&ICG increased in a time-dependent manner. Under 808 nm irradiation at 0.8 W cm−2, a 200 μg mL−1 dispersion reached 52.5 °C within 5 min, while the equivalent free ICG and PBS reached only 44.6 °C and 36.1 °C, respectively (Fig. 3H and I). This improvement was primarily attributed to MOF-mediated protection that suppresses ICG photodegradation in aqueous media, yielding superior photothermal conversion. To quantify the laser-to-heat conversion efficiency (η), we analysed the transient states of the system under heating and cooling (laser on/off; Fig. 3K); using a standard method, we obtained η ≈ 49.51 % (Fig. 3M). The 200 μg mL−1 sample also demonstrated excellent photostability across four on/off cycles (Fig. 3L). Taken together, these results indicated that this MOF nanoplatform was a satisfactory candidate photothermal agent for tumor suppression. To probe the catalytic effects relevant to CDT, we assessed POD-like activity from CeO2 nanogranules and Zn ions released by ZIF-8 using the commercial •OH reporter 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB). Upon •OH oxidation, TMB forms a bluish-green product (oxTMB) with characteristic bands at ∼370 and 652 nm. All MOF formulations were preincubated at pH 6.5 for 36 h and then treated with H2O2 plus TMB. ZIF-Ce&ICG produced markedly higher oxTMB absorbance than ZIF&ICG, which can be ascribed to the efficient POD activity of the CeO2 nanograins measuring ∼5 nm. In particular, ZIF-Ce&ICG combined with 808 nm irradiation generated the most pronounced ROS storm (Fig. 3I). In vitro synergistic suppression of breast tumor cells

Effective intracellular uptake of nanoplatforms is pivotal for antitumor efficacy [47,48]. To probe the internalization of ZIF-Ce&ICGP by tumor cells, we monitored cellular uptake by CLSM using 633 nm laser excitation. 4T1 breast cancer cells were coincubated with ZIF-Ce&ICGP for a range of incubation times. The CLSM images revealed a time-dependent increase in cytoplasmic red fluorescence, which reached a maximum at 9 h and started to decrease at 12 h (Fig. 4A, S7, S8). In parallel, we coincubated ZIF-Ce&ICGP with cells and quantified the intracellular fluorescence by flow cytometry. These results were in excellent agreement with the CLSM observations, showing a pronounced time-dependent increase in uptake efficiency (Fig. 4I). Unexpectedly, ZIF-Ce&ICGP presented substantially better endocytosis than ZIF&ICGP (Fig. 4B–S9). The elevated endocytic uptake is likely attributable to the roughened surface morphology of ZIF-Ce&ICGP. ROS include superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and others. DCFH-DA is commonly used for ROS detection. Green emission (Ex 488 nm; Em 550 nm) served as the readout for cytoplasmic ROS. A weak green signal was observed in 4T1 cells treated with ZIF&ICGP, indicating limited Zn-mediated CDT in the cytosol. In contrast, cells receiving ZIF-Ce&ICGP plus 808 nm irradiation displayed markedly stronger fluorescence than the ZIF&ICGP + L group (Fig. 4C), which aligns with the solution-phase results and reflects the potential of the MOF nanoplatform for synergistically enhanced ROS-based therapy under laser exposure. To visualize •OH generation stemming from synergistic multi-ion catalysis inside cells, flow cytometry with a commercial •OH probe, hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF), was employed. Consistent with the cytosolic ROS findings, the ZIF-Ce&ICGP plus 808 nm laser treatment resulted in substantially greater green fluorescence than the other treatments, indicating that localized hyperthermia significantly increased the oxidative effects of ROS (Fig. 4H). To corroborate these observations, the cytotoxicity of ZnCe&ICGP was evaluated in both normal cells (specifically, HUVECs) and cancer cells. Only 55 % of 4T1 cells remained viable at the highest ZnCe&ICGP dose (Fig. 4D). As expected, combining ZIF-Ce&ICGP with an 808 nm laser led to a pronounced decrease in viability, with an approximately threefold (18 %) reduction in live cells relative to that with ZIF-Ce&ICGP alone (Fig. 4E and F). Meaningfully, a clear disparity in viability was discovered between tumor and normal cells: after 9 h of incubation with 350 μg/mL ZnCe&ICGP, more than 80 % of normal cells remained viable, indicating low cytotoxicity (Fig. 4G). Mechanistically, once internalized by tumor cells, the ZIF-Ce&ICGP underwent sequential disassembly in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment, releasing Zn and Ce ions that drove CDT and POD activity, respectively. In this work, the heightened activity of small Ce oxides was attributed to their greater number of active sites, which mediated increased ROS production. Furthermore, the nanoplatform endowed the nanocomposites with photothermal functionality; under NIR exposure, the generated heat accelerated dual-ion-mediated ROS formation. To quantify cell death, CLSM imaging was subsequently performed on cells co-stained with calcein AM and PI. As shown in Fig. 4D–K, PBS and ICG + L caused no apparent damage to tumor cells. Encouragingly, in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP + L group, only 25 % of the cells were still viable, representing an approximately 4.6-fold increase in the number of dying cancer cells compared with that in the ZIF&ICGP + L group, indicating that the precise delivery of CeO2 nanogranules markedly increased cell death under laser irradiation. The synergy between the ROS storm and PTT was validated by flow cytometry. In contrast to the minimal apoptosis and necrosis in the ICG and ICG + L groups (12.5 % and 19.6 %, respectively), the number of cells that did not undergo apoptosis or necrosis in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP + L group was only one-quarter that in the ZIF&ICGP + L group (Fig. 4J–L). Under the same reaction conditions, we also prepared pure CeO2 nanozymes with a size of 10 nm (Fig. S10). The killing effects of the nanozymes alone and the nanozymes combined with laser irradiation on tumor cells were the same as those of the ICG + L group (Figs. S11 and S12), which demonstrated that the killing effects of pure photothermal treatment and pure enzyme-like ROS generation on tumors were limited. In summary, in this work, ZIF-Ce&ICGP significantly increased the photostability of ICG and enabled precise delivery to tumor cells. Moreover, for the first time, in this study, specific and efficient catalysis with 5 nm CeO2 nanozymes within tumor cells was achieved. In vivo breast tumor tissue recognition and PTT determination

Motivated by the strong in vitro tumor recognition and theranostic performance of ZIF-Ce&ICGP, we next examined the in vivo tumor accumulation and PTT efficiency of this novel nanoplatform. Biodistribution and tumor targeting were first assessed in BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 murine mammary tumors by using NIR-II fluorescence imaging. After intravenous administration of free ICG, ZIF&ICGP, or ZIF-Ce&ICGP, the mice were imaged on a small-animal system with 808 nm excitation and a 1000 nm LP filter. As shown in Fig. 5A, both MOF formulations gradually accumulated at the tumor site by 6 h post-injection. Notably, ZIF-Ce&ICGP produced a stronger NIR-II signal than ZIF&ICGP, suggesting preferential internalization by tumor cells. We attributed this advantage to the roughened surface formed after CeO2 deposition on the MOF surface, which increased cellular adhesion and uptake (Fig. 5A). The tumor fluorescence of ZIF-Ce&ICGP peaked at 12 h and then waned, becoming faint by 48 h. In contrast, free ICG displayed only a weak signal at 12 h and negligible tumor fluorescence thereafter. Ex vivo NIR-II imaging of vital organs and tumors from the three groups at 36 h post-administration confirmed that tumors from the ZIF-Ce&ICGP group exhibited the greatest accumulation, while all three groups had comparably low uptake in reticuloendothelial system (RES) organs (Fig. 5B). The 20–400 nm size window of these carriers favours passive tumor targeting via the increased permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Accordingly, the EPR effect, together with increased endocytosis enabled by the uniquely rough surface of ZIF-Ce&ICGP, markedly enhanced tumor targeting and identification, with optimal performance at 12 h post-injection. This time point was therefore selected for early breast cancer diagnosis and photoactivated therapy. On the basis of these results, the intratumoral temperature was monitored under 808 nm irradiation at 12 h post-injection of PBS, ICG, ZIF&ICGP, or ZIF-Ce&ICGP. The initial temperatures were almost the same in all four groups (Fig. S13). Encouragingly, in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP group, the temperature increased rapidly to ∼58.8 °C after 7 min of continuous NIR exposure, whereas the temperature of the tumor tissues of the ZIF&ICGP group reached 50.5 °C (Fig. 5D and E). Consistent with the in vitro CLSM and in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging results, the rough interface formed after coating with CeO2 efficiently induced the enrichment and endocytosis of tumor cells. Therefore, the maximum temperature of ZIF-Ce&ICGP was significantly higher than that of ZIF&ICGP. Under the same conditions, the maximum temperatures of the PBS and ICG groups were ∼34.4 °C and 39.2 °C, respectively (Fig. 5D and E). Temperatures above 50 °C are effective for tumor ablation while sparing surrounding healthy tissues from significant damage. Owing to precise tumor targeting and minimal RES sequestration, ZIF-Ce&ICGP mitigated concerns over systemic toxicity. Blood retention and fluorescence imaging-guided tumor surgery

Rough-surfaced nanoparticles have shown considerable potential for increased blood retention. Our findings revealed that ZIF-Ce&ICGP, with a half-life of 11.99 h, had a longer blood circulation time than ZIF&ICGP, which had a half-life of 6.87 h (Fig. 6A). The formation of a protein corona around both types of MOF-based nanoplatforms was confirmed by SDS‒PAGE, which revealed visible protein bands after Coomassie brilliant blue staining, indicating the adsorption of serum proteins. Notably, after 3 h of incubation with ZIF-Ce&ICGP, greater enrichment of the protein corona was observed than in the ZIF&ICGP group, as evidenced by the more intense bands (Fig. 6B and C). The extended blood retention can be attributed to the ability of the rough-surfaced nanoplatforms to more readily acquire a substantial protein corona. In particular, in vivo NIR-II fluorescence images were used to calculate the ratio of tumor to background tissue (RTB). As anticipated, the RTB values in the ZIF-based groups were significantly greater than those in the ICG-only group (Fig. 6D). Remarkably, the highest RTB was observed in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP group at 12 h post-injection, reaching nearly 1.4 times that of the ZIF&ICGP group, which confirmed the effective tumor accumulation of the CeO2-anchored MOF. Excitingly, the exact fluorescence intensities at the ZIF-Ce&ICGP-treated tumor sites demonstrated the greatest tumor enhancement by a substantial margin at 12 h after tail-vein injection (Fig. 6E). In particular, the mean fluorescence intensities of the ex vivo NIR-II signals from organs and tumors confirmed that the tumor signal intensity in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP group at 36 h post-injection was markedly greater than that in the other vital organs and in the tumors of the other two groups (Fig. 5, Fig. 6F). Considering the maximum RTB and peak tumor accumulation, this time point was selected as the optimal window for tumor resection under the guidance of NIR-II fluorescence imaging. We subsequently performed tumor surgery using this technique. Excitingly, NIR-II fluorescence imaging at 12 h after the injection of ZnCe&ICGP provided a clear tumor outline (Fig. 6G). The solid tumor (P1) was then surgically excised as completely as possible under white light. Afterwards, the tumor margins were examined using the NIR-II imaging system to check for any remaining fluorescence signal. Any residual tissue (P2) illuminated by the remaining NIR-II signal was then carefully resected under the guidance of fluorescence. When all NIR-II fluorescence signals had ceased to be visible, the tumor was considered to have been completely removed. Moreover, after excising the tumor, we harvested muscle (M) from the surgical bed to examine whether any postoperative remnants remained. Ex vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging of the resected tumor tissues and surrounding muscle tissue demonstrated overwhelmingly stronger fluorescence in the tumor samples, confirming the accuracy of the resection (Fig. 6I). Further confirmation was obtained from H&E staining of P1 and P2, which illustrated clear boundaries between tumor and normal tissues, with no residual breast tumor cells present in the surrounding muscle tissue (Fig. 6J). In contrast, after tumor removal under white light, we also harvested a sample of normal tissue (white light–M) from the surrounding resected tumor tissues and performed H&E staining. The findings revealed that some residual tumor cells were still present, indicating limited tumor resection under white light in clinical surgery. Finally, 30 days post-surgery, recurrence and metastasis were visualized by bioluminescence imaging with intravenous injection of D-luciferase potassium, which specifically triggered green luminescence from residual 4T1-luc cells. No bioluminescence signal was detected in the NIR-II fluorescence imaging group, indicating no tumor recurrence, whereas the white light group exhibited recurrence in 100 % of the mice (Fig. 6H). In vivo synergistic breast tumor suppression under NIR laser illumination

Prior to in vivo tumor suppression, compared with PBS, ZIF-Ce&ICGP had no significant haemolytic effect even at a concentration of 300 μg/mL (Fig. 7A), demonstrating excellent biocompatibility. We then proceeded to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ZIF-Ce&ICGP in vivo. BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4) to receive different treatments: (1) control (PBS), (2) PBS with laser (PBS + L), (3) ZIF-Ce&ICGP, and (4) ZIF-Ce&ICGP with laser (ZIF-Ce&ICGP + L). For the three laser-irradiated groups, 1.0 W cm−2 of illumination with an 808 nm laser was applied for 7 min starting 12 h post-injection. The relative tumor volumes and body weights of all groups were meticulously documented over time. Surprisingly, the combination therapy of PTT and ROS storm led to nearly complete tumor regression after an 18-day treatment period (Fig. 7B–E). Additionally, rapid tumor growth was observed in the PBS and PBS + L groups, whereas ZIF-Ce&ICGP significantly inhibited tumors, likely due to the potent anti-tumor effects of POD and CDT, which are known for their strong ROS generation catalysed by the two bimetallic catalysts. Compared with the other treatments, the synergistic therapy of ZIF-Ce&ICGP + L resulted in markedly suppressed tumor growth throughout the treatment period. On Day 18, tumors from all groups were excised and weighed (Fig. 7C–H). The combined photothermal and robust-ROS therapy resulted in superior therapeutic effects compared to ROS treatment alone. Furthermore, histological analysis with H&E and TUNEL staining (Fig. 7G) indicated that the combined PTT and increased ROS generation led to more pronounced tumor ablation than ROS production alone, resulting in extensive apoptosis and necrosis. To assess the biosafety and practical clinical application potential of ZIF-Ce&ICGP, in vivo toxicity was evaluated through body weight changes and histological examination. In our study, compared with those in the PBS group, no significant body weight fluctuations or histological abnormalities were observed in the nanoagent-treated group (Fig. 7F–I, S14). Moreover, blood biochemistry and routine blood parameters in the ZIF-Ce&ICGP-treated groups remained normal at both 1 day and 3 weeks following tumor ablation (Figs. S15 and S16). Next, we studied the in vivo metabolism of ZIF-Ce&ICGP by determining the concentration of Ce ions through ICP‒OES. In the early phase, the ZIF-Ce&ICGP nanoparticles that accumulate in the liver are excreted mainly through bile into the faeces. Additionally, by the seventh day, the degraded nanoparticles were eliminated via the kidneys, contributing to urine production (Fig. S17). On Day 10, a peak in nanoparticle excretion through urine was observed. These in vivo metabolic findings further indicate that ZIF-Ce&ICGP has excellent biocompatibility. All the preliminary toxicity data indicate that ZIF-Ce&ICGP did not induce any significant toxicity, suggesting its potential as a biocompatible theranostic nanoplatform. Conclusion

We developed a tumor-microenvironment-responsive nanoplatform based on ICG-encapsulated ZIF-8 nanoframes modified with ∼5 nm CeO2 nanozymes. This system enables synergistic PTT-induced ROS generation via POD-like catalytic activity combined with CDT. The integrated nanoplatform facilitates precise intraoperative tumor resection navigation and effectively suppresses solid tumors through this synergistic effect. Key features include (1) increased tumor accumulation via the EPR effect and subsequent endocytosis promoted by its rough surface, (2) NIR-II fluorescence capabilities for bioimaging, and (3) a programmable therapeutic response combining localized hyperthermia with ROS-mediated oxidative damage. Furthermore, the nanoplatform demonstrated favourable biosafety profiles in vivo. This advanced nanotherapeutic holds significant potential to improve clinical breast cancer treatment and accelerate the translation of research advancements into clinical practice.
