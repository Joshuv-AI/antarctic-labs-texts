Hydroxyhydroquinone as a novel modulator of mitochondrial metabolism with potential to restore CoQ10 homeostasis
Authors: Paulo Roberto dos Santos, Joseph Rastegar, Mariana Roesch-Ely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of diverse metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders, often linked to impaired coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) homeostasis. Here, we have evaluated the activity of hydroxyhydroquinone (HHQ) as a novel modulator of mitochondrial metabolism. Molecular simulations revealed that HHQ can act as an alternative aromatic substrate for human COQ2 in the CoQ10 biosynthetic pathway. In cultured cells, HHQ exposure (5.10− 5 mol.L− 1) enhanced complex I activity while maintaining stable ATP levels. HHQ reduced nitric oxide accumulation without altering superoxide dismutase activity, suggesting selective redox modulation. By bypassing the 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (PHBA) pathway, HHQ restores mitochondrial homeostasis and supports aerobic metabolism. These findings highlight HHQ as a small aromatic compound with strong redox potential that may favor metabolic functions driven by CoQ10 deficiency and mitochondrial dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11010-026-05687-8.

Introduction

Hydroxyhydroquinone (HHQ), or 1,2,4-trihydroxy benzene, is an aromatic molecule with an efficient redox potential, which can bind or release electrons according to the environment upon cellular metabolic demand [1]. Regardless of the variety of molecular targets that HHQ may play and interact with tissues and organs, it is known that HHQ participates actively in the mitochondrial energy metabolism [2]. HHQ can act as ubiquinol (CoQH2), a reduced form of Coenzyme Q (CoQ), and help transport electrons in the respiratory chain via the HHQ–HBQ (hydroxyhydroquinone–2-hydroxybenzoquinone) redox pair in the mitochondrial membrane. Coenzyme Q can also be found in a completely oxidized state (CoQ or ubiquinone), or in a semi-reduced state known as a semi-ubiquinone (CoQH⋅). It is well described that CoQ plays an essential role in the Electron Transport Chain (ETC), maintaining the electron flux from complexes I and II to complex III, which ultimately leads to a chemiosmotic gradient used to produce ATP by oxidative phosphorylation process [2, 3]. ETC represents one of the most complex redox biochemical systems found in cells. The components of this biochemical machinery present in all mitochondria operate in synchrony and with high specificity, which maintains a high conserved pathway across all eukaryotes.

The preserved regulatory mechanism of the biosynthesis pathway ultimately guaranties CoQ10 homeostasis [4]. CoQ10 is an essential element for mitochondrial function and provides antioxidant protection of cell membranes and plasma lipids [3]. The oxidation/reduction cycle of CoQ10 confers to this molecule the property to transfer electrons in different biological processes and to act as an antioxidant at cell membranes preserving lipids, protein, and nucleic acids from oxidative damage. Imbalances in either the biosynthesis pathway or in mitochondrial functions that impact CoQ10 homeostasis can lead to the development of various diseases, particularly those in which mitochondrial dysfunction plays a role [4, 5]. CoQ10 deficiency may affect tissues with higher CoQ10 levels and bioenergetic requirements. Generally, loss of homeostasis in CoQ10 pathway has been associated with age-related diseases [6], neurological disorders [7], kidney and liver diseases [8], and metabolic syndrome [3], among others.

CoQ10supplementation is commonly recommended for patients with mitochondrial diseases or secondary mitochondrial dysfunction. However, some special conditions such as genetic defects and poor bioavailability may limit the effectiveness of exogenous supplementations. CoQ levels can be secondarily decreased in a wide variety of mitochondrial diseases or can be reduced in a group of mitochondrial disorders such as primary CoQ deficiencies. Oral supplementation with high doses of CoQ10 is reported to show limited efficacy, particularly in patients with neurological symptoms [9]. This limitation maybe attributed to poor absorption and bioavailability of exogenous CoQ10, which restricts its access to affected tissues [10].

Literature has pointed out that exogenous CoQ10 treatment can be ineffective, most likely due to properties such as hydrophobicity and high molecular weight, generating poor solubility and bioavailability of CoQ10compared to shorter tail analogues like CoQ4. According to Steenberge, LH et al. (2023) [11], less hydrophobic CoQ species with shorter isoprenoid tails can serve as viable substitutes for CoQ10 in human cells. Their study demonstrate that CoQ4 can perform multiple functions of CoQ10 in CoQ-deficient cells at lower treatment concentrations, motivating further investigation of CoQ4 as a supplement for CoQ10 deficiencies.

Therefore, emerging therapies utilizing CoQ₁₀ analogs, biosynthetic precursors, and combination treatments, are emerging as alternatives to restore cellular energy. Novel therapeutic strategies employing structural analogs of the CoQ precursor 4-hydroxybenzoic acid alone or in combination are being developed. González-García, P et al. (2025) [10] investigated a combination of CoQ10 with vanillic acid, a structural analog of 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and evaluated the effects in both murine and human models of primary CoQ deciency. The co-treatment revealed the liver displaying the most pronounced metabolic adaptations, restoring the expression of genes involved in sulfide oxidation and one-carbon metabolism pathways.

This paper identifies HHQ as a novel modulator of mitochondrial metabolism with potential to restore CoQ10 homeostasis. Strategies can improve mitochondrial and metabolic activities and decrease oxidative damage, one example is supplementation with CoQ10 [3, 5, 9]. In this complex scenario, HHQ arises as a metabolic alternative and powerful player that can bypass several steps in biosynthesis of CoQ10 pathway and reestablish homeostasis. In an analogous way, HHQ also plays an efficient redox potential accordingly to its concentration and bioavailability in different organisms. Literature has pointed tolerable doses of HHQ in rodents, from 0.1 g.L− 1 to 0.5 g.L− 1 [12] and in humans, from 0.1 to 0.7 mg.day− 1 [13] as a molecule present in roast green coffee beans [12, 14]. The LD50 from HHQ in rats is reported at doses between 0.35 and 0.5 g.kg− 1 of body weight for acute oral toxicity, with maximal non-lethal dose is 2 g.kg− 1 bw for dermal toxicity in rats [15]. HHQ shows promising potential in the fields of pharmacology and cosmetics, as it can effectively safeguard living cells from oxidative stress when administered at safe dosage levels [16, 17]. Most reports focus on the effects of HHQ present in roasted coffee in fat and vascular metabolism through redox balance. Studies have demonstrated that HHQ inhibits the antihypertensive effects of chlorogenic acid compounds found in coffee, both in rodents [18, 19] and humans [1, 14, 20, 21]. Reduction of fat catabolism after HHQ ingestion has also been investigated, although the exact mechanism of action remains unclear [12]. HHQ was able to decrease lipid catabolism in humans through ingestion of HHQ-reduced coffee, leading to significantly higher fat utilization in healthy humans [1]. From our previous in vitro studies, HHQ showed to have a strong redox potential at concentrations up to 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1 against insulin resistant cell line model (HepG2/IRM), acting as a reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger to ameliorate mitochondria activity and modulate ATP generation. ROS and ATP activities were maintained at relatively stable levels while great improvement in glucose uptake was observed after HHQ exposition to HepG2/IRM [22]. Free radicals often triggers a variety of diseases and research in literature suggests that they can have both positive and negative effects on health conditions, serving as regulators of cellular homeostasis [6, 23, 24]. A selective redox modulation is observed after HHQ exposition to cells and we believe that these events are strongly related with CoQ10 activity.

The biosynthesis of CoQ10 has been systematically investigated and occurs in a sequence of steps, where a series of membrane proteins positioned on the inner face of the mitochondrial matrix build the Coenzyme Q molecule and deliver it directly for use by the ETC [25]. The first enzyme in the series is a transmembrane protein called COQ2, a prenyltransferase of the UbiA superfamily that joins the substrates decaprenyl pyrophosphate and PHBA to form 3-decaprenyl-4-hydroxybenzoate. This initial intermediate is then transferred across the lipid membrane to subsequent enzymatic sites to be converted into CoQ10. Mutations in the human COQ2 gene [26] are responsible for a series of syndromes linked to CoQ10 production, among them neonatal type II diabetes mellitus and congenital cardiopathies [27, 28]. Specific mutations from a variety of UbiAs may lead to changes in substrate selectivity. Such changes are reported in literature and one example occurs in ApUbiA from the mushroom Clitocybe clavipes [29]. These studies have shown the key change in substrate selectivity after a mutation process, enabling this enzyme to transfer prenyl pyrophosphate chains to 1,4-hidroquinones (HQs), instead of PHBA. This phenomenon most likely changes selectivity of the enzymatic site of human homologous UbiA for the aromatic substrate, producing common CoQ10 intermediates to the known biochemical pathways.

In the present study we highlight a schematic pathway of known mitochondrial COQ10 biosynthesis in mammals that starts with L-tyrosine to produce PHBA as aromatic building block. We bring a new pathway that bypasses the known model that begins with HHQ sharing some nonspecific COQ2 with PHBA on a key step of established model. Changes in substrate selectivity during biosynthesis of CoQ10 generate intermediates after HHQ exposure to cells, which stimulates complex 1 from the ETC while maintaining stable ATP levels. It is known that hepatocytes and neurons perform the methylation of catechol groups via the catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) pathway to prevent their oxidation to 1,2-quinones, which are cytotoxic by inducing ROS formation [30]. Therefore, hepatocyte cultures tend to convert HHQ to 2-methoxy-1,4-hydroquinone (MHQ) as a protective mechanism. Another phenol derivatization mechanism is the sulfation of OH groups via the sulfotransferase (SULT) pathway [31]. This reaction is particularly important because it prevents the conversion of HHQ to 2-hydroxy-1,4-benzoquinone (HBQ), also cytotoxic due to reactive oxygen species production and its inhibition of the electron transport chain complex I [32].

Understanding HHQ as an acceptor of decaprenyl pyrophosphate chains in the biosynthesis of CoQ10 bring us towards a new potential metabolic route to be investigated, with novel molecular interactions that have not been described elsewhere. In this context, we investigated the hypothesis of HHQ, O-sulfated and 3-O-methylated derivatives metabolites integrating with the biosynthetic pathway of CoQ10 by assimilation through non-specific human COQ2 enzyme (hCoQ2). Therefore, we performed molecular docking simulations to evaluate the ability of HHQ likely derivative 2- methoxy-1,4-hydroquinone-4-sulfate (MHQ-4-sulfate) to act as aromatic substrates of hCOQ2. A series of biochemical experiments on insulin resistant model cell line were also conducted to support our theory by assessing specific in vitro pathways of CoQ10, such as the activity of ETC complexes I and II, nitric oxide (⋅NO) generation, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and ATP synthesis.

Results

All results of biochemical experiments exposing insulin resistant cell cultures (HepG2/IRM) to treatments with different biochemical inducers are summarized in Fig. 2: complex I and II of the electron transport chain, activity of nitric oxide, superoxide dismutase enzyme and ATP production. Cell viability through MTT was first performed to guarantee absence of cytotoxicity at all concentrations tested.

Evaluation of the enzymatic activity of electron transport chain complex I, complex II

The enzymatic activity data of NADH: Ubiquinone reductase complex (Fig. 2a) revealed a generous increase in complex 1 activity in HHQ group compared to PHBA and control, while compounds PABA, METF, and TBHP presented characteristic of deactivators. It is noteworthy that complex I modulation was much more efficient in cells exposed to HHQ (+ 70% of control) compared to the known aromatic precursor for this purpose - PHBA (+ 10% of control), which also presented a slight positive shift in Complex I activity. PHBA showed a positive variation in activity but was not significant compared to the negative control. As for PABA, it showed a significant reduction in Complex I activity (−42% of control). It also revealed suppressive levels in METF group, most likely due to its insulin mimetic activity. TBHP was used as a positive control for oxidative stress induction, showing a suppressive effect of −69% compared to the negative control. In an interesting way, complex II activity showed similar patterns for HHQ, PABA and METF groups (+ 90% of control), with decrease levels for PHBA (+ 50% of control). TBHP was the only compounds that showed a reduction in Complex II activity (−40% of control) compared to all groups (Fig. 2b). Although HHQ is efficient in activating both complexes I and II, it plays a major function in the NADH ubiquinone reductase complex (C1). CoQ is reduced by NADH through Complex I activity, this cofactor selectively oxidizes NADH to NAD + 31.

Nitric oxide (⋅NO), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and ATP activity

In the present study the HHQ group presented the greatest suppressive power on ⋅NO production, with a reduction of 25% compared to the negative control in the presence of insulin (Fig. 2c). ⋅NO levels were also suppressed for PABA, PHBA and METF, although in a less expressive manner. As expected, a great induction of ⋅NO was observed in the TBHP group (+ 50% of control). Despite HHQ presented the greatest inhibition of ⋅NO (−25% of control), HHQ treated group was not able to modulate SOD activity compared to the negative control. Compounds like PABA, PHBA, and METF exhibited significant increases in SOD activity after insulin intake, in the order of 51%, 42%, and 47%, respectively (Fig. 2d). Regardless of the precise mechanism of action, the results indicate that no overload of the antioxidant SOD defense complex was seen. It is worth noting that this experiment aimed not to determine the SOD enzyme expression, but to measure its activity through the production of the chromophore adrenochrome in vitro.

The positive control TBHP showed − 42% of SOD activity compared to the negative control, indicating a reduction in respiratory activity due to mitochondrial poisoning. These effects could be monitored both in the insulin resistance model and the cells without insulin. Since SOD is expressed in the mitochondrial matrix to capture the superoxide radical anion from Complex IV of the electron transport chain, the suppression of its activity due to poisoning of the catalytic site compromises mitochondrial defense.

We have recently investigated the in vitro ATP levels in cells using an insulin resistance model (HepG2/IRM) after HHQ exposition [22]. ATP activity from our previous results was monitored through a Luminescent Cell Viability Assay. This protocol allows determining the number of viable cells in culture based on the quantitation of ATP through the addition of luciferase, generating a luminescent signal. The results showed that ATP levels maintained stable after HHQ (5.10− 5 mol.L− 1) exposure in comparison to a significant decrease in ATP activity on HepG2/IRM treated with metformin (5.10− 6 mol.L− 1) and TBHP (2.10− 5 mol.L− 1). These results may indicate that the extra availability of glucose promoted by metformin can induce less profitable ATP pathways. In the present study we have monitored ATP through the Luminescent ATP for bioluminescence assay, using recombinant firefly luciferase and its substrate D-luciferin. The assay is based on luciferaseʼs requirement for ATP in producing light. ATP levels after HHQ exposition to cells were again consistent with our previous published results using other detection system, showing similar ATP levels from the HHQ group compared to the control. The other experimental conditions analyzed, such as the analogous PABA, PHBA, revealed significant lower ATP levels, compatible to the samples exposed to METF and TBHP (Fig. 2e).

Cell viability was monitored for all groups through MTT viability test (Fig. 2f). We also performed cell viability using a cytotoxicity detection kit method for quantifying cytotoxicity based on measuring the activity of the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase released from damaged cells. The results were consistent with the ones found for MTT that was used as a colorimetric assay measuring cellular metabolic activity to assess cell viability. The viability of cultures exposed to different treatments over a 24-hour interval was determined by measuring formazan production by mitochondrial activity using the MTT protocols [33]. The working concentrations obtained for the compounds HHQ, PABA and PHBA showed viabilities exceeding 100% of the control at concentrations of 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1; METF at 5.10− 6 mol.L− 1, and THBP at 2.10− 5 mol.L− 1. To establish an insulin-resistant cell model (HepG2/IRM), a gradient of insulin at different concentrations was initially tested under high glucose concentration (2.5.10− 2 mol.L− 1), as previous publications from our group [22]. Best parameters for insulin concentration was selected for insulin-resistance model and further experiments were processed with insulin at 5.10− 6 mol.L− 1 for 24 h. From the results published we could observe that glucose levels in cells after HHQ exposure revealed a significant increase compared to control. In addition, cells treated with METF and TBHP presented the lowest glucose uptake in comparison to control and HHQ [22].

Metabolomic studies (LC-HRMS)

The cellular lysates obtained from the six treatments were analyzed by LC-ESI-HRMS to verify variations in the metabolomic profiles of the HEPG2/IRM cell line (Fig. 3; Table 1). The method allowed the identification of 13 catabolites of ubiquinol (A–M) originating from ω-oxidation of the side chain initiated by the CYP2E1 enzyme, followed by successive oxidations up to the third isoprenyl unit of ubiquinol [34, 35]. Compounds A, B, C, D, E, G, I, K and M derive from ubiquinol deactivated by 1,4 O-acetylation [36], whereas compounds F, H, J, and L are derived from ubiquinone. Compound H is the most intense peak in the control experiment (CTRL) and maintained a constant chromatographic area across all treatments, being assigned as the internal reference peak for comparative analyses (100%) for the other chromatographic peaks. Compounds F, H, J, and L were identified as a group of oxidized ubiquinone isomers. H states from internal reference peak used in the experiment. The treatment with HHQ activated both complex I and II, which brings information concerning variations in the catalytic activities of the respiratory chain (RC) I and II complexes. Such changes in cell metabolism may lead to increase in mitochondrial CoQ10concentration. Literature points out that CoQ10deactivation occurs through processes such as acetylation, phosphorylation, and sulfation [32, 36, 37], when the reduced form (CoQ10H2) reaches a threshold level, leading the deactivated conjugate to be metabolized via the oxidative pathway, which results in water-soluble compounds that are easily excreted.

Compound 1 was identified as a probable tyrosine catabolism derivative, o-fucose-4-(3-hydroxypropyl)phenol (Fig. 3 (1). Fucosylation pathways are highly expressed in the HepG2 cell line, with recent studies employing GDP-fucose expression inhibitors to suppress liver tumor growth [38]. GDP-fucose is required for post-translational modifications involved in the production of glycoproteins, glycolipids, and oligosaccharides essential for cellular metabolism [39]. The reduced expression of the o-fucosylated derivative in HHQ and PHBA treatments suggests suppression of this fucosylation pathway, thereby inhibiting a probable PHBA production pathway. Suppression of the endogenous PHBA pathway is evident in HHQ and PHBA treatments, indicating that both aromatic compounds are required by the cell for CoQ10 production, as the cellular demand for substrates of the CoQ10 anabolic pathway was met by aromatic analogs. A proposed catabolic pathway from L-tyrosine to the hypothetical endogenous PHBA precursor is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Compound 2 was identified as desmethoxy CoQ1 (m/z 221.1183) and its expression remained without substantial change across all treatments (Fig. 3 (2)). Compound 3 was identified as deoxycholic acid, originating from the mitochondrial ω-oxidation of cholesterol, and is a common bile acid in hepatic metabolism [40] (Fig. 3 (3)). Treatments with HHQ and TBHP activate the biochemical pathway of deoxycholic acid, while PHBA, PABA, and METF reduce its expression below that of CTRL.

The compounds identified that were derived from ubiquinol after treatment with METF suppressed the O-acetylation and ω-oxidation pathways of ubiquinol to basal levels compared to CTRL. The result indicates an apparent suppression of mitochondrial activity and a consequent reduction in the cell’s oxygen demand. All the 9 compounds (A, B,C, D,E, G,I, K,M) derived from ubiquinol presented an increase for HHQ on the extracted chromatographic area plots compared to all other groups.

Molecular docking simulations

UbiA hCOQ2 is shown to be a non-selective enzyme [29, 41]. Prenylation reaction seems to be determined by thermodynamic tendency, following the Friedel-Crafts electrophilic substitution mechanism. Based on these premises, we conducted computational simulations to investigate the likely assimilation route of HHQ through the CoQ10 biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 4). In silico evaluations were processed to assess the interactions of HHQ metabolites competing with PHBA for the active site of the UbiA enzyme (COQ2). A potential prenylation of these compounds over -PHBA were also evaluated. The rigid docking results show that 2-methoxy-1,4-hidroquinone-4-sulfate (MHQ-4-sulfate) stabilize in the same cavity occupied by PHBA, and their positions seem to favor the interaction between cofactor and substrate. MHQ-4-sulfate is apparently more activated than PHBA and may react more readily at position C6 on aromatic ring (Fig. 4F).

The choice of the molecular docking model used was based on known biochemical pathways [28]. It is known that hCoQ2 is a prenyltransferase of the UBiA superfamily that joins the substrates decaprenyl pyrophosphate and PHBA to form 3-decaprenyl-4-hydroxybenzoate. Literature brings classical chemical reacting sites for COQ2 by eukaryotes, where PHBA in shown in two symmetrical positions to react, C2 and C6 (Fig. 5E). Predictions of alternative docking poses are highlighted in the Fig. SI 19–21. A proposed bypass pathways to produce COQ5 substrate demanding mitochondrial aryl sulfatase (Fig. 5F) is compared to a bypass pathway proposed by Acosta, et al., (2019) [41] using vanillic acid as UbiA substrate to produce COQ4 intermediate. From the results presented, a mechanism of action is proposed for HHQ, with anabolic and catabolic pathways (Fig. 6). The anabolic is leading to Golgi complex and/or bypassing ubiquinone biosynthesis in the mitochondrial compartment. The catabolic pathway bellow produces hydroxymuconic acid, which may be excreted by renal clearance.

Discussion

In this study we investigated a new potential metabolic route for HHQ that integrates with the biosynthetic pathway of CoQ10, revealing novel molecular interactions not been published until present. We performed molecular docking assays to evaluate the ability of HHQ and its likely derivative, methoxy-1,4-hydroquinone-sulfate (MHQ sulfate), to act as aromatic substrate of human COQ2. We believe that HHQ is most likely acting as an acceptor of prenyl pyrophosphate chains in initial steps of the biosynthesis of ubiquinol (Fig. 7).

The molecular docking model applied in the present study was based on known biochemical pathways published and are in accordance to the results presented in this paper [29, 42]. Literature has demonstrated evidence that UbiA may be affected by mutations in various active sites, playing an important role in subtract selectivity. One of the studies that supports our theory was performed in Escherichia coli, in which human homologous ApUbiA is expressed in a mutant E. coli containing inactive cofactor geranyl S-pyrophosphate. The results allowed for the determination of the enzyme’s active site, as the precursor PHBA can be crystallized in the unreacted form near the active site [29].

Also of great support is a recent study published by María de los Ángeles Vargas-Pérezare et al. (2024) [42], which evaluates strategies based on COQ2 homology that explores possible substrate binding sites of available ligand-bound homologs with solved structures. This study evaluates structural superposition in the AlphaFold predicted model for COQ2 and highlight some residues located on the central cavity or the matrix loops. From a series of different variants associated with primary CoQ deficiency onto the COQ2, a special attention is a mutation predicted in this study at the p.Arg123His site.

We have further performed a series of biochemical experiments on HepG2/IRM to support our theory by assessing specific in vitro pathways of CoQ10. We first explored the activity of ETC complex 1 (C1) after all treatments. The results in this study highlighted a substantial increase in C1 after HHQ exposition (+ 70% of control) to HepG2/IRM compared to the known aromatic precursor for this purpose PHBA (+ 10% of control), indicating a great activation of NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase. According to publications, PHBA administration improves CoQ10 levels in animal models with induced deficiency in tyrosine catabolism, validating the thesis of this carboxylic acid being the precursor of CoQ10 [28].

PABA was also tested and a significant reduction in Complex I activity (−42% of control) was observed in our study. PABA is described as a precursor to other forms of CoQ found in yeast and bacteria (CoQ6 and CoQ9) using a biochemical pathway similar to humans, with the intermediate 3-(n-prenyl)−4-aminobenzoate being active in CoQ biosynthesis [43]. PHBA administration improves CoQ10 levels in animal models with induced deficiency in tyrosine catabolism, validating the thesis of this carboxylic acid being the precursor of CoQ1026. PABA showed a significant reduction in Complex I activity, probably due to the inhibitory effect on the active site of the UBiA enzyme, probably due to the inhibitory effect on the active site of the UBiA enzyme, or even for producing an inactive prenylated compound. Apart from the suppressive activity found for PABA, Complex 1 activity revealed suppressive levels also for metformin (METF) treated group. METF is an antidiabetic drug that induces anaerobic glycolysis, thereby reducing oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria, increasing lactic acid production, and consequently, reducing electron transport chain activity as a whole [44].

CoQ is reduced by NADH through Complex I activity [45]. C1 is the first fixed protein complex in ETC, with ubiquinone and cytochrome C as mobile components. Ubiquinone, also known as Coenzyme Q or CoQ10, is a lipophilic component that moves through the lipid membrane on the inner face of the mitochondrial matrix, acting as an energy carrier from complexes I and II to complex III by interconversion of the ubiquinone: ubiquinol redox pair. In the ETC, CoQ is in a permanent equilibrium between a reduced form after receiving two electrons, CoQH2 or ubiquinol, and an oxidized form, CoQ or ubiquinone [46].

CoQ is reduced by mitochondrial glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase, which receives electrons from sugars, but can also receive electrons from the electron transport flavoprotein dehydrogenases family, key enzymes of the fatty acid β-oxidation and branched-chain amino acid oxidation pathways [47, 48]. Furthermore, CoQ can be reduced by mitochondrial dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, a key enzyme of the pyrimidine nucleotide biosynthesis pathway [49], which can influence the reactive oxygen species production, partially contributing to ROS signaling in mitochondria [50]. There are many different points of ROS production in the respiratory chain, however the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) site, or the more controversial CoQ-binding site in Complex I, and the CoQ10 site of Complex III, are reported to be the most important ones [51, 52].

Recent results from our group have shown that ROS levels increased in cells with insulin resistant model (HepG2/IRM), but were maintained lower in the group treated with HHQ than those observed in the control group [22]. It is well reported that most of the reactive oxygen species production takes place in the mitochondrial respiratory chain [53], mainly in the form of superoxide that is transformed to hydrogen peroxide [54, 55]. Some reports highlight the importance of the retrograde electron transport (RET) mechanism that happens when accumulation of reduced CoQ occurs and generates superoxide, driving electrons throughout Complex I to NAD+, this mechanism balances the ubiquinone/ubiquinol ratio [56, 57].

ROS are generated inside the cells in the form of superoxide radical anion (O2⋅−) or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The respiratory chain is one of the major sites of metabolic ROS generation within the cell [58]. They are first generated in the form of O2⋅−, which are instable and very short-lived. O2⋅− is rapidly converted to the more stable H2O2 by superoxide dismutase located in the cytosol and the mitochondrial inter-membrane space and matrix. Although production of superoxide anions (O2⋅−) are reported to be stimulated by HHQ [12], our results showed no increase in SOD activity after insulin and HHQ exposition, even though a generous increase in C1 was observed after HHQ treatment. It is important to remind that compounds like PABA, PHBA, and METF tested in the present study exhibited significant increases in SOD activity after insulin intake. SOD activity is directly linked to the mitochondrial ability to deplete ROS, especially the superoxide radical anion (O2⋅−).

Not only reactive oxygen species are involved as important signal compounds in many systems that control a host of cellular functions. Reactive nitrogen species are also key players and together with ROS represent small inorganic molecules that can diffuse easily to their site of action and disrupt redox cell state. Nitric oxide (⋅NO) has been reported to occur preferably in the cytosol and on the inner cell plasma membrane and its levels are related to metabolic changes not only directly linked to energy production but also to cellular signaling metabolism. A great suppressive power on ⋅NO production was observed in this study after HHQ exposition, with a reduction of 25% compared to the negative control in the presence of insulin. ⋅NO levels were also suppressed for PABA, PHBA and METF, although in a less expressive manner. It is known that nitric oxide can bind to many heme-containing prosthetic groups in enzymes and alter their function. This may lead to changes in electron transport chain activity in mitochondria. In some enzymes, ⋅NO is also able to bind to iron not associated to heme groups. An example are enzymes containing iron-sulphur complexes such as complex 1 of the mitochondria electron transport chain [59]. Those interactions are possible to happen and may sustain the suppressive levels on ⋅NO production after HHQ exposition.

Literature points that oxidative stress following HHQ consumption is likely to decreases fat catabolism by reducing ⋅NO availability. An indirect calorimetry study showed that one-week ingestion of HHQ-reduced coffee led to significantly higher fat utilization in healthy humans [1]. Further studies of the group revealed that the reduction of HHQ levels in roasted coffee stimulated fat utilization by increasing ⋅NO availability (Keiko Ishida, et al., (2018). One theory taken is that HHQ-derived superoxide anions can rapidly inactivate ⋅NO to form peroxynitrite and inhibit NOS activity [60]. There is an clear involvement of the endothelium-derived vasodilator NO in regulation of fatty acid metabolism [61–63]. In vascular tissues, nitric oxide activates guanylyl cyclase, which causes muscle relaxation and blood flow through the vassels. Reduction of the bioavailability of ⋅NO has been associated with vascular metabolism as an effect of HHQ intake in rodents [18, 19] and humans [14, 21].

Nitric oxide is also involved in many signaling processes of neuronal physiology. It has been implicated in the regulation of neurotransmitter release and might affect differentiation and regeneration of neuronal tissue. In an interesting manner, coenzyme Q10 is reported to be present in the Golgi complex of neuronal cells as a lipid soluble antioxidant synthesized in an endogenous way. It has been demonstrated that Q10 could help to balance redox signaling pathway counteracting the excess of ROS production that is responsible for cellular oxidative damage such as lipid peroxidation, protein nitrosylation and DNA oxidation [64]. It is known that Q10 is a mobile lipophilic electron carrier critical for electron transfer in the mitochondrial and Golgi compartments and plasma membrane. Among distinct functions, Q10 is also responsible for NAD(P)H-oxidoreductase-dependent reactions, which includes NO synthesis [65].

The results obtained using in vitro experiments presented solid evidence of increased ubiquinone production by human hepatocytes when treated with HHQ for 24 h, especially substantial increases in the activity of complexes I and II, stabilization of SOD activity, reduction in nitric oxide expression and increasing ubiquinol catabolism.

Molecular docking studies with MHQ-4-sulfate variant show that the sulfate groups are oriented towards the guanidinium-containing side chain of arginine p.123, similar to the carboxylate group of PHBA, stabilized at an average distance of 3 Å. The hydroxyl groups at C1 stabilize at an average distance of 2.6 Å from the O-C position of the substrate geranyl pyrophosphate, consistent with experiments conducted by Yang, et al., (2023) [29] using the same optimized UbiA model (PDB 4OD5). The molecular docking simulation with PHBA yielded nine stable binding poses, with binding energies ranging from − 5.7 to −5.2 kcal·mol⁻¹. Likely, MHQ-4-sulfate also produced nine binding poses, with binding energies ranging from − 6.2 to −4.0 kcal·mol⁻¹ (Fig. SI 21). Both substrates exhibited energetically favorable binding poses that are compatible with the completion of the enzymatic reaction.

Our simulations indicate that the MHQ-4-sulfate compound is the most viable derivative to be used as a substrate for COQ2. A study presenting results in accordance with Acosta, et al., (2019) [41], using cell cultures showed that vanillic acid is also assimilated by COQ2, leading to the methoxylated intermediate capable of supplementing deficiencies in COQ10 production caused by COQ3 mutations. This evidence reinforces the thesis that MHQ-4-sulfate is a viable substrate for COQ2, since the compound shares a similar chemical configuration to vanillate.

From the results here presented, which highlights the increase in the activities of ETC complexes I and II, the reduction in ⋅NO production, and the stabilization of SOD activity, authors are pointing to the assimilation of HHQ as a substrate in CoQ10 biosynthesis. HHQ showed superior results to PHBA in all experiments, with PHBA being the known aromatic precursor of CoQ10. Computational simulation assays demonstrated that sulfated derivatives of HHQ can be assimilated by native human COQ2. The hypothesis raised in this study requires the action of a mitochondrial sulfatase enzyme (SULF), such as aryl sulfatase B (ARSB) [66], and the Golgi complex for the hydrolysis of the sulfate ester and the consequent release of hydroquinone intermediates for subsequent enzymes. It is worth noting that HHQ is an endogenous compound already cited as a product of PHBA catabolism. These authors study the possibility of HHQ being produced by the catabolism of catecholamine neurotransmitters by gut microbiota, followed by assimilation in a pathway similar to p-cresol sulfate. We also investigated the hypothesis of HHQ being produced independently of aromatic amino acids, being a product of D-Chiro-Inositol (DCI) catabolism in the liver. The pharmacological potential of HHQ and sulfated derivatives as drugs or dietary supplements for a series of disorders such as treatment of type II diabetes, diseases resulting from metabolic syndrome, and syndromes resulting from mutations affecting the biosynthetic pathway of CoQ10.

Materials and methods

Chemical compounds

HHQ (A22 × 03233 - Alfa Chemistry, purity 98%), PABA (A9878 - Sigma-Aldrich), PHBA (H20059 - Sigma-Aldrich) were suspended in ultrapure water and tests were conducted at final concentration of 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1. METF (317240 - Sigma-Aldrich) was tested with 5.10− 6 mol.L− 1, and TBHP (458139 - Sigma-Aldrich).

Cell culture and cytotoxic assay

The established human HepG2 hepatoma cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (MD, USA). HepG2 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum with humidified atmosphere, 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Cell viability of HepG2 against different treatment was measured using the MTT assay in 96-well plates at a density of 8.104 cells.mL− 1. After 24 h, cells were treated with different concentrations of extracts and incubated for 24 h. Negative controls were treated with the same amounts of solution for 24 h. MTT dye was placed in serum-free medium and and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. To obtain a formazan violet product, 100 mL of DMSO was dissolved in each well. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Spectra Max M2e, Molecular Devices, USA) at l 570 nm. All readings were compared with the control, which represented 100% viability. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least four times.

Insulin resistance model (HepG2/IRM)

The experiments in this study followed similar protocol published recently by our group for insulin resistance model [22] and was adapted from Kheirollahzadeh et al. (2022) [67]. Briefly, to establish an insulin-resistant cell model (HepG2/IRM), a gradient of insulin at different concentrations was initially tested for viability through MTT. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 8.104 cells.mL− 1 in DMEM supplemented with a high glucose concentration (2.5.10− 2 mol.L− 1). After 24 h incubation, cells were treated with insulin at concentrations of 10− 4 mol.L− 1 to 10− 7 mol.L− 1. Best parameters for insulin concentration was selected for IR model and further experiments were processed with insulin at 5.10− 6 mol.L− 1 for 24 h. Further treatments were performed with different compounds and left for 24 h, as following: HHQ 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1; PABA 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1; PHBA 5.10− 5 mol.L− 1; METF (5.10− 6 mol.L− 1) and TBHP (2.10− 5 mol.L− 1).

Mitochondrial complex I (CI) and complex II (CII) activities

The methodology of Spinazzi et al. (2012) [45] was used to verify the activity of complex I (NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase) of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Complex I activity was measured at λ 600 nm for 3 min with positive control rotenone (10− 3mol.L− 1). Enzymatic activity was read at 37 °C using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu model UV-1700). The results were presented as percentages of control. Each value comes from four readings for each sample (2 with and 2 without inhibitor). The assessment of Mitochondrial Complex II (CII, succinate dehydrogenase) activity was also adapted by [45] and used to verify the activity of complex II of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. The cell lysate was exposed to 2-thenoyltrifluoroacetone TTFA 5.10− 4 mol.L− 1, and the activity was monitored for 3 min at λ 600 nm (spectrophotometer Shimadzu model UV-1700) at 37 °C, after an incubation period of 10 min. The results were presented as percentage in relation to the control.

Nitric oxide (⋅NO) assay

Cells were plated in 6-well plates at 3.105 cells mL− 1 and exposed to different treatments, similar protocol applied to the ATP assay. Cells were lysed after treatment using 100 µL of RIPA buffer (5.10− 2 mol.L− 1 Tris - pH 8,0; 1.5.10− 1mol.L− 1 NaCl; 1,0% NP-40; 0,5% sodium deoxycholate; 0,1% SDS) per well, during 10 min at 4 °C. Cell lysate was collected from each well and centrifuged for 10 min at 14.000 rpm. Supernatant was collected and total protein content was measured using a Qubit protein assay kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to manufactory instructions. Levels of nitric oxide were determined as nitrite (NO2−) production, which is a NO metabolite, using the Griess reaction-based method [68]. For the assay, 50 µL of the sample were reacted with an equal volume of Griess reagent (0.1% naphthyl ethylenediamine and 1% sulfanilamide in 5% H3PO4) for 10 min at room temperature, and the absorbance read at λ 550 nm in a microplate reader. Sodium nitroprusside was used as the standard. The results were expressed as percentage of control.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

Cells lysates were collected after treatment with RIPA buffer for each well using a 6-well plate, using a similar protocol as applied in NO assay. SOD activity was measured by the inhibition of self-catalytic adrenochrome formation rate at λ 480 nm, in a reaction medium containing 1 mmol/L adrenaline (pH 2.0) and 5.10− 2 mol.L− 1 glycine (pH 10.2) at 30 °C for 3 min [69]. The results from kinetics were presented as percentages.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) activity

ATP activity was monitored through the Luminescent ATP Determination Kit (A22066 -Sigma Aldrich). Quantitative determination of ATP bioluminescence assay is evaluated with recombinant firefly luciferase and its substrate D-luciferin. The assay is based on luciferaseʼs requirement for ATP in producing light. A standard curve was prepared with ATP concentrations ranging from 10− 6 mol.L− 1 to 10− 5 mol.L− 1, substituting ATP-containing samples for the ATP standard solutions provided in the kit with total equal volume of the experimental sample assays. Cells were plated in 6-well plates at 3.105 cells.mL− 1 and exposed to different treatments. After adherence, cells were treated with the highest non-cytotoxic concentration of HHQ, precursor acids and controls for 24 h. Cell cultures were lysed using 100 uL of TE buffer solution (10− 1 mol.L− 1 Tris-base; 4.10− 1 mol.L− 1 EDTA; pH 7,5) and samples were heated for 7 min in water bath. Briefly, 10 uL of cell lysed was mixed with 90 uL of standard reaction solution provided in the kit. Measurement was performed using a Luminometer (SpectraMax L – Molecular Devices) with emission maximum ~ λ 560 nm at pH 7.8. Results are expressed in relative light unit (RLU) that measures the light emitted when ATP interacts with the luciferase enzyme, producing a bioluminescent reaction. RLU are the units used for most luminescence measurements and comparison between samples was taken using a ATP standard curve.

HPLC-HRMS analysis

Prior to identification, cells were lysed with 150 uL of RIPA buffer (5.10–2 mol.L-1 Tris - pH 8,0; 1.5.10–1 mol.L-1 NaCl; 1,0% NP-40; 0,5% sodium deoxycholate; 0,1% SDS) per well, during 10 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts (75uL) of KCl 150mM and methanol were added and samples were vortexed and centrifuged for 5 min at 15.000 rpm. The supernatant was transferred to new tubes, and 250 µL of hexane was added. The samples were mixed again and centrifuged to extract the lipophilic fraction, then slowly dried under nitrogen gas. Matabolomic study was performed according Nicoll, et al., 2024 [70]. Analytical serie was carried out on UHPLC system (Shimadzu Proeminence 20 A series) coupled to electrospray ionization q-TOF high resolution mass spectrometry (ESI–qTOF-HRMS) to detect negative and positive ions. Chromatographic system configuration: Collumn UCT Selectra® C18 100 × 2.1 mm, particle 3 μm performed at 25 °C. Binary gradient components: water: formic acid 0.1% (A) and Acetonitrile: formic acid 0.1% (B); gradient program: 0.0–0.1 min (98% A: 2%B), 2.0–32.0 min (44% A: 66% B), 32.0–35.0 min (98% A: 2%B); flow 0.2 mL min− 1; injection volume 20 µL. ESI–qTOF-HRMS setup: MicroTOF Q-II series spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics), ESI parameters: Nebulizer pressure (N2): 2.0 bar, dry heater: 200 °C, dry gas flow: 6.0 L min− 1, capillary set: 2500 V, MS parameters: set scan range: 80–2000 m/z, Funnel 1 RF: 300 Vpp, set collision cell (broad band Collision Induced Dissociation – bbCID): 25–50 eV, Acquisition data mode: MRM to MS and bbCID to MS2. Data extraction and treatment: Bruker Compass DataAnalysis software, version 4.3 (Build 110.102.1532); data filters used: Base Peak Chromatogram (BPC) of all MS, bbCID-BPC of all MS2, Extracted Ion Chromatogram (EIC) of all MS to integration areas.

Molecular modeling studies

Computational experiments were performed on UCSF Chimera version 1.17.3 [71] with AutoDock Vina plug-in version 1.1.2 [72]. Docking images were generated on ChimeraX version 1.7.1 [73]. The human COQ2 isoform 1 (hCOQ2) [26] were generated from Phyre2 server [74] using ApUbiA homolog of Aeropyrum pernix K1 (PDB 4OD5) as fold [75]. These PDB model 4OD5 is the available reference containing both n-prenyl-pyrophosphate and PHBA substrates crystallized at the lowest energy coordinates of the enzyme’s site, besides maintaining a high homology degree (63%) of hCOQ2. The aromatic ligands were modeled in their anionic forms with a pontual charge on the sulfate group. The minimized energy conformers were generated using the ChemOffice 21.0.0.28 package (free trial).

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments performed in triplicate, used to assess normal distribution data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey post-hoc test. Statistically significant of mean differences was accepted at a level of p ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses and plots were performed using OriginPro, Version 2024, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA.
