Rupture of a thyroid arteriovenous malformation supplied by the superior thyroid artery causing acute airway compromise
Authors: Yoshiki Furumura, Ryota Nanbu, Haruna Makita, Yasuji Ryu, Takahiko Nakajima, Kojiro Hirota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous thyroid hemorrhage is rare but can cause life-threatening airway compromise. Rupture associated with a thyroid arteriovenous malformation is exceptionally rare; to our knowledge, only one case has been previously reported. We report a case of an 82-year-old Japanese woman who presented with acute neck swelling and airway compromise secondary to rupture of a thyroid arteriovenous malformation supplied by the superior thyroid artery. The patient underwent early intubation for airway protection, followed by interventional radiology and surgical resection, which resulted in complete recovery. This case highlights the importance of prioritizing airway management and a multidisciplinary approach that combines interventional radiology and surgery for life-threatening thyroid hemorrhage.Keywords: rupture, thyroid arteriovenous malformation, airway management, interventional radiology, surgery

Introduction

Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are vascular anomalies characterized by a network of abnormal vessels (nidus) in which arterial feeders communicate directly with venous outflows, bypassing the normal capillary bed. Thyroid AVMs are rare. To the best of our knowledge, only 8 thyroid AVMs have been documented to date, including lesions arising within or adjacent to the thyroid gland [1-8]. Spontaneous rupture of the thyroid arteries is rare [9]. Among these, only one involved a thyroid AVM [3]. Here, we report a case of spontaneous rupture of a thyroid AVM supplied by the superior thyroid artery.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old Japanese woman was transferred to the emergency department because of sudden neck swelling that occurred after blowing her nose. The patient also complained of shortness of breath. She had no previous history of goiter, thyroid mass, or trauma. The patient had been receiving warfarin for thrombophlebitis and prednisolone for erythema at another hospital, where she had been followed for 11 years prior to presentation. Prednisolone was maintained at a low dose due to recurrence of erythema upon discontinuation. During long-term warfarin therapy, her prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT–INR) values showed fluctuation, with periods of both prolongation and subtherapeutic levels. On arrival, physical examination revealed a tender, firm mass on the left side of the neck. The patient drooled and had dysphagia. Subsequently, the patient developed progressive dyspnea. Her SpO2 decreased to 85% on room air. The cardiovascular function remained stable.

Immediate bedside ultrasonography was performed to evaluate the rapidly expanding neck mass. It demonstrated a large heterogeneous mass in the left thyroid lobe, and Color Doppler imaging showed pulsatile blood flow signals within the mass (Fig. 1).

Ultrasonography images of the left thyroid lobe in transverse (A) and longitudinal (B) views demonstrate a heterogeneous mass measuring 44 mm × 40 mm × 30 mm. Color Doppler images (C, D), both in the transverse views, showing pulsatile blood flow signals within the mass.

For airway protection, orotracheal intubation was successfully performed using videolaryngoscopy (Pentax-AWS S200; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan), with an anesthesiologist and otorhinolaryngologist on standby.

Diagnostic assessment

After initial stabilization, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a large hematoma in the left thyroid lobe, with pooling of contrast medium, indicating bleeding from the thyroid artery (Fig. 2).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck reveals a large hematoma in the left thyroid lobe, with pooling of contrast medium within the hematoma, indicating bleeding from the thyroid artery.

Thyroid function tests revealed the following: free thyroxine (FT4), 0.7 ng/dL (9.0 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.7-1.7 ng/dL [9.0-21.9 pmol/L]); free triiodothyronine (FT3), 2.2 pg/mL (3.4 pmol/L) (reference range, 2.3-4.1 pg/mL [3.5-6.3 pmol/L]); and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), 5.37 mIU/L (reference range, 0.50-4.30 mIU/L). All measurements were performed using the Lumipulse system (Fujirebio, Tokyo, Japan). The serum thyroglobulin level was 476 ng/mL (476 μg/L) (reference range, ≤33.7 ng/mL [≤33.7 μg/L]), as measured using the Elecsys Tg II assay (Roche Diagnostics KK, Tokyo, Japan), with negative antithyroglobulin antibody. The platelet count was within the normal range, and coagulation tests, including prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen degradation product, and D-dimer, yielded normal results, indicating no bleeding tendency.

A hemorrhagic vascular lesion was suspected based on the contrast-enhanced CT findings.

Treatment

Surgical resection was planned to achieve hemostasis and relieve the tracheal compression. However, because of the high risk of perioperative bleeding, the surgical, radiology, and intensive care teams jointly decided to perform interventional radiology (IVR) before surgery to control bleeding.

Angiography revealed extravasation from a branch of the left superior thyroid artery, which was identified as the source of bleeding (Fig. 3). Embolization was subsequently performed via a transfemoral approach through the right common femoral artery, using a mixture of N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA; Histoacryl, B. Braun, Melsungen AG) and ethiodized oil in a 1:5 ratio.

Interventional radiology image shows extravasation (arrowhead) from a branch of the left superior thyroid artery, identified as the bleeding source.

The following day, a left thyroid lobectomy was performed to achieve vascular control and relieve tracheal compression. The left superior thyroid artery was ligated, and the hematoma was removed (Fig. 4). No active bleeding was observed, and blood loss was minimal. Postoperatively, tracheal deviation improved (Fig. 5).

(A) Clinical photograph showing anterior cervical swelling and subcutaneous ecchymosis before surgery. (B) Intraoperative view before ligation of the left superior thyroid artery. (C) Intraoperative view after ligation of the left superior thyroid artery and removal of the hematoma.

Chest radiographs obtained before (A) and after (B) surgery showing improvement in tracheal deviation following hematoma removal.

Owing to persistent pharyngolaryngeal edema, tracheostomy was performed on postoperative day 6. Subsequent laryngofiberscopy confirmed resolution of the edema.

Outcome and follow-up

On postoperative day 34, thyroid function tests revealed the following values: FT4, 0.9 ng/dL (11.6 pmol/L); FT3, 2.3 pg/mL (3.5 pmol/L); and TSH, 5.69 mIU/L. Thyroid hormone replacement therapy was not required. Successful decannulation was achieved on postoperative day 48. The patient underwent rehabilitation and was discharged without any sequelae.

Histopathological examination revealed vascular niduses within the thyroid, consistent with an AVM, and a ruptured portion of the nidus (Fig. 6). We concluded that the hemorrhagic vascular lesion was a ruptured thyroid AVM supplied by the superior thyroid artery.

Pathological findings of the resected left thyroid lobe. (A) Gross photograph of the cut surface showing abnormally dilated (arrow) and ruptured (arrowhead) vessels. (B) Microscopic image (hematoxylin and eosin staining, magnification ×40) showing vascular niduses composed of vessels that could not be clearly distinguished as arteries or veins, consistent with an arteriovenous malformation. (C) Microscopic image (hematoxylin and eosin staining, magnification ×40) showing a ruptured portion of the nidus. (D) Microscopic image (Elastica–Van Gieson staining, magnification ×40) of the same ruptured area shown in (C), highlighting the loss of smooth muscle fibers, indicating structural immaturity and fragility of the vessel wall.

Discussion

In this case, the rupture of a branch of the left superior thyroid artery led to the formation of a large cervical hematoma.

In addition to tumor-associated, traumatic, or iatrogenic etiologies [10-12], spontaneous rupture of a thyroid artery has been reported in association with arterial aneurysms, including dissected aneurysms [9]. In the literature, most reported cases of spontaneous thyroid artery rupture have involved the inferior thyroid artery [9]. The previously reported case of thyroid AVM rupture also involved the inferior thyroid artery [3]. To the best of our knowledge, rupture of a thyroid AVM supplied by the superior thyroid artery is exceptionally rare.

In our patient, contrast-enhanced CT findings suggestive of a hemorrhagic vascular lesion, together with the absence of a prior thyroid mass, trauma, or recent invasive neck procedures, supported a primary vascular etiology. Previous reports have described coughing or physical exertion as possible triggers before rupture [9]. In this case, nose blowing may have contributed to the bleeding. In addition, age-related vascular fragility, chronic prednisolone use, and anticoagulant therapy with warfarin may have increased our patient's susceptibility to arterial rupture—although the PT–INR was within the normal range at presentation. Given the variability observed during long-term warfarin therapy, this normal value was not inconsistent with the overall course of anticoagulation control.

Cervical hemorrhage, of which intrathyroidal hemorrhage is a rare form, can rapidly compromise the airway and may be fatal [3, 10, 11]. The first and most crucial step in the management of cervical hemorrhage is securing the airway, followed by diagnostic assessment and appropriate treatment [9]. In our case, timely and controlled intubation successfully stabilized the airway and facilitated a comprehensive evaluation.

Therapeutic options for cervical hemorrhage due to rupture of the thyroid arteries include IVR and surgery. Reported management strategies range from IVR-guided embolization alone [12] and surgical management [10, 11] to a combined endovascular and surgical approach [9, 13]. In cases of AVM, massive intraoperative bleeding has been reported when surgical resection was performed without prior embolization [4]. Therefore, preoperative embolization is considered beneficial for reducing blood loss and facilitating safer resection. A previous report suggested that surgery within 3 days after embolization may be preferable, as AVM recanalization or neovascularization can occur approximately 10 days later [5]. Although the available literature is limited, we based our management strategy on previously published reports and achieved a successful outcome using a combined endovascular and surgical approach, thereby providing additional support for this strategy.

This case highlights the importance of prioritizing airway management and a multidisciplinary approach that combines IVR and surgery for life-threatening thyroid hemorrhage. Although spontaneous thyroid hemorrhage is rare, clinicians should consider it as a potential cause of sudden neck swelling or acute airway compromise. The management of thyroid artery rupture requires a prompt and structured approach consisting of airway protection, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment with IVR and/or surgery.
