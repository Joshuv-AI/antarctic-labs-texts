Linking inflammatory and structural neuroimaging biomarkers in treatment-resistant depression: Current evidence and emerging hypotheses
Authors: Simge Seren Kirlioglu Balcioglu, Marco Paolini, Bernhard T Baune, Igor Branchi, Annamaria Cattaneo, Marion Leboyer, Benedetta Vai, Livia De Picker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•TRD is associated with immune alterations across peripheral and central domains.•TRD may show hippocampal and white matter changes that may be linked to immune-related processes.•Integrating immune and neuroimaging findings may contribute to biologically informed stratification of treatment-resistant depression.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and multifaceted mental illness arising from the interplay of genetic, environmental, biological, and psychological factors (Marx et al., 2023). Affecting more than 280 million individuals worldwide, it represents the leading cause of disability due to chronic functional impairment (Friedrich, 2017; World Health Organization). A particular challenge in managing MDD is the high rate of insufficient response to treatment. Up to 60% of patients may show an incomplete response to their first antidepressant trial, and around 30% respond poorly to multiple courses of appropriate pharmacotherapy, a condition often referred to as treatment-resistant depression (TRD) (Furukawa et al., 2021; Rush et al., 2006).

Although the concept of TRD has evolved since its initial formulation in the 1970s, most frameworks broadly converge on defining TRD as inadequate improvement despite at least one or two adequately delivered antidepressant treatments (European Medicines Agency, 2023; Heimann, 1974; Thase and Rush, 1997; U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, 2018). However, these definitions remain primarily operational rather than biologically informed. The absence of validated biomarkers hampers diagnostic precision, limits comparability across studies, and impedes progress toward mechanism-based interventions. This gap underscores the importance of identifying biological signatures specific to TRD; in this regard, immune and inflammatory markers may help explain mechanisms of resistance and guide the development of targeted interventions.

Over the past two decades, increasing evidence has highlighted aberrant inflammatory signaling, such as elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in individuals meeting TRD criteria, suggesting that a subset of patients may exhibit a distinct immunobiological phenotype (Chamberlain et al., 2019; Strawbridge et al., 2015). However, these peripheral markers have often been examined independently of other biological domains. In parallel, structural neuroimaging research has consistently demonstrated alterations in cortical and subcortical regions and white matter tracts implicated in mood regulation and stress response in TRD. Yet, the associations between inflammatory abnormalities and these neuroanatomical alterations remain poorly integrated and insufficiently understood (Han et al., 2024; Klok et al., 2019).

Given the multifactorial and heterogeneous nature of TRD, there is a need to synthesize findings across biological domains in order to better contextualize immune–inflammatory abnormalities within a broader neurobiological framework. Accordingly, this review aims to summarize and integrate existing evidence on immune–inflammatory biomarkers in TRD, examine their reported convergence with structural neuroimaging findings, and discuss how these alterations may be relevant to understanding heterogeneity in antidepressant efficacy, with particular reference to plausible biological mechanisms proposed in the literature.

This review was conducted as a narrative synthesis aimed at integrating findings from immune-inflammatory and structural neuroimaging studies in TRD. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed and Web of Science using combinations of keywords including “treatment-resistant depression”, “inflammation”, “cytokines”, and “structural neuroimaging”.

Studies investigating immune or structural neuroimaging alterations in TRD were broadly considered, without restricting inclusion to studies explicitly examining their interaction. This approach was adopted because relatively few studies have directly investigated the relationship between immune-inflammatory markers and neuroimaging findings. The aim was therefore to provide a comprehensive overview of both domains and to discuss their potential links at a conceptual level. Given the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, biomarkers, imaging modalities, and outcome measures, a formal systematic review or meta-analysis approach was not feasible.

Peripheral immuno-inflammatory markers of treatment-resistant depression

Peripheral immune-inflammatory abnormalities in TRD encompass both baseline alterations relative to healthy controls and differential patterns of change following treatment. While several inflammatory markers are elevated prior to treatment exposure, emerging evidence indicates that some normalize with therapeutic interventions, whereas others persist despite clinical improvement, suggesting the coexistence of state-dependent and trait-like immune alterations in TRD.

Baseline peripheral inflammatory abnormalities in TRD compared with non-TRD depression and healthy controls

Acute-phase response and hematological markers

Early studies suggested that major depression is accompanied by a chronic acute-phase response, reflected by reduced total serum protein and albumin levels and altered globulin fractions, with limited normalization following antidepressant treatment, an effect that appeared more pronounced in TRD (Van Hunsel et al., 1996). Although absolute protein concentrations did not differ between TRD and non-TRD, TRD patients exhibited lower α1 and α2 globulin fractions, a pattern interpreted as exhaustion of positive acute-phase protein synthesis in longstanding, treatment-resistant illness.

Consistent with this inflammatory profile, patients with TRD exhibited significantly elevated white blood cell, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, as well as higher levels of CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and complement components C3 and C4 than controls (Lauden et al., 2021). Together, these findings support the presence of a chronic, inadequately resolved acute-phase response in TRD.

C-reactive protein and systemic inflammation

CRP has been one of the most extensively studied biomarkers in TRD, although findings remain heterogeneous. Several studies reported elevated baseline CRP levels in TRD compared with healthy controls, even after adjustment for body mass index (Galvão-Coelho et al., 2020; Strawbridge et al., 2021). In addition, a higher proportion of individuals with CRP exceeding 3 mg/L has been observed in TRD relative to non-TRD patients (Congio et al., 2020). Furthermore, large-scale analyses have shown that TRD patients display markedly increased inflammatory activity, a pattern that has been associated with poorer treatment outcomes (Lauden et al., 2021; Strawbridge et al., 2019). Notably, after body mass index adjustment, CRP elevation appears to be specific to TRD, with no significant increase observed in treatment-responsive or untreated depression, indicating a distinct inflammatory phenotype associated with resistance rather than depression per se (Chamberlain et al., 2019).

Other studies however reported no significant differences in CRP levels between TRD, non-TRD, and healthy controls (Huang et al., 2018; Uint et al., 2019), and machine-learning approaches suggested that CRP does not robustly discriminate TRD from non-TRD (Sánchez-Carro et al., 2023); further, some investigations found CRP levels in TRD to be higher than in healthy controls but lower than in non-TRD patients, suggesting a non-linear relationship between inflammatory burden and treatment resistance (Wu et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings may indicate that CRP reflects inflammatory heterogeneity in TRD rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic biomarker.

Cytokines and soluble immune receptors

Cytokine profiling provides further evidence for immune dysregulation in TRD. Early studies demonstrated that while both IL-6 and IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) peripheral levels are elevated in MDD compared with healthy controls, only IL-6 appears to be consistently increased in TRD relative to controls, with minimal or no differences between TRD and non-TRD patients (Maes et al., 1997a). Subsequent studies have provided mixed but converging support for enhanced inflammatory signaling in TRD. Several investigations reported significantly elevated plasma IL-6 levels in TRD compared with healthy controls, independent of body mass index (Rengasamy et al., 2018), whereas other cohorts found comparable IL-6 concentrations after metabolic adjustment (Galvão-Coelho et al., 2020) highlighting interindividual variability in inflammatory phenotypes.

Beyond IL-6, broader cytokine profiling suggests widespread immune dysregulation in TRD. Compared with healthy controls, patients with TRD have shown altered basal serum levels of multiple cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-4, and interferon-γ, indicative of both pro- and anti-inflammatory pathway activation (Strawbridge et al., 2019; Zincir et al., 2016). Meta-analytic evidence indicates that persistently elevated TNF-α is a robust correlate of treatment resistance, with non-responders showing higher baseline inflammatory burden and limited normalization over time (Strawbridge et al., 2015). In line with this, several studies reported higher TNF-α levels in TRD compared with healthy controls (Strawbridge et al., 2021).

Soluble cytokine receptors further refine this picture. Serum TNF-α receptor-1 (sTNF-R1) levels were significantly higher in patients with TRD compared with both non-TRD patients and healthy controls, whereas no significant group differences were detected for soluble IL-2 receptor or soluble IL-6 receptor levels, indicating selective activation of TNF-related signaling pathways in TRD (Huang et al., 2018).

Cellular immune composition and immune regulation

Beyond circulating cytokines, TRD is associated with alterations in immune cell composition and regulatory factors. Reduced serum dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP-IV), a regulator of T-cell activation and cytokine signaling, has been observed in both TRD and non-TRD compared with healthy controls and correlates positively with CD4+ T-cell counts, suggesting impaired immune regulation in depression (Maes et al., 1997a). By contrast, serum zinc, an acute-phase–sensitive micronutrient, was significantly lower in TRD than in both non-TRD patients and controls, supporting its potential relevance to treatment resistance (Maes et al., 1997b, Maes et al., 1997c).

At the cellular level, a study comparing T-cell subpopulations and cytokine profiles between TRD patients and healthy controls reported lower percentages of T cells expressing activation markers (CD25 and CD95) and reduced serum IL-12p70 and TNF-α levels, but significantly higher IL-8 levels in TRD patients. IL-8 levels above a defined threshold were strongly associated with TRD, implicating persistent chemotactic signaling despite attenuated adaptive immune activation (Szałach et al., 2022). More recently, an immune cell profiling study suggests that TRD is characterized by shifts in immune cell composition rather than uniform hyperactivation, including reduced proportions of neutrophils, mast cells, and natural killer cells alongside increased CD4+ T-cell, indicating altered innate–adaptive immune balance (Sirés et al., 2025).

Neuroendocrine–immune interactions

Neuroendocrine dysregulation represents a key dimension of immune–inflammatory alterations in TRD. Classical studies described impaired glucocorticoid receptor–mediated feedback despite normal or elevated basal cortisol levels, indicating glucocorticoid resistance (Bauer et al., 2002, 2003). Markopoulou et al. (2009) further demonstrated elevated cortisol levels and increased cortisol/Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) ratios in TRD compared with healthy controls, suggesting unopposed glucocorticoid activity (Markopoulou et al., 2009).

Subsequent work identified heterogeneity within TRD, including subgroups with hypocortisolemia and blunted cortisol awakening responses that normalize following effective intervention (Galvão et al., 2018). At the molecular level, reduced glucocorticoid receptor expression and increased FKBP5 further support glucocorticoid resistance as a biological signature of TRD (Cattaneo et al., 2020).

Immunometabolic, neurotrophic, and cellular aging markers

Beyond classical inflammatory pathways, metabolic, neurotrophic, and cellular aging markers also distinguish TRD. Dysregulation of amino acid and biopterin metabolism has been reported, with lower biopterin and tryptophan levels and higher neopterin/biopterin ratios in TRD compared with healthy controls, supporting immune–monoaminergic dysfunction (Hoekstra et al., 2001). Elevated leptin levels in TRD particularly among patients with elevated hs-CRP, suggest metabolic–inflammatory alterations not observed in non-TRD (Congio et al., 2020).

Neurotrophic findings are mixed, with studies reporting reduced brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-C alongside increased Tie2 signaling in TRD (Pisoni et al., 2018), while others observing no differences or even elevated BDNF levels compared with controls (Maes et al., 1997a). These inconsistencies may reflect differences in study populations, treatment status, and methodological factors, including assay variability and sample handling. In addition, potential publication bias and small sample sizes may further contribute to variability across studies, limiting the strength of current conclusions.

At the cellular level, TRD patients exhibited shorter leukocyte telomere length and increased mitochondrial DNA copy number compared with healthy controls, indicating accelerated cellular aging and trait-like biological vulnerability (Squassina et al., 2025).

Finally, recent advances in extracellular vesicle research may provide a peripheral window into central neuroinflammation. At baseline, patients with TRD showed elevated astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicle markers (glial fibrillary acidic protein, S100β, CD81) and increased inflammatory cytokines compared with healthy controls, supporting heightened astrocyte activation and neuroinflammation (Xu et al., 2023). Together, these findings converge on the view that TRD represents a biologically distinct subtype of depression characterized by persistent immune–inflammatory activation, altered immune cell composition, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and metabolic and cellular aging signatures.

Although a wide range of peripheral biomarkers has been investigated in TRD, findings are often heterogeneous and effect sizes are modest. Across studies, however, certain markers, particularly IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP emerge more consistently, suggesting that they may represent the most reproducible indicators of immune dysregulation in TRD. Nevertheless, their clinical utility remains limited by variability across populations and study designs.

Differences between responders and non-responders within TRD

Baseline case–control differences alone are insufficient to explain treatment resistance. Instead, responder–non-responder comparisons within TRD provide critical insight into biological mechanisms linked to symptom improvement or persistence.

Early studies consistently demonstrated that several immune-related abnormalities in TRD appear to be treatment-independent. Reduced serum DPP-IV activity remained unchanged despite clinical improvement and showed strong pre–post correlations, supporting its interpretation as a stable, trait-like immune abnormality rather than a state marker of symptom severity (Maes et al., 1997a). Similarly, IL-6 and IL-1Ra levels remained elevated following antidepressant treatment and did not distinguish treatment-responsive depression from TRD, indicating shared inflammatory alterations across depressive subtypes (Maes et al., 1997a). In contrast, persistently low serum zinc which is closely linked to immune-inflammatory markers and the degree of prior treatment resistance emerged as a more specific biological feature differentiating TRD from treatment-responsive depression, despite treatment-related reductions in serum copper (Maes et al., 1997b, Maes et al., 1997c).

Neuroendocrine studies further suggest that treatment response in TRD is not driven by cortisol levels per se, but rather by the balance between cortisol and DHEA (Markopoulou et al., 2009). Responders exhibited lower baseline DHEA levels and a persistently higher cortisol/DHEA ratio, whereas non-responders showed a relatively preserved DHEA balance and minimal endocrine change with treatment, pointing to differences in HPA-axis flexibility rather than overall activation.

Several ECT studies provide converging evidence that immune changes associated with clinical response in TRD are predominantly central rather than peripheral. While ECT did not induce overall pre-to-post changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of IL-6, neopterin, sCD14, sCD163, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), or monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, responders and remitters showed selective reductions in markers of microglial/macrophage activation, whereas non-responders did not (Kranaster et al., 2018). Notably, higher baseline CSF inflammatory activity predicted greater symptom improvement, suggesting the presence of an inflammation-sensitive TRD subtype. Complementary findings from a broader CSF cytokine panel indicated that although ECT exerts nonspecific immunomodulatory effects, reductions in IL-17, macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1α, Regulated upon Activation Normal T Cell Expressed and Secreted (RANTES/CCL5), and IL-2R were specific to responders, while attenuated interferon-γ–induced protein 10 (IP-10 or CXCL10) increases characterized remitters, consistent with a more regulated central inflammatory response (Mindt et al., 2020).

Beyond classical inflammatory markers, growth factor and cellular aging studies further reinforce biological heterogeneity within TRD. Stratification by treatment response revealed that low baseline VEGF-D predicted non-response, and that non-responders showed progressive declines in VEGF and VEGF-C during inpatient treatment, whereas responders maintained a relatively preserved neurotrophic profile (Pisoni et al., 2018). In contrast, markers of cellular aging including leukocyte telomere length and mitochondrial DNA copy number, were altered in TRD relative to controls but did not differentiate responders from non-responders following ECT, supporting their role as trait-like disease markers rather than predictors of treatment response (Squassina et al., 2025).

Longitudinal inflammatory profiling further indicates that while short-term responder–non-responder differences may be subtle, elevated post-treatment inflammation robustly predicts poorer long-term outcomes. Higher levels of IL-6, TNF-α, CRP, and related markers after treatment were associated with greater symptom persistence at follow-up, underscoring the prognostic relevance of sustained inflammatory activity in TRD (Strawbridge et al., 2019).

Taken together, responder–non-responder comparisons within TRD suggest that treatment responsiveness is associated with selective modulation of central immune and neurotrophic pathways, whereas persistent non-response reflects stable, trait-like immune, neuroendocrine, and cellular abnormalities. These findings suggest that TRD may not represent a unitary condition, but rather a biologically heterogeneous syndrome that may comprise inflammation-sensitive and inflammation-insensitive subtypes, although current evidence remains heterogeneous and does not yet allow for clear biological stratification. Possible pathophysiological links between immune system and treatment resistance

Both cross-sectional studies examining baseline immune marker differences and longitudinal studies assessing immune changes over the course of treatment in TRD may point to relevant pathophysiological mechanisms. Despite these advances, a complete and exhaustive biological understanding of treatment-resistant depression remains elusive. Against this background, the following section discusses possible pathophysiological links between immune dysregulation and treatment resistance.

Unresolved inflammation and failure of resolution

Evidence from responder–non-responder comparisons within TRD suggests that treatment resistance is characterized not merely by heightened inflammatory activity, but by a failure to appropriately resolve inflammation. Across multiple studies, clinical non-response has been associated with persistence or even escalation of pro-inflammatory and chemotactic signals, particularly within the central nervous system. Non-responders show sustained or increasing CSF levels of IL-17, RANTES, MIP-1α, IL-2R, and IP-10, whereas responders exhibit selective down-regulation of these pathways following effective treatment (Mindt et al., 2020). Similarly, neuroimmune mediators such as MIF decrease only in remitters, while non-remitters show stable or increasing levels, further supporting the notion that immune resolution rather than non-specific downregulation of inflammatory activity per se, is critical for clinical improvement (Kranaster et al., 2018).

Baseline inflammatory burden also differentiates TRD from non-TRD depression, with IL-6 levels following a graded pattern (TRD > non-TRD > controls) accompanied by elevated IL-1Ra (Maes et al., 1997a). In addition, CRP, TNF-α, IL-8, MIP-1α, MCP-4, IP-10, and thymus and activation regulated chemokine (TARC) are consistently elevated in TRD or persistent non-responders, and are associated with poorer clinical outcomes (Strawbridge et al., 2019). This inflammatory profile is accompanied by reductions in acute-phase–related proteins, including DPP-IV, albumin, transferrin, and zinc, all of which correlate with IL-6–driven immune activation, reflecting a chronic acute-phase response (Maes et al., 1997a, Maes et al., 1997b, Maes et al., 1997c). Collectively, these findings support a model in which responders show a shift toward inflammatory down-regulation, whereas non-responders exhibit sustained or worsening immune activation.

Impaired neurotrophic and angiogenic responses

In addition to inflammatory dysregulation, TRD is associated with impaired neurotrophic and angiogenic support, implicating altered plasticity and vascular signaling in treatment resistance. Baseline deficits in BDNF, VEGF-C, and VEGF-D, together with elevated Tie2 levels, have been observed in TRD compared with controls (Pisoni et al., 2018). Longitudinal analyses further demonstrate that only non-responders show progressive declines in VEGF and VEGF-C during treatment, whereas responders maintain relatively stable levels, suggesting preserved neurotrophic resilience in treatment-responsive patients (Pisoni et al., 2018). Moreover, higher baseline VEGF-D levels predict subsequent clinical response, indicating that intact angiogenic capacity may be a key determinant of treatment sensitivity in TRD (Pisoni et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings suggest that TRD involves reduced neurotrophic and angiogenic support, particularly in patients who fail to respond to treatment (Pisoni et al., 2018).

Microglia–T cell axis and CNS-specific inflammation

Studies examining immune changes following ECT highlight the central, rather than peripheral, nature of immune mechanisms underlying treatment resistance. Across TRD samples, most CSF and serum inflammatory markers show minimal overall change after ECT; however, responders and remitters exhibit selective normalization of central innate immune markers (Kranaster et al., 2018; Mindt et al., 2020). Specifically, CSF MIF levels decrease significantly in remitters, with sCD14 and IL-6 showing remission-specific downward trends, while no corresponding changes are observed in serum, underscoring CNS-specific immune modulation (Kranaster et al., 2018).

At baseline, higher CSF sCD14 levels strongly predict greater reductions in depressive symptom severity, with CSF sCD163 showing a similar association at trend level, suggesting that elevated but modifiable microglial activation may characterize an ECT-responsive TRD subgroup (Kranaster et al., 2018). Beyond innate immune signaling, responder–non-responder divergence is further evident within the central T-cell and chemokine network. Responders exhibit reductions in CSF levels of IL-17, IL-2R, RANTES, MIP-1α, and IP-10 following treatment, whereas non-responders show persistence or increases in these mediators, indicating sustained adaptive immune activation within the CNS (Mindt et al., 2020).

Importantly, higher baseline IP-10 levels in both CSF and serum predict better response to ECT, suggesting that active but reversible chemokine signaling defines an inflammation-sensitive TRD subtype (Mindt et al., 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRD is characterized by heightened central immune activation involving both microglial and T-cell–related pathways, and that clinical response corresponds to selective down-regulation of CNS immune networks, while persistent activation marks treatment resistance (Kranaster et al., 2018; Mindt et al., 2020).

HPA axis dysregulation and immune–endocrine coupling

TRD is also characterized by dysregulation of the HPA axis, reflected in elevated cortisol levels and increased cortisol/DHEA ratios (Markopoulou et al., 2009). Notably, the highest cortisol/DHEA ratios are observed in responders rather than non-responders, suggesting that greater endocrine activation does not preclude treatment response and may reflect preserved biological flexibility (Markopoulou et al., 2009). This endocrine profile is closely linked to immune alterations, including elevated IL-6 and IL-1Ra and reduced acute-phase proteins, reinforcing the bidirectional coupling between chronic inflammation and glucocorticoid signaling in TRD (Maes et al., 1997a, 1997b). Overall, these findings suggest that treatment resistance may be related more to rigidity of immune–endocrine regulation than to the absolute magnitude of HPA activation (Markopoulou et al., 2009).

However, much of the evidence supporting central immune mechanisms is derived from relatively small CSF studies, which may limit generalizability. Future research should aim to validate these findings in larger samples and to develop more scalable approaches to assess central immune processes, including peripheral proxies and imaging-based markers.

Moreover, these mechanisms remain largely inferential, and future longitudinal and interventional studies will be essential to establish causal relationships between immune alterations, brain changes, and treatment resistance. Integrative approaches combining peripheral and central measures may further clarify underlying mechanisms.

Structural neuroimaging markers of TRD

If immune-inflammatory alterations are relevant to treatment resistance, their effects may be reflected in brain regions, involved in neuroplasticity, stress regulation, and large-scale network integration. Structural neuroimaging studies therefore provide a valuable context for examining associations between immune dysregulation and persistent depressive symptoms in TRD (Benedetti and Vai, 2023). Given the limited number of studies specifically examining TRD, evidence from broader MDD populations is discussed where relevant to provide context for potential mechanisms.

In contrast with the high number of neuroimaging studies investigating brain correlates of antidepressant response (Enneking et al., 2020), fewer focused specifically on TRD (Klok et al., 2019), and specific differences between TRD and non-TRD patients have not been extensively explored (Runia et al., 2022). Across studies, TRD patients seem to present lower grey matter volumes when compared to both healthy controls and non-TRD ones (Klok et al., 2019; Runia et al., 2022).

Concerning studies that directly compare patients groups divided by resistance status, reduced hippocampal volumes are a consistent finding in TRD (Abdallah et al., 2015; de Diego-Adeliño et al., 2013; Bravi et al., 2025), possibly pointing to the involvement of neurogenesis and neuroplasticity processes in antidepressant action and resistance (Boku et al., 2018; Harmer et al., 2017). Bilateral caudate nucleus has also been repeatedly found to be reduced in TRD (Ma et al., 2012; Shah et al., 2002), while results for other brain regions appear to be less robust, with some studies reporting reduced volumes in medial frontal, cingulate and insular cortices (Serra-Blasco et al., 2013), and others failing to find such an association (Ma et al., 2012). Treatment resistance appears also to be associated with overall brain atrophy and enlarged ventricles (Bravi et al., 2025; Paolini et al., 2025). More consistent results are reported by studies comparing TRD patients and healthy controls, with widespread volumetric reductions in the former in medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula, middle and superior temporal gyri and cerebellum (Machino et al., 2014; Lan et al., 2016); however, many of these alterations have also been identified in MDD patients by large mega-analyses regardless of resistance status (Schmaal et al.; Schmaal et al., 2016); and do no therefore appear to be specific of TRD patients.

Concerning white matter, reduced fractional anisotropy (FA), classically interpreted as impaired microstructural white matter integrity, has been identified in TRD patients when compared to both healthy controls (Peng et al., 2013) and, more importantly, to non resistant depressed patients (de Diego-Adeliño et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2011); among white matter tracts found to be involved, the corpus callosum, longitudinal fasciculum, internal and external capsule were frequently reported. Interestingly, one study reported reduced FA specifically in white matter tracts surrounding bilateral hippocampi (Zhou et al., 2011).

Fractional anisotropy, reflecting the degree of diffusion directionality of water molecules within white matter fibers, is usually regarded as an index of microstructural white matter integrity (Kochunov et al., 2012). Impaired myelinated fibers are thought to compromise rapid communication between distant brain regions (Zhai et al., 2020); provided that many of the identified white matter tracts connect grey matter hubs of the limbic system (Torrico and Abdijadid, 2023), a set of brain regions believed to be particularly involved in mood disorder pathophysiology (Price and Drevets, 2010). Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data seems to suggest that altered structural connectivity among these brain regions, renders antidepressant induced resolution of depressive symptoms less likely. Indeed, altered limbic functional connectivity has also been reported in TRD (Lui et al., 2011).

A higher load of white matter hyperintensities (WMH) has also been identified in TRD patients compared to treatment-sensitive ones (Paolini et al., 2025). WMH are white matter lesions that appear hyperintense in FLAIR sequences; they are linked to cardiovascular risk factors (albeit their exact etiology and pathophysiology is still controversial) and a regular finding in cerebral small vessel disease (Wardlaw et al., 2015; Koohi et al., 2023). Beside the effect of white matter damage on hampered antidepressant efficacy, additional factors might contribute to their relation with treatment resistance. Indeed, BDNF signaling, the putative final path through which antidepressants exert their action (Castrén and Monteggia, 2021), has been found to be reduced and linked to the development of depressive symptoms in coronary artery disease (Jin et al., 2018; Kuhlmann et al., 2017), and also associated to WMH (Pikula et al., 2013). Impaired BDNF signaling therefore, possibly related to low cardiovascular health, might underlie antidepressant resistance and its association with WMH. Links between inflammatory and neuroimaging data

Robust neuroimaging correlates of resistant depression are surprisingly sparse: Furthermore, methodological limitations might be particularly relevant for studies directly comparing TRD and treatment-sensitive patients: the most robust and replicated results were found by region of interest studies, in which differences between groups were investigated for the volume of a pre-determined brain region (mainly the hippocampus) (Abdallah et al., 2015; de Diego-Adeliño et al., 2013; Bravi et al., 2025); studies relying on a whole brain approach usually had small sample sizes, and largely failed to provide convergent results (Ma et al., 2012; Serra-Blasco et al., 2013; de Diego-Adeliño et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2011). Furthermore, TRD definition varied across studies, and the accepted definition of failure of at least two antidepressant trials with adequate dose and duration (European Medicines Agency, 2023; Heimann, 1974; Thase and Rush, 1997; U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, 2018) was not always employed.

Overall, TRD patients seem to be characterized by alterations in both grey and white matter, with reduced hippocampal and caudate volumes, overall brain atrophy, reduced FA and increased WMH load. Importantly, all these features have been found to be related to immune markers in MDD.

Reduced hippocampal volumes have been associated to serum levels of numerous inflammatory markers, including IL-6 (Frodl et al., 2012; Kakeda et al., 2018; Ironside et al., 2020), CRP (Frodl et al., 2012) and TNF- α (Ho et al., 2024). Concerning immune cells, neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (indicative of the balance between innate and adaptive immunity) was associated to smaller hippocampi, which in turn mediated its effects on antidepressant response in females (Paolini et al., 2023). Concerning glucocorticoids levels, while basal or post-dexamethasone suppression cortisol levels do not associate with hippocampal volumes (Kaymak et al., 2010), reduced expression of glucocorticoid-inducible genes, possibly indicative of glucocorticoid resistance, is strongly linked with smaller hippocampi (Frodl et al., 2012). Volumes of the hippocampus have been repeatedly found to positively correlate with the ratio between kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid, reflective of the balance between neuroprotective and neurotoxic metabolites produced in the kynurenine metabolic pathway (Savitz et al., 2015a, 2015b; Paul et al., 2022). While less extensively investigated, also caudate volumes have been associated to altered inflammatory markers and kynurenine metabolites (Savitz et al., 2015a, 2015b). Finally, CRP gene methylation, an indicator of chronic inflammation more stable than CRP serum measurement, has been negatively associated to global grey matter and cortical volumes (Green et al., 2021a).

Robust evidence also links immune status and white matter microstructure. IL-1β serum levels were negatively associated with FA both in drug naïve (Sugimoto et al., 2018), and in treated MDD patients (for which a negative association also emerged for TNF-α levels) (Sammer et al., 2022). Similar associations were also identified for CRP levels, with inverse associations with FA regardless of medication status (Thomas et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2022). Interestingly a well powered study found CRP levels to negatively associate with FA only in chronic MDD patients, defined as having a duration of the current depressive episode longer than two years (Cha et al., 2025). While few data exist on glucocorticoid resistance, higher levels of morning cortisol, possibly indicative of glucocorticoid resistance, have also been associated with reduced FA (Liu et al., 2016), while serum kynurenic acid to quinolinic acid ratio is positively associated with FA (Zheng et al., 2022).

While rarely investigated, WMH have also been associated to inflammation (specifically to CRP-related methylation, a putative index of longer-term inflammation compared to serum CRP measurement) (Green et al., 2021b). Furthermore, their presence has been robustly associated to cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes and smoking (Paolini et al., 2025; Iosifescu et al., 2006; Hickie et al., 2005; Dalby et al., 2010), whose relation with enhanced chronic inflammation has been robustly established (Løfblad et al., 2021).

While the impact of the immune system on brain biomarkers associated with treatment resistance has therefore been robustly established, very few studies tested possible indirect effects, i.e. a possible effect of immune/inflammatory alterations on antidepressant resistance through their effect on brain structure.

A recent multimodal clustering study performed on a sample of individuals with MDD, identified a subgroup of patients characterized by reduced grey matter volumes and cortical thinning, reduced FA, higher pro-inflammatory markers, and higher TRD prevalence (Colombo et al., 2024). Grey matter volumes of the right middle frontal gyrus were found to mediate the relation between serum IL-1β levels and antidepressant response (Li et al., 2025), and left hippocampal volumes mediated the effect of neutrophil to lymphocite ratio on depressive symptom reduction only in females MDD patients (Paolini et al., 2023). While not explicitly testing immune/inflammatory alterations, a mediating role of WMH on the relation between cardiovascular risk factors and treatment resistance was identified in a sample of depressed inpatients (Paolini et al., 2025).

Future studies might address this research gap investigating brain structural correlates of multiple immune markers (peripheral analytes concentration, immune cells count, subpopulations and gene expression patterns, CSF markers) and their relation with antidepressant efficacy. In this regard, the mechanistic interpretation of such data would be strongly enhanced by a longitudinal rather than cross sectional approach, that could clearly establish a temporal sequence between immune/inflammatory alterations, brain changes and treatment resistance.

Furthermore many confounding factors that could impact the effects we described were not regularly controlled for or explicitly tested. Peripheral inflammation was found to affect antidepressant efficacy through its effect on hippocampal volumes only in females (Paolini et al., 2023), and biological sex is known to have a strong effect on the immune system (Sharma et al., 2025). Age is usually used as a covariate in statistical analyses, but whether the relation between immune status, brain structure and antidepressant efficacy might differ between age groups remains an open research question. Overweight and adiposity might contribute to treatment resistance in part through their effect on inflammatory status, in a complex interplay between somatic illness and risk factors, brain health and antidepressant efficacy (Karageorgiou et al., 2023). Moreover, some data suggests that inflammation affects the efficacy of various antidepressant classes differently, possibly highlighting the need for a more refined definition of treatment resistance when investigating its relation to immune function (Arteaga-Henríquez et al., 2019).

Towards an integrated approach

While a significant number of studies reported inflammatory or structural neuroimaging alterations in TRD, very few explored the relation between the two, and whether brain correlates of treatment resistance might be at least partially reflective of immune/inflammatory alteration remains therefore an open research question. However, existing literature allows us to formulate some causal, albeit indirectly so hypotheses.

Grey matter volumetric reductions appear to characterize treatment resistant depression, with reduced hippocampal volume being possibly the most robust correlate (Abdallah et al., 2015; de Diego-Adeliño et al., 2013; Bravi et al., 2025). Two closely related and not mutually exclusive hypotheses have been put forward to account for its volumetric alterations: the neuroplasticity and the neurogenesis hypotheses (Boku et al., 2018). The first posits that hippocampal volumetric reductions in MDD might result from neuronal atrophy, with dendrites shortening and spines density reduction, possibly induced by chronic stress and excessive glucocorticoid signaling, among others (Conrad et al., 1999). The neurogenesis hypothesis on the other hand traces reduced hippocampal volumes to a decrease in newborn neurons and neuronal precursor cells (Boku et al., 2018). The hippocampus is indeed among the few regions where neurogenesis – the formation of new neurons – occurs also in adulthood (Toda et al., 2019), and evidence of a strong correlation between MRI measured hippocampal volumes and neuronal count in general, and newborn neurons specifically, have been provided (Bobinski et al., 1999; Biedermann et al., 2016).

Drugs with antidepressant efficacy exhibit numerous mechanisms of action, including enhancement of monoamine neurotransmission, modulation of glutamate and GABA receptors and action of second messenger systems (Sheffler et al., 2023). Furthermore, other types of interventions exhibit antidepressant activity, such as neuromodulation (Lipsman et al., 2014) and psychotherapy (Voderholzer et al., 2024). How such a plethora of possible treatments for depression, with different mechanisms of action, could lead to antidepressant efficacy remains an open research question (Harmer et al., 2017). In this regard, neurotrophic factors, and particularly enhancement of BDNF-TrkB signaling, are emerging as a putative common final path through which different antidepressants acts (Castrén and Monteggia, 2021; Björkholm and Monteggia, 2016), with some authors even hypothesizing a direct effect of some antidepressants on TrkB, BDNF high affinity receptor (Casarotto et al., 2022). BDNF is the most abundant neurotrophin in the adult brain (Bazzari and Bazzari, 2022), and plays crucial roles in neuronal survival and growth, but also in neurogenesis and neuroplasticity (Boku et al., 2018; Miranda et al., 2019; Bathina and Das, 2015).

A bidirectional relation exists between immune/inflammatory alterations and BDNF signaling: inflammation hampers BDNF expression both in preclinical models, where treatment with lipopolysaccharide or IL-1β reduces BDNF mRNA in several brain regions, and in humans, where treatment with INFα is associated to reduced BDNF levels (Porter and O'Connor, 2022). At the same time BDNF has been shown to have anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities on microglial cells (Lai et al., 2018; Charlton et al., 2023). It is therefore possible to speculate that, in patients where the balance between inflammatory and neurotrophic signals is shifted towards the former, antidepressants are not able to restore adequate BDNF function, thus leading to hampered neuroplasticity and neurogenesis (reflected in reduced hippocampal volumes) and ultimately to treatment resistance.

A surprising emerging role of BDNF as a modulator of kynurenine balance is emerging, which further strengthens its ties with the immune system: indeed, in preclinical models BDNF deficiency is associated to reduced production of neuroprotective kynurenic acid and enhanced formation of neurotoxic kynurenine in response to both stress and inflammatory challenges (Dugan et al., 2016; Parrott et al., 2021). The kynurenine pathway may indeed represent a crucial mechanistic path linking immune function and brain pathology: inflammation upregulates indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), shifting tryptophan metabolism from serotonin formation to kynurenine production; this compound can be further converted by astrocites in neuroprotective kynurenic acid or, by activated microglia, in quinolinic acid, which is both pro-oxidant and excitotoxic (Mithaiwala et al., 2021; Kim and Jeon, 2018). Indeed, the ratio between serum kynurenic and quinolinic acid has been found to positively associate to hippocampal volumes and to FA, directly reflective of white matter integrity (Savitz et al., 2015a, 2015b; Paul et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2022).

Concerning white matter, many other mechanistic pathways could lead from altered immune/inflammatory state to impaired myelination, including a direct detrimental effect of cytokines on oligodendrocytes formation and survival (Lim et al., 2021; He et al., 2022), and microglial activation with oxidative stress, excitotoxic injury and impaired white matter repair (Xia et al., 2025).

Finally, a particularly intriguing marker related to both immune status and brain structure is the choroid plexus, whose volume was recently reported to be associated to both TRD and hampered antidepressant response (Bravi et al., 2025; Murck et al., 2024). The choroid plexus is located in lateral ventricles, and has classically been regarded to be responsible of cerebrospinal fluid production (Lun et al., 2015); however, in recent years its involvement in brain pathological states characterized by neuroinflammation is progressively being recognized, being a crucial interface between the brain and the immune system responsible for immune cell trafficking across the blood brain barrier (Castellani et al., 2023; Fleischer et al., 2021). Choroid plexus volumes have been found to be increased both in neurological (Choi et al., 2022; Müller et al., 2022) and psychiatric conditions (Zeng et al., 2024; Althubaity et al., 2022; Bravi et al., 2024), possibly serving as a general marker of brain immune/inflammatory alterations (Fleischer et al., 2021). Its association with treatment resistance and reduced antidepressant response therefore might highlight the involvement of immune alterations in TRD, providing a unique bridge between immunopsychiatry and neuroimaging data. It must be noted however that most of the research on this structure has been performed in recent years, likely fueled by the advent of automatic segmentation algorithms which by themselves are not immune from methodological limitations, and has therefore to be considered preliminary in its nature (Yazdan-Panah et al., 2023).

Several limitations of the current literature should be acknowledged. Many studies include relatively small sample sizes, particularly those examining central immune mechanisms, which may limit generalizability. In addition, there is considerable heterogeneity in the definition of TRD across studies, as well as variability in study design, treatment status, and biomarker assessment methods. Differences in assay techniques, sample handling, and analytical approaches may further contribute to inconsistent findings. Similarly, neuroimaging studies are characterized by variability in imaging protocols, analytical methods, and sample characteristics, which may limit reproducibility and comparability across studies. Together, these factors limit comparability across studies and highlight the need for standardized definitions and methodologies in future research.

Despite growing interest in biological mechanisms of TRD, several important gaps remain. In particular, there is a lack of large, well-characterized longitudinal studies integrating peripheral and central biomarkers, as well as limited evidence directly linking immune alterations to structural brain changes and treatment outcomes. Future research should prioritize standardized definitions of TRD, multimodal study designs, and the identification of reproducible biomarkers to support clinically meaningful stratification and targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Accumulating evidence indicates that TRD is a biologically heterogeneous condition characterized by persistent immune–inflammatory dysregulation, impaired immune resolution, and alterations in neurotrophic and stress-related systems. Rather than reflecting a uniform increase in inflammatory activity, TRD appears to involve a combination of stable, trait-like immune abnormalities and state-dependent changes that differentiate responders from non-responders.

Converging findings indicate that central immune mechanisms may be more relevant to treatment resistance than peripheral inflammation alone. Findings from CSF studies, microglia–T cell signaling pathways, and choroid plexus alterations suggest that CNS-specific immune activation and its failure to normalize with treatment, is associated with the persistence of depressive symptoms.

Structural neuroimaging findings are consistent with this framework, indicating that brain regions involved in neuroplasticity and large-scale network integration, particularly the hippocampus and major white matter tracts, may reflect the cumulative impact of chronic immune and neuroendocrine dysregulation. Emerging mediational studies further suggest that immune-related brain alterations could partially account for the association between inflammation and antidepressant non-response.

Taken together, these observations suggest that treatment-resistant depression may encompass potentially distinct biological patterns of resistance, including inflammation-sensitive forms. While current evidence does not yet establish formal biological stratification, integrative approaches combining peripheral and central immune markers with longitudinal neuroimaging and interventional designs will be essential to determine whether such heterogeneity can be translated into biologically informed stratification and clinically meaningful treatment strategies. From a translational perspective, these findings may support the development of biomarker-guided treatment approaches. For example, patients with elevated inflammatory markers could be considered for adjunctive anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory strategies, whereas individuals with preserved neurotrophic signaling may be more likely to benefit from conventional antidepressant or neuromodulation-based treatments. Although such approaches remain preliminary, they illustrate potential pathways toward clinically meaningful stratification in TRD.
