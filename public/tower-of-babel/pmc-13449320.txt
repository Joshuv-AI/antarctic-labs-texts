Prognostic value of the hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) in predicting mortality and rehospitalization in patients with chronic heart failure
Authors: Samira Arami, Bahare Gholami Chaboki, Negin Letafatkar, Arsalan Salari, Seyed Mehdi Mousavi, Zahra Ahmadnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHeart failure (HF) represents a significant and growing global health burden. The hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR), a novel composite marker integrating oxygen-carrying capacity (hemoglobin) and red cell distribution width (RDW), has shown prognostic value in various diseases, but its role in chronic HF remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of HRR for mortality and 12-month rehospitalization in patients with chronic HF.ResultsA total of 419 patients with chronic heart failure were included in the analysis. A lower HRR was independently associated with increased all-cause mortality. Each unit increase in HRR was associated with a 17% reduction in mortality odds (Adjusted OR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.73–0.95; p = 0.006). This inverse relationship was significant in anemic patients but not in non-anemic patients. In contrast, HRR was not associated with rehospitalization in the overall or anemic populations, but a higher HRR was significantly associated with reduced rehospitalization odds in non-anemic patients (Adjusted OR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.66–0.99; p < 0.05).Keywords: Heart failure, Red blood cell distribution width, Prognosis

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a major and growing global public health problem, affecting approximately 55.5 million individuals worldwide and contributing substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures [1]. Despite advances in pharmacological and device-based therapies that have improved survival, the overall prevalence of HF continues to increase due to population aging, improved post-myocardial infarction survival, and prolonged disease duration [1–3]. Consequently, identifying reliable and cost-effective biomarkers for risk stratification remains a clinical priority, particularly for predicting adverse outcomes such as mortality and rehospitalization.

In recent years, hematologic parameters derived from routine blood tests have attracted considerable attention as prognostic indicators in cardiovascular diseases, including HF. Hemoglobin (Hb), an essential determinant of oxygen transport capacity, has been associated with disease severity and poor outcomes in patients with HF, particularly in the presence of anemia [4, 5]. Likewise, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), a measure of erythrocyte size variability, has emerged as an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Elevated RDW has been linked to inflammation, oxidative stress, nutritional deficiencies, impaired erythropoiesis, and neurohormonal dysregulation, all of which are involved in the pathophysiology and progression of chronic HF [6–8].

More recently, the hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) has emerged as a novel composite hematologic biomarker integrating information from both Hb and RDW. Compared with individual parameters alone, HRR may offer superior prognostic utility by simultaneously reflecting oxygen-carrying capacity and erythrocyte heterogeneity, thereby capturing multiple interrelated pathophysiological processes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and ineffective erythropoiesis [9, 10]. A lower HRR may indicate the coexistence of anemia and elevated RDW-related inflammatory burden, potentially identifying patients at higher risk for adverse clinical outcomes.

Growing evidence supports the clinical relevance of HRR-based indices in cardiovascular and inflammatory conditions. Previous studies have demonstrated the prognostic significance of HRR in coronary artery disease and other systemic disorders [11–13]. In addition, recent investigations have highlighted the value of HRR and related erythrocyte indices in inflammatory cardiovascular diseases. Previous studies reported that HRR and RDW-to-albumin ratio (RAR) were associated with disease severity and inflammatory status in patients with pericarditis [14]. Similarly, HRR was shown to be associated with clinical and laboratory parameters in myocarditis, further supporting its role as an accessible and inexpensive marker reflecting inflammatory cardiovascular burden [15]. These findings strengthen the biological plausibility and clinical rationale for investigating HRR in chronic HF.

However, despite increasing interest in HRR as a prognostic biomarker, evidence regarding its predictive role in patients with chronic HF remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of HRR in predicting mortality and 12-month rehospitalization among patients with chronic HF.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort conducted between 2023 and 2024. The primary objective was to evaluate the relationship between the hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) and adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality and rehospitalization within 12 months after discharge, among patients with chronic heart failure.

Study population

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of chronic heart failure based on clinical and echocardiographic assessments, who had been undergoing treatment for at least three months since diagnosis and were admitted to or followed in the hospital’s heart failure clinic during the study period. Patients were selected using a convenience sampling approach.

Exclusion criteria included: [1] acute MI; [2] cardiogenic shock; [3] systolic blood pressure less than 90 mmHg; [4] Inotrope requirement at the beginning of hospitalization; [5] hemoglobin level below 9 g/dL or hematocrit below 30%; [6] hereditary hemoglobinopathies such as thalassemia or sickle cell disease; [7] severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²); [8] active inflammatory or autoimmune diseases indicated by elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR > 20 mm/hr) or C-reactive protein (CRP > 0.3 mg/dL); [9] malignancy or myelodysplastic syndrome; and [10] recent major bleeding or blood transfusion within the past three months.

Patients with hemoglobin < 9 g/dL were excluded to minimize the disproportionate influence of severe anemia and hematologic instability on HRR values, which could potentially confound the assessment of its prognostic relationship with heart failure outcomes.

Anemia was defined according to hemoglobin concentration, using sex-specific thresholds of hemoglobin < 13 g/dL in men and < 12 g/dL in women.

Data collection

At baseline, demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded, including age, sex, comorbidities, medication history, and echocardiographic findings such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Echocardiographic examinations were performed according to standard clinical practice, and LVEF was assessed using the biplane Simpson method. Laboratory data were obtained from venous blood samples collected on admission. Hb and red cell distribution width (RDW) were measured using standard automated hematology analyzers, and the Hemoglobin-to-RDW ratio (HRR) was calculated by dividing Hb (g/dL) by RDW (%).

Follow-up and outcomes

Each participant was monitored over a 12-month period following the index admission. Follow-up information was obtained through scheduled outpatient visits and telephone interviews conducted by trained research personnel. The primary outcomes were mortality and rehospitalization due to cardiac causes within 12 months of discharge. Mortality was defined as all-cause mortality during the 12-month follow-up period. Cardiac rehospitalization was defined as hospital admission due to cardiovascular causes, including worsening heart failure, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, or other cardiovascular indications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent-samples t-test or one-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of HRR for predicting mortality and rehospitalization. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding p-values were calculated.

For subgroup analyses, participants were categorized into tertiles according to baseline HRR values: tertile 1 (< 7.22), tertile 2 (7.23–9.07), and tertile 3 (> 9.07). Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes were compared across HRR tertiles.

Associations between HRR and study outcomes were examined using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for clinically relevant confounders. Variables entered into the adjusted models included age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), prior myocardial infarction (MI), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Selection of covariates was based on clinical relevance and previous literature regarding predictors of adverse outcomes in heart failure. Although additional prognostic variables such as New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, natriuretic peptide levels (BNP/NT-proBNP), renal function indices, and guideline-directed medical therapy are recognized predictors of heart failure outcomes, these data were not systematically available for all participants and therefore were not included in multivariable analyses. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences, Rasht, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.GUMS.REC.1402.301). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment, and patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 506 individuals with chronic heart failure were initially screened for eligibility. After applying the exclusion criteria, 419 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age of participants was 63.71 ± 11.67 years, and 60.1% were male. Patients in the lowest HRR tertile had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM), prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and prior anemia compared with higher tertiles. In addition, laboratory indices related to hemoglobin status, including Hb, hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), differed significantly across tertiles, with lower values observed in the lowest HRR group. Mortality was significantly more frequent in the lowest HRR tertile (26.4%) than in the highest tertile (10.8%). No statistically significant differences were observed across HRR tertiles regarding age, sex, marital status, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), acute myocardial infarction, or rehospitalization due to heart failure (Table 1).

When HRR was analyzed according to tertiles, patients in the highest tertile had significantly lower odds of mortality compared with those in the lowest tertile (OR: 0.33; 95% CI: 0.17–0.64; p = 0.001). This inverse association between HRR and mortality remained significant among patients with anemia (OR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.67–0.97; p = 0.027), but not among non-anemic patients. In contrast, HRR was not significantly associated with rehospitalization in the overall cohort or in anemic patients. However, among non-anemic patients, higher HRR was associated with reduced odds of rehospitalization in the adjusted models (OR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.66–0.99; p < 0.05).

ROC curve analysis

The predictive performance of HRR for all-cause mortality and rehospitalization was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, as summarized in Table 3; Fig. 2. For all-cause mortality, HRR demonstrated modest but statistically significant discriminative ability in the overall cohort (AUC = 0.643, p = 0.003) and among anemic patients (AUC = 0.639, p = 0.027). In non-anemic patients, the AUC did not reach statistical significance (AUC = 0.632, p = 0.173). For rehospitalization, HRR showed statistically significant discriminative ability only in the non-anemic subgroup (AUC = 0.630, p = 0.045), while the AUC values for the overall cohort and anemic subgroup were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Our findings support the prognostic relevance of HRR in patients with chronic heart failure, particularly regarding mortality risk. A lower HRR may reflect the coexistence of anemia, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired erythropoiesis, all of which contribute to disease progression in HF [16, 17]. Hemoglobin reduction may impair oxygen delivery to peripheral tissues, while elevated RDW has been associated with chronic inflammation, nutritional deficiency, neurohormonal activation, and ineffective erythropoiesis, mechanisms closely linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [6, 8, 18]. Therefore, HRR, as a composite index integrating both hemoglobin concentration and erythrocyte heterogeneity, may better reflect the complex pathophysiological burden of HF than either parameter alone.

Recently, several inflammation-based prognostic biomarkers have shown promising utility in patients with heart failure. The Scottish Inflammatory Prognostic Score (SIPS), which integrates inflammatory parameters, has been associated with adverse outcomes in patients with acute heart failure, supporting the role of systemic inflammation in disease progression [19]. Likewise, the Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX), reflecting endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory stress, has demonstrated prognostic significance in patients with reduced ejection fraction [20]. Furthermore, the Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), a marker based on systemic inflammatory and nutritional status, has also been associated with poor outcomes in heart failure populations [21]. Similar to these biomarkers, HRR may reflect inflammatory and physiologic dysregulation; however, HRR differs conceptually by integrating both oxygen-carrying capacity and erythrocyte heterogeneity through routinely available hematologic parameters [10]. Therefore, HRR may provide complementary prognostic information while remaining inexpensive, widely accessible, and easily applicable in routine clinical practice.

A possible explanation for the association between lower HRR and mortality, but not rehospitalization, may lie in the multifactorial nature of rehospitalization in chronic heart failure. Mortality is more directly associated with systemic inflammation, anemia-related impaired oxygen delivery, oxidative stress, and ineffective erythropoiesis, all of which may be reflected by a lower HRR. In contrast, rehospitalization may be influenced by several non-biological factors, including medication adherence, outpatient follow-up, healthcare accessibility, physician decision-making, socioeconomic status, and comorbidity burden. Therefore, although HRR may effectively reflect disease severity and mortality risk, it may be less sensitive in predicting rehospitalization outcomes that depend on broader healthcare-related determinants. The significant association observed only among non-anemic patients may suggest that, in the absence of overt anemia, HRR more sensitively reflects RDW-related inflammatory, oxidative, or erythropoietic disturbances, whereas in anemic patients, the dominant effect of reduced oxygen-carrying capacity may limit its additional discriminatory value for rehospitalization.

This study has several strengths. First, it evaluated HRR, a simple, inexpensive, and routinely available hematologic index, in a relatively large number of patients with chronic heart failure. Second, the analysis included both mortality and rehospitalization outcomes during a 12-month follow-up period. Third, multivariable models were used to adjust for several clinically relevant confounders. These features support the potential clinical relevance of HRR as a complementary biomarker for risk stratification, while recognizing that further validation in larger multicenter studies is needed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design and convenience sampling strategy may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Second, HRR was assessed only at hospital admission and was not repeatedly measured during hospitalization or follow-up. Because hematologic parameters may fluctuate over time, a single baseline measurement obtained during a period of clinical instability may not fully reflect longitudinal disease progression or the dynamic prognostic value of HRR. Future studies incorporating serial HRR measurements are warranted.

Third, patients with hemoglobin levels < 9 g/dL were excluded to reduce the potential confounding effects of severe anemia on HRR values. However, this approach may limit the applicability of our findings to patients with advanced anemia or more severe heart failure. In addition, due to the lack of consistently available time-to-event data, survival analyses such as Cox proportional hazards modeling could not be performed, and future investigations should evaluate HRR using time-dependent approaches.

Finally, several established prognostic variables in heart failure, including NYHA functional class, natriuretic peptides (BNP/NT-proBNP), renal function indices, inflammatory markers, nutritional parameters, and detailed treatment-related information, were not systematically available for all participants and therefore could not be fully incorporated into multivariable analyses. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, HRR appears to be a simple, inexpensive, and readily available biomarker with potential prognostic relevance in patients with chronic heart failure. While lower HRR was associated with increased mortality risk, its predictive performance was modest and should be interpreted as complementary to established prognostic indicators rather than a stand-alone marker. The differential association according to anemia status highlights the complex pathophysiology of heart failure and warrants further investigation in larger prospective studies.
