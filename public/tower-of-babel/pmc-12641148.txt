Development and Validation of a Nomogram for Predicting Intravenous Amiodarone‐Induced Phlebitis: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jiexia Li, Rui Huang, Chunrong Li, Fujia Qin, Zhishuang Tan, Xiaoyan Lin, Haimin Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntravenous amiodarone is primarily used to treat hemodynamically unstable atrial or ventricular arrhythmias and prevent atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery. However, when administered via peripheral intravenous infusion, the incidence of phlebitis is relatively high. This increase in complication rate not only increases patient discomfort and prolongs hospitalization but may also adversely affect treatment efficacy and quality of life. Therefore, early identification of high‐risk patients and timely intervention are crucial for reducing the occurrence of phlebitis, enhancing treatment safety, and improving patient prognosis.AimThis study aimed to develop and validate a nomogram to predict the risk of phlebitis associated with peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone.DesignThis study is a prospective cohort study.MethodsThe research was carried out at Guigang People's Hospital in China. A total of 393 cases who underwent intravenous infusion of amiodarone through the peripheral vein between January 2022 and December 2023 constituted the study cohort. The cohort was randomly divided using computer‐generated randomization numbers, with stratification maintained for key clinical characteristics (including age, infusion duration, and concentration). A 70:30 ratio allocation was applied to create a training set (n = 275) for model development and a validation set (n = 118) for performance evaluation. Logistic regression was used to construct the model. A scatterplot was used to test the predictive performance of the model.ResultsAge, infusion time, infusion concentration, and whether to perform 2 h of alternating indwelling needle were factors influencing the occurrence of phlebitis associated with peripheral intravenous use of amiodarone. A nomogram model of the risk of developing phlebitis with peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone was successfully constructed. Bootstrap results showed that the calibration curves fitted well with the ideal curves. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the training and validation sets were 0.812 (95% CI: 0.757–0.867) and 0.798 (95% CI: 0.712–0.883), respectively. The results of Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test revealed χ 2 = 3.414 and p = 0.906 for the training set and χ 2 = 11.835 and p = 0.159 for the validation set.ConclusionsThe model showed a good predictive effect, and it can be effectively used as a tool for clinically assessing the risk of phlebitis following peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone.Keywords: amiodarone, nursing care, peripheral vein, phlebitis, predictive modeling

Introduction

IV amiodarone has multiple indications, and it is used in various settings, with its primary use to treat patients who are hemodynamically unstable with atrial or ventricular arrhythmias. It can be used to prevent post‐thoracic surgery atrial fibrillation [1, 2, 3]. Amiodarone injection should preferably be administered via a central venous line whenever possible in a clinical setting to minimize the risk of phlebitis associated with peripheral intravenous administration. Arrhythmias are mostly sudden‐onset disorders, and temporary emergency drug administration is relatively common; therefore, continuous infusion of the drug via the peripheral vein is often required. However, due to the acidic nature of amiodarone hydrochloride (pH 2.5–4.0) [4], intravenous injections can cause changes in vascular permeability, intravascular osmotic pressure at the infusion site, and local metabolism of the vessel wall, subsequently leading to phlebitis [5]. Studies have shown that amiodarone hydrochloride injections administered via peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) have resulted in a high incidence of phlebitis ranging from 8.0% to 54% [6], far exceeding the acceptable incidence (5%) of phlebitis, due to drug infusion via the peripheral vein as recommended by the guidelines of the Infusion Nurses Society (INS) [7]. Phlebitis often leads to problems such as forced interruption of therapy, recanalization, increased infection rates, development of negative patient morale, increased healthcare costs, and compromised outcomes [8]. Awareness regarding amiodarone‐induced phlebitis has gradually increased because the best available evidence for peripheral intravenous amiodarone administration is incorporated into clinical practice and nurses take on a more active role in guiding nursing practice through evidence‐based care. However, despite this heightened awareness and attention, the incidence of phlebitis has not shown a significant decline [9]. Therefore, early identification of infusion‐induced phlebitis with subsequent timely and effective interventions is essential to reduce its incidence. A nomogram is a graphical prediction tool that quantifies individual risk by integrating multiple predictors. It is widely used in vascular access research owing to its clinical usability. This study aimed to investigate the incidence of peripheral intravenous infusion of amiodarone‐associated phlebitis and construct a risk‐prediction model to provide a theoretical basis for the early identification of the risk of phlebitis and provide early intervention in a clinical setting.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

The baseline data of patients were collected first and then followed up to observe the outcomes, thus employing a prospective cohort. A total of 393 patients receiving peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone in Guigang People's Hospital in China between January 2022 and December 2023 were selected as the study subjects. For all included patients (n = 393), none were lost to follow‐up, and the full course of observation was completed. The patients were randomly allocated to a training set (n = 275) or a validation set (n = 118) based on computer‐generated random numbers, with a target allocation ratio of 7:3. Considering that all the cases were followed up in‐hospital, with an average follow‐up duration ranging from 12 to 48 h, which was relatively short, no cases of loss to follow‐up were reported. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) coagulation disorders, (2) peripheral vascular disease, (3) diabetes mellitus, (4) autoimmune vasculitis or chronic kidney disease, and (5) malignant tumors. Endothelial dysfunction and hemodynamic changes could interfere with the attribution analysis of amiodarone‐related venous injury. (6) Patients receiving amiodarone administration through central venous catheters or alternative nonperipheral intravenous access methods. (7) Patients with arm restrictions and (8) those under continuous sedation or had impaired consciousness that prevented effective communication were excluded to ensure the accuracy of clinical data collection and control the interference of catheter‐related variables on the results (Figure 1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guigang People's Hospital (Approval No.: GYLLPJ‐20220811‐74), and all subjects provided written informed consent.

Assessment of Phlebitis

Phlebitis was assessed on the basis of the relevant standards set by INS [7] as follows: Grade 0: asymptomatic; Grade I: local pain, erythema or edema, vein without striated changes, and no palpable nodes; Grade II: local pain, erythema or edema, vein with striated changes, and no palpable nodes; Grade III: local pain, erythema or edema, vein with striated changes, and palpable nodes; and Grade IV: local pain, erythema or edema, venous cord‐like changes, palpable sclerosis with length > 2.5 cm, may be accompanied by pus. Grades I and above were considered as phlebitis. This scale has validity evidence for use in China, and many studies in China [10, 11, 12] have applied this phlebitis assessment standard.

Predictors

The predictor variables for the nomogram were systematically identified through a three‐step process. (1) A comprehensive literature review of PubMed, CINAHL, and CNKI (2010–2023) was conducted using the keywords *“phlebitis,” “amiodarone,”* and *“peripheral intravenous catheter,”* on the basis of previous studies [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. A total of 13 predictive variables were selected: gender, age, number of successful punctures, infusion concentration, infusion rate, infusion duration, indwelling needle size, presence of hypertension, presence of vascular sclerosis, presence of limb edema, presence of hemiplegia, whether indwelling needle was used alternately every 2 h, and whether administering other nonirritating drugs was continued after the infusion was completed. (2) Single‐ and multifactor analyses were conducted on the collected predictive variables to screen out those with statistical significance (age, infusion time, infusion concentration, and whether the indwelling needle was alternately used every 2 h). (3) A nomogram model was constructed using predictive variables with statistical significance.

Amiodarone Infusion Process

A newly inserted PIVC was required prior to initiating amiodarone infusion. (It should be noted here that the administration method of amiodarone is via a micro‐pump for continuous infusion, with a dosage of 300 mg mixed with 50 mL. The infusion amount is adjusted according to the patient's heart rhythm condition during the medication period). Administration through an existing IV access site was prohibited by the protocol to eliminate confounding irritation from previous infusions. PIV catheters were inserted exclusively by certified registered nurses who have completed competency‐based training in intravenous therapy. A standardized kit that included an intravenous catheter, with a gauge ranging from 18 to 22, with a polyurethane tip was used. The site was secured with a sterile, transparent, and semi‐permeable dressing to allow for continuous visual monitoring. Catheter insertion followed a strict aseptic protocol. If two insertion attempts by a trained nurse were unsuccessful, the protocol mandated escalation to a specialized vascular access team. Two PIV catheters were retained and alternated during amiodarone infusion. The same catheter must not be used for other fluids when amiodarone was not being infused (a notable detail that this is a rational recommended protocol for all patients included in the study who received intravenous amiodarone administration, not a mandatory protocol). If the anticipated infusion duration exceeded 24 h or the maximum recommended concentration for peripheral administration was reached, the clinical team was advised to place a central venous catheter for infusion.

Data Collection

The general data of the patients were collected by two trained nurses in accordance with the content of the forms, and a follow‐up observation was carried out immediately after the patients were injected with peripheral intravenous amiodarone. If Grade I or higher phlebitis was detected, the infusion was immediately discontinued, and the catheter was removed. According to the institutional protocol, Grade I: cold compress + catheter removal + documentation; Grades II–IV: additional 50% magnesium sulfate wet compress + physician notification. The event was documented. Patients without phlebitis (Grade 0) should undergo continuous monitoring until 48 h after the infusion is completed. The grading of phlebitis was considered 0 during the period of peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone. The final grade was regarded as Grade 0. If the grading was I or above at any time during the peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone, the current grading was recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was calculated using the event per variable method [19], and 393 patients were finally included in this study. The patients were simply and randomly allocated to a training set (n = 275) or a validation set (n = 118) in a 7:3 ratio. The training set was used for single‐ and multifactor analyses to analyze influencing factors, construct the model, and perform internal validation. The validation set was solely used to evaluate the performance of the model. SPSS (version 26.0) software was used to analyze data. Count data are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using χ 2 test or Fisher's exact probability method. Mean and standard deviation were used for continuous variables that conformed to normal distribution, and comparisons between groups were performed using t‐test for independent samples. Medians and quartiles were used for non‐normally distributed measurements, and comparisons between groups were conducted using Mann–Whitney U test. Binary logistic regression was used to analyze the risk factors for the development of phlebitis due to amiodarone injection via peripheral veins. R (version 3.6.1) software was used to construct a column chart model for predicting the risk, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was determined to assess the differences of the column chart model. Calibration curves were plotted using bootstrap method with 1000 repetitive samples, and the accuracy of the column chart model was assessed via internal validation of the column chart by using Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. A two‐sided test was used, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

A total of 393 patients were included in this study, of which 208 (52.9%) were males and 185 (47.1%) were females. Phlebitis was noted in 100 patients (incidence: 25.4%). Phlebitis occurred in 71 of 275 patients in the training set (incidence: 25.8%) and in 29 of 118 patients in the validation set (incidence: 24.5%). No significant differences (p > 0.05) were found among the general patient data (Table 1).

Development of Nomogram Model

A nomogram model was successfully constructed to predict the risk of phlebitis associated with peripheral intravenous amiodarone administration, using age, infusion time, infusion concentration, and whether 2 h of alternating use of indwelling needle was performed as predictive factors. Among them, the most significant factor contributing to the prediction of phlebitis was infusion concentration (Figure 2).

Nomogram for predicting phlebitis risk after peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone. The nomogram consists of graphical lines that include risk factors (age, infusion time, infusion concentration, and whether 2 h of alternating use of indwelling needles was performed), score, total score, and probability of developing phlebitis. The line segment corresponding to each risk factor is marked with a scale, indicating the possible range of values for that factor, and the length of the line segment reflects the contribution of that factor to predicting the occurrence of phlebitis. The “score” at the top of the graph represents the score corresponding to the risk factor at different values. The individual scores of all risk factors are added up to obtain the total score. The bottom scale corresponding to the total score is the predictive probability of the risk of phlebitis after peripheral intravenous injection of amiodarone.

Validation of Nomogram Model

As shown in Figure 3, the areas under the ROC curve for the training and validation sets were 0.812 (95% CI: 0.757–0.867) and 0.798 (95% CI: 0.712–0.883), respectively. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test was χ 2 = 3.414 and p = 0.906 for the training set and χ 2 = 11.835 and p = 0.159 for the validation set. The calibration curve and Hosmer–Lemeshow test showed that the calibration curve is close to the 45° oblique line, indicating that the model has a good degree of calibration (Figure 4). The results of the clinical decision curve of nomogram prediction of the risk of phlebitis after peripheral intravenous amiodarone injection showed that the “Model line” was far away from the “None line” and the “All line,” indicating that the nomogram has good clinical application value (Figure 5).

Discussion

Amiodarone hydrochloride is a commonly used rescue drug for patients with arrhythmias, commonly administered as peripheral intravenous infusion. In this study, the incidence of phlebitis in patients receiving amiodarone via peripheral intravenous infusion was 25.8%, which falls within the range of 20%–70% reported in previous studies [20] but exceeds the acceptable incidence rate of 5% recommended by INS [7]. This finding can be attributed to the strong irritation of amiodarone injection to blood vessels. Therefore, early identification of the risk of phlebitis associated with amiodarone infusion via peripheral veins and taking preventive measures are particularly important to enhance medication safety and ensure patient treatment efficacy. Machine learning algorithms have demonstrated superior predictive performance in critical care scenarios through their ability to process high‐dimensional clinical data. Recent applications include pressure injury prediction and patients with ischemic stroke in ICU [21, 22], showcasing their capacity to detect nonlinear interactions between risk factors, a critical advantage for modeling complex pharmacological reactions like amiodarone‐induced vascular injury.

This study pioneered the development of a predictive model for the risk of phlebitis after amiodarone administration via peripheral intravenous injection, demonstrating good predictive performance. The model exhibited strong predictive ability in the training data set (AUC: 0.812) and the validation data set (AUC: 0.798). This finding proves that the model is stable, with good predictive power and clinical application value. Predictive models for the occurrence of PIVC‐related phlebitis have been reported in patients with orthopedic and critical illnesses [23, 24, 25]. However, to the authors' knowledge, previous studies have developed prediction models for phlebitis associated with common medications, such as intravenous saline infusions, only. No reports on prediction models for phlebitis in patients receiving peripheral intravenous amiodarone are available. Therefore, this model can be used to predict the high‐risk population for phlebitis caused by amiodarone infusion via peripheral veins and provide a reference for the use of preventive measures.

In addition to the different study populations, the research model used a unique combination of variables compared with the Bayesian regression model used in Lee et al.'s study [25]. This approach improves the predictive accuracy of the model for the risk of phlebitis in patients undergoing peripheral intravenous infusion of amiodarone.

This study found that age is a risk factor for the occurrence of phlebitis in patients undergoing peripheral intravenous infusion of amiodarone, with a higher risk for older patients, consistent with the findings of Decker et al. [26]. This finding can be attributed to age‐related thinning of the skin and loss of protective fat layers, making the skin of elderly patients more susceptible to tearing and damage. Some of the previous studies have reported that [27] phlebitis is associated with gender; however, in the present study, no association between gender and phlebitis was observed, consistent with the findings of earlier studies by Ben Abdelaziz et al. [28] and Webster et al. [29]. The present study also revealed that the infusion concentration and duration of amiodarone are risk factors for the occurrence of phlebitis, with a higher risk associated with higher concentrations, may be because higher concentrations of amiodarone have a stronger irritation effect on the vascular intima. Under the stimulation of cytokines, the nuclear factors in the body are phosphorylated and degraded, leading to the entry of phosphorylated nuclear factor‐kappa B dimers (p50 and p65) into the nucleus, resulting in the expression of inflammatory factors, adhesion molecules, apoptosis‐suppressing and ‐promoting genes, and angiogenesis‐suppressing factors. Endothelial cell apoptosis leads to endothelial dysfunction, resulting in inflammatory imbalance and thus inflammation [30, 31]. Furthermore, as the duration of drug infusion increases, vascular endothelial cell apoptosis and vascular damage worsen, further stimulating the inflammatory response and increasing the incidence of phlebitis [32]. Notably, the model in the present study introduced novel insights by incorporating the protective effect of alternating indwelling needle every 2 h, a factor not extensively explored in earlier predictive models. While Lee et al. [33] developed a phlebitis prediction model for orthopedic patients, their focus on catheter dwell time and site differs from the emphasis of the present study on pharmacodynamic variables (e.g., concentration) and procedural interventions (e.g., needle alternation). This distinction enhanced the specificity of the model for amiodarone‐related phlebitis, addressing a gap in existing literature.

The developed nomogram offers transformative potential for clinical nursing. By integrating key variables (age, infusion time, concentration, and needle alternation), nurses can rapidly stratify patients into risk categories, thereby enabling early interventions. High‐risk patients (e.g., elderly individuals requiring prolonged, high‐concentration infusions) can be prioritized for central venous access or shortened peripheral infusion cycles. Implementing 2 h of indwelling needle alternation, which was validated in this study as a protective factor, can reduce cumulative vascular irritation.

This study has limitations. First, as a single‐center study recruiting participants exclusively from one hospital, the generalizability of the findings may be limited to other clinical settings. Second, the prediction model lacked external validation, necessitating further confirmation in broader populations. Third, the catheter insertion site, which is a potential critical risk factor, was excluded from the analysis. This decision was driven by the institutional protocol requiring nurses to prioritize forearm sites for PIVC placement in amiodarone‐treated patients. Consequently, nearly all participants in this cohort underwent forearm punctures, rendering comparative analysis of insertion sites unfeasible. Finally, catheter indwelling duration was not evaluated. This omission stems from the study design: amiodarone was administered exclusively through dedicated PIVCs without concurrent infusion of other medications. Thus, catheter indwelling time directly corresponded to amiodarone infusion duration (from initiation to discontinuation), and the catheter was removed immediately after infusion completion. Consequently, only amiodarone infusion duration was analyzed.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated an effective predictive model for assessing the risk of phlebitis following peripheral intravenous administration of amiodarone. By integrating risk prediction with actionable clinical strategies, the model holds promise for enhancing clinical decision‐making. Despite its potential, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations such as its single‐center design, lack of external validation, absence of catheter insertion site details, and insufficient information on medical devices (e.g., catheter length, closed systems, and needle‐free connectors). Future research should focus on external validation in larger and more diverse populations, refining data on influencing factors, and further optimizing the model's predictive performance.
