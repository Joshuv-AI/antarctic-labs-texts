Acute neuromuscular and short-term recovery responses to low-load exercise with individualized blood flow restriction in elite male volleyball players
Authors: Xin Zhang, Xin Zhang, Shijun Liu, Bin Lu, Xiaoqing Sun, Zhejia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood flow restriction (BFR) training is widely used to augment the effects of low-load resistance exercise, yet the influence of individualized BFR intensity on acute neuromuscular responses and early recovery remains incompletely understood, particularly in elite athletes. This study examined acute neuromuscular, morphological, and tolerability responses to low-load leg press exercise performed under individualized BFR conditions in elite male volleyball players. Thirty-three athletes completed four repeated exercise sessions at 0%, 40%, 60%, and 80% of arterial occlusion pressure. Vastus lateralis surface electromyography and ultrasound-derived muscle thickness were assessed before exercise and during the first 30 min of post-exercise recovery under standardized conditions. Session RPE, cuff-related discomfort/pain, and paresthesia were also recorded. Linear mixed-effects models were used for neuromuscular and morphological outcomes, and appropriate repeated-measures non-parametric tests were used for tolerability outcomes. Low-load exercise with individualized BFR elicited acute changes in neuromuscular and morphological indices. Higher occlusion pressures were associated with small but statistically detectable overall differences in response magnitude when responses were averaged across time. In contrast, recovery profiles over the first 30 min after exercise were comparable across restriction levels, consistent with the absence of condition-dependent time-course effects. All athletes completed all four conditions; however, tolerability worsened as occlusion pressure increased, with higher session RPE and more frequent cuff-related discomfort/pain and paresthesia at higher pressures. In elite male volleyball players, variation in individualized BFR intensity appears to influence the overall magnitude of acute responses more than the short-term recovery trajectory itself. The present data do not provide evidence that higher occlusion pressures confer superior short-term recovery characteristics in this elite cohort. Introduction

Blood flow restriction (BFR) exercise has emerged as a practical strategy for augmenting the physiological stimulus of low-load resistance training while limiting external mechanical stress1. By applying external pressure to the proximal limb, BFR partially restricts arterial inflow and venous outflow, thereby modifying the intramuscular metabolic environment during exercise2. This approach enables substantial metabolic perturbation at loads substantially lower than those typically required to stimulate comparable responses under unrestricted conditions. In high-performance sport settings—where joint loading, cumulative fatigue, and training density must be carefully managed—such low-load strategies may provide a means of introducing additional physiological stress without excessive mechanical burden. In elite athletes, annual improvements in key performance metrics are often marginal (commonly < 1–2%)3; consequently, even small, repeatable perturbations may be practically meaningful if they can be integrated without disrupting recovery or performance readiness. However, translating BFR research into elite contexts requires methodologically rigorous designs that ensure physiological interpretability and dose control4.

Elite male volleyball athletes represent a relevant yet underexamined population in this context. Volleyball performance is characterized by repeated high-intensity jumping, rapid force production, and precise neuromuscular coordination performed under conditions of high competition density5. These demands may constrain the routine use of high-load resistance exercise during congested periods, potentially increasing the appeal of low-load strategies such as BFR. At the same time, the advanced neuromuscular efficiency and training status of elite volleyball players may modify their acute response patterns compared with recreational cohorts. Accordingly, findings derived from non-elite populations cannot be assumed to generalize directly to this group, underscoring the need for investigation under rigorously controlled and sport-relevant conditions6.

A central methodological issue in BFR research is the prescription of cuff pressure. Fixed absolute pressures expressed in mmHg can produce highly variable levels of vascular restriction across individuals due to differences in limb circumference, tissue composition, cuff width, and vascular characteristics7,8. Contemporary guidelines therefore recommend determining arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) and prescribing restriction intensity as a percentage of AOP (%AOP) to improve standardization and interpretability2–4. Individualized pressure prescription is particularly relevant when comparing multiple restriction intensities within the same cohort, as inadequate dose normalization may confound true condition-dependent responses. This consideration may be especially important in elite athletes, whose limb morphology and vascular adaptations may differ from recreational or clinical populations3,9. Without individualized normalization, apparent differences between restriction conditions may reflect uncontrolled inter-individual variability rather than genuine intensity-dependent effects.

Although the acute responses to BFR exercise have been widely investigated1,4, considerably less attention has been given to the very early post-exercise recovery phase, particularly within elite athletic populations3. In practical high-performance environments, resistance sessions are often embedded within dense training schedules or same-day technical sessions5,6. Under such circumstances, understanding the magnitude and temporal pattern of neuromuscular perturbation during the first 30 min following exercise may inform decisions regarding session sequencing, supplementary loading, or recovery management. Early recovery responses may also provide insight into whether increasing occlusion pressure meaningfully alters the short-term neuromuscular state beyond the exercise bout itself2,4. Despite this potential applied relevance, dose–response evidence examining recovery trajectories under individualized %AOP conditions in elite athletes remains limited1–3.

Characterizing early neuromuscular recovery requires careful consideration of assessment methodology. Surface electromyography (sEMG) parameters such as root mean square (RMS) amplitude and median frequency (MF) are commonly used descriptors of neuromuscular behavior; however, these measures are highly sensitive to contraction mode, joint angle, muscle length, and contraction velocity10–12. Moreover, signal conditioning and processing choices (e.g., filtering, window length, and spectral estimation method) can materially influence amplitude- and frequency-domain outcomes, further emphasizing the need for standardized procedures10,11. EMG signals obtained during rest, dynamic movement, and isometric contractions therefore represent distinct neuromechanical states and should not be directly equated10,11,13. When outcomes derived from non-equivalent tasks are compared, interpretation may be compromised. Standardizing assessment using force-targeted isometric contractions performed at fixed joint angles provides a defensible framework for isolating post-exercise changes in neuromuscular output under controlled mechanical conditions13,14. Within such a framework, MF can serve as a global spectral descriptor of time-dependent post-exercise shifts, while RMS amplitude reflects relative activation requirements during a force-matched task. Importantly, these indices should be interpreted here as descriptive markers of acute perturbation rather than as indicators of fiber-type–specific recruitment, selective motor unit fatigue, or “neural drive” in a mechanistic sense11,15.

In addition to neural indices, ultrasound-derived muscle thickness offers a complementary perspective on acute morphological responses following BFR exercise. Transient increases in muscle thickness are often attributed to fluid redistribution or edema-like swelling16,17. Although such changes do not reflect chronic hypertrophy, they may provide information regarding the magnitude of acute intramuscular perturbation. However, thickness measures are sensitive to posture, probe pressure, and timing relative to exercise, necessitating strict standardization and cautious interpretation—particularly when examining short recovery windows.

Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to characterize acute neuromuscular and morphological responses, as well as short-term recovery trajectories, following low-load leg press exercise performed under individualized BFR conditions (0%, 40%, 60%, and 80% AOP) in elite male volleyball athletes. Restriction intensity was prescribed relative to Doppler-determined AOP to enhance physiological interpretability. It was hypothesized that MF would exhibit a time-dependent reduction immediately following exercise, consistent with an acute spectral shift, whereas RMS amplitude and muscle thickness would demonstrate modest condition-related differences without clearly differentiated recovery trajectories across the applied %AOP range. The study was designed to describe condition- and time-dependent response patterns within a tightly standardized assessment framework, rather than to infer long-term training adaptations.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty-three male volleyball athletes competing at the international-level from a single high-performance training squad volunteered to participate. All participants were actively training and competing during the data-collection period. The sample size (n = 33) reflects the total number of eligible athletes within the squad during the designated testing period; no eligible athlete declined participation. All enrolled participants completed every experimental session and all outcome assessments, and no data were excluded from analysis.

Descriptive characteristics (age, stature, body mass, leg press 1RM, and training age) are reported in Table 1. All testing was conducted indoors under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 1 °C; 50 ± 5% relative humidity), with session start times standardized between 14:00 and 17:00 to minimize potential diurnal variation.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (i) classification as international-level volleyball athletes, defined by participation in at least one international competition within the previous year, (ii) ≥5 years of systematic volleyball training with regular resistance-training experience, and (iii) ability to complete the experimental low-load leg press protocol without pain or functional limitation.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) current musculoskeletal injury or pain affecting lower-limb performance, (ii) lower-limb surgery within the previous 12 months, (iii) neurological disorders affecting neuromuscular function, and (iv) any medical condition or medication use known to influence vascular function, coagulation, or neuromuscular outcomes.

BFR-specific safety screening

Prior to enrollment, all athletes underwent a structured BFR safety screening procedure designed to operationalize the vascular- and hematological-related exclusion criteria described above. The screening focused specifically on established contraindications and precautionary conditions for blood flow restriction exercise, including a history of thromboembolic events, uncontrolled hypertension, diagnosed vascular disease, coagulation disorders, or other clinically relevant cardiovascular or hematological conditions2,18.

Medical history was reviewed in collaboration with the team’s medical staff to confirm eligibility. Athletes meeting any contraindication criteria were excluded from participation in BFR conditions. No athlete met exclusion criteria during the screening process.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All athletes received a detailed explanation of procedures and potential risks/discomfort associated with BFR and provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Pre-test standardization

Participants were instructed to: (i) avoid additional lower-body resistance training and strenuous exercise for ≥ 24 h before each session, (ii) maintain habitual diet and hydration, and (iii) refrain from alcohol and stimulant intake (e.g., high-dose caffeine) on the day of testing when feasible within the team schedule. Sessions were scheduled consistently within the team training timetable19.

All experimental sessions were conducted during a planned post-competition recovery period immediately following a major international competition period. During this phase of the annual training cycle, athletes were scheduled for recovery and load reduction, and no structured team training or resistance training was prescribed. This timing wasselected to minimize variability in residual fatigue and neuromuscular state, thereby enhancing the internal validity of within-subject comparisons across BFR conditions. Athletes remained in a centralized high-performance training environment under standardized team-management and anti-doping procedures.

Study design overview

A randomized, counterbalanced, fully crossed within-subject crossover design was used. Each athlete completed four experimental conditions on separate days: 0% AOP (control; cuff applied without inflation), 40% AOP, 60% AOP, and 80% AOP. Condition order was computer-randomized and counterbalanced across participants. Sessions were separated by ≥ 48 h to minimize carryover and residual fatigue effects20. Primary outcomes were acute neuromuscular responses (sEMG) and transient vastus lateralis (VL) muscle thickness changes assessed across a standardized post-exercise recovery time course (Pre, Post-0, Post-10, Post-20, Post-30 min) (Fig. 1).

For terminological precision, ‘BFR conditions’ denote cuff inflation at the prescribed percentage of arterial occlusion pressure (%AOP), whereas the ‘control condition’ denotes cuff placement without inflation (0% AOP).

One-repetition maximum (1RM) leg press assessment

Maximal lower-limb strength was assessed using a bilateral supine leg press machine positioned at a 45° incline (Technogym, Cesena, Italy). Testing followed established resistance-testing guidelines to ensure standardized execution and reliability21.

To minimize learning effects, all athletes completed a familiarization session ≥ 24 h before experimental testing. Familiarization included instruction and supervised practice of leg press technique (breathing strategy, foot placement, and range-of-motion constraints), and a full simulation of cuff placement and BFR procedures22.

On the 1RM testing day, participants completed: (i) 5 min low-intensity cycling, (ii) dynamic lower-limb mobilization, and (iii) progressive leg press–specific warm-up sets (8–10 repetitions at ~ 40–50% estimated 1RM, then 3–5 repetitions at ~ 60–70%), with 2–3 min rest between sets. Load was then increased incrementally until only one repetition could be completed through the predefined range of motion with proper technique. Range of motion was standardized using mechanical stops integrated into the sled (starting at ~ 90° knee flexion and ending near full extension without knee lockout). Foot placement was replicated using machine reference markings. Rest intervals of 3–5 min were provided between maximal attempts. The highest successfully completed load was recorded as 1RM and used to prescribe the experimental exercise load (20% 1RM)21.

Blood flow restriction (BFR) procedures

Cuff system and placement

BFR was applied using a pneumatic cuff system (B-STRONG, USA) with a thigh cuff width of 13.5 cm. The cuff was positioned at the most proximal portion of the dominant thigh, immediately distal to the inguinal fold. Cuff placement (height, orientation, and initial tightness) was standardized by the same investigator across all sessions. All AOP and exercise procedures were performed in a consistent supine posture to reduce variability in occlusion characteristics2,23.

Individual arterial occlusion pressure determination

AOP was determined bilaterally at the start of each experimental session using a handheld Doppler ultrasound device to detect the posterior tibial artery pulse. With the participant positioned supine and fully relaxed, cuff pressure was increased gradually until the distal arterial pulse was no longer detectable; this pressure was defined as that session’s limb-specific AOP2,7,23.

To enhance reliability, AOP was assessed twice for each limb with complete cuff deflation between trials. If the two values differed by > 10 mmHg, a third measurement was obtained and the median value was used. This repeated-measure approach was implemented to reduce measurement noise and improve the stability of %AOP prescription across sessions2,9.

To account for potential inter-limb differences in occlusion characteristics and to ensure conservative, physiologically interpretable dosing, restriction intensity was prescribed using the lower of the two limb-specific AOP values (“lowest AOP”) as the reference AOP for that session9. Accordingly, target restriction levels were set at 40%, 60%, and 80% of the session-specific lowest AOP.

Pressure conditions

For each session, cuff pressure was set to the assigned condition (0%, 40%, 60%, or 80% of that session’s reference AOP). In the 0% AOP condition, the cuff was positioned but not inflated (no restriction). In BFR conditions (40–80% AOP), cuff pressure was maintained continuously throughout the exercise bout and then fully deflated immediately upon completion of the final repetition. All post-exercise neuromuscular and ultrasound measurements were collected during recovery with the cuff fully deflated to characterize post-exercise responses rather than ongoing restriction effects2,18.

Exercise protocol

Each session began with a standardized warm-up consisting of 5 min low-intensity aerobic activity and dynamic lower-limb mobilization, followed by a brief leg press warm-up using light loads. The exercise task consisted of bilateral supine leg press on a 45° incline sled at 20% 1RM. The repetition structure followed a standardized low-load BFR format: 4 sets (30 + 15 + 15 + 15 repetitions), 30 s inter-set rest, continuous cuff inflation across the entire 4-set bout for BFR conditions (40%, 60%, 80% AOP). Repetition cadence was standardized using an auditory metronome (2 s concentric / 2 s eccentric). Range of motion was replicated across visits using mechanical stops and consistent foot placement markings, and repetitions were performed without compensatory pelvic movement24,25.

Cuff deflation and post-exercise recovery measurements

For all BFR conditions (40%, 60%, and 80% AOP), cuff pressure was maintained continuously throughout the four-set exercise bout and fully released immediately upon completion of the final repetition. All post-exercise neuromuscular and ultrasound measurements (Post-0, Post-10, Post-20, and Post-30 min) were conducted with the cuff completely deflated, ensuring that outcome measures reflected post-exercise recovery responses rather than ongoing vascular restriction2.

Safety monitoring and tolerability outcomes

Participants were continuously monitored for BFR intolerance symptoms (e.g., paresthesia, excessive discomfort, abnormal discoloration, dizziness). Predefined termination criteria were implemented: the protocol was discontinued immediately if participants reported intolerable discomfort, progressive paresthesia, loss of voluntary control, dizziness, or any signs suggestive of compromised vascular or neurological status. Immediately after completion of the exercise bout, session perceived exertion was recorded using the Borg 6–20 rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale. At the same time point, cuff-related discomfort/pain and paresthesia were recorded as binary yes/no outcomes for subsequent tolerability analyses.

Surface electromyography (sEMG)

Rationale for task standardization

To ensure physiological comparability and to avoid invalid comparisons across non-equivalent contraction modes, all inferential analyses were restricted to standardized isometric contractions performed at identical joint angles across all conditions and timepoints. EMG recorded during dynamic leg press repetitions was not used for inferential statistics15.

Instrumentation and electrode placement

Bipolar sEMG signals were recorded using a wireless multi-channel EMG system (Delsys, USA) at 1500 Hz. Recordings were obtained from VL of the dominant limb, which was defined a priori as the primary outcome limb. Electrode placement followed SENIAM14 recommendations. Skin was shaved if necessary, lightly abraded, and cleaned with alcohol to reduce impedance. Electrodes were aligned parallel to the estimated fiber orientation and secured with adhesive interfaces and elastic fixation. Electrode locations were marked using indelible ink to replicate placement across sessions.

Participant positioning and joint-angle control

All sEMG recordings were collected with participants supine against the leg press platform. Joint angles were standardized to ~ 45° knee flexion and ~ 90° hip flexion and verified at each timepoint using a digital goniometer. Participants were instructed to maintain consistent posture and avoid extraneous movement during the isometric task26.

Force-targeted standardized isometric task

To objectively standardize contraction intensity across conditions and timepoints, neuromuscular assessments were based on force-targeted submaximal isometric contractions. Isometric force was measured via a uniaxial load cell integrated into the leg press platform, providing real-time visual feedback of platform reaction force (N) to the participant14,15.

At the beginning of each session, participants performed two maximal voluntary isometric contractions (MVICs) at the standardized joint angles, each lasting 3–4 s, with 60–90 s rest between attempts. The highest peak force value was defined as the session-specific MVIC27. The target force for all subsequent standardized isometric trials was set to 30% of the session-specific MVIC. At each timepoint (Pre, Post-0, Post-10, Post-20, Post-30 min), participants gradually increased force to the target and maintained a steady contraction for ~ 3 s, guided by standardized verbal cues and a visual timing reference28. Trials were accepted if mean force during the analysis window fell within ± 10% of the target; otherwise, the contraction was repeated once after a brief rest13.

Ultrasound assessment of vastus lateralis muscle thickness

VL muscle thickness was assessed on the dominant limb using B-mode ultrasonography (Philips CX50, Philips Medical Systems, USA) with a 7.5-MHz linear-array transducer. Imaging parameters (depth, gain, focal zone) were held constant within participant across sessions29. Measurements were obtained with participants positioned supine against the leg press platform, matching the sEMG posture. Joint angles were standardized (~ 45° knee flexion; ~90° hip flexion) and verified using a digital goniometer. Participants remained fully relaxed during image acquisition30.

The imaging site was defined using standardized anatomical landmarks, marked with indelible ink to ensure reproducible probe placement. The transducer was aligned perpendicular to the skin and oriented transverse to the longitudinal axis of the muscle. Images were acquired at baseline (Pre; after ≥ 5 min passive rest) and during recovery at Post-0, Post-10, Post-20, and Post-30 min. To minimize artefacts, images were captured at end-expiration using minimal probe pressure. Three consecutive images were obtained at each timepoint, and the mean value was used for analysis31. All scans were performed by the same experienced operator, with intra-rater reliability assessed prior to the study (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC > 0.95). Muscle thickness outcomes were interpreted as transient morphological responses (e.g., acute swelling/fluid shifts) rather than indicators of chronic hypertrophy32.

Data processing

EMG signal processing

sEMG signals were processed offline using EMGWorks® 4.0 (Delsys, USA). Raw recordings were visually inspected for artefacts. Signals were band-pass filtered between 20 and 500 Hz using a zero-lag digital filter, full-wave rectified, and smoothed using a 100-ms moving RMS window. RMS amplitude and MF were computed exclusively from the standardized force-targeted isometric contractions performed at fixed joint angles33.

For each isometric contraction, the analysis window was defined as the most stable force-maintenance segment of the hold (central portion of the ~ 3-s contraction), minimizing contamination from force ramp-up and termination phases. RMS amplitude was normalized to the maximum RMS value obtained during the session-specific MVIC performed at the same standardized joint angles. Normalized RMS values are reported as RMS% to facilitate within-subject comparisons across conditions and timepoints while accounting for session-to-session variability in signal amplitude34. MF was computed using fast Fourier transform and interpreted as a global spectral descriptor of the sEMG signal. Consistent with established guidance, MF outcomes were not used to infer fiber-type composition or selective fast-twitch recruitment26.

Ultrasound processing

Ultrasound images were analyzed using semi-automated software with standardized fascial-border identification procedures. All measurements were visually verified by the same investigator, blinded to condition. Processing parameters were defined a priori and applied consistently across participants and conditions31.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were screened for completeness and obvious recording artefacts before analysis. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Continuous descriptive data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Session RPE is summarized as median and interquartile range, and binary tolerability outcomes are summarized as n (%), as appropriate.

The primary outcomes were vastus lateralis median frequency, RMS amplitude normalized to the session-specific maximal voluntary isometric contraction (RMS%), and vastus lateralis muscle thickness. In accordance with the predefined analytical framework, inferential analyses for these outcomes were based on measurements obtained from the dominant limb. Each outcome was analysed using a prespecified linear mixed-effects model including Condition (0%, 40%, 60%, and 80% of arterial occlusion pressure), Time (Pre, Post-0, Post-10, Post-20, and Post-30), and the Condition × Time interaction as fixed effects, with participant included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations within athletes. The repeated observations across Time within each participant were modelled using a compound symmetry covariance structure. Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML)35, and denominator degrees of freedom were approximated using the Satterthwaite method (SPSS MIXED)36.

Inference for Condition, Time, and the Condition × Time interaction was obtained from Type III tests of fixed effects derived from the same full interaction model. When the Condition × Time interaction was not significant, interpretation was restricted to the marginal main effects estimated from that same model; no reduced additive model was refitted. When follow-up evaluation was warranted, pairwise comparisons were derived from estimated marginal means within the same modelling framework. In the absence of a significant interaction, post hoc contrasts were limited to marginal comparisons of Condition collapsed across Time and/or Time collapsed across Condition; condition comparisons at individual time points were not performed. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm procedure to control the family-wise error rate within each family of contrasts37. Model-based results are reported as estimated marginal means with corresponding standard errors, mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and adjusted P values, as appropriate. Non-significant contrasts are explicitly identified.

Post-exercise tolerability outcomes were analysed at the session level according to variable type. Session RPE was compared across conditions using Friedman tests, followed, where appropriate, by Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise post hoc comparisons. Binary tolerability outcomes, including cuff-related discomfort/pain and paresthesia, were compared across conditions using Cochran’s Q tests, with Holm-adjusted McNemar tests performed when warranted. Outcomes showing no between-condition variability were summarized descriptively only. No data imputation was performed.

Figures display individual participant responses alongside group means to illustrate within-subject response patterns and inter-individual variability.

Safety monitoring and tolerability

All 33 athletes completed all four experimental conditions, yielding complete tolerability data for 132 sessions. Session RPE differed across conditions (Friedman χ²(3) = 96.99, p < 0.001). Median (IQR) RPE values were 11 (10–12) at 0% AOP, 13 (12–13) at 40% AOP, 15 (14–15) at 60% AOP, and 16 (16–17) at 80% AOP. Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests indicated that each condition differed from every other condition (all adjusted p < 0.001).

Cuff-related discomfort/pain also differed across conditions (Cochran’s Q = 26.56, p < 0.001). The proportion of athletes reporting discomfort/pain was 0/33 (0.0%) at 0% AOP, 1/33 (3.0%) at 40% AOP, 6/33 (18.2%) at 60% AOP, and 12/33 (36.4%) at 80% AOP. Holm-adjusted McNemar tests showed higher discomfort/pain occurrence at 80% AOP than at 0% AOP (adjusted p = 0.003) and 40% AOP (adjusted p = 0.005), whereas the remaining pairwise contrasts were not significant (all adjusted p ≥ 0.125).

Paresthesia differed across conditions (Cochran’s Q = 19.00, p < 0.001). No athlete reported paresthesia at 0% or 40% AOP, whereas paresthesia was reported by 3/33 athletes (9.1%) at 60% AOP and 8/33 athletes (24.2%) at 80% AOP. Holm-adjusted McNemar tests showed higher paresthesia occurrence at 80% AOP than at 0% AOP (adjusted p = 0.047) and 40% AOP (adjusted p = 0.047), whereas the remaining pairwise contrasts were not significant (all adjusted p ≥ 0.250).

Discussion

Overview and principal findings

This study examined acute neuromuscular and morphological responses to low-load resistance exercise performed under individualized levels of blood flow restriction in elite male volleyball players. Using standardized post-exercise assessments, we observed clear main effects of Condition and Time for MF, RMS%, and VL thickness; importantly, no Condition × Time interactions were detected, indicating that the temporal pattern of change during the first 30 min of recovery was comparable across BFR intensities. Thus, the present findings support the interpretation that increasing occlusion pressure induces small, systematic offsets in group-level responses (i.e., a shift in the overall level of the indices), but does not produce condition-specific recovery trajectories within the short recovery window examined2,3.

From an inference standpoint, the absence of interactions also allows an alternative, conservative interpretation: although statistical differences between conditions were detectable when averaged across time, the current protocol does not provide clear evidence that BFR meaningfully enhances short-term recovery relative to the control condition (0% AOP) in elite volleyball athletes1,2,4. Therefore, any inference about training or performance benefits based solely on these short-term recovery indices would be speculative. Collectively, these results refine current understanding of acute BFR responses in highly trained athletes by showing that individualized occlusion pressure influences the magnitude of neuromuscular and morphological indices, yet does not substantively alter early recovery dynamics under standardized assessment conditions4,5.

Methodological features that strengthen interpretability in elite athletes

A key design priority was to maximize physiological interpretability and address common methodological concerns in BFR research involving athletes. Restriction intensity was individualized using Doppler-derived AOP and expressed as %AOP, thereby reducing inter-individual variability inherent to fixed mmHg prescriptions and strengthening the validity of dose comparisons within a trained cohort2,4. Additionally, because inter-limb AOP can differ within athletes, the bilateral AOP characterization supported a conservative “lowest AOP” approach for prescription, limiting the likelihood that a given relative target unintentionally approaches full occlusion in the limb with the lower occlusion threshold2,7,8. This aspect is particularly relevant in applied settings where individualized prescription must remain practical and safe across repeated sessions.

Equally important, neuromuscular outcomes were derived from force-targeted isometric contractions at fixed joint angles, avoiding direct comparisons of EMG metrics across dynamic repetitions and isometric tasks—conditions that are not physiologically interchangeable13,33. This standardized “probe task” approach strengthens inference regarding post-exercise changes in EMG amplitude and spectral features during early recovery, while acknowledging that neuromuscular behavior during the dynamic exercise itself may differ10,11,15. Notably, the inclusion of systematic safety monitoring and tolerability outcomes provides additional applied context for interpreting dose selection in elite athletes, in whom feasibility and symptom burden can strongly influence uptake.

Comparison with prior BFR recovery studies and considerations for elite-specific responses

Most acute BFR resistance exercise studies conducted in recreationally active or moderately trained individuals consistently report elevated perceptual strain and marked metabolic perturbation during exercise, with post-exercise changes in neuromuscular or morphological indices that are often small and method-dependent1,2,4,24. Importantly, the short-term recovery literature remains comparatively limited, and true dose–response evidence is especially sparse when outcomes are assessed using standardized force-matched probe tasks rather than being derived from the occluded exercise itself2,4. In addition, although individualized prescription using %AOP is increasingly recommended to improve dose control and interpretability2,7,8, relatively few studies have contrasted multiple individualized restriction levels within the same participants while simultaneously tracking early post-exercise recovery trajectories. In elite sport contexts, the available BFR evidence has largely focused on performance-oriented adaptations or cohort responses rather than on immediate recovery dynamics3,9. For example, elite canoe athletes have demonstrated improvements in strength- and performance-related outcomes following BFR interventions9, and world-class rowers have shown small but meaningful time-trial performance changes in association with BFR training exposure3, yet these studies do not directly establish that increasing occlusion pressure confers superior short-term neuromuscular recovery.

Against this background, the present findings refine the translational interpretation of BFR “dose” in elite volleyball athletes. Across 0–80% AOP, we detected small overall condition-related offsets in neuromuscular indices when responses were averaged across time, but the recovery time course over the first 30 min was comparable across restriction levels, consistent with the absence of condition-dependent temporal effects2,4. This pattern can be interpreted conservatively in two non-exclusive ways: (i) within the applied range and under controlled probe conditions, occlusion pressure primarily modulates the overall magnitude of the acute perturbation without materially altering early recovery dynamics; and/or (ii) any pressure-dependent differences in recovery are too small to be distinguished from measurement noise and biological variability in highly trained athletes using the present probe task approach19,20. From an applied perspective, these results do not support escalating occlusion pressure with the expectation of improved short-term recovery in elite players. Rather, pressure selection in this context may be better guided by feasibility and tolerability considerations—particularly given that higher pressures can increase discomfort and transient sensory symptoms without producing clearly divergent recovery trajectories2.

Interpretation of MF: time-dependent spectral shift without evidence of pressure-modulated recovery

The robust Time effect for MF indicates a systematic post-exercise spectral shift during early recovery15,33. Under the present standardized assessment conditions, MF decreased progressively across the 30-min recovery window, rather than showing the common “drop-and-rebound” pattern that might be expected if recovery toward baseline occurred rapidly. This observation directly addresses the interpretive concern raised by visual inspection: in the present dataset, MF did not return toward baseline within 30 min. Importantly, MF is a global spectral descriptor influenced by multiple factors, including changes in muscle fiber conduction velocity, motor unit firing behavior, and local fluid and temperature states; consequently, it should not be interpreted as evidence of fiber-type–specific recruitment or selective fast-twitch fatigue11,12,15. The present findings are therefore best described as a time-dependent MF shift under controlled probe conditions, reflecting a persistent neuromuscular perturbation following the exercise bout10,15,33.

Despite a statistically significant Condition main effect for MF, the absence of a Condition × Time interaction indicates that occlusion pressure primarily shifted the overall MF level rather than altering the shape of the recovery trajectory. From a practical standpoint, the magnitude of these offsets was small, and the similarity of temporal patterns across conditions suggests that escalating BFR intensity within the applied range does not produce clearly distinguishable short-term MF recovery dynamics in elite volleyball athletes. This pattern is consistent with either genuinely small dose–response effects in highly trained athletes or limited sensitivity of MF, when assessed using force-matched isometric probing, to discriminate among moderate-to-high restriction levels2,4.

Interpretation of RMS%: modest condition-related offsets without distinct recovery trajectories

RMS% showed significant main effects of Condition and Time, yet no Condition × Time interaction, indicating that RMS% increased across recovery and that higher occlusion pressures were associated with small upward shifts in overall RMS% values. Given the multifactorial determinants of sEMG amplitude—including non-neural influences such as skin–electrode impedance, changes in muscle geometry, and potential mechanical effects related to cuff application—these condition-related offsets should be interpreted cautiously and narrowly33,34. Specifically, they do not necessarily indicate greater “activation” in a physiological sense, nor do they establish a pressure-dependent fatigue mechanism.

From an applied perspective, the present findings suggest that, in elite volleyball players, RMS% obtained during a standardized isometric probe is more likely to detect small between-condition offsets than to reveal condition-specific recovery dynamics over a short window10,33. Critically, because the temporal pattern did not diverge by condition, the current data do not support strong claims that higher BFR pressures improve or impair short-term recovery relative to the control condition. Future studies aiming to clarify functional relevance should incorporate outcomes with clearer physiological mapping and practical meaning for performance (e.g., maximal isometric force, rate of force development, evoked twitch measures, neuromuscular efficiency indices, or sport-specific power measures)].

VL thickness: delayed post-exercise increase and limited dose discrimination in early recovery

Ultrasound-derived VL thickness exhibited significant main effects of Condition and Time, with thickness increasing during the recovery window and showing a small overall elevation with higher occlusion pressures16,17. The time course—characterized by a delayed increase during recovery—is consistent with acute morphological responses such as fluid shifts and transient swelling following exercise, phenomena that can occur even at low loads and may be accentuated by restriction-induced hemodynamic and osmotic factors32. However, the absence of a Condition × Time interaction indicates that occlusion pressure did not meaningfully alter the trajectory of thickness change over time within the first 30 min.

While statistically detectable, the magnitude of condition-related differences was small29,30. As such, thickness responses over short recovery windows should be viewed primarily as markers of acute fluid-related morphological perturbation rather than evidence of training-relevant hypertrophic signaling16,17. Methodological factors intrinsic to acute ultrasound measures (probe pressure, posture control, timing, and operator dependence) can further constrain sensitivity, even when standardized procedures are used31,38. Accordingly, although VL thickness can capture acute post-exercise changes in elite athletes, discriminating a clear pressure–response relationship in early recovery using this endpoint alone is challenging32.

Practical implications in an elite-performance context

Elite athletes operate under “diminishing returns,” and even small physiological perturbations may matter if they are repeatable, tolerable, and can be integrated without compromising training quality3,5,21,22. The present study provides two applied messages. First, low-load exercise with individualized BFR was operationally feasible in elite male volleyball players, as all athletes completed all four experimental conditions. However, tolerability worsened as occlusion pressure increased: session RPE rose progressively across conditions, cuff-related discomfort/pain became more frequent at higher pressures, and paresthesia was observed only in the 60% and 80% AOP conditions. Second, within the applied range, increasing occlusion pressure produced only small main-effect offsets in neuromuscular and morphological indices and did not yield condition-specific recovery trajectories during the first 30 min post-exercise2,4. Therefore, the present results do not provide clear evidence that escalating BFR intensity confers superior short-term recovery characteristics or practical advantages over 0% AOP in this elite cohort1,2.

In high-performance settings, dose selection should be guided not only by physiological rationale but also by feasibility and tolerability2,39. Because the clearest symptom burden occurred at the highest pressure without any corresponding divergence in recovery trajectories, routine escalation to 80% AOP appears difficult to justify when the immediate objective is short-term recovery management or integration into dense training schedules4,18. Importantly, the present study was not designed to test training adaptations; thus, claims regarding performance or hypertrophic benefits of BFR in elite volleyball athletes cannot be made based on these short-term recovery outcomes alone1,4. Establishing practical utility will require longer-term interventions and endpoints that directly reflect performance capacity and training quality, as well as mechanistic measures with clearer physiological interpretation3,6,22,28.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations constrain inference. First, although %AOP standardizes restriction intensity, the study examined only acute responses and cannot be used to infer chronic adaptation or performance transfer. Second, the monitoring window was limited to 30 min; longer recovery tracking may be required to determine when MF returns to baseline and whether subtle pressure effects emerge later. Third, neuromuscular outcomes were derived from a force-matched isometric probe to ensure task equivalence; while this strengthens interpretability, it may reduce sensitivity to detect differences that manifest primarily during dynamic movement. Finally, while Holm adjustment was applied to control family-wise error, the combination of small effect sizes, elite-cohort variability, and relatively coarse session-level tolerability profiling suggests that future work should consider larger samples, more granular symptom-intensity measures, additional mechanistic endpoints, and designs explicitly powered to test equivalence or minimal clinically important differences.

Conclusions

In elite male volleyball players, low-load resistance exercise performed with individualized blood flow restriction elicited acute, measurable neuromuscular and morphological responses. Across the range of restriction intensities tested (40–80% of arterial occlusion pressure), higher occlusion pressures were associated with small overall differences in MF, RMS%, and vastus lateralis thickness when responses were averaged across time. However, recovery profiles over the first 30 min after exercise did not diverge across restriction levels, consistent with the absence of condition-dependent time-course effects.

Accordingly, within this controlled short-term recovery framework, varying BFR intensity appears to influence the overall magnitude of acute responses more than the recovery trajectory itself. The present data do not provide evidence that higher occlusion pressures confer superior short-term recovery characteristics in this elite cohort. Given that tolerability worsened as occlusion pressure increased, routine escalation to the highest pressure appears difficult to justify when the immediate objective is short-term recovery management. Future studies should determine whether these pressure-related differences translate into meaningful adaptations over repeated training exposures and whether longer recovery windows or alternative outcome measures reveal additional intensity-specific effects.
