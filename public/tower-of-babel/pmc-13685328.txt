Integrated proteomics and machine learning reveal AHNAK as a discriminative biomarker of atrial fibrillation in heart failure
Authors: Wengen Zhu, Zhenbang Gu, Yuwen Huang, Min Ye, Ruicong Xue, Yugang Dong, Yuzhong Wu, Chen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a frequent complication of heart failure (HF), markedly increasing morbidity and mortality. Although atrial fibrosis is increasingly recognized as a central mechanistic substrate linking HF to AF, the molecular determinants driving fibrotic remodeling and enabling early AF discrimination in HF remain poorly defined.MethodsWe integrated label-free quantitative plasma proteomics with machine learning-based feature selection to analyze samples from 59 age- and sex-matched patients hospitalized with acute decompensated HF. Differentially expressed proteins were subjected to functional and pathway enrichment analyses, and candidate biomarkers were prioritized using an incremental feature selection framework. AHNAK, the top-ranked candidate, was functionally interrogated in vitro using TGF-β1-stimulated primary mouse atrial fibroblasts with siRNA-mediated knockdown and in vivo using a pressure overload-induced HF model in AHNAK heterozygous mice. A two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis was performed to evaluate the association between genetically proxied AHNAK expression and AF risk.ResultsPlasma proteomic profiling identified 126 proteins that were significantly dysregulated in HF patients with AF compared with those in sinus rhythm, among which AHNAK was the most upregulated. Machine learning-based feature selection consistently ranked AHNAK as the most discriminative protein, with robust performance for AF discrimination in HF patients (AUC = 0.897, 95% confidence interval: 0.82–0.97). Functionally, AHNAK heterozygous deficiency promoted a profibrotic phenotype, characterized by increased expression of α-smooth muscle actin, fibronectin, and collagen Iα in atrial fibroblasts and exacerbated atrial fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction in pressure-overloaded mice. Mendelian randomization analysis demonstrated a significant protective effect of higher AHNAK expression against AF, with no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy.ConclusionsThis study identifies AHNAK as a discriminative plasma biomarker for AF in HF patients and implicates AHNAK deficiency as a contributor to atrial fibrotic remodeling and AF pathogenesis. External validation in larger and prospective HF cohorts will be required to confirm the clinical utility of AHNAK for AF risk stratification and early detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05079-1.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Heart failure, AHNAK protein, Atrial fibrosis, Biomarkers

Background

Heart failure (HF) is a complex and life-threatening syndrome that affects over 60 million people worldwide [1]. Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common sustained arrhythmia, significantly elevates the risk of stroke, leading to substantial mortality and disability [2]. They also share several clinical risk factors and frequently coexist, each condition exacerbating the other in clinical settings [3]. Atrial fibrosis, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix deposition in the atria, is a hallmark in the development of AF and is particularly prevalent in HF patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that HF patients with AF often present distinct clinical profiles, experience worse outcomes, and exhibit reduced responsiveness to β-blockers than those without AF [4–6]. This suggests potential differences in the underlying pathophysiology between HF patients with AF and those in sinus rhythm, although the precise mechanisms driving AF onset in the HF population remain poorly understood [7].

Early prediction of AF in HF patients is particularly important given the high prevalence of AF in this population and its association with increased morbidity and mortality. Timely identification of AF can facilitate early intervention potentially slowing HF progression and improving overall prognosis. Several risk stratification tools, including the Framingham risk score, C2HEST, CHARGE-AF, and artificial intelligence-based models, have been developed to predict AF in the general population [8–12]. However, specific predictive strategies tailored to the HF population remain scarce, and existing tools often lack adequate discrimination when applied to HF cohorts.

Recent advances in genomics, proteomics, and imaging technologies hold promise for improving AF prediction in HF, yet significant gaps remain [3]. Notably, there is a pressing need for biomarkers that can more accurately stratify AF risk among HF patients. Machine learning models offer the potential to uncover complex patterns in high-dimensional proteomic data [13], facilitating the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers that may aid in early disease diagnosis such as in neurodegenerative diseases [14]. By using such approaches, new biomarkers may be identified, thereby enhancing early detection and enabling more personalized intervention strategies for AF in HF patients.

In this study, we aimed to identify novel diagnostic biomarkers by analyzing plasma samples from age- and sex-matched cases (HF patients with AF) and controls (HF patients in sinus rhythm) using mass spectrometry-based quantitative proteomics. We then applied machine-learning algorithms to screen for potential fluid biomarkers by identifying proteins that exhibited significant differential expression between the two groups. Subsequently, we validated the newly identified biomarkers selected through proteomics at both the cellular and animal levels, investigating their effects on atrial fibrosis. Additionally, we performed a Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore the potential causal association between the selected biomarker and AF.

Methods

Plasma proteomics of HF patients

The present study was a retrospective proteomic analysis nested within a single-center prospective registry of patients hospitalized for acute decompensated HF at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Patients hospitalized between May 2018 and March 2021 constituted the study-period source cohort (N = 2243). Eligibility screening was first performed based on the availability of adequate baseline clinical data, confirmed rhythm status (AF versus sinus rhythm), and sufficient high-quality plasma samples suitable for mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis. To minimize potential confounding by key demographic variables, an age- and sex-matching strategy was applied prior to proteomic profiling. After eligibility screening and matching, 59 patients were selected for plasma proteomic analysis. All included samples underwent identical pre-analytical processing and quality control procedures prior to Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.

The diagnosis of acute decompensated HF was independently confirmed by two cardiologists in accordance with contemporaneous guideline criteria, including the presence of at least one HF-related symptom (dyspnea, fatigue, or reduced exercise tolerance), at least one objective sign (peripheral edema or pulmonary rales), and either elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP ≥ 300 pg/mL) and/or structural or functional cardiac abnormalities documented by transthoracic echocardiography, or radiographic evidence of pulmonary congestion on chest X-ray. Critically ill patients who died during hospitalization or for whom adequate plasma samples were unavailable were excluded from analysis (Additional file 1: Figure S1). The study protocol conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Approval ID: [2016]190).

During hospitalization, all participants provided written informed consent permitting the use of their blood samples for non-genetic experimental analyses. Comprehensive clinical data, including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory parameters, echocardiographic measurements, and medication use, were systematically collected from electronic medical records. Peripheral venous blood samples were obtained, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Samples were transported on dry ice to Jingjie PTM BioLabs (Hangzhou, China) for label-free quantitative proteomic profiling.

To reduce the interference of highly abundant plasma proteins, such as albumin and immunoglobulins, on downstream mass spectrometry-based analyses, plasma samples were depleted using Pierce™ Top 14 Abundant Protein Depletion Spin Columns (Thermo Scientific) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, frozen plasma aliquots were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes and subjected to high-abundance protein depletion. Protein concentrations in the depleted fractions were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, with all standards and samples measured in technical triplicates (Additional file 1: Table S1). Equal amounts of total protein were subsequently used for enzymatic digestion to ensure comparability across samples. Depletion efficiency and protein integrity were evaluated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) before and after depletion (Additional file 1: Figure S2), and only samples demonstrating satisfactory depletion efficiency and intact protein profiles were advanced for LC-MS/MS analysis. Samples with visible hemolysis or flagged as having a high risk of pre-analytical bias were excluded.

LC-MS/MS analysis and database search

Tryptic peptides were reconstituted in solvent A and directly loaded onto a self-packed reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm in length, 100 μm internal diameter). Chromatographic separation was performed using an EASY-nLC 1200 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with solvent A consisting of 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water, and solvent B consisting of 0.1% formic acid and 90% acetonitrile in water. Peptides were eluted at a constant flow rate of 500 nL/min using a linear gradient program: 4–20% solvent B over 0–68 min, 20–32% over 68–82 min, 32–80% over 82–86 min, followed by a wash at 80% solvent B from 86 to 90 min.

Eluted peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a nano-electrospray ionization source. The spray voltage was set to 2.3 kV, and high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry was applied using compensation voltages of − 70 V and − 45 V. Full MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 60,000 across an m/z range of 400-1,200. Tandem MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 30,000 with the first mass fixed at m/z 110. TurboTMT mode was disabled to maintain compatibility with label-free quantification. The top 15 most intense precursor ions were selected for higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 27%, and dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s. The automatic gain control target was set to 75%, with an intensity threshold of 10,000 ions/s and a maximum injection time of 100 ms.

Raw MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer software (version 2.4.1.15). Spectra were searched against the Homo_sapiens_9606_PR_20201214.fasta database (75,777 entries), concatenated with a reverse decoy database to enable false discovery rate (FDR) estimation. Trypsin was specified as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The minimum peptide length was set to six amino acids, and up to three variable modifications per peptide were permitted. Precursor mass tolerance was set at 10 ppm, and fragment ion tolerance at 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was defined as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine, protein N-terminal acetylation, methionine loss, and methionine loss followed by acetylation were specified as variable modifications. FDR was stringently controlled at < 1% at the peptide-spectrum match, peptide, and protein levels.

Proteomic profiling and enrichment analysis

Global proteomic patterns were initially explored using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to visualize sample clustering and assess overall group separation. Differentially expressed proteins between HF patients with and without AF were identified based on statistical comparison, and results were visualized using histograms and volcano plots. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases to characterize enriched biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and signaling pathways. Overrepresentation of GO terms and KEGG pathways was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. To enhance biological interpretability, differentially expressed proteins were further stratified by directionality (upregulated versus downregulated), and enriched terms were organized into coherent functional modules.

Feature selection and model development

To identify candidate biomarkers with discriminative potential, machine learning-based modeling strategies were employed. The dataset was stratified using hierarchical clustering to preserve cohort structure during model development. When class imbalance was present in the training dataset, oversampling techniques, including the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) and Support Vector Machine-SMOTE (SVM-SMOTE), were applied to mitigate bias.

Feature selection and classifier construction were performed using an incremental feature selection framework with logistic regression as the base learner. Proteins were ranked using a univariate two-tailed Student’s t-test, and feature subsets were generated sequentially by adding proteins in ascending order of p values. Predictive performance for each feature subset was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, with the Matthews correlation coefficient serving as the primary evaluation metric and classification accuracy as a secondary measure. The feature subset achieving the highest Matthews correlation coefficient was selected as the optimal model and used to train the final classifier. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for the five proteins demonstrating the strongest discriminatory capacity between AF and non-AF groups.

Primary atrial fibroblast isolation, transfection, and treatment

Primary atrial fibroblasts were isolated from male C57BL/6J mice aged 3–5 weeks. Following euthanasia by cervical dislocation, hearts were rapidly excised and immersed in ice-cold D-Hanks solution. After removal of residual blood and major vessels, atrial tissue was dissected along the margin of the left atrial appendage, rinsed thoroughly, and minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments. Fibroblasts were enriched using a differential adhesion method following collagenase type II digestion. Tissue fragments were digested in collagenase solution at 37 °C with gentle agitation for 15 min, triturated, and the supernatant collected. This process was repeated twice. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and plated. After 5 h, non-adherent cells were removed, yielding an enriched fibroblast population.

At passage 2, fibroblasts were cultured to 50–60% confluence before transfection. AHNAK expression was silenced using 50 nM AHNAK-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) or scrambled control siRNA delivered with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX in serum-free DMEM/F-12. After 4 h, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 20 h. To induce a profibrotic phenotype, cells were treated with transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1, 2 ng/mL) for 24 h prior to downstream analyses.

Generation of AHNAK heterozygous mice and animal experiments

AHNAK knockout mice were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). The genotyping strategy is illustrated in Additional file 1: Figure S3. Breeding of heterozygous AHNAK-deficient mice did not yield viable homozygous offspring, likely due to embryonic lethality; therefore, all experiments were conducted using heterozygous (AHNAK Het) mice, which exhibit approximately 70% reduction in AHNAK expression. Animal housing, breeding, genotyping, and experimental procedures were performed at the Animal Experimental Center of the Northern Campus of Sun Yat-sen University in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University.

After genotype confirmation, eligible WT and AHNAK Het mice were randomly assigned to sham or aortic banding (AB) surgery groups. Male AHNAK Het mice aged 8–10 weeks were subjected to pressure overload-induced HF via AB, as previously described [15]. Wild-type (WT) littermates served as controls, and sham-operated mice underwent identical procedures without aortic constriction. Cardiac structure and function were evaluated four weeks post-surgery using transthoracic echocardiography and histological assessment of atrial fibrosis by Sirius Red staining. Although AB does not induce spontaneous AF, it reliably recapitulates HF-associated atrial structural remodeling, a recognized substrate predisposing to AF. The experimental unit was the individual mouse for the in vivo experiment. The animal experimental design, conduct, and reporting were prepared with reference to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (Additional file 3).

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed 28 days after induction of pressure overload. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (1.5-2.0% in 100% oxygen) and positioned supine on a temperature-controlled platform. Cardiac images were acquired using a Vevo 2100 high-resolution ultrasound system equipped with a 24–30 MHz transducer. Parasternal long-axis and short-axis views at the level of the papillary muscles were obtained, and M-mode recordings were collected over at least five consecutive cardiac cycles. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated to assess systolic function, and ventricular dimensions and wall thicknesses were measured to evaluate remodeling. Values were averaged across five cardiac cycles for each parameter.

RNA isolation, quantitative PCR, and western blotting

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA synthesis was performed using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was conducted using SYBR Green chemistry, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2. Western blot analysis was performed following established protocols [16], and protein expression levels were quantified by densitometry.

Mendelian randomization analysis

A two-sample MR analysis was performed to assess the causal effect of AHNAK expression on AF risk. Three core assumptions underpinned the MR framework: (1) selected genetic instruments must be strongly associated with AHNAK expression; (2) instruments should not be confounded by external factors; and (3) instruments must affect AF exclusively through AHNAK expression [17]. Cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL) variants located within ± 1 Mb of the AHNAK gene locus were extracted from the eQTLGen Consortium as candidate genetic instruments for AHNAK expression. From this source, a total of 596 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were retrieved from 31,684 whole blood samples and are listed in Additional file 1: Table S3 [18]. Variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 1% or FDR-adjusted association P values ≥ 0.05 were excluded. To ensure instrument independence, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was then performed at an r² threshold of < 0.01, and only independent variants were retained for the MR analysis.

AF summary statistics were derived from FinnGen Release 11 [19]. Sensitivity analyses included Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) [20], leave-one-out analysis, funnel plots, and density plots to assess pleiotropy and robustness. To enhance reporting transparency, this analysis was prepared with reference to the STROBE-MR guideline (Additional file 4).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using appropriate descriptive statistics. Between-group comparisons were performed using χ² tests, Student’s t-tests, or Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests, as appropriate. Differential protein expression was assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Given the hypothesis-generating nature of the proteomic analysis, nominal p values were reported without formal multiple-testing correction.

Statistical analyses of the in vivo and in vitro experiments were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Pairwise comparisons were assessed by Student’s t-test, whereas multiple group comparisons employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Two-sample MR analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8) in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The inverse-variance weighted method served as the primary analytical approach [21]. F statistics were calculated to assess instrument strength, with values > 10 indicating robust instrumental variables (IVs). Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of HF patients with and without AF

Baseline characteristics of the 59 HF patients (30 with AF and 29 in sinus rhythm) are summarized in Table 1. The overall mean age was 73.0 ± 9.2 years, and 35.6% of the cohort were female. Mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures were 119.2 ± 15.6 mmHg and 67.2 ± 10.3 mmHg, respectively. Compared with the sinus-rhythm group, patients with AF exhibited a higher resting heart rate (85.4 ± 12.1 bpm vs. 78.2 ± 11.4 bpm; P = 0.042) and a larger left atrial diameter (49.5 ± 6.8 mm vs. 41.2 ± 5.3 mm; P < 0.001). In addition, anticoagulant therapy was more frequently prescribed in the AF group (70.0% vs. 13.8%; P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in age, sex distribution, systolic or diastolic blood pressure, or LVEF between the two groups.

Quantitative plasma proteomic profiling in HF patients with and without AF

Label-free quantitative proteomic analysis identified a total of 13,801 peptides corresponding to 12,619 unique peptide sequences, which collectively mapped to 2,153 plasma proteins. Of these, 1,985 proteins met stringent quality and quantifiability criteria and were included in downstream analyses (Additional file 1: Figure S4). Quality control metrics, including peptide length distribution, peptide number per protein, sequence coverage, and molecular weight distribution, demonstrated high data integrity and analytical robustness (Additional file 1: Figure S5). The majority of identified peptides ranged from 7 to 20 amino acids in length, and most proteins exhibited < 30% sequence coverage, a distribution consistent with high-quality plasma proteomic datasets.

Unsupervised dimensionality reduction using UMAP revealed partial separation between HF patients with AF and those in sinus rhythm, primarily along the y-axis, with substantial overlap between groups (Fig. 1A). This pattern suggests moderate but biologically meaningful global proteomic differences, rather than complete molecular segregation, between the two clinical phenotypes. Differential expression analysis identified 49 proteins that were significantly upregulated and 77 proteins that were significantly downregulated in the AF group compared with the sinus-rhythm group (Fig. 1B; Additional file 1: Tables S4 and S5).

Among the most prominently upregulated proteins were AHNAK (neuroblast differentiation-associated protein), albumin (ALB), and procollagen-lysine,2-oxoglutarate 5-dioxygenase 1 (PLOD1), whereas caspase-8 (CASP8) and troponin I, slow skeletal muscle isoform (TNNI1) were among the most significantly downregulated proteins (Fig. 1C). AHNAK exhibited the largest fold increase (2.02-fold), while CASP8 showed the greatest reduction (fold change 0.28). Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed proteins further demonstrated distinct expression patterns between AF and sinus-rhythm patients (Additional file 1: Figure S6), supporting the presence of a disease-associated plasma proteomic signature.

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses

GO enrichment analysis of proteins upregulated in the AF group revealed significant overrepresentation of biological processes related to cellular organization, metabolic regulation, and immune responses, with molecular functions predominantly associated with binding and catalytic activity (Fig. 2). Notably, several upregulated proteins, including tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 1 (TIMP1) [22], fibulin-2 (FBLN2) [23], secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) [24], and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1 (IGFBP1) [25], have previously been implicated in extracellular matrix remodeling and atrial fibrosis, providing biological plausibility for their association with AF in HF. Enriched biological processes further included oxygen and nitric oxide transport as well as hydrogen peroxide catabolism. In contrast, proteins downregulated in the AF group were significantly enriched for biological processes related to apoptosis, telomere organization, and DNA replication (Additional file 1: Figure S7).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified multiple dysregulated signaling pathways relevant to AF pathogenesis. Specifically, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, and cardiac muscle contraction pathways were significantly enriched among differentially expressed proteins in AF patients (Additional file 1: Figure S8). When organized into functional modules, cardiac-specific pathways were predominantly concentrated within the downregulated protein module, including enrichment for cardiac muscle contraction (KEGG hsa04260; Fisher’s exact test P = 0.0495; fold enrichment = 5.44), driven primarily by reduced expression of MYL2 and MYL4. Concordantly, GO terms related to the contractile apparatus, such as cardiac myofibril (GO:0097512; P = 0.0073) and actin-myosin filament sliding (GO:0033275; P = 0.0079), were also significantly enriched. In parallel, an extracellular matrix and cell-adhesion module encompassing proteins such as FN1, AGRN, and TNR was enriched for biological processes related to cell-matrix interactions, including calcium-independent cell-matrix adhesion (GO:0007161; P = 0.0079) and integrin activation (GO:0033622; P = 0.0129).

Identification of candidate plasma biomarkers using machine learning

To prioritize candidate plasma biomarkers with discriminative potential for AF, a machine learning-based feature selection and classification pipeline was implemented (Fig. 3A). The proteomic dataset (59 samples by approximately 120 candidate protein features) was partitioned into training and testing sets using hierarchical clustering-based stratification to preserve underlying data structure. Assessment of class distribution confirmed no significant imbalance between AF and sinus-rhythm samples, obviating the need for oversampling approaches such as SMOTE or SVM-SMOTE.

Protein features were ranked based on statistical relevance, and a logistic regression-based incremental feature selection framework was applied. A voting classifier integrating multiple logistic regression models was trained to distinguish AF from sinus rhythm (Additional file 1: Figure S9). The optimal feature subset identified by the incremental feature selection procedure achieved excellent discriminative performance, yielding an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.988 in the independent test set (Additional file 1: Figure S10).

The five most strongly upregulated proteins within the optimal feature set (AHNAK, ALB, TIMP1, RTN4R, and KIF5B) demonstrated clear separation between AF and sinus-rhythm groups, as visualized by heatmap analysis (Fig. 3B). Individual ROC analyses confirmed robust discriminatory performance for each protein, with AHNAK exhibiting the highest AUC (0.897) (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings identify AHNAK as the most discriminative plasma biomarker for AF among patients with HF.

Effects of AHNAK deficiency on fibrotic remodeling in cellular and animal models

To investigate the functional role of AHNAK in atrial fibrosis, primary mouse atrial fibroblasts were transfected with AHNAK-targeting siRNA or scrambled control siRNA. Efficient knockdown of AHNAK was confirmed by a significant reduction in AHNAK mRNA expression in the si-AHNAK group (Fig. 4A). Exposure to TGF-β1 induced a profibrotic response in both groups, as evidenced by increased α-SMA mRNA expression (Fig. 4B).

MR analysis supports a protective role of AHNAK against AF

From the cis-eQTL analysis, 26 independent SNPs were selected as IVs for AHNAK expression (Additional file 1: Table S6). Using the inverse-variance weighted method, higher genetically predicted AHNAK expression was associated with a significantly lower risk of AF (odds ratio 0.92, 95% confidence interval 0.86–0.99; P = 0.019) (Additional file 1: Figures S12-S14). All instruments exhibited F statistics greater than 10 (mean = 53), indicating strong instrument strength.

No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected, supporting the validity of the IVs. MR-PRESSO analysis yielded concordant results (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99; P = 0.027) and identified no outlier variants. Reverse MR analysis using AF as the exposure failed to identify genome-wide significant IVs (P < 5 × 10− 8, r² < 0.01), precluding inference of reverse causality. Furthermore, summary-data-based MR analysis using eQTLGen data confirmed a protective association between AHNAK expression and AF risk (β_SMR = − 0.005), with no evidence of heterogeneity by the heterogeneity in dependent instruments test.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized an integrated approach combining unbiased quantitative proteomics, machine learning, functional validation, and MR analysis to identify AHNAK as a candidate antifibrotic regulator and discriminative plasma biomarker for AF in HF patients. Among 126 differentially expressed plasma proteins between HF patients with and without AF, AHNAK exhibited the highest fold-change increase and the greatest discriminative performance. Functional studies further revealed that AHNAK deficiency consistently promoted atrial fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix production, as evidenced by concordant transcript- and protein-level changes in primary atrial fibroblasts and by exacerbated atrial fibrosis in pressure-overloaded AHNAK heterozygous mice. Furthermore, MR analysis provided genetic evidence supporting a potential causal association of AHNAK in AF, independent of horizontal pleiotropy. Overall, these findings identify AHNAK as a biologically meaningful candidate associated with atrial fibrotic remodeling and AF susceptibility in HF.

Previously, Santema et al. [25] employed network-based analyses of a fixed panel of 92 circulating proteins and identified biomarkers such as spondin-1 and IGFBP1 as being elevated in HF patients with AF. However, the post hoc design and reliance on a targeted panel may have restricted the discovery of previously unrecognized proteins relevant to AF pathophysiology. Zhang et al. [26] conducted integrative bioinformatic analyses across multiple atrial transcriptomic datasets and proposed GLUL, NCF2, S100A12, and SRGN as candidate genes shared between HF and AF. Although these findings provided valuable genetic insights, they lacked validation at the circulating protein level, an important consideration given the frequently modest correlation between mRNA expression and protein abundance in human disease states.

To overcome these limitations, we implemented a discovery-driven, mass spectrometry-based plasma proteomic approach in a well-phenotyped cohort of HF patients with and without AF. This strategy enabled an unbiased interrogation of the circulating proteome beyond predefined biomarker panels, thereby capturing a broader spectrum of disease-associated proteins. To facilitate cross-study comparison, we systematically queried previously reported AF-HF biomarkers within our LC-MS/MS dataset and summarized their detectability, quantitative completeness, and differential abundance. While several proteins identified by Santema et al. [25], including SPON1, IGFBP1, and IGFBP7, were readily detectable, most did not retain statistical significance after correction for multiple testing, despite nominal trends toward higher levels of IGFBP1 and IGFBP7 in HF patients with AF (Additional file 1: Table S7). Notably, our findings were concordant with prior reports implicating IGFBP1 and SPP1 in AF-related fibrotic remodeling [24, 25, 27]. In contrast, proteins corresponding to transcriptomic signatures reported by Zhang et al. [26] were detectable in plasma but did not exhibit significant protein-level differences after false discovery rate correction. This discrepancy underscores a broader methodological consideration: transcriptome-derived signals do not necessarily translate into measurable changes in the circulating proteome, particularly in discovery LC-MS/MS workflows characterized by wide dynamic range, missingness, and potential co-depletion effects during high-abundance protein removal. Our present observations emphasize the complementary value of proteomic profiling [28–30] in capturing post-transcriptional regulation and protein-level dynamics that are directly relevant to biomarker development and clinical translation.

AHNAK is a large multifunctional scaffolding protein (approximately 700 kDa) that is widely expressed across tissues, including the myocardium [31, 32]. Accumulating evidence indicates that AHNAK functions as a multifunctional adaptor involved in membrane-associated signaling, cytoskeletal organization, and calcium homeostasis, processes central to cardiac excitation-contraction coupling [33, 34]. In cardiomyocytes, AHNAK directly interacts with the β2-subunit of L-type voltage-gated calcium channels, thereby modulating calcium influx and electrical activity [35]. Genetic and biochemical studies have demonstrated that deletion or functional disruption of AHNAK alters L-type calcium channel properties under both basal and stimulated conditions, highlighting its role in cardiac calcium handling [33–36]. Moreover, AHNAK has been shown to regulate calcium mobilization through natriuretic peptide receptor-C, a signaling axis previously implicated in atrial fibrosis and AF susceptibility [36, 37].

Beyond calcium signaling, emerging evidence suggests that AHNAK may also participate in fibrotic regulation [38]. In oncologic models, AHNAK suppresses epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastatic progression through modulation of the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling axis [38]. Notably, AHNAK2, a paralog of AHNAK, has been shown to attenuate experimental pulmonary fibrosis by downregulating TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling [39], suggesting a conserved role of the AHNAK protein family in profibrotic pathways. Collectively, these studies provide a mechanistic backdrop for our observations and support the concept that AHNAK may integrate calcium handling and fibrotic signaling, thereby linking structural remodeling to arrhythmogenic susceptibility in HF-associated AF.

TGF-β1 is a master regulator of fibrosis across organ systems, driving myofibroblast differentiation and extracellular matrix accumulation through Smad-dependent mechanisms [40–42]. Mechanistically, AHNAK may influence fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling by modulating TGF-β-dependent profibrotic programs, including CTGF, fibronectin, and collagen synthesis. Under basal conditions, silencing AHNAK induced a broad profibrotic shift in atrial fibroblasts, characterized by upregulation of CTGF, fibronectin, and COL1α at both transcript and protein levels. In contrast, under a strong 24-hour TGF-β1 stimulus, the incremental effect of AHNAK reduction on CTGF and COL1α expression was diminished, whereas fibronectin remained responsive at the transcript level. This pattern suggests context- and time-dependent modulation, potentially reflecting a ceiling effect of TGF-β1-driven induction. Given that fibronectin is a secreted extracellular matrix protein, altered extracellular deposition and assembly may further contribute to the observed transcript-protein divergence. In vivo, AHNAK heterozygous mice subjected to pressure overload exhibited exaggerated atrial fibrosis and increased expression of fibrosis-related genes, thereby providing integrated cellular and histological validation of AHNAK’s antifibrotic role in the atria.

In the MR analysis, genetically proxied higher AHNAK expression was associated with a reduced risk of AF, with consistent results across sensitivity analyses and no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Reverse MR analyses did not support an effect of AF liability on AHNAK expression, reinforcing directionality from AHNAK to AF. Nevertheless, MR provides statistical evidence supporting causal inference under instrumental-variable assumptions rather than direct biological proof. Moreover, heart-specific expression quantitative trait loci for AHNAK were not identifiable in currently available datasets, including GTEx v8 left atrial appendage and left ventricle, precluding tissue-specific MR analyses. As with all MR studies, these findings should therefore be interpreted within the methodological assumptions of the approach and viewed as supportive, rather than definitive, evidence of causality.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although machine-learning techniques were employed to prioritize candidate biomarkers, the model was trained and evaluated in a single cohort with a modest sample size (n = 59). Therefore, we did not perform subgroup analyses and any subgroup-level inferences should be interpreted with caution. In addition, cohort-specific factors (e.g., age/sex distribution, HF phenotype, comorbidities, medication use, and disease stage) may introduce dataset bias and affect model generalizability. External validation in larger and independent cohorts is essential to confirm the robustness and generalizability of AHNAK as a discriminative biomarker. Second, differential protein abundance was initially assessed using nominal p-values without correction for multiple testing, reflecting the hypothesis-generating nature of plasma discovery proteomics and its inherent power constraints. This approach may increase the risk of false-positive findings and underscores the importance of external validation. Third, owing to the cross-sectional design of the clinical cohort, the present study supports AHNAK primarily as a discriminative biomarker for prevalent AF in HF rather than as a validated predictor of incident AF. Prospective longitudinal studies are required to determine whether circulating AHNAK can predict incident AF in HF patients. Fourth, primary atrial fibroblasts used for in vitro experiments were derived from young mice, which may not fully recapitulate the phenotypic and epigenetic characteristics of fibroblasts in aged human atria, where senescence-associated changes may influence fibrotic responses. Finally, MR analyses relied on whole-blood eQTL data, which provides statistical support for causal inference under instrumental variable assumptions but may limit tissue specificity and the precision of causal interpretation for cardiac phenotypes. Future work incorporating tissue specificity instruments will help refine the causal understanding of AHNAK in AF and other cardiac diseases.

Clinical implications and future directions

The identification of AHNAK as a diagnostically informative biomarker offers several potential clinical applications. From a therapeutic perspective, modulation of AHNAK or its downstream signaling pathways may represent a potential antifibrotic strategy in AF. Given the large molecular size and predominantly intracellular localization of AHNAK, direct protein replacement or antibody-based targeting is unlikely to be feasible. Future translational efforts may therefore focus on enhancing endogenous AHNAK expression or activity in atrial fibroblasts, or on therapeutically targeting profibrotic signaling cascades that become unmasked in the setting of AHNAK deficiency. This approach could involve targeting fibrosis-related pathways downstream of AHNAK, particularly TGF-β signaling, which is implicated in atrial fibrosis and AF. Moreover, therapies designed to increase AHNAK expression or enhance its activity in atrial fibroblasts could provide a more feasible and effective strategy for managing fibrosis in HF patients with AF. Future functional studies should incorporate aging models, using aged mice or human atrial fibroblasts, to better reflect the biological and fibrotic characteristics observed in elderly HF patients. This will enhance the translational relevance of AHNAK as a therapeutic target. In parallel, AHNAK-based plasma stratification approaches may be leveraged to enrich clinical trials with HF patients at increased risk of AF, thereby improving trial efficiency and therapeutic signal detection.

Conclusions

This integrative proteomic, experimental, and genetic investigation identifies AHNAK as a biologically plausible and clinically relevant discriminative biomarker of AF in patients with HF. Circulating AHNAK may provide insight into antifibrotic treatment responses, supporting its relevance as a translational biomarker rather than an immediate direct drug target. External validation in independent, larger, and prospective HF cohorts will be essential to establish its role in AF risk stratification and early detection.
