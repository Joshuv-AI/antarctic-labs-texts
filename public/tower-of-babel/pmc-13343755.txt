Effectiveness of a digital communication and active recall strategy to improve pediatric influenza vaccination in primary care: a prospective observational study
Authors: Antonio Di Mauro, Ilaria Farella, Mariano Manzionna, Letizia Alessia Rizzo, Federica Di Mauro, Chiara Greco, Paolo Petrone, Maria Elisabetta Baldassarre, Silvio Tafuri, Nicola Laforgia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfluenza remains a significant health problem in early childhood, with vaccination uptake still below optimal levels. Background

Seasonal influenza represents a major cause of morbidity among children, particularly those under six years of age, who are more susceptible to infection and also serve as significant vectors of viral transmission within households and communities [1]. Annual vaccination is the best way to prevent influenza and is recommended for all children over six months [2].

Despite these recommendations, influenza vaccination coverage among pediatric populations remains suboptimal in many countries, and falls below the thresholds necessary to achieve substantial population-level protection [3]. National data across multiple influenza seasons show that over the past decade pediatric influenza vaccination coverage in Italy has remained consistently very low, often well below 20%, with marked regional disparities [4, 5]. Several barriers contribute to the low vaccination rates in this age group, including parental hesitancy, lack of awareness, misperceptions about the severity of influenza, and potential difficulties in accessing vaccination services [6]. Traditional approaches to communication and recall have shown limited effectiveness in addressing these issues, especially in settings where primary care providers manage large patient panels with limited resources [7]. In recent years, digital tools have emerged as promising means to enhance vaccine communication and acceptance. Social media, mobile health applications, and automated reminder systems offer new opportunities to reach families with timely, tailored information and to facilitate appointment scheduling and follow-up [8, 9]. Evidence suggests that when digital strategies are trusted, well-integrated, and directly linked to pediatric care providers, they can significantly improve parental confidence and vaccination adherence [10]. Building on recent insights into the transformative role of social media and digital tools in pediatric health communication [11], the present study investigated the effectiveness of a multicomponent digital intervention in a primary care context, to enhance influenza vaccination adherence among families of children aged 6 months to 6 years.

Methods

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a communication combined intervention in promoting influenza vaccination among families of children aged 6 months to 6 years. Parental perceptions and acceptance of the intervention were also assessed.

Secondary aims include comparing the incidence of ILI and confirmed influenza cases in 2024–2025 between vaccinated and unvaccinated children to estimate the vaccine’s clinical and virological effectiveness. The study also compares flu-positive swabs to estimate vaccine effectiveness against confirmed infections. A further secondary objective is to conduct a subgroup analysis among vaccinated children aged 2 to 6 years, comparing those who received the intranasal live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) with those who received the inactivated intramuscular vaccine.

Study design and population

This prospective observational study was conducted from October 2024 to March 2025 in a pediatric primary care practice within the Italian National Health Service, Local Health Unit, Bari (LHU BA). Children aged 6 months to 6 years at the start of the 2024–2025 influenza vaccination campaign were eligible for inclusion. Eligibility required registration with the participating pediatric primary care practice and availability for follow-up during the study period. Exclusion criteria included age under 6 months and documented contraindications to influenza vaccination, such as a history of severe allergic reaction to a previous vaccine dose or to vaccine components. For children receiving the LAIV, additional exclusions were applied according to national recommendations, including clinical immunodeficiency, high-dose immunosuppressive therapy, severe asthma or active wheezing, and ongoing salicylate therapy [12]. For comparative analyses, vaccination coverage for the 2024–2025 season was extracted from the regional epidemiological database (Edotto/GIAVA) by an authorized expert user of LHU BA. Vaccination uptake in the study cohort was then compared with vaccination coverage observed at the local (LHU BA) and regional (Apulia Region) levels. To ensure independence between groups, all patients followed at the pediatric primary care practice under study were excluded from the local and regional datasets prior to analysis.

Digital communication and reminder intervention

The intervention consisted of a twofold digital strategy to promote vaccine uptake: a social media awareness campaign and a digital reminder service. The intervention involved the dissemination of digital informational materials, including social media cards, infographics, and educational content, specifically designed to be easily understandable and widely shareable. The communication strategy was informed by evidence-based health communication approaches aimed at improving vaccine confidence and addressing parental hesitancy [9, 13–15], following a structured and proactive seasonal plan. The materials focused specifically on vaccine safety and effectiveness, the risks of influenza infection in children, the benefits of immunization for vulnerable groups, and the concept of herd immunity. Content was developed using clear, non-technical language while maintaining scientific accuracy. These materials were regularly published on the official social media channels of the pediatric practice (Facebook). On these platforms, science-based content is routinely shared to provide accurate and up-to-date information on pediatric and public health topics, including vaccination, using clear and accessible language.

In addition to informative posts, the intervention included interactive digital engagement through live social media sessions and online group counseling. During live broadcasts, parents’ questions and concerns about influenza vaccination were addressed in real time. The recall system followed a structured seasonal schedule. At the beginning of the vaccination campaign, families of eligible children were proactively contacted to inform them about vaccine availability and encourage appointment booking.

Personalized vaccination reminders were sent via the Pediatotem application—already in use within the pediatric practice for scheduling and follow-up visits—or through direct phone calls from the medical staff. Subsequent reminder contacts were conducted at three-week intervals throughout the influenza season. This periodic outreach continued until all available vaccine doses were administered.

Data collection

Data on vaccine administration (including route and dose completeness), episodes of influenza-like illness (ILI), and laboratory-confirmed influenza were prospectively collected during the 2024–2025 influenza season (October 2024–March 2025), corresponding to the period of active influenza circulation in the study area. Vaccination status was verified through the regional immunization registry (GIAVA), while ILI cases were reported by the participating pediatrician within the national sentinel surveillance network RespivirNet, coordinated by the Istituto Superiore di Sanità.

Nasopharyngeal swabs were obtained from children presenting with ILI symptoms lasting more than three days, based on clinical judgment, and tested using a rapid antigen assay (JusChek Influenza A + B Antigen Combo Rapid Test, Hangzhou AllTest Biotech Co., Ltd., China), a qualitative immunochromatographic test for the detection of influenza A and B nucleoprotein antigens [16]. Children who tested positive for influenza were classified as cases, while those with a negative test result were considered controls, according to a test-negative design approach used for vaccine effectiveness estimation. Results were interpreted visually after 10 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reported sensitivity and specificity compared with real-time RT-PCR were 95.77% and 99.59% for influenza A and 94.12% and 99.41% for influenza B; in lay-user validation studies, sensitivity and specificity were 92.98% and 98.99% for influenza A and 92.06% and 98.36% for influenza B.

ILI was defined according to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control criteria as the sudden onset of at least one systemic symptom (fever > 37.5 °C, malaise/fatigue, headache, or myalgia) and at least one respiratory symptom (cough, sore throat, or shortness of breath) [17]. In children too young to report systemic symptoms, irritability, persistent crying, or loss of appetite were considered indicative of ILI. Cases with alternative diagnoses (e.g., streptococcal pharyngotonsillitis, acute otitis media, pneumonia, acute gastroenteritis, or urinary tract infection) were excluded, whereas episodes associated with laryngotracheitis, bronchitis, or conjunctivitis accompanied by fever were included.

During the 2024–2025 vaccination campaign, the Apulia Region supplied both the intranasal live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) and the inactivated intramuscular vaccine (Vaxigrip). Vaccination coverage data from the local health authority (LHU BA) and the regional surveillance systems (Edotto/GIAVA) were used to evaluate outcomes and assess the impact of the intervention.

Assessment of parental satisfaction

To evaluate the efficacy and acceptability of the vaccination counseling strategy, a structured satisfaction questionnaire was administered digitally and anonymously to parents and caregivers. The questionnaire consisted of four multiple-choice questions in a structured multiple-choice questionnaire with ordinal response categories, designed to gather feedback on key components of the intervention.

Participants were invited to share their opinions on the usefulness of automated reminders and active recall phone calls, both aimed at facilitating vaccination scheduling and follow-up. The survey also explored perceptions regarding the clarity and relevance of the digital health information shared through the Pediatotem app and the practice’s official social media channels.

Parents also evaluated the educational initiatives (live sessions, webinars, in-person courses) and the organization of vaccination services during the campaign. Each item provided predefined response options to facilitate descriptive analysis, reflecting varying levels of perceived usefulness and satisfaction. The questionnaire was distributed through digital platforms to all families registered with the practice, and responses were collected anonymously.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test for independent samples, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Vaccination coverage and ILI were compared between vaccinated and unvaccinated children. Relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to estimate the association between vaccination and the occurrence of ILI. For the analysis of laboratory-confirmed influenza within the test-negative design, odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI were estimated using logistic regression. The proportion of laboratory-confirmed influenza among ILI episodes was compared between groups. Vaccine effectiveness (VE) against virologically confirmed influenza was estimated within the test-negative design framework and calculated as VE = (1 − OR) × 100, where the OR was obtained from logistic regression models. A subgroup analysis among vaccinated children aged 2–6 years compared the intranasal LAIV with the inactivated intramuscular vaccine. Differences in ILI incidence and laboratory-confirmed influenza were evaluated using logistic regression models adjusted for age and relevant covariates. Parental satisfaction with each formulation was described using descriptive statistics. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Vaccination uptake

Out 792 eligible children for seasonal influenza vaccination, 375 (47.4%; 95%CI: 43.9–50.8%) received at least one vaccine dose, while 417 (52.6%) remained unvaccinated. Of those vaccinated, 358 children (95.5%) completed the full vaccination schedule appropriate to their age and prior immunization status. The remaining 17 children (4.5%) received only a single dose due to temporary vaccine unavailability. In terms of vaccine formulation, 281 children (74.9%) were administered the inactivated intramuscular influenza vaccine; of these, 17 did not complete the full course. The remaining 94 children (25.1%) received the live-attenuated intranasal vaccine, and all completed the recommended schedule. At baseline, mean age was 3.1 ± 2.0 years, with no significant difference between fully vaccinated and unvaccinated children (3.2 ± 2.0 vs. 3.0 ± 2.1 years; p = 0.148). Overall, 55.1% were male, with a comparable sex distribution (53.1% vs. 56.9%; p = 0.329).

In the LHU BA, 17,884 were vaccinated out of 53,950 eligible children (33.1%; 95%CI: 32.8–33.5%). In Puglia Region, 49,704 were vaccinated out of 167,374 eligible children (29.7%; 95%CI: 29.5–29.9%). The vaccination rate of our cohort was significantly higher than LHU BA and Puglia Region (both p < 0.001).

A total of 228 parents and caregivers (28.7%) completed the satisfaction questionnaire.

Incidence of uncomplicated ILI

Of the 792 children initially enrolled, 779 completed the follow-up period; 13 were excluded due to a change in the referring pediatrician. During the surveillance period, 625 medical visits for symptoms potentially consistent with ILI were recorded. After excluding cases with alternative diagnoses or complications, 259 uncomplicated ILI episodes met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis.

Among the 358 fully vaccinated children, 94 experienced at least one ILI episode (26.3%), compared with 160 cases among the 404 unvaccinated children (39.6%). An additional 5 cases occurred among the 17 partially vaccinated children. The difference in ILI incidence between fully vaccinated and unvaccinated children was statistically significant (χ² = 15.1, p < 0.001).

The risk of ILI was lower among fully vaccinated than unvaccinated children (26.3% vs. 39.6%; RR 0.66), corresponding to a 34% relative risk reduction.

Influenza testing and confirmed cases

Rapid influenza antigen testing was performed in children presenting with ILI symptoms lasting more than three days. A total of 169 tests were performed: 75 in vaccinated children, 89 in unvaccinated, and 5 in partially vaccinated children.

Influenza was confirmed in 7 vaccinated children (1 vaccinated with the intranasal formulation and 6 with the intramuscular one), in 41 unvaccinated children, and in 4 partially vaccinated children.

The proportion of positive tests differed significantly between groups: 9.3% (7/75) among vaccinated, 46.1% (41/89) among unvaccinated, and 80% (4/5) among partially vaccinated children (χ² = 31.65, p < 0.001).

Estimated vaccine effectiveness

In the tested subgroup, influenza positivity was lower in fully vaccinated than unvaccinated children (9.3% vs. 46.1%). Comparison between intramuscular and intranasal vaccines

A secondary analysis was conducted among children aged 2 to 6 years. Among the 403 children in this subgroup, 85 received the intranasal vaccine and 112 received the intramuscular formulation.

A total of 39 ILI episodes were recorded: 25 in the intramuscular group and 14 in the intranasal group. Although a lower number of ILI cases was observed in the intranasal group, the difference was not statistically significant (χ² = 1.05, p = 0.30).

Discussion

This study suggests that an integrated digital strategy may effectively support seasonal influenza vaccination adherence in a pediatric primary care setting. By combining educational content disseminated via social media and mobile health tools with an active recall system, this approach was associated with satisfactory vaccination coverage and high levels of parental satisfaction in our setting. Unlike standard regional methods based on mass communication, our strategy used proactive digital tools and active recall—currently absent in LHU BA and Apulia—to support pediatric flu vaccination. The vaccination uptake observed in our cohort was notably higher than both the local and regional averages for the same age group during the 2024–2025 campaign.

Our findings align with previous evidence showing that reminder and recall interventions can improve influenza vaccination uptake in pediatric populations. Randomized trials conducted in primary care and integrated health systems have demonstrated that text-message reminders and electronic messaging increase timely influenza vaccination when embedded into routine workflows and designed to address missed opportunities [18]. However, most of these studies evaluated single-modality interventions within controlled trial settings. In contrast, our study adopted a prospective real-world observational design and implemented a multicomponent strategy that combined pediatrician-led digital education—delivered through trusted social media channels and a dedicated mobile application—with active recall and facilitated scheduling.

Evidence regarding patient portal–based reminders has been heterogeneous, with limited impact on first-dose uptake but greater effectiveness for subsequent doses, suggesting that both message modality and the vaccination outcome assessed (initiation versus completion) influence results [19]. Our integrated approach may therefore have achieved higher coverage by addressing not only logistical barriers through reminders but also informational and relational determinants of vaccine hesitancy through physician-led digital engagement [13–15].

In the Italian context, where influenza vaccination delivery and communication strategies vary across regions and systematic reminder systems are not uniformly implemented, multilevel approaches integrating communication, service organization, and accessible delivery models have been advocated [20]. Within this framework, our experience provides practice-level real-world evidence that combining digital education with active recall is feasible in routine pediatric care and may contribute to higher vaccination coverage compared with local and regional benchmarks.

Consistent with existing literature on automated outreach systems [21] more than 80% of participating parents perceived the digital reminder and active recall strategy as useful. The integration of the Pediatotem app with individualized counseling preserved relational continuity while optimizing scheduling efficiency, a balance that appears particularly relevant given the behavioral and emotional factors influencing parental vaccination decisions [22].

Health information disseminated through the Pediatotem platform and the practice’s social media.

channels was also highly appreciated, with over 90% of respondents describing the content as useful or very useful. This aligns with research on digital health literacy, which highlights that parents increasingly rely on online sources for vaccine-related information—especially when such content is endorsed by a familiar and trusted [23]. The consistency and transparency of communication on the used social pages likely contributed to this trust, reinforcing the role of digital continuity of care in building vaccine confidence [24, 25].

Educational initiatives promoted by the practice—including webinars and in-person courses—were also well received. Although participation was not universal, 50% of respondents rated these offerings as very useful, and an additional segment expressed interest despite not having attended.

This suggests potential for expanding structured educational opportunities in future campaigns, particularly to engage undecided or marginally hesitant parents.

Importantly, the clinical outcomes observed during the influenza season further support the efficacy of this approach. The RR of ILI was 0.66, corresponding to a vaccine effectiveness of 34.0%. Children who received a full vaccination schedule experienced significantly fewer ILI episodes (26.3%) compared to unvaccinated peers (39.6%), confirming the protective value of vaccination in daily pediatric practice. Additionally, fewer ILI cases imply reduced sick visits, lower diagnostic costs, and more time for preventive care—contributing to better clinical workload management during the respiratory virus season. The reduced rate of laboratory-confirmed influenza among vaccinated children (9.3% vs. 46.1% in unvaccinated peers) and the high estimated vaccine effectiveness (85%) confirm the biological effectiveness of influenza vaccination within this intervention context. However, the added value of this study lies in the contextual framing of these outcomes within a digitally mediated, community-based vaccination strategy consistent with the biological effectiveness of influenza vaccination within this intervention context.

The comparison between the LAIV and the inactivated intramuscular formulation should be considered exploratory. The relatively small number of children in the intranasal group limited the statistical power of the analysis, and no significant differences in ILI incidence were observed between the two vaccine types. Although a numerical trend toward fewer ILI episodes was noted in the intranasal group, the study was not adequately powered to detect meaningful differences in comparative effectiveness. Larger, adequately powered studies are required to clarify potential differences in clinical outcomes and acceptability between the two formulations. This study has several limitations. Its observational design and the absence of a control group limit the ability to draw causal conclusions regarding the impact of the digital intervention on vaccination uptake and clinical outcomes. The intervention was implemented within a single pediatric primary care practice, which may introduce contextual and organizational factors that influenced parental engagement and vaccination behavior. In particular, the pediatrician’s established digital presence and ongoing communication with families may have enhanced trust and responsiveness to the campaign, limiting the generalizability of the findings to settings with different levels of digital integration or physician-led communication. In addition, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Factors such as parental attitudes toward vaccination, baseline health-seeking behaviors, socioeconomic characteristics, prior adherence to vaccination schedules, or concurrent public health messaging may have contributed to the observed differences between vaccinated and unvaccinated children. Although comparisons were conducted within the same practice population, the lack of randomization and the inability to adjust for all potential confounders prevent definitive attribution of the observed effects to the intervention itself. Moreover, socio-demographic variables were not systematically collected as part of the study protocol, which limited the possibility of exploring the influence of factors such as parental education level, socioeconomic status, or cultural background on vaccination uptake and outcomes. Furthermore We were unable to apply a standardized 14-day post-vaccination immunity window due to the lack of precise temporal data on vaccination and symptom onset. Therefore, early cases occurring shortly after vaccination cannot be completely excluded, which may have led to a conservative estimate of vaccine effectiveness. The voluntary nature of participation and the 28.7% response rate to the parental satisfaction survey also raise the possibility of selection bias, as families more engaged with digital tools or more favorable toward vaccination may have been more likely to respond. Vaccine effectiveness estimates were based on rapid antigen testing, which is less sensitive than molecular methods such as real-time PCR [16]. This may have led to misclassification of some influenza cases and potentially biased effectiveness estimates. Antigen test performance may also vary according to timing of testing and infection prevalence, which should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that an integrated digital strategy, combining online information and personalized reminders, effectively boosts influenza vaccination rates in pediatric care. Vaccinated children showed fewer flu-like illnesses, and positive parental feedback highlights the feasibility of this approach. This model offers a practical solution for improving vaccine delivery and can be adapted by other primary care settings to integrate preventive care strategies.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was a non-interventional prospective observational assessment conducted within routine pediatric primary care. No experimental procedures, investigational products, or additional diagnostic tests were performed for research purposes, and clinical pathways were not altered. According to current Italian regulations on observational studies in healthcare settings, non-interventional research that analyzes routinely collected clinical information and voluntary, non-sensitive questionnaires does not require formal approval by a Research Ethics Committee.

All pediatric clinical data were handled in compliance with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 2016/679). Only aggregated results are reported in this manuscript, and no identifiable personal information was analyzed for research purposes.

The parental satisfaction questionnaire consisted of non-sensitive items and was voluntary and anonymous; completion and return of the questionnaire was interpreted as implied consent to participate. Parents and legal guardians were informed as part of standard care and were free to accept or decline vaccination.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
