Superior patient‐reported clinical outcomes after patient‐individual total knee arthroplasty compared with off‐the‐shelf robotic‐assisted total knee arthroplasty in valgus knee phenotypes
Authors: Lars‐Rene Tuecking, Peter Savov, Mara Hold, Ricarda Stauss, Henning Windhagen, Max Ettinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare early patient‐reported clinical outcomes of patient‐individual total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with individualized trochlear design versus robotic‐assisted TKA using off‐the‐shelf implants in patients with valgus knee phenotypes and pronounced femoral valgus (lateral distal femoral angle [LDFA] < 85°) at 3 and 12 months postoperatively in a prospective matched case–control study.MethodsThis prospective matched case–control study based on predefined anatomical criteria included 42 patients who underwent primary TKA between October 2021 and October 2023. Twenty‐one patients received patient‐individual posterior‐stabilized implants. Twenty‐one matched controls underwent robotic‐assisted TKA with off‐the‐shelf implants. All procedures followed restricted kinematic alignment (KA) principles with valgus undercorrection. Primary outcomes were patient‐reported scores (Forgotten Joint Score [FJS], Kujala Score, Oxford Knee Score [OKS] and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index [WOMAC]), assessed preoperatively and at 3 and 12 months.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated comparable preoperative clinical status and comparable degrees of preoperative valgus deformity, with no significant differences in hip–knee–ankle angle (HKA, 185.6 ± 6.3° vs. 188.8 ± 4.1°; p = 0.060) or mechanical LDFA (84.0 ± 0.9° vs. 83.7 ± 1.2°; p = 0.261). At 3 and 12 months, patient‐individual total knee arthroplasty (pTKA) demonstrated significantly better scores across all patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) (p < 0.05). The largest difference was observed in the Kujala score at 12 months (79.5 ± 12.8 vs. 59.7 ± 19.0, p = 0.001). FJS improved more rapidly in the pTKA group (from 9.0 to 49.1 at 3 months, and 52.5 at 12 months) compared to robotic‐assisted total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) (26.9 and 36.0, respectively). One revision for suspected infection occurred in the pTKA cohort; none were reported after rTKA.ConclusionsPatient‐individual TKA with individualized trochlear design achieved superior early functional outcomes compared to robotic‐assisted TKA with standard implants in severe valgus morphotypes. Individualized trochlear orientation may improve patellofemoral mechanics and facilitate faster recovery in patients with severe femoral valgus.Level of EvidenceLevel III.Keywords: CPAK, patient‐individual, robotic‐assisted, total knee arthroplasty, valgus

INTRODUCTION

To address a remaining dissatisfaction rate of 10%–20% in primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [4], surgical strategies have increasingly focused on improving functional outcomes by better restoring native knee anatomy and joint mechanics [14]. More advanced methods have introduced more refined alignment philosophies that attempt to reconstruct the patient's native alignment and joint line orientation [16]. However, even personalized alignment strategies using off‐the‐shelf implants might not always show consistent results across all anatomical phenotypes [7, 19].

Particularly, valgus knee morphotypes such as Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) Types III and VI have reported inferior outcomes following personalized alignment philosophies [7]. These patients typically have increased lateral distal femoral angles (LDFAs), necessitating valgus orientation of the femoral component in these alignment techniques. This alignment may produce a medially displaced trochlear groove compared with the quadriceps vector, which might lead to impaired patellar tracking or lateral overcompression—both of which can potentially play a role in leading to inferior clinical results [8]. One potential disadvantage of off‐the‐shelf standard implants is their fixed trochlear orientation, especially in the case of high femoral valgus [11]. Patient‐individual implants with personalized trochlear design offer a potential alternative by more closely approximating native anatomy, trochlear orientation and potentially evading patellofemoral complications [2, 12].

The purpose of this prospective comparative study was to evaluate whether patient‐individual TKA with an individualized trochlear design results in superior patient‐reported clinical outcomes compared with robotic‐assisted TKA using off‐the‐shelf implants in patients with valgus morphotypes and high femoral valgus (LDFA < 85°) at early postoperative state. We hypothesized that patient‐individual implants, by allowing customization of trochlear orientation, would lead to improved patellofemoral‐related outcomes and overall patient outcome scores compared with standard implant designs in this challenging valgus population.

METHODS

In this prospective, comparative cohort study, a total of 42 patients who underwent TKA between October 2021 and October 2023 were included. During this period, a consecutive case series of 21 patients received patient‐individual implants (Origin PS; Symbios, Switzerland), selected according to predefined inclusion criteria. All patients presented with valgus morphotypes corresponding to CPAK Types III or VI. Additional inclusion criteria included a LDFA of less than 85°, and a constitutional valgus leg alignment, defined by an arithmetic hip–knee–ankle angle (aHKA) greater than 5° (Table 1). A partial correction within the physical examination of the valgus deformity was required in all cases.

The control group consisted of the first 21 consecutive patients identified from the institutional prospective knee arthroplasty registry who received robotic‐assisted off‐the‐shelf implants (Journey II BCS; Smith & Nephew, USA) during the same study period. Matching was based exclusively on predefined anatomical and radiographic criteria, including CPAK Type (III/VI), LDFA < 85°, constitutional valgus alignment (aHKA > 5°) and partial reducibility of the valgus deformity. Treatment allocation was not randomized. During a defined implementation interval of patient‐individual TKA at our institution, all consecutive patients meeting the predefined anatomical inclusion criteria received the individualized implant. Outside this defined interval, patients fulfilling identical criteria were treated with robotic‐assisted off‐the‐shelf TKA according to standard institutional practice. Allocation was therefore based on institutional treatment pathways and implant availability rather than patient‐specific prognostic factors. To further minimize variability, all procedures in both groups were performed by a single, experienced surgeon. Perioperative management did not differ in between groups, the same perioperative management protocol was used for all patients included in this study.

Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (Reference No.: 8808_BO_K_2019), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study inclusion.

All procedures were performed using a valgus undercorrection strategy consistent with the principles of restricted kinematic alignment (rKA) without use of patellar resurfacing. In the patient‐individual implant group, patient‐specific instrumentation (PSI) based on preoperative computed tomography (CT) was used. In the control group, off‐the‐shelf implants were implanted using an imageless robotic‐assisted system (CORI, Smith&Nephew, USA). Both techniques aimed to achieve undercorrection of valgus alignment, targeting a residual mechanical axis of ≤3° valgus and a maximum component inclination of 5° for both femoral and tibial cuts.

In robotic‐assisted procedures, additional efforts were made to reconstruct the native trochlear orientation within the permitted alignment boundaries. For patient‐individual implants, the trochlear groove was individually planned to match the patient's anatomy, allowing for decoupling of the tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints. In cases of lateral compartment stiffness, soft‐tissue balancing was performed in a stepwise manner following established protocols as previously described [18].

The primary outcome was clinical performance assessed via validated patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS), Kujala Score, Oxford Knee Score (OKS) and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC). PROMs were recorded preoperatively and at two postoperative time points: 3 and 12 months. Additionally, revision events, including time point and reason for revision, were documented at final follow‐up.

Standardized, full‐length, weight‐bearing anteroposterior radiographs of the lower extremity were obtained preoperatively and postoperatively in all patients. Radiographic measurements included the HKA, aHKA, mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA), mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA) and joint line obliquity (JLO). All measurements were performed according to established definitions using calibrated digital imaging software (Carestream; Carestream Health Inc., USA).

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software with the EZR (Easy R) plugin. Demographic variables and baseline PROM scores were compared prior to outcome analysis to assess initial group comparability. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Between‐group comparisons were performed using unpaired, nonparametric t tests and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for nominal data. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Between‐group effect sizes were calculated as Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals based on group means and standard deviations. Post hoc (observed) power was estimated using a two‐sided α = 0.05 for independent samples; effect sizes and confidence intervals were considered the primary measures of clinical and statistical relevance.

Demographic characteristics showed good comparability between the patient‐individual and the robotic‐assisted control group (Table 2). Radiographic analysis confirmed similar valgus alignment patterns across groups, with no significant differences in LDFA and aHKA (Table 2). Preoperative PROMs were comparable between both cohorts (Table 3). Postoperatively, radiological outcome parameters were comparable between both groups, except for differences for postoperative HKA (Table 2).

Patient‐reported outcomes

At both 3‐ and 12‐month follow‐up, patient‐individual total knee arthroplasty (pTKA) reported significantly better patient‐reported outcomes compared to robotic‐assisted TKA (rTKA) as shown in Table 3. The pTKA group performed superior in Kujal Score (Figure 1), FJS (Figure 2), WOMAC Score and OKS at all postoperative time points (p < 0.05 for all scores). The most pronounced difference was observed in the Kujala Score at 12 months, with pTKA patients achieving a mean score of 79.5 ± 12.8 compared to 59.7 ± 19.0 in the rTKA group (p = 0.001), indicating enhanced functional recovery and patellofemoral joint‐related satisfaction in the patient‐individual cohort.

Inter‐group comparisons revealed both groups to have postoperative improvement over short‐term follow‐up. The pTKA group exhibited a rapid early improvement across all PROMs, particularly in the first 3 months. Notably, the FJS increased from 9.0 ± 7.6 preoperatively to 49.1 ± 24.8 at 3 months and further to 52.5 ± 21.1 at 12 months. In contrast, the rTKA group showed a more gradual increase, reaching only 26.9 ± 21.5 at 3 months and 36.0 ± 23.0 at 12 months.

At 12 months, patient‐individual TKA showed a clinically relevant superiority over the reference implant across multiple PROMs. In addition to the Kujala score (mean difference +19.8 points; 95% CI +9.7 to +29.9; Hedges' g = 1.20), for which the post hoc (observed) power was high (~0.97), meaningful benefits were observed for the OKS (mean difference +6.3; 95% CI +1.6 to +11.0; g = 0.83) and the FJS (mean difference +16.5; 95% CI +2.7 to +30.3), both indicating moderate‐to‐large standardized effects. Furthermore, WOMAC pain scores were substantially lower in the patient‐individual TKA group (mean difference −17.3; 95% CI −25.7 to −8.9; g = −1.28). Collectively, these effect magnitudes and confidence intervals exceed established thresholds for clinical importance, underscoring a consistent and patient‐perceived benefit rather than isolated statistical significance.

Within the 12‐month follow‐up period, one revision procedure was required in the pTKA group due to suspected periprosthetic joint infection, managed by isolated polyethylene insert exchange. No revision surgeries occurred in the rTKA cohort.

DISCUSSION

The main finding of this study indicates that pTKA utilizing an individualized trochlear design resulted in superior early functional and self‐reported clinical outcome compared to rTKA with standard off‐the‐shelf implants in patients with valgus morphotypes (CPAK Type III and VI) and high femoral valgus using a rKA principle. Particularly, patellofemoral joint function scores were markedly improved during the early postoperative phase in pTKA, which may be attributed to the individualized orientation of the prosthetic trochlea in these cases.

Analogous to the improved clinical outcomes observed with KA compared to mechanical alignment (MA) in varus morphotypes [5], superior results have also been demonstrated for KA in valgus knee anatomies when compared to MA TKA [1]. KA is observed to enhance joint biomechanics by increasing stability through preserved medial collateral ligament isometry [20], avoiding increased constraint levels [1, 18] and improving patellar tracking and patella loading [10, 15]. Despite possible advantages of KA, limitations arise when using standard off‐the‐shelf femoral components, which are not optimized for valgus component alignment. Maintaining severe femoral valgus orientation can lead to medialization of the prosthetic trochlear groove to the quadriceps vector in off‐the‐shelf implants due to a fixed trochlear orientation in those femoral components [8]. This mismatch contributes to lateral patellar overstuffing and impaired patellar tracking, both of which are associated with reduced patient satisfaction and delayed early recovery [7].

Independent of the alignment strategy used, there is substantial variation in distal femoral anatomy between individuals, including marked differences in trochlear orientation relative to the distal femoral joint line and the posterior condylar axis [6]. Large CT‐based studies have shown that the shape and orientation of the native trochlea vary widely among patients and are often not aligned with the standardized reference axes used in conventional implant designs [13]. Several studies have therefore described a mismatch between native trochlear orientation and the fixed trochlear geometry of off‐the‐shelf femoral components, regardless of the alignment philosophy applied [17]. Such a mismatch may negatively affect patellofemoral mechanics, particularly when standard implants are used in anatomically complex knees. The use of individualized trochlear orientation in the pTKA femoral components likely addressed these issues in patients with valgus phenotypes with pronounced femoral valgus, which may explain the significantly higher Kujala scores observed in this study. These findings are consistent with prior reports that emphasize the importance of restoring native trochlear anatomy to optimize clinical outcomes [9].

In addition to superior absolute outcomes, the pTKA group exhibited a more rapid improvement in PROMs during the early postoperative period. This was most notable in the FJS, which increased from 9.0 preoperatively to 49.1 at 3 months, compared to a more modest increase in the rTKA group. This pattern suggests that personalized implant geometry may facilitate a faster recovery with reduced joint awareness, possibly due to reduced soft‐tissue irritation or better restoration of native joint mechanics. Similar early benefits have been described in kinematically aligned or functionally aligned TKA cohorts [21, 22], further supporting the potential role of personalization in enhancing early recovery. These results underscore the potential advantages of patient‐individual implant systems in addressing the complex anatomy of valgus knees, particularly in cases of high femoral valgus (LDFA < 85°) configurations. The ability to decouple tibiofemoral and patellofemoral design—as achieved through patient‐individualized implants—may offer distinct biomechanical and functional benefits in these cases [3]. This supports a tailored approach to TKA planning that extends beyond alignment philosophy alone and incorporates anatomical customization.

Despite its strengths, including the prospective design, strict inclusion criteria and consistent surgical technique, this study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and 12‐month follow‐up preclude conclusions regarding long‐term implant survival and durability. Nevertheless, the consistently moderate‐to‐large effect sizes with corresponding confidence intervals—particularly for patellofemoral‐related outcomes—suggest that the observed differences are unlikely to be explained by random variation alone. Second, the study design inherently combines several factors that may act as potential confounders, including patient‐individual versus off‐the‐shelf implant design and PSI versus robotic assistance. Although all procedures followed predefined rKA principles, minor intraoperative adjustments within the permitted alignment boundaries may have resembled elements of functional adaptation. This represents a potential source of residual confounding. In addition, despite standardized soft‐tissue balancing protocols, subtle differences in intraoperative soft‐tissue management between groups cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, although patients were matched based on predefined valgus phenotype criteria, preoperative and postoperative hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angles were not fully comparable between groups. The patient‐individual TKA group demonstrated slightly more valgus postoperative HKA values and greater deviation from a neutral limb axis, yet achieved superior clinical outcomes. However, preoperative joint laxity and soft‐tissue characteristics—assessed clinically but not quantitatively measured—may have influenced postoperative alignment and early functional recovery. These factors should therefore be considered when interpreting the results. The single‐center design and use of a specific implant platform limit generalizability. Finally, as this study represents a prospective matched case–control design, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded and definitive causal conclusions require confirmation in adequately powered prospective randomized controlled trials.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, patient‐individual TKA appears to offer meaningful improvements in early functional outcomes in valgus morphotype with high femoral valgus compared to robotic‐assisted TKA with off‐the‐shelf implants. These findings support the hypothesis that anatomical customization, particularly of the trochlear groove, may play a critical role in optimizing outcomes in complex knee morphotypes.
