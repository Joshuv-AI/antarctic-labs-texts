Biomechanical and finite element analysis of a novel annular multi-axial locking plate for patellar fracture fixation
Authors: Hanlin Zou, Lin Zhou, Qiang Zhou, Jianwei Yang, Yunchu Sun, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional tension band structures for patellar fractures have multiple complications such as wires lossening and soft tissue irritation. In response to the above problems, a new type of annular multi-axis locking plate has been designed for the treatment of patellar fractures. This study aims to compare the biomechanical performance of two methods for stabilizing patellar fractures.MethodsA three-dimensional finite element model was established to evaluate the biomechanical performance of annular multi-axis locking steel plate fixation for patellar fractures at knee flexion angles of 20, 45, and 90°. Furthermore, four pairs of matched fresh-frozen cadaveric transverse patellar fracture models (AO/OTA 34B1) stabilized with annular multi-axis locking steel plates (AMALP, experimental group) and cable tension band (CTB, control group) were established to compare the biomechanical performance of the two internal fixation methods. Dynamic fatigue testing was performed by moving the knee from 90to 0° under a load of 6 kg until 200 cycles were completed or the internal device failures. Using a testing machine to evaluate the ultimate force required for the failure of each internal device at knee flexion angles of 20, 45, and 90°. The fracture displacement distance and the ultimate load were recorded.ResultsThe outcome of finite element analysis (FEA) demonstrated the max value of Von-Mises stress of the three knee flexion angles simulated were between 45.328 MPa to 109.191 MPa,which is lower than the yield strength value of the plate(351–429 MPa).The displacement value was between 0.1645 and 0.3487 mm, which is acceptable according to clinical criterion(< 2 mm).After 200 cycles of knee flexion fatigue testing, neither group showed any failure of internal fixation. After 100, 150, and 200 cycles of knee flexion fatigue testing, the displacement of the fracture on the anterior surface (0.36 ± 0.05 mm, 0.41 ± 0.04 mm, 0.49 ± 0.08 mm) and joint surface (0.29 ± 0.05 mm, 0.36 ± 0.07 mm, 0.44 ± 0.09 mm) in AMALP were significantly lower than those in the CTB (anterior surface: 0.54 ± 0.09 mm, 0.66 ± 0.11 mm, 0.73 ± 0.09 mm) (joint surface: 0.45 ± 0.06 mm, 0.52 ± 0.06 mm, 0.59 ± 0.08 mm) (P < 0.05).Across all three knee flexion angles (20, 45, and 90°), the AMALP exhibited significantly higher ultimate failure loads (812.40 ± 46.62 N, 546.59 ± 45.22 N, and 370.52 ± 36.41 N, respectively) compared to the CTB(669.60 ± 65.43 N, 423.71 ± 39.46 N, and 264.57 ± 48.15 N, respectively; P < 0.05).ConclusionThe novel plate features an anatomically contoured profile that closely conforms to the patellar surface, adhering to the principles of tension-band fixation. The screw trajectories are designed with a centripetal orientation, generating intrinsic compressive forces that facilitate axial compression across the fracture site. This not only helps maintain patellar integrity but also enhances knee joint stability, aligning well with established biomechanical principles. Collectively, these design characteristics position the novel plate as a promising new internal fixation option for the treatment of patellar fractures.Keywords: Patellar fracture, Annular multi-axis locking plate, Biomechanics test

Introduction

Patellar fracture is a common intra-articular fracture, accounting for approximately 1.65% of all fractures [1]. With societal advancement and the development of modern transportation, the incidence of patellar fractures is increasing. As an intra-articular injury, any active or passive knee flexion or extension generates stress across the fracture site: tensile stress acts on the anterior surface of the patella, while compressive stress is borne by the posterior articular surface, thereby creating a three-point bending stress [2]. The maximum physiological loads at the quadricep tendon and patellar ligament end of the patella can reach 3200 N and 2800 N, respectively, while the patellar articular surface is subjected to compressive forces exceeding sevenfold body weight [3]. Thus, the treatment of patellar fractures involves not only restoring anatomical alignment and achieving interfragmentary compression but also ensuring resistance to tensile forces on the anterior patellar surface. Patellar fractures accompanied by extensor mechanism injury, articular step-off exceeding 2 mm, or a fracture gap greater than 3 mm are generally considered surgical indications [4]. The principles of treating patellar fractures include restoring articular surface congruity, achieving anatomical reduction, ensuring stable internal fixation, initiating early functional rehabilitation, and restoring the biomechanical function of the patellofemoral joint.

The conventional management of patellar fractures involves cable tension band using Kirschner wires—a technique widely adopted in clinical settings. Nevertheless, this method is associated with notable limitations, including K-wire migration or backing out, reduced efficacy of the tension-to-compression mechanism during knee extension, and insufficient tensile strength of the cable [5, 6]. Patellar plates have gained increasing clinical adoption in recent years due to their technical simplicity, shortened surgical duration, and decreased reliance on intraoperative fluoroscopy [7, 8]. Nevertheless, existing designs are limited by their bulky profile, which exerts excessive pressure on the overlying soft tissues, as well as inadequate capacity for axial compression.

To address the shortcomings of current patellar fixation systems, the research group designed a novel annular multi-axis locking plate specifically engineered for patellar fractures. Distinct from traditional patellar locking plates, this novel annular multi-axis locking plate has a four-wing design that wraps securely around the patella to enhance the tension-band effect. Its 2.7-mm locking screws are oriented parallel to the patellofemoral joint surface, delivering compression directly at the articular fracture site in full accordance with patellar biomechanics. Through biomechanical analysis of patellar fracture models, this study demonstrates that the novel annular multi-axis locking plate exhibits superior biomechanical performance.

Materials and methods

Steel plate parameters

Guided by three-dimensional morphometric analysis of the adult patella, we developed a novel annular multi-axis locking plate for the patella, incorporating four anatomically contoured wing-like arms. To accommodate variations in patellar size, the plate was designed in three sizes-large, medium, and small-with diameters of 60, 54, and 50 mm, respectively; a uniform thickness of 1.5 mm; and is made of titanium alloy. Its structural integrity and load-bearing capacity were confirmed through comprehensive mechanical stress testing. The novel annular multi-axis locking patellar plate exhibits a tensile strength of 351–429 MPa. Plate dimensions and specifications are presented in Fig. 1. Model construction and finite element structural analysis

A three-dimensional (3D) finite element model was developed to simulate patellar fracture fixation. The geometric model of an adult human patella was reconstructed from point cloud data acquired via spiral computed tomography using reverse engineering techniques. The raw scan data were processed and converted into a high-fidelity 3D model of the patella. A transverse fracture (AO/OTA 34B1) configuration was then virtually created in this model to replicate the most common clinical fracture pattern.

Each fracture fragment was further segmented into cortical and cancellous bone components using Boolean operations, thereby enabling distinct material property assignments based on bone type (Fig. 3).

A Patellar specimen; B 3D patellar model; C Patellar transverse fracture 3D model

Based on the reconstructed patellar geometry, a novel annular multi-axis locking plate was designed using Unigraphics NX (UG) computer-aided design (CAD) software. The plate features eight screw holes and a uniform thickness of 1.5 mm, specifically engineered to accommodate 2.7 mm cortical bone locking screws. The construct stabilizes the fracture by securing both fragments with angularly stable locking screws that conform to the curved anatomy of the patella (Fig. 4).

Mesh generation, contact definition, and boundary conditions

Considering the minimal impact of thread geometry on mechanical analysis, the screw threads on both the locking plate and screws were omitted to simplify the model. This simplification facilitated subsequent mesh generation and reduced computational complexity while maintaining the accuracy of the simulation results. The patella, locking screws, and the novel annular multi-axis locking patella plate were discretized using tetrahedral or hexahedral elements with an element size of 1.0 mm (Fig. 5 and Table 2).

Boundary conditions were defined as follows: Fracture surfaces at both ends of the fracture line were modeled as face-to-face contact with a friction coefficient of 0.04. All other interfaces between bone fragments were defined as bonded contacts to ensure no relative motion occurred during the simulation. For the purpose of this study, it was assumed that there would be no relative movement between the patella, the novel locking plate, and the locking screws. Therefore, all degrees of freedom at the interfaces between these components were fully constrained, ensuring complete immobilization in all directions [9, 10].

Loading protocol

A finite element model of a transverse patellar fracture fixed with the novel annular multi-axis locking plate was subjected to physiologically relevant loads at three knee flexion angles: 20, 45, and 90°.

Biomechanical data indicate that patellofemoral contact stress is negligible in full extension, rises to approximately 2.0 MPa at 20° flexion, peaks at around 4.4 MPa at 90°, and declines slightly beyond that. The location of contact between the patella and femoral trochlea shifts proximally as the knee flexes (20°: contact occurs at the distal patellar articular surface; 45°: contact involves the middle third; 90°: contact moves to the proximal third).

The patellar geometry used in this study measured 42.6 mm in height, 43.7 mm in width, and 18.64 mm in thickness, with transverse and longitudinal arc lengths of 40.85 and 36.72 mm, respectively—consistent with an average adult patella. A medium-sized annular locking plate was selected and secured with eight 2.7 mm × 16 mm locking screws. Accordingly, uniform pressure loads were applied perpendicular to the corresponding articular regions: 2.4 MPa on the distal one-third ( 20°flexion), 3.4 MPa on the middle one-third (45°flexion), 4.4 MPa on the proximal one-third (90°flexion).These values reflect scaled estimates of in vivo patellofemoral contact pressures and were applied directly to the cartilage-facing surface of the bone model (Fig. 6).

Cadaveric biomechanical testing

Specimen preparation

This study utilized eight knee joint specimens from four donors (two male, two female), aged 52–77 years (mean: 67.25 ± 10.72 years). The skin and subcutaneous tissues were removed, while partial segments of the femur and tibia, the intact patella, quadriceps tendon, patellar tendon, knee joint capsule, and patellar retinacula were preserved. The specimens were obtained from an institutional-approved tissue bank following ethical approval and donor consent. A transverse fracture was created in the patella using an oscillating saw under fluoroscopic guidance (Fig. 7A). This method ensured precise control over the fracture orientation and consistency across all specimens. The fracture was carefully positioned at the mid-patellar level to simulate a clinically relevant transverse patellar fracture (AO/OTA 34B1).

A: Transverse patellar fracture model, B–C: Fixation with the novel annular multi-axis locking plate (AMALP)

Specimen grouping and surgical fixation technique

The eight cadaveric patellar fracture models were organized into four matched left–right pairs based on anatomical symmetry and bone quality, and then randomly allocated to either the control group or the experimental group, ensuring that each donor contributed one specimen to each fixation method.

Control Group (Titanium Cable Tension Band, CTB): Two 2.0 mm Kirschner wires were inserted in a parallel, longitudinal orientation from the superior pole of the patella, traversing the transverse fracture line to engage both proximal and distal fragments. A 1.2 mm high-strength titanium cable was passed deep to the patellar tendon and anterior to the distal tips of the Kirschner wires, then tensioned in a figure-of-eight configuration over the anterior surface of the patella, encircling the protruding proximal ends of the Kirschner wires. The cable was tensioned using a dedicated crimping device to generate interfragmentary compression, leveraging the biomechanical principle of converting tensile forces from the extensor mechanism into compressive forces at the fracture site—characteristic of tension band fixation. After securing the construct, the protruding ends of the Kirschner wires were bent, trimmed, and buried within the surrounding soft tissue to reduce the risk of irritation or migration.

Experimental Group (Annular Multi-Axis Locking Plate Fixation, AMALP): Fractures were stabilized using a novel annular multi-axis locking plate specifically engineered for patellar anatomy. The plate was contoured to match the natural anterior curvature of the patella and positioned centrally across the fracture line. Through its pre-angled locking holes, 2.7 mm cortical bone locking screws were inserted into both fracture fragments. Critically, the peripheral screws were oriented parallel to the patellofemoral articular surface to avoid intra-articular penetration and to provide optimal subchondral support, thereby enhancing construct stability while preserving joint congruity (Fig. 7B and C).

Biomechanical experiments

All eight knee specimens (four pairs) were mounted in custom-designed fixtures and secured to a CMT5105 computer-controlled electromechanical universal testing machine. The testing system consisted of a femoral fixation unit, a traction mechanism, a load cell, and calibrated weights.

A steel cable was attached to the quadriceps tendon via a metal clamp on one end, while the other end passed over a low-friction pulley and connected to the actuator of the testing machine. The cable was also linked in series with a load cell to monitor applied forces in real time. Traction of the cable simulated active knee extension and flexion by pulling the quadriceps tendon.

Interfragmentary displacement after cyclic flexion–extension fatigue testing: To simulate physiological loading during daily activities, a 6 kg weight (≈58.8 N) was suspended from the tibia at a point 25 mm distal to the inferior pole of the patella, representing the effective weight of the lower leg. The quadriceps tendon was then pulled at a constant speed of 25 mm/s, cycling the knee joint between 90°of flexion and full extension (0°) for 200 cycles. Before and after cyclic loading, the interfragmentary gap was measured at two locations: the anterior surface and the articular surface of the patellar fracture. The displacement induced by fatigue testing was calculated as the difference between post- and pre-cyclic measurements. If hardware failure occurred during testing, the cycle number at failure was recorded. Fixation failure was defined as a separation of 2.0 mm or greater between fracture fragments. (Fig. 8A).

A Fatigue test; B Maximum load test

Maximum Load to Failure at Three Knee Flexion Angles: The knee joint was rigidly fixed at three distinct angles—20, 45, and 90°—to represent different degrees of knee flexion during functional activities. A tensile force was applied to the quadriceps tendon via the steel cable to generate distraction stress across the patellar fracture site. Loading began at 50 N and increased at a constant rate of 10 N/s until failure. Fracture gap displacement was monitored using a digital caliper with 0.01 mm resolution. Fixation failure was defined as a fracture separation of 2.0 mm, and the corresponding load (in newtons) was recorded as the ultimate failure load for each specimen and fixation method (Fig. 8B).

Statistics analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Given the matched-pair design—where each donor contributed one specimen to each fixation group—comparisons between the titanium cable tension band (CTB) and the novel annular multi-axis locking plate (AMALP) were conducted using paired t-tests. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results of the FEA

At 20° knee flexion, the maximum von Mises stress in the coronal plane was 45.328 MPa, localized near the fracture line on the distal side. The maximum fracture displacement was 0.1645 mm. At 45° flexion, as the patellofemoral contact area migrated proximally and joint contact pressure increased, the peak von Mises stress rose markedly to 68.462 MPa, again concentrated adjacent to the fracture line. The corresponding maximum displacement was 0.2533 mm. At 90° flexion, the maximum von Mises stress further increased to 109.191 MPa, now located near the fracture line on the proximal side, with a maximum fracture displacement of 0.3487 mm (Table 3, Fig. 9).

Discussion

The patella, the largest sesamoid bone in the human body, is roughly circular in shape. Its anterior surface is non-articular and serves as an attachment site for tendinous and ligamentous structures, while approximately three-quarters of its posterior surface is covered with articular cartilage, divided by a vertical ridge into medial and lateral facets that articulate with the femoral trochlea [11]. The quadriceps tendon inserts onto the superior pole, the patellar ligament originates from the inferior pole, and the medial and lateral retinacula—formed by fibers from the vastus medialis, vastus lateralis, and deep fascia—stabilize the patella on either side, collectively constituting the extensor mechanism of the knee [12]. Biomechanically, the patella enhances the efficiency of the quadriceps by increasing the moment arm of the extensor mechanism. Its thickness displaces the patellar ligament anteriorly relative to the center of rotation of the patellofemoral joint, thereby amplifying torque generation. Patellectomy has been shown to reduce quadriceps torque by up to 40% [13].

Patellar fractures are most commonly caused by direct trauma to the patella. Due to the resultant disruption of the quadriceps mechanism and the high incidence of long-term complications, most scholars recommend open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) as the primary treatment for displaced patellar fractures [14, 15]. The goals of surgical intervention are to restore articular congruity and achieve stable fixation, thereby facilitating early postoperative mobilization. However, consensus on the optimal internal fixation technique remains lacking.

Several internal fixation techniques are currently used for patellar fractures, each presenting a unique balance of biomechanical efficacy and clinical limitations. The Kirschner wire tension band construct remains one of the most commonly employed methods due to its relatively favorable biomechanical profile and historical familiarity. Nevertheless, it is associated with several well-recognized complications. K-wires may back out during early mobilization, and the accompanying cerclage wire can cut through soft tissue or even the patellar bone itself, particularly in osteoporotic or comminuted fractures, potentially leading to loss of reduction and fixation failure. Moreover, when the knee approaches full extension—near 0°—the tensile forces exerted by the quadriceps tendon and patellar ligament dominate, and the tension band mechanism loses its ability to convert tensile stress into compressive force across the fracture site as effectively as it does in flexion. This can allow fracture fragments to slide along the pins, resulting in articular incongruity. Additional concerns include the limited fatigue resistance of stainless steel wire, which may fracture under cyclic loading, and the frequent irritation caused by protruding pin ends, often necessitating hardware removal [16–18]. Shape-memory alloy patellar claws represent another option, leveraging the material’s thermal memory properties to achieve dynamic compression. However, their design lacks lateral arms in the coronal plane, limiting their effectiveness in longitudinal fractures and offering insufficient stability in severely comminuted patterns. Furthermore, due to interindividual anatomical variation and the finite range of available implant sizes, the claws often fail to achieve intimate contact with the irregular patellar surface, leading to incomplete apposition or a “floating” configuration that predisposes to loosening or hook fracture during joint motion. Disparities in the number and recoil force between the superior and inferior claw arms may also generate uneven compression during reduction or postoperative knee movement, potentially contributing to step-off deformities at the articular surface [19, 20]. Lag screw fixation is primarily suited for simple transverse or vertical fractures, where it provides effective interfragmentary compression and enhances stability by eliminating gaps between fracture ends. Despite these advantages, this technique lacks a counteracting tension-band element on the anterior (tensile) side of the patella, rendering it less capable of resisting the substantial bending moments generated during knee flexion. Additionally, successful lag screw placement requires adequate bone quality and volume for secure purchase, which poses a significant challenge in osteoporotic patients or those with extensive comminution, often limiting its applicability in complex fracture scenarios [21, 22].

Compared with the aforementioned fixation methods, patellar locking plates offer several practical advantages, including simpler surgical technique, shorter operative duration, and reduced intraoperative fluoroscopy requirements. By virtue of their unique design, locking plates integrate screws and plate into a fixed-angle construct that functions as an internal fixator, potentially preserving periosteal vascularity while achieving a stable plate-bone interface and robust mechanical fixation [8, 23]. Furthermore, variable circumferential polyaxial locking systems enable flexible adjustment of screw trajectories according to the morphology of individual fracture fragments, effectively overcoming the challenge of securing stable fixation on the irregular articular surface of the patella. With continuous advances in implant manufacturing and evolving clinical understanding, newer generations of low-profile, high-strength locking plates have been developed and increasingly applied in the management of patellar fractures, demonstrating promising early outcomes. Nevertheless, current patellar locking plates are not without limitations. Thelen et al. [24, 25], in a cadaveric biomechanical study comparing multi-row, multi-hole patellar locking plates with traditional Kirschner wire tension bands and cannulated screw tension bands, reported superior mechanical performance for the locking plate construct. However, the relatively bulky profile of these multi-hole mesh-like plates, combined with the thin soft tissue envelope over the anterior patella, often leads to excessive pressure on the overlying skin and soft tissues, increasing the risk of irritation or even wound complications. In a clinical series, Tyler et al. [26] treated 36 patients with patellar fractures using a 4-hole locking plate and reported satisfactory functional results. Yet, the plate they employed featured fixed-angle locking holes without circumferential polyaxial adjustability, and its limited number of screw holes (only four) rendered it suboptimal for achieving stable fixation in highly comminuted fracture patterns.

To address the limitations of current patellar locking plates, our research group has developed a novel circumferential polyaxial locking plate specifically designed for patellar fractures. This new implant incorporates several biomechanically advantageous features. First, it is a true locking plate system in which screws are interlocked with the plate, forming an integrated screw-plate construct that provides greater stability compared to conventional non-locking screws. Second, all eight screw holes are configured as polyaxial locking ports, enabling versatile screw angulation to effectively engage complex and irregular fracture fragments. Notably, screws placed near the articular surface can be oriented parallel to the patellofemoral joint plane, facilitating axial compression across the fracture site and thereby enhancing fragment stability. Third, the plate is positioned on the anterior (tensile) side of the patella—the primary tension-bearing region during knee flexion—thereby counteracting the natural tendency of fracture fragments to separate under bending loads. The plate also features an anatomical curvature matching the native patellar contour and can be further contoured intraoperatively to achieve optimal surface conformity, promoting more physiological load transfer.

Biomechanical testing using a simulated patellar fracture model further substantiated the performance of this novel plate. Under cyclic knee flexion loading at 100, 150, and 200 cycles, no instances of fixation failure were observed. Moreover, displacement at both the anterior fracture margin and the articular surface was significantly lower in the experimental group compared to the control group (anterior displacement: 0.36 ± 0.05 mm, 0.41 ± 0.04 mm, and 0.49 ± 0.08 mm vs. 0.54 ± 0.09 mm, 0.66 ± 0.11 mm, and 0.73 ± 0.09 mm; articular displacement: 0.29 ± 0.05 mm, 0.36 ± 0.07 mm, and 0.44 ± 0.09 mm vs. 0.45 ± 0.06 mm, 0.52 ± 0.06 mm, and 0.59 ± 0.08 mm; P < 0.05). Additionally, under static loading at knee flexion angles of 20, 45, and 90°, the experimental group demonstrated significantly higher maximum load-to-failure values (812.40 ± 46.62 N, 546.59 ± 45.22 N, and 370.52 ± 36.41 N, respectively) compared to the control group (669.60 ± 65.43 N, 423.71 ± 39.46 N, and 264.57 ± 48.15 N; P < 0.05), confirming its superior mechanical stability and fixation efficacy.

The cadaveric biomechanical testing employed in this study represents a dynamic experimental model that offers distinct advantages over alternative methodologies. During physiological knee extension, the contact area between the patella and femur undergoes continuous change, resulting in dynamically varying contact pressures across the patellofemoral joint surface [27]. In contrast, both finite element analysis and synthetic resin bone models are inherently static in nature and therefore unable to fully replicate the complex, time-dependent mechanical environment experienced under true physiological loading conditions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, our investigation focused exclusively on transverse patellar fractures, and comminuted fracture patterns were not included in the experimental design. Consequently, the biomechanical performance of the novel plate in the context of highly comminuted patellar fractures remains to be evaluated in future studies. Second, the number of cadaveric knee specimens used in this study was relatively small; a larger sample size would enhance the statistical power and improve the reliability of the biomechanical data.

Conclusion

The novel plate features an anatomically contoured profile that closely conforms to the patellar surface, adhering to the principles of tension-band fixation. The screw trajectories are designed with a centripetal orientation, generating intrinsic compressive forces that facilitate axial compression across the fracture site. This not only helps maintain patellar integrity but also enhances knee joint stability, aligning well with established biomechanical principles. Collectively, these design characteristics position the novel plate as a promising new internal fixation option for the treatment of patellar fractures.
