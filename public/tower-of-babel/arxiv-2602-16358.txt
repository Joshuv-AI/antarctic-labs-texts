System Identification under Constraints and Disturbance: A Bayesian Estimation Approach
Sergi Martinez, Steve Tonneau, Carlos Mastalli

Abstract
We introduce a Bayesian system identification (SysID) framework for jointly estimating robot’s state trajectories and physical parameters with high accuracy. It embeds physically consistent inverse dynamics, contact and loop-closure constraints, and fully featured joint friction models as hard, stage-wise equality constraints. It relies on energy-based regressors to enhance parameter observability, supports both equality and inequality priors on inertial and actuation parameters, enforces dynamically consistent disturbance projections, and augments proprioceptive measurements with energy observations to disambiguate nonlinear friction effects. To ensure scalability, we derive a parameterized equality-constrained Riccati recursion that preserves the banded structure of the problem, achieving linear complexity in the time horizon, and develop computationally efficient derivatives. Simulation studies on representative robotic systems, together with hardware experiments on a Unitree B1 equipped with a Z1 arm, demonstrate faster convergence, lower inertial and friction estimation errors, and improved contact consistency compared to forward-dynamics and decoupled identification baselines. When deployed within model predictive control frameworks, the resulting models yield measurable improvements in tracking performance during locomotion over challenging environments.

Frequentist approaches estimate the “true” parameters by minimizing an objective function such as the least-squares prediction error or the likelihood, yielding a single best-fit parameter vector that maximizes agreement with the observed data.
However, when datasets are limited (e.g., short trajectories, insufficient excitation, or missing sensor data) or the model is imperfect (e.g., simplified friction models, unmodeled compliance, or sensor delays), these methods often overfit, resulting in poor generalization and unreliable identification.

In contrast, Bayesian approaches mitigate these issues by incorporating prior knowledge and inferring a distribution over the model parameters rather than a single point estimate.
This probabilistic formulation provides a principled framework for uncertainty quantification and prior integration.
Although Bayesian methods are typically more computationally demanding, their complexity can be substantially reduced by exploiting the Markovian and parametric structure of the dynamics—drawing inspiration from efficient solvers developed for
MPC
with full-system dynamics (e.g.,
[

33

I-A

Forward and Inverse Dynamics

Forward dynamics formulations are sensitive to coarse integration timesteps and can suffer from numerical instability, as frequently reported in the
MPC
literature
[
11
,
31
]
.
By decoupling the integrator from the dynamics, inverse dynamics formulations yield better-conditioned optimization problems, leading to faster convergence, improved robustness.
Inverse dynamics makes the physical-consistency relations explicit through equality constraints that couple motion, actuation inputs, and contact interactions.
Incorporating inverse dynamics within
SysID
frameworks can therefore enhance identification accuracy and physical consistency.
However, this formulation introduces additional decision variables compared to forward dynamics, increasing computational complexity despite its sparser structure.
Specifically, it augments the optimization problem with generalized accelerations and contact forces at each timestep, adding
N({n_{v}}+{n_{c}})
decision variables.
To address this, we exploit the problem’s structured, allowing us to derive an efficient
SysID
optimization solver (
Section

VIII
).

I-B

Kinematic Loops and Bilateral Constraints

Beyond the inverse dynamics, the motion of a robot is also governed by constraints arising from intermittent contacts, closed kinematic loops, and discrete events such as touchdown and liftoff.
These phenomena restrict the admissible state-control space and introduce discontinuities in the dynamics.
When such effects are neglected,
SysID
algorithms often compensate by distorting inertial or friction parameters, resulting in biased models and inaccurate state estimation.
Accurately enforcing contact constraints—especially during multi-contact motions—is therefore essential to preserve physical consistency and ensure reliable
SysID
.
Similarly, kinematic loops must be explicitly modeled, as their coupling structure allows friction to be correctly identified on the motor side rather than being absorbed at the joint level.
This improves parameter consistency across the transmission, but requires to develop analytical derivatives for mode and reset maps (
Section

IV
) and an especial treatment of external disturbance (
Section

V
).

I-C

Incorporating Physical and Parametric Constraints in Estimation

Incorporating additional constraints provides a principled means to integrate prior knowledge, enforce physical consistency and hardware limitations within the

Such constraints serves a dual purpose: they encode prior information about the robot’s physical parameters while ensuring that the identified models remains dynamically and physical consistent.
For example, many robots exhibit discrete symmetries (such as left-right symmetry in bipeds), which can be imposed as equality constraints on the parameters
[
35
]
.
Similarly, known quantities such as the total mass or bounds on friction coefficients can be enforced through equality or inequality constraints, thereby improving parameter identifiability and estimation accuracy (

VI

These constraints are formally represented by

To fully exploit this structure, our framework combines Lie-algebra–based parameterizations (

Section

I-D

Friction and its Role in Energy Dissipation

Traditional identification methods often neglects the joint friction, especially nonlinear effects such as stiction.
Friction introduces nonlinear, discontinuous, and hysteresis effects that are difficult to model accurately
[
34
]
.
This challenge is exacerbated in modern torque-controlled robots, which typically lack direct torque sensing—making only the combined effects of friction and limb inertia externally observable.
Consequently, accurate identifying friction parameters becomes difficult, and localization must be tightly integrated within the overall
SysID
process.
To mitigate this issue, we leverage energy-based observations (
Section

VII
), inspired by classical energy regressors
[
17
]
.
By explicitly incorporating measurements of input power and dissipated energy, our framework provides additional information to dissentagle friction effects from inertial dynamics, improving the identifiability of friction parameters even in the absence of direct torque measurements.

In this work, we introduce a unified and physically consistent framework for Bayesian system identification of floating-base robots subject to contact and loop constraints (
Figure

2
).
Our approach integrates accurate dynamic modeling, constraint-aware noise handling, and structure-exploiting optimization to achieve robust and energy-consistent identification across complex motion regimes.
Specifically, our framework:

1.

embeds a parameterized forward or inverse dynamics model with analytical derivatives,

2.

handles closed-loop kinematics, bilateral contacts, and discrete reset maps in a unified formulation,

3.

enforces dynamically consistent additive noise that respects contact and closed-loop constraints,

4.

incorporates a smooth and differentiable friction model capturing stiction, dry, viscous, and Stribeck effects,

5.

leverages energy-based observations to improve the accuracy and observability of identified dynamics, and

6.

employs an efficient stagewise optimizer combined with manifold-based parameterizations to enforce physical consistency while maintaining computational scalability.

The following section reviews related research efforts in system identification and physically consistent estimation.

II
Related Work

Reliable system identification in robotics builds upon decades of work spanning classical rigid-body regression, probabilistic estimation, and structure-exploiting optimization.
Yet, many state-of-the-art methods remain simplified; for example, they decouple localization from
SysID
, rely predominantly on frequentist formulations, and often neglect energy consistency, friction, actuation effects, and closed-loop mechanisms.
This section reviews key developments relevant to our approach—covering traditional identification methods, joint estimation of states and parameters, probabilistic factor-graph frameworks, and recent advances in dynamics-constrained optimization and physically consistent modeling.

II-A

Classical Identification Methods

Early rigid body parameter identification writes inverse dynamics as a regression in the inertial parameters and solves least-squares from sufficiently excited trajectories
[
15
,
42
,
32
,
25
]
.
A key challenge is
identifiability
: many parameters are linearly dependent, producing rank-deficient regressors and nonphysical solutions.
Two main approaches address this issue.
First, convex formulations using
linear matrix inequalities (LMIs)
enforce
physical consistency
by constraining the identified parameters to correspond to realizable mass distributions
[
40
,
49
]
.
Second, manifold parameterizations guarantees full physical consistency by construction, e.g., by encoding triangle inequalities on principal moments.
This allows smooth unconstrained optimization on minimal coordinates
[
28
,
44
]
.
To improve robustness to feedback noise,
closed-loop output-error
(CLOE/DIDIM) methods minimize simulation mismatch between measured and simulated torques in a closed-loop setting
[
14
]
.
Nevertheless, these approaches assume accurate state and contact trajectories; even small pose or velocities biases can corrupt parameter identification, especially in contact-rich scenarios.
In this context, a promising alternative to traditional
SysID
approaches is Bayesian optimization.

II-B

Joint State-Parameter Estimation

To overcome state-parameter coupling, recent work performs joint estimation of localization, contact forces, and dynamics parameters within a unified optimization framework.
Kolev and Todorov
[
24
]
showed that, in contact-rich tasks, decoupled pipelines are fragile—millimeter-level state biases can make
SysID
ill-posed—while jointly optimizing trajectories, contact forces, and parameters yields consistent solutions.
Other examples include constrained least squares for identifying friction and motor inertia on the Digit humanoid
[
50
]
and physically consistent contact identification on the Spot quadruped
[
22
]
.
This latter example eliminates unknown contact forces through nullspace projections of whole-body dynamics while enforcing
LMIs
-based physical consistency.
To overcome these pitfalls,
SysID
frameworks that do not rely on known state trajectories are preferable alternatives.

II-C

Probabilistic and Factor-Graph Formulations

A common batch realization of joint estimation is
maximum-a-posteriori (MAP)
inference used on factor graphs.
Factor graphs fuse measurements with dynamics and kinematic models while maintaining handling manifold consistency via retractions
[
10
]
.
Such frameworks naturally incorporate priors over both states and parameters, and can encode contact or loop-closure constraints directly as factors—reducing estimator bias compared with measurement-driven approaches.
However, generic factor-graph solvers often fail to exploit the sparsity and Markovian structure of rigid-body dynamics, resulting in slower convergence and the need for kinematic models only
[
10
]
.
Scalability is particularly important in
SysID
, as joint friction identification often requires handling large, high-frequency datasets.

II-D

Structure-Exploiting Solvers

To achieve scalability, recent works draw inspiration from optimal control.
These methods exploit the Markovian structure of dynamics through Riccati recursion derived from the Bellman equation.
Algorithms such as DDP/iLQR handles constraints via projection or augmented Lagrangian penalties while retaining efficient fast Riccati recursion in their backward pass
[
43
,
30
,
19
,
20
]
.
Related equality-constrained Riccati recursions further tighten the link between second-order methods and structure-exploiting linear algebra for constrained optimal control
[
47
,
31
,
36
]
.
Although originally developed for control, these solvers are directly relevant to system identification: they preserve computational efficiency—which is important to handle large dataset—while enforcing physical and contact constraints within the estimation process.
However, efficient solvers for
SysID
require extending these by introducing a parametrized stagewise approach derived from the Bellman equation.

II-E

Closed Kinematic Loops and Structural Constraints

Many robotic platforms include closed kinematic loops and transmissions that violate open-chain assumptions
[
13
]
.
Early work introduce elimination procedures to account for such loops
[
21
,
3
,
16
,
38
]
, while more recent
constraint embedding
formulations restore recursive efficiency by incorporating loop closure directly in the dynamics
[
8
]
.
This propagation-based view generalizes the
articulated-body algorithm (ABA)
to looped subsystem, improving computational efficiency and numerical stability.
In control, explicitly enforcing constraints improve solution quality and expand feasible motion sets
[
9
]
.
These insights suggest that
SysID
pipelines should likewise
embed
loop-closure constraints rather than approximate them as serial chains.
By leveraging hybrid dynamics and their analytical derivatives to handle bilateral contact, closed-loop mechanism, and reset maps, we strength the capabilities of current
SysID
frameworks.

II-F

Energy- and Power-Based Identification

While embedding loop and transmissions constraints improves modeling fidelity, observability can remain limited—particularly for frictional effects.
Energy-based formulations exploit work–energy principle to identify inertial and friction parameters without explicitly differentiating accelerations
[
5
,
18
]
.
Such formulations are inherently energy-aware and often yield better-conditioned estimates.
When combined with physical-consistency constraints
[
40
,
44
,
49
]
, they enable parameter identification consistent with observed energy flow, even without direct force/torque sensing.
This offers a valuable signal source when internal actuator sensing is limited.
However, incorporating energy-based observations into Bayesian optimization poses significant challenges for computing analytical derivatives.

Next, we describe the modeling of hybrid dynamics and closed-loop mechanisms, along with the computation of their analytical derivatives.

Hybrid dynamics form the foundation of control, planning, and estimation in legged robotics (

They capture the relationship between the forces acting on the system and the constraints they impose.

III-B

Dynamics with Implicit Motion Constraints

We refer to

Such constraints capture chords or cycles in the connectivity graph, which typically emerge when modeling bilateral contacts or closed-loop mechanisms.
In hybrid dynamics, the most relevant cases include:
(i) manifold-valued configurations (e.g.,

(ii) bilateral contact conditions,
(iii) holonomic constraints from structural closed-loop kinematics, and

While we handle manifold-valued configurations using Lie algebra

During a hybrid event (impact, contact engage/disengage), the Newton–Euler equations are integrated over an infinitesimal time interval

By analogy with continuous modes, the Gauss impact principle defines the impact law as the post-impact velocity
\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}
that deviates as little as possible from pre-impact velocity
\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\,\left\|\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}-\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})}

(7)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}=\mathbf{v}_{b}(\mathbf{q}).

where
\mathbf{v}_{b}
encodes the constraint velocity after impact (i.e.,
bias velocity
).
From the
KKT
conditions of
Equation

7
, we obtain the system

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}\\
-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})&\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})&\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix},

(8)

with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{}
is the generalized constraint impulse.
It arises from the following KKT residuals:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{i}\\
\mathbf{r}_{v}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})(\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}-\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-})-\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}-\mathbf{v}_{b}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix},

(9)

where
\mathbf{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
and
\mathbf{r}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}}
are the
KKT
residuals associated to impact law and velocity-level constraints, respectively.
This directly yields the classical impulse-momentum relation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\big(\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}-\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-}\big)\;=\;\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{}.

(10)

For bilateral contacts, the bias velocity recovers the standard
impulse-momentum law
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}_{b}}_{c}(\mathbf{q})=-\epsilon\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{c}}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-},

(11)

with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{c}\geq\mathbf{0}
,
\epsilon\in[0,1]
the coefficient of restitution, where
\epsilon=0
corresponds to a perfectly plastic impact and
\epsilon=1
to a perfectly elastic impact.

While the modeling of bilateral constraints is well established, computing analytical derivatives for systems with closed-loop kinematics remains nontrivial and is often a source of numerical and implementation complexity.

IV
Analytical Derivatives of Hybrid Dynamics with Closed-Loop Mechanism

Bayesian estimation in
SysID
require the sensitivities of the acceleration and constraint forces in
Equation

5
(or velocity and impulse constraints) with respect to the generalized position
\mathbf{q}
, velocity
\mathbf{v}
, and dynamics parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
These sensitivities can be obtained by differentiating the KKT system in
Equation

5
in a forward-mode manner.
For an arbitrary variable
\mathbf{y}
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\dot{v}}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\
-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}{}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})&\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})&\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{d}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\
\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{a}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix},

(12)

which is equivalent to applying the implicit function theorem as introduced in
[
29
]
.
The right-hand-side terms can be computed analytically, i.e.,
\partial\mathbf{\dot{v}}=-{\nabla\mathbf{r}}^{-1}\partial\mathbf{r}
and
\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}{}={\nabla\mathbf{r}}^{-1}\partial\mathbf{r}
, where
\partial\mathbf{r}
contains the partial derivatives of both dynamics and acceleration residuals (
\mathbf{r}_{d}
,
\mathbf{r}_{a}
).
In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{d}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\partial\mathrm{RNEA}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})}{\partial\mathbf{y}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\theta}}}
denotes the vector of dynamics parameters, including inertial, actuation, and joint-friction parameters.
The terms
\partial\mathrm{RNEA}/\partial\mathbf{q}
and
\partial\mathrm{RNEA}/\partial\mathbf{v}
correspond to the analytical derivatives of the
RNEA
, as derived in
[
6
]
.

IV-
1
Analytical derivatives within reset maps

Analogously to
Equation

12
, the forward-mode derivatives of the reset maps (i.e.,
Equation

8
) are computed as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\
-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})&\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})&\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{i}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\
\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{v}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

where the term
\frac{\partial\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}
inside
\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}
is a third-order tensor.
To avoid explicitly forming such tensors, the upper block of the right-hand side vector in

14
can be computed more efficently by exploting the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\partial\mathrm{RNEA}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}-\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}},

(15)

where, for
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the analytical derivatives are computed as described in
[
6
]
.

In the following, we outline how to compute the analytical derivatives of the acceleration bias
\mathbf{a}_{b}
(and the velocity bias
\mathbf{v}_{b}
for reset maps) in the presence of bilateral contacts and closed-loop mechanisms.
For the actuation forces
\boldsymbol{\tau}
incorporating Coulomb, viscous, and Stribeck friction effects, we later describe how to compute the corresponding derivatives.

Bilateral Contacts and Closed-Loop Mechanisms

When the robots contains closed mechanisms, the algebraic loop-closure constraints

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\!c}(\mathbf{q})\;

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\!k}(\mathbf{q})\;

\displaystyle=\;\mathrm{Log}(\prescript{{k_{2}}}{}{\mathbf{M}_{k_{1}}}),

(loop closure)

\prescript{c}{}{\mathbf{M}_{1}}

\prescript{{k_{2}}}{}{\mathbf{M}_{k_{1}}}

These placements are computed by the forward kinematics, and the superscript

is the logarithm map from

to

and
\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-}

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{k}}(\mathbf{q})\;

\displaystyle=\;\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{J}_{1}}(\mathbf{q})-\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{k2}}(\mathbf{q})\,\prescript{k2}{}{\mathbf{J}_{2}}(\mathbf{q}),

(loop closure)

\displaystyle\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{J}_{1}}=\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{1}}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{1}},\quad\prescript{k2}{}{\mathbf{J}_{1}}=\prescript{k2}{}{\mathbf{X}_{2}}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{2}},

\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{1}}
and

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{v}^{c}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\;

Differentiating the holonomic constraints a second time yields the acceleration-level conditions for bilateral contacts and closed-loop mechanisms, which are used in the forward dynamics phase of each mode. These conditions take the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\ddot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{c}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})

\displaystyle=\prescript{c}{}{\mathbf{X}_{1}}\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}},\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{\ddot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{k}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})

\displaystyle=\prescript{k1}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}-\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{k2}}\prescript{k2}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}}

\displaystyle+[\prescript{k1}{}{\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}]_{\times}\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{k2}}\prescript{k2}{}{\boldsymbol{v}_{2}},\quad\hskip-3.00003pt

\displaystyle\prescript{k1}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}=\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{1}}\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}},\quad\prescript{k2}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}=\prescript{k2}{}{\mathbf{X}_{2}}\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}},

(24)

where
\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}
and
\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}}
are the spatial accelerations of the first and second bodies, expressed in their respective local coordinates, and the third term is derived similarly to

We define the total motion-constraint residual as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\ddot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})=\mathbf{\ddot{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{c}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})-\mathbf{a}_{b}^{*}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v}),

where the bias acceleration

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{a}}_{b}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})\;

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{a}^{c}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\;

\displaystyle=\;\prescript{c}{}{\mathbf{X}_{1}}\,\frac{\partial\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}_{b_{c}}^{*}}{\partial\mathbf{q}},\quad\hskip 47.00003pt\text{(bilateral contact)}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{k2}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\prescript{k2}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}}+\left[\frac{\partial\prescript{k1}{}{\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\right]_{\times}\prescript{k1}{}{\mathbf{X}_{k2}}\prescript{k2}{}{\boldsymbol{v}_{2}}

where
\frac{\partial\!\prescript{}{}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{a}^{c}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\;

V
Hybrid Dynamics under External Disturbances

To avoid relying on known state trajectories in
SysID
, we must consider the state evolution as a
stochastic
process with additive Gaussian noise
[
48
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\dot{v}})\oplus\mathbf{w},\quad\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}}),

(34)

where
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})\in\mathcal{X}
denotes the current state,
\boldsymbol{\Psi}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is the numerical integrator that maps the current state and accelerations to the next state
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}}
is the covariance of the process noise
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
.
To ensure consistency, the process noise must be defined in a way that respects implicit constraints, as detailed in the next section.

V-A

Disturbance with Implicit Motion Constraints

When implicit motion constraints are present, admissible accelerations belong to the tangent space of the implicit constraint manifold
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{Q}\mid\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}_{c}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{\dot{v}}=\mathbf{a}_{b}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})\}
.
To preserve consistency with the system’s connectivity graph, process noise must be injected only along feasible directions.
We achieve this by projecting any process vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
onto the tangent space of the constraints
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{C}
.
This ensures that the resulting uncertainty remains consistent with respect the system’s kinematic structure.

The projection is defined by a matrix
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{q}}\times{n_{q}}}
that spans the kernel of the constraint Jacobian
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})
and captures the feasible motion directions.
From
Equation

5
, the constrained-acceleration update is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{v}}=\mathbf{\dot{v}}_{\text{free}}+\mathbf{M}^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal}(\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal})^{-1}(\mathbf{a}_{b}-\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{\dot{v}}_{\text{free}}),

(35)

from which we identify the
dynamically consistent projector
onto the constraint nullspace as

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})\coloneqq\ \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M}^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal}(\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal})^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}.

(36)

This projector enforces energy-momentum consistency, which explains the appearance of the mass matrix as a weighting term.
For comparison, a purely kinematic projector onto the constraint nullspace can be written as:
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{I}-\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal}\,(\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}^{\intercal})^{-1}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}
,
which is computationally cheaper and independent of inertial parameters.
However, the dynamically consistent projector guarantees physically meaningful accelerations and forces, making it essential for
SysID
and Bayesian estimation.
Concretely, we project the process noise as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{w}}\;=\;\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})&\\
&\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q})}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{\mathbf{q}}\\
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbf{v}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}}\;=\;\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{w}}\,\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal},

(37)

where
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbf{q}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbf{v}}
are the position and velocity components of
\mathbf{w}
, respectively;
\mathbf{\tilde{w}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}}
denote the projected noise and covariance, respectively, consistent with the implicit motion constraints.

V-B

Analytical Derivatives of the Dynamically Consistent Projector

The projector introduced in
Equation

36
can be seen as the result of applying Gauss’s principle of least constraint to the process noise, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|\mathbf{\tilde{w}}-\mathbf{w}\bigr\|^{2}_{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})}

(38)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{\tilde{w}}=\mathbf{0},

where the
\mathbf{y}
subscript refers to either
\mathbf{q}
or
\mathbf{v}
.
The corresponding
KKT
conditions of
Equation

38
are:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\tilde{w}}\\
-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})&\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})&\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{w}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},

(39)

whose solution yields
Equation

36
, where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
denotes the Lagrange multiplier associated with the motion constraint.
Similarly to the previous section, we define the corresponding residuals as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{w}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{r}_{\zeta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})(\mathbf{\tilde{w}}-\mathbf{w})-\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\\[2.0pt]
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{\tilde{w}}\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

Analogously to
Equation

14
, the forward-mode derivatives of the Gauss projection
KKT
system are

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{\mathbf{y}}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\[2.0pt]
-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\zeta}}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})&\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q})&\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{w}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\\
\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{\zeta}(\mathbf{q})}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\end{bmatrix},

(41)

where the residual derivatives are similarly computed as in
Equation

15
with
\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{+}
,
\mathbf{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^{-}
replaced by
\mathbf{\tilde{w}}
,
\mathbf{w}
, respectively.
Additionally, we reuse the factorization of
Equation

5
for enhancing computational efficiency.
Next, we describe the dynamic parameters typically considered in
SysID
.

VI
Inertial and Actuation Parameters

The dynamics in
Equations

2
,
5
and
8
can be parameterized by the inertial parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{10{n_{b}}}
and actuation parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\theta_{a}}}}

For efficiency, both the dynamics and their analytical derivatives (see
Sections

III
and
IV

inertial matrix method (IMM)

[

]
.
Consistency with the

VI-A

Inertial Parameters and Physical Consistency

Fig. 6
:

(mass, first moment, and inertia) are illustrated by the body frame
\mathcal{B}

The unconstrained coordinates
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
(shown as a mesh) are mapped to a physically consistent inertia via the exponential map, guaranteeing
m\geq 0
,
\mathbf{I}_{c}\succeq\mathbf{0}
, and triangle inequalities on the principal moments; the inverse (logarithmic) map recovers
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}
from
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

The inertial parameters of a rigid body
i
are represented by the vector
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}&\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\intercal}&\mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{I}_{i})^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{10},

(42)

where
m_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the body mass,
\mathbf{h}_{i}=m_{i}\,\mathbf{c}_{i}
is the first mass moment with
center of mass (COM)

\mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and rotational inertia
\mathbf{I}_{i}
expressed in the body frame.
The rotational inertia at the
COM
is obtained as

\mathbf{I}_{c}=\mathbf{I}-\tfrac{1}{m}[\mathbf{h}]_{\times}[\mathbf{h}]_{\times}^{\intercal}
, with
[\cdot]_{\times}
denoting the skew-symmetric operator.

Stacking all bodies’ parameters yields
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i})_{i=0}^{n_{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10{n_{b}}}
.
Following
[
1
]
, the generalized torques can be then expressed as an affine function of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})=\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\dot{v}})\boldsymbol{\pi},

(43)

where
\mathbf{Y}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{{n_{q}}\times 10{n_{b}}}
denotes the joint-torque regressor matrix.

The inertial parameters
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}
are
fully physically consistent

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
, if they satisfy:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad&\mathbf{I}_{c}\succeq\mathbf{0},\\
D_{x}<D_{y}+D_{z},\quad D_{y}<D_{x}+&D_{z},\quad D_{z}<D_{x}+D_{y}.\end{aligned}

(44)

where
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(D_{x},D_{y},D_{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
are the principal moments of inertia, obtained from
\mathbf{I}_{c}=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{D}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}^{\intercal}
with
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}\in SO(3)
.
The condition
\mathbf{I}_{c}\succeq\mathbf{0}
ensures that the principal components of inertia are nonnegative, while the triangle inequalities guarantee the
physical realization
of the second moments of mass, i.e., consistency with a positive mass distribution
[
45
]
.
These inequalities can be compactly written as
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
, where
\mathbf{L}=(L_{x},L_{y},L_{z})
denotes the second moment of inertia, related to the principal moments by

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{L}\right),\,\,\text{with}\,\,\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&1\\
1&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

To enforce these conditions by construction, we adopt the
Exponential–Eigenvalue
(EE) parametrization
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\exp(\sigma_{m}),\quad\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}=\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\exp(\sigma_{x})&\exp(\sigma_{y})&\exp(\sigma_{z})\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal},

where
\mathrm{Exp}
is the exponential map of the
SO(3)
.
This parametrization enforces positivity by construction, yields smooth derivatives, and admits a Lie algebra representation suitable for interpretable priors (see
Figure

6
).
The inertial parameters are finally reparametrized as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{m}&h_{x}&h_{y}&h_{z}&\omega_{x}&\omega_{y}&\omega_{z}&\sigma_{x}&\sigma_{y}&\sigma_{z}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{10},

(47)

where
(\sigma_{m},\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
ensures positivity through exponentiation,
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{x},\omega_{y},\omega_{z})
are the rotation parameters in
\mathfrak{so}(3)
, and
(h_{x},h_{y},h_{z})
define the first mass moment.

VI-A
1
Derivatives of the inertial parametrization

The derivatives
\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}
are obtained in closed form by grouping contributions per parameter block.
The mass and first mass moment give

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m}{\partial\sigma_{m}}

\displaystyle=m,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}}{\partial(h_{x},h_{y},h_{z})}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{3}.

(48)

For the rotational inertia, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}}{\partial\sigma_{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}}{\partial m}\frac{\partial m}{\partial\sigma_{m}}=-\frac{1}{m}[\mathbf{h}]_{\times}[\mathbf{h}]_{\times}^{\intercal},

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}}{\partial h_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial([\mathbf{h}]_{\times}[\mathbf{h}]_{\times}^{\intercal})}{\partial h_{k}},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\Bigl(2h_{k}\mathbf{1}_{3}-\mathbf{e}_{k}\mathbf{h}^{\intercal}-\mathbf{h}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\intercal}\Bigr),\qquad k\in\{x,y,z\},

where
\mathbf{e}_{x},\mathbf{e}_{y},\mathbf{e}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the canonical basis vectors.
For the rotation parameters, if we denote
\mathbf{J}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
as the left Jacobian of
SO(3)
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{J}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\,\mathbf{e}_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\omega_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}[\mathbf{u}_{i}]_{\times},\qquad i\in\{x,y,z\}.

(50)

Since
\mathbf{I}_{c}=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{D}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}^{\intercal}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}}{\partial\omega_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}_{c}}{\partial\omega_{i}}=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}[\mathbf{u}_{i}]_{\times}\mathbf{D}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}^{\intercal}+\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{D}[\mathbf{u}_{i}]_{\times}^{\intercal}\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}^{\intercal},\qquad i\in\{x,y,z\}.

(51)

For the exponential eigenvalues, from
Equation

46
we have

\mathbf{L}=(L_{x},L_{y},L_{z})
with
L_{\alpha}=\exp(\sigma_{\alpha})
and

\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{L})
.
Hence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{L}}{\partial\sigma_{x}}

\displaystyle=[L_{x},0,0]^{\intercal},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{L}}{\partial\sigma_{y}}

\displaystyle=[0,L_{y},0]^{\intercal},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{L}}{\partial\sigma_{z}}

\displaystyle=[0,0,L_{z}]^{\intercal},

(52)

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial\sigma_{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(0,L_{x},L_{x}),\quad\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial\sigma_{y}}=\mathrm{diag}(L_{y},0,L_{y}),

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial\sigma_{z}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(L_{z},L_{z},0),

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}}{\partial\sigma_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial\sigma_{\alpha}}\,\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{R}}^{\intercal},\qquad\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}.

(54)

The Jacobian
\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times 10}
is finally obtained by stacking,
for each parameter in
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}
, the derivative of
m
,
\mathbf{h}
, and each of the six independent entries of rotational inertia.

VI-B

Actuation and Joint-Friction Parameters

The mapping from commanded inputs to generalized torques is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})\;=\;\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\,\Big(\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\gamma})\Big),

(55)

where
\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is the parameterized selection matrix mapping actuated joints to generalized coordinates,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}
denotes the commanded effort (e.g., motor torque), and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\gamma})
represents the joint friction coefficients.
In adition to identifying the friction model, parametrizing the elements of
\mathbf{S}
enables identification of actuation coefficients such as gear ratios or momentum-thrust coupling in systems like quadrotors or underwater vehicles.

Unlike classical approaches, we explicitly model the full nonlinear joint friction using the smooth and differentiable formulation proposed in
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\tau_{f}(v,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\;=\;

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}\!\left(\tanh(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}v)-\tanh(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}v)\right)

(56)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{3}\tanh(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{4}v)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{5}v,

where
\tau_{f}
,
v
denote the joint friction and velocity, respectively.
This compact model captures stiction, Coulomb, viscous, and Stribeck friction within a single smooth formulation.
Its analytical derivatives make it particularly suitable for gradient-based system identification.

Figure

7
illustrates the contributions of each friction components.

Fig. 7
:

Visualization of friction models evaluated at different smoothing coefficients.
The friction model remains smooth and differentiable while capturing the Coulomb, viscous, and Stribeck effects.

Despite its expressiveness, the model in
Equation

56
does not explicitly enforce physical consistency, and may therefore allow parameter values under which friction appears to generate, rather than dissipate energy.
To ensure
physical plausibility
, we impose constraints that guarantee friction always resists motion:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{3},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{4},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{5}\geq 0,\qquad\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}\geq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2},

(57)

where positivity is enforced via exponential maps:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}=\mathrm{exp}(\mu_{0}),\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}=\mathrm{exp}(\mu_{1}),\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{3}=\mathrm{exp}(\mu_{3}),

(58)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{4}=\mathrm{exp}(\mu_{4}),\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{5}=\mathrm{exp}(\mu_{5}),

with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\mu_{3}&\mu_{4}&\mu_{5}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
are joint-friction parameters, and the inequality
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}\!\geq\!\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}
is handled using the stagewise optimizer described in
Section

VIII
.

VI-B
1
Derivatives of the joint friction

We require the derivatives of the friction torque

\tau_{f}(v,\boldsymbol{\gamma})
with respect to the joint velocity
v
and the
actuation parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}
.
The velocity derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial v}=-\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}c_{1}(v)+\gamma_{0}\gamma_{2}c_{2}(v)+\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}c_{4}(v)+\gamma_{5}.

with
c_{i}(v)\coloneqq 1-\tanh^{2}(\gamma_{i}v)
for
i\in\{1,2,4\}
.
The derivatives with respect to the joint-friction parameters are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{0}}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\tanh(\gamma_{1}v)-\tanh(\gamma_{2}v)\Big),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{1}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{0}\,v\,c_{1}(v),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\,v\,c_{2}(v),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{3}}

\displaystyle=\tanh(\gamma_{4}v),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{4}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{3}\,v\,c_{4}(v),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\gamma_{5}}

\displaystyle=v.

(59)

To account for the use of exponential mappings to enforce the constraints in
Equation

57
, the Jacobian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}
is obtained via the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}}=\frac{\partial\tau_{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}}.

(60)

Finally, the derivatives of the generalized
joint-friction effort
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=-\,\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})}{\partial\mathbf{v}},

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{S}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})\bigr)-\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}},

where
\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}/\partial\mathbf{v}
and

\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}
are assembled from the scalar expressions above for each actuated joint.

Estimating joint friction jointly with inertia parameters is challenging; we therefore adopt an energy-based approach, described in the next section.

VII
Energy-based observations

The principle of energy conservation can be leveraged by introducing energy-based observations in
SysID
.
This principle states that the total mechanical energy of a system remains constant in the absence of external work.
However, since the total energy cannot be directly measured, nor accurately computed without known physical parameters, it is more practical to express this relationship in terms of actuation power
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{a}^{b}\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\tau}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{input energy}}=\underbrace{T_{b}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}})-T_{a}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})}_{\text{change in mechanical energy}},

(62)

where
T=K+U\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the total mechanical energy of the system, composed of kinetic
K
and potential
U
terms, and the subscripts
a
and
b
correspond to the integration limits or discrete timesteps.
Therefore, we define an energy observation model as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{E}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})

\displaystyle=T^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}})-T(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{d}})+T_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})

(63)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\dot{v}};\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}),\quad\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{E}=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}\,dt,

\displaystyle T_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}(\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{{a}})\,dt,

where
\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{E}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the observed input power, obtained from the joint observations, and
T_{f}
is the dissipative energy due to joint-friction effects computed from the actuation model.
This observation enables the identification of trajectories whose rate of change of mechanical energy is consistent not only with the supplied power, but also with a joint-friction model that accurately captures dissipative effects.

We apply the chain rule to derive the analytical derivatives of
Equation

63
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}_{E}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial\mathbf{x}}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}\\
\frac{\partial T_{f}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}_{E}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{\partial T^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}_{E}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T^{\prime}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+(\frac{\partial T^{\prime}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}})\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\frac{\partial T_{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(66)

with:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mathbf{q}}+\frac{\partial U}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\\
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\frac{\partial T}{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}+\frac{\partial U}{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are the Jacobians of the hybrid dynamics
\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\dot{v}};\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}})\right)
and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\pi}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the derivative of the inertial parametrization, which are described in
Section

IV
and
Section

VI-A1
, respectively.
Instead, to compute the derivatives of the kinetic and potential energies, we first introduce the corresponding energy regressors
[
17
]
.

VII-A

Energy Regressors

The mechanical energy of a rigid-body system is defined as sum of its kinetic and potential energy, where

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\pi})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q};\boldsymbol{\pi})\mathbf{v}

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{{n_{b}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\boldsymbol{v}_{k}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})

is the kinetic energy,
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
is the spatial velocity, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{k}
the spatial inertia of the
k
-th body
[
13
]
.
The potential energy is defined as:

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{q};\boldsymbol{\pi})=-\sum_{k=1}^{{n_{b}}}\mathbf{\hat{g}}^{\intercal}\ \mathbf{h}_{k}(\mathbf{q}),

(68)

where
\mathbf{\hat{g}}
is the gravity acceleration and
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}):\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
is the
k
-th body’s center of mass.

Similarly to the generalized torques, the total energy of the system (kinetic + potential) is affine to the inertial parameters, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v};\boldsymbol{\pi})&U(\mathbf{q};\boldsymbol{\pi})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Y}_{K}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{v})&\mathbf{Y}_{U}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\pi},

(69)

where
\mathbf{Y}_{K}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{10{n_{b}}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{U}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{10{n_{b}}}
are the kinetic and potential energy regressors, respectively.

VII-B

Analytical Derivatives of Energy Regressors

Deriving the first expression of
Equation

67
can be computationally expensive as it requires to perform a tensor-vector multiplication.
Instead, we derive the second expression as it exploits the structure of the dynamics and allows us to reuse computations, thus:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\\
\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{k=0}^{{n_{b}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}^{\intercal}\textbf{I}_{k}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\\

In general, identifying dynamic parameters requires processing a large number of data points, often on the order of hundred of thousands.
This, in turn, necessitates an efficient solution strategy for the resulting optimization problem.
Below, we describe an approach that exploits both the temporal and parametric structure of the problem through a parameterized Riccati recursion.

VIII

To leverage its temporal structure, we first analyze the optimality conditions through a Bellman perspective, which leads to an equality-constrained, parameterized Riccati recursion.
This recursion propagates a coupled state-parameter value function backward in time.

In this section, we analyze our
SysID
framework through a series of simulation and hardware experiments.
The experiments are designed to validate the framework’s ability to simultaneously estimate the state trajectory and physical parameters of robotic systems under realistic conditions, including noise, contact dynamics, and closed-loop constraints.
We compare the performance of the inverse dynamics formulation against the forward dynamics formulation, analyze the impact of incorporating parameter constraints, and demonstrate the benefits of energy-based observations for improving parameter identifiability.
Finally, we validate the framework on hardware platforms, including the Unitree B1 quadruped and Z1 manipulator, showcasing its practical applicability to real-world robotic systems.

IX-A

We evaluated the our
SysID

Reference trajectories were synthesized by solving the optimal-excitation program described in Appendix
B

Equation

5

8

This yielded ground-truth state and parameter-consistent signals against which we tested system identification as shown in
Figure

For each experiment, we provided the Bayesian estimator with a standard set of proprioceptive measurements:
(i) simulated joint encoders
(\mathbf{\hat{q}},\mathbf{\hat{v}})

(ii) commanded joint efforts
\boldsymbol{\hat{\tau}}

and (iii) a torso IMU (linear acceleration
\mathbf{\hat{a}}

\boldsymbol{\hat{\omega}}

Measurements were corrupted by zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariances chosen to mimic typical datasheets.
Formally, at each node

IX-B

Forward vs Inverse Dynamics Formulations

We compared forward dynamics and inverse dynamics formulations across five robotic systems, a double pendulum, manipulator, quadrotor, biped, and quadruped.
We provided the Bayesian estimator with the same measurements, noise, priors, horizon, without enforcing additional constraints.

Figure

10
summarizes normalized cost decay over iterations, state-trajectory error and parameter error.
Across all systems, inverse dynamics converges in fewer iterations than forward dynamics, with the gap most pronounced for contact-rich cases (biped, quadruped).
This is because inverse dynamics’ explicit handling of constraint consistency prevented the estimator from “bending” parameters to explain the mismatch between the dynamics and observations.

The trajectory error is often smaller under inverse dynamics, as forward dynamics more often compensates the mismatch between dynamics and observations by injecting process noise in the trajectory, resulting in higher costs and parameter errors.
This is because inverse dynamics decouples numerical integration from the system dynamics and preserves the affine dependence of the constraints on the inertial parameters, thereby improving numerical conditioning.
This also enforces contact consistency at each step, avoiding bias in both state and parameters.

IX-C

Benefits of Parameter Constraints

We compared our inverse-dynamics
SysID
approach with a parameter-constrained variant that enforces the parameter equalities/inequalities in
Equations

1e
and
1f
.
We chose to enforce left-right inertial symmetries on mirrored limbs and total mass constraints.
As reported in
Figure

SysID

SysID

However, the constrained formulation consistently yields smaller inertial‐parameter errors.
The advantage is most evident on symmetric platforms (biped, quadruped), where including the concept of discrete symmetries removes redundant directions
[
35

IX-D

We evaluated the impact of energy-based observation by averaging
100

The pendulum is already well conditioned, so the effect on trajectories is negligible, but the energy-based observations still reduced friction uncertainty by half.

IX-E

Comparison with Frequentist Identification Methods

To assess the contribution of energy information and the impact of our constrained Bayesian formulation, we compared three identification strategies: (i) a classical inverse-dynamics regressor (ID), (ii) an energy-based regressor using work–energy relations, and (iii) our proposed method, which combines inverse dynamics with energy-based observations inside a constrained Bayesian estimation framework.
All three methods used the same rigid-body model, excitation trajectories, and friction parameterization, and were evaluated under identical noise conditions.
Importantly, to ensure physical plausibility, both regression baselines were implemented using exponential–eigenvalue inertial parametrization, as in our Bayesian approach.

For the ID-regressor baseline, generalized torques were expressed as a linear function of inertial and actuation parameters, which were estimated by minimizing a least-squares discrepancy between measured and predicted torques.
Since this regressor depends on joint accelerations, accelerations were obtained via numerical differentiation of encoder measurements and filtered using a Savitzky–Golay filter, with the window length tuned empirically.
For the energy-based regressor, work–energy consistency was enforced over each time interval by matching the measured input work (integrated actuation power) to the model-predicted change in mechanical energy plus dissipated frictional energy.
This formulation provided an identification signal that was less sensitive to acceleration estimation and instead aggregated information across joints.
As shown in Table
II
, our approach consistently achieved the lowest identification error across all noise levels.
These results demonstrate that combining inverse-dynamics consistency with energy-based observations within a constrained Bayesian framework improves robustness and mitigates inertial–friction coupling while preserving physical consistency.

X
Conclusion

We introduced a Bayesian approach for system and disturbance identification that embeds inverse dynamics as hard equality constraints and enforces state, contact, loop–closure, and parameter-consistency constraints.
Our Riccati solver preserves the
SysID
structure with a linear-time backward complexity, keeps numerical integration decoupled from the hybrid and closed-loop dynamics, and accommodates physically consistent inertial and friction parameterizations.
We also incorporated energy-based observations that couple measured actuation power to changes in mechanical energy, providing complementary information when actuation sensing is limited or indirect.

In simulation across a range of systems, we showed that inverse dynamics increases the quality of the identified parameters and disturbances compared to forward dynamics.
Specifically, it consistently achieved lower parameter error, with the largest gains in contact-rich and underactuated regimes.
Moreover, enforcing parameter constraints such as symmetry and total mass improved inertial estimates without sacrificing trajectory fit, while energy observations further enhanced identifiability—most notably reducing friction error—with minimal impact on trajectory accuracy.
On hardware, experiments trails on a Unitree B1 equipped with a Z1 arm showed the importance of simultaneously modeling foot contacts and closed-loop leg kinematics, recovering physically plausible inertial and friction parameters, and maintaining consistency with measured input-power balances during motion.

Overall, our work provides a principled and scalable route to physically consistent joint localization and system identification.
By unifying dynamics, constraints, and energy information within a single optimization program, it moves beyond decoupled pipelines and supports accurate model construction for agile manipulation and locomotion.
