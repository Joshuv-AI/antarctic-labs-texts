Near-optimal pure state estimation with adaptive Fisher-symmetric measurements
C. Vargas, A. Delgado, L. Pereira

Abstract
Quantum state estimation is important for various quantum information processes, including quantum communications, computation, and metrology, which require the characterization of quantum states for evaluation and optimization. We present a three-stage adaptive method for estimating arbitrary d d -dimensional pure quantum states using locally informationally complete Fisher symmetric measurements (FSM) and a single-shot measurement basis. We derive finite-sample high-probability error bounds for the protocol and demonstrate that our approach scales as O ⁡ ( d / N ) O(d/N) for large sample sizes, thereby guaranteeing the advantage of adaptation. Moreover, numerical simulations indicate that the protocol achieves an average infidelity close to the optimal given by the Gill-Massar lower bound (GMB). The total number of measurement outcomes scales linearly with 7 ​ d − 3 7d-3 , avoiding the need for collective measurements on multiple copies of the unknown state. This work highlights the potential of adaptive estimation techniques in quantum state characterization while maintaining efficiency in the number of measurement outcomes.

FSMs use the smallest possible total number of measurement outcomes and achieve the best possible accuracy through separable measurements on a sample of equally prepared copies of the unknown state. In this Letter, we propose a three-stage adaptive pure-state estimation method based on three FSMs and a single-shot measurement basis. This method also achieves the GMB and, apart from the purity of the unknown state, requires no other a priori information. In this way, we extend the applicability of FSMs to any unknown state. We first show that for a given fiducial state, it is possible to construct two FSMs that estimate all pure states together except those that are orthogonal to the fiducial state. To avoid this possibility, we introduce a preliminary stage in which we use an arbitrary single-shot measurement basis on the unknown state and select the fiducial state as the one measured. This procedure generates a first estimate of the unknown state that is sufficiently close to it to serve as a fiducial state for a third near-optimal FSM. We derive explicit high-probability bounds on reconstruction infidelity, showing that it scales as
O(d/N)
for sufficiently large sample sizes. In addition, we quantify the impact of fiducial overlap and approximation error. The total number of measurement outcomes of our method scales linearly as

Fisher Symmetric Measurements.—
Gill-Massar bound (GMB) establishes a fundamental limitation on the estimation of a state
\ket*{\Psi}
when only individual measurements are performed. Let
I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})=1-|\innerproduct*{\hat\Psi}{\Psi}|^{2}
be the infidelity between
\ket*{\Psi}
and its estimator
\ket*{\hat\Psi}
. According to the GMB, the average infidelity in the set of estimates
\Lambda
is lower bounded by

\bar{I}(\Psi)=\int_{\Lambda}{\rm d}\hat{\Psi}I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\geq\bar{I}_{\rm GM}=\frac{d-1}{N}.

(1)

A protocol is optimal when it saturates the GMB. Fisher Symmetric Measurements (FSM) achieve this. Let us consider the
d
-dimensional unknown pure state

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=a_{0}|0\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}z_{k}|k\rangle,

(2)

with
z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
,
|z_{k}|^{2}\ll 1
, and
a_{0}^{2}=1-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|z_{k}|^{2}\approx 1
, so that it lies in the neighborhood of the state
|0\rangle
, and an arbitrary POVM of
n
rank-1 operators
\mathcal{E}=\{|\varphi^{\alpha}\rangle\langle\varphi^{\alpha}|\}_{\alpha=1}^{n}
, with
|\varphi^{\alpha}\rangle=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}|0\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}|k\rangle
,
\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\geq 0
and
\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}

[
71
,
32
]
. POVM
\mathcal{E}
is an FSM for state
\ket*{\Psi}
if it saturates the GMB of Eq. (
1
) and if all parameters
\{z_{k}\}
are determined with the same precision. Both conditions are fulfilled when
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}=0
for
j,k\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
and
n\geq 2d-1
. According to this, FSM achieves optimal accuracy in estimating pure states, that is,
\bar{I}(\Psi)\approx\bar{I}_{\rm GM}
, with a minimal number of outcomes,
n=2d-1
.

FSMs present two drawbacks. First, there is no analytical inversion method to derive the estimator of
\ket*{\Psi}
from the FSM
\mathcal{E}
, which means that a statistical inference tool, such as the maximum likelihood estimation, must be used to obtain it. Second, FSMs are locally informationally complete, that is, they can only accurately reconstruct states close to
|0\rangle
. It remains unclear how close a state
\ket*{\Psi}
needs to be to
\ket*{0}
to be effectively estimated. In the Supplemental Material (SM)
[
1
]
, we address both drawbacks. First, we present an analytical procedure to obtain a FSM estimator from the measurement statistics. Second, we demonstrate that to estimate a state with an average infidelity near the GMB, it must have an overlap satisfying
a_{0}^{2}\gg 1-\sqrt{2/(2d-1)}
. This implies that as the dimensions increase, the overlap between
\ket*{\Psi}
and
\ket*{0}
has to increase with
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{1/d})
. If this condition is met, the FSM estimator guarantees infidelity such as
\mathcal{O}(d/a_{0}^{2}N)
. Otherwise, the error due to approximations dominates, fundamentally upper-bounding the accuracy by

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\kappa^{2}=\left(1-a_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}\frac{2d-1}{2},

(3)

which is independent of the sample size
N
and could be far from the GMB.

Adaptive Fisher Symmetric Measurements.—
We consider two FSMs given by POVMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\{|\varphi^{\alpha}_{\pm}\rangle\langle\varphi^{\alpha}_{\pm}|\}_{\alpha=1}^{n}
with

|\varphi^{\alpha}_{\pm}\rangle=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}|0\rangle\pm\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}|k\rangle,

(4)

where the coefficients
\{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\}
satisfy the conditions of being an FSM. Each of these FSMs estimates pure states in a neighborhood of the fiducial state
|0\rangle
. However, we showed that, taken together, they can estimate an arbitrary pure state
|\Psi\rangle
except those orthogonal to the fiducial one. In fact, from the measurement statistic
P_{\pm}^{\alpha}=|\langle\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}|\Psi\rangle|^{2}
we have that

\displaystyle a_{0}z_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)(P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha}),

(5)

which together with the normalization condition
\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|z_{k}|^{2}=1-a_{0}^{2}
allows us to directly determine a first estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
of the state
|\Psi\rangle
, except when
|\Psi\rangle
has no overlap with the fiducial state
|0\rangle
. The latter case can be avoided by introducing a single-shot measurement on an arbitrary basis. The outcome obtained from this measurement has non-null overlap with the state
|\Psi\rangle
, so it can be used as a fiducial state to construct the FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
. Notice that this adaptation does not ensure that the unknown state
|\Psi\rangle
is close to the fiducial state, so with high probability
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
are non-optimal for
\ket*{\Psi}
. However, this guarantees that both non-optimal FSMs together are informationally complete to estimate
\ket*{\Psi}
, providing an estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
with infidelity
\mathcal{O}(d/a_{0}^{4}N)
for almost all states except when
a_{0}^{2}\approx 1/2
, where the infidelity scales in the worst case as
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/a_{0}^{6}N)
. For a random state such as
a_{0}^{2}\sim 1/d
, the infidelity scales as
O(d^{3}/N)
. Another observation is that a new POVM with
4d-2
outcomes can be generated by combining POVMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
by flipping a coin with probabilities
p_{\pm}
. This is a new FSM that estimates states close to the fiducial state and achieves the GMB. The demonstrations are presented in the SM
[
1
]
.

The estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
obtained from the FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
allows us to perform a near-optimal estimation of
|\Psi\rangle
by adapting the FSM
\mathcal{E}
. Let
U
be a unitary operator that transforms the fiducial state
|0\rangle
into the estimate of two-FSMs, that is,
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle=U|0\rangle
. The unitary
U
adapts the FSM
\mathcal{E}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\{U|\varphi^{\alpha}\rangle\langle\varphi^{\alpha}|U^{\dagger}\}_{\alpha=1}^{n}
, which has as fiducial state
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
.
As long as the estimation with two non-optimal FSMs is performed with a sufficient sample size, the estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
is close enough to the unknown state
|\Psi\rangle
to ensure that the adapted FSM
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is near-optimal.
The complete estimation method reads as follows:

(0)

Single-shot measurement on an arbitrary basis to select a fiducial state that has a nonzero overlap with the unknown pure state
|\Psi\rangle
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a).

(1)

Acquisition of data through FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
to obtain probabilities
P_{\pm}^{\alpha}
from a sample size
N_{1}
. These probabilities are used to obtain an estimate of
|\Psi\rangle
by solving the system of equations  (
5
). This preliminary estimate serves as a starting point for maximum likelihood estimation
[
23
,
56
]
, generating the first estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
. This is shown in Fig.
1
(b).

(2)

Acquisition of data using an adapted FSM
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
to obtain probabilities
\tilde{P}^{\alpha}
from a sample size
N_{2}
. These probabilities, along with those from the previous step
P_{\pm}^{\alpha}
, are used to derive a final estimate
|\hat{\Psi}\rangle
with
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
samples using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
[
56
]
, with the first estimate
|\tilde{\Psi}\rangle
serving as the starting point for optimization. This is shown in Fig.
1
(c).

Considering a concentration inequality for vector-valued
[
11
]
and multinomial
[
64
,
39
]
random variables, and supposing Gaussian distributed parameters
[
77
,
27
,
10
,
66
,
7
,
30
]
, we derive a finite-sample high-probability bound on the infidelity of the MLE estimator
\ket*{\hat\Psi}
. The derivation is given in the SM
[
1
]
. This states that given a sufficiently large sample size
N
so that the approximation error
\kappa
is negligible, with probability
1-\delta
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq&\frac{d-1}{N_{2}}+\frac{1}{N_{2}}\left(\sqrt{2(d-1)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}+\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right).\end{split}

(6)

The first term corresponds to the Gill-Massar lower bound, while the second term represents a deviation that scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}/N_{2})
. Setting
N_{2}=N/Z
, with
Z>1
, the total infidelity is therefore guaranteed to scale almost as
\mathcal{O}(Zd/N)
. If the sample is divided into half, that is
Z=2
, then the scaling is
\mathcal{O}(2d/N)
. Although conservative, this bound provides rigorous finite-sample performance and certifies the advantage of AFSM for large enough
N
. For smaller sample sizes, approximation errors may become non-negligible, and attaining the Gill-Massar bound cannot be rigorously ensured. Nevertheless, given the conservative nature of Eq. (
6
), this does not preclude improved performance in this regime, which must instead be assessed numerically. In addition, it can be shown that a linear estimator leads to the bound
I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq O(d/N_{2})
, which is consistent with the bound for the MLE estimator. See SM
[
1
]
for details.

Conclusions.—
We introduce a three-stage adaptive estimation method for pure
d
-dimensional states, which is based on FSMs. We derived a high-probability error bound for infidelity that guarantees an advantage with adaptation for a sufficiently large sample size, scaling as
\mathcal{O}(d/N)
close to the GMB. In addition, numerical simulations suggest that AFSM provides an advantage and approaches the GMB across all sample regimes. Thus, our three-stage adaptive estimation method achieves a nearly optimal estimation accuracy.

Estimation using two non-optimal FSMs and AFSM offers advantages in infidelity scaling and the number of outcomes or bases compared with other well-known estimation methods for pure states
[
53
,
73
,
34
,
61
,
67
,
6
,
74
]
. A paradigmatic example is the 5-bases-based tomography (5BBT)
[
17
,
72
]
, which, with measurements in 5 bases, allows the estimation of a pure state of any dimension with an average infidelity scaling as
\mathcal{O}(d^{1.87}/N)
. The two non-optimal FSMs can be implemented with 4 bases
[
31
]
, one fewer than 5BBT, or with a single FSM having more measurement outcomes. Therefore, the estimate obtained after stage (1) achieves better accuracy than the one obtained by 5BBT, and with a smaller number of measurement outcomes. Furthermore, the third AFSM can be replaced by two measurement bases, yielding a total of 7 bases for our method. This indicates that, with 2 additional bases beyond the 5 used by the 5BBT, it is possible to estimate pure states at the GMB. Another example is projected least squares tomography (PLST)
[
19
]
, which employs global 2-designs to estimate rank-one density matrices with a trace-norm error upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d\log d/N})
. This implies that the infidelity is also upper-bounded by the same quantity, so our method provides a quadratic advantage in sample size
N
. In addition, implementing 2-design requires either a
d^{2}
-outcome SIC-POVM or
d+1
MUBs, which is far more costly than implementing the AFSMs via 3 POVMs with
2d-1
outcome or 7 bases. All of this indicates that AFSM combines near-optimal accuracy with reduced measurement overhead, providing an efficient approach for the characterization and benchmarking of quantum hardware.

The GMB can be reached by a sequence of FSMs, where the next FSM uses the estimate of the previous one as its fiducial state. Similarly, pure-state estimation can be posed as an infidelity-optimization problem, which is also solved by a sequence of adapted measurement bases that converge to the GMB
[
13
,
65
,
75
,
18
,
12
]
. In both cases, the GMB is reached after a series of iterations that require many more measurements than our method, which uses just two adaptation steps, one to set the fiducial state and the other to adapt the FSM. It is possible to reduce the number of FSMs even further to one while achieving the GMB, at the expense of collective measurements across multiple copies of the unknown state, which is not required by our method.

Integrated photonic processors (IPPs)
[
50
,
5
]
are ideal for the experimental testing of AFSMs. These devices, fabricated via the femtosecond laser-writing technique, are universal networks of reconfigurable beam splitters implemented by Mach-Zehnder interferometers controlled by thermal-phase shifters. These act onto single-photon states that encode spatial qudits across many spatial modes. Universality and reconfigurability of IPPs allow AFSMs to be implemented through measurement bases or POVMs via a direct-sum representation
[
36
]
. Quantum computers can also implement AFSMs thanks to recent advances in the optimal implementation of POVMs
[
70
,
54
,
57
,
24
,
59
]
.

An interesting possible extension of our method is the estimation of pure states affected by a depolarizing channel. We explore this possibility in the SM
[
1
]
and find that, by including a measurement in the computational basis, the depolarizing parameter can be estimated. Subsequently, the pure state prior to depolarization can be estimated from the statistics of the FSMs. Numerical simulations indicate that this state can be estimated at twice the GMB under weak depolarizing noise. Furthermore, it has recently been shown that optimal infidelity scaling can be achieved to estimate a density matrix by first purifying and then applying a tomographic method for rank-one density matrices
[
49
]
. Our method can be combined with this strategy to achieve near-optimal infidelity estimation for arbitrary mixed states with respect to the Gill-Massar bound. Another extension would be the introduction of classical shadows
[
2
,
68
]
to reduce the complexity of data acquisition, and tensor networks
[
9
,
29
,
3
]
or neural networks
[
63
,
55
]
to reduce the computational cost of maximum likelihood estimation.

Appendix A
Quantum State Estimation

This section presents an introduction to the estimation of the quantum state, which refers to a collection of statistical methods used to reconstruct the density matrix of a quantum system from measurement data
[
62
]
. Furthermore, it establishes fundamental limits on the accuracy of such estimations through the Cramér-Rao bound and the Gill-Massar inequality.

A.1
Cramér-Rao bound

Consider a
d
-dimensional quantum system in a state
\rho(\bm{s})
characterized by the parameters
\bm{s}=\matrixquantity[\lx@physics@matrix s_1, \cdots, s_n\endlx@physics@matrix]^{\top}
, which we aim to estimate. Given a set of measurement operators
\{E_{i}\}
that satisfies the completeness relation
\sum_{i}E_{i}=I
, the probability of obtaining the outcome
i
in a measurement is given by

\displaystyle p(i|\bm{s})=\Tr\big(E_i\rho(\bm{s})\big).

(8)

From the experiment, we obtain estimates
\bm{\hat{s}}(x)=[\hat{s}_{1}(x),\cdots,\hat{s}_{n}(x)]^{\top}
of the original parameters, which are a function of the experimental results. The uncertainty of the estimation of the parameters
\bm{s}
is given by the covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{ij}=Cov(\hat{s}_{i},\hat{s}_{j})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{s}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{s}_{i})\right)\left(\hat{s}_{j}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{s}_{j})\right)\right].

(9)

The Cramér-Rao inequality imposes a fundamental lower bound for
\mathbb{C}
. This is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\geq G^{\top}C^{-1}G,

(10)

where
G_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left(\hat{s}_{j}(x)\partialderivative{s_i}\ln p(x|\bm{s})\right)
and
C
is the classical Fisher information matrix

\displaystyle C_{ij}=\mathbb{E}\left(\partialderivative{s_i}\ln p(x|\bm{s})\partialderivative{s_j}\ln p(x|\bm{s})\right).

(11)

This can be used to measure how much information about
\bm{s}
is likely to be obtained from a random experiment. A specific case of this inequality arises when the estimator is unbiased, that is,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\hat{s}}(x)]=\bm{s}
. Then, the Cramer-Rao bound for an unbiased estimator becomes

\mathbb{C}\geq C^{-1}.

(12)

We will now extend this result to the quantum case. Substituting the probability distribution (
8
) into the classical Fisher information matrix (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{p(k|\bm{s})}\Tr\Big(E_k\pdv{\rho(\bm{s})}{s_i} \Big)\Tr\Big(E_k\pdv{\rho(\bm{s})}{s_j} \Big).

(13)

From this equation, we can see that the classical Fisher information matrix
C
depends on the quantum state
\rho
, but also the set of measurements
\{E_{k}\}
. Maximizing
C
on the set of quantum measurements
\{E_{k}\}
we obtain a superior limit for the classical Fisher information matrix
C
,

\displaystyle C\leq Q,

(14)

where
Q
is the quantum Fisher information matrix. This is explicitly given by

\displaystyle Q_{ij}(\bm{s})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Bigg(\rho(\bm{s})\Big[L_i(\bm{s}) L_j(\bm{s}) +L_j(\bm{s}) L_i(\bm{s}) \Big] \Bigg),

(15)

where
L_{i}(\bm{s})
are the
Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative
(SLD) operators, which are implicitly defined by

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho(\bm{s})}{s_i}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\rho(\bm{s})L_{i}(\bm{s})+L_{i}(\bm{s})\rho(\bm{s})\Big).

(16)

The quantum Fisher information matrix
Q
depends only on the quantum state of the system. It is the maximum amount of information that can be retrieved from the system via quantum measurements.

The Quantum Cramér-Rao bound establishes a fundamental limit on the precision of parameter estimation in quantum systems. This bound is expressed as an inequality for the covariance matrix
C
of an unbiased estimator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\geq C^{-1}\geq Q^{-1}.

(17)

Let
\mathbb{C}^{(N)}(\bm{\hat{s}})
be the covariance matrix and
C^{(N)}(\bm{s})
be the classical Fisher information matrix for an estimate with sample size
N
. The quantum Cramér-Rao bound becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{(N)}\geq\big[C^{(N)}\big]^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{N}Q^{-1},

(18)

where
Q
corresponds to the quantum Fisher information matrix for a single copy.

A.2
Gill-Massar inequality

The quantum Cramér-Rao bound sets the fundamental lower limit over all possible quantum measurements. However, it has been shown that in the context of quantum state estimation using individual measurement, that is, performing separate measurements on each copy of the state
\rho
, this bound cannot generally be saturated. In particular, no single POVM can produce a classical Fisher information matrix that matches the quantum Fisher information matrix for all parameters
[
15
]
.

In fact, the Gill-Massar inequality establishes a fundamental limitation on state estimation when only individual measurements are performed. It states that

\Tr\left(C^{(N)}Q^{-1}\right)\leq N(d-1),

(19)

where
d
is the dimension of the Hilbert space to which
\rho
belongs. Using this inequality, we can also derive a bound for the weighted mean squared error
\Tr(W\mathbb{C})
, with
W
being a positive weight matrix and
\mathbb{C}
the covariance matrix
[
78
]
. This is given by

\displaystyle\Tr(W \mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N(d-1)}\left(\Tr\sqrt{Q^{-1/2}WQ^{-1/2}}\right)^{2}.

(20)

The minimum is achieved when
\mathbb{C}=(C^{(N)})^{-1}
and when

\displaystyle C^{(N)}=N(d-1)Q^{1/2}\frac{\sqrt{Q^{-1/2}WQ^{-1/2}}}{\Tr\sqrt{Q^{-1/2}WQ^{-1/2}}}Q^{1/2}.

(21)

Taking into account
W=Q(\rho)/4
, the weighted mean squared error becomes the mean Bures distance, which is related to the mean infidelity
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
as

\displaystyle\Tr(W\mathbb{C})=\bar{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4N(d-1)},\qquad C=\frac{(d-1)}{n}Q,

(22)

with
n
the number of parameters that are estimated,
d
the dimension of the Hilbert space of the states to be estimated, and
N
the size of the ensemble. If we restrict ourselves to the estimation of pure states, which are defined by
n=2(d-1)
real parameters, the lower bound of the mean infidelity becomes

\bar{\mathcal{I}}(|\psi\rangle)_{pure}\geq\frac{d-1}{N}.

(23)

Thereby, we can use the average infidelity as a metric for the error in estimating pure states.

Appendix B
Fisher Symmetric Measurements

Consider an unknown quantum pure state
\ket*{\Psi(\bm{x})}
that depends on a vector of real parameters
\bm{x}
and is close to a known fiducial pure state
\ket*{0}
. This state can be written as

\ket*{\Psi(\bm{x})}=a_{0}(\bm{x})\ket*{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(x_{j0}+ix_{j1})\ket*{j},

(24)

with
\bm{x}=[x_{0,0},x_{0,1},\cdots,x_{d-1,0},x_{d-1,1}]^{\top}
and
|x_{k\sigma}|\ll 1
infinitesimal real parameters. The parameter
a_{0}\approx 1
is the overlap with the fiducial state and is explicitly given by

\displaystyle a_{0}(\bm{x})=\sqrt{1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{1}(x_{j\sigma})^{2}}.

(25)

Writing the pure state
\ket*{\Psi(\bm{x})}
as a density matrix and retaining only the linear order in
x_{j\sigma}
, we obtain

\rho(\bm{x})=\ket*{0}\bra*{0}+\sum_{j\sigma}x_{j\sigma}X_{j\sigma},

(26)

where

X_{j\sigma}=(-i)^{\sigma}(\ket*{0}\bra*{j}+(-1)^{\sigma}\ket*{j}\bra*{0}).

(27)

These hermitian operators satisfy the orthogonality condition
Tr(X_{j\sigma}X_{k\tau})=2\delta_{jk}\delta_{\sigma\tau}
. For simplicity, we are going to omit the dependence on
\bm{x}
except when it is strictly necessary.

A positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
[
45
]
is a Fisher symmetric measurement (FSM)
[
31
]
if it saturates the Gill-Massar Inequality
[
15
]

tr(Q^{-1}C)=d-1,

(28)

and the corresponding classical Fisher information matrix (FIM) is as evenly distributed as possible over all parameters. Both conditions are fulfilled when the classical FIM
C
is related to the quantum FIM
Q
by
[
31
]

\displaystyle C=\frac{1}{2}Q.

(29)

In the following sections, we calculate both quantum and classical Fisher information matrices for the state (
24
) and find the conditions for a POVM to be a FSM.

B.1
Quantum Fisher Information

The quantum FIM of the state
\rho
is a symmetric matrix
Q
with elements

Q_{j\sigma,k\tau}=\frac{1}{2}tr[\rho(L_{j\sigma}L_{k\tau}+L_{k\tau}L_{j\sigma})],

(30)

where
\{L_{j\sigma}\}
are the symmetric logarithmic derivative operators, implicitly determined by the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho(\bm{x})}{\partial x_{j\sigma}}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{j\sigma}\rho+\rho L_{j\sigma}).

(31)

When the state is pure, that is,
\rho(\bm{x})=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, the quantum Fisher information matrix is simply given by

\displaystyle Q_{j\sigma,k\tau}=4{\rm Re}\left[\langle\partial_{j\sigma}\psi|\partial_{k\tau}\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\partial_{j\sigma}\psi\rangle\langle\partial_{k\tau}\psi|\psi\rangle\right],

(32)

where

\displaystyle|\partial_{j\sigma}\psi\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j\sigma}}|\psi\rangle.

(33)

Thus, the quantum Fisher information matrix of the state
|\Psi(x)\rangle
is

\displaystyle Q_{j\sigma,k\tau}=2tr(X_{j\sigma}X_{k\tau})=4\delta_{j\sigma,k\tau},

(34)

or equivalently
Q(\rho)=4\mathbb{I}_{2d-2}
.

B.2
Classical Fisher Information

Consider an arbitrary POVM with
n
rank-1 elements,
\mathcal{E}=\{E^{\alpha}=\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}\bra*{\varphi^\alpha}\}
, where

\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\ket*{k}.

(35)

with
\{\beta_{k}^{\alpha}\}
and
\{\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}\}
being real coefficients. Taking into account the completeness condition,
\sum_{\alpha}E^{\alpha}=\mathbb{I}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}E^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{k,j}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}-i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\ket*{k}\bra*{j}

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{k,j}\left(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}\beta_{j}^{\alpha}+\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}+i(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}-\beta_{k}^{\alpha}\gamma_{j}^{\alpha})\right)\ket*{k}\bra*{j}\equiv\sum_{k,j}\delta_{k,j}\ket*{k}\bra*{j}.

(37)

Defining the
n
-dimensional real vectors
\bm{\beta}_{k}=[\beta_{k}^{1},\dots,\beta_{k}^{n}]^{\top}
and
\bm{\gamma}_{k}=[\gamma_{k}^{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}^{n}]^{\top}
, the completeness condition becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\beta}_{k}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{j}+\bm{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{j}&=\delta_{k,j},\quad j,k=0,\dots,d-1,\\
\bm{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{k}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{j}&=0,\quad j,k=0,\dots,d-1.\end{split}

(38)

where the centered dot represents the inner product of the real vectors. Since we can choose the global phase of each POVM vector
\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}
, we can set the constant accompanying the vector
\ket*{0}
to be real, i.e.
\gamma_{0}=0
,

\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\ket*{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\ket*{k}.

(39)

With this, we also have the following conditions for
\beta_{0}
, where we define the real vector
\bm{\beta}_{0}=[\beta_{0}^{1},\cdots,\beta_{0}^{n}]^{\top}

\begin{split}\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{0}&=1\\
\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{j}&=\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{j}=0,\quad j=1,...,d-1.\end{split}

(40)

The classical FIM of the state
\ket*{\Psi}
when measured by
\mathcal{E}
is

C_{j\sigma,k\tau}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{p(\alpha|x)}\frac{\partial p(\alpha|x)}{\partial x_{j\sigma}}\frac{\partial p(\alpha|x)}{\partial x_{k\tau}},

(41)

where
p(\alpha|x)
is the probability distribution. Calculating the probabilities for the POVM
\mathcal{E}
, we obtain

\displaystyle p(\alpha|x)

\displaystyle=Tr(E^{\alpha}\rho(x)),

\displaystyle=Tr\left[E^{\alpha}\left(\ket*{0}\bra*{0}+\sum_{j\sigma}x_{j\sigma}X_{j\sigma}\right)\right],

(42)

\displaystyle=\bra*{0}E^{\alpha}\ket*{0}+\sum_{j\sigma}x_{j\sigma}(-i)^{\sigma}[\bra*{j}E^{\alpha}\ket*{0}+(-1)^{\sigma}\bra*{0}E^{\alpha}\ket*{j}].

(43)

Calculating the matrix elements of each operator
E^{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\bra*{0}E^{\alpha}\ket*{0}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{0}^{\alpha})(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}-i\gamma_{0}^{\alpha})=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2},

(44)

\displaystyle\bra*{j}E^{\alpha}\ket*{0}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{j}^{\alpha})(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}-i\gamma_{0}^{\alpha})=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}),

(45)

\displaystyle\bra*{0}E^{\alpha}\ket*{j}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{0}^{\alpha})(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}-i\gamma_{j}^{\alpha})=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}-i\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}).

(46)

we can rewrite the probabilities as

\displaystyle p(\alpha|x)

\displaystyle=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}+\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\sum_{j}\left(x_{j0}\beta_{j}^{\alpha}+x_{j1}\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}\right)

(47)

Thereby, the derivatives of the probabilities are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(\alpha|x)}{\partial x_{j0}}=2\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\beta_{j}^{\alpha}

(48)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(\alpha|x)}{\partial x_{j1}}=2\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}

(49)

Replacing
p(\alpha|x)
and
\partial p(\alpha|x)/\partial x_{j\sigma}
in Eq.(
41
) and approximating to first order, we obtain the classical FIM

\begin{split}C_{j0,k0}&=4\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k},\\
C_{j1,k1}&=4\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k},\\
C_{j0,k1}&=4\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k},\end{split}\quad j,k=1,...,d-1.

(51)

B.3
Explicit construction of FSM

Imposing the relation between classical and quantum FIMs
C=Q/2
, we obtain the conditions

\displaystyle 2\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\delta_{jk},

(52)

\displaystyle 2\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}

\displaystyle=\delta_{jk},

(53)

\displaystyle 2\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

(54)

for
j,k=1,\dots,d-1
. Equivalently,

\begin{split}\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{0}&=1,\\
\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{j}&=\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{j}=\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}=0,\\
\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}&=\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk}.\end{split}

(55)

Thus, the set of
2d-1
real vectors
\{\bm{\beta}_{0},\sqrt{2}\bm{\beta}_{k},\sqrt{2}\bm{\gamma}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
forms an orthonormal set. Consequently, the number of elements of the POVM must satisfy
n\geq 2d-1
since the dimension of the vectors must be equal to or greater than the number of states to have an orthonormal set. This defines an efficient procedure for the construction of FSMs
[
31
]
, which involves generating a set of
n
real vectors of dimension
2d-1
and using their components to construct the vectors
\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}
. Following this procedure, we can construct the next minimal FSM with fiducial state
\ket*{0}
,

\displaystyle\ket*{\varphi^0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\ket*{0}\pm e^{i\pi/4}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\ket*{j}\right],

(56)

\displaystyle\ket*{\varphi^{2k-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\ket*{0}\pm e^{i\pi/4}\left(z\ket*{k}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}+1}\sum_{k\neq j=1}^{d-1}\ket*{j}\right)\right],

(57)

\displaystyle\ket*{\varphi^{2k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\ket*{0}\pm e^{i\pi/4}\left(z^{*}\ket*{k}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}+1}\sum_{k\neq j=1}^{d-1}\ket*{j}\right)\right],

(58)

where
k=1,...,d-1
and
z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}+1}-\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}e^{-i\pi/4}
.

In the following subsection, we present some side results about FSMs: 1) orthogonality relation between the coefficients of FSM, 2) including an arbitrary local phase in an FSM also yields an FSM, and 3) the combination of several FSMs is also a FSM.

B.3.1
Orthogonality relation

The conditions in Eq. (
38
) and (
55
) can be written more compactly, considering the complex coefficients
\omega_{k}^{\alpha}=\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\omega_{j}^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle 0,\quad j,k=1,\dots,d-1,

(59)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\omega_{j}^{\alpha*}=

\displaystyle\delta_{jk},\quad j,k=0,\dots,d-1.

(60)

The first equation represents the orthogonality condition between the real vectors used to construct the FSM, while the second is the completeness relation. Moreover, for
n>2d-1
we can complete the basis
\{\bm{\beta}_{0},\sqrt{2}\bm{\beta}_{j},\sqrt{2}\bm{\gamma}_{j}\}
with
n-2d+1
real vectors
\bm{\nu}_{j}
. Then, this new basis satisfies the following orthogonality relation,

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\beta_{0}^{\alpha^{\prime}}+2\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(\beta_{j}^{\alpha}\beta_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}+\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}\gamma_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}})+\sum_{j=1}^{n-2d+1}\nu_{j}^{\alpha}\nu_{j}^{\alpha^{\prime}}=\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}.

(61)

This expression leads to an upper bound for the norm of the elements of a FSM,

\displaystyle|\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}|_{2}^{2}=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}[(\beta_{j}^{\alpha})^{2}+(\gamma_{j}^{\alpha})^{2}]\leq\frac{1+(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}}{2}.

(62)

B.3.2
Arbitrary Phase FSM

In this section, we demonstrate that if
\mathcal{E}=\{\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}\bra*{\varphi^\alpha}\}
is an FSM, with
\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}
given by Eq. (
39
), then POVM
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{\ket*{\varphi'^\alpha}\bra*{\varphi'^\alpha}\}
, with

\displaystyle\ket*{\varphi'^\alpha}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\ket*{0}+e^{i\phi}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\ket*{k},\quad\phi\in\mathbb{R},

(63)

is also an FSM. Let us define the new coefficients

\displaystyle\beta_{k}^{\prime\alpha}

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\left(e^{i\phi}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\right)=\cos(\phi)\beta_{k}^{\alpha}-\sin(\phi)\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}.

(64)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}^{\prime\alpha}

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\left(e^{i\phi}(\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha})\right)=\cos(\phi)\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}+\sin(\phi)\beta_{k}^{\alpha},

(65)

and their respective real vectors
\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{k}=(\beta^{\prime 0}_{k},\dots,\beta^{\prime n}_{k})
and
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{k}=(\gamma^{\prime 0}_{k},\dots,\gamma^{\prime n}_{k})
.
Vectors
\bm{\beta}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\gamma}_{k}^{\prime}
satisfy the FSM conditions. In fact,

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

(66)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}-\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k})=0,

(67)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}+\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k})=0,

(68)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2(\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}-\sin(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j})\cdot(\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}+\cos(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}),

\displaystyle=(\cos(\phi)\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}+\cos^{2}(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\sin^{2}(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}-\cos(\phi)\sin(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k})=0,

(69)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{j}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2(\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}-\sin(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j})\cdot(\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{k}-\sin(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{k}),

\displaystyle=(\cos^{2}(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}-\sin(\phi)\cos(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}-\sin(\phi)\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}+\sin^{2}(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk}.

(70)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2(\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}+\cos(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j})\cdot(\sin(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{k}+\cos(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{k}),

\displaystyle=(\sin^{2}(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}+\sin(\phi)\cos(\phi)\bm{\beta}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k}+\sin(\phi)\cos(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}_{k}+\cos^{2}(\phi)\bm{\gamma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk}.

(71)

with
j,k=1,\ldots,d-1
. We can see that the new coefficients
\{\beta^{\prime\alpha}_{0},\beta^{\prime\alpha}_{j},\gamma^{\prime\alpha}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d-1}
of the new POVM satisfy the conditions to be a FSM, Eq. (
55
), therefore
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is also a FSM.

B.3.3
Collections of FSMs are also FSMs

In this section, we demonstrate that a collection of
m
FSMs
\{\mathcal{E}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
, with
\mathcal{E}_{i}=\{\ket*{\varphi_i^{\alpha}}\}_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}
,
n\geq 2d-1
,
\alpha=0,...,n-1
, and

\ket*{\varphi_i^{\alpha}}=\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha}\ket*{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\beta_{i,k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha})\ket*{k},\quad\beta_{i,0}^{l}>0,\quad\beta_{i,k}^{\alpha},\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R},

(72)

is also an FSM. The coefficients
\{\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha},\beta_{i,k}^{\alpha},\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha})\}
satisfy the FSM conditions, Eq. (
55
), for each
i=0,\dots,m-1
.

Consider POVM
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{\tau_{i}\ket*{\varphi_i^{\alpha}}\}
, with positive coefficients
\{\tau_{i}\}
such as
\sum_{i}|\tau_{i}|^{2}=1
. Defining the coefficient of each POVM element,

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\alpha}_{i,0}=

\displaystyle\tau_{i}\beta^{\alpha}_{i,0},

(73)

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\alpha}_{i,k}=

\displaystyle\tau_{i}\beta^{\alpha}_{i,k},

(74)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\alpha}_{i,k}=

\displaystyle\tau_{i}\gamma^{\alpha}_{i,k},

(75)

and their respective
mn-
dimensional vectors
\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{0}=(\beta^{\prime 0}_{0,0},\dots,\beta^{\prime n}_{m,0})
,
\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{k}=(\beta^{\prime 0}_{0,k},\dots,\beta^{\prime n}_{m,k})
,
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{0}=(\gamma^{\prime 0}_{0,k},\dots,\gamma^{\prime n}_{m,k})
, we can verify if
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
satisfies the conditions for being an FSM, Eq. (
55
),

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha}\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha}=1,

(76)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{0}\cdot\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha}\beta_{i,k}^{\alpha}=0,

(77)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{0}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\beta_{i,0}^{\alpha}\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha}=0,

(78)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\beta_{i,j}^{\alpha}\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha}=0,

(79)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{j}\cdot\bm{\beta}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\beta_{i,j}^{\alpha}\beta_{i,k}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk},

(80)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{j}\cdot\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}|\tau_{i}|^{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n_{i}-1}\gamma_{i,j}^{\alpha}\gamma_{i,k}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk}.

(81)

From these equations, we can see that the POVM
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
satisfies the FSM conditions. Thus, the set
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
corresponds to an FSM composed of
mn
elements.

B.4
Analytical reconstruction

In this section, we describe how to retrieve the parameters
\{x_{j\sigma}\}
from the probabilities
p(\alpha|x)
Eq. (
47
) after measuring a FSM.

Let us consider the following linear combination of the probabilities,

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}p_{\alpha},

(82)

with
\omega_{j}^{\alpha}=\beta_{j}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{j}^{\alpha}
the coefficients of the FSM. Using the conditions on a POVM to be an FSM, Eqs. (
59
) and (
60
), we obtain the following non-linear system of equations

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}=a_{0}(x_{j0}+ix_{j1}).

(83)

Combining this equation with the normalization condition, we obtain an equation only for the parameter
a_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}=a_{0}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{1}(x_{j\sigma})^{2}=a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2}).

(84)

Suppose that
a_{0}\approx 1
, we can write
a_{0}^{2}=1-\xi^{2}
, with
\xi\approx 0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}=(1-\xi^{2})\xi^{2}\approx\xi^{2}.

(85)

Therefore, we find that
a_{0}^{2}
is approximately given by

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}\approx 1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}.

(86)

Changing
a_{0}^{2}
in Eq. (
83
), we are able to isolate all parameters as

\displaystyle x_{j0}={\rm Re}\left(\frac{\Delta_{j}}{\sqrt{1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}}}\right),\qquad x_{j1}={\rm Im}\left(\frac{\Delta_{j}}{\sqrt{1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}}}\right).

(87)

B.5
Error analysis

The FSM allows us to obtain an estimator that saturates the Gill-Massar inequality and achieves a mean infidelity of
\bar{I}=(d-1)/N
. However, this result is an average across all possible estimators, so individual estimators may perform better or worse. Additionally, the infidelity of the estimator deteriorates as the overlap with the fiducial, denoted by
a_{0}
, decreases. Due to these two reasons, the infidelity in the estimation may deviate from the Gill-Massar bound. In this section, we derive a high-probability upper bound on infidelity in estimation with FSM, expressed in terms of the sample size
N
and the overlap
a_{0}
with the fiducial state.

B.5.1
Linear estimator

Recalling Eq. (
83
), we define the
(d-1)
-dimensional vector

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\Delta_{j}\ket*{j}.

(88)

Note that the vector
\ket*{\Phi}
is different from the state
\ket*{\Psi}
. Furthemore, its 2-norm is

\displaystyle||\ket*{\Phi}||_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}}=\sqrt{a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})}\leq\frac{1}{2},

(89)

so it is not a normalized vector, then it is not a quantum state either. However, it has the advantage of being expressed as a linear estimator of the probabilities,

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}p_{\alpha}\ket*{\phi_\alpha},

(90)

where
\{\ket*{\phi_\alpha}\}
is a set of known vectors, depending only on the FSM, given by

\displaystyle\ket*{\phi_\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\ket*{j}.

(91)

Moreover, we can interpret
\ket*{\Phi}
as the expected value of the vector-valued random variable
\{\ket*{\phi_\alpha}\}
, that is,

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=\mathbb{E}(\ket*{\phi_\alpha}).

(92)

Note that, according to Eq. (
87
), estimating
\ket*{\Phi}
is equivalent to
\ket*{\Psi}
. This equivalence allows us to upper bound the error in estimating
\ket*{\Psi}
by the error in estimating
\ket*{\Phi}
.

B.5.2
Upper-bound of error in 2-norm

Let
|\hat{\Psi}\rangle
be an estimate of
\ket*{\Psi}
. We will consider the 2-norm distance between
\ket*{\Psi}
and
|\hat{\Psi}\rangle
as the error metric. Defining
\ket*{\Psi_1}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(x_{j0}+ix_{j1})\ket*{j}
, we have that

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}\leq|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+|\ket*{\Psi_1}-|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle|_{2},

(93)

where
\hat{a}_{0}
and
|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle
are estimators of
a_{0}
and
\ket*{\Psi_1}
, respectively. Scaling by
a_{0}
, rearranging terms, and applying the triangular inequality, we find

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+\frac{1}{a_{0}}|a_{0}\ket*{\Psi_1}-a_{0}|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle|_{2}

(94)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+\frac{1}{a_{0}}|a_{0}\ket*{\Psi_1}+\hat{a}_{0}|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle-\hat{a}_{0}|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle-a_{0}|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle|_{2}

(95)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+\frac{1}{a_{0}}\left(|a_{0}\ket*{\Psi_1}-\hat{a}_{0}|\hat{\Psi}_{1}\rangle|_{2}+|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\right)

(96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{a_{0}}\right)|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+\frac{1}{a_{0}}|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2},

(97)

where
|\hat{\Phi}\rangle
is an estimator of
\ket*{\Phi}
. Thus, the error in estimating the state
\ket*{\Psi}
can be upper bounded in terms of the errors in estimating the coefficient
a_{0}
and the vector
\ket*{\Phi}
. Given that the coefficient
a_{0}
can be obtained from
\ket*{\Phi}
, their errors can also be related. Recalling that we can write
a_{0}^{2}\approx 1-\xi^{2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{1-\xi^{2}}-\sqrt{1-\hat{\xi}^{2}}\right|

(98)

\displaystyle\approx\left|(1-\xi^{2}/2)-(1-\hat{\xi}^{2}/2)\right|

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\xi^{2}-\hat{\xi}^{2}|,

(100)

with
\hat{\xi}^{2}
the estimation of
\xi^{2}
. Noticing from Eq. (
85
) that
\xi^{2}=\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}
and that
|\ket*{\Phi}|_{2}\leq 1/2
, we can upper bound the error in estimating
\xi^{2}
as follows,

\displaystyle|\xi^{2}-\hat{\xi}^{2}|=|\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}-\innerproduct*{\hat\Phi}{\hat\Phi}|\leq 2|\ket*{\Phi}|_{2}\times|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}.

(101)

Then, the error in estimating
a_{0}
is bounded by the error in estimating
\ket*{\Phi}
,

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}.

(102)

Using the previous equation in Eq. (
97
), we obtain the following.

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{a_{0}}|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}.

(103)

In conclusion, the error in estimating the state
\ket*{\Psi}
is upper-bounded by the error in estimating
\ket*{\Phi}
. Consequently, by constructing a concentration inequality for
\ket*{\Phi}
, we obtain a concentration inequality for the full state
\ket*{\Psi}
.

B.5.3
Confidence region

Confidence regions provide guarantees of the accuracy of a tomographic method as a function of the sample size
N
. These arise from interpreting the tomographic estimator as a sum of matrix-valued random variables and applying concentration inequalities
[
19
]
. In our case
\ket*{\Phi}
can be interpreted as a sum of vector-valued random variables
\ket*{\phi_\alpha}
, so we can employ the following concentration inequality
[
11
]
:

Let
\ket*{X_1},\dots,\ket*{X_N}
be
N
independent, zero-mean, vector-valued random variables. Then, the following concentration inequality holds for any
\epsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ket*{X_i}\right|_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 8\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}w\left(\epsilon\frac{L}{\sigma}\right)\right),

(104)

where
w(x)=\frac{3}{3+x}
and

\displaystyle L\geq|\ket*{X_i}|_{2},\qquad\sigma^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[|\ket*{X_i}|_{2}^{2}].

(105)

We can apply this concentration inequality to the FSM case by setting
\ket*{X_i}=\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\phi_{\alpha_i}}
. The next step is to calculate the bounds
L
and
\sigma^{2}
. To obtain the bound
L
, notice that

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}\leq|\ket*{\Phi}|_{2}+|\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}+|\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}.

(106)

Using Eq. (
62
), we have

\displaystyle|\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\sqrt{\sum_{j}|\omega_{j}^{\alpha}|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right).

(107)

Thus,

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)

(108)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)

(109)

\displaystyle<\sqrt{2}\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)\equiv L.

(110)

For the bound
\sigma^{2}
, it follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\phi_{\alpha_i}}|_{2}^{2}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(|\ket*{\Phi}|_{2}^{2}+|\ket*{\phi_{\alpha_i}}|^{2}_{2}-2{\rm Re}(\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\phi_{\alpha_i}})\right)

(111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(|\ket*{\phi_{\alpha_i}}|_{2}^{2}\right)-|\ket*{\Phi}|_{2}^{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\equiv\sigma^{2}.

(113)

Defining the constant

\displaystyle\Omega=\max_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right),

(114)

we conclude that

\displaystyle L=\sqrt{2}\Omega,\qquad\sigma^{2}=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2}.

(115)

Notice that the coefficient
\Omega
is related to the number of outcomes
n
of the FSM by
\Omega^{2}\geq n
, so the bounds
L
and
\sigma^{2}
are tighter for a minimal FSM with
n=2d-1
. Replacing Eq. (
115
), the concentration inequality becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 8\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right),

(116)

where we suppose that
\epsilon
is small enough so that
w(2\epsilon)\approx 1
, and replace
\ket*{\hat\Phi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ket*{\phi_{\alpha_i}}
. Taking into account Eq. (
103
), we can rewrite the concentration inequality in terms of the state
\ket*{\Psi}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{a_{0}}{2}|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}<\epsilon\right]>1-8\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right),

(117)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 8\exp\left(-\frac{a_{0}^{2}N\epsilon^{2}}{4\Omega^{2}}\right).

(118)

We can use this equation to obtain an upper bound for the 2-norm error
\epsilon
. Then, with probability at least of
1-\delta
, the estimation has an error
\epsilon
such as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\leq\frac{4\Omega^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}N}\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right).

(119)

B.5.4
Upper-bound for infidelity

In the previous section, we obtained an upper bound for the error in the 2-norm, which is not directly comparable to the Gill-Massar bound, which is a lower bound for infidelity. Due to that, we will use Eq. (
119
) to obtain an upper bound for infidelity. Expanding the error in 2-norm, we have

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}^{2}=2\left(1-{\rm Re}(\innerproduct*{\Psi}{\hat\Psi})\right)\geq 2(1-|\innerproduct*{\Psi}{\hat\Psi}|).

(120)

Isolating the fidelity
|\innerproduct*{\Psi}{\hat\Psi}|^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\innerproduct*{\Psi}{\hat\Psi}|^{2}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\geq 1-|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}^{2}.

(121)

Thus, the 2-norm error upper bounds the infidelity,

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})=1-|\innerproduct*{\Psi}{\hat\Psi}|^{2}\leq|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|^{2}_{2}.

(122)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the infidelity is upper bounded by

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\frac{4\Omega^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}N}\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right).

(123)

Considering the particular case of the explicit FSM introduced in section
B.3
, we have
\Omega^{2}=n=2d-1
. Therefore, the upper bound for infidelity becomes

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\frac{4(2d-1)}{a_{0}^{2}N}\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right).

(124)

This indicates that the infidelity scales as
\mathcal{O}(8d/a_{0}^{2}N)
, making it at least eight times worse than the Gill-Massar bound, which scales as
\mathcal{O}(d/N)
. Furthermore, the upper bound decreases as the fiducial overlap
a_{0}
diminishes. This degradation does not represent an inherent limitation of Fisher-symmetric measurements; rather, it stems from the conservativeness of the analytical bound. This is derived by combining a norm inequality with a worst-case concentration estimate, leading to an overestimation of the typical reconstruction error. Therefore, the expression above should be considered a worst-case upper bound. In contrast, the Gill-Massar bound is asymptotically tight, providing a lower bound on the achievable infidelity. In practice, we expect protocols based on Fisher-Symmetric Measurements (FSM) to perform significantly closer to the Gill-Massar scaling than this conservative estimate suggests.

B.5.5
Error bound due to approximation

The previous calculations were performed by approximating the probabilities up to first order in the parameters. Nevertheless, from a real experiment, we measure the actual probabilities without approximation, which introduces a systematic error in the estimation
[
37
]
. The density matrix Eq. (
26
) without approximation can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}=\rho+(1-a_{0}^{2})\sigma,

(125)

where
\sigma
is the density matrix,

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{1}{1-a_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d-1}(x_{j0}-ix_{j1})(x_{k0}+ix_{k1})|j\rangle\thinspace\langle k|.

(126)

Thus, the actual probability distribution is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\alpha}=p_{\alpha}+(1-a_{0}^{2})q_{\alpha},

(127)

where
q_{\alpha}
is another probability distribution defined by
\sigma
. The resulting linear estimator becomes

\displaystyle|\tilde{\Phi}\rangle=\ket*{\Phi}+(1-a_{0}^{2})\ket*{Q},

(128)

with
\ket*{Q}=\sum_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}\ket*{\phi_\alpha}
. This implies that the total error between
\ket*{\Phi}
and its estimator
|\hat{\tilde{\Phi}}\rangle
from
\tilde{p}_{\alpha}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\tilde{\Phi}}\rangle|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat{\Phi}}|_{2}+|\ket*{\hat{\Phi}}-\ket*{\hat{\tilde{\Phi}}}|_{2}

(129)

\displaystyle\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}+(1-a_{0}^{2})|\ket*{Q}|_{2}

(130)

\displaystyle\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}+(1-a_{0}^{2})\sum_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}|\ket*{\phi_\alpha}|_{2}

(131)

\displaystyle\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|_{2}+(1-a_{0}^{2})\frac{\Omega}{\sqrt{2}}.

(132)

The first term on the right side of the previous equation is the estimation error considering the approximation, and the second term is a bias due to the approximation. Therefore, in order to omit the second term and validate the approximation, we need a fiducial overlap
a_{0}
such as

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}\gg 1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\Omega}.

(133)

For the particular case of the explicit FSM introduced in section
B.3
, we have

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}\gg 1-\sqrt{\frac{2}{2d-1}}.

(134)

Let us consider
|\ket*{\Phi}-|\hat{\Phi}\rangle|\leq\epsilon
, with
\epsilon
given by Eq. (
119
), and that

\displaystyle\kappa=(1-a_{0}^{2})\frac{\Omega}{\sqrt{2}}\ll 1.

(135)

Then, we have the following bound for infidelity

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\tilde{\Psi}})\leq\epsilon^{2}+2\epsilon\kappa+\kappa^{2}.

(136)

Therefore, depending on the relationship between
\epsilon
and
\kappa
, the statistical error
\epsilon
could be hidden by the approximation error
\kappa
. This means that an increase in the sample size
N
does not improve the quality of the reconstruction because
I(\Psi,\hat{\tilde{\Psi}})\approx\kappa^{2}
, a behavior observed in experiments
[
37
]
.

Appendix C
Pure state estimation with two FSM

In this section, we demonstrate that any pure state, except a null subset, can be estimated with a measurement into two FSMs. We then perform an error analysis of this method.

C.1
Analytical reconstruction

Consider a POVM composed of two FSM
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\{\ket*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}\bra*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}\}
, where

\displaystyle\ket*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\ket*{0}\pm\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\ket*{k},

(137)

with
\beta_{0}^{\alpha}
and
\omega_{k}^{\alpha}
satisfying the orthogonality conditions presented in section
B.3.1
, and with
\alpha=0,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,d-1
. Let us consider an arbitrary state given by Eq. (
24
), but without assuming that the parameters are small. This means that our state could be far from the fiducial state
\ket*{0}
. Measuring this state with the POVM composed of two FSM, we get

\displaystyle P_{\pm}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=|\langle\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}|\Psi\rangle|^{2}=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}a_{0}^{2}+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}z_{j}\right|^{2}\pm\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right],

(138)

where we defined
z_{j}=x_{j0}+ix_{j1}
for simplicity. Subtracting these expressions,

\displaystyle P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha}=2a_{0}\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right].

(139)

Multiplying the previous expression by
\omega_{k}^{\alpha}/\beta_{0}^{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)(P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2a_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right],

(140)

Summing over all
\alpha
and using the orthogonality conditions of Eqs. (
59
) and (
60
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)(P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2a_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right]=2a_{0}z_{k}.

(141)

This expression motivates us to define the quantities

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)(P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha}).

(142)

The quantities
\Delta_{k}
can be measured experimentally, so we can use them to find the coefficients of the unknown pure state
\ket*{\Psi}
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}=a_{0}z_{k}.

(143)

To isolate the parameters
z_{k}
from Eq. (
143
), we have to find the parameter
a_{0}
. This can be done by noticing that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left|\Delta_{k}\right|^{2}=a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2}),

(144)

so, we have a quadratic equation for
a_{0}^{2}
, whose solutions are

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-4\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left|\Delta_{k}\right|^{2}}\right).

(145)

We have two possible solutions for
a_{0}^{2}
, but only one of them is valid. To determine the correct solution, notice that when we add the probabilities
P_{\pm}^{\alpha}
, we find

P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha}=2(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}a_{0}^{2}+2\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{k}\right|^{2}.

(146)

Multiplying the previous equation by
a_{0}^{2}
, we obtain

a_{0}^{2}(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})=2(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}(a_{0}^{2})^{2}+2(a_{0})^{2}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{k}\right|^{2}.

(147)

Recalling Eqs. (
143
) and  (
144
), we find a set of
n
linear equations for
a_{0}^{2}
,

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\left(a_{0}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}\right)+2\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}\Delta_{k}\right|^{2}.

(148)

Solving these equations, we obtain
n
estimators of
a_{0}
,

\displaystyle a_{0}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}\Delta_{k}\right|^{2}-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}}{(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}}}.

(149)

Using these estimators of
a_{0}
we can discriminate which is the correct solution in Eq. (
145
), or moreover, define an average estimator

\displaystyle a_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}a_{0}^{\alpha}.

(150)

Having
a_{0}
, we can determine the coefficients
z_{k}
by substituting
a_{0}
into Eq. (
143
). However, this method fails when
a_{0}=0
, as Eq. (
139
) would become null for all values of
\alpha
. Therefore, with measurements from two FSMs, we can identify the parameters of any arbitrary pure state, except for the null subspace where
a_{0}=0
.

C.2
Error Analysis

C.2.1
Linear Estimator

Similarly to the optimal FSM case, we can define a linear estimator for the vector

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\Delta_{j}\ket*{j}

(151)

Replacing Eq. (
142
), we obtain

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{1}(\pm P_{\pm}^{\alpha})\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\ket*{k}

(152)

Defining the random vectors

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi_\pm^\alpha}=(\pm)\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\frac{(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\ket*{k},

(153)

we find that
\ket*{\Phi}
is given by the expected value of
\{\ket*{\Phi_\pm^\alpha}\}
on the probability distribution
\{P^{\alpha}_{\pm}/2\}
, that is,

\displaystyle\ket*{\Phi}=\mathbb{E}(\ket*{\Phi_s^\alpha}).

(154)

C.2.2
Error upper bound

The inequality given by Eq. (
97
) also holds for this case, so

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-|\hat{\Psi}\rangle|_{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{a_{0}}\right)|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|+\frac{1}{a_{0}}|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}.

(155)

Using the concentration inequality presented in section
B.5.3
, we have the same result as before, that is
L=\sqrt{2}\Omega
and
\sigma^{2}=\Omega/2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta=\mathbb{P}\left[|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}\geq\epsilon\right]\leq 8\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right).

(156)

This inequality establishes that
|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}\leq\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
, so we only have to deal with the upper bound of
|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|
.

The upper bound for the error of
a_{0}
can be obtained using error propagation in Eq. (
144
). Taking into account the definition of
\ket*{\Phi}
, this equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})=\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}.

(157)

From Eq. (
101
), we know that

\displaystyle|\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}-\innerproduct*{\hat\Phi}{\hat\Phi}|\leq|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|_{2}\leq\epsilon.

(158)

In order to propagate the error, we have to derive both sides by
\xi^{2}=\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}
, obtaining

\displaystyle 2a_{0}(1-a_{0}^{2})\derivative{a_0}{\xi^2}+a_{0}^{2}\left(-2a_{0}\derivative{a_0}{\xi^2}\right)=1.

(159)

Isolating the derivative, we have

\displaystyle\derivative{a_0}{\xi^2}=\frac{1}{2a_{0}(1-2a_{0}^{2})}.

(160)

Thus, the error of
a_{0}
is upper-bounded by

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\leq\frac{|\xi^{2}-\hat{\xi}^{2}|}{2a_{0}|1-2a_{0}^{2}|}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2a_{0}|1-2a_{0}^{2}|}.

(161)

This equation establishes that the error of
a_{0}
estimated by Eq. (
145
) scales the same as
\ket*{\Phi}
, except when
a_{0}\approx 0
and
a_{0}^{2}\approx 1/2
, where the upper bound diverges. The first divergence around
a_{0}\approx 0
occurs because the protocol with two FSMs requires a fiducial with non-zero overlap with the unknown state. The second divergence around
a_{0}^{2}\approx 1/2
can be eliminated using the estimators given by Eq. (
149
). This equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(a_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}=\frac{|\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\Phi}|^{2}-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}}{(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}},

(162)

with
\ket*{\omega^\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\ket*{k}
. Let us define

\displaystyle A^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle|\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\Phi}|^{2}-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi},

(163)

\displaystyle B^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2},

(164)

so that
(a_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}=A^{\alpha}/B^{\alpha}
. This permits us to upper bound the error of
a_{0}^{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle|a_{0}^{2}-(\hat{a}_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}|=\left|\frac{A^{\alpha}}{B^{\alpha}}-\frac{\hat{A}^{\alpha}}{\hat{B}^{\alpha}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{|B^{\alpha}|}|A^{\alpha}-\hat{A}^{\alpha}|+\frac{A^{\alpha}}{(B^{\alpha})^{2}}|B^{\alpha}-\hat{B}^{\alpha}|.

(165)

Therefore, we need to upper bound the errors for
A^{\alpha}
and
B^{\alpha}
. For
A^{\alpha}
we have

\displaystyle|A^{\alpha}-\hat{A}^{\alpha}|=

\displaystyle\left||\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\Phi}|^{2}-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}-|\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\hat\Phi}|^{2}+(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\innerproduct*{\hat\Phi}{\hat\Phi}\right|

(166)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left||\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\Phi}|^{2}-|\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\hat\Phi}|^{2}\right|+(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\left|\innerproduct*{\Phi}{\Phi}-\innerproduct*{\hat\Phi}{\hat\Phi}\right|

(167)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2})|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|+(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|

(168)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Phi}-\ket*{\hat\Phi}|.

(169)

So, the concentration inequality for
\ket*{\Phi}
also works for
A^{\alpha}
, so
|A^{\alpha}-\hat{A}^{\alpha}|\leq\epsilon
.

To calculate the error upper-bound for
B^{\alpha}
, we apply the Bretagnolle-Huber-Carol inequality
[
64
,
39
]
to the multinomial random variable
P^{\alpha}=(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2
. It reads

\displaystyle\delta_{0}=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}|P^{\alpha}-\hat{P}^{\alpha}|\geq\epsilon_{0}\right]\leq 2^{n}\exp( -N \epsilon_0^2/2 ).

(170)

Let us define error per probability
P^{\alpha}
as
\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}=|P^{\alpha}-\hat{P}^{\alpha}|
, so that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}.

(171)

In this way, we have
|B^{\alpha}-\hat{B}^{\alpha}|=|P^{\alpha}-\hat{P}^{\alpha}|\leq\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}
. The upper bound for the error of
(a_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}
becomes

\displaystyle|a_{0}^{2}-(\hat{a}_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}|\leq\frac{1}{|B^{\alpha}|}\left(\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}\right).

(172)

Using the Taylor series of square root, we can derive a bound for the error of
a_{0}
,

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}^{\alpha}|\leq\frac{1}{2a_{0}}\frac{\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}}{|B^{\alpha}|}.

(173)

The error becomes significant with a small
B_{\alpha}
. However, this can only occur for all
\alpha
when
a_{0}=0
. To illustrate this, we note that
B_{\alpha}
can be expressed as the expected value of the observable,

\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\outerproduct{\varphi^\alpha_+}{\varphi^\alpha_+}+\outerproduct{\varphi^\alpha_-}{\varphi^\alpha_-}\right)-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\mathbb{I}

in the state
\ket*{\Psi}
. For
B^{\alpha}=0
, the state
\ket*{\Psi}
must be within the kernel of
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
. To characterize this kernel, we shall explicitly replace
\ket*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}
, yielding

\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}=\outerproduct{\omega^\alpha}{\omega^\alpha}-(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\outerproduct{j}{j}.

From the equation, we observe that
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
can have a kernel of either one or two dimensions. If
\innerproduct*{\omega^\alpha}{\omega^\alpha}=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}
, then
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
has a two-dimensional kernel spanned by the vectors
\{\ket*{0},\ket*{\omega^\alpha}\}
. Otherwise, the kernel is one-dimensional, spanned solely by
\{\ket*{0}\}
. Therefore,
B^{\alpha}
can only be zero if
\ket*{\Psi}
is a linear combination of
\ket*{0}
and
\ket*{\omega^\alpha}
. If
\ket*{\Psi}
is a Haar-random state, the probability of this happening is zero since it corresponds to a subspace of measure zero. However, if we are unlucky and this event does occur, only one
B^{\alpha}
will become null, not all at the same time. If this situation arises, we can proceed with the protocol simply by disregarding that particular estimator. In conclusion, with high probability,
B^{\alpha}
is finite. Furthermore, if
\ket*{\Psi}
is Haar-random, there is a high probability of
(d-1)/d
that the state
\ket*{\Psi}
is orthogonal to
\ket*{\omega^\alpha}
. Hence, with high probability, we have the following

\displaystyle B^{\alpha}\approx-(1-a_{0}^{2})(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}.

(174)

Considering that there is no null
B^{\alpha}
, then the error of each
a_{0}^{\alpha}
becomes

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}^{\alpha}|\leq\frac{1}{2a_{0}}\frac{\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}}{(1-a_{0}^{2})(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}}\leq\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2a_{0}}\frac{\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}}{(1-a_{0}^{2})},

(175)

where
\Omega=\max_{\alpha}(1/\beta_{0}^{\alpha})
.

The final estimator for
a_{0}
is given by the average of the estimators
a_{0}^{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{0}^{\alpha}.

(176)

The error of this estimator is upper bounded by

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}|a_{0}-\hat{a}^{\alpha}_{0}|^{2}}=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2na_{0}(1-a_{0}^{2})}\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}(\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}}.

(177)

This error can also be upper bounded by

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\leq\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2na_{0}(1-a_{0}^{2})}\left(\sqrt{n}\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\epsilon_{0}^{\alpha}\right)=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2na_{0}(1-a_{0}^{2})}\left(\sqrt{n}\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}\right).

(178)

The total probability of failure
{\delta^{\prime}}
of estimating
a_{0}
with this error is bounded by

\displaystyle{\delta^{\prime}}=\delta+\delta_{0}\leq 8\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right)+2^{n}\exp( -N \epsilon_0^2/2 ).

(179)

Let us consider failure probabilities
\delta=\delta_{0}=\delta^{\prime}/2
, so the total failure probability remains as
\delta^{\prime}
, the errors
\epsilon
and
\epsilon_{0}
are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega^{2}}{N}\log\left(\frac{16}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{4\Omega^{2}\log(2)}{N}-\frac{\log(\delta')}{N},

(180)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2(n+1)\log(2)}{N}-\frac{2}{N}\log(\delta')\leq\frac{3n\log(2)}{N}-\frac{2}{N}\log(\delta').

(181)

Given that
\Omega^{2}\geq n
, we can state that
\epsilon>\epsilon_{0}
. Returning to Eq. (
178
), we can see that the total error scales as
\sqrt{n}\epsilon+a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{0}
, so
\epsilon
contributes
\sqrt{n}/a_{0}^{2}
times more than
\epsilon_{0}
, and asymtotically only
\epsilon
is relevant. For this reason, we are going to approximate the error simply as

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\lesssim\frac{\Omega^{2}\epsilon}{2\sqrt{n}a_{0}(1-a_{0}^{2})},

(182)

with failure probability

\displaystyle{\delta^{\prime}}\leq 16\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\right),

(183)

where we consider
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
. We can see that the new bound given by Eq. (
182
) has several differences with the previous bound given by Eq. (
161
). The first is that the new bound is much worse due to the factor
\Omega^{2}
, which scales at least as
\mathcal{O}(d)
. This means that the procedure given by Eq. (
145
) to estimate
a_{0}
is more accurate than the provided by Eq. (
149
). Despite this, the second estimation procedure offers a key advantage over the first, namely that the error bound does not blow up around
a_{0}^{2}=1/2
. This means that, complementing both estimation procedures, we can have the following upper bound for the error in estimating
a_{0}^{2}
that only blows up around
a_{0}=0
,

\displaystyle|a_{0}-\hat{a}_{0}|\lesssim\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(a_{0})\epsilon,

(184)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma(a_{0})=\min\left(\frac{1}{a_{0}|1-2a_{0}^{2}|},\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})}\right).

(185)

We can obtain an explicit expression for
\Gamma(a_{0})
by computing the intersection of the functions
f_{1}(a_{0})=1/a_{0}\lvert 1-2a_{0}^{2}\rvert
and
f_{2}(a_{0})=\Omega^{2}/\sqrt{n}a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})
.
In general, this requires solving a cubic equation. However, the problem simplifies in a neighborhood of
a_{0}=1/2
and the intersection points can be approximated by solving a quadratic equation, yielding

\displaystyle\left(a_{0\pm}^{\mathrm{int}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Omega^{4}}{n}+1}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(186)

Consequently,
\Gamma(a_{0})
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(a_{0})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{a_{0}\lvert 1-2a_{0}^{2}\rvert},&\text{if }a_{0}<a_{0-}^{\mathrm{int}}\text{ or }a_{0+}^{\mathrm{int}}<a_{0},\\
\dfrac{\Omega^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\dfrac{1}{a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})},&\text{if }a_{0-}^{\mathrm{int}}\leq a_{0}\leq a_{0+}^{\mathrm{int}}.\end{cases}

(187)

Figure
4
shows the functions
f_{1}(a_{0})
,
f_{2}(a_{0})
, and
\Gamma(a_{0})
, together with the approximate intersection points of
f_{1}(a_{0})
and
f_{2}(a_{0})
for dimensions
d=2,10,64
. We observe that
\Gamma(a_{0})
attains its maximum at
a_{0}=1/2
, and that
\Gamma(1/2)=4\Omega^{2}/\sqrt{n}\geq 4\sqrt{d}
increases with the dimension. However, the interval over which
\Gamma(a_{0})
exceeds
f_{2}(a_{0})
becomes narrower as the dimension grows. This indicates that, asymptotically,
f_{1}(a_{0})
provides a better description of the overall behavior.

\Gamma(a_{0})
(red zone),

Introducing Eq. (
184
) into Eq.  (
155
), we can establish the following upper bound for estimating the unknown state
\ket*{\Psi}
,

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-\ket*{\hat{\Psi}}|_{2}\leq\frac{\Gamma(a_{0})}{a_{0}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\epsilon}{a_{0}}.

(188)

Considering that
\Gamma(a_{0})\geq 4\sqrt{d}
for
a_{0}^{2}=1/2
, we can make the following approximation,

\displaystyle|\ket*{\Psi}-\ket*{\hat{\Psi}}|_{2}\leq\frac{2\Gamma(a_{0}){\epsilon}}{a_{0}}

(189)

Redefining the error as
{\epsilon}^{\prime}=2\Gamma(a_{0})\epsilon/a_{0}
, we state the following inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|\ket*{\Psi}-\ket*{\hat{\Psi}}|_{2}\geq{\epsilon}^{\prime}\right]\leq 16\exp\left(-\frac{Na_{0}^{2}{\epsilon}^{\prime 2}}{4\Omega^{2}\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}}\right).

(190)

Now we are going to specialize this bound for the FSM presented in section
B.3
. This FSM is determined by
\Omega^{2}=n=2d-1
, so the inequality becomes

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}\left[|\ket*{\Psi}-\ket*{\hat{\Psi}}|_{2}\geq{\epsilon}^{\prime}\right]\leq 16\exp\left(-\frac{Na_{0}^{2}{\epsilon}^{\prime 2}}{4(2d-1)\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}}\right),

(191)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma(a_{0})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{a_{0}\lvert 1-2a_{0}^{2}\rvert},&\text{if }a_{0}<a_{0-}^{\mathrm{int}}\text{ or }a_{0+}^{\mathrm{int}}<a_{0},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{\sqrt{2d-1}}{a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})},&\text{if }a_{0-}^{\mathrm{int}}\leq a_{0}\leq a_{0+}^{\mathrm{int}},\end{cases}

(192)

and

\displaystyle\left(a_{0\pm}^{\mathrm{int}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{2d}-\sqrt{2d-1}\right).

(193)

This allows us to establish the following upper bound for infidelity

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\frac{4(2d-1)\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}}{Na_{0}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{16}{\delta^{\prime}}\right).

(194)

This bound indicates that the sample complexity scales as
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/a_{0}^{6})
when
a_{0}\approx 1/2
and
\mathcal{O}(d/a_{0}^{4})
for the remaining cases. We observe that it is
\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}
times less favorable than the estimation provided by the optimal FSM, as given in Eq. (
124
). However, it is important to emphasize that the upper bound described in Eq. (
194
) is conservative and is likely to overestimate the actual infidelity. We expect the actual estimation error to be significantly better.

Appendix D
Pure state estimation with adapted FSMs

In this section, we derive high-probability error bounds and sample complexity for pure-state estimation using the adapted Fisher-symmetric measurements (AFSM). The protocol consists of three stages. In the first stage, we perform a single-shot projective measurement in a randomly chosen basis to select a fiducial state with non-vanishing overlap
a_{0}^{2}
with the unknown state. This step ensures that the subsequent FSM construction is well-defined and, with high probability, yields a fiducial overlap of moderate magnitude.

In the second stage, we carry out tomography using the two non-optimal FSMs

\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\{\ket*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}\bra*{\varphi^\alpha_\pm}\},

with a total sample size
N_{1}
. This produces an estimator with statistical error
\epsilon_{1}
and failure probability
\delta_{1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\delta_{1}\leq 16\exp\!\left(-\frac{N_{1}a_{0}^{2}\epsilon_{1}^{2}}{4(2d-1)\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}}\right).

(195)

In the third stage, the estimator obtained in the previous step is used as the fiducial state for a new, adaptively aligned FSM

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}=\{U\ket*{\varphi^\alpha}\bra*{\varphi^\alpha}U^{\dagger}\},

where
U
is the unitary implementing the adaptation. Since the preliminary estimator has infidelity at most
\epsilon_{1}^{2}
, the overlap between the adapted fiducial state and the true state, namely
\bar{a}_{0}
, satisfies
\bar{a}_{0}^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon_{1}^{2}
. Performing the estimation with the near-optimal FSM
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
using
N_{2}
samples yields an estimator with statistical error
\epsilon_{2}
and failure probability
\delta_{2}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\leq 8\exp\!\left(-\frac{\bar{a}_{0}^{2}N_{2}\epsilon_{2}^{2}}{4(2d-1)}\right).

(196)

As shown in Sec.
B.5.5
, the linearized reconstruction employed in the third stage induces an approximation error

\displaystyle\kappa\leq(1-\bar{a}_{0}^{2})\sqrt{\frac{2d-1}{2}}\leq\epsilon_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2d-1}{2}}.

(197)

Consequently, the total error of the adaptive protocol is bounded by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{T}=\min\!\bigl(\epsilon_{1},\;\epsilon_{2}+\kappa\bigr),

(198)

with total failure probability
\delta_{T}=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
. Explicitly, the errors satisfy

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(2d-1)\Gamma(a_{0})^{2}}{N_{1}a_{0}^{2}}\log\!\left(\frac{16}{\delta_{1}}\right),

(199)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(2d-1)}{N_{2}(1-\epsilon_{1}^{2})}\log\!\left(\frac{8}{\delta_{2}}\right).

(200)

The resulting infidelity is therefore bounded as

\displaystyle I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\epsilon_{T}^{2}.

(201)

To gain a genuine advantage from the adaptive strategy, the approximation error
\kappa
must not dominate the statistical error
\epsilon_{1}
of the first estimator. This can be achieved by employing a sample size
N_{1}
such that
\epsilon_{1}\ll\sqrt{2/(2d-1)}
. Furthermore, when
N_{1}
is sufficiently large, specifically when
\kappa\lesssim\epsilon_{2}
, the total error is governed by the near-optimal FSM, leading to an infidelity

I(\Psi,\hat{\Psi})\leq\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{N_{2}}\right).

(202)

This result aligns with the dimensional scaling of the Gill-Massar bound, provided
N_{2}
is proportional to
N
. It implies that while the adaptive protocol may not significantly improve infidelity for small
N
, for large
N
it asymptotically scales as
\mathcal{O}(d/N)
. Nonetheless, this bound remains quantitatively loose and is still significantly above the Gill-Massar bound. This suggests that the reconstruction procedure outlined in Section
B.4
is generally suboptimal.

The figure

displays the error bounds
\epsilon_{1}^{2}
,
\epsilon_{2}
, and
\epsilon_{T}^{2}
, along with the Gill-Massar bound for sample sizes
N_{1}=N_{2}=N/2
, with
N\in\{10^{6},10^{7},10^{8},10^{9}\}
up to a dimension
d=64
. Since the error bounds depend on the fiducial overlap
a_{0}
, we calculate the average of each bound over the Hilbert space as follows: we generate a random state according to the Haar distribution and perform a single-shot measurement to obtain
a_{0}
. Then, we compute the error bounds
\epsilon_{1}
,
\epsilon_{2}
,
\kappa
, and
\epsilon_{T}
for this specific instance of
a_{0}
. This procedure is repeated for
10^{4}
states to create a set of
10^{4}
values for each error bound, which we then use to calculate their averages. We can see that for the lowest sample sizes
N\in\{10^{6},10^{7}\}
, the protocol only guarantees an advantage for low dimensions, and that
\epsilon_{T}
is far above
\epsilon_{2}
, indicating that the approximation error
\kappa
dominates. For the largest sample size
N\in\{10^{8},10^{9}\}
, we have guarantees of advantage for intermediate dimensions, but the approximation error persists. For small dimensions, the total error
\epsilon_{T}
approaches
\epsilon_{2}
, indicating that in this regime the FSM dominates. To gain a guaranteed advantage in dimension 64, we need to use a sample size larger than
10^{9}
.

The presence of the approximation error
\kappa
in Eq. (

) constitutes the main drawback of the present analysis. This error arises because each individual FSM is only locally informationally complete. However, the combined measurement data from the two non-optimal FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
and the near-optimal FSM
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
are informationally complete for any pure state with
a_{0}\neq 0
. Therefore, an estimation procedure that jointly processes all measurement outcomes should not incur the approximation error
\kappa
, leaving the statistical error as the dominant contribution. This observation motivates the use of maximum-likelihood estimation to construct the final estimator, as discussed in the next section.

Appendix E
Attaining Gill-Massar bound with Maximum Likelihood Estimation

In this section, we show that employing a maximum-likelihood estimator significantly improves the performance, yielding an estimation error that deviates from the Gill-Massar bound by at most
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}/N)
. As a result, the proposed estimation strategy approaches optimality in the high-sample regime.

Consider that we want to estimate the state
\ket*{\Psi(\bm{x})}
given by Eq. (
24
) using information from an optimal FSM. Let
\bm{y}
denote the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of the parameter vector
\bm{x}
. Under standard regularity conditions,
\bm{y}
satisfies the central limit theorem as the sample size
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(\Psi,E^{\alpha})\right),

(203)

where
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
denotes a normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma
, and
\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(\Psi,E^{\alpha})
is the covariance matrix associated with performing
N
measurements of the state
\ket*{\Psi}
using the FSM
E^{\alpha}
.

This result implies that, for sufficiently large
N
, the estimator
\bm{y}
is approximately Gaussian distributed with mean
\bm{x}
and covariance
\mathrm{Cov}(\ket*{\Psi},E^{\alpha})
. Strictly speaking, the approximation is exact only in the asymptotic limit
N\to\infty
, but for finite sample sizes, deviations are expected. Nevertheless, previous work in quantum state tomography has shown that the Gaussian approximation performs well even for moderate values of
N
, especially when one is primarily interested in standard figures of merit such as the Hilbert-Schmidt distance or trace distance
[
77
,
27
]
. The same approximation has also been used to define confidence regions in state tomography
[
10
]
. For these reasons, we adopt the Gaussian approximation in the following discussion.

In this setting, the Wald hypothesis test
[
66
,
7
]
provides a natural way to construct confidence regions. Specifically, when the null hypothesis
\bm{x}=\bm{y}
holds, the Wald statistics

\displaystyle W_{N}(\bm{y})=(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(\Psi,E^{\alpha})^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}),

(204)

converges in distribution to a chi-squared distribution with
K
degrees of freedom as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle W_{N}(\bm{y})\Longrightarrow\chi^{2}(K).

(205)

Thus, for a significance level
\delta
, the rejection region of the test is given by the upper tail of the chi-squared distribution,

\displaystyle W_{N}(\bm{y})>\chi_{1-\delta}^{2}(K),

(206)

where
\chi_{1-\delta}^{2}(K)
denotes the
(1-\delta)
-quantile of the
\chi^{2}(K)
distribution. In the asymptotic limit, this implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[W_{N}(\bm{y})>\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K)\right]=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\chi^{2}(K)>\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K)\right]=\delta.

(207)

We therefore obtain the following confidence region for an estimator constructed via MLE,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(\Psi,E^{\alpha})^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})>\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K)\right]=\delta.

(208)

In the next section, we will use this inequality to derive an upper bound on the state infidelity in terms of the system dimension
d
, the sample size
N
, and the probability of error
\delta
.

Recalling the Classical Cramér-Rao bound
\mathrm{Cov}_{N}(\ket*{\Psi},E^{\alpha})\geq C_{N}^{-1}
, with
C_{N}
the Classical FIM of the
N
samples, we can rewrite the inequality as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}C_{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})>\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K)\right]=\delta.

(209)

Then, with at least probability
1-\delta
, the MLE estimator satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}C_{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\leq\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K).

(210)

For a FSM, the Classical FIM is given by
C_{N}=Q/2N
, with
Q
the Quantum FIM. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}Q(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\leq\frac{1}{N}\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K).

(211)

The left-hand side of this inequality corresponds to the squared Bures distance between two nearby pure states parameterized by
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

[
4
,
48
]
,

\displaystyle d_{B}(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))^{2}\approx\frac{1}{4}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{\top}Q(\bm{x}-\bm{y}).

(212)

Combining these expressions, we obtain an upper bound on the Bures distance:

\displaystyle d_{B}(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2N}\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K).

(213)

Noticing that the Bures distance upper bounds the infidelity between two pure states,

\displaystyle I(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))=1-|\innerproduct*{\Psi(\bm{x})}{\Psi(\bm{y} )}|^{2}\leq d_{B}(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))^{2},

(214)

we can establish the following upper bound for the infidelity,

\displaystyle I(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))\leq\frac{1}{2N}\chi^{2}_{1-\delta}(K).

(215)

We can rewrite the right side of the inequality explicitly in terms of
d
,
\delta
, and
N
, applying the following upper bound for
\chi_{1-\delta}^{2}(K)

[
30
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\chi_{1-\delta}^{2}(K)>K+2\sqrt{K\log(1/\delta)}+2\log(1/\delta)\right]=\delta.

(216)

In this way, we find that with probability at least
1-\alpha
, the following bound holds

\displaystyle I(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}K+\sqrt{K\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta)\right).

(217)

Changing the degrees of freedom with the number of parameters of a pure state
K=2(d-1)
, we have

\displaystyle I(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))\leq\frac{d-1}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\left(\sqrt{2(d-1)\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta)\right).

(218)

The first term on the right-hand side of this equation corresponds to the Gill-Massar lower bound for infidelity. In contrast, the second term represents a deviation due to uncertainty in the estimation procedure, which scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}/N)
. This indicates that the estimators obtained by MLE are at most a distance of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}/N)
from the Gill-Massar bound. This improvement motivates using MLE as a post-processing step, rather than the procedure discussed in section
B.4
. Another advantage of using MLE is the mitigation of the approximation error
\kappa
, since the combined measurement data from the two non-optimal FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
and the near-optimal FSM
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
are informationally complete for any pure state with
a_{0}\neq 0
. Thereby, the infidelity of the estimation with the adapted FSM should satisfy

\displaystyle I(\Psi(\bm{x}),\Psi(\bm{y}))\lesssim\frac{d-1}{N_{2}}+\frac{1}{N_{2}}\left(\sqrt{2(d-1)\log(2/\delta)}+\log(2/\delta)\right),

(219)

with
N_{2}
the sample size used for measuring
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
. Note that we assumed that the success probability in the first estimation with a non-optimal FSM is at least
\delta
, so the total success probability after the adaptation is
1-2\delta
, which introduces a factor of 2 in the logarithms.

Appendix G
Estimation of mixed states

In this section, we study the estimation of slightly mixed states, pure states affected by white noise. They consist of a pure state component plus a maximally mixed state,

\rho(\bm{x},\lambda)=(1-\lambda)\ket*{\Psi}\bra*{\Psi}+\frac{\lambda}{d}\mathbb{I}.

(227)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is an unknown constant and
\bm{x}=[x_{0,0},x_{0,1},\cdots,x_{d-1,0},x_{d-1,1}]^{\top}
and
|x_{k\sigma}|\ll 1
are infinitesimal real parameters. Considering the form of the pure state on the computational basis,

\ket*{\Psi}=a_{0}\ket*{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(x_{k0}+ix_{k1})\ket*{k}.

(228)

We will demonstrate that measurements into two FSM
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\{\ket*{\varphi_\pm^\alpha}\bra*{\varphi_\pm^\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,n}
and the computational basis
\{\ket*{k}\}_{k=0}^{d-1}
are enough to characterize the pure state
\ket*{\Psi}
and the parameter
\lambda
.

G.1
Analytical reconstruction

The probabilities of measuring the state
\rho
on the computational basis
\{\ket*{k}\}_{k=0}^{d-1}
are

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle=\bra*{k}\rho\ket*{k},

(229)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)|\langle k|\Psi\rangle|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{d},

(230)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)|z_{k}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{d},

(231)

with
z_{k}=x_{k0}+ix_{k1}
and
z_{0}=a_{0}
for simplicity. Rearranging terms, we find a set of equations for the coefficients
z_{k}
,

|z_{k}|^{2}=\frac{P_{k}-\lambda/d}{1-\lambda}.

(232)

We chose as fiducial state the one with the biggest probability of measurement.

Considering the FSM with elements,

\ket*{\varphi_\pm^\alpha}=\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\ket*{0}\pm\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}\ket*{k},\;\;\beta_{0}^{\alpha}>0,\;\alpha=0,\dots,n-1,

(233)

where
\omega_{k}^{\alpha}=\beta_{k}^{\alpha}+i\gamma_{k}^{\alpha}
, and
n\geq 2d-1
the number of rank-1 elements of the FSM. And fiducial state the one chosen in the previous step. The probabilities of measuring the slightly mixed state
\rho
with the FSM are given by

\displaystyle P_{\pm}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=Tr(\mathcal{E}_{\pm}^{\alpha}\;\rho),

(234)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)|\langle\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}|{\Psi}\rangle|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{d}\langle\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}|\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}\rangle.

(235)

By calculating the projection of the pure state onto the measurement elements, we obtain

\displaystyle|\innerproduct*{\varphi_\pm^\alpha}{\Psi}|^{2}=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}a_{0}^{2}+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}z_{j}\right|^{2}\pm\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right].

(236)

In addition, defining

\displaystyle\varphi^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}|\varphi_{\pm}^{\alpha}\rangle

(237)

\displaystyle=\left(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}\langle 0|\pm\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}\langle k|\right)\left(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}|0\rangle\pm\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\omega_{k}^{\alpha}|k\rangle\right),

(238)

\displaystyle=(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\omega_{k}^{\alpha}|^{2},

(239)

the probabilities become

\displaystyle P_{\pm}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\left[(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}a_{0}^{2}+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}z_{j}\right|^{2}\pm\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right]\right]+\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha}.

(240)

Now, by adding and subtracting these expressions, we obtain,

\displaystyle P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2(1-\lambda)\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left[\left(\omega_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{*}z_{j}+\omega_{j}^{\alpha}z_{j}^{*}\right],

(241)

\displaystyle P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2(1-\lambda)\left[(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0})^{2}+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{j}^{\alpha})^{*}z_{j}\right|^{2}\right]+2\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha}.

(242)

From the subtraction of the probabilities (
241
), we can define the following expression

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\omega_{k}^{\alpha}}{\beta_{0}^{\alpha}}\right)(P_{+}^{\alpha}-P_{-}^{\alpha}),

(243)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)a_{0}z_{k}.

(244)

To isolate the parameters
z_{k}
, we have to find the parameter
a_{0}
. This can be done by noticing that

|\Delta_{k}|^{2}=(1-\lambda)^{2}a_{0}^{2}\,|z_{k}|^{2}.

(245)

From the probabilities in the computational basis, we obtain the expressions (
232
) for the coefficients
|z_{k}|^{2}
as a function of
\lambda
. Substituting into the previous equation,

|\Delta_{k}|^{2}=(P_{0}-\lambda/d)(P_{k}-\lambda/d),\;k=1,\dots,d-1,

(246)

we arrive at a quadratic equation for
\lambda
,

\lambda^{2}-\lambda\,d(P_{0}+P_{k})+d^{2}\,(P_{0}P_{k}-|\Delta_{k}|^{2})=0.

(247)

With solutions,

\lambda_{k}=\frac{d}{2}\left[P_{0}+P_{k}\pm\sqrt{(P_{0}-P_{k})^{2}+4|\Delta_{k}|^{2}}\right].

(248)

Since
\lambda
is small, we consider the negative solution. As we have
d-1
equations for
\lambda
, we take the average of the solutions as our estimate,

\lambda=\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{k}.

(249)

The purity of the state
\rho
is related to the value of
\lambda
by

Tr(\rho^{2})=(1-\lambda)^{2}+\frac{\lambda(2-\lambda)}{d}.

(250)

This demonstrates that the tomography protocol enables us to verify the purity of the state. Knowing
\lambda
, we can obtain estimates of the coefficients
a_{0}
and
z_{k}
from equation (
232
). However, we can also obtain them from the following quadratic equation,

\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}=(1-\lambda)^{2}a_{0}^{2}(1-a_{0}^{2}),

(251)

From which we obtain a quadratic equation for
a_{0}^{2}
,

a_{0}^{4}-a_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}=0.

(252)

Now, from the expression for the sum of probabilities, we have that

\displaystyle P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2(1-\lambda)\left[(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0})^{2}+\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}z_{k}\right|^{2}\right]+2\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha}.

(253)

Furthermore, from the expression for the difference of probabilities, we can rearrange to obtain

z_{k}=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{a_{0}(1-\lambda)}.

(254)

Substituting this into the previous expression, we get

\displaystyle P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2(1-\lambda)\left[(\beta_{0}^{\alpha}a_{0})^{2}+\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}\frac{\Delta_{k}}{a_{0}(1-\lambda)}\right|^{2}\right]+2\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha}.

(255)

Rearranging terms, we obtain an equation for
a_{0}
,

a_{0}^{4}(1-\lambda)^{2}(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}-a_{0}^{2}(1-\lambda)((P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha})+\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}\Delta_{k}\right|^{2}=0.

(256)

By equating equations (
252
) and (
256
), we obtain a quadratic equation for
a_{0}
,

\displaystyle a_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{0}^{2}\frac{((P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha})}{(1-\lambda)(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}}-\frac{|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}\Delta_{k}|^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}},

(257)

Since
a_{0}>0
, we consider the positive solution,

a_{0}^{\alpha}=\sqrt{\frac{|\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\omega_{k}^{\alpha})^{*}\Delta_{k}|^{2}-(1-\lambda)(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}|\Delta_{k}|^{2}}{(1-\lambda)\left[(P_{+}^{\alpha}+P_{-}^{\alpha})/2-(1-\lambda)(\beta_{0}^{\alpha})^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{d}\varphi^{\alpha}\right]}}.

(259)

Noting that we have a solution for each
\alpha
, we take the average of all solutions as our estimate for
a_{0}
,

a_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}a_{0}^{\alpha}.

(260)

Finally, having the estimated values of
\lambda
and
a_{0}
, we can find the values of the coefficients
z_{k}
from expression (
244
),

z_{k}=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{2(1-\lambda)a_{0}}.

(261)

Thereby, we can estimate the unknown slightly mixed state
\rho
.

Given that
\rho
is highly pure, we expect that performing a near-optimal FSM for the state
\ket*{\Psi}
will improve the accuracy of its estimation. To achieve this, we use the preliminary estimator obtained from measurements in the computational basis together with the two non-optimal FSMs
\mathcal{E}_{\pm}
to construct a near-optimal FSM
\mathcal{E}
for
\ket*{\Psi}
. By combining the measurement data from all these settings, we obtain an enhanced final estimator of
\rho
.
