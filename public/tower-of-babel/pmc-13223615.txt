Global gaps in how we assess undergraduate medical students’ mental health: a scoping review protocol

Introduction

Mental health problems among healthcare professions represent a major and growing public health concern worldwide, emerging early during training and shaped by the cumulative impact of demanding educational pathways, emotional labour and organisational pressures.1 2 Healthcare students are exposed from the outset of their training to competitive learning environments, emotional stressors and, in some contexts, early clinical responsibility.3 These pressures have been associated with elevated levels of depression, anxiety, psychological distress and suicidal ideation, with potential consequences not only for individual well-being but also for professional development, patient safety and long-term workforce sustainability.1

Within this broad group, medical students have received particular attention due to the intensity and length of medical training, the early exposure to illness and death and the strong normative cultures surrounding performance and resilience.4,6 A substantial body of literature has documented high prevalence rates of psychological symptoms among medical students, often exceeding those observed in age-matched peers in the general population.7,9 These mental health challenges have been linked to impaired academic performance, reduced empathy, increased risk of burnout later in training and attrition from the medical profession.9 10 Despite the growth of this literature, substantially less attention has been paid to how mental health is actually assessed and operationalised across studies.

Undergraduate medical education combines academic overload, frequent examinations, transitions between preclinical and clinical phases and identity formation as future physicians.11 As such, undergraduate medical students constitute a distinct population whose mental health is shaped by both individual vulnerabilities and structural features of medical education systems.12 These challenges may be further exacerbated by inequities, such as sex, gender, ethnicity or disability, which can differentially influence exposure to stressors, access to support resources and experiences of inclusion or discrimination within medical training.13 14

Over the past two decades, research on the mental health of medical students has expanded rapidly. However, this expansion has occurred in the context of an increasingly heterogeneous and fragmented evidence base, reflecting wide variation in educational contexts, methodological approaches and conceptual frameworks used to define and measure mental health. Studies differ substantially in their settings, measurement tools, study designs and domains of interest, contributing to a literature that has grown in volume but lacks an overarching analytical framework.15

There is currently no comprehensive mapping of the global research landscape focused on the mental health of undergraduate medical students. In particular, a broader perspective remains lacking regarding how mental health is operationalised and studied across the literature, including the trajectory of research production over time, the distribution of thematic domains, methodological approaches, measurement practices and the structural organisation of the field, including geographical inequities, patterns of international collaboration, analytical strategies and educational contexts. As a result, it remains unclear whether the rapid increase in publications has been accompanied by proportional diversification in research topics, geographical settings, and methodological or analytical approaches.

Establishing such a structured overview is essential for organising future research, identifying priority gaps and avoiding further fragmentation of the evidence base. Moreover, a clearer understanding of how the field is currently structured may help align research efforts with policy needs, thereby enhancing the potential of student mental health research to inform prevention strategies and educational environments. Given the breadth of the field and the anticipated large volume of eligible studies, a scoping review is the most appropriate methodological approach to comprehensively map the existing literature and its gaps. This protocol outlines the methods for a global scoping review designed to map how mental health is operationalised, measured and studied across the quantitative literature on undergraduate medical students: examining the tools and constructs used, the thematic and methodological choices made and the geographical, equity-related and collaborative patterns that structure the field.

Methods and analysis

A preliminary search of PROSPERO, MEDLINE (Ovid), the Cochrane Library (Wiley) and BMJ Open was conducted prior to protocol development, combining keywords and subject headings related to ‘medical students’, ‘mental health’ and ‘scoping review’ or ‘systematic review’, with no restrictions on date or country. It identified no ongoing or published scoping reviews aiming to map the global quantitative literature on undergraduate medical student mental health. The review is planned to be conducted between April 2026 and December 2026, including literature search, screening, data extraction, and synthesis.

Study design and reporting standards

This scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews,16 and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR, online supplemental material).17 A scoping review design was selected because the literature on undergraduate medical students’ mental health is broad, multidisciplinary and methodologically heterogeneous, encompassing diverse thematic domains, conceptual frameworks, measurement tools and study designs across multiple geographical and educational settings. The objective of this review is therefore not to estimate pooled effect sizes or evaluate intervention effectiveness, but rather to comprehensively map the structure of the literature, characterise methodological and thematic patterns, identify research gaps and examine how mental health is currently assessed across studies and regions.17 The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/2EHNU).18

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria are defined according to the Population–Concept–Context framework.16

Population

Studies will be eligible if they focus on undergraduate medical students enrolled in education institutions, including universities, colleges and equivalent international post-secondary settings. Studies focusing exclusively on other academic disciplines, medical residents or interns and working healthcare practitioners will therefore be excluded to maintain a clear focus on the student academic experience.

Concept

The review will include quantitative studies assessing student mental health using standardised or clearly defined quantitative measures (at least one previously validated multi-item scale per study). Studies using exclusively open-ended questionnaire formats without quantitative scoring or standardised response options will be excluded. Studies that do not use any quantitative mental health measure will be excluded. This restriction was chosen to ensure methodological consistency and enable meaningful comparisons across studies, geographical regions, thematic domains and analytical approaches. While qualitative and non-standardised approaches may provide important complementary perspectives on mental health experiences and conceptualisations, the objective of the present review is specifically to map how mental health constructs are operationalised, measured and analysed within the global quantitative literature. The exclusion of qualitative studies and non-standardised approaches will be acknowledged as a limitation of the review.

To preserve the exploratory and mapping objectives of this scoping review, the concept of mental health will be intentionally defined in broad and inclusive terms. Operationally, mental health will encompass psychological symptoms and distress, as well as related constructs commonly used in the literature to capture students’ psychological functioning and well-being across personal, relational, academic and professional domains. This inclusive definition aligns with internationally recognised frameworks that conceptualise mental health as a multidimensional continuum subsuming both deficit-based indicators of mental illness and strength-based constructs such as subjective well-being and positive functioning, rather than a discrete clinical state.18,20 By adopting a deliberately broad operational definition while requiring validated quantitative measurement, this review aims to capture the diversity of constructs and approaches used across disciplines and regions, without restricting inclusion to specific diagnoses, theoretical models, or clinical thresholds.18,20

Context

All geographical regions and country income levels will be eligible, with no restriction on cultural or institutional context. Eligible study designs include cross-sectional studies, cohort studies and experimental or quasi-experimental studies reporting quantitative mental health outcomes. Editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts without full data, reviews, study protocols and purely qualitative studies will be excluded. No publication date or language restrictions will be applied.17 For studies published in languages other than English, translation will be sought from a native or fluent speaker of the language whenever feasible. When this is not possible, artificial intelligence-based translation tools will be used. Two independent translation tools will be applied and their outputs compared; in cases of substantive discrepancy, a third tool will be consulted to resolve inconsistencies.

Information sources

The following electronic databases will be searched: MEDLINE (Ovid), Web of Science (Clarivate), Cochrane Library (Wiley) and PsycINFO (Ovid). Grey literature will not be systematically searched. This decision reflects the primary aim of this review, which is to characterise peer-reviewed academic research output in order to examine how student mental health has been conceptualised, measured and prioritised within the scientific literature, rather than to map policy documents or implementation reports.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed by the author team, bringing together expertise in medical education research, quantitative methodology and evidence synthesis. It combines terms related to medical students with terms related to mental health, well-being and psychological outcomes. The search strategy was peer-reviewed by a different author with expertise in information retrieval (ADJ) using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines.21 The full MEDLINE search strategy is provided in table 1.

Study selection

All stages of study selection will be conducted manually. All records retrieved from the database searches will be imported into reference management software and duplicates will be removed.16 Titles and abstracts will then be screened independently by two reviewers (FM and JP).16 Full texts of potentially eligible articles will be assessed independently by the same reviewers.16 Discrepancies at any stage will be resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer if necessary (GC).16 The study selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.22

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form will be developed and pilot-tested on a subset of included studies by two authors (FM and JP).16 For each included study, the following information will be extracted: bibliographical characteristics (study identifier, first author, year of publication); geographic information (country of data collection, number of countries involved); and study size.

Study design characteristics will include whether the study is longitudinal or cross-sectional, interventional or observational, and single-centre or multicentre, as well as whether the study was conducted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Detailed information on measurement tools will be collected, including the number of mental health scales used per study and the specific instruments employed, with scale-level details recorded for each measure.

In addition, data will be extracted on the consideration of equity-related variables, including sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, race or ethnicity, and socioeconomic status based on their reporting and analytical use within each study. Information on the total number of questionnaire items will also be recorded to characterise measurement burden. These variables will enable a comprehensive description of methodological patterns, thematic emphasis, and equity integration across the literature, as well as indicators of international collaboration.

Thematic classification framework

An a priori thematic classification framework was developed by the author team based on an iterative review of the literature and expert consensus among investigators with expertise in mental health research, medical education and evidence synthesis.423,25 Each scale used in the included studies will be classified according to a predefined thematic domain and subdomain (table 2). Five distinct thematic domains were defined to capture the breadth of mental health constructs assessed in the literature, encompassing (1) psychological symptoms and distress, (2) psychological resources, (3) the academic environment, (4) social support and (5) physical health and lifestyle-related factors. Operationalisation of equity variables

The consideration of equity-related variables will be primarily operationalised dichotomously, based on their analytical use within each study. Equity variables will be classified as either analytically used, when incorporated into statistical models as covariates, primary independent variables or effect modifiers, or not analytically used, when they are absent from analyses or reported descriptively only.

To better capture the heterogeneity in the depth of equity integration, a secondary, more granular classification will also be applied. Studies will be further categorised according to the level of analytical engagement with equity variables, distinguishing: (1) univariate or subgroup analyses, (2) multivariable adjustment and (3) more advanced approaches, including intersectional analyses or explicitly equity-focused frameworks. This complementary categorisation will allow a more nuanced interpretation of how equity is operationalised across the literature.

Data synthesis

Results will be synthesised descriptively in accordance with JBI methodological guidance for scoping reviews.16 Frequencies and proportions will be used to summarise study characteristics across time periods, geographical regions, thematic domains and equity categories. Findings will be presented using tables and visualisations, including a PRISMA flow diagram, a global map of study distribution, temporal trend figures and thematic representations. Consistent with scoping review methodology and the objective of mapping the evidence base rather than estimating effects, no formal risk of bias assessment will be conducted.17

Patient and public involvement

Undergraduate medical students or members of the public were not directly involved in the design of this scoping review. As this study synthesises bibliometric and methodological characteristics of published research rather than individual-level outcomes, direct student involvement was limited during the protocol development phase. However, informal input was sought from student representatives and student organisations during the development of the thematic framework to enhance its relevance and interpretability. To enhance the relevance and accessibility of the findings, a plain-language summary of the results will be produced and shared with student organisations, university networks and academic stakeholders as part of the dissemination strategy.

Protocol amendments

Any deviations from this protocol will be documented, dated and justified in the final publication.

Discussion of the protocol

This scoping review protocol outlines a comprehensive strategy to map the global quantitative literature on undergraduate medical students’ mental health, with a specific focus on thematic domains, geographical distribution, methodological design and equity-related reporting. The proposed work addresses an important gap in the research landscape by systematically characterising not only the volume of research, but also patterns of diversity and persistent blind spots, including the representation of low- and middle-income countries and the analytical depth of equity-oriented variables. By adopting a broad inclusion framework and a detailed thematic classification, this review will provide a timely and structured atlas of existing evidence capable of informing future research agendas and decision-making processes.

Beyond academic mapping, these findings have the potential to inform funding priorities and strategic decision-making by universities and higher education institutions, national ministries of health and education and public or philanthropic funding agencies. By identifying under-researched thematic determinants and neglected student subgroups, this review may support more evidence-informed planning of mental health policies and resource allocation.19 In particular, the identification of thematic and geographical gaps may help shift future efforts from a narrow focus on symptom surveillance toward preventive strategies, academic and learning environment interventions and institution-level policies aimed at addressing structural drivers of psychological distress among students.

This scoping review protocol has several important strengths. First, it addresses a clearly defined and policy-relevant gap by focusing specifically on undergraduate medical students, a population exposed to unique academic, emotional and institutional pressures during a formative stage of professional development.4 By restricting inclusion to quantitative studies using validated measurement instruments, the review will enable a structured and comparable mapping of how mental health has been assessed across studies, regions and time periods. The use of an a priori thematic classification framework, developed through literature review and expert consensus, provides a coherent structure to synthesise a highly heterogeneous body of evidence spanning psychological symptoms, resources, academic environments, social support and physical and lifestyle-related factors. Then, the protocol is underpinned by a rigorous and transparent methodological approach. Adherence to PRISMA-ScR guidelines, peer review of the search strategy using the PRESS framework and duplicate independent screening and data extraction strengthen reproducibility and minimise selection bias. The operationalisation of equity-related variables based on their analytical use (rather than descriptive reporting alone) represents a methodological strength, allowing a focused assessment of whether and how equity considerations are meaningfully integrated into quantitative analyses. In addition, the planned public release of the full extracted dataset and analytical code reflects a strong commitment to open science, transparency and reusability.26 27

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the decision not to systematically search grey literature may result in the omission of institutional reports or policy documents relevant to medical student mental health, particularly in settings where academic publication is less prevalent. However, this choice is consistent with the primary objective of characterising peer-reviewed research output and avoids further heterogeneity in study design and measurement quality. Second, although no language restrictions will be applied, reliance on translation (whether through fluent speakers or artificial intelligence-based tools) may introduce minor inaccuracies in the interpretation of non-English studies, particularly for nuanced methodological details. Third, the exclusive focus on quantitative studies necessarily excludes qualitative research, which can provide in-depth insights into lived experiences, contextual factors and mechanisms underlying psychological distress. Consequently, this review will not capture experiential or narrative dimensions of mental health. In addition, excluding qualitative and non-standardised approaches may limit the ability of the review to fully reflect the diversity of assessment practices and conceptualisations present within the broader literature. Finally, the scope of this review precludes formal appraisal of individual study quality, which is consistent with scoping review methodology but limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding the strength of evidence for specific associations or causal relationships.

Despite these limitations, this scoping review protocol outlines a robust and systematic approach to mapping a complex and fragmented field. By providing a comprehensive overview of thematic, geographical, methodological and equity-related patterns in the literature, this scoping review will offer a valuable framework to guide future research priorities and support the development of more targeted, equitable and context-sensitive approaches to mental health in undergraduate medical education.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will involve neither human participants nor unpublished data. Consequently, approval from a human research ethics committee is not required. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations. We will not use the assistance of a professional writer. To promote transparency, reproducibility and reuse, the full extracted dataset and all analytical code will be made openly available in a public repository.
