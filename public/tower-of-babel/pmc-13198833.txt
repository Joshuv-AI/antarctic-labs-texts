Skin-Colored Nodular Facial Swelling in a Middle-Aged Female
Authors: Apoorva Sharma, Debajyoti Chatterjee, Keshavamurthy Vinay
Published: 2026

A female in her late forties with no prior comorbidities presented with a slowly enlarging, asymptomatic nodular swelling on the face for the past 3 years. Examination revealed a skin-colored to light pink, dome-shaped, well-circumscribed nodular lesion of size 1.5 cm × 1.2 cm with a smooth surface showing a single indentation, with no ulceration or crusting, present on the right side of the face just below the malar eminence [Figure 1]. It was firm in consistency, non-tender and mobile over the deeper structures. There was no regional lymphadenopathy. Dermoscopy revealed a pinkish background, ill-focused vessels, arborising and short-linear vessels and a white veil [Figure 2]. Gross examination of the cut surface of the specimen revealed a tumor that was predominantly solid (95%) with cystic areas (5%). The solid component was gray-white, homogeneous, and firm.

Dermoscopy revealed the presence of a pink background, arborising and short linear, ill-defined vessels and white veil (Dermlite IV, polarized, ×10)

Representative histopathology sections showed a relatively well-circumscribed tumor in the dermis with a focal epidermal connection. The tumor showed multiple nodules with solid lobular proliferations and cystic spaces filled with homogenous eosinophilic material and focal calcification. Some lobules showed duct-like structures or tubular lumina, consistent with sweat gland origin. The tumor cells within these lobules showed variable morphology. One population of cells was epidermoid with eosinophilic cytoplasm, while another population of tumor cells had abundant clear cytoplasm. Some of the tumor cells had intracytoplasmic mucin. The tumor cells showed mild atypia with scarce mitotic activity [Figure 3].

What is the diagnosis?

Answer:

Nodular hidradenoma

Hidradenoma is an uncommon sweat gland tumor with either eccrine or apocrine differentiation. Although the tumor is regarded as a benign neoplasm, it has a high recurrence rate and a low likelihood of malignant transformation; hence, it should be removed with safety margins. Additional dermoscopic features, other than the ones described in the present case are hairpin-like veins and amorphous whitish areas.[1] A close differential diagnosis considered was eccrine poroma with similar clinical and dermoscopic features. Nevertheless, the glomerular, leaf and flower-like blood vessels, and interlacing white cords indicative of eccrine poroma were not observed.[2] Poroma histologically shows small, uniform basaloid cells forming broad anastomosing bands or nests connected to the epidermis, with ductal differentiation and minimal cytologic atypia, distinguishing it from nodular hidradenoma. Another differential considered in the present case was basal cell carcinoma (BCC). Nodular BCC is frequently seen as a pearly or translucent papule/nodule, often with telangiectasia and surface ulceration. Dermoscopic features include arborising vessels, blue-grey ovoid nests, maple leaf-like areas, and spoke-wheel areas, none of which were seen in the present case. The literature has also reported a rare clear cell hidradenoma-basal cell carcinoma collision tumor.[3] Pilomatricoma commonly presents as a firm, solitary, skin-colored to reddish-blue nodule; it often has a chalky or gritty feel and can calcify. Overlying skin may show telangiectasia or ulceration, and the lesion may be tethered. Histopathology shows a mixture of basophilic cells (peripheral) and eosinophilic “ghost” or shadow cells (central), which is a characteristic finding of pilomatricoma.[4]

Immunohistochemical markers such as epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and cytokeratin (CK) 7 are typically positive in nodular hidradenoma [Table 1], highlighting ductal differentiation, whereas Ber-EP4 and B-cell lymphoma (Bcl-2) protein are more characteristic of nodular BCC, and β-catenin or lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1) may help identify pilomatricoma, thereby assisting in differentiating these morphologically overlapping tumors.[5]

Nodular hidradenoma most commonly occurs in adults between 30 and 50 years of age and favors the head, neck, and upper extremities. Two cell types are found in the solid areas: clear cells with glycogen and polygonal cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm. One crucial diagnostic characteristic is ductal differentiation. In some cases, immunohistochemistry may reveal positive results for CK and EMA, both epithelial markers. Benign nodular hidradenoma is typically well-circumscribed, shows a biphasic cell population, may exhibit mild atypia and rare mitoses, but lacks necrosis or vascular/perineural invasion. In contrast, hidradenocarcinoma demonstrates infiltrative margins, marked cytologic atypia, increased (often atypical) mitoses, necrosis, and may show vascular or perineural invasion.[6]

Nodular hidradenoma poses significant diagnostic challenges due to its clinical and histopathological resemblance to other adnexal tumors. Recognition of its characteristic biphasic cell population, ductal differentiation, and solid–cystic architecture is essential for accurate diagnosis. Awareness of this entity among clinicians and pathologists ensures timely diagnosis, appropriate management, and better long-term outcomes for affected patients.
