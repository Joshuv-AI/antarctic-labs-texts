Less is More: Efficient Black-box Attribution via Minimal Interpretable Subset Selection
Ruoyu Chen, Siyuan Liang, Jingzhi Li, Shiming Liu, Li Liu, Hua Zhang, Xiaochun Cao

Abstract
To develop a trustworthy AI system, it is essential to understand its behavior using attribution methods, which aim to identify the input regions that most influence the model’s decisions. For discrete inputs such as images, however, accurately identifying input–prediction interactions is particularly challenging because of combinatorial explosion. We formulate black-box attribution as an ordered interpretable subset-selection problem. Inspired by the marginal-gain principle of submodular optimization, the formulation permits redundancy, positive complementarity, and negative interference among input sub-regions. We propose LiMA ( Less input is More faithful for Attribution ), which ranks sub-regions by their contributions along a sequence of selected subsets. LiMA employs a composite objective that evaluates subsets through semantic consistency, collaboration, prediction confidence, and semantic effectiveness. We further design a bidirectional greedy search to efficiently identify important and non-critical sub-regions. Experiments on eight foundation models (CLIP, ImageBind, LanguageBind, QuiltNet, etc. ) and six datasets (ImageNet, VGG-Sound, CUB-200-2011, etc. ) demonstrate strong generalization and faithful interpretations with fewer sub-regions. LiMA achieves state-of-the-art Insertion, Deletion, and average highest confidence, with average improvements of 36.3% in Insertion and 39.6% in Deletion; it is also 1.6 times faster than naive greedy search. For failure attribution on incorrect predictions, LiMA improves average highest confidence by 86.1% over state-of-the-art methods. The code is available at https://github.com/RuoyuChen10/LIMA .

The core of the attribution problem is to
determine the importance of individual sub-regions and identify the subset of sub-regions
that has the most significant impact on the final decision.

Inspired by submodular optimization
[
30
]
, which evaluates candidate elements through their marginal gains to a selected subset, we seek to enhance model interpretability by selecting and ordering fewer sub-regions. However, attribution objectives induced by modern neural networks need not satisfy the classical diminishing-returns assumption. Redundant sub-regions may exhibit overlapping utility, whereas complementary sub-regions may produce positive synergy, such that their joint response is greater than the sum of their individual responses. Background or distractor sub-regions may also produce negative marginal gains. We therefore formulate attribution as an ordered subset-selection problem and use submodular optimization as a design inspiration for marginal-gain-based search, without assuming that the resulting attribution objective is globally submodular or monotone. In this paper, we propose
LiMA
(
Less input is More faithful for Attribution
), a novel and efficient black-box attribution mechanism based on ordered interpretable subset selection. LiMA first sparsifies an input into a limited set of fine-grained sub-regions using a semantic- or patch-based division method. It then uses a composite objective function to evaluate candidate subsets from four complementary perspectives: semantic consistency, collective effect, prediction confidence, and semantic effectiveness. A bidirectional greedy search algorithm simultaneously identifies important and non-critical sub-regions, producing an ordered sequence whose prefixes provide explanations at different region budgets.

II
Related Work

II-A

Attribution Methods

Inner propagation, activation, and gradient-based methods
analyze the internal responses of the network to identify the most important regions of the input. These methods are often referred to as white-box attribution methods because they require access to the model’s internals. Some methods attribute importance by propagating scores back through the layers until reaching the input layer
[
21
]
.
Other methods rely solely on decision gradients for attribution
[
41
]
, but these approaches evaluate only the individual effects of pixels or features, without accounting for their collective impact
[
42
]
.
Some methods combine network activations and gradients for attribution, including Grad-CAM
[
23
]
, Grad-CAM++
[
43
]
, Score-CAM
[
44
]
, ViT-CX
[
45
]
, and Grad-ECLIP
[
25
]
. However, the effectiveness of these methods depends heavily on the selection of network layers, which significantly influences the quality of interpretation
[
12
]
. Moreover, these methods are largely heuristic and lack theoretical guarantees. Path-based methods
[
22
,
24
]
achieve attribution by selecting a specific integral path. However, this approach is highly sensitive to parameters such as baseline and path choice. Additionally, since the gradient must be integrated, applying this method to large models may be limited by computational resources.

Shapley value estimation-based methods
attribute model predictions by estimating the Shapley value
[
46
]
of each input region or feature. This is accomplished by calculating the marginal contribution of each region or feature and combining these contributions linearly. However, calculating the Shapley value is an exponential complexity problem. To address this, some model-agnostic methods estimate SHAP values by sampling subsets of features. For example, Kernel SHAP
[
26
]
determines feature importance through sampling and the application of specially weighted linear regression. EAC
[
27
]
estimates Shapley values by sampling sub-regions defined by the Segment Anything model and using a linear surrogate model. In addition, model-specific methods have been developed to reduce redundant subsets by leveraging unique model structures. HarsanyiNet
[
47
]
, for instance, employs a specialized network based on a CNN architecture to accurately estimate Shapley values in a single propagation, though its scalability is limited.
Despite these innovations, the exact computation of Shapley values remains generally impractical due to the exponential complexity associated with increasing data dimensions, making it nearly impossible to apply these methods to large models. Additionally, Kumar
et al.

[
48
]
observed that feature importance evaluation methods based on the Shapley value estimation may risk underestimating the importance of relevant features, resulting in all such features being assigned lower importance values.

Peturbation-based methods
operate under the assumption that the internal parameters of the model are unknowable. They assess the importance of input regions by perturbing the input and observing the resulting changes in the output. LIME
[
49
]
locally approximates the predictions of a black-box model with a linear model, by only slightly perturbing the input. RISE
[
20
]
perturbs the model by inputting multiple random masks and weighting the masks to get the final saliency map. HSIC-Attribution method
[
12
]
measures the dependence between the input image and the model output through Hilbert Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) based on RISE.
Although these methods are highly portable and applicable to various network architectures, their attribution performance is affected by the grid size used for perturbation
[
12
]
. If the grid size is too large, the attribution performance may decline, resulting in insufficient granularity in the attribution regions
[
11
]
.

Search-based methods
attribute the importance of different regions by ranking the sub-regions in order. Shitole
et al.

[
50
]
defined minimal sufficient explanation, which involves identifying a limited region whose confidence response to the model is at least 90% of the response of the entire image. They utilized a heuristic beam search to find these regions.
Accordingly, their objective is to identify a compact set of regions that is sufficient to preserve at least 90% of the model confidence obtained from the entire image.
However, this method is only effective when the model exhibits a high prediction confidence, and searching for sub-regions based solely on changes in confidence is often unstable
[
11
]
.
The objective of MoXI
[
51
]
is to identify a group of pixels that yields high prediction confidence by jointly modeling the individual contributions of pixels and their cooperative effects using Shapley values and Shapley interactions.
Chen
et al.

[
11
]
addressed the challenge of searching for high-confidence regions by modeling the image attribution problem as a submodular subset selection problem. However, the performance of this method is influenced by the quality of a prior saliency map.

Multimodal Attribution:
Traditional explanation methods mainly focus on single-modal models like DNNs and CNNs, with emerging approaches for multi-modal models. Some studies have investigated attention mechanisms in multimodal ViT models for attributing input or observation influence on outputs
[
52
]
, but these methods often lack transferability as they rely on access to internal parameters. Darcet
et al.

[
53
]
leveraged attention maps in ViT as guidance and addressed artifacts by incorporating additional tokens. However, attention maps merely reflect intermediate response strengths without explicitly capturing input-output relationships. Zhao
et al.

[
25
]
attributed the CLIP model using gradients and feature maps, achieving better explanation results than self-attention. Gandelsman
et al.

[
54
]
approximated CLIP token attribution via MLP and multi-head attention decomposition, but the results were limited by omitting layer normalization.

In this paper, we propose an ordered subset-selection approach inspired by the marginal-gain principle of submodular optimization. By sparsifying the input, we refine it into fine-grained sub-regions and identify important evidence through a context-dependent composite objective. Unlike classical submodular formulations, our objective permits complementary interactions and negative marginal contributions, which commonly arise in neural attribution.

II-B

Error Explanation

Identifying and explaining a model’s errors allows us to understand its flaws better and address them more efficiently.
Wu
et al.

[
55
]
identified unstable concepts as false attributes and intervened in these concepts to correct model decisions. However, this process requires precise concept annotations, which is cumbersome. Abid
et al.

[
56
]
evaluated the impact of specific concepts on misclassified images using concept activation vectors, thereby explaining model prediction errors from a conceptual perspective. However, concerns remain about whether traditional unimodal models can truly learn explicit concepts and accurately assess their importance
[
57
]
.

In this paper, our method analyzes an individual misclassified sample at the input level by identifying a compact region whose revelation reverses the model prediction to the ground-truth class. This intervention provides model-level counterfactual evidence associated with prediction reversal.

Submodular Optimization

Submodular optimization
[
30
]
has been successfully studied in multiple application scenarios
[
58
]
, for example, He
et al.

[
59
]
combined submodular subset selection with a loss function and Shapley value to evaluate and select modality importance in multimodal learning. A small number of studies have also applied this theory to explore model interpretability. Catav
et al.

[
60
]
utilized the maximum marginal contribution from all possible subsets to explain the data’s contribution. Elenberg
et al.

[
61
]
frame the interpretability of black-box classifiers as a combinatorial maximization problem, it achieves similar results to LIME and is more efficient. Chen
et al.

[
62
]
propose a learnable network for instance-wise feature selection to explain deep models, but its scalability is limited. Pervez
et al.

[
63
]
proposed a simple subset sampling alternative based on conditional Poisson sampling, which they applied to interpret both image and text recognition tasks.
However, these methods only retained the selected important pixels and observed the recognition accuracy
[
62
]
. Chhabra
et al.

[
64
]
interpreted the feature space through submodular subset selection to determine which data is helpful to improve model performance.

In this paper, we propose an attribution method based on ordered interpretable subset selection, achieving state-of-the-art empirical performance according to standard attribution metrics. The method is evaluated across diverse datasets, modalities, and network architectures, including analyses of both correct and incorrect predictions.

II-D

Greedy Search

The greedy search algorithm
[
65
]
, the most commonly used approach, optimizes submodular functions by iterating through unselected examples and calculating the gain in function value from adding each to the selected set. Some accelerated greedy search algorithms have been proposed. Mirzasoleiman
et al.

[
66
]
proposed a stochastic-greedy algorithm, which randomly samples a portion of the unselected examples and performs greedy selection on that subset. Joseph
et al.

[
67
]
partition the samples into equal-sized, non-overlapping sets, perform greedy selection within each, then merge and further select elements.
Note that most of the above acceleration algorithms focus on subset selection rather than the order of samples. Consequently, they are unsuitable for attribution tasks requiring ordered subset search
[
11
]
.

In this paper, we propose a bidirectional greedy search strategy to reduce the practical candidate-evaluation overhead and measured runtime of attribution for large models while identifying both important and non-critical sub-regions. During the forward search, the method generates candidates with low marginal objective values and uses them to construct the reverse sequence. Combining the two sequences produces a complete ordered sequence.

III
Preliminaries

We first introduce the marginal-gain principle that motivates our ordered subset-selection formulation. Let
V
be a finite set of sub-regions and let
\mathcal{F}:2^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a set-dependent objective. The marginal gain of a candidate sub-region
\alpha\in V\setminus S
is
\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha\mid S)=\mathcal{F}(S\cup\{\alpha\})-\mathcal{F}(S)
. Classical submodular optimization formalizes a diminishing-returns relation between such marginal gains:

Definition 1

(Submodular function
[
68
]
)
.

For any
S_{a}\subseteq S_{b}\subseteq V
and
\alpha\in V\setminus S_{b}
, a set function
\mathcal{F}
is submodular if

\mathcal{F}\left(S_{a}\cup\{\alpha\}\right)-\mathcal{F}\left(S_{a}\right)\geq\mathcal{F}\left(S_{b}\cup\{\alpha\}\right)-\mathcal{F}\left(S_{b}\right).

(1)

Monotonicity is a separate property requiring
\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha\mid S)\geq 0
. We do not impose either global submodularity or monotonicity on the proposed attribution objective. Neural model responses can contain redundant evidence that resembles diminishing returns, complementary evidence that yields increasing marginal gains, and distractors that yield negative marginal gains. LiMA therefore borrows the marginal-gain-based selection principle from submodular optimization, while optimizing a general, potentially non-submodular and non-monotone objective.

Fig. 2:
The framework of the proposed
LiMA
method. We first divide the input into interpretable sub-regions using a superpixel- or semantic-based strategy. A composite objective and bidirectional greedy search then identify important and non-critical sub-regions and arrange them into an ordered sequence. Finally, consistency- and collaboration-based marginal changes are used to assign attribution scores. The resulting prefixes provide explanations at different region budgets and are evaluated using faithfulness metrics.

Problem formulation:
We divide an input data
\mathbf{I}
into a finite number of sub-regions with a division algorithm
\texttt{Div}(\cdot)
, denoted as
V=\texttt{Div}(\mathbf{I})=\left\{\mathbf{I}^{M}_{1},\mathbf{I}^{M}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{I}^{M}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}\right\}
, where
N=|V|
denotes the number of divided sub-regions, and

M
indicates a sub-region
\mathbf{I}^{M}
formed by masking part of
\mathbf{I}
.

Throughout this paper, a
sub-region
denotes an atomic interpretable unit generated by
\operatorname{Div}(\cdot)
and contained in the set
V
, whereas a
region
refers to a spatial area formed by one or more sub-regions. The term
element
is reserved for generic set-theoretic definitions and proofs.

Attribution seeks an ordering
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{N})
of the sub-regions. Its prefix
S_{t}=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{t}\}
represents the explanation available under a budget of
t
sub-regions. Given the composite objective
\mathcal{F}
, a forward marginal-gain order is constructed by

\displaystyle\pi_{t}

\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{\alpha\in V\setminus S_{t-1}}\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha\mid S_{t-1}),

(2)

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle=S_{t-1}\cup\{\pi_{t}\}.

A standard forward greedy ranking performs
\mathcal{O}(kN)
candidate-objective evaluations for a prefix of length
k
. Specifically, it performs
kN-\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)
candidate evaluations, and ranking all
N
sub-regions performs
\frac{1}{2}N^{2}+\frac{1}{2}N
evaluations, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
.

IV-A

Sub-region Division

To obtain the interpretable region in an image, we partition the image
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times h\times 3}
into
m
sub-regions
\mathbf{I}^{M}
with a division algorithm
\texttt{Div}(\cdot)
, where
M
indicates a sub-region
\mathbf{I}^{M}
formed by masking part of the image
\mathbf{I}
. The division algorithm determines the quality of the search space, thereby influencing the effectiveness of the attribution
[
11
]
. Our recommended division strategies
\texttt{Div}(\cdot)
are as follows:

•

Superpixel-based division
involves clustering pixels with similar characteristics. For image modality, methods like SLICO
[
69
]
can be employed to perform this division, thereby improving attribution efficiency.

•

Semantic-based division
can more effectively distinguish between different concepts and enhance human understanding. Segment Anything (SAM)
[
70
]
can be used for zero-shot division. However, since the regions segmented by SAM may overlap, we propose a SAM-based division method. Details can be found in Appendix
E
, Algorithm
2
.

•

Patch-based division
has been studied in traditional methods
[
71
,
20
]
, which can divide the input into regular patch regions.

We can select the appropriate division method for attribution based on the specific input modality.

IV-B

Composite Objective Function Design

In this section,
we construct an objective function to evaluate candidate subsets and determine an importance order over interpretable sub-regions. The objective combines four complementary criteria: consistency, collaboration, confidence, and effectiveness. These criteria capture different aspects of subset quality.

Consistency Score:

We aim to make the representation of the identified image region consistent with the original semantics. Given a target semantic feature or mapping function,
\boldsymbol{f}_{s}
, we make the semantic features of the searched image region close to the target semantic features. We introduce the consistency score:

s_{\mathrm{cons.}}\left(S,\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)=\frac{F\left(\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M}\right)}{\left\|F\left(\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M}\right)\right\|}\circ\boldsymbol{f}_{s},

(3)

where
F(\cdot)
denotes a pre-trained feature extractor. The target semantic feature
\boldsymbol{f}_{s}
, can either adopt the features computed from the original image using the pre-trained feature extractor, expressed as
\boldsymbol{f}_{s}=F\left(\mathbf{I}\right)
, or directly implement the fully connected layer of the classifier for a specified class.
By incorporating the
s_{\mathrm{cons.}}
, our method targets regions that reinforce the desired semantic response. This approach ensures a precise selection that aligns closely with our specific semantic goals.

Collaboration Score:
Some individual
sub-regions
may lack significant individual effects in isolation, but when placed within the context of a group or system, they exhibit an indispensable collective effect. Therefore, we introduce the collaboration score, which is defined as:

s_{\mathrm{colla.}}\left(S,\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)=1-\frac{F\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M}\right)}{\left\|F\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M}\right)\right\|}\circ\boldsymbol{f}_{s},

(4)

where
F(\cdot)
denotes a pre-trained feature extractor,
\boldsymbol{f}_{s}
is the target semantic feature. By introducing the collaboration score, we can judge the collective effect of the
sub-region
. By incorporating the
s_{\mathrm{colla.}}
, our method pinpoints regions whose exclusion markedly affects the model’s predictive confidence. This effect underscores the pivotal role of these regions, indicative of a significant collective impact. Such a metric is particularly valuable in the initial stages of the search, highlighting regions essential for sustaining the model’s accuracy and reliability.

Confidence Score:
The model’s confidence in its own predictions is also closely related to interpretability
[
72
]
and the entropy of the model output is a key indicator for measuring the uncertainty of the model’s predictions
[
73
,
74
]
. Therefore, we use the entropy of the network output to calculate the confidence score.
In the inference, the predictive uncertainty can be calculated as
u=-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{C}P(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x})\log{P(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x})}}{\log{C}}
, where
u\in[0,1]
,
P(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x})
is the probability of a specific class output,
C
is the total number of classes. Thus, the confidence score of the sample
\mathbf{x}
predicted by the network can be expressed as:

s_{\mathrm{conf.}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=1-u=1+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{C}P(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x})\log{P(y_{i}\mid\mathbf{x})}}{\log{C}}.

(5)

By incorporating the
s_{\mathrm{conf.}}
, we can ensure that the selected regions align closely with the In-Distribution (InD). This score acts as a reliable metric to distinguish regions from out-of-distribution, ensuring alignment with the InD.

Effectiveness Score:
Adding different sub-regions may exhibit OR interactions, meaning they have similar marginal contribution scores
[
28
]
. We expect to maximize the response of valuable information with fewer regions since some image regions have the same semantic representation. Given
a candidate sub-region

\alpha
, and a subset
S
, we measure the distance between the
sub-region

\alpha
and all
candidate sub-regions
in the set, and calculate the smallest distance as the effectiveness score of the judgment
candidate sub-region

\alpha
for the subset
S
:

s_{e}\left(\alpha\mid S\right)=\min_{s_{i}\in S}{\mathrm{dist}\left(F\left(\alpha\right),F\left(s_{i}\right)\right)},

(6)

where
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the equation to calculate the distance between two
candidate sub-regions
.

When
S=\varnothing
, we define
s_{e}(\alpha\mid S)=0
, since there is no selected sub-region against which to measure redundancy.
Traditional distance measurement methods
[
75
,
76
]
are tailored to maximize the decision margins between classes during model training, involving operations like feature scaling and increasing angle margins. In contrast, our method focuses solely on calculating the relative distance between features, for which we utilize the general cosine distance.

F(\cdot)
denotes a pre-trained feature extractor. To calculate the effectiveness score of a set, we can compute the sum of the effectiveness scores for each
candidate sub-region
:

s_{\mathrm{eff.}}\left(S\right)=\sum_{s_{i}\in S}\min_{s_{j}\in S,s_{i}\neq s_{j}}{\mathrm{dist}\left(F\left(s_{i}\right),F\left(s_{j}\right)\right)}.

(7)

By maximizing the
s_{\mathrm{eff.}}
, we aim to limit the selection of regions with similar semantic representations, thereby increasing the diversity and improving the overall quality of region selection.

Composite Objective Function:
We combine the above scores into the following objective for selecting sub-regions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S)=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}s_{\mathrm{cons.}}\left(S,\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)+\lambda_{2}s_{\mathrm{colla.}}\left(S,\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{f}_{s}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{3}s_{\mathrm{conf.}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M}\right)+\lambda_{4}s_{\mathrm{eff.}}\left(S\right),

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
, and
\lambda_{4}
represent the weighting factors used to balance each score. According to the importance and experience of the scores, we set these parameters to
\lambda_{1}=20
,
\lambda_{2}=5
,
\lambda_{3}=0.05
, and
\lambda_{4}=0.01
, respectively.

The raw scales of the four criteria are not inherently aligned. Consistency and collaboration inherit the scale of representation similarity, confidence is based on normalized predictive entropy, and effectiveness aggregates representation distances and therefore also varies with the number of selected sub-regions. The coefficients consequently provide empirical scale and trade-off control across these complementary criteria.

The coefficients
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
, and
\lambda_{4}
balance terms with different numerical scales and practical roles. We use
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})=(20,5,0.05,0.01)
as an empirically selected default and keep it fixed throughout the main comparative evaluations.

The four criteria play complementary roles. Consistency rewards subsets that preserve the target semantics, whereas collaboration rewards subsets whose removal disrupts those semantics and thus captures removal-based necessity. Confidence discourages highly uncertain predictions on masked inputs, and effectiveness promotes diversity among selected sub-regions by penalizing semantic redundancy. Accordingly, the coefficients balance the different numerical scales and practical contributions of these criteria.

Relation to Submodular Optimization:
The construction of ordered subsets through marginal gains is inspired by submodular optimization. Nevertheless, Eq.
8
is a general model-dependent objective rather than a submodular function. Redundant sub-regions can exhibit overlapping utility and diminishing marginal effects, but complementary evidence can produce positive synergy—informally, a “
1+1>2
” response—and distractors can produce negative marginal gains. Consequently, neither global diminishing returns nor monotonicity is assumed. The bidirectional greedy procedure below is therefore used as a practical ranking strategy, and its effectiveness is evaluated empirically rather than justified by a submodular approximation guarantee. The quality of the sub-region division still determines the available search space and influences attribution performance.

IV-C

Bidirectional Greedy Search Algorithm

Given
V=\{\mathbf{I}^{M}_{1},\mathbf{I}^{M}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{I}^{M}_{N}\}
, we construct an ordered sequence by repeatedly evaluating Eq.
8
and selecting the candidate with the largest marginal objective value for the current prefix. This greedy construction is motivated by marginal-gain-based subset selection
[
66
,
77
]
, but it is applied here to the general objective in Eq.
8
. A forward ranking must traverse all remaining sub-regions in
V\setminus S
at every iteration, which leads to a high inference cost for large black-box models. Randomly sampling candidates may miss important evidence and is therefore undesirable when the goal is to rank all sub-regions.

We propose a bidirectional greedy search algorithm that simultaneously identifies important and non-critical sub-regions, as illustrated in Algorithm
1
. The forward sequence
S_{\mathrm{forward}}
incrementally collects candidates with high marginal objective values, whereas the reverse sequence
S_{\mathrm{reverse}}
accumulates candidates with low values. At each forward iteration, the algorithm evaluates all sub-regions in
V\setminus(\operatorname{set}(S_{\mathrm{forward}})\cup\operatorname{set}(S_{\mathrm{reverse}}))
and selects the largest-gain candidate. The same evaluations also produce a pool
S_{p}
containing at most
n_{p}
low-value candidates. The reverse step selects from this pool according to

\min_{\alpha\in S_{p}}{\mathcal{F}\left(\{\alpha\}\cup\operatorname{set}(S_{\mathrm{reverse}})\right)}.

(9)

This reverse selection is a ranking heuristic: low objective values indicate sub-regions that are non-critical or potentially counterproductive in the current context. The candidate-pool size
n_{p}
controls the empirical trade-off between ranking faithfulness and inference cost. Algorithm
1
retains the
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
asymptotic complexity of standard greedy ranking. It reuses the forward-sweep scores to construct a low-value candidate pool and restricts each reverse search to at most
n_{p}
candidates, rather than performing a second exhaustive sweep over all remaining sub-regions. For the default
N=49
and
n_{p}=8
, the pseudocode performs 805 candidate-objective evaluations, compared with 1,225 for a complete standard forward ranking. Under the evaluated multimodal objective, the corresponding query upper bounds are 1,660 and 2,500, respectively. Thus, the method improves practical inference efficiency without changing the asymptotic complexity order. We report measured wall-clock times in Section
VI
.

IV-D

Sub-region Importance Assessment

Although the sub-regions are ranked by importance, the ranking does not consider the degree of difference between them. The importance differences between adjacent sub-regions are unlikely to be uniform. To address this, we propose a simple first-order assignment method based on marginal contribution scores. Equations
3
and
4
are closely related to the model’s decision-making process, allowing us to quantify the marginal effects by analyzing their variations to capture the differences between
sub-regions
. The attribution score
a_{i}
for each
sub-region

s_{i}
in
S
is given by:

a_{i}=\begin{cases}b_{\text{base}}&\text{if }i=1,\\
a_{i-1}-\left|\left(s_{\mathrm{cons.}}(S_{[i]})+s_{\mathrm{colla.}}(S_{[i]})\right)\right.&\\
\quad\left.-\left(s_{\mathrm{cons.}}(S_{[i-1]})+s_{\mathrm{colla.}}(S_{[i-1]})\right)\right|&\text{if }i>1,\end{cases}

(10)

where
b_{\text{base}}
denotes a baseline value, and
S_{[i]}
represents the set containing the top
i

sub-regions
in the set
S
. When a new
sub-region
is added, if the marginal effect does not increase significantly, it suggests that the importance of this
sub-region
is comparable to that of the previous one. If the marginal effect is negative—indicating that the remaining
sub-regions
have a counterproductive effect on the model’s decision—the negative impact of the
sub-region
can be evaluated based on the absolute value of the marginal effect. By assessing the importance of all
sub-regions
in this manner, we can better visualize which regions are most important and distinguish the differences in importance across sub-regions, enhancing human understandability.

V
Method Analysis

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of our approach, identify the scenarios in which it is most applicable, and compare it against other baseline attribution algorithms.

Context-dependent Response Analysis:
Let
G(S)=F(\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M})/\|F(\sum_{\mathbf{I}^{M}\in S}\mathbf{I}^{M})\|\circ\boldsymbol{f}_{s}
denote the target-semantic response of a subset, and let
H(S)=s_{\mathrm{cons.}}(S)+s_{\mathrm{colla.}}(S)
. For the ordered prefix
S_{[i]}
and the newly added sub-region
\alpha_{i}=S_{[i]}\setminus S_{[i-1]}
, define insertion- and removal-side finite differences by
d_{i}^{\mathrm{ins}}=G(S_{[i]})-G(S_{[i-1]})
and
d_{i}^{\mathrm{rem}}=G(V\setminus S_{[i-1]})-G(V\setminus S_{[i]})
. From the consistency and collaboration definitions, their combined change is exactly

H(S_{[i]})-H(S_{[i-1]})=d_{i}^{\mathrm{ins}}+d_{i}^{\mathrm{rem}}.

(11)

Thus, the importance assessment records the finite change in model response when a sub-region is evaluated under both the currently revealed and complementary contexts. Unlike an isolated-region score, this quantity is conditional on previously selected sub-regions. It can reflect synergy along the discovered ordering when a candidate becomes more influential after complementary evidence enters the prefix; redundant or distracting sub-regions may instead produce small or negative changes. This context dependence neither constitutes a unique decomposition of all higher-order interactions nor implies global submodularity.

Comparison with Existing Attribution Mechanisms:
Existing attribution methods estimate region importance through gradients, propagation, Shapley-value approximations, or sampled perturbations
[
13
,
48
,
28
]
. Averaging over sampled contexts can obscure strongly context-dependent effects when complementary regions are rarely observed together or different contexts yield opposing responses. LiMA instead evaluates each candidate conditionally on an evolving subset and its complementary context. This design does not guarantee recovery of every interaction and remains dependent on the discovered search path, but it provides a direct mechanism for retaining context-dependent changes during ranking. We evaluate its practical benefit empirically in Section
VI
.

Empirical Response-transition Analysis:
We use abrupt changes along the insertion trajectory as an empirical indicator of context-dependent model behavior. This statistic is not a formal test of synergy, because a large transition may also be caused by a strong individual effect. We conduct two analyses. First, we attribute 1,000 correctly predicted samples using three models and count insertion steps whose absolute response change exceeds 0.5. Fig.
3
A shows more such transitions for the evaluated models with larger-scale pre-training or greater model capacity. Second, using ImageBind, we compare 1,000 correctly predicted and 1,000 misclassified samples and count insertion steps with a response decrease greater than 0.5. Fig.
3
B shows that these negative transitions occur more frequently among the evaluated misclassified samples. These observations motivate, but do not prove, the following empirical hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1
.

Context-dependent subset evaluation may provide larger empirical benefits in settings that exhibit more frequent large response transitions, including the evaluated larger-scale models and misclassified samples.

The comparative attribution experiments in the following section test the practical consequence of this hypothesis; they are not used to infer a universal interaction structure or set-function property.

VI-B

Evaluation Metrics

Since attribution methods aim to identify which inputs influenced a model’s decision, the resulting explanations should be evaluated not by humans but by objective faithfulness metrics. We employ four fidelity metrics to evaluate our attribution methods.

The first is the
Deletion AUC score

[
20
]
, which quantifies the reduction in the model’s output when important regions are replaced with a baseline value. A sharp decline in performance indicates that the explanation method effectively identifies the key variables influencing the decision. Let
\mathbf{x}_{[x_{T}=x_{0}]}
denote the input where the
T
most important variables, according to the attribution map, are set to the baseline value
x_{0}=0
. Given a set
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{0},T_{1},\cdots,T_{n}\}
, where
T_{0}=0
and
T_{n}
is the input size of
\mathbf{x}
, this set represents the selected numbers of the most important regions. Then, the Deletion AUC score is given by:

\text{Del.}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{[\mathbf{x}_{T_{i}}=x_{0}]})+f(\mathbf{x}_{[\mathbf{x}_{T_{i-1}}=x_{0}]})\right)\cdot\left(T_{i}-T_{i-1}\right)}{2T_{n}},

(12)

the lower this metric, the better the attribution performance.

The second metric is the
Insertion AUC score

[
20
]
, which quantifies the increase in the model’s output as important regions are progressively revealed. This metric is defined as follows:

\text{Ins.}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{[\mathbf{x}_{\bar{T}_{i}}=x_{0}]})+f(\mathbf{x}_{[\mathbf{x}_{\bar{T}_{i-1}}=x_{0}]})\right)\cdot\left(T_{i}-T_{i-1}\right)}{2T_{n}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{x}_{[x_{\bar{T}}=x_{0}]}
denotes the input where elements not belonging to the set
T
are set to the baseline value
x_{0}=0
. The higher this metric, the better the attribution performance.

In this section, we conduct failure attribution to analyze misclassified images and assess whether the attribution algorithm can accurately identify the causes of the model’s prediction errors. Specifically, our goal is to perform a counterfactual analysis by masking the local regions responsible for the incorrect prediction, such that the model’s output can be corrected from the original wrong class to the ground-truth class.

VI-E
1
Ablation of the Bidirectional Greedy Search Algorithm

Section
IV-C
discusses the computational complexity and candidate-reuse mechanism of the proposed bidirectional greedy search without asserting a submodular approximation bound. Table
XIII
empirically evaluates the resulting quality–efficiency trade-off. Although naive forward greedy ranking achieves the strongest faithfulness scores, it requires substantially more execution time. Bidirectional search reduces the measured attribution time by reusing forward evaluations to construct a restricted reverse candidate pool. The pool size
n_{p}
controls this trade-off: larger values increase search time but generally improve attribution quality. For the default
N=49
and
n_{p}=8
, the pseudocode performs 805 candidate-objective evaluations, compared with 1,225 for a complete naive forward ranking. These algorithmic evaluation counts are distinguished from external API queries, whose number can depend on the model interface and caching strategy. In our ImageBind–ImageNet experiment on an NVIDIA 4090 GPU,
n_{p}=8
provides a practical balance and reduces the measured attribution time from 32 seconds to 20 seconds, or 62.5% of the naive greedy runtime.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce
LiMA
, a novel and efficient black-box attribution method based on ordered interpretable subset selection. Inspired by the marginal-gain-based search principle of submodular optimization, LiMA uses a composite objective to accommodate both redundant and complementary evidence among input sub-regions, without assuming that the objective is globally submodular or monotone. The proposed bidirectional greedy search reduces the inference cost of ranking important and non-critical sub-regions. We validate the effectiveness of
LiMA
across multiple datasets and multimodal models, achieving state-of-the-art faithfulness. The results show that the method provides faithful explanations for correctly predicted samples and useful counterfactual evidence for analyzing incorrect predictions.

Appendix A
Coefficient Sensitivity Analysis

We evaluate the empirical sensitivity of
LiMA
to the four coefficients in its composite objective by varying one coefficient at a time around the default setting
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})=(20,5,0.05,0.01)
. The study uses a randomly sampled subset of 100 inputs and covers ImageNet and VGG-Sound, image and audio inputs, and CLIP, ImageBind, and ViT backbones. Table
reports the resulting Insertion and Deletion scores.

The results reveal an empirical trade-off rather than a single setting that is optimal for every metric. Increasing
\lambda_{1}
generally favors Insertion, while smaller values can improve Deletion. Removing
\lambda_{2}
substantially worsens both metrics across the evaluated settings, whereas larger
\lambda_{2}
generally favors Deletion with smaller changes in Insertion. The
examined
variations of
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{4}
produce comparatively smaller changes. We therefore use the default coefficients as one fixed operating point that balances the evaluated Insertion–Deletion trade-off, rather than as a universally optimal configuration.

The zero-weight rows are component-removal ablations and are not treated as alternative tuning points. Across all examined nonzero, one-at-a-time coefficient perturbations, the largest relative decrease in Insertion among the four evaluated settings is approximately
3.0\%
. Deletion varies more noticeably, reflecting its trade-off with Insertion, and several perturbations improve rather than degrade it. These results show that reasonable coefficient variation does not cause a collapse in faithfulness, while completely removing either principal criterion can substantially degrade performance.

Appendix B
Deployment Scope and Limitations

The absolute-cost results in the main manuscript position
LiMA
as a high-faithfulness, moderate-query attribution method rather than the universally cheapest method. With
N=49
, standard greedy ranking requires up to 2,500 queries for the multimodal objective, whereas the corresponding
LiMA
upper bound is 1,660 queries, a reduction of 33.6%. This operating point retains the strong faithfulness reported in the main experiments while using substantially fewer queries than full standard-greedy ranking. Nevertheless, fixed-budget perturbation baselines such as RISE and HSIC-Attribution can use fewer queries, and gradient methods can be faster when white-box backward access is available.

LiMA
is therefore most appropriate when attribution faithfulness is prioritized over minimum latency, for example in offline model auditing, batch analysis, research evaluation, or local/open-weight model inspection. Its cost can be less suitable for latency-critical applications, high-price proprietary APIs, or clinical deployment with strict query or response-time constraints. Batching can reduce local wall-clock latency but does not necessarily reduce the number of model evaluations billed by an external provider.

Appendix F
Failure-Case Analysis of SAM-Based Sub-Regions

We analyze the dependence of
LiMA
on the sub-region generator by holding the attribution objective and search procedure fixed and changing only the input partition between SLICO and SAM. Fig.
18
presents two complementary groups of diagnostic examples. For each method, the dashed vertical line marks the searched prefix shown beside its insertion curve, and the percentage below the searched-region image reports the revealed image area.

Fig.
18
A contains cases in which SLICO yields a more compact and effective prefix. For the ruddy turnstone, Australian Kelpie, and marmot examples, SLICO reveals 26.4%, 25.9%, and 12.4% of the image, whereas SAM reveals 83.6%, 52.4%, and 63.4%, respectively. The mean revealed areas are therefore 21.6% for SLICO and 66.5% for SAM. In these examples, SAM produces an oversized region that includes the target together with surrounding content or combines several portions of the same semantic object. Semantic coherence does not guarantee
decision homogeneity
: pixels within one semantic region may have different, or even opposing, effects on the model response. Because each generated region is an atomic candidate in the search, useful and detrimental evidence inside one large mask cannot be ranked independently. This explains both the delayed response and the non-monotonic portions of some SAM insertion curves. The overlap-resolution procedure in Algorithm
2
makes candidates disjoint, but it cannot guarantee that the resulting masks are sufficiently small or decision homogeneous.

Fig.
18
B contains the complementary cases in which SAM provides a better partition. For the toaster, green mamba, and mobile phone examples, the SAM prefixes reveal 5.5%, 29.2%, and 19.7%, whereas the SLICO prefixes reveal 96.0%, 55.9%, and 32.1%. The corresponding means are 18.1% and 61.3%. Here the SAM masks more precisely follow semantic boundaries or isolate discriminative object parts. SLICO instead fragments the relevant semantic evidence across multiple low-level cells, so a larger collection of candidates must be accumulated before the model response rises. SAM therefore permits a much earlier response when its masks are both fine-grained and aligned with the model’s discriminative evidence.

These examples show that neither SAM nor SLICO uniformly dominates. The decisive property is whether the generated candidates are sufficiently fine and
decision aligned
for the evaluated model, rather than semantic plausibility, boundary quality, or region size alone. Practical safeguards include removing near-duplicate masks, imposing a maximum mask-area threshold, subdividing oversized SAM masks with SLICO, or constructing a hybrid candidate pool and selecting its configuration on held-out data. The six examples are intended to diagnose the two mechanisms, not to estimate their prevalence over the full dataset.
