Examining the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths of students at the faculty of health sciences with structural equation modeling
Authors: Sıdıka Özlem Cengizhan, Semiha Aydın Özkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to examine the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths among health sciences students using structural equation modeling.MethodsDesigned as cross-sectional research, data were collected from students in the midwifery and nursing departments of a university in Southeastern Anatolia. The Personal Information Form, Sexual Health Literacy Scale (SHLS), and Sexual Myths Scale (SMS) were used. Ethical approval was obtained (Decision number: 29/1 1/2023–477). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM) with SPSS 25 and JAMOVI 2.3.28.ResultsResults showed that 58.6% of participants were aged 18–21, 79.4% were female, 38.7% had a family member to talk to about sexuality, and 46.8% used the internet as a source of sexual information. The mean SHLS score was 52.07 ± 8.11, while the mean SMS score was 68.04 ± 15.59. A negative, weak, and statistically significant correlation was found between SHLS and SMS (r = −0.155; p = 0.001). The SEM analysis indicated that sexual myths were significantly associated with sexual health literacy, accounting for approximately 2.4% of the variance in SHLS scores within the tested model.ConclusionsThe mean SHLS score was slightly above the midpoint of the possible scale range, suggesting a moderate level of sexual health literacy among the participants. According to the structural equation model, sexual health literacy (dependent variable) and sexual myths (independent variable) explained a small proportion of the variance. Higher sexual health literacy scores were associated with lower levels of belief in sexual myths.Keywords: Sexual health literacy, Sexual myths, Sexuality, Health sciences students, Structural equation modeling

Background

The importance of university youth in a society, reflecting the most dynamic and trendy elements of the socio-cultural structure of that society, cannot be underestimated. University youth are bright generations who will take on decision-making and managerial roles with knowledge for the country’s future [1]. Universities are also institutions where young people from many ethnic and cultural backgrounds come together [2]. The process that begins with the discovery and curiosity of sexuality in adolescence turns into a process in which increasing sexual interest and experiences are experienced, and sexual development and changes occur during the university years [3, 4]

Sexual health, one of the various dimensions of health, is also an indicator of community well-being. For positive sexual health, sexual health literacy must be at a certain level. The concept of sexual health literacy is defined as the knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, motivations and skills that individuals have in accessing, understanding, evaluating and applying information about sexual health, thus making it possible to create and evaluate decisions about sexual health [5, 6]. Sexual health literacy is more broadly related to various competencies in the field of sexual health, such as sexual development, sexual education, information about birth control methods, unwanted pregnancies, safe sex, and managing partner relationships [7]. Sexual health literacy plays a key role in preventing health-hazardous behaviors among young people and promoting healthy development. It enables young people to seek, interact with and use up-to-date health information or make informed health decisions and improve problems if any [8]. Studies show that university students’ sexual health literacy and knowledge about sexuality are inadequate or incorrect and that they are at risk for STDs and unwanted pregnancies due to unsafe sexual behaviors [9–11]. Increasing sexual health literacy is also of great importance in reducing these risky situations [5].

The youth period is a long period in which the individual tries to find his/her place in society and matures physically, psychologically, and socially. Social change processes also affect and change individuals’ perspectives on sexuality in general [12]. University students with different family structures, personal differences, historical origins, and traditions each have different beliefs or false beliefs about sexuality [2, 13]. All of these are considered sexual myths. Sexual myths are exaggerated and false beliefs about sexuality that are spread by word of mouth without any scientific basis on sexuality issues and can often be shaped by the imagination of society [14]. The fact that sexuality is considered shameful, sinful, forbidden, and secret in society and is not talked about causes the formation of informal information about sexuality and the spread of myths. It is stated in the literature that sexual myths can cause sexual dysfunction in individuals or family problems in couples [2]. Previous studies conducted among university students, including those in health sciences faculties, have reported that sexual myths are highly prevalent among students [13, 14]. The prevalence of sexual myths in studies conducted with health sciences faculties where midwifery and nursing students receive education makes it important to investigate sexual health literacy in student groups who are expected to provide education and consultancy services to society on sexual and reproductive health in their future professional lives [13–15]. In the literature, no study has been found examining the relationship between the sexual health literacy of students in health sciences faculties and sexual myths using structural equation modeling. In this context, the research aims to examine the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths of students at the faculty of health sciences using structural equation modeling.

Research design

The research was designed as a cross-sectional study to examine the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths of students at the faculty of health sciences using the structural equation modeling.

Population and sample of the research

The population of the study consists of students studying in the Department of Midwifery and Nursing at Adıyaman University, Faculty of Health Sciences. Structural equation modeling generally requires a minimum sample size of approximately 200 participants or at least 10 participants per observed variable to obtain stable parameter estimates [16, 17]. No sampling method was used; all students who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate were included in the study. 600 midwifery and nursing students were actively receiving education at ADYU Faculty of Health Sciences. The aim was to reach all students. 500 students who volunteered for the study were reached. 19 of them were not included in the study due to missing data in the survey form. As a result, 80.2% of the population was reached with 481 students, and their data were analyzed. The sample size of 481 participants in the present study was therefore considered adequate for SEM analysis.

Data collection tools

Data collection tools are the Personal Information Form, Sexual Myths Scale, and Sexual Health Literacy Scale.

Personal information form

The Personal Information Form consisted of 13 items developed by the researchers based on the literature [3, 13, 18] to collect information about the sociodemographic characteristics and sexuality-related experiences of the participants. In addition to demographic variables, the form included items related to communication about sexuality, attitudes toward sexuality, and sources of sexual information.

Sexual health literacy scale (SHLS)

The Sexual Health Literacy Scale (SHLS), developed by Üstgörül (2022), consists of 17 items rated on a five-point Likert scale and includes two subscales: sexual knowledge and sexual attitude. The sexual knowledge subscale consists of 12 items, with scores ranging from 12 to 60, and higher scores indicate higher levels of sexual health literacy. The sexual attitude subscale includes five items and assesses attitudes toward sexual health information. These items are reverse-coded; therefore, higher raw scores indicate more negative attitudes toward sexual health information. During the calculation of the total SHLS score, reverse scoring was applied to the relevant items so that higher total scores consistently represent higher levels of sexual health literacy.Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was stated as 0.875 [19]. In this study, Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient was determined as 0.731.

Sexual Myths Scale (SMS)

The scale, developed by Gölbaşı et al. in 2016, consists of a 5-point Likert type and 28 questions. It has subscales as Sexual orientation (Items 1–5), Gender (Items 6–11), Age and sexuality (Items 12–15), Sexual behavior (Items 16–18), Masturbation (Items 19–20), Sexual violence (Items 21–24), Sexual intercourse (Items 25–26), and Sexual satisfaction (Items 27–28). The total score of the scale is obtained by adding the scores given to each item. The total score varies between 28 and 140. In addition, the scores of the items belonging to the subscales can be obtained by adding them. There is no cut-off point for the scale, and a high score indicates that the sexual myths are high. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported as 0.91 [20]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was determined as 0.877.

Data collection

Data were collected from students studying at Adıyaman University, Faculty of Health Sciences via face-to-face surveys at a time convenient for the researcher and students (outside of class hours). Each survey lasted approximately 5–10 minutes. Data were collected between January 1, 2024 and April 1, 2024.

Data analysis

The data set obtained as a result of the application of the questionnaire forms was first subjected to reliability analysis and exploratory factor analysis using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) 25 software. It was determined that the data showed a normal distribution in normality analyses. Number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation analyses were used in descriptive statistics. Linear regression analysis was used to determine the association of the dependent variable on the independent variable. In addition, it was determined that Cronbach’s alpha values were within the desired ranges for the SHLS and SMS scales. Finally, a Structural Equation Model (SEM) named “Explanation Model of Sexual Health Literacy and Sexual Myths of Students” was established using the JAMOVI 2.3.28 solid package program to reveal the structural relationships between variables, and the goodness of fit and test values of the model were interpreted. In the proposed structural equation model, sexual myths were specified as the independent variable and sexual health literacy as the dependent variable to examine the structural association between these constructs. However, considering the cross-sectional design of the study, the model was used to test the hypothesized relationship and model fit rather than to infer causal association.The statistical significance level was taken as 0.05.

Discussion

This study was conducted to examine the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths of students at the Faculty of Health Sciences using structural equation modeling. University students studying in health sciences constitute an important population because they are future health professionals expected to provide counseling and education on sexual and reproductive health. Therefore, examining sexual health literacy and sexual myths in this specific group is essential for understanding their preparedness to deliver accurate sexual health information to the community. The originality of the study is that there is no study in the literature that addresses the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths.

Sexual health literacy is a set of knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, incentives, and personal abilities to access, perceive, evaluate, and use sexual health information in a person’s daily life [21]. Recent international studies have also emphasized the importance of sexual health literacy among young adults and university students. For example, research conducted in different countries has shown that higher levels of sexual health literacy are associated with improved sexual health knowledge, safer sexual behaviors, and better reproductive health outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening sexual health education programs for young populations, particularly among university students who are in a critical developmental period for acquiring accurate sexual health information [6, 8, 22–24]. In this study, the mean score of the SHLS’s subscale of sexual knowledge was 35.23 ± 7.58, the mean score of the SHLS’s subscale of sexual attitude was 16.83 ± 3.86, and the mean score of the SHLS was 52.07 ± 8.11. There are studies in the literature examining health literacy levels in different sample groups [25–27]. However, most of these studies were conducted with different populations such as married women, adolescents, or general university samples, which may limit direct comparability with the present study that specifically focused on health sciences students. Students in midwifery and nursing programs receive education related to reproductive and sexual health during their training, which may contribute to relatively higher levels of sexual health literacy compared with other student populations. Our research findings were discussed concerning these studies. In a study conducted in Iran with a sexual health literacy scale in which 200 married women could receive a maximum of 100 points, it was reported that the sexual health literacy mean score was 75.64 ± 12.81 and 82.5% was at the desired level [25]. In a study examining general health literacy and female reproductive knowledge, it was stated that 95% of the participants had high general health literacy [26]. In a study conducted on high school students, it was reported that more than half of the participants had low sexual health and reproductive health literacy mean scores [24]. In a study examining female sexual function and health literacy, it was reported that general health literacy was insufficient and borderline [27]. In line with these studies, the fact that our sample group was university students and students receiving education in the field of health may explain the high level of sexual health literacy. Due to the education systems of universities, educational environment, amount of information, and different education methods, it can be said that individuals with a university education may be more capable of searching for content, accessing and understanding information. In addition, all individuals with university education experience student life independent of their families, and this is thought to have an association on the sexual health literacy level of the participants.

Sexual myths are false or misleading beliefs about sexuality that have no scientific basis [14]. In this study, the SMS mean score was found to be 68.04 ± 15.59. Similar studies conducted among midwifery and nursing students reported comparable levels of sexual myths, suggesting that misconceptions about sexuality may persist even among students receiving health-related education. However, methodological differences between studies, including differences in measurement tools, cultural contexts, and educational backgrounds of participants, should be considered when interpreting these comparisons. In a study conducted with midwifery and nursing students, the SMS mean score was reported as 76.43 ± 17.09 [28]. It has been reported that sexual myths decreased significantly before and after the sexual health information course in their study with nursing students to whom they gave a sexual health course [29]. Similarly, another study reported that the rate of approval of sexual myths decreased significantly with the education given. [30]. However, both studies could not state that sexual myths completely changed. For this reason, it is thought that sexual education should start in the family before university education and continue throughout life. However, traditional values that may restrict access to information about sexual attitudes and behaviors may be the major barriers to discussions about sexual issues among families and youth in traditional societies.

In the SEM established in this study, by examining the extent to which the dependent variable, sexual health literacy levels, were explained by the independent variable, sexual myths, it was determined that the scores obtained from the sexual myths scale explained 2.4% of the total variance of the mean score obtained from the Sexual Health Literacy Scale. Although sexual myths were modeled as the independent variable and sexual health literacy as the dependent variable in the SEM analysis, this specification was used to examine the structural relationship between the variables. In addition, in the established model, it was seen that a 1-unit increase in the mean score of the sexual myths scale corresponded to a 0.080-unit (β1) decrease in the sexual health literacy mean score. In addition, it was seen that a 1-standard deviation increase in the mean score of the sexual myths scale corresponded to a −0.155 standard deviation (β2) decrease in the sexual health literacy scale mean score. This finding suggests that higher sexual myth scores are associated with lower sexual health literacy levels. In the literature, no study has been found that examines the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths of students at the faculty of health sciences. However, in a study conducted with university students including all faculties, it was stated that health literacy, sexual health knowledge, and sexual myths are closely related to each other [31]. In another study conducted with Turkish university students, it was reported that there was a negative and statistically significant correlation between sexual myths and sexual health knowledge levels [32] and that sexual health knowledge levels increased and sexual myths decreased in university students who were given sexual health education [33]. Both the findings of this study and the findings of the studies conducted in the literature presented that there is a negative correlation between sexual knowledge levels and sexual myths. It is thought that providing sexual health information to university students may play an effective role in reducing sexual myths. From a theoretical perspective, these findings support the idea that improving sexual health literacy may contribute to correcting misinformation and reducing misconceptions about sexuality. Practically, integrating comprehensive sexual health education into university curricula, particularly in health sciences programs, may help future healthcare professionals provide evidence-based counseling and education to individuals and communities. Moreover, we believe that increasing the sexual health literacy of university students in the Faculty of Health Sciences will strengthen the service of these students, who are expected to provide education and consultancy to society on sexual and reproductive health in their future professional lives, with accurate sexual health information that is free from myths. If we consider the issue from this perspective, it is thought that the findings obtained with SEM will make a significant contribution to the relevant literature.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences between sexual health literacy and sexual myths. Second, the data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which may be affected by response bias or social desirability. Third, the study was conducted in a single university among health sciences students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other student populations or cultural contexts. Despite these limitations, the relatively large sample size and the use of structural equation modeling strengthen the contribution of the study to the literature.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study examined the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths among students in a faculty of health sciences using structural equation modeling. The findings indicate that sexual health literacy and beliefs in sexual myths are significantly associated among health sciences students. Students with higher levels of sexual health literacy tend to report lower levels of belief in sexual myths.

These findings highlight the importance of strengthening sexual health literacy among university students, particularly those studying in health-related disciplines who are expected to provide education and counseling on sexual and reproductive health in their future professional roles. Improving sexual health literacy may contribute to reducing misconceptions and promoting evidence-based sexual health knowledge.

In this context, integrating comprehensive sexual health education programs into university curricula may help reduce sexual myths and support the development of informed future health professionals. Future studies conducted with larger and more diverse populations are recommended to further explore the relationship between sexual health literacy and sexual myths.
