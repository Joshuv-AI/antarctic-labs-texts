Can Large Models Fool the Eye? A New Turing Test for Biological Animation
Zijian Chen, Lirong Deng, Zhengyu Chen, Kaiwei Zhang, Qi Jia, Yuan Tian, Yucheng Zhu, Guangtao Zhai

Abstract
Evaluating the abilities of large models and manifesting their gaps are challenging. Current benchmarks adopt either ground-truth-based score-form evaluation on static datasets or indistinct textual chatbot-style human preferences collection, which may not provide users with immediate, intuitive, and perceptible feedback on performance differences. In this paper, we introduce BioMotion Arena, a novel framework for evaluating large language models (LLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) via visual animation. Our methodology draws inspiration from the inherent visual perception of motion patterns characteristic of living organisms that utilizes point-light source imaging to amplify the performance discrepancies between models. Specifically, we employ a pairwise comparison evaluation and collect more than 45k votes for 53 mainstream LLMs and MLLMs on 90 biological motion variants. Data analyses show that the crowd-sourced human votes are in good agreement with those of expert raters, demonstrating the superiority of our BioMotion Arena in offering discriminative feedback. We also find that over 90% of evaluated models, including the cutting-edge open-source InternVL3 and proprietary Claude-4 series, fail to produce fundamental humanoid point-light groups, much less smooth and biologically plausible motions. This enables BioMotion Arena to serve as a challenging benchmark for performance visualization and a flexible evaluation framework without restrictions on ground-truth.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown groundbreaking performance across various applications, including routine visual question answering
(
Bai et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
, native audio outputs
(
Mallick and Kilpatrick 2025
)
, code
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
, mathematics
(
Gulati et al. 2024
)
, and multidisciplinary tasks
(
Yue et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025a
)
, necessitating enhanced evaluation approaches to keep up with their rapid advancements.
Currently, most benchmarks often fail to convey subtle performance differences and align with user preferences in real-world scenarios.

The mainstream benchmarks can be classified into two categories: the ground-truth-based closed-ended benchmark and the human preference-oriented open-ended benchmark, as shown in Fig.
1
. The former relies on meticulous dataset construction and task design but is susceptible to data contamination and incurs high maintenance costs.
The latter, especially those text response-based human preference evaluations, such as Chatbot Arena
(
Chiang et al. 2024
)
, Arena-Hard
(
Li et al. 2024c
)
, and GameArena
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
, may be affected by subjective bias (e.g., age, gender, and educational background) given the noisy nature of human preferences
(
Lehmann and Seufert 2020
)
.
Moreover, the closeness of scores and output formats can hardly convey performance disparities across models to users intuitively. Consequently, it is imperative to build an open, intuitive, unbiased evaluation framework that can well present the performance.

To this end, we propose BioMotion Arena, a visual benchmarking approach for large models that features easily perceptible and ground-truth-free comparisons in a lightweight setting.
BioMotion Arena draws inspiration from a stunning example of the resourcefulness of human vision, namely the remarkable capacity to perceive ‘biological motion’, which has a far higher degree of complexity than the patterns of simple mechanical motions and was first shown in earlier cinematic works
(
Neri, Morrone, and Burr 1998
)
.
A point-light display, illuminating only the major joints of a walking person, is sufficient to generate a vivid and coherent percept of human animation. This perception, however, disintegrates into a jumble of meaningless dots when the walker remains stationary. It is worth noting that such a phenomenon happens in young infants
(
Fox and McDaniel 1982
)
and can be interpreted easily without any knowledge foundation.

Armed with this, we first focus on ten typical human motions and introduce fine-grained control over gender, weight, mood, and direction. Concretely, in BioMotion Arena, a user can prompt to generate a human animation that satisfies the above combination of action requirements and get visual answers from two anonymous large models.
Afterwards, the user is instructed to vote for the model that delivers the preferred animation.
Such a lightweight setup enables efficient collection of diverse prompts on varying motions and simplifies the process of comparing different models using visual preference data, providing an intuitive presentation to evaluate a large model’s grasp of the underlying mechanisms of biological motion.

We have conducted extensive experiments with 53 LLMs and MLLMs, where over 50 human annotators participated, with more than 45k votes collected. Based on this, we construct a leaderboard by translating pairwise comparisons into ranking through Elo score
(
Elo 1967
)
, identifying the state-of-the-art models, such as DeepSeek-R1-20250528, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and OpenAI’s o3, in animating biological motions.
The winning fraction heatmaps reveal that the reasoning or thinking mode in some newly released models can improve the realism of generated motions.
We also performed several case studies for qualitative analysis, demonstrating that existing models are still far from generating vivid, smooth, and realistic biological motions.
We are committed to making BioMotion Arena an open-access online evaluation platform and releasing the preference data periodically.

The BioMotion Arena

Overview

BioMotion Arena aims to evaluate the understanding of biological motion patterns of large models and is constructed in response to two problems. First, the current benchmarks predominantly present model performance using single numerical scores, failing to effectively convey the nuanced ability differences between models to users and developers. Second, the existing subjective preference datasets, particularly those collected from chatbot-style systems, primarily rely on votes on textual content.
Such a scheme is highly susceptible to biases stemming from annotators’ diverse backgrounds (e.g., age, education, and cognitive level), thereby increasing inter-group variance and introducing noise into the preference data.

Specifically, we gain inspiration from the fundamental biological perception mechanism that human vision has the ability to see ‘biological motion’. For example, illumination of only the joints of a walking person is enough to convey a vivid, compelling impression of human animation, which is sufficient to discriminate the sex and other details of the walker, and can be easily interpreted by young infants
(
Neri, Morrone, and Burr 1998
)
.
Previous studies
(
Johansson 1973
;
Thornton et al. 2006
)
have demonstrated that the human visual apparatus is very sensitive to small deviations from such standard patterns, as we can immediately see whether a person is walking, running, dancing, or moving forward.

In this paper, we simulate different biological motions by generating point-light stimuli with code prompts, in which each dot represents specific joints of a human performing an action. Grounded in fundamental biological perception, the subjective feedback of the resulting human gait animations represents the performance of the model evaluated, offering a more intuitive mode of judgment than the textual format.

2
provides an overview of BioMotion Arena.

Biological Motion Generation

Motion and Posture Space.

Following the definition of point-light stimuli that the body posture is encoded in the location of the point-lights
(
Theusner, de Lussanet, and Lappe 2014
)
, we mainly focus on ten typical human actions with pronounced limb movements, including walking, running, waving a hand, jumping up, jumping forward, bowing, lying down, sitting down, turning around, and forward rolling.
Moreover, since biological motion contains information about several different emotions, intentions, personality traits, and biological attributes of the agent
(
Troje 2002
;
Troje 2008
)
, which can be retrieved by a person at a glance, we further incorporate finer-grained dimensions, including gender, weight, happiness, and direction. This poses stricter scenarios for large models to understand and generate.

To obtain the
golden
reference motion animation, we utilize the joint kinematics function formulated in
(
Troje et al. 2002
)
, which is fitted based on the real walking data from 50 male and 50 female walkers.
Their posture visualization as a point-light walker is shown in Fig.
2
(lower-left part). We can observe that the sexual dimorphism in human body proportions is evident, with males demonstrating significantly greater biacromial breadth and a wider gait stance compared to females. In terms of body weight, people with a higher weight tend to exhibit greater distances between corresponding points on the upper and lower limbs, which is particularly pronounced in the waist region.
Besides, people with a happier mood tend to exhibit a greater amplitude in their arm swing during locomotion, whereas individuals experiencing sadness display a pronounced slumping or dropping of the shoulders.
Beyond action categories, we also treat the number of point-lights as a variable, given its critical impact on the perceived coherence of the biological motion
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
. See more visualizations in the Appendix.

Prompt Design.
The BioMotion Arena is fundamentally a code generation scenario that follows the standard setup for generating code from natural language
(
Jain et al. 2025
)
.
Specifically, the template begins with a system prompt of the role assigned to the model, followed by the inclusion of point-by-point detailed requirement texts with interleaved reference images. Then the model is tasked with generating an appropriate solution for visually vivid biological motions.
For LLMs and MLLMs, we adopt the text-only and image-text prompting, respectively. One frame from the golden reference motion of forward walking is used as the input image.
The lower right part of Fig.
2
provides an example scenario for evaluating LLMs.

Human Preference Data Collection

User Interface.

BioMotion Arena aims to collect dynamic visual content-based feedback from users for model evaluation.
Different from the common quality assessment for AI-generated content
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2025b
)
that employs unified grading criteria or continuous scales, we adopt a pairwise comparison mechanism, where users are instructed to compare the output animation and vote for the better one.
A screenshot of the interface is shown in the Appendix.
To avoid subjective bias towards the models themselves, in each round, we only provide the target type of the generated action while concealing the specific model from which it originated.
Two code scripts are randomly sampled and executed sequentially, and then users compare the generated biological motions side-by-side and vote for the preferred one. We also add two neutral buttons, i.e., “it’s a tie” and “both are bad”, for ambiguous pairs and failed scenarios.
In the future, we plan to make this platform online to better collect human votes longitudinally.

Participants.
We recruited 53 participants from universities and society through hybrid online–offline channels to collect sufficient human votes.
All of them have normal or corrected eyesight with no color blindness. Before annotations, we obtained a signed and informed agreement from each person to address the ethical concerns for the usage of subjective preference data.

Translating Pairwise Comparisons into Ranking.

To construct a dynamic, predictive, and sustainably updated leaderboard based on BioMotion Arena, we use the Elo score
(
Elo 1967
)
. Its mathematical foundation rests on two key formulas. First, the expected score (
E_{A}
) of model A against model B is calculated based on their current ratings,
R_{A}
and
R_{B}
, using a logistic function:

E_{A}=\frac{1}{1+10^{\left(R_{B}-R_{A}\right)/400}}.

(1)

The expected score for model B can be calculated by
E_{B}=1-E_{A}
. After a round of comparison, the ratings are updated based on the actual outcome (
S_{A}
) compared to the expected score (
E_{A}
):

R_{A}^{\prime}=R_{A}+K\cdot\left(S_{A}-E_{A}\right),

(2)

where
K
is a constant that determines the magnitude of the score adjustment.
As a result, a win battle over a lower-rated opponent yields fewer points for the higher-rated model than what is lost in a defeat.
In this work, all models are initialized with a rating of
1500
, and
K
is set to
32
.

Data Analysis

Main Results

Pairwise Comparison.

In Fig.
4
, we visualize the heatmap of win fractions of twelve representative LLMs and MLLMs out of the 53 models supported in the BioMotion Arena. For battles between LLMs, deepseek-R1-0528 consistently outperforms the others (except for OpenAI’s o3-mini) by a large margin, winning 62% of its battles against the second-best model, OpenAI’s o1.
Among the other open-source LLMs, DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3-32B relatively lead, though there still remains a significant gap between them and the proprietary models.
For battles between MLLMs, where reference images are provided during motion generation, Gemini 2.5 Pro establishes a commanding lead over the entire field of competitors. Notably, it obtained a perfect win rate in its matchups against both Qwen2.5-VL-72B and Gemma-3-4B.
Claude 4 Opus and o3 follow closely behind Gemini 2.5 Pro, winning 23% and 45% of their respective battles, while other models demonstrate lower winning rates.
We also conducted cross-modality comparisons between LLMs and MLLMs, as shown in the right part of Fig.
4
.
It can be seen that deepseek-R1-0528 exceeds most MLLMs while achieving only a 17% and 44% win rate of its matches against Gemini 2.5 Pro and o3, respectively.
In general, LLMs exhibit slightly inferior generative performance compared to MLLMs, primarily due to their lack of visual grounding. Nonetheless, their counterparts equipped with reasoning capabilities, such as grok-3-reasoning, o3-mini, and o1, continue to demonstrate impressive performance, showing their intrinsic understanding of the biological movement patterns.

Poor Understanding of Biological Motions.

As shown in Fig.
5
, the average occurrence rate of ‘Both-are-bad’ in basic motions and fine-grained variants is up to 79.3% and 94.8% in the code-specific comparisons, respectively, indicating the severe inferiority of the existing code-specific LLMs in understanding the patterns of biological motions.
In Fig.

, we provide the leaderboard of BioMotion Arena for 28 models in terms of the Elo score. It can be observed that the BioMotion Arena effectively reveals a significant performance gap between proprietary and most open models. Besides, models featuring multi-step reasoning capabilities perform better than traditional LLMs, demonstrating the potential of such a strategy in enhancing the understanding ability of models. Similar conclusion can also be drawn from the length of the generated code, as shown in Fig.

.

In addition to comparing the relative performance, we further conduct subjective experiments to investigate the absolute rating distribution. As shown in Fig.

, we elaborate on a five-point Likert rating scale, and 10 subjects were invited to participate. We can see that even the
top

Can BioMotion Arena Distinguish Models?

We also study whether biological motion prompts in the BioMotion Arena can differentiate models. Designing challenging prompts has become increasingly difficult due to the rapid advancements of large models with potential data contamination.
A case in point is that models with large parameters, such as Qwen2.5-72B and Llama3.3-70B-Instruct, are capable of solving most scenarios in daily life as good as GPT-4o, but not in domains such as math or coding.
However, this phenomenon could, in turn, be upended by a later model like Qwen3, highlighting the importance of achieving consistent results in a standardized task.
Here, we sample the battles between Claude 3.7 Sonnet versus o4-mini-20250416 and GPT-4o-20241120 by segmenting them into ten actions and compiling statistics on their respective win rates and collapse rates, as summarized in Tab.
1
.
We notice that Claude 3.7 Sonnet and its opponents exhibit significantly lower collapse rates in actions with low-amplitude movements, such as walking, waving a hand, and lying down, while failing more when encountering a greater range of motions.
Similarly, o4-mini achieves a higher win-rate in actions with slight movements (
\approx
95%), which drops to about 30% in vigorous motions.
In general, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of BioMotion Arena in distinguishing models’ strengths.

Effects of Point-Light Quantity.

In Fig.
8
, we visualize the walking motion of a man generated from the
top-3
models in BioMotion Arena using different numbers of points.
It can be observed that none of them can perfectly render rational biological motion across a varying number of points.
Among them, Gemini 2.5 Pro produces a relatively well-formed humanoid limb across various point configurations (except for the 8-point case, where the head was missing), while exhibiting the highest degree of motion smoothness.
DeepSeek-R1 and o3 both produce varying degrees of joint misalignment and unnatural point clustering, resulting in irrational motion postures. Moreover, the results show that motion generation tasks involving either too many or too few point-lights pose a greater challenge to the models, suggesting the feasibility of such methods for more rigorous model evaluation.

Validating Vote Quality

To evaluate the quality of crowdsourced votes, we randomly sampled 150 battles between OpenAI’s o3 and Qwen3-32B, as well as o3 and Gemini 2.0 Flash, for scrutiny.
We invited two experts with research backgrounds in LLMs and asked them to annotate their preferences in each round of comparison.
We adopted the same rating strategy as in crowdsourcing, where experts were given the target motions and votes blindly.
For reference, we also employed GPT-4o as a third-party judge. The agreement rates between crowd-users, experts, and the GPT-4o judge are shown in Tab.
2
. The corresponding win-rates are presented in Tab.
3
.

To summarize, we observe high agreement rates (84.3% to 88.7%) between BioMotion Arena public participants and experts in both battles. Surprisingly, the agreement rates between two experts are extremely high and close (94.9% and 96.2%).
As for the rest 5% disagreements, we speculate that this was caused by either the generation of exceedingly poor point-light motion by both models or errors in the execution of the generated script.
Moreover, we find that GPT-4o has a uniformly high degree of consistency with crowd-users and experts, indicating the effectiveness of BioMotion Arena in conveying performance differences. The win-rates listed in Tab.
3
further demonstrate the quality of the preference data in BioMotion Arena.

Comparison to Other Benchmarks.

In Fig.

, we compare BioMotion Arena against two widely used LLM benchmarks, ChatBot Arena
(
Chiang et al. 2024
)
and LiveCodeBench-Hard
(

We also compute the Pearson’s correlation coefficient of model scores across the benchmarks, where BioMotion Arena owns a 0.7763 and 0.8204 correlation with ChatBot Arena and LiveCodeBench-Hard, respectively.
Based on the plots and correlations, we notice that although the overall performance trend of the two benchmarks aligns with that of the BioMotion Arena, some models have starkly contrasting performance.
Specifically, claude-opus-4-20250514 performs substantially better on ChatBot Arena and relatively worse on LiveCodeBench-Hard compared to BioMotion Arena.
In contrast, o3 achieves the top-1 performance in LiveCodeBench-Hard while exhibiting a little inferiority in BioMotion Arena.
We hypothesize that this discrepancy can be attributed to their differing evaluation mechanisms, i.e., LiveCodeBench is ground-truth-based, whereas the ChatBot Arena and our BioMotion Arena rely on open-ended subjective human preferences.

Conclusion

In this work, we present BioMotion Arena, a biologically inspired visual preference evaluation framework for large models, which enables intuitive, efficient, and easily perceptible performance comparison.
We conduct comparative insights across over 50 models by collecting over 45k human votes on 90 biological motion variants and utilize the Elo score to transfer the pairwise comparisons to relative scores, allowing for continuous updating of model performance.
Experimental results show that even cutting-edge reasoning LLMs or MLLMs fail to generate smooth and rational biological motions with a poor understanding of their kinetic-geometric patterns.
Additionally, we find a high agreement on preference votes from crowds and experts, demonstrating the superiority of BioMotion Arena in reflecting performance differences.
We plan to make this platform online to better collect human votes longitudinally and release more pairwise preference votes for future research.

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Directions

Although our BioMotion Arena provides a comprehensive evaluation platform for LLMs and MLLMs in visual biological motion generation tasks, there are several potential limitations acknowledged below.
First, since the model base in our BioMotion Arena is extensive, the number of battles for each model pair may incline to those popular one in future online collection, which results from the rapid updates and the potential user base of large models. This inclination may result in a biased distribution of scores. Therefore, optimizing the ranking algorithm is necessary to keep the arena fair.
Second, the current motion space may be limited, where only 10 typical human actions are included. This source might not accurately reflect the performance of large models in real-world environments or specialized domains, potentially leading to a skewed preference distribution.

In the future, we plan to make BioMotion Arena online, where public user can freely upload their specific biological motion generation requests, and develop comprehensive visual content leaderboards for both LLMs and MLLMs in less constrained ways, catering to more complex motions.
