Protocols for minimizing artifacts in estimating dental fluoride uptake from F-varnishes
Authors: Robson de Sousa Ferreira, Jaime Aparecido Cury, Cínthia Pereira Machado Tabchoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionProper removal of fluoride varnish (FV) residue is essential to avoid interference in fluoride uptake measurements in research studies.MethodsThis study evaluated five FV removal protocols applied to inert slabs (n = 8 per group): (I) scalpel blade, (II) acetone-soaked cotton swab, (III) combination of I and II, (IV) II plus 30-s acetone wash, and (V) plastic spatula + II + three 10-s vortexed acetone washes. A water-soluble fluoride gel served as control. Alkali-soluble residual contaminant fluoride (RCF) was quantified.Results%RCF values were: I (3.35 ± 1.27)A, II (0.18 ± 0.20)B, III (0.10 ± 0.13)BC, IV (0.01 ± 0.01)C, V (0.03 ± 0.01)C, and control (0.02 ± 0.02)C.ConclusionThe combination of mechanical and chemical steps particularly with acetone and vortex washes minimize FV residues.

Introduction

Dental caries is a chronic disease caused by acid production from dental biofilm bacteria metabolizing fermentable carbohydrates. The resulting imbalance between demineralization and remineralization leads to progressive mineral loss and cavitation. Despite significant preventive advances, caries remains a major public health issue, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations with limited access to dental care [1].

Fluoride plays a central role in caries prevention, with professional topical application promoting remineralization and inhibiting demineralization [2]. These effects are mainly due to the formation of calcium fluoride-like reservoirs [3], which serve as a proxy for anticaries efficacy [4]. A recent scoping review emphasized the importance of fluoride-based strategies and the need for improvements in fluoride uptake studies [5].

Among professional topical vehicles, fluoride gels and varnishes are widely used, especially for patients at high risk of caries [6, 7]. Gels provide immediate bioavailability, whereas varnishes rely on contact time and gradual fluoride release [8, 9]. Their clinical efficacy is well documented, with gels reducing caries by 28% [10] and varnishes by 43% [11].

In vitro studies assessing fluoride uptake after varnish application must overcome a key challenge: residual varnish on the specimen surface may distort results. Because to its low solubility and strong adhesion, varnish is difficult to remove completely, leading to potential overestimation of fluoride uptake [12–14].

Although various removal methods — mechanical, chemical or both — have been proposed, few studies have rigorously compared their effectiveness. Moreover, varnish composition affects removal outcomes. For example, resin-, polyurethane-, and cyanoacrylate-based varnishes differ in fluoride release and physical properties [15, 16], which may influence both removal efficiency and contamination.

This study aimed to compare established and novel varnish removal protocols using an inert substrate to eliminate fluoride-enamel interaction, improving the accuracy and standardization of fluoride uptake evaluations in vitro.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation and varnish application

Teflon slabs (3 × 3 × 2 mm) were selected as the substrate due to their inert properties. Sample size (n-8/group) was calculated with BioEstat 5.3, using pilot data, with 95% confidence and 85% power. All slab surfaces were protected with wax #7 except for the application area, measured using a digital caliper (± 0.01 mm). Duraphat varnish (5% NaF) was applied with disposable micro-bruhes (5 mg/slab), accurately weighed. Slabs were immersed in artificial saliva (1.25 mL/mm²) for 12 h under continuous flow (2 mL/min) [8].

Varnish removal

Varnish was removed using one of five protocols (n = 8/group): (I) scalpel blade [17], (II) acetone-soaked cotton swab [18], (III) I + II [19], (IV) II + acetone wash [8], (V) plastic spatula + II + 3 × 10-s vortexed acetone washes (new protocol). After each removal procedure, slabs were rinsed with purified water. A positive control group was treated with acidulated phosphate fluoride gel (4 min), followed by 1-min water rinse. Additionally, an untreated baseline group was included, rinsed only with purified water.

Fluoride residual extraction

After varnish removal, each slab was immersed in 0.5 mL of 1 M KOH [20] for 12 h to extract residual fluoride. The KOH solution was then neutralized and buffered with TISAB II containing 1 M HCl. The concentration of residual contaminant fluoride (RCF) was determined using a specific ion electrode, calibrated with standards ranging from 0.03125 to 8.0 µg F/mL and the results expressed in µg F/cm².

Fluoride calculation

Fluoride content (µg) applied per slab was calculated based on varnish concentration and mass. Released fluoride in artificial saliva was averaged, and the residual fluoride remaining after exposure was calculated.

Residual fluoride contaminant determination

Baseline fluoride levels (from untreated slabs) were subtracted from post-treatment RCF. Percentage of residual fluoride was calculated as:

% RCF = 100 – [(F _ after application – F _ after removal) / F _ after application × 100].

Statistical analysis

Data were log-transformed to meet normality (Shapiro-Wilk test). ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc test (α = 5%) was performed using SPSS 21.0.

Results

The percentage of residual fluoride contaminant (% RCF) after varnish removal varied significantly among techniques (Fig. 1). The scalpel blade (I) had the highest contamination (3.4%), significantly greater than other methods. Acetone-based techniques (II-V) achieved %RCF < 0.2%. Prococols IV and V, which added solvent washes, were the most effective, showing no significant difference from each other or from the fluoride gel control.

These findings highlight that acetone-based methods with wash steps effectively minimize residual fluoride, whereas mechanical removal alone is inadequate.

Discussion

This study compared several varnish removal techniques from the literature alongside a new protocol (V). The scalpel blade (I) was the least effective, leaving 3.35% residual fluoride content (RCF), confirming the limitations of purely mechanical methods. Due to the strong adhesion and low solubility of varnish in water and saliva, mechanical removal alone is insufficient and can damage enamel by creating microabrasions. This underscores the need for organic solvents like acetone.

Acetone-based techniques (II-V) were highly effective, with RCF below 0.18%. Acetone efficiently dissolves the polymeric components of the varnish without causing enamel damage, ensuring thorough removal. Techniques IV and V, which combine mechanical and chemical steps, showed statistically significant improvements over I, II, and III and performed comparably to the fluoride gel control. The additional solvent washing step in IV and V maximized varnish removal, making them the most effective approaches.

The newly developed protocol (V) replaced the scalpel blade with a plastic spatula to minimize enamel damage and incorporated vortex-assisted acetone washes, preserving specimen integrity and enhancing varnish removal. These modifications are relevant for minimizing artifacts in fluoride uptake quantification.

Additionally, Teflon slabs were used as an inert substrate [21] to avoid chemical interactions between fluoride and enamel, which could confound quantification of residual varnish. While this approach ensures analytical precision, future studies should also validate the removal protocols on actual dental specimens to assess potential enamel surface alterations and the clinical applicability of the findings.

In conclusion, combining mechanical and chemical approaches — particularly protocols incorporating acetone and vortex-assisted washing—significantly improves the removal of fluoride varnish residues. These methods minimize the presence of residual contaminant fluoride, thereby improving the reliability of fluoride uptake measurements in vitro. Further research is needed to assess protocol performance across diverse varnish formulations.

Limitations
