Impact of ‘spin’ on medical decision-making among healthcare professionals and other health-related decision makers: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Inhu Bae, ShiHoon Choi, Seaun Ryu, Hyeonsun Park, Mingi Kim, Hyangsook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction‘Spin’ refers to reporting practices that distort the interpretation of results and mislead readers’ impression of the research findings so that the results are viewed in a more favourable light. This systematic review aims to assess the impact of spin on the impressions and/or interpretation of research findings among healthcare professionals and decision makers involved in health-related decision-making and to evaluate interventions/strategies designed to reduce the influence of spin.Methods and analysisThis systematic review will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. MEDLINE (via Ovid), EMBASE, the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) and the Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) will be searched from their inceptions to 27 January 2026. Randomised controlled trials evaluating the impact of spin on participants’ impressions and/or interpretation of research findings, as well as trials assessing the effect of interventions/strategies on the recognition and interpretation of spin, will be considered. Study selection, data extraction and risk of bias (RoB) assessment with the Cochrane RoB2 tool will be performed independently by multiple reviewers. Where sufficient data are available, meta-analyses will be conducted.Ethics and disseminationAs this study is based on a review of publicly available literature, ethical approval is not required. The findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals, presentations at international conferences and press releases.Trial registration numberINPLASY202620006.Keywords: Systematic Review, Clinical Decision-Making, Randomized Controlled Trial, Protocols & guidelines

Introduction

Medical professionals, including physicians and clinical staff, and related stakeholders—such as patients, caregivers and decision makers in the field of healthcare—make their decisions based on the related research articles, news reports and large language models trained on such articles.1 2 However, contrary to the rigour and truthfulness we expect in scientific reporting, prior studies have shown many researchers exaggerate their benefits or distract readers from statistically non-significant results.3 Regardless of whether authors’ intentions are conscious or unconscious, distorted presentation of research findings—commonly referred to as ‘spin’ or, by some authors, ‘narrative bias’—has long been recognised as an important issue in the field of scientific transparency.4

However, despite numerous studies examining the prevalence of spin, the medical fields in which it occurs, and the types of studies in which it arises,5,7 there is surprisingly no systematic review investigating whether spin actually affects readers, including healthcare professionals and other stakeholders, and leads them to misinterpret scientific findings. If readers are found to be unaffected by such distorted reporting, it raises the question of whether the extensive efforts devoted to identifying and preventing spin are truly warranted. Conversely, if spin is shown to exert a substantial influence on readers’ interpretations, this would provide a compelling rationale for urgently discouraging and eliminating such distorted reporting practices. However, studies that have examined readers’ responses to spin are scarce, and their findings are likewise inconsistent.8 9

In this context, we will systematically evaluate whether spin in medical research articles influences the impression or the interpretation of the research findings by healthcare professionals, patients, policymakers and/or other relevant stakeholders and explore the types and methods of interventions/strategies that could reduce such influence. Additionally, this review will seek to identify the resulting knowledge gaps and highlight the need for further research.

Methods and analysis

This protocol for systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on 2 Feb 2026 in the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY) database (registration number: INPLASY202620006). Furthermore, this protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) (online supplemental appendix A for the PRISMA-P checklist).10 Any deviations from the protocol will be documented and justified in the final review.

Definition of spin

We define spin as ‘any reporting practices—intentional or unintentional—that, regardless of the result type (eg, treatment effect, adverse effect and diagnostic accuracy), study design or setting, fail to appropriately reflect the nature and interpretable range of the results and distort readers’ impression of the findings.’ Spin commonly occurs from narrative emphasis on research results or the minimisation of adverse effects, which can lead readers to perceive the results in a more favourable light.

Although various definitions of spin have been proposed, our definition draws on key elements from Yavchitz et al,7 Boutron et al6 and Chiu et al.11 This reflects our view that spin should be conceptualised as a phenomenon fundamentally related to 'perception’. We emphasise the importance of caution regarding spin because it can shape readers’ perceptions and, in turn, affect medical decision-making. Additionally, since readers consider not only statistical significance but also the target patient population and adverse effects altogether when making clinical decisions,12 it was deemed necessary to incorporate these elements rather than focusing on a single type of outcome.

In this review, we will include any form of spin corresponding to any of the three categories proposed by Yavchitz et al,7 ie, misleading reporting, misleading interpretation and inappropriate extrapolation, regardless of the clinical field of the source article (eg, surgery, oncology, etc).

Literature searching

We will search the following online databases: MEDLINE (via Ovid), EMBASE and CENTRAL. In addition, considering the possibility that relevant randomised controlled trials (RCTs) may be indexed within the field of medical education, we will also search Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) via EBSCOhost.13 All databases will be searched from their inceptions to 27 January 2026. Search strategies will be composed of two parts: (1) spin and frequently used words related to spin (eg, ‘mislead’ and ‘overstated’) and (2) RCT filters modified and combined from The InterTASC Information Specialists’ Sub-Group Search Filter Resource (ISSG).14 15 In addition, we will manually search the reference lists of the included and relevant studies.

We will restrict the publication date to studies published from 2010 onwards, as the concept of spin was first methodologically recognised in 2010.4 No restrictions will be imposed on publication language and type of publication, such as conference abstracts, as long as it provides sufficient detail that is available for data extraction. Full search strategies for each database can be found in the online supplemental appendix B.

Eligibility criteria

We will include RCTs investigating (1) the impact of spin and (2) the effect of interventions/trainings to mitigate the influence of spin. Both types of RCTs involve the same participant population: healthcare professionals and stakeholders involved in health-related decision-making, including doctors, nursing professionals, patients, caregivers, students in health-related disciplines, researchers, reporters or healthcare-related policymakers.

Inclusion criteria for RCTs evaluating the impact of spin

We will include RCTs that analyse whether participants’ impressions and/or interpretations of study findings are influenced by the presence or absence of spin in experimental content derived from research articles (including all types of scientific articles, news or any products based on articles). Studies should provide participants with experimental content that is either artificially generated without spin or based on existing publications that have been edited to minimise or remove spin. The control group should be given (unedited) content in which spin is present or enhanced. The outcome of interest is whether exposure to spin influences participants’ interpretation or evaluative judgement of research findings presented in the experimental content. These outcomes may include (1) perceived benefit or effectiveness, (2) perceived safety, (3) perceived importance, (4) impressions, (5) preferences or (6) related self-reported judgements of research findings but will not be limited to these outcomes. Data will be included from time points immediately after exposure to content with spin.

Inclusion criteria for RCTs evaluating the impact of interventions/trainings to mitigate spin

We will include RCTs that examine how participants’ impressions and/or interpretations change after receiving interventions or trainings about spin. Studies should test any type of intervention, such as critical appraisal training (CAT) or evidence-based medicine (EBM) training, to identify spin and interpret the research findings appropriately. The control group should receive either no training or other types of intervention that are irrelevant to spin. However, interventions will not be limited to training. Any type of intervention can be included as long as it aims to improve identifying spin and interpreting research results appropriately. Such interventions may include the adjunct use of artificial intelligence (AI) models or the provision of checklists. Outcomes will include participants’ ability to recognise spin and appropriately interpret research findings. These may include (1) correct identification of the presence or absence of spin, (2) appropriateness of interpretation of study findings, (3) susceptibility to misleading conclusions, (4) confidence in interpretation and (5) other related performance-based or self-reported measures but will not be limited to these outcomes. Data will be included from time points immediately after receiving intervention or training about spin or at follow-ups.

These outcomes in both types of RCTs can be measured using self-reported instruments (eg, Likert-scale questionnaires or numeric rating scales), comprehension or interpretation tests or objective measures such as the proportion of correct responses. Given the expected diversity in outcome measures across randomised trials assessing spin, we will not restrict studies based on the type of outcome measure.

Exclusion criteria

Studies not investigating human participants or studies involving non-health-related decision-making participants will be excluded. Non-randomised trials, protocols without results, reviews, case reports and observational studies will also be excluded. Because this systematic review will evaluate human readers, trials focusing on developing or training large language models or other generative AI systems to identify spin in research articles will be excluded, as these models are not considered decision-making agents but rather tools requiring training. Studies investigating outcome reporting bias instead of spin will also be excluded, as outcome reporting bias involves selective reporting of results rather than providing a distorted narrative about the results.16 Studies that exclusively examine the prevalence or severity of spin without assessing its impact or evaluating the interventions aimed at correctly identifying spin will be excluded. CAT or EBM training or any kind of intervention that is not specifically focused on identifying or interpreting spin will also be excluded. Studies with mixed interventions where the impact of spin or the effect of interventions to identify spin and correctly interpret research results cannot be separately evaluated will also be excluded. Interviews and qualitative studies without exposure to spin or without training specifically designed to address spin will not be considered.

Selection process

All retrieved articles will first be imported into Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/), where duplicates will be removed, and the remaining studies will be screened using Covidence’s integrated Cochrane RCT classifier and by title and abstract. The records will then be screened independently by four reviewers (HP, SR, MK and SC). A list of excluded studies will be retained and provided in the final review. If multiple publications appear to originate from the same study project, only the publication providing the most complete dataset will be included. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion among reviewers. Study selection procedures will be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.17

Data collection process

Data will be extracted using a pre-designed data extraction form. One reviewer (IB) will perform the initial data extraction, and a second reviewer (HL) will verify the accuracy and completeness of the extracted data. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Extracted data will be imported into and managed using Microsoft Excel (Office 2024, 64-bit). When outcome data are presented only in graphical form, numeric values will be extracted using Web PlotDigitizer V. 4.7 (Automeris LLC).19 If essential data are missing, we will contact the first or corresponding author; if the data remain unavailable, the study will be included only in the descriptive synthesis and excluded from quantitative pooling.

Risk of bias assessment

We will assess the methodological quality of the included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool,20 which comprises six domains: (1) bias arising from the randomisation process, (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in outcome measurement, (5) bias in the selection of the reported result and (6) the overall risk of bias. Each domain will be judged as having a low risk of bias, a high risk of bias or some concerns. This assessment will be conducted independently by two reviewers (MK and IB), and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (HL).

Data synthesis

If sufficient data are available, meta-analyses will be conducted. We will conduct a pairwise meta-analysis using Cochrane RevMan Web.21 Studies will be grouped into two categories: (1) RCTs that artificially manipulate spin content (generating or removing spin) to examine its impact on participants’ impression and/or interpretation of the results and (2) RCTs that test the interventions (such as CAT or EBM training) to enable participants to identify spin and appropriately interpret research results.

For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios with 95% CIs will be calculated. If results are expressed as short ordinal variables (eg, three-point Likert scales), impressions or interpretations of the results may need to be converted into dichotomous data (eg,‘preferred’ vs ‘disliked’ about results after reading content with spin). In such cases, the midpoint will be used as the reference point,22 23 and results will also be presented as a risk ratio.24 Longer ordinal variables will be analysed using a standardised mean difference (SMD), treating the data as approximately continuous for synthesis purposes.23 25 If sufficient data are available to permit a clinically meaningful alternative categorisation, additional sensitivity analyses will be considered. For continuous outcomes such as numerical rating scales, we will compute SMD, rather than mean differences, to account for the expected variability in measurement scales across studies. If SD are not reported, they will be calculated according to the methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook.24

Given the expected diversity in participant backgrounds and study characteristics, a random-effects model will be applied. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic.26 Study findings will be presented in forest plots. If substantial heterogeneity or other methodological concerns prevent quantitative synthesis, a descriptive analysis will be conducted. Specifically, a structured narrative synthesis will be undertaken, in which results from each study will be summarised and presented individually in tables and text, including key study characteristics, direction and magnitude of impacts of spin, and methodological features. Where appropriate, results will be grouped by outcomes or participants, and effect estimates will be converted to a common metric where feasible to facilitate comparison across studies.27

Subgroup analysis and investigation of heterogeneity

If substantial heterogeneity is detected, we will explore its potential sources through subgroup analyses. These analyses will be conducted based on pre-specified variables that are expected to influence the effect, including participants’ prior research experience, their role in the healthcare system, ie, by health worker classification, their history of training in EBM or EBP (including the duration and frequency of such training), and for those currently engaged in clinical practice, their years of clinical experience.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses will be performed by sequentially excluding studies judged to have a high risk of bias. In addition, where short ordinal variables are converted into dichotomous data, sensitivity analyses using alternative prespecified cut points will be undertaken where feasible to assess the robustness of the findings. If sufficient data are available to permit a clinically meaningful alternative categorisation of longer ordinal variables, additional sensitivity analyses will also be considered.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias will be assessed using a funnel plot, and asymmetry will be evaluated with Egger’s test. However, if fewer than ten studies are included, Egger’s test will not be performed due to insufficient statistical power.28

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the plans of this review protocol.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this systematic review as it will involve analysis of data from previously published studies. The results will be disseminated through presentations at international conferences and publications in peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

This systematic review will comprehensively evaluate the existing evidence on whether spin influences participants’ impressions and/or interpretation of research findings, as well as the effectiveness of interventions/trainings designed to improve the recognition of spin and appropriate interpretation of study findings. Although previous studies have reported the prevalence of spin across different medical fields29 30 and proposed various methodological frameworks to prevent spin,31 systematic evidence examining the actual impact of spin on readers’ interpretation and the effectiveness of interventions aimed at mitigating this impact remains limited.

A major strength of this review lies in its focus on the reader’s perspective rather than the producer’s perspective. Spin is not problematic merely because it represents inappropriate reporting but because it may influence readers’ impressions and interpretations of research findings.32 By focusing on participants’ impressions and/or interpretation as primary outcomes, this review addresses a critical gap in the current literature and provides a reader-centred assessment of the consequences of spin. Another strength of this review is the comprehensive search strategy that extends beyond core medical databases to include educational databases, considering interventions such as training or education designed to improve the identification of spin and appropriate interpretation of research findings. To this end, we will conduct a systematic search that includes ERIC, the leading database in the education field offering the most comprehensive coverage,13 in addition to major medical databases.33 This intervention-focused, comprehensive database search strategy represents an essential component for optimal evidence retrieval.

This review also has several limitations. First, because we limited the study design to RCTs, we may miss other designs widely used to evaluate educational interventions.34 However, after discussion within the review team, RCTs were prioritised to minimise bias and establish causal relationships regarding the impact of spin and the effectiveness of interventions aimed at mitigating this impact.35 Furthermore, it has been documented in previous research that the use of RCTs in educational research has grown substantially over the past 15 years, encompassing various educational interventions across diverse contexts.36 We therefore anticipate that restricting our inclusion criteria to RCTs will still yield a substantial body of relevant evidence. Second, the included participants, such as physicians, nurses, patients, medical students and other healthcare stakeholders, represent heterogeneous populations with varying levels of background knowledge and prior training, which may limit the feasibility of extensive quantitative synthesis. Third, as this review focuses on healthcare-related participants, studies conducted in other disciplines, such as economics or psychology, will not be included, which may limit the generalisability of the findings beyond the healthcare context.

Despite these limitations, we anticipate that this review will provide valuable insights into the range to which spin influences readers’ impressions and interpretation of research findings, as well as the potential role of educational interventions in mitigating these effects. Should sufficient evidence be identified, clarifying the magnitude of spin’s influence may inform researchers, readers, journal editors, and reviewers about whether additional frameworks or guidelines are necessary or whether existing efforts sufficiently address this problem. Ultimately, if the anticipated body of evidence is retrieved, this review has the potential to contribute to improved awareness of spin and to support evidence-based strategies for interpreting research findings more appropriately.
