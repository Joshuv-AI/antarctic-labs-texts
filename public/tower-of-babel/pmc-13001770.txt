MANF improves cognitive function and attenuates neuroinflammation in APP/PS1 transgenic mice through the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway
Authors: Yanchao Tang, Enba Zhuo, Pengman Li, Chen Wang, Xudong Hu, Xuesheng Liu, Guanghong Xu, Qiying Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, characterized by progressive cognitive decline, as well as pathological features such as β-amyloid (Aβ) plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal loss. Growing evidence suggests that neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis play critical roles in the pathogenesis of AD, thereby contributing to neuronal damage and cognitive decline. Mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), an endoplasmic reticulum stress-inducible protein, has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects by modulating immune responses, alleviating oxidative stress, and suppressing apoptosis in various neurological disease models. These properties suggest that MANF could serve as a promising therapeutic candidate treating AD. This study investigated the therapeutic potential of MANF in improving cognitive function and ameliorating pathological changes in APP/PS1 transgenic (Tg) mice by modulating neuroinflammation and enhancing neural plasticity.MethodsBeginning at 10 months of age, male APP/PS1 transgenic mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of recombinant human MANF (rhMANF) at a dose of 1 μg/g for a duration of one month. Behavioral assessments, including the Morris water maze, open field, and fear conditioning tests, were conducted to evaluate cognitive function. Brain tissue was analyzed for β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis using immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, western blotting, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and a BV2 microglial/HT22 neuronal co-culture system were used to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying MANF’s effects.ResultsrhMANF treatment significantly improved cognitive function in APP/PS1 Tg mice by reducing Aβ deposition, inhibiting microglial activation, and suppressing inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6). MANF also mitigated oxidative stress, reduced neuronal apoptosis, and restored synaptic protein levels, including those of Postsynaptic density protein-95(PSD95) and synaptophysin (SYN). In vitro studies confirmed that MANF effectively counteracts Aβ1–42-induced toxicity in the BV2/HT22 co-culture system. Transcriptomic analysis identified that MANF exerts its protective effects by regulating the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing inflammation and promoting synaptic plasticity.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the protective role of MANF in AD and establishes MANF as a promising therapeutic candidate for AD and aging-related neurodegenerative disorders.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and presents a challenge to global public health.1 In 2019, it was estimated that approximately 50 million people worldwide were living with AD, a number expected to triple to 152 million by 2050, with one new diagnosis occurring every 3 s.2 Despite significant progress in understanding AD pathology, no effective treatment has been developed, emphasizing the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches.

The hallmark pathological features of AD include extracellular Aβ plaque accumulation, tau protein hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss.3 Neuroinflammation is pivotal in AD progression, driven largely by the abnormal activation of microglia. This chronic inflammatory response exacerbates synaptic dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β,4, 5, 6 ultimately contributing to cognitive decline.7, 8 Aβ initiates this inflammatory cascade by activating the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which then recruits adaptor proteins such as TRIF to trigger the p38 MAPK signaling pathway.9 This signaling pathway promotes the excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby perpetuating a harmful cycle of inflammation and neuronal damage. NF-κB(p65), a key transcription factor, plays a particularly critical role in mediating neuroinflammation in AD.10 The hippocampus is one of the earliest and most profoundly affected brain regions in AD and plays a crucial role in learning and memory.11 Numerous studies have demonstrated that hippocampal dysfunction is closely associated with the cognitive deficits observed in AD patients, making it a key region for investigating disease mechanisms and therapeutic interventions.12, 13

Mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) is an endoplasmic reticulum stress-responsive protein that exhibits neuroprotective properties in several neurological disorders.14, 15, 16 Although MANF is expressed in various brain regions, its potential role in modulating AD-related pathology remains underexplored. Studies have demonstrated that intranasal administration of rhMANF can effectively penetrate the blood-brain barrier and exert potent anti-inflammatory effects.17 In addition, MANF has shown promising neuroprotective effects in various models of neurodegenerative diseases.18, 19, 20 For example, it mitigates Aβ-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress toxicity16 and enhances mitochondrial function in Parkinson’s disease models.18, 21 Preliminary findings also suggest that MANF can suppress the expression of inflammatory mediators by modulating NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways.9, 22 Given the hippocampus’s critical role in cognitive function and its vulnerability in AD, investigating the expression pattern and functional role of MANF in the hippocampus may offer important insights into AD pathogenesis and reveal novel therapeutic targets.

In this study, APP/PS1 transgenic (Tg) mice were used as an experimental model to investigate the role of MANF in AD. The effects of MANF on cognitive function, neuroinflammation, synaptic integrity, and neuronal survival in APP/PS1 transgenic mice were evaluated. Furthermore, the underlying molecular mechanisms were further investigated.

Materials and methods

Animals

In this research, we utilized ten-month-old APP/PS1 Tg alongside their wild-type counterparts, all obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. in Nanjing, China. The mice were housed under controlled conditions with regulated temperature, a 12-hour light-dark cycle, and free access to standard feed and water. The APP/PS1 Tg were randomly divided into a control group and a rhMANF treatment group. The MANF group was given daily intraperitoneal injections of MANF (1 μg/g/d, prepared in phosphate-buffered saline) over the course of a month, while the wild-type and APP/PS1 control groups was given the same volume of PBS. A double-blind protocol was employed. Throughout the behavioral assessments and data collection, the researchers conducting the experiments and those performing the data analysis were blinded to the group allocations. At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized, and a selection of half-brains from each group was randomly chosen for further investigation. Analytical methods included paraffin sectioning for immunohistochemical staining, frozen sectioning for immunofluorescence, RNA sequencing, and Western blot analysis. Additionally, the cortex and hippocampus were dissected as needed for more in-depth analyses. All the experiments were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (approval number: LLSC20190466), and all experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Cells

To assess the regulatory influence of MANF on neuroinflammation and apoptosis, two distinct cell lines were utilized: HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cells and BV2 mouse microglial cells (Procell, China). The HT22 cells were maintained in a high-glucose DMEM environment enriched with 10 % fetal bovine serum, along with 100 U/mL of penicillin and 100 µg/mL of streptomycin. Meanwhile, BV2 cells were grown in DMEM/F-12 medium, adhering to the same culture conditions as those for HT22 cells. Both cell lines were kept at 37 °C within a humidified chamber containing 5 % CO₂ and 95 % air, with regular updates to the culture medium to ensure optimal growth conditions.

Preparation of MANF

rhMANF protein was prepared as described in our previous study.23 The rhMANF solution was portioned into sterile EP tubes and kept at – 80 °C until required. Prior to use, the protein was diluted to a working concentration of 2 mg/mL in 0.9 % saline, sterilized by passage through a 0.22-μm filter, and adjusted to the target concentration. Ten-month-old APP/PS1 Tg mice were administered daily intraperitoneal injections of rhMANF (1 µg/g/d) over a month, while the control group was given PBS under the same protocol.

Preparation of Aβ1–42

The Aβ1–42 peptide was sourced from Shanghai Genery Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and prepared following a well-documented protocol.24 Initially, the peptide was dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a concentration of 1 mM. The solution was subsequently left uncapped in a biosafety cabinet overnight to allow complete evaporation of HFIP. Once dried, the peptide was reconstituted in DMSO to reach a final concentration of 5 mM and further diluted to 100 µM using phenol red-free F12 medium. After incubating at 4 °C for 24 h, the Aβ1–42 solution was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, enriched with Aβ1–42 oligomers, was carefully collected and quantified using a Micro BCA assay (Thermo, #23325, Waltham, MA, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining of cell cultures

BV2 cells were either left untreated or pretreated with 10 μg/mL MANF, followed by exposure to 10 μM Aβ1–42 for 24 h. Post-treatment, the supernatants were collected for subsequent co-culture analysis. The cells were then washed twice with PBS and put in 4 % PFA. The cells were fixed and then blocked with 5 % BSA (HY-D0842, MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA)). They were then treated with the primary anti-IBA-1 antibody (1:4000, ab178846, Abcam, USA) for one night at 4 °C. The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then left at room temperature for two hours with the Alexa-594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody. Lastly, DAPI was used to stain the nuclei for visualization.

The One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (C1088, Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to check for apoptosis in HT22 cells. All steps followed the reagent manufacturer’s instructions. After staining, a BX-51 upright microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to take the pictures.

Behavioral tests

Morris water maze test

The Morris Water Maze (MWM) test was performed on 11-month-old mice. Wild-type (WT) mice, APP/PS1 Tg + PBS mice, and APP/PS1 Tg rhMANF mice were the three groups included in the experimental design. Each group consisted of eight specific animals. The testing environment was a circular pool, measuring 120 cm in diameter, containing a submerged escape platform (10 cm in diameter) just beneath the water’s surface. To obscure visibility, we added non-toxic titanium dioxide powder to the water, creating a murky setting. Over the course of the experiment, the temperature of the water was kept at a constant 22 °C, with a standard deviation of one degree Celsius. The MWM test included a training phase lasting five days, followed by a probe trial on the final day. For the duration of the training period, each mouse participated in four sessions each day, with a 15-min break in between each session. The mice need to find the hidden platform within 90 s. During each session, the mice successfully located the hidden platform within the allotted 90 s. In the probe trial, the platform was withdrawn. This allowed us to evaluate cognitive performance based on two primary measures: the number of crossings over the former platform location and the time spent in the target quadrant.

Open field test

The Open Field Test (OFT) was carried out in a square arena that was completely opaque and measured forty centimeters in length, breadth, and height. The central portion of the arena, referred to as the “center zone”, spanned 20 cm by 20 cm, while the remaining area was labeled the “peripheral zone”. Each mouse was given the opportunity to freely explore the arena for a period of five minutes, and then the arena was meticulously cleaned to remove any pervasive odors. The total distance traveled, the time spent in the center, the freezing time (the total duration of complete immobility excluding respiration) and the latency (the time in seconds for the mice to reach to the location at the first attempt) to the center by each mouse during the test was precisely recorded to assess activity levels.

Fear conditioning test

Before the experiment commenced, the mice were allowed to acclimatize to the testing environment for half an hour. The training procedure involved a 2-min exploration phase of the context, followed by two 1-s foot shocks at 0.6 mA, with a 60-s interval in between. After the second shock, the mice were placed back in their cages for 1 min. After a period of twenty-four hours, the mice were brought back to the training chamber and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. During this time, instances of freezing behavior were recorded in 5-s intervals, categorized as either “freezing” or “not freezing”. The freezing percentage was calculated as the ratio of intervals exhibiting freezing behavior to the total number of 5-s intervals.

Y maze test

The Y-maze test can be used to assess the memory function. The test used a horizontal maze consisting of three arms, each at a 120-degree angle to the others. During the test, one arm (defined as the novel arm) was blocked to prevent the mice from entering, and the mice were placed on one arm (defined as the starting arm). The remaining arm was defined as the old arm, and the mice were allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. After a 20-min interval, the blockage on the novel arm was removed, allowing the mice to freely explore all three arms in 5 min. The number of times the mouse enters each arm and the duration of stay within each arm were recorded using an EthoVisionXT video imaging system. Finally, the number of times the mouse enters the novel arm and the duration of stay were counted.

Buried food test

During the 3-day acclimatization phase, each mouse was given two pieces of sweet cereal. One hour before the formal test, the mice were placed in a test cage with 5 cm of clean bedding to allow them to acclimatize. One piece of sweet cereal was buried 0.5 cm below the surface of the bedding, with the location randomly selected each time. The mouse was placed in the center of the test cage. The time from when the mouse was placed in the test cage to when it used its front paws and/or teeth to pry open the food piece was recorded and this was defined as the latency to eat food. The observation period was 5 min. After each test, the bedding was changed, and the cage was cleaned with a 70 % ethanol solution to avoid olfactory cues.

RNA sequencing

In this study, hippocampi were isolated from APP/PS1 Tg + PBS and APP/PS1 Tg + MANF mice for sequencing. To get the total RNA from hippocampus, the Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used. After enrichment, fragmentation, and reverse transcription of mRNA into cDNA, the resultant cDNA library was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 by means of the sequencing technique.

Cell viability assays

Following the instructions provided by the manufacturer, a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay was used in order to determine the viability of the cells. Plates with 96 wells were used to plate around 8000–10,000 cells each well, allowing cells to grow for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of Aβ1–42, MANF, a combination of Aβ1–42 and MANF, or a control culture medium. After the drug treatment period, 10 μL of CCK-8 detection solution was diluted with 90 μL of culture medium and added to each well. The plates were then incubated for 1 h, followed by absorbance measurement at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (TECAN, Switzerland).

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was quantified using the BBoxiProbe ROS Assay Kit (BB-470516). Cryosections, unfixed and ranging in thickness from 10 to 20 μm, were prepared and treated with 200 μL of a washing solution. These sections were then left to incubate at room temperature for a brief period of 3–5 min. After removal of the washing solution, 100–200 μL of a working solution containing the dye probe was added. The sections were subsequently incubated in a light-protected chamber at 37 °C for a duration of 20–60 min. Following the removal of the dye solution, the sections were either rinsed with PBS or mounted using glycerol and a coverslip. Fluorescence microscopy was employed to capture images and assess variations in fluorescence intensity.

Western blot

Utilizing a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer that included a combination of protease and phosphatase inhibitors, proteins were extracted from cellular and tissue samples throughout the process of protein isolation. The protein samples were separated using a technique known as sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then they were transferred to membranes made of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF). For the purpose of preventing nonspecific binding, the membranes were suspended in a solution of 5 % skim milk and allowed to incubate for one hour at room temperature. After this, they were allowed to incubate with a variety of primary antibodies for a whole night at a temperature of 4 °C. All primary antibodies used are listed in Table S1. After extensive washing, the membranes were treated with the suitable secondary antibodies for an hour, including goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, ab205719, ab97051, Abcam, USA). Detection of signals was carried out using the Immobilon Western HRP substrate Luminol reagent (Millipore, USA), and the protein bands were visualized with a Tanon imaging system (Shanghai, China). Band intensity quantification was accomplished using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

TUNEL and NeuN dual staining

To assess hippocampal neuronal apoptosis among different groups of mice, immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Apoptotic cells were identified through in situ staining with a one-step TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime), which marked positive cells with green fluorescence. Neurons were then specifically labeled using a primary antibody targeting NeuN (Abcam, 1:100 dilution, ab177487). Following this, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (blue fluorescence, BL105A, Biosharp) at 37 °C for 10 min to highlight cell nuclei. The prepared samples were subsequently examined under a microscope for detailed observation.

Immunohistochemistry staining

A multiplex immunofluorescence kit was used to stain multiple indicators requiring co-localization (RS0111, Immunoway, USA). The brain sections underwent a step-by-step process involving treatment with xylene and ethanol solutions to achieve dehydration and fixation. In order to retrieve the antigen, the samples were heated in a citrate buffer with a concentration of 10 mM and a pH of 6.0 for a period of fifteen minutes in a microwave. An endogenous peroxidase inhibitor (Zhongshan Golden Bridge, China) was applied after the samples reached room temperature to prevent any endogenous peroxidase activity. After that, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for a whole night at a temperature of 4 °C, as directed by the corresponding manufacturer. Anti-Aβ1–42 (1:1000, ab201060, Abcam, USA) and anti-IBA-1 (1:1000, YA5582, MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA)) were the main antibodies used in this study. Following the day before, the sections were subjected to a secondary antibody treatment at 37 °C for a duration of one hour. DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) staining was carried out to visualize the antibody interactions, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. For the quantitative analysis of integrated optical density (IOD), the hippocampal region and selected cortical areas were examined under uniform magnification across all regions of interest (ROIs). Three mice were selected from each group, and two brain sections were randomly chosen from each mouse, resulting in a total of six sections per group. Observations and imaging were performed using a BX-51 upright microscope (Olympus), and the positively stained areas were quantitatively assessed utilizing the thresholding tool in ImageJ software.

Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy

The NF-κB activity was evaluated using the NF-κB Activation-Nuclear Translocation Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Bv2 cells were inoculated onto cell slides inside 24-well plates. Following exposure to Aβ1–42 and incubation with or without MANF for 24 h, the media was discarded, and the cells were then fixed and blocked. The cells were subsequently coated with a prepared NF-κB p65 primary antibody solution overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, Bv2 cells were treated with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody for one hour at ambient temperature. Subsequent to washing, the cells were labeled with DAPI, subjected to further washing, and observed using a confocal microscope (Sony, Japan).

H&E staining

Morphological alterations in the hippocampal area were examined through H&E staining. The paraffin-embedded sections of hippocampal tissue underwent deparaffinization via a series of graded ethanol treatments and were then rehydrated in water. Following this, the sections were stained with an H&E staining kit (Solarbio, China), adhering to the guidelines set by the manufacturer. Imaging was conducted using an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

ELISA assay

The levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in the hippocampal regions of mice, as well as in the supernatants of BV2 cells, were assessed using ELISA kits (EM0183, Wuhan Fine Biotech, China; ELK1271, ELK Biotechnology, China; KE1418, Immunoway, USA), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer (n = 4 for each group). Each sample was evaluated in triplicate. Measurements were taken at a wavelength of 450 nm with a microplate reader(Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Following one-way or two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied to assess intergroup differences. P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****) were the criteria used to determine the degree of significance.

Compared with wild-type (WT) mice, APP/PS1 Tg mice exhibited a significant upregulation of MANF expression in the hippocampus, as demonstrated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 1A,B). To identify the cellular source of MANF in the context of Alzheimer’s disease pathology, we conducted multiplex immunofluorescence staining on hippocampal sections from APP/PS1 Tg mice. MANF improves cognitive deficits in 10-month-old APP/PS1 Tg mice

The experimental workflow of this study is summarized in Fig. 2A. First, we confirmed that MANF injection alone did not affect cognitive function in WT mice (Fig. S2). Next, we evaluated the effect of MANF on cognitive function in APP/PS1 Tg mice. In the MWM training phase, APP/PS1 Tg mice displayed longer escape latencies than WT mice, reflecting impaired spatial learning. These deficits were significantly alleviated by treatment with rhMANF (Fig. 2B). Trajectory maps from the MWM test (Fig. 2E) further illustrate these findings. During the testing phase, APP/PS1 Tg mice spent significantly less time in the target quadrant and crossed fewer platforms than WT mice. These effects were reversed by rhMANF treatment (Fig. 2C,D). The OFT showed no significant differences in the total distance traveled among the three groups, indicating that locomotor activity was unaffected (Fig. 2F,G). However, in the Fear conditioning test, APP/PS1 Tg mice exhibited a marked reduction in freezing time, indicating impaired associative memory. This deficit was also significantly improved after rhMANF treatment (Fig. 2H,I). MANF alleviates pathological features of APP/PS1 Tg mice and protects against neuronal loss and apoptosis in the hippocampus

As shown in Fig. 3A, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that rhMANF treatment significantly reduced Aβ plaque deposition in the dentate gyrus (DG) and CA1 regions of the hippocampus in APP/PS1 Tg mice (Fig. 3B–D). Further immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that the markedly increased neuronal apoptosis observed in APP/PS1 Tg mice (vs. WT) was significantly alleviated following rhMANF treatment (Fig. 3E,F). This protective effect was corroborated by H&E staining, demonstrating disrupted neuronal organization, blurred boundaries, and pronounced neuronal loss in the DG region of APP/PS1 Tg mice (Fig. 3G). In contrast, the MANF-treated group exhibited improved tissue structure with well-organized neuronal arrangements, indicating that MANF protects against neuronal degeneration. Western blot analysis provided additional evidence of the neuroprotective effects of MANF. rhMANF treatment significantly enhanced the expression of the synapse-associated proteins PSD95 and SYN in APP/PS1 Tg mice (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, it increased the levels of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2, while reducing pro-apoptotic markers, including BAX and C-caspase 3 (Fig. 3I–M). Together, these findings demonstrate that MANF mitigates pathological features in the brains of APP/PS1 Tg mice by reducing Aβ plaque deposition and protecting hippocampal neurons from apoptosis. MANF reduces reactive microglia, inflammation, and oxidative stress in the brains of APP/PS1 Tg mice

MANF alleviates Aβ1–42-induced neuroinflammation in BV2 cells via the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB pathway

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of MANF's neuroprotective effects, a neuroinflammatory model was established using BV2 microglial cells stimulated with Aβ1–42 (Fig. 6A). Cell viability assessed by the CCK-8 assay revealed that MANF alone had no effect on BV2 viability, whereas Aβ1–42 significantly decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S1A). Importantly, MANF treatment effectively alleviated Aβ1–42-induced cytotoxicity and restored cell viability (Fig. S1B). Based on these findings, 10 μg/mL MANF and 10 μM Aβ1–42 were selected for subsequent experiments. ELISA analysis demonstrated that MANF significantly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, following Aβ1–42 exposure (Fig. 6B–D). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that MANF attenuated BV2 microglial activation induced by Aβ1–42 (Fig. 6E,F). To investigate the effect of MANF on NF-κB signaling, confocal microscopy was employed to assess the nuclear translocation of NF-κB in BV2 cells. Aβ1–42 stimulation induced robust nuclear translocation of NF-κB, while MANF treatment markedly suppressed this translocation (Fig. 6G), indicating that MANF can inhibit NF-κB activation and downstream inflammatory responses. Western blot analysis further confirmed that MANF downregulated the expression of key components of the NF-κB pathway, including TLR4, p-p65, and phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα), which were otherwise upregulated by Aβ1–42 stimulation (Fig. 6H–L). Together, these results suggest that MANF significantly attenuates Aβ1–42-induced neuroinflammation in BV2 microglial cells by suppressing activation of the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Discussion

The exact pathogenesis of AD is unclear and there are no effective prevention and treatment methods. The neuroinflammation induced by Aβ plays an important role in the development of AD.25 Endoplasmic reticulum stress plays a significant role in the neuroinflammation induced by Aβ.26 MANF is both a neurotrophic factor and an endoplasmic reticulum stress-inducing protein. It has been proven to exert protective effects in various central nervous system diseases, including ischemic brain injury17 and Parkinson’s disease.27 This study is the first to demonstrate that exogenous administration of recombinant human MANF protein exerts a protective effect in an AD mouse model. Specifically, it alleviates the cognitive impairment of AD mice, inhibits synaptic loss and neuronal cell apoptosis. The specific cellular and molecular mechanisms may be closely related to MANF's inhibition of the abnormal activation of the TLR4/MYD88/NF-kB signaling pathway in microglia and the mediation of neuroinflammation.

MANF can exhibit increased expression under various disease conditions. In particular, the upregulation of its expression in immune-regulatory cells such as mononuclear macrophages is considered to have protective effects, such as inhibiting inflammation.28 In this study, MANF expression was found to be markedly elevated in microglia, suggesting a potential intrinsic protective response during AD-associated neuroinflammation. However, despite this upregulation, APP/PS1 Tg mice exhibited pronounced microglial activation, indicating that the endogenous levels of MANF were insufficient to counteract Aβ-mediated neurotoxicity. These observations raise the possibility that exogenous administration of MANF may serve as a more effective therapeutic strategy. Furthermore, APP/PS1 Tg mice pretreated with recombinant MANF exhibited significant improvements in cognitive performance, accompanied by a marked attenuation of neuroinflammatory responses. These findings provide compelling evidence that MANF plays a pivotal role in mitigating cognitive impairment in AD, likely through modulation of glial activation and inflammatory pathways.

Synaptic loss induced by Aβ is closely associated with learning and memory deficits in AD,29 and Aβ production increases as AD progresses.30 This excessive accumulation impairs both synaptic number and plasticity, and the resulting synapses may exhibit abnormal morphology or composition.31 Aβ also disrupts synaptic vesicle transport.32 In AD patients, the presence of Aβ is associated with reduced levels of synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25), SYN, synaptotagmin, and other presynaptic proteins.33 It was found that the levels of PSD95 and SYN were significantly reduced in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 transgenic mice. While, administration of MANF reversed this decline, suggesting that it may restore synaptic function and improve cognitive deficits by upregulating PSD95 and SYN expression in the hippocampus.

Microglial activation is a key hallmark of neuroinflammation.34 Microglia, the resident macrophages of the central nervous system, continuously survey their environment, and their activation and proliferation are central features of various neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and Parkinson’s disease.35 In this study, a marked increase in microglial activation was observed in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 Tg mice, predominantly exhibiting the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Treatment with MANF significantly reduced microglial activation and decreased the proportion of M1-type microglia, indicating that MANF dampens neuroinflammatory responses in AD. Mechanistically, these data suggest that the therapeutic effects of MANF involve the inhibition of TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, specifically by blocking nuclear translocation of p65. Notably, microglial polarization toward the M1 phenotype is closely associated with increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which further exacerbate neuronal dysfunction and apoptosis, ultimately leading to cognitive impairment.

Both neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis are pathological features shared across multiple neurodegenerative conditions.36, 37 Previous studies have shown that developing brain tissue exposed to sevoflurane undergoes neuronal apoptosis via activation of the JNK/c-JUN/AP-1 signaling pathway, leading to a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio, mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), and intrinsic apoptosis, thereby impairing adolescent cognitive performance.38 In our study, TUNEL staining revealed that MANF treatment alleviated neuronal apoptosis in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 Tg mice, suggesting a role for MANF in modulating neuronal survival. Furthermore, in vitro co-culture experiments using Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells and HT22 neurons showed that conditioned media from Aβ-treated BV2 cells induced neuronal apoptosis—an effect mitigated by MANF pre-treatment. These findings align with previous reports and support the notion that MANF interferes with Aβ-induced microglia-mediated neurotoxicity.

As mentioned above, the upregulation of MANF in various pathological states of multiple organ systems has been proven to have protective effects. Notably, a recent study by Zhang et al. demonstrated overexpression or knockdown of endogenous MANF protein in neurons of the hippocampal region would correspondingly exacerbate or alleviate the behavioral abnormalities and synaptic changes in AD model mice.39 These findings have prompted considerable interest and thoughtful consideration in our research. It is well established that overexpression of proteins can lead to cell death through unfolded protein response, energy metabolism abnormalities, etc. This has been confirmed in the results of Zhang et al.’s study. At the same time, their research found that knocking down MANF in neurons could alleviate the behavioral abnormalities and synaptic abnormalities related to AD. Combining our results and this discovery, it is hypothesized that MANF derived from microglia plays an inhibitory role in the process of inhibiting AD, while exogenous MANF mainly exerts its inhibitory effect by suppressing excessive activation of microglia to reduce inflammation and reduce neuronal apoptosis. Certainly, numerous aspects concerning the influence of MANF on AD remain to be further elucidated, including further exploration of cellular and molecular mechanisms, whether there are differences in the effects on AD with different administration routes and doses, and ultimately the transformation from cell experiments to animal models and clinical applications.

Despite the promising results, this study has some limitations. First, the experiments were conducted exclusively on 10-month-old APP/PS1 Tg mice. Future studies should examine the effects of MANF in mice of different age groups to better understand the treatment time course and efficacy. Second, although the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway was the primary focus, other potential mechanisms, including the p38 MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways, remain to be further investigated. The method of administration, dosage, and long-term safety of MANF require optimization for clinical translation. Additional studies using diverse animal models and preclinical trials are crucial for establishing the therapeutic potential of MANF.

In summary, this study demonstrates that endogenous MANF expression is elevated in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 Tg mice, particularly in microglia. Exogenous administration of MANF ameliorates cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 Tg mice, primarily by suppressing TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling and subsequent nuclear translocation of p65. This inhibition attenuates neuroinflammation, restores synaptic integrity and reduces neuronal apoptosis, highlighting MANF as a promising therapeutic candidate for AD.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge，this study is the first to demonstrate that MANF exerts neuroprotective effects in APP/PS1 Tg mice by inhibiting the TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. rhMANF treatment significantly improved cognitive function, reduced Aβ plaque deposition and neuroinflammation and suppressed neuronal apoptosis in APP/PS1 Tg mice. Mechanistic investigations have highlighted the broad applicability of MANF in neurodegenerative diseases, extending from AD to other neuroinflammatory disorders. These findings suggest that MANF is a novel therapeutic target for treating AD. Future research should investigate the involvement of MANF in other related pathways and promote its clinical translation to provide more effective treatment options for patients with AD.
