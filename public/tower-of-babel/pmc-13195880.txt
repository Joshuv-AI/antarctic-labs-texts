Efficacy of PD-1 inhibitor plus induction chemotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma with high-grade extranodal extension: a multicenter retrospective analysis
Authors: Pingting Zhou, Xunmei Liu, Shuang Huang, Yunxiu Luo, Yuefeng Hu, Fapeng Wang, Rensheng Wang, Tingting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsHigh-grade radiologic extranodal extension (G2/3-rENE) is a well-established adverse prognostic factor in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), as demonstrated in our previous research. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining a PD-1 inhibitor with induction chemotherapy (IC) in this high-risk patient population.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 217 histologically confirmed non-metastatic NPC patients from three centers. Group A (n = 104) received IC plus anti-PD-1 therapy followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), while Group B (n = 113) received IC alone followed by CCRT. Clinical response rates, survival outcomes, and treatment-related toxicities were compared, and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsPost-IC, Group A demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to Group B, with a significantly higher complete response rate (CRR: 21.15% vs. 7.96%, p = 0.001) and objective response rate (ORR: 97.12% vs. 88.50%, p = 0.001). Furthermore, the combination group showed markedly improved rates of 2-year progression-free survival (PFS: 93.2% vs. 81.0%) and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS: 97.1% vs. 84.8%). Subgroup analyses revealed that patients with baseline EBV DNA < 400 IU/mL, LDH < 2×ULN, T3–4 or N2-3 stage, stage IVa disease, and G3-rENE benefited more from combined treatment. Post-treatment immune profiling revealed reduced total lymphocyte counts with decreased CD4⁺and increased CD8⁺T cells, resulting in a lower CD4⁺/CD8⁺ratio. Morover, patients exhibiting a less pronounced decrease in this ratio were more likely to achieve a complete response.ConclusionsPD-1 blockade with IC significantly improves disease control in high-risk NPC patients with G2/3-rENE. T cell subset dynamics may help predict treatment response, supporting the incorporation of immunotherapy regimens as frontline treatment for this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15995-3.

Background

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial malignancy arising from the nasopharyngeal mucosa, with over 70% of patients presenting with cervical lymph node involvement at diagnosis [1–2]. Currently, the standard treatment for patients with cervical lymph node metastasis is induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) [3–4]. However, patients with high-risk lymph node metastasis have poorer prognosis, and applying a uniform treatment approach clearly cannot meet the therapeutic intensity required for these patients [5]. Unlike other head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, surgery is not typically performed as an initial treatment for NPC, which is mainly managed with radiotherapy. Consequently, lymph node staging in NPC does not include pathological criteria. However, studies have shown that, similar to other head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, radiological extranodal extension (rENE) is a decisive prognostic factor in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [6]. Both our previous research and other studies have shown that high-grade rENE (G2 or G3) is consistently linked to worse clinical outcomes—including reduced rates of clinical complete remission, nodal disease-free survival, progression-free survival (PFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and overall survival (OS), compared to those without or with G1-rENE [7–11]. Notably, the 9th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system of the NPC incorporated G3-rENE as N3 disease due to its correlation with poor prognosis [12]. Thus, optimizing therapeutic strategies for high-risk G2/3-rENE subgroup remains a clinical imperative to prolong NPC patient survival.

​ NPC is marked by intense lymphocytic infiltration in the tumor microenvironment and the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) on tumor cells, creating a biologically immunosensitive setting. These features support the rationale for immune checkpoint blockade, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, as a promising treatment strategy [13]. Recently, three large phase III clinical trials have confirmed that PD-1 inhibitors combined with gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GP) chemotherapy regimens significantly prolonged PFS and OS in recurrent/metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma, establishing this therapeutic approach as the standard first-line treatment for advanced NPC [14–16]. Prompted by these significant efficacy improvements in the advanced setting, subsequent research has explored adding immunotherapy to chemoradiotherapy regimens for locally advanced NPC. Notably, in 2023, the CONTINUUM trial demonstrated improved 3-year event-free survival (EFS) rates for patients receiving PD-1 inhibitors alongside chemoradiotherapy compared to chemoradiotherapy alone [17]. As a result, this combination approach has been incorporated into the latest Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines as a Category IB treatment option [18]. However, it remains unknown whether the clinical benefits of immune checkpoint inhibitors extend equally across rENE status in NPC. While direct evidence in NPC is lacking, related research offers some insight. A multicenter, phase 2 trial investigating neoadjuvant pembrolizumab in resectable, HPV-unrelated head and neck cancer defined high-risk pathology as the presence of positive surgical margins and/or ENE [19]. The study found that after a single dose, 22% of patients achieved a pathologic tumor response (pTR) of over 50%, and another 22% achieved a pTR of 10–49%. Furthermore, the finding that the study’s post-neoadjuvant high-risk cohort was smaller than anticipated (50% vs. an estimated 80%) may indirectly suggest the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors in this patient population, including those with ENE. Nevertheless, the specific efficacy of adopting this induction immunotherapy model for NPC patients with high-grade ENE remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the real-world efficacy and safety of adding a PD-1 inhibitor to standard IC followed by CCRT in patients with G2/3-rENE-positive NPC. The goal of this approach was to explore whether this intensified approach offers survival advantages for a biologically aggressive subset of NPC patients.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Eligible participants were identified from three tertiary medical centers—The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, Hainan Cancer Hospital, and Zhejiang Cancer Hospital—between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022. This study is a pooled post-hoc analysis of patients predominantly derived from three clinical trials (NCT05340270, NCT04907370, and NCT03984357), along with a subset of patients from non-trial, real-world clinival practice. All patients were newly diagnosed with non-metastatic NPC and received either PD-1 inhibitor plus IC (Group A), or IC alone (Group B), both followed by CCRT. Clinical data were extracted from hospital databases, encompassing demographics (age, sex, height, weight), treatment regimens, therapeutic responses, and treatment-emergent adverse events. Additional data sources included nasoendoscopy, imaging (contrast-enhanced CT, MRI of the nasopharynx and neck, chest CT, abdominal ultrasound or CT, bone ECT, and/or whole-body PET-CT), and laboratory tests (e.g., complete blood counts, serum biochemistry, and EBV DNA quantification).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Histologically confirmed NPC; (3) Imaging-confirmed G2 or G3 rENE; (4) ECOG performance status of 0–1; (5) Availability of complete clinical records; (6) Receipt of at least two cycles of IC with or without a PD-1 inhibitor, followed by CCRT; and (7) Use of intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) for the nasopharynx and cervical nodes (excluding Gamma Knife or other local therapies). Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete clinical records; (2) Presence of other malignancies; (3) Premature treatment discontinuation; or (4) Prior exposure to anti-tumor therapy.

The rENE grading criteria were defined as follows [7]: Grade 0 (G0) indicates no evidence of rENE; Grade 1 (G1) is assigned for infiltration into the surrounding fat; Grade 2 (G2) describes coalescent lymph nodes; and Grade 3 (G3) represents advanced rENE with invasion of adjacent skin and muscles. Two experienced radiologists independently reviewed the pre-treatment MRI scans to identify cases with grade 2 or 3 radiologic extranodal extension (G2/3-rENE). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency. All patients were staged based on the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system of the NPC.

Treatment protocol

Immunotherapy: Patients in the immunotherapy arm received one of the following PD-1 inhibitors during the induction phase: camrelizumab, pembrolizumab, toripalimab, tislelizumab, sintilimab, or penpulimab. These agents were administered intravenously at fixed 3-week intervals, for a minimum of two immunotherapy cycles during IC.

Chemotherapy: (a) IC: Two primary chemotherapy regimens were employed—GP (gemcitabine plus cisplatin) and TP (docetaxel plus cisplatin). In the GP protocol, patients received gemcitabine at a dose of 1.0 g/m² on Days 1 and 8, combined with cisplatin at 80 mg/m² on Days 1. For the TP regimen, docetaxel 75 mg/m² was administered in conjunction with cisplatin 80 mg/m² on Days 1. Both regimens were delivered every 3 weeks for up to four cycles; (b) CCRT: concurrent chemotherapy was predominantly platinum-based. Either cisplatin or nedaplatin was given at a dosage of 100 mg/m², initiated on the first day of radiotherapy and repeated every 3 weeks for up to three cycles. Treatment regimens, dosages, and administration schedules were determined by senior oncologists based on the latest guidelines for the treatment of NPC and a holistic evaluation including patient demographics (age, sex, body surface area), performance status, disease burden, and chemotherapy tolerance.

Radiotherapy: All patients received intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) using a 6 MV linear accelerator. Target volumes included GTVp (Primary nasopharyngeal tumors); GTVn (Metastatic cervical lymph nodes); CTV1 (High-risk subclinical target volumes of the primary tumor); CTV2 (Low-risk region and prophylactic nodal drainage areas). Planning target volumes (PTVs) were generated by adding a 0.3–0.5 cm margin, which was reduced to 0.1 cm when adjacent to organs at risk (OARs). Prescribed radiation doses were as follows: PGTVp 70–72 Gy, PGTVn 66–70 Gy, PCTV1 60–64 Gy, and PCTV2 54–56 Gy, all delivered over 30–33 fractions. All plans were evaluated using DVH; OAR constraints were checked using the corresponding Dmax, D0.03 cc/D1cc, Dmean, and/or Vx metrics, and target-dose reporting followed ICRU 83 (reporting D98, D50, and D2 for the PTV and limiting excessive hot spots). All patients received intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), with a median prescribed dose to the primary tumor of 70.29 Gy (range, 69.96–72.0 Gy); all patients initiated and completed the full planned course of radiotherapy, and the median interval between completion of induction chemotherapy and initiation of radiotherapy was 21 days (range, 21–30 days).

Adjuvant therapy: Adjuvant therapy was administered to a subset of patients following definitive radiotherapy. The primary adjuvant regimens consisted of: (1) PD-1 inhibitor alone; (2) capecitabine chemotherapy (1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1–14); or (3) combination therapy with PD-1 inhibitors plus capecitabine (using the same capecitabine schedule). All regimens were administered in 3-week cycles for a maximum duration of one year.

Treatment Groups: Group A (Experimental), PD-1 inhibitor + induction chemotherapy + concurrent chemoradiotherapy (± adjuvant therapy); Group B (Control), Induction chemotherapy + concurrent chemoradiotherapy (± adjuvant therapy).

Efficacy evaluation and adverse events

Study endpoints

The primary outcome was the complete response rate (CRR) of locoregional lymph nodes following IC. Secondary outcomes included tumor response rates and survival metrics: objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), locoregional relapse-free survival (LRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and overall survival (OS).

Short-term response assessment

Tumor response evaluations were conducted using contrast-enhanced MRI of the nasopharynx and neck, plasma EBV DNA quantification, contrast-enhanced CT/MRI, bone scintigraphy, PET/CT scans, and nasopharyngoscopy. Assessments occurred after IC, at the end of radiotherapy, and at every 3-month follow-up. Treatment response was assessed according to RECIST v1.1 criteria. Response definitions were as follows: Complete Response (CR): Disappearance of all target lesions (including residual nodal disease < 1 cm), normalization of tumor markers, maintained for > 4 weeks; Partial Response (PR): ≥ 30% reduction in the sum of diameters of target lesions from baseline; Progressive Disease (PD): ≥20% increase in lesion size or appearance of new lesions; and Stable Disease (SD): Does not qualify as CR, PR, or PD. Response rates were defined as: Complete Response Rate (CRR): Proportion of patients achieving CR; Objective Response Rate (ORR): Proportion of patients achieving CR or PR; and Disease Control Rate (DCR): Proportion of patients achieving CR, PR, or SD.

Survival analyses

PFS was measured as the time from treatment initiation to documented progression or death. OS was measured as the time from treatment initiation to death or last follow-up. DMFS was measured as the time from treatment initiation to first distant metastasis or last follow-up. LRFS was measured as the time from treatment initiation to locoregional recurrence or last follow-up.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted within Group A (immunotherapy plus IC and CCRT), stratifying patients by sex, age, body mass index, WHO histological classification, number of immunotherapy cycles, radiotherapy dose and fractionation, baseline EBV DNA level, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte count, and pre-treatment LDH concentration to explore factors associated with achieving a CR to the combination regimen.

Treatment-related adverse events

Adverse events were recorded and graded according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0. For statistical comparisons, the most severe adverse event experienced by each patient during and after treatment was included.

Follow up

All included patients underwent a structured follow-up protocol: quarterly during the first two years, semiannually from years 2 to 5, and annually thereafter. The final follow-up was completed in January 2025. A multimodal follow-up system, including telephone calls, SMS, WeChat, written correspondence, and outpatient visits was used to collect data on symptom progression, treatment-related toxicities, and imaging/biomarker outcomes. Monitoring tools encompassed nasopharyngoscopy, complete blood counts, serum biochemistry, plasma EBV DNA quantification, lymphocyte subset analysis, contrast-enhanced CT/MRI, bone scintigraphy, or PET/CT scans. If the patient has undergone PET/CT scanning, contrast-enhanced CT and bone scintigraphy are no longer required.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of categorical variables (including baseline characteristics, adverse events, and tumor response rates) were performed using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests were used to compare time-to-event outcomes. Univariate prognostic factors were assessed, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were developed to estimate the risk of distant metastasis, locoregional failure, and death, using a backward stepwise elimination method (retention threshold: p < 0.05; elimination threshold: p > 0.10). All statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc.), with forest and box-line plots generated using R version 4.4.3. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. All clinical variables had a missing data rate of less than 5%. Missing continuous variables were imputed with the median, and categorical variables were imputed with the mode.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This retrospective analysis included a total of 217 NPC patients, with 104 allocated to Group A and 113 to Group B. The median age was 51 years (range: 19-77) in Group A and 53 years (range: 20-81) in Group B. The proportion of patients with EBV DNA copies greater than or equal to 400 was 66.3% in Group A and 70.0% in Group B. Histopathologically, the predominant subtype was undifferentiated non-keratinizing carcinoma (WHO type III). In Group A, 56.7% of patients had G2-rENE and 43.3% had G3-rENE; in Group B, the proportions were 57.5% and 42.5%, respectively. All patients had a primary tumor stage of III or IVa. The proportion of patients who completed three or more cycles of IC was 82.7% in Group A and 74.3% in Group B. Comparative analysis confirmed that demographic and clinical characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups, with no significant differences observed (Table 1).

Short-term efficacy evaluation following induction phase with subgroup analysis

After all IC cycles, the addition of anti-PD-1 therapy to IC significantly improved both the CRR (21.15% vs. 7.96%, P = 0.001) and ORR ( 97.12% vs. 88.5%, P = 0.001) compared to IC alone, significantly increasing the likelihood of achieving a complete response (odds ratio [OR] = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.141–0.738). For Cervical lymph node, further analysis revealed significantly higher CRR and ORR in Group A (CRR: 25%; ORR: 99.04%) compared to Group B (CRR: 11.5%; ORR: 92.04%) (P = 0.003 for CRR, P = 0.002 for ORR; Table 2).

Lymphocyte subset differences before and after IC + anti-PD-1 therapy

In Group A, peripheral blood lymphocyte subset analyses were performed for all patients at two predefined time points: (1) at baseline, prior to initiation of any anticancer treatment, and (2) after completion of the induction phase. The therapy resulted in a substantial 42.3% reduction in total lymphocyte count (P < 0.001). Post-treatment, CD4 + T cell percentages declined significantly (P = 0.012), whereas CD8 + T cell proportions increased (P = 0.003), leading to a marked decrease in the CD4+/CD8 + ratio (P = 0.001). Although the overall number of CD3 + T cells trended downward, the change did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.052). Natural killer (NK) cell counts remained largely unchanged. (Table 3; Fig. 2).

We further compared lymphocyte subset differences between patients achieving CR and non-CR. Both groups experienced a significant decline in total lymphocyte counts post-treatment—44.3% in the CR group and 43.0% in the non-CR group (P < 0.001 for both). CD4 + T cell depletion was more pronounced in the non-CR group than in the CR group (21.8% vs. 11.6%, P = 0.024). Both groups exhibited significant CD8 + T cell expansion, though no significant intergroup difference was observed. Correspondingly, the post-treatment CD4+/CD8 + ratio was significantly higher in the CR group compared to the non-CR group (1.28 ± 0.61 vs. 0.98 ± 0.52, P = 0.011). NK cell levels remained stable across time points in both groups. These findings imply that preservation of CD4 + T cell function and maintenance of a balanced CD4+/CD8 + ratio may be associated with better therapeutic outcomes (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Survival analysis and patterns of treatment failure

As of the data cutoff on December 31, 2024, the 2-year OS rate was 98.1% (95% CI: 95.6% – 100%) in Group A and 97.3% (95% CI: 94.3% – 100%) in Group B. The median OS was not reached in either group, and no statistically significant difference in OS was observed (χ² = 1.005, p = 0.32). In terms of PFS, Group A exhibited a significantly higher 2-year PFS rate at 93.2% (95% CI: 88.3% – 98.1%), compared to 81.0% (95% CI: 73.6% – 88.4%) in Group B for an absolute difference of 12.2% (95% CI: 3.6% – 20.8%, χ² = 9.076, P = 0.0026). The 2-year DMFS was also superior in Group A at 97.1% (95% CI: 94.0% – 100%), compared to 84.8% (95% CI: 78.1% – 91.5%) in Group B, reflecting a 12.3% absolute improvement (95% CI: 4.6% – 20.0%, χ² = 8.592, P = 0.0034). LRFS at 2 years was 96.1% (95% CI: 92.4% – 99.8%) in Group A and 92.0% (95% CI: 87.1% – 96.9%) in Group B, the difference was not statistically significant (χ² = 2.743, P = 0.098). Kaplan–Meier survival curves for OS, PFS, DMFS, and LRFS are presented in Fig. 4.

Over a median follow-up duration of 38 months (range: 11–59 months) from the initiation of treatment, a total of 8 deaths were documented. The median follow-up time was 36 months (range, 11–58) in Group A and 38 months (range, 11–59) in Group B. Group A reported 2 deaths—one from hepatic and retroperitoneal lymph node metastases and another due to a gastric second primary malignancy—whereas Group B reported 6 deaths, which included one case of retropharyngeal nodal recurrence, two from cervical lymph node recurrences, two from distant metastases, and one from non-cancer-related causes. Overall, 41 patients (18.89%) experienced treatment failure, with 10 cases in Group A and 31 in Group B (details on treatment failure for both groups are provided in Table 5). Among the failures, 14 cases (6.45%) were local recurrences—primarily involving extranasopharyngeal and cervical lymph nodes—while 34 cases (15.67%) developed distant metastases. The liver (n = 15), lungs (n = 9), and bones (n = 14) emerged as the most frequent sites of distant spread. The median time to distant metastasis was 36 months (range: 3–59 months), identifying distant metastasis as the dominant mode of failure.

Treatment-related adverse events

The majority of patients in both Group A and Group B experienced treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) of varying severity throughout the treatment course. Most TRAEs were classified as Grade 1–2, with Grade 3–4 events occurring less frequently. Importantly, no Grade 5 toxicities or treatment-related mortalities were reported in either group. As detailed in Table 6, adverse events with an incidence of ≥ 10% in either group included: nausea (100% vs. 99.1%), vomiting (100% vs. 100%), diarrhea (30.8% vs. 24.8%), constipation (11.5% vs. 17.7%), appetite loss (97.1% vs. 98.2%), leukopenia (67.3% vs. 57.5%), neutropenia (79.9% vs. 81.4%), thrombocytopenia (47.1% vs. 48.7%), anemia (66.3% vs. 67.2%), hepatic impairment (37.5% vs. 41.7%), renal dysfunction (18.3% vs. 10.6%), hypothyroidism (37.5% vs. 29.2%), treatment-related pneumonitis (18.3% vs. 14.2%), radiation-induced pharyngitis (100% vs. 100%), radiation dermatitis (80.8% vs. 86.8%), xerostomia (100% vs. 97.4%), peripheral neuropathy (31.7% vs. 31.8%), fatigue (93.3% vs. 90.2%), and dysphagia (91.3% vs. 83.2%). Among these, certain toxicities—including Grade1-2 renal impairment and xerostomia, and Grade3-4 dysphagia—were more prevalent in the combination therapy group, suggesting that the addition of PD-1 inhibitors may enhance the risk of specific systemic and mucosal side effects. Furthermore, A particular AE, capillary endothelial proliferation, was observed exclusively in the combination group, affecting four patients—three of whom experienced Grade 3 severity. This event was not reported in the IC-only group, suggesting it may be a unique immune-related side effect associated with PD-1 blockade. In Group A, immune-mediated pneumonitis occurred in 4 patients (3.8%); all cases were grade 1–2 (grade 1, 1.9%; grade 2, 1.9%). The incidence of radiation pneumonitis was 14.5%, and all cases were grade 1. In Group B, pneumonitis events were radiation-induced, including 2 grade 2 cases (1.8%) and 14 grade 1 cases (12.4%).

Discussion

Previous clinical evidence underscores the critical importance of treatment timing in leveraging the immunologic benefits of PD-1 inhibitors for malignancies [16, 20–22]. Some argue that in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and NPC, requiring large-volume irradiation of lymphatic drainage areas during radiotherapy, radiation may directly eliminate memory lymphocytes and alter the tumor microenvironment, thereby diminishing immunotherapy’s efficacy. Indeed, several representative clinical studies investigating immunotherapy during CCRT for HNSCC have failed [23–25]. Consequently, initiating PD-1 blockade prior to radiotherapy has been proposed as a strategy to better preserve and potentiate antitumor immunity [23]. The advantage of immunoinduction therapy lies in its ability to reduce tumor burden, creating more favorable conditions for subsequent curative-intent surgery or radiotherapy. Moreover, activated CD8+ T-cells target tumor cells, eliminate micrometastases, and extend survival [26]. This approach was notably validated in the resectable lung cancer and triple-negative breast cancer, where adding immunotherapy to IC resulted in significantly longer EFS and a higher pathological CR rate compared to chemotherapy alone [27, 28]. Furthermore, Phase II clinical studies in HNSCC have also demonstrated that incorporating immunotherapy during the induction phase can achieve higher tumor response rates [29, 30]. Concurrently, the outcomes of the KEYNOTE-689 [31] and GORTEC-2018-01 trials [32] are reshaping the treatment paradigm for locally advanced HNSCC by demonstrating the expanding role of immunotherapy and informing new strategies for optimizing the timing of its integration. In particular, the neoadjuvant component of the KEYNOTE-689 regimen—its impact on pathological tumor response and its potential to prime the tumor microenvironment—has attracted considerable interest among researchers.

Our study data revealed a CRR of 21.15% in the combination group—almost three times higher than that seen with IC alone (p = 0.001). The benefit was also especially pronounced in terms of lymph node regression rates with G2/3 ENE (26.0% vs. 11.5%, p = 0.003). Given the established association between high-grade ENE and increased metastatic potential, early immune intervention may improve locoregional control and reduce metastasis through enhanced immune activation [33]. Survival analysis from our study further demonstrated the therapeutic advantage of the combined regimen, with 2-year PFS and DMFS rates reaching 93.2% and 97.1%, respectively—representing absolute improvements of 12.2% and 12.3% over the IC-only group. These outcomes suggest that immunotherapy augments systemic disease control possibly by targeting subclinical micrometastases, and complementing the local efficacy of chemoradiation. Real-world data also support this notion; Yu et al. demonstrated superior 1-year DMFS in NPC patients receiving IC plus PD-1 blockade (96.9% vs. 89.6%, P = 0.03) [34]. Mechanistically, Chen et al. reported that PD-1 inhibition enhances effector T-cell function and mitigates immune exhaustion, thereby facilitating the clearance of disseminated tumor cells [35]. Collectively, these findings endorse the integration of immunotherapy into IC regimens as a means to optimize outcomes in patients predisposed to distant relapse.

Subgroup analysis from our study revealed that patients with baseline EBV DNA < 400 IU/mL, LDH levels < 2×ULN, T3–4/N2-3 classification, stage IVa disease, or G3-rENE derived a significantly higher CRR from the combination regimen. These results highlight the heterogeneous nature of immune responsiveness in NPC. Low EBV DNA levels may signify reduced tumor burden and immune escape, thereby enabling more robust immune-mediated tumor elimination [36]. Elevated LDH, a surrogate for tumor proliferation and hypoxia, is often associated with resistance to immunotherapy [37]. Notably, patients with more advanced T or N staging, typically associated with a greater risk of metastasis, achieved better distant control with the addition of PD-1 blockade. This supports the idea that high-risk populations may gain greater benefit from immunotherapy’s systemic effects, as also observed by Wu et al., who reported improved outcomes in IVA NPC with immuno-chemotherapeutic combinations [38]. Taken together, the present results underscore the necessity for refined patient stratification strategies based on clinical staging and molecular biomarkers. Precision treatment planning incorporating tumor extent, EBV DNA levels, metabolic indicators like LDH, and immune microenvironment characteristics may facilitate the delivery of personalized, effective care in NPC.

Furthermore, we observed a significant reduction in total peripheral blood lymphocyte counts following treatment with IC plus PD-1 inhibitors. Among the lymphocyte populations, CD4⁺ T cells exhibited a greater decline than CD8⁺ T cells, leading to a marked decrease in the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio. This disproportionate loss of CD4⁺ T cells may reflect an impaired immunoregulatory capacity, potentially impacting the host’s ability to mount a balanced immune response. Further stratification of patients based on treatment response revealed that individuals who achieved a CR retained higher post-treatment CD4⁺ T cell levels and exhibited a significantly greater CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio compared to those in the non-CR subgroup. These data suggest that preservation of CD4⁺ T cell populations during treatment may be associated with enhanced immunotherapeutic efficacy and improved treatment durability. Interestingly, we detected a modest increase in CD8⁺ T cell counts following combination therapy. This finding raises the possibility that CD8⁺ T cells may be selectively activated or preferentially expand in response to PD-1 inhibition. Previous studies have indicated that CD8⁺ T cell subsets possess superior proliferative potential and functional resilience during the early immune recovery phase post-chemotherapy [39]. These characteristics may contribute to their role as potential biomarkers of predictive treatment response.

Although patients receiving the combined IC and PD-1 inhibitor regimen experienced a higher overall incidence of TRAEs, most toxicities were mild to moderate (Grade 1–2) and were effectively managed with supportive interventions. Common irAEs observed in this cohort included hypothyroidism, treatment-related pneumonitis, and renal dysfunction, aligning with the safety profiles reported in prior immunotherapy trials for NPC [33–34, 38]. Additionally, this study observed a significantly higher incidence of grade 3–4 dysphagia in the combination therapy group compared to the control group (6.7% vs. 0%, P = 0.003). This phenomenon may involve multiple mechanisms. Primarily, the synergistic effect of PD-1 inhibitor with chemoradiotherapy may exacerbate mucosal inflammation in the pharyngeal region, leading to dysphagia [40]. Secondly, immune checkpoint inhibitors may induce pharyngeal constrictor weakness, with clinical manifestations resembling immune-mediated myositis. However, given the limited sample size and relatively short follow-up duration of the current study, these findings require further validation. Prospective studies with expanded cohorts, objective assessments such as videofluoroscopic swallowing studies, and extended follow-up are warranted to confirm the clinical significance and monitor for delayed toxicities. For renal injury, the higher severity in the immunotherapy group may be related to PD-1 inhibitor–associated nephrotoxicity (e.g., creatinine elevation/immune-related renal injury), consistent with findings from the DIPPER study, in which creatinine above the upper limit of normal was more frequent in the immunotherapy group than in the non-immunotherapy group (12.2% vs. 4.1%) [21]. Notably, there were no treatment-related fatalities across the study population, and the overall safety profile were acceptable with appropriate monitoring and management strategies. These findings affirm the tolerability and safety of incorporating PD-1 blockade into induction regimens for NPC patients. For patients with poorer ECOG status or significant comorbidities, careful benefit-risk assessment and enhanced toxicity monitoring and management are required.

This study is subject to several limitations, primarily due to its retrospective design. Although baseline characteristics were well-balanced between treatment groups, the possibility of residual confounding and selection bias cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the exclusive enrollment of ECOG 0–1 patients, while ensuring tolerance for intensive therapy, limits generalizability to frailer populations. The absence of PD-L1 expression data represents another constraint, as it precludes assessment of its potential predictive value for treatment response. Moreover, because induction immunochemotherapy was not a clinical standard at the time of treatment, multiple PD-1 inhibitors were used; although this increased the sample size, it introduced therapeutic heterogeneity. Additionally, the OS data are currently immature, owing to a relatively short follow-up duration. Extended monitoring is necessary to determine whether the observed improvements in PFS and DMSF ultimately confer a long-term survival benefit. To strengthen the clinical relevance of these findings, prospective randomized trials are warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, this real-world study supports the integration of PD-1 blockade into IC regimens for patients with NPC and G2/3-rENE. The addition of immunotherapy significantly improved early tumor response and disease control, and despite the intensified therapy, the overall safety profile remained acceptable. These findings underscore the clinical value of early immunotherapeutic intervention in high-risk NPC and provide a foundation for future precision medicine strategies aimed at improving outcomes in this challenging patient population.
