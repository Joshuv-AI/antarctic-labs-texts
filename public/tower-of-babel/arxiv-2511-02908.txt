Abstract
Direct observations of Lyman continuum (LyC) radiation from galaxies during the Epoch of Reionization (EoR) are impeded by absorption in the intergalactic medium, requiring indirect methods to infer the escape fraction of ionizing photons ( f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} ). One approach is to develop and validate such methods on local analogues of the high-redshift galaxies with directly detected LyC leakage. In this study, we constrain f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} using a Bayesian spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting framework built on Prospector , which incorporates a non-parametric star-formation history, a flexible dust attenuation curve, self-consistent nebular emission, and fiber aperture-loss corrections. Our methodology jointly fits broadband photometry and emission line fluxes. We apply six models to the Low-redshift LyC Survey (LzLCS), a sample of local galaxies with physical properties comparable to EoR galaxies, and evaluate them based on their ability to recover the observed LyC flux and their relative Bayesian evidence. The best-performing model is further assessed through a parameter recovery test, demonstrating that f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} can be recovered within uncertainties. Building on these results, we present updated f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} estimates for the LzLCS sample, with a median of 0.3%, corresponding to very low leakage, and values reaching as high as 70%, with six of 64 galaxies having a cosmologically relevant f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} ( > 5 % >5\% ). Additionally, we present a revised UV β \beta -slope vs log 10 ⁡ ( f esc LyC ) \log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}) relation, derived using symbolic regression with PySR trained on a synthetic dataset generated from our best-performing model: log 10 ⁡ ( f esc LyC ) = ( − 3.59 ​ β − 9.45 ) ± 0.30 \log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}})=(-3.59\beta-9.45)\,\pm\,0.30 . The relation successfully reproduces the f esc LyC f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}} obtained from full SED fitting of the LzLCS sample within uncertainties.



1
Introduction

The Epoch of Reionization (EoR) marks the Universe’s last major hydrogen phase transition, during which the intergalactic medium (IGM) was transformed from neutral to ionized. Observations constrain the end of this process to
z\approx 5.7

(
Bosman et al. 2022
;
Keating et al. 2020
)
, but its exact time evolution and topology remains uncertain. The progression of reionization depends on three key quantities: the number density of ionizing sources, their efficiency in producing hydrogen-ionizing photons with
E>13.6~\mathrm{eV}
(
\xi_{\mathrm{ion}}
), and the fraction of these photons that escape (
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
) out of the system into the IGM. Because these quantities depend on galaxy mass, metallicity, and redshift, different assumptions lead to very different reionization histories. For instance, should reionization be driven by numerous faint sources (
M_{\mathrm{UV}}>-17
AB mag) with low
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, it would have begun earlier and proceeded gradually. Conversely, if dominated by rarer, luminous sources with high
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, reionization would have started later and progressed more rapidly. Constraining the population distributions of these parameters is therefore important for understanding the evolution of the EoR.

The production rate of ionizing photons of individual galaxies can be estimated with stellar population synthesis models
(
Kennicutt 1998
;
Simmonds et al. 2023
;
Simmonds et al. 2024
;
Pahl et al. 2025
)
, while recent
James Webb Space Telescope
(JWST) results provide measurements of the number density of sources at
z>9

(
Harikane et al. 2025
;
Whitler et al. 2025
)
. In contrast, constraining
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
remains far more difficult, owing to the complex and inhomogeneous structure of the inter stellar medium (ISM)
(
Gazagnes et al. 2018
)
. In addition to the complex ISM structures that hinder estimations of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, the intervening neutral hydrogen makes LyC detection essentially impossible at
z>4

(
Inoue et al. 2014
)
. As a result, studies must rely on indirect tracers.

Common diagnostics include the UV continuum slope (
\beta
)
(
Chisholm et al. 2022
)
(defined as
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta}
;
Calzetti et al. 1994
), the H
\beta
equivalent width
(
Pahl et al. 2020
)
, and emission line ratios such as [O
iii
]/[O
ii
] (
O_{32}
)
(
Izotov et al. 2018
)
. Additional indicators are Ly
\alpha
peak separation
(
Verhamme et al. 2015
;
Izotov et al. 2020
)
, Ly
\alpha
equivalent width
(
Pahl et al. 2020
;
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2022
;
Gazagnes et al. 2020
)
, and Mg
ii
doublet ratios
(
Chisholm et al. 2020
;
Katz et al. 2022
)
. Other proposed tracers include strong C
iv
emission
(
Schaerer et al. 2022
;
Saxena et al. 2022
)
, S
ii
deficiency
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, and combined measurements of UV absorption lines with dust attenuation
(
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2022
)
, but attempts to connect metallic absorption lines to
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
have shown promise mainly in extreme cases (e.g.,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}=0
or
>30\%
)
(
Mauerhofer et al. 2021
)
. Furthermore, survival analysis methods integrate multiple observational indicators
(
Jaskot et al. 2024
)
, while spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting offers a self-consistent approach by incorporating multi-wavelength photometry and spectroscopy. A comprehensive overview of current methods is provided in
Jaskot 2025
.

To calibrate and test such tracers, we turn to local analogues of EoR galaxies. Compact (
r_{50,~\mathrm{NUV}}\leq 1~\mathrm{kpc}
), star-forming (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}\geq 0.1M_{\odot}~\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\mathrm{kpc}^{-2}
), low-metallicity systems are the most widely used candidates
(
Flury et al. 2022b
)
, though their comparability to high-redshift populations remains debated. Discrepancies between high- and low-redshift measurements of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
may arise from either a redshift evolution in the underlying relations or from differences in the galaxy populations probed at different epochs
(
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2023
;
Pahl et al. 2024
)
. For example,
Citro et al. 2025
report non-detections of LyC in galaxies at
z\approx 2.3
that exhibit strong Ly
\alpha
emission and very blue UV continuum slopes (
\beta\sim-2.4
). Nevertheless, local analogues can provide valuable laboratories for probing the mechanisms that regulate LyC escape.
Spatially resolved observations of local galaxies, such as
Haro 11
at
z=0.02

(
Komarova et al. 2024
)
, reveal that the regions dominating the observed LyC flux are not necessarily the same as those driving the emission of indirect tracers like [O
iii
] or H
\alpha

(
Östlin et al. 2021
)
. This implies that the spatial origin of leaking LyC does not necessarily coincide with the regions dominating nebular tracers such as [O III] or H
\alpha
, offering a natural explanation for the large scatter observed in correlations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and indirect indicators.
It is important to note that even for low-redshift galaxies, there is no such thing as a directly observed
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. Since
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
is defined as the ratio of escaped to intrinsically produced LyC photons, assumptions about the intrinsic production are always required. This introduces strong model dependencies and makes it difficult to compare
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
across different analyses. Moreover, line-of-sight effects can further complicate the picture, as leakage along one direction may not reflect the global escape fraction of the galaxy.

An alternative approach to study
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
is through numerical simulations, but translating their results into observationally testable predictions is challenging. Numerous radiation hydrodynamics simulations show that the escape of H-ionizing photons highly depends on the properties of the host galaxy and is highly anisotropic
(
Paardekooper et al. 2015
;
Barrow et al. 2020
;
Rosdahl et al. 2022
;
Smith et al. 2022
)
. Line-of-sight
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
are not Gaussian-distributed around the global escape fraction. Instead, galaxies often appear non-leaking along most lines of sight, even if their integrated escape fraction is nonzero
(
Mauerhofer et al. 2021
)
.
The complex, time-dependent relation of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
seems to be closely connected to the presence of both radiative and mechanical stellar feedback
(
Kimm & Cen 2014
;
Trebitsch et al. 2017
)
.
An additional challenge of approaching
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
with simulations lies in the range of resolution that is necessary to simulate star-forming clouds in detail.
Also, determining
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
through forward modelling of galaxy structures and ionizing photon transport with numerical simulations is highly dependent on assumptions about galaxy formation, feedback prescriptions, the treatment of the multiphase ISM, and dust attenuation. Predictions from such simulations generally indicate that a global average escape fraction of
5
–
20\%
is required to reproduce the timeline of cosmic reionization
(
Trebitsch et al. 2022
)
. When obscured star formation is accounted for, the predicted escape fractions are lower, with values of
5
–
10\%
found in
Thesan

(
Yeh et al. 2023
)
and
Obelisk

(
Trebitsch et al. 2021
)
, and below
5\%
in
Sphinx

(
Rosdahl et al. 2022
)
. The
Sphinx
simulations also show that escape fractions fluctuate strongly within individual galaxies on Myr timescales, regulated by the interplay of supernovae and radiative feedback. Beyond global escape fractions, radiative transfer simulations of the ISM have highlighted that line ratios, diffuse ionized gas, and related diagnostics provide powerful probes of ISM physics and feedback processes, and help identify which stellar populations dominate the ionization budget
(
Tacchella et al. 2022
;
Smith et al. 2022
;
McClymont et al. 2024
)
.

In this paper, we use SED modelling to re-derive the stellar population properties of the Low-z LyC Survey
(
Flury et al. 2022a
)
galaxies, with a particular focus on their
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. We (a) build a physically flexible SED model that explicitly includes birth-cloud vs diffuse dust and a parameter for runaway ionizing photons, (b) test multiple prior and dust prescriptions, and (c) assess their reliability with parameter recovery tests.

A concise overview of the dataset is presented in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we describe the set of models adopted for fitting each galaxy and quantify the impact of different priors and model assumptions. Section
4
presents our re-derived estimates of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and other galaxy properties, which we compare to recent high-redshift observations from JWST to assess whether the LzLCS galaxies provide suitable local analogues of EoR systems. In Section
5
, we revisit correlations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and the newly inferred stellar properties by employing symbolic regression to derive an analytic relation linking
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
to directly observable quantities. Finally, we discuss the challenges of our analysis and future work in Section
6
and summarize our findings in Section
7
.

Throughout this work, we assume a
Planck
2018 flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
H_{0}=67.36\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
,
\Omega_{m}=0.3153
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.6847

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. Reported parameter values correspond to the posterior median and 16th–84th percentile range. For derived quantities (i.e., those computed from fitted parameters), we evaluate them across the full posterior distribution and quote the median and percentiles of the resulting distribution.

2
Data

The Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS) on the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) enables the detection of escaping LyC photons from galaxies at
z\sim 0.3
, leading to the assembly of the Low-Redshift Lyman Continuum Survey (LzLCS; PI: Jaskot, HST Project ID: 15626,
Flury et al. 2022a
). The galaxies in this sample meet several criteria that suggest they are suitable analogs for high-redshift Reionization-era galaxies: they are metal-poor (
12+\log_{10}(O/H)\sim 8.1
), actively star-forming and compact (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}>0.1~M_{\odot}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\mathrm{kpc}^{-2}
). We discuss how their properties are in line with high-redshift galaxies in Section
4
. In this work, we reanalyse the LzLCS using a more flexible modelling framework than the original study
(
Flury et al. 2022a
)
, with the aim of deriving a consistent set of galaxy properties. In contrast to the initial analysis, our Bayesian fitting approach simultaneously incorporates both photometry and emission-line measurements, allowing for a more comprehensive and self-consistent characterization of each galaxy.

2.1
Sample Definition and Selection Criteria

The galaxy sample was selected from optical spectroscopy in the 16th Data Release of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS;
Ahumada et al. 2020
) and far-ultraviolet (FUV) photometry from GALEX
(
Martin 2005
)
. The redshift range was chosen such that the Lyman continuum (LyC) falls within the more sensitive region of the
HST
/COS detector. Details of the data reduction are provided in
Flury et al. 2022a
. We acknowledge that the use of SDSS fibre spectroscopy and photometry, GALEX imaging, and
HST
/COS LyC spectroscopy introduces potential aperture mismatches due to differing spatial coverage. We account for these fibre / aperture mismatches in the modelling via a free photometry–spectroscopy scaling parameter (
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
; Section
3
).

The LzLCS was explicitly designed to test three proposed diagnostics of LyC escape:

•

Star formation rate surface density:

\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}>0.1\,M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-2}
, characteristic of compact starbursts where feedback may facilitate LyC leakage
(
Heckman et al. 2001
;
Clarke & Oey 2002
)
.

•

Ionization state:

O32=[{\rm O\,III}]\,\lambda 5007/[{\rm O\,II}]\,\lambda 3727\geq 3
, indicative of hard ionizing spectra and proposed as an indirect tracer of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}

(
Izotov et al. 2018
)
.

•

UV continuum slope:

\beta<-2
, derived from GALEX FUV–NUV colours, consistent with young, dust-poor stellar populations
(
Wilkins et al. 2012
;
Wilkins et al. 2013
;
Calabrò et al. 2021
)
.

While these criteria target galaxies with conditions thought to favor LyC escape, they also introduce a selection bias. Moreover, galaxies with similar
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
,
O32
, and
\beta
can still exhibit a wide range of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
values
(
Izotov et al. 2018
)
, highlighting the limitations of relying on these diagnostics alone.

2.3
Measuring the Lyman Continuum

In the LzLCS,
Flury et al. 2022b
excluded all wavelengths longward of 1180 Å (observed frame) to remove contamination from geocoronal Ly
\alpha

\lambda

= 840 and

= 890, depending on the galaxy. Throughout this paper, we denote any measurement and SED-inferred values restricted to this wavelength interval with the suffix “_restricted”.

Flury et al. 2022b

f_{\mathrm{esc,~UV}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
), the H
\beta

F_{900}^{\mathrm{rest}}/F_{1100}^{\mathrm{rest}}
.While this flux ratio can serve as a proxy for f
{}_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}

(
Wang et al. 2019
)
, other studies have argued that this wavelength range is unsuitable for estimating

(
Simmonds et al. 2024

Izotov et al. 2025

Inferring the Escape Fraction Indirectly

To infer

without access to the ionizing spectrum, we model the LzLCS data with the Bayesian SED fitting code
Prospector

f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}

f_{\mathrm{run}}^{\mathrm{OB}}
), and test the inclusion of a parameter accounting for fibre-loss corrections. The different models are summarized in Tab.
2
. A parameter recovery test validating the framework is provided in Appendix
A
.

We adopt a two-component dust attenuation model following

<

-1

The second component represents a diffuse dust screen that attenuates all stellar and nebular emission across the galaxy. Its attenuation curve follows the prescription of
Kriek & Conroy 2013
, where the slope of the attenuation law is tied to the UV bump strength. The parameter
dust_index
is modelled as an offset relative to the
Calzetti et al. 1994

[-1,4]
. For the diffuse optical depth (
\tau_{2}

0.0

4.0

\mu=0.3

d2

\tau_{1}/\tau_{2}
), assuming a clipped normal prior centered at 1.0.

In a subset of models (

>10

Prospector

Byler et al. 2017

Cloudy

(
Ferland et al. 2013

gas_logu

[-4,-1]

Nebular emission is scaled to the ionizing photon output predicted at each single stellar population (SSP) age for the specified star formation history (SFH). The grid is truncated at
\log U=-1

The SFH is modelled non-parametrically across eight lookback-time bins. The first two are fixed at 5 and 10 Myr, while the remaining six are logarithmically spaced back to

3.1
Uniform Prior Model

The most straightforward assumption is to adopt a uniform prior for
f_{\mathrm{run}}^{\mathrm{OB}}
(model
d2uni
). However, it is important to note that a uniform prior does not imply a lack of informative assumptions. In practice, the prior on the escape fraction is implicitly shaped by the choice of priors on the dust attenuation parameters,
\tau_{2}
. We repeat the setup of
d2uni
including the three component dust model (’
d3uni
’).

3.2
NoScale Model

The NoScale model (
d2uniNoScale
) is identical to the
d2uni
setup, except that it omits the parameter
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
. By removing this parameter,
d2uniNoScale
isolates the influence of fibre-loss corrections on the inferred value of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. We find that excluding
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
leads to slightly poorer fits compared to
d2uni
, with the total
\chi^{2}
increasing on average by a factor of 1.03, while the inferred
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
remains unaffected.

When focusing specifically on the
\chi^{2}
contribution from the emission lines, the
d2uniNoScale
model yields an average value 1.3 times higher than that of the
d2uni
model. Based on this degradation in the emission line fit quality, we conclude that including
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
as a free parameter is necessary and therefore retain it in our default model setup.

(model
d2log20
). This prior is motivated by the distribution of escape fractions inferred from the LzLCS sample and allows us to evaluate the sensitivity of our results to prior assumptions that favor lower escape fractions.

We then extend this setup by incorporating the three-component dust model, resulting in the
d3log20
configuration. Finally, we include a variant,
d3log20Nod2
, which is identical to
d3log20
but omits the second dust component (
dust2
). This configuration approximates a picket-fence scenario, where radiation from young stars is either fully attenuated by the birth-cloud dust or escapes without additional diffuse attenuation.

3.4
Performance Across Models

All models successfully reproduce both photometry and emission lines, achieving a median photometric recovery of
\chi^{2}\approx 3.5
for the galaxy sample. A comparison of all model performances is shown in Figure
11
in Appendix
A
, and an example fit to a galaxy’s photometry is provided in Figure
13
, also in the Appendix.
The best model to fit each galaxy is selected using a hybrid criterion: we first compare models using the Bayes factor; when the Bayes factor is non-decisive (
<5
), we select the model that best reproduces the LyC flux measurements. This strategy is motivated by the fact that the LyC flux was not used in the fit itself and therefore provides an external consistency check. However, this selection method has inherent limitations: In particular, the measured LyC flux is inherently line-of-sight dependent, whereas our SED model returns a global (angle-averaged) prediction. Discrepancies between the measured and predicted fluxes do not necessarily indicate a poor fit.
The remainder of our analysis is based on the best fitting model for each galaxy. A comparison between the observed
F_{\mathrm{LyC}}
and the prediction from our models can be found in Fig.
2
. With an outlier fraction (defined as a
3\sigma
difference between the predicted and measured value) of 25% (16/64) for the entire sample and 20% (7/34) when considering only detected LyC, we are in good agreement with the observations.

Depending on our hybrid
\chi^{2}
–Bayes factor criterion, we find that the
d2log20
model provides the most consistent overall performance across the sample. To assess its reliability, we performed a parameter recovery test, described in detail in Appendix
A
. This test shows that the model can recover
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
within the reported uncertainties across most of parameter space. A key limitation arises in cases with very low escape fractions (
\log f_{\mathrm{esc}}<-2.5
) in dust-free systems, where
Prospector
overpredicts
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. When
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
is low and dust is absent, nearly all ionizing photons are absorbed by the nebular gas, leading to very high ionization states.
However, the nebular emission is modeled using a pre-computed grid that imposes an upper limit on the ionization parameter and is further constrained by a prior that disfavors extreme ionization states. As a result, the model cannot reproduce the observed emission line strengths under the correct physical conditions and instead compensates by overestimating the escape fraction.

Nevertheless, as long as low escape fractions are not driven solely by absorption in neutral gas, our framework can robustly distinguish between galaxies with
\log f_{\mathrm{esc}}\lesssim-2
and those with
\log f_{\mathrm{esc}}\gtrsim-0.5
. Finer distinctions are possible, but the corresponding posterior distributions begin to overlap.

Parameter estimates across our models are generally consistent, agreeing within
2\sigma
for stellar mass, gas-phase metallicity, and, most importantly,
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. In Fig.
14
in the Appendix we show a comparison of the results by plotting the parameters against the results from our best performing model
d2log20
. The notable exception of the overall agreement is the
d3log20nod2
model, which systematically yields higher
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, with median values offset by nearly 2 dex compared to all other models. Unlike the other setups,
d3log20nod2
excludes a diffuse dust component while retaining separate dust screens for young and old stellar populations. This configuration effectively mimics a “picket-fence” scenario, in which LyC photons escape through low-density channels in the ISM. Although the model reproduces the photometry and emission lines with comparable statistical accuracy to the other models, it consistently overpredicts the observed LyC flux.

f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}

4.1
), gas-phase enrichment (Section
4.2
), and the size–mass relation (Section
4.3
).

Having established our modelling framework and validated its robustness in the previous subsection, we now present the key results obtained for individual galaxies. In Figure
3
, we compare our inferred
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
values to the UV-based estimates of
Flury et al. 2022a
. Overall, our analysis yields systematically lower escape fractions, and we find a mild anti-correlation for galaxies with
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc,~UV}}^{\mathrm{LyC}})>-2
. When accounting for the ionization parameter (
\log(U)
), we find that in the
Flury et al. 2022a
analysis, galaxies with the highest
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
also have the highest
\log(U)
, producing a correlation (
R\approx 0.45
). This likely arises because their method does not fit for the
\log(U)

f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. In our framework,
\log(U)
is included as a free parameter, breaking this degeneracy. As a result, we recover
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
that are not linearly coupled to the ionization state (
R\approx 0.0
) unlike the UV-based estimates.

The
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
results are summarized in Tab.
3
. For each galaxy, we report the value from its individual best-fitting model, from the globally preferred model (
d2log20
), and from fits restricted to the parameter range used by
Flury et al. 2022a
, based on their LyC measurements avoiding contamination. Overall, most models yield consistent
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. The only exceptions are the three most extreme cases, where
d2log20
predicts values up to two orders of magnitude lower. Comparing
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
to the restricted
f_{\mathrm{esc,res}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, we find that the inferred
f_{\mathrm{esc,res}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
are on average 0.08 dex lower when using the restricted range, thus smaller than the uncertainties connected to our estimations of significant
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
.

We highlight the case of J164849+495751, a system in our sample with a non-zero
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
in both analyses:
12^{+19}_{-9}\%
from our modelling and
3^{+5}_{-1}\%
from
Flury et al. 2022a
. Both results are consistent within uncertainties. Our fit reproduces the measured
F_{\mathrm{LyC}}
, strengthening confidence that the true line-of-sight escape fraction lies within our estimated range. This galaxy is nearly dust-free, with a diffuse optical depth of
\tau_{2}=0.01^{+0.02}_{-0.01}
and a comparable value for the birth-cloud component (
\tau_{1}/\tau_{2}\approx 0.9
). Its specific star formation rate is typical for the LzLCS, with
\mathrm{sSFR}\sim 4\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
. Figure
4
presents a subset of the posterior distributions for key parameters of J164849+495751. The full set of posteriors is provided in Appendix
15
. When examining the posterior distributions for this galaxy, we recover the expected degeneracies between the SFH parameters and the stellar mass, as well as between stellar metallicity, stellar age, and dust attenuation. We also identify a mild degeneracy between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
, accounting for a mismatch between the photometric and spectroscopic flux calibration. Physically, a poor constraint on
f_{\mathrm{scale}}
could lead to a misattribution of escaping ionizing photons to fibre losses or vice versa. However, the degeneracy is sufficiently weak that it does not significantly impact our inference of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
beyond what is already captured by the posterior uncertainty.
Interestingly, for galaxies with low inferred escape fractions (
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}})<-3

f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and stellar metallicity (see example in Appendix
4
). Specifically, lower metallicity corresponds to lower escape fractions. Lower-metallicity stellar populations produce a higher intrinsic ionizing photon output. Therefore, if the observed spectrum does not indicate a correspondingly high LyC escape, the model compensates by inferring a lower
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. Given that the escape fractions in this regime are already close to zero, the degeneracy has a negligible impact on our conclusions.

4.2
Gas-Phase Enrichment

We find that the gas-phase metallicities in our sample are systematically higher than the stellar metallicities, with an average offset of 0.67 dex. The stellar metallicities are subject to considerable uncertainty, primarily due to the limited constraints from absorption features in our data. Furthermore, the two metallicity indicators trace different chemical elements: gas-phase metallicities primarily reflect the abundance of
\alpha
-elements such as oxygen, while stellar metallicities are sensitive to a combination of
\alpha
- and iron-peak elements. The relative contribution of these elements depends on the wavelength regime; stellar metallicities derived from optical spectra are sensitive to both
\alpha
- and Fe-group elements, whereas UV absorption features tend to be more strongly dominated by iron. As a result, gas-phase metallicities are expected to exceed stellar metallicities by a factor of
\sim
2–5
(
Strom et al. 2022
;
Arellano-Córdova et al. 2022
)
.

In Figure

, we place our measurements in the context of the mass-metallicity relation (MZR) across cosmic time and compare them to estimates from
Flury et al. 2022a
. Our results show good agreement with their electron-temperature-based metallicities derived from the [O

]
\lambda\lambda 4363,4959,5007

z\sim 0.1
relation from SDSS
(
Curti et al. 2020
)

-0.28
dex at
\log(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})<9

-0.38
dex at
9<\log(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})<10
, and
-0.40
dex at
\log(M_{\ast}/M_{\odot})>10
. In contrast, the agreement with intermediate-redshift galaxies (
z\sim 3.3
;
Sanders et al. 2021
) is significantly better, with much smaller offsets of
+0.10
,
-0.02

-0.13

The comparison with JWST observations at

Symbolic regression is a supervised learning technique that searches the space of analytical expressions to identify mathematical formulas that best describe a dataset. Unlike traditional black-box regression models, SR produces closed-form expressions that are both interpretable and physically transparent. We apply SR with the Python–Julia package

5.2
The Regression Model

During training, we allow the symbolic regression model to use standard binary operators (
+
,
-
,
\times
,
\div
) as well as unary functions including
exp
,
log
, and
abs
. The model is trained by minimizing an element-wise loss function defined as the mean squared error:
L(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}
. We set a maximum expression size (
maxsize
) of 20 to balance model complexity and interpretability.

To evaluate model performance, we compare two outputs from the regression process: the overall best model (
best
), which optimally balances accuracy and simplicity, and the most accurate model (
accuracy
), which prioritizes predictive performance.

We experiment with various training set configurations: Specifically, we aim to recover escape fractions as accurately as possible in the observable range, while recognizing that extreme precision is not required for very low values (e.g., distinguishing between
10^{-6}
and
10^{-20}
). To implement this, we test two strategies: (1) excluding data points with
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}})<-5
during training, and evaluating model performance across the full dataset; and (2) setting all escape fractions below the threshold to the threshold value, while retaining their associated parameters. We find that the most effective approach is to train the model on the full dataset without any thresholding, enabling it to learn a more general representation of the underlying physics.

5.3
Regression Results

Even when supplied with a broader set of input variables, symbolic regression consistently identifies a linear relationship model between
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})
and the UV
\beta
slope when balancing model complexity and loss minimization. This motivates us to perform a weighted linear regression on the full set of 4.95 million synthetic samples. Both approaches yield consistent results. We obtain:

\displaystyle\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})=-3.595\,\beta-9.493.

(1)

To estimate the uncertainty in this relation, we perform a Bayesian linear regression using the following model:

\displaystyle\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})=-a\cdot\beta-b+\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(2)

where
a
and
b
are the slope and intercept, respectively, and
\epsilon
represents the scatter around the regression line, modelled as Gaussian scatter with standard deviation
\sigma
. This term captures the expected deviation between predicted and observed escape fractions. The inferred relation is:

\displaystyle\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})=(-3.59\cdot\beta-9.45)\,\pm\,0.30.

(3)

The predicted
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})
has a 68% credible interval of approximately
\pm 0.30
dex, reflecting the intrinsic scatter of the model.

We then apply our derived formula to predict the escape fractions for the LzLCS sample. A comparison between the escape fractions obtained from full SED fitting with
Prospector
and those predicted using our symbolic regression model is shown in Figure
10
. Our regression (Eq.
3
) recovers
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
values from full
Prospector
fits within
3\sigma
, with an outlier fraction of 18%, substantially lower than the 38% obtained with the
Chisholm et al. 2022
relation. This indicates that our calibrated relation provides a more reliable reproduction of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
estimates across different
Prospector
models.
Notably, this updated relation between
\beta
and
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
is not valid for UV continuum slopes bluer than
\beta=-2.8
and should not be extrapolated beyond the range of the training data. It serves as an approximate estimator of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
under specific model assumptions and is intended as a substitute only when full SED fitting is computationally prohibitive. The formula replicates results that can be obtained with greater precision through full SED modelling and should only be applied in contexts where the assumptions about the IMF, isochrones, etc. used in the model – outlined in Section

6
Discussion

We have explored a suite of models and priors (Section
3
), validated their performance with a parameter recovery test (Appendix
A
), and derived a new set of escape fraction estimates for the LzLCS sample (Table
3
). By comparing the inferred stellar properties to EoR galaxies, enabled by recent JWST observations (Sections
4.1
–
4.3
), we confirm that the LzLCS galaxies provide a useful local analogue for high-redshift systems. Importantly, we find that the most extreme LyC leakers do not necessarily exhibit extreme stellar properties.

To probe potential predictors of
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, we applied symbolic regression to high-redshift observables (Section
5
). Among the tested relations, the optimal balance of accuracy and interpretability is achieved with a linear relation between
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}})
and the UV continuum slope
\beta
(Figure
10
). While such a correlation has been reported previously
(
Chisholm et al. 2022
)
, our analysis yields an updated calibration of the relation.

6.1
Implications

Our results carry several key implications.
First, by recovering the observed and unseen
F_{\mathrm{LyC}}
, we demonstrate that Bayesian inference–based SED fitting can place robust constraints on
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. The fact that the strongest LyC leakers do not necessarily exhibit the most extreme stellar properties indicates that simple global proxies, such as star formation rate or ionisation state, are insufficient predictors of LyC escape. This underscores the importance of models that capture the complex interplay between stars, gas, and dust.

Second, the comparison of gas-phase metallicities and stellar properties to both local and high-redshift populations (Sections
4.2
–
4.3
) supports the interpretation of the LzLCS galaxies as partial analogues of early galaxies. Although they are offset from local scaling relations, their metallicities and sizes are closer to those of systems at
z\sim 3
–6. This suggests that the LzLCS provides a valuable bridge population for testing physical models of LyC leakage under conditions intermediate between the nearby Universe and the Epoch of Reionization.

Finally, the symbolic regression analysis (Section
5
) offers a data-driven way to search for observable predictors of LyC escape. While the updated calibration of the
\beta
–
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
relation remains affected by large uncertainties, the inability of the network to identify a simple analytic form involving additional observables (e.g., to break the degeneracy between
\beta
, dust, and nebular properties) suggests that the underlying relations are intrinsically complex and cannot be fully captured by global galaxy properties alone.

6.2
Caveats

While 60% of our sample are best fit with the
d2log20
model, the most extreme leakers are preferentially fit with
d3log20nod2
. In principle, a single galaxy can be adequately described by multiple models, but identifying the most appropriate choice is challenging, particularly at low signal-to-noise. In no case did the Bayes factor alone decisively select the preferred model (
\Delta\log Z<5
); rather, it reduced the set of plausible models, with
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{LyC}}
acting as the tie breaker. This approach is not feasible for high-redshift galaxies, where direct LyC measurements are unavailable.

For most parameters like stellar mass, SFR, and gas-phase metallicity, the models yield consistent estimates as shown in the Appendix in Fig.
14
. The major discrepancy arises for
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
:
d3log20nod2
systematically predicts significantly higher escape fractions, with values of 15–70% for
\sim
4% of the sample, compared to 0–25% from
d2log20
. This disagreement highlights the difficulty of establishing robust escape fraction constraints, especially for the more extreme objects. One pragmatic option for high-redshift applications is to adopt the
d2log20
model, which provides the best fits for the majority of the sample, at the possible cost of underestimating
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
.

While it is inherently unreliable to infer the global
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
due to the strongly anisotropic nature of the ISM, we find that our model is capable of reliably recovering the line-of-sight
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. While forward modelling may ultimately provide a more robust path for inferring the global, cosmologically relevant
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
, the distribution of line-of-sight
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
still offers valuable insights for comparison with observations.

We explored a range of dust prescriptions to identify the best performing model. However, throughout this work we adopted only a simplified treatment of nebular emission; a parameter that significantly affects
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
.
A key limitation lies in our modelling of nebular emission through pre-computed
Cloudy
grids, which only account for ionization-bounded regions. This setup assumes that all ionizing photons entering the gas are absorbed and reprocessed, preventing any leakage through low-density, density-bounded channels. As a result, our framework does not currently permit the escape of ionizing photons that would otherwise traverse such channels. Improving the treatment of nebular emission to self-consistently include both ionization- and density-bounded regions remains an important direction for future work.

When comparing the properties of the LzLCS galaxies, we find that many align more closely with those of higher-redshift systems than with local analogue galaxies. However, being “closer” does not necessarily imply that they are fully representative. One limitation arises from the strong ongoing star formation: young stellar populations can outshine older ones, leaving long tails of possible earlier star formation that cannot be fully excluded. Such hidden older populations may contribute to the integrated properties of a galaxy, yet would be unlikely in systems at
z>6
due to their age. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether correlations with
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
evolve with redshift, or whether galaxies in the Epoch of Reionization are intrinsically different from the local systems available for study. Future
JWST
observations will help clarify these issues, and upcoming missions with far-UV capabilities (e.g., the NASA Habitable Worlds Observatory, HWO) may extend LyC escape measurements to new low-redshift candidates, thereby broadening the parameter space and refining the use of local analogues.

Another challenge concerns scale: how do the local conditions that enable LyC escape connect to the global properties we infer for entire galaxies? Progress may come from shifting toward a more spatially resolved view of LyC leakage, for example by fitting escape fractions on a pixel-by-pixel basis in IFU data cubes. Such approaches would allow correlations to be explored in a more localized context, complementing globally defined escape fractions and leading to a more nuanced understanding of the physical drivers of LyC escape.

7
Conclusion

In this study, we used the Bayesian inference-based SED fitting tool
Prospector
to analyze the photometric and emission line data of the LzLCS galaxy sample. We explored a suite of physical models with varying assumptions about dust attenuation, star formation history, and the fraction of runaway stars. Among these, the best-performing configuration for recovering the unobserved LyC flux employed a two-component dust model and a log-uniform prior between
10^{-2}
and 0 for the fraction of runaway massive stars (
frac_obrun
).

Our results yield a median inferred escape fraction of approximately 1%, with some galaxies reaching values as high as 70%, with the highest
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
stemming from the
d3lognod2
framework, with a different dust component for old and young stars and no diffuse dust. To test the robustness of our modelling framework, we conducted a parameter recovery test by generating mock observations—synthetic photometry and emission lines with added noise—and refitting them using
Prospector
. We find that the escape fraction is generally recovered within uncertainties. However, in cases where both the true escape fraction and dust optical depth are very low, the model systematically overpredicts
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
. This suggests a shortcoming in the current implementation when neutral hydrogen alone, rather than together with dust, dominates the attenuation of ionizing photons.

A likely origin of this limitation lies in the nebular emission modelling used in
Prospector
, which interpolates from a fixed
Cloudy
-based grid generated for a specific IMF and ionizing spectrum. The grid assumes ionization-bounded regions only, thereby trapping all ionizing photons within the nebular component and disallowing any escape. This constraint is particularly problematic when modelling LyC leakage through density-bounded channels.

To provide a computationally inexpensive alternative to full SED fitting and explore correlations between
f_{\mathrm{esc}}^{\mathrm{LyC}}
and other galaxy properties, we trained a symbolic regression model using a grid of synthetic data generated with
Prospector
. Using only high-redshift-accessible observables — namely the UV
\beta
slope, the Balmer decrement, and the [O
iii
]/[O
ii
] ratio — we find that the regression consistently identifies a simple linear relation between
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})
and
\beta
as the optimal solution in terms of complexity and predictive power. A subsequent Bayesian linear fit for the scatter yields
\log_{10}(f_{\mathrm{esc}})=-3.59\cdot\beta-9.45\pm 0.30
. This relation should only be applied within the validity range of our model assumptions (see Sec.
3
) and must not be extrapolated beyond
\beta<-2.8
, where symbolic regression suggests a diversion from a linear relation.

Overall, our results highlight both the potential and the limitations of inferring LyC escape fractions from indirect observables. While ratios like
f_{\mathrm{esc}}
were originally conceived to eliminate certain observational uncertainties, the anisotropic nature of LyC escape, which is well established through simulations and lensing-based observations, raises concerns about interpreting such quantities from a single line of sight.
