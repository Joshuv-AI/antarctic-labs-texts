Probing Dark Sector Particles Coupling to Neutrinos with Double Beta Decay
Noor-Ines Boudjema, Frank F. Deppisch, Antonio Herrero-Brocal, Chayan Majumdar, Supriya Senapati

Abstract
Motivated by the observation of non-zero neutrino masses and the potential for discovering physics beyond the Standard Model, numerous experiments are actively searching for neutrinoless double beta ( 0 ​ ν ​ β ​ β ) (0\nu\beta\beta) decay. In all of these searches, a substantial amount of data on two-neutrino double beta ( 2 ​ ν ​ β ​ β ) (2\nu\beta\beta) decay has been collected. In this work, we explore the sensitivity of current and future double beta decay experiments to a massive Majoron-like scalar particle coupled to neutrinos and potentially dark sector fermions, and compare their reach to the relevant cosmological constraints. On- and off-shell production of such scalar particles leads to characteristic distortions in the double beta decay electron spectrum. We investigate how these distortions would manifest in current and future double beta decay experiments, deriving the sensitivity to such scenarios. We project the reach of future experiments which can probe scalar-neutrino couplings of | a ν | ≈ 2 × 10 − 6 |a_{\nu}|\approx 2\times 10^{-6} for sub-MeV scalar particles and remain sensitive to off-shell production above the Q Q -value of double beta decay isotopes.

II.1
Model I

We construct a model featuring a neutral fermion sector composed of left- and right-handed neutrinos
(\nu_{L}^{\prime},\nu_{R}^{\prime})
and additional singlet fermions
(\chi_{L}^{\prime},\chi_{R}^{\prime})

L_{\nu_{L}^{\prime}}=+1=L_{\nu_{R}^{\prime}},L_{\chi_{L}^{\prime}}=+1=L_{\chi_{R}^{\prime}},L_{S}=2

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=m_{\nu}\overline{\nu_{R}^{\prime}}\nu_{L}^{\prime}+m_{\nu\chi}\overline{\chi_{R}^{\prime}}\nu_{L}^{\prime}+m_{\chi\nu}\overline{\nu_{R}^{\prime}}\chi_{L}^{\prime}+M\overline{\chi_{R}^{\prime}}\chi_{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+(a_{\nu}\overline{\nu^{\prime c}_{L}}\nu_{L}^{\prime}S^{*}+b_{\nu}\overline{\nu^{\prime c}_{R}}\nu_{R}^{\prime}S^{*}+a_{\nu\chi}\overline{\nu^{\prime c}_{L}}\chi_{L}^{\prime}S^{*}+b_{\nu\chi}\overline{\nu^{\prime c}_{R}}\chi_{R}^{\prime}S^{*}+a_{\chi}\overline{\chi^{\prime c}_{L}}\chi_{L}^{\prime}S^{*}+b_{\chi}\overline{\chi^{\prime c}_{R}}\chi_{R}^{\prime}S^{*})+\text{h.c.}.

For simplicity, we consider a single generation of each fermion species. The neutral fermion mass matrix in the basis of

\displaystyle m_{\text{light,heavy}}=\frac{1}{2}\left|(M+m_{\nu})\mp\sqrt{(M-m_{\nu})^{2}+4m_{\nu\chi}^{2}}\right|.

\displaystyle m_{\text{light}}\approx\left|m_{\nu}-\frac{m_{\nu\chi}^{2}}{M-m_{\nu}}\right|,\qquad m_{\text{heavy}}\approx M.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\nu^{\prime}_{L,R}\\

\displaystyle\theta\approx\frac{m_{\nu\chi}}{M}=10^{-4}.

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nu^{c}}\{\left(a_{\nu}-a_{\nu\chi}\theta+a_{\chi}\theta^{2}\right)P_{L}+\left(b_{\nu}-b_{\nu\chi}\theta+b_{\chi}\theta^{2}\right)P_{R}\}\nu S^{*}

(17)

(\nu^{\prime}_{L},\nu_{R}^{\prime c},\chi^{\prime}_{1L},\chi_{1R}^{\prime c},\chi_{2})^{T}
takes the form

\mathcal{M}\sim\begin{pmatrix}0&m_{\nu}&0&0&0\\
m_{\nu}&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&M_{1}&0\\
0&0&M_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&M_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(18)

In this framework, we have three mass-decoupled neutral fermions: the active neutrinos form a Dirac state
\nu^{\prime}=\nu=\nu^{\prime}_{L}+\nu^{\prime}_{R}
with mass
m_{\nu}
, the field
\chi^{\prime}_{1}=\chi_{1}=\chi^{\prime}_{1L}+\chi^{\prime}_{1R}
becomes a Dirac state with mass
m_{\chi_{1}}=M_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
is a Majorana fermion with mass
M_{2}
. In the mass basis, the interactions are

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nu^{c}}\{a_{\nu}P_{L}+b_{\nu}P_{R}\}\nu S^{*}+\overline{\chi_{1}}\{a_{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}}P_{L}+b_{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}}P_{R}\}\chi_{2}S^{*}+\text{h.c.}.

(19)

This framework is mainly intended to highlight that interactions involving both Dirac and Majorana neutral fermions through the scalar mediator are possible.

III
Double Beta Decay

In Sec.
II
, we saw that the presence of BSM neutral fermions could lead to new phenomenology in double beta decay experiments. In particular, Sec.
II
revealed that the most promising scenario involves a scalar mediator in an
s
-channel. Let us now analyze this possibility. We will work with two different
\chi
particles and with different couplings for the right and left-handed fermions for the sake of generality.

From Fig.
1
, it can be observed that the nuclear structure of both processes (
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
decay and scalar emission) shares the same nuclear structure as the
0\nu\beta\beta
decay, since the corresponding Feynman diagrams are identical except for the emission of a scalar particle. Consequently, all the constants involved in these processes are those appearing in
0\nu\beta\beta
decay, with the addition of the new interaction terms. We therefore denote these common constants generically as
\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}
, which we match to the conventions used in the literature in order to ensure the correct normalization of the nuclear matrix elements (NMEs). See Appendix
B
for further details. We here present the derivation of the decay width for the generic
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
decay via an
s
-channel scalar. We also show the results for the interference with the standard
2\nu\beta\beta
decay, in the case
S\to\bar{\nu}\bar{\nu}
, i.e., for the emission of two active anti-neutrinos. For comparison and later analysis we also show the rates for scalar particle emission
0\nu\beta\beta S
(with ordinary Majoron emission
0\nu\beta\beta J
as massless limit) and the SM
2\nu\beta\beta
decay. Here note that we omit the calculation of
2\nu_{t-\text{channel}~S}\beta\beta
via the exchange of a
t
-channel scalar as we are here mainly interested in the modification of the spectrum near the scalar production threshold and this contribution is expected to be sub-dominant due to the higher momentum
|q^{2}|\approx(100~\text{MeV})^{2}
in the propagator
34
. It would also require the calculation of new types of NMEs.

III.1
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
Decay

The effective Lagrangian can thus be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\nu^{c}}\left(a_{\nu}P_{L}+b_{\nu}P_{R}\right)\nu S^{*}-\overline{\chi^{c}_{i}}\left(a_{\chi}P_{L}+b_{\chi}P_{R}\right)\chi_{j}S^{*}+\text{h.c.}.

(25)

As we noted in Sec.
II
, we can recover the pure scalar and pseudoscalar interactions by imposing
a_{\nu}=\pm b_{\nu}
. In this section, we will treat
\chi_{i}
and
\chi_{j}
as different particles. If they are identical particles, a symmetry factor of
1/2
must be included.

The interactions above lead to two different scenarios depending on whether the mediator is produced on- or off-shell. The corresponding Feynman diagrams are shown in Figure
1
. The diagram on the left represents
0\nu\beta\beta S
decay, which occurs when the production of the scalar mediator
S
is kinematically allowed, i.e.,
m_{S}<Q
. The second diagram represents a potential portal to probe new fermions. Here, we focus on the case where the
\chi
particles are massive, which leads to a distinctive kink in the decay spectrum, provided that the condition
m_{\chi_{1}}+m_{\chi_{2}}<Q
is satisfied. We begin by computing the amplitude for the process as depicted in Figure
1
(right). The leptonic part of the matrix element for the
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
decay is given by

\displaystyle L^{\mu\nu}_{2\chi}=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}}{p^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}}

\displaystyle\left[\bar{u}(p_{1})\gamma^{\mu}P_{L}\frac{\not{q}+m_{\nu}}{q^{2}-m_{\nu}^{2}}\left(a_{\nu}^{*}P_{R}+b_{\nu}^{*}P_{L}\right)\frac{\not{p}-\not{q}+m_{\nu}}{(p-q)^{2}-m_{\nu}^{2}}\gamma^{\nu}P_{R}v(p_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\bar{u}(p_{3})\left(a_{\chi}^{*}P_{R}+b_{\chi}^{*}P_{L}\right)v(p_{4})\right].

(26)

The outgoing electrons have momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
whereas the exotic fermions
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
have momenta
p_{3}
and
p_{4}
, respectively. The neutrino mass is
m_{\nu}
, and
m_{S}
and
\Gamma_{S}
are the mass and decay width for the scalar mediator
S
, respectively, which has momentum
p=p_{3}+p_{4}
. Due to the small
Q\sim
MeV value in double beta decay isotopes and the tiny neutrino masses
m_{\nu}\lesssim 0.01
eV, we can assume that the final state lepton momenta and the neutrino masses are negligible compared to the momentum exchanged through the neutrino propagators,
|q^{2}|\approx(100~\text{MeV})^{2}\gg|p^{2}|,m_{\nu}^{2}
, and the amplitude simplifies

L^{\mu\nu}_{2\chi}\simeq\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}a_{\nu}^{*}}{q^{2}}\frac{g^{\mu\nu}}{p^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}}\left[\bar{u}(p_{1})P_{R}v(p_{2})\right]\left[\bar{u}(p_{3})\left(a_{\chi}^{*}P_{R}+b_{\chi}^{*}P_{L}\right)v(p_{4})\right].

(27)

Here, we have used the symmetry under the
\mu\leftrightarrow\nu
exchange of the hadronic contribution.

To compute the leptonic phase space of the process, we adopt the
S_{1/2}
approximation for the outgoing electrons. For each electron, we include the Fermi function
F_{0}(Z_{f},E_{i})
(
i=1,2
), which depends on the energy of the electron. It describes the modification of the electron wave function by the electromagnetic potential of the final state nucleus with charge
Z_{f}=Z+2

38
,

F_{0}(Z_{f},E)=4\frac{(2R\sqrt{E^{2}-m_{e}^{2}})^{2(\gamma_{0}-1)}}{\Gamma^{2}(1+2\gamma_{0})}e^{\pi y}\left|\Gamma(\gamma_{0}+iy)\right|^{2}.

(28)

Here,
R=1.2~\text{fm}\cdot A^{1/3}
denotes the nuclear radius,
\Gamma(x)
is the Gamma function,
\gamma_{0}=\sqrt{1-(\alpha Z_{f})^{2}}
, and
y=\alpha Z_{f}E/\sqrt{E^{2}-m_{e}^{2}}
, where
\alpha=1/137
is the fine-structure constant. As in
0\nu\beta\beta
decay, we assume the closure approximation, which allows us to factorize the amplitude of the process into separate leptonic and hadronic contributions. The squared amplitude can then be expressed as

\displaystyle|iM_{2\chi}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}a_{\nu}}{p^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}}\right|^{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times 4p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\left[\left(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2}\right)p_{3}\cdot p_{4}-2\text{Re}\left[(a_{\chi})^{*}b_{\chi}\right]m_{\chi_{1}}m_{\chi_{2}}\right],

(29)

where the NME,
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
, is identical to the one in standard
0\nu\beta\beta
decay, due to the same hadronic structure. We will write the total
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
decay width as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2\chi}=\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}\,\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2},

(30)

The phase space factor
\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}
is calculated by integrating

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}|a_{\nu}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int\prod_{i=1}^{4}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}4p_{1}\cdot p_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\frac{\left(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2}\right)p_{3}\cdot p_{4}-2\text{Re}\left[a_{\chi}^{*}\,b_{\chi}\right]m_{\chi_{1}}m_{\chi_{2}}}{\left|p^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(Q-T-E_{3}-E_{4}\right),

(31)

over the phase space of the final state fermions with
p_{i}=(E_{i},\mathbf{p}_{i})
. First, we integrate over the phase space of the invisible fermions
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
, yielding two integrals

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=3}^{4}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}\frac{p_{3}\cdot p_{4}}{\left|\left(p_{3}+p_{4}\right)^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}\right|^{2}}\delta\left(Q-T-E_{3}-E_{4}\right),

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=m_{\chi_{1}}m_{\chi_{2}}\int\prod_{i=3}^{4}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}\frac{1}{\left|\left(p_{3}+p_{4}\right)^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+im_{S}\Gamma_{S}\right|^{2}}\delta\left(Q-T-E_{3}-E_{4}\right),

(32)

with the total kinetic energy of the electrons
T=E_{1}+E_{2}-2m_{e}
. Defining

\displaystyle x=\frac{2|\mathbf{p}_{3}||\mathbf{p}_{4}|}{m_{S}^{2}-m_{\chi_{1}}^{2}-m_{\chi_{2}}^{2}-2E_{3}E_{4}},

(33)

with
|\mathbf{p}_{3}|=(E_{3}^{2}-m_{\chi_{1}}^{2})^{1/2}
,
|\mathbf{p}_{4}|=(E_{4}^{2}-m_{\chi_{2}}^{2})^{1/2}
and
E_{4}=Q-T-E_{3}
, we can integrate analytically over
E_{4}
and the angles of
\mathbf{p}_{3}
and
\mathbf{p}_{4}
, to express the integrals as

\displaystyle I_{1}(T)=\frac{1}{4(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{m_{\chi_{1}}}^{Q-T-m_{\chi_{2}}}dE_{3}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{m_{S}^{2}-m_{\chi_{1}}^{2}-m_{\chi_{2}}^{2}}{2m_{S}\Gamma_{S}}\left[\arctan\zeta_{+}(E_{3})+\arctan\zeta_{-}(E_{3})\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1}{4}\log\left[\frac{\zeta_{-}^{2}(E_{3})+1}{\zeta_{+}^{2}(E_{3})+1}\right]\right\},

(34)

and

\displaystyle I_{2}(T)=\frac{1}{4(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{m_{\chi_{1}}m_{\chi_{2}}}{m_{S}\Gamma_{S}}\int_{m_{\chi_{1}}}^{Q-T-m_{\chi_{2}}}dE_{3}\left[\arctan\zeta_{+}(E_{3})+\arctan\zeta_{-}(E_{3})\right].

(35)

Here,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\pm}(E_{3})=\frac{2|\mathbf{p}_{3}||\mathbf{p}_{4}|(x\pm 1)}{m_{S}\Gamma_{S}x}.

(36)

In Eqs. (
III.1
) and (
35
), we expressed the integrands using the relation

\displaystyle\arctan\zeta_{+}+\arctan\zeta_{-}=\arctan\left(\frac{\zeta_{+}+\zeta_{-}}{1-\zeta_{+}\zeta_{-}}\right)

(37)

as this turns out to be more numerically stable for physical, i.e., small values of
\Gamma_{S}
when evaluating the integrands.

Then,
\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}|a_{\nu}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int\prod_{i=1}^{2}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}4p_{1}\cdot p_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\left[\left(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2}\right)I_{1}(T)-2\text{Re}\left[a_{\chi}^{*}b_{\chi}\right]I_{2}(T)\right].

(38)

It is convenient to transform this expression into one that depends on the electron kinetic energies,
T_{1}=E_{1}-m_{e}
and
T_{2}=E_{2}-m_{e}
, and the angle
\theta_{12}
between the emitted electrons, with
\mathbf{p}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{2}=|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|\cos\theta_{12}
. The fully differential decay rate can then be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\Gamma^{2\chi}_{S}}{dT_{1}dT_{2}d\cos\theta_{12}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0\nu}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{2R}\right)^{2}\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}|\mathbf{p_{1}}||\mathbf{p_{2}}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\left(1-\frac{|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|}{E_{1}E_{2}}\cos\theta_{12}\right)

\displaystyle\times|a_{\nu}|^{2}\left\{\left(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2}\right)I_{1}(T_{1}+T_{2})-2\text{Re}\left[a_{\chi}^{*}b_{\chi}\right]I_{2}(T_{1}+T_{2})\right\},

(39)

where we have explicitly extracted the factor
m_{e}/(2R)
to emphasize that
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
is dimensionless and that the phase space factor
\mathcal{G}_{2\chi}
has units of
\text{yr}^{-1}
, to conform to the usual conventions employed in
0\nu\beta\beta
decay calculations. Here
|\mathbf{p}_{i}|
and
E_{i}
are understood to be expressed in terms of the
T_{i}
and the factor
\kappa_{0\nu}
has been obtained using the correct normalization for
\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0\nu}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}R^{2}}{2\pi^{4}m_{e}^{2}}=\frac{G_{F}^{4}\cos^{4}\theta_{C}}{8\pi^{5}m_{e}^{2}},

(40)

with the Cabibbo angle
\theta_{C}
.

Most double beta decay experiments measure only the differential decay rate
d\Gamma/dT
with respect to the total electron kinetic energy
T=T_{1}+T_{2}
. Integrating Eq. (
III.1
) over the angle and energy difference
\Delta T=T_{1}-T_{2}
yields the result

\displaystyle\frac{d\Gamma^{2\chi}_{S}}{dT}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0\nu}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{2R}\right)^{2}\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}|a_{\nu}|^{2}\left\{\left(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2}\right)I_{1}(T)-2\text{Re}\left[a_{\chi}^{*}b_{\chi}\right]I_{2}(T)\right\}g(T)

\displaystyle\times\Theta(Q-m_{\chi_{1}}-m_{\chi_{2}}-T),

(41)

with the Heaviside function
\Theta(x)
and

\displaystyle g(T)=\int_{-T}^{T}d\Delta T|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(|\mathbf{p}_{1}|)F_{0}(|\mathbf{p}_{2}|),

(42)

where we express the integrand as
E_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(T+\Delta T)+m_{e}
,
E_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(T-\Delta T)+m_{e}
and
|\mathbf{p}_{i}|=(E_{i}^{2}-m_{e}^{2})^{1/2}
(
i=1,2
). Finally, to compute the total decay width, we integrate over
T
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2\chi}_{S}=\int_{0}^{Q-m_{\chi_{1}}-m_{\chi_{2}}}dT\frac{d\Gamma_{2\chi}}{dT}.

(43)

The above generic calculation for distinct, massive
\chi_{1}
,
\chi_{2}
is applicable to all contributions considered here, with an additional symmetry factor of
1/2
for identical particles, i.e.,
2\nu_{S}\beta\beta
and
2\chi_{S}\beta\beta
. The above expressions can be simplified for numerical evaluation if one or both
\chi
are massless, due to
|\mathbf{p}_{i}|=E_{i}
.

III.2
Interference between SM
2\nu\beta\beta
and Scalar-induced
2\nu_{S}\beta\beta

In the case of the scalar
S
producing two active anti-neutrinos, its contribution will interfere with the standard
2\nu\beta\beta
decay. This interference is determined by the complex-valued integral

\displaystyle I_{\text{int}}(T)

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=3}^{4}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}\frac{p_{3}\cdot p_{4}}{\left(p_{3}+p_{4}\right)^{2}-m_{S}^{2}+i\,m_{S}\,\Gamma_{S}}\delta\left(Q-T-E_{3}-E_{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{S}^{2}-i\,\Gamma_{S}m_{S}}{8(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{0}^{Q-T}dE_{3}\Bigg\{\frac{4E_{3}E_{4}}{m_{S}^{2}-i\,\Gamma_{S}\,m_{S}}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\left(m_{S}^{2}-4\,E_{3}E_{4}\right)^{2}+\Gamma_{S}^{2}m_{S}^{2}}{m_{S}^{2}(\Gamma_{S}^{2}+m_{S}^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+i\left[\text{arccot}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{S}m_{S}}{m_{S}^{2}-4\,E_{3}E_{4}}\right)-\text{arccot}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{S}}{m_{S}}\right)\right]\Bigg\}.

(44)

Here, we note that the convention for
\text{arccot}(x)
is such that it has the range
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\text{arccot}(x)<\frac{\pi}{2}
.

The contribution of the decay rate from this interference is given by

\frac{d\Gamma^{2\nu}_{\text{int}}}{dT}=4\,\pi\,\kappa_{0\nu}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{2R}\right)\text{Re}\left[a_{\nu}^{2}\,\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\,\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}^{*}\,I_{\text{int}}(T)\right]g(T)\,\Theta(Q-T).

(45)

It thus depends on both the
0\nu\beta\beta
decay NME
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
and the
2\nu\beta\beta
decay NME
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
. As an interference between a SM and exotic contribution, it is proportional to the small exotic coupling to second power only. It is also enhanced relative to SM
2\nu\beta\beta
decay by the appearance of the larger NME
M_{0\nu}
. We still expect that its contribution will be small as the interference requires a sizeable width
\Gamma_{S}
or off-shell
S
production,
T>Q-m_{S}
, which is strongly suppressed compared to the on-shell production.

III.3
Scalar Emission

Taking the limit of zero width,
\Gamma_{S}\to 0
and considering the emission of
S
as a real particle, we recover the case of scalar emission
0\nu\beta\beta S
,

\frac{d\Gamma_{S}}{dT}=\frac{\kappa_{0\nu}}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{2R}\right)^{2}\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}|a_{\nu}|^{2}g(T)\sqrt{(Q-T)^{2}-m_{S}^{2}}\,\Theta(Q-m_{S}-T).

(46)

We note that this limit is, strictly speaking, not self-consistent as at least the decay to massless active neutrinos is always allowed if
m_{S}>0
and thus
\Gamma_{S}>0
. If all couplings involved are small, this is still a well justified approximation. For
m_{S}=0
, the above expression gives the standard Majoron contribution
0\nu\beta\beta J

. As we express our result as distributions over
T
and
\Delta T
instead of the electron energies, an additional factor of
1/2
appears in the pre-factor compared to the expression commonly found in the literature
47
;
24

IV
Cosmology and Laboratory Constraints

Interactions between neutrinos and other light particles produce a range of observable effects which can be constrained by imposing astrophysical, cosmological and laboratory bounds. We begin by reviewing the existing constraints on the frameworks presented in Sec.
II
, specifically Model I
′
as the exotic Dirac fermion
\chi
is stable under the presence of a discrete
Z_{2}
symmetry. These include limits on neutrino and exotic fermion (self-)interactions as well as their couplings to mediator particles. The constraints arise from a variety of sources, including cosmological observations such as those from Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN), collisional damping, and thermal relic density considerations, as well as laboratory searches involving meson decays.

IV.1
Cosmology Constraints

IV.1.1
Thermal Relic

Considering equal number densities for DM particles and their antiparticles, the thermally averaged annihilation cross section (multiplied by the relative velocity) required to account for the observed DM abundance in present day universe, which can be expressed by a series expansion in relative velocity
v_{r}
(considering non-relativistic DM scenario with
v_{r}\ll c
) is
67
;
58
,

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v_{r}\rangle=a_{r}+b_{r}v_{r}^{2}+d_{r}v_{r}^{4}.

(50)

To avoid DM overabundance, the upper limit on the DM thermal relic density
2
,
\Omega_{\chi}h^{2}\leq 0.120\pm 0.001
can be approximately translated to the lower limit
52
;
61
on
\langle\sigma v_{r}\rangle\geq 3\times 10^{-26}\rm{cm^{3}}/\rm{s}
for a constant cross section at leading order in the expansion stated above, where
\sigma
corresponds to the annihilation cross section of DM species to active neutrino pair,
\chi\chi\to\nu\nu
. Additionally, if the cross section, at leading-order, depends on
v_{r}^{2}
or
v_{r}^{4}
, the required value at freeze-out will be
\langle\sigma v_{r}\rangle\simeq 6\times 10^{-29}\ \rm{cm^{3}}/\rm{s}
or
\langle\sigma v_{r}\rangle\simeq 9\times 10^{-26}\ \rm{cm^{3}}/\rm{s}
, respectively
44
.

IV.1.2
Collisional Damping

Interactions between neutrinos and DM can significantly affect the distribution of matter and radiation in the Universe, particularly by affecting the CMB spectrum and the formation of large-scale structures (LSS)
18
;
21
;
22
. There are two primary effects to consider in this perspective: the considered DM is no longer collisionless due to its interaction with neutrinos and the mediator particle, and the neutrinos remain collisional for a longer period compared to the standard cosmological scenario. This reduces the length of DM free streaming and enhances their ability to cluster more efficiently which eventually contributes to the formation of large-scale structures. By comparing theoretical predictions with CMB and LSS observations, constraints can be placed on the strength of
\nu
-DM interactions. In this regard, Planck data suggests that the
\nu
-DM elastic scattering cross section for the process
\nu\chi\to\nu\chi
must be
5
;
37

\sigma_{\text{el}}<10^{-29}\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{\rm{MeV}}\right)~\text{cm}^{2}.

(51)

IV.1.3
Neutrino Reheating Bounds

DM annihilations into neutrinos after they have decoupled from electrons, at temperatures
T\lesssim T_{D}\sim 1
MeV (where
T_{D}
is the decoupling temperature of DM from electrons)

;
62

19
;
48
;
65
;
20
;
56
;
64
;
57

N_{\rm{eff}}

\rho_{\nu}\equiv\rho_{\gamma}\left[1+\frac{7}{8}\left(\frac{T_{\nu}}{T_{\gamma}}\right)^{4/3}N_{\rm{eff}}\right],

(52)

where
\rho_{\gamma}
represents the photon energy density and
T_{\nu},T_{\gamma}
correspond to the temperature of neutrino and photons, respectively. In our framework, because of interactions with neutrinos, the DM particle remains in thermal equilibrium for a longer time. Therefore, the effective number of light relativistic species becomes
20
;
69

\displaystyle N_{\text{eff}}=N_{\nu}\left[1+\frac{1}{N_{\nu}}\frac{g_{\chi}}{2}F\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{T_{D}}\right)^{4/3}\right],

(53)

where
N_{\nu}=3.046

\displaystyle F(x)=\frac{30}{7\pi^{4}}\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\,\frac{(4y^{2}-x^{2})\sqrt{y^{2}-x^{2}}}{e^{y}\pm 1}.

(54)

IV.1.4
CMB-NSI

Neutrino non-standard interactions (NSI) influence the epoch of neutrino free streaming and their coupling to the photon–baryon fluid in the early Universe. Such effects manifest as modifications in the phase shift and amplitude of the matter power spectrum, leading to stringent cosmological bounds. These constraints apply not only to neutrino self-interactions but also to interactions with a light dark sector, with implications for alleviating long-standing tensions in cosmological data, such as the
H_{0}
and
\sum m_{\nu}
tensions
37

G_{\text{eff}}

G_{\text{eff}}\simeq\frac{y_{\nu}^{2}}{m_{S}^{2}}<5.6\times 10^{-5}~\text{MeV}^{-2},

(55)

where

CMB-

Additional constraints on

IV.1.6
BBN

If both the mediator and light particles are at the MeV scale, additional constraints arise from Big Bang Nucleosynthesis observation. For light particles, the annihilation process

at

which eventually excludes the parameter space where exotic particle remains in equilibrium with the SM plasma and effectively increasing the

IV.2
Meson Decays

For a light scalar mediator

37

Z

V
Statistical Procedure

Our goal is to estimate the sensitivity of current and future double beta decay experiments to the different models discussed in Sec.
II
, and we follow the standard frequentist approach
66
. The total double beta decay rate with respect to the electrons’ kinetic energy
T
in our case is then generally written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\Gamma(\mathbf{x})}{dT}=\frac{d\Gamma^{2\nu}_{\text{SM}}}{dT}+\frac{d\Gamma_{\text{BSM}}(\bf x)}{dT}.

(57)

Here,
d\Gamma^{2\nu}_{\text{BSM}}/dT
represents the standard
2\nu\beta\beta
decay contribution in the SM, which will act as background to the search for an exotic contribution
d\Gamma_{\text{BSM}}(\mathbf{x})/dT
. Here,
\mathbf{x}
represents the parameters of the model in question. For example, in the simplest scenario of scalar emission,
\mathbf{x}=(a_{\nu},m_{S})
. The BSM contribution will vanish in a certain parameter limit
\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{0}
, e.g., for
a_{\nu}\to 0
in the scalar emission case.

Experiments observe events of double electron emission distributed over energy bins. Theoretically, the expected number of events within an energy interval
T_{i}<T<T_{i}+\Delta T
is calculated as

\displaystyle N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})=N_{A}m_{\text{iso}}^{-1}\mathcal{E}\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i}+\Delta T}dT\frac{d\Gamma(\mathbf{x})}{dT}.

(58)

The factor in front of the integral gives the effective number of decaying nuclei with Avogadro’s number
N_{A}=6.022\times 10^{23}
mol
-1
, the molar mass
m_{\text{iso}}
of the isotope in question, and the fiducial exposure
\mathcal{E}
of the experiment in units of kg
\cdot
yr.

Having observed a set of events, the log-likelihood, or
\chi^{2}(\mathbf{x})
, of the binned data
\mathbf{D}=(N_{\text{obs}}^{(i)})
given a BSM hypothesis is

\displaystyle-2\log\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=2\sum^{N_{\text{bins}}}_{i}\left\{N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})-N^{(i)}_{\text{obs}}+N^{(i)}_{\text{obs}}\log\left(\frac{N^{(i)}_{\text{obs}}}{N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\chi^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\sum^{N_{\text{bins}}}_{i}\frac{\left(N^{(i)}_{\text{obs}}-N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}}{N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})},

(59)

where the second line applies if there are a sufficiently many events per bin
70
. For the standard
2\nu\beta\beta
decay as common contribution, this is the case for the current and future experimental exposures considered below.

In order to estimate the sensitivity of an experiment, we assume that it does not see a distribution that differs significantly from the SM prediction, setting the number of observed events to
N^{(i)}_{\text{obs}}=N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{0})
. This represents the so-called Asimov data set
25
which provides a good approximation to Monte Carlo simulations of the experiment that would otherwise be required
32
.

In addition to the statistical uncertainty, we include two types of systematic errors:

1.

Systematic experimental errors, assumed proportional to the number of events in a bin,
\sigma_{\text{sys}}^{(i)}=\sigma_{f}N_{\text{exp}}^{(i)}
, are added in quadrature to the statistical uncertainty. We take
\sigma_{f}
as constant for a given experiment.

2.

The main theoretical uncertainty arises from the
2\nu\beta\beta
and
0\nu\beta\beta
NMEs that enter the decay rate calculations. The standard
2\nu\beta\beta
decay depends on
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
whereas the BSM contributions, due to their ordinary Majoron structure, mainly scale with
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
(the interference of the scalar-mediated
2\nu_{S}\beta\beta
with SM
2\nu\beta\beta
will also depend on
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
). Calculations in different nuclear structure models differ by factors of 2-3 in a given isotope. In order to include this effect, we treat both
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
as Gaussian-distributed nuisance parameters with best fit values and standard deviations,
\langle\mathcal{M}_{n\nu}\rangle\pm\delta\mathcal{M}_{n\nu}
, derived from a survey of NME values
4
. We also include a potential correlation between
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
. While this correlation is not direct and perfect, it is observed statistically in several NME calculations and is encoded by a statistical correlation factor
\rho

49
. Jointly,
\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
are assumed to follow a bi-variate, potentially tilted, Gaussian distribution.

We thus have the
\chi^{2}
function

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum^{N_{\text{bins}}}_{i}\frac{\left(N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{0})-N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}}{N^{(i)}_{\text{exp}}(\mathbf{x})+(\sigma_{\text{sys}}^{(i)})^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\left[\frac{(\eta-\langle\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\rangle)^{2}}{(\delta\mathcal{M}_{2\nu})^{2}}+\frac{(\kappa-\langle\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\rangle)^{2}}{(\delta\mathcal{M}_{0\nu})^{2}}-2\rho\frac{(\eta-\langle\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\rangle)(\kappa-\langle\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\rangle)}{\delta\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\cdot\delta\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}}\right],

(60)

where the set of parameters
\mathbf{x}
now includes the two NME nuisance parameters
\eta
,
\kappa
, and the Asimov data set is calculated using the best fit value
\eta=\langle\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\rangle
. Thus, by construction,
\chi^{2}(\mathbf{0})=0
for the Asimov data set, i.e., the SM
2\nu\beta\beta
decay case with
\langle\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\rangle
as NME.

We are mainly interested in the overall sensitivity to the BSM scenarios considered. We thus do not attempt fit all model parameters at the same time. Instead, we choose one coupling constant
g
as fitting parameter while keeping all other parameters fixed, specifically the particle masses involved. For example, in the simple scalar emission model, we calculate
\chi^{2}
using
a_{\nu}
for a fixed scalar mass
m_{S}
. In order to find the 90% confidence level (CL) limit on such a coupling constant
g
, we first minimize over the NME nuisance parameters for a given
g
,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(g)=\underset{\eta,\kappa}{\text{min}}\left[\chi^{2}(g,\eta,\kappa)\right].

(61)

This marginalized profile function then follows a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom and to find the 90% CL upper limit on
g
, we set
\chi^{2}(g)=2.71
.

VII
Conclusion

Double beta decay processes are sensitive avenues to probe physics beyond the SM. While the neutrinoless mode,
0\nu\beta\beta
is the key process to probe the Majorana nature of neutrinos, in presence of new light particles, one can also expect distinct signatures over the SM
2\nu\beta\beta
decay spectrum. In this work, we have explored a class of scenarios where a light scalar,
S
, couples both to active neutrinos and new fermionic states
\chi
. Owing to these new interactions, on-shell and off-shell production of
S
followed by its decay into neutrinos or exotic fermions, a spectral distortion in the double beta decay spectrum can be observed. We have constructed simplified model scenarios that generate the effective four-fermion operators responsible for exotic double beta decay with the emission of
\chi
. Focusing on scalar
s
-channel mediation, we constructed two Dirac neutrino scenarios (Model I and I
′
) differing by the presence or absence of
\nu
–
\chi
mixing, the latter enforced through a stabilizing
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
symmetry that makes
\chi
a potential dark matter candidate. We further introduced a third model (Model II) involving both Dirac and Majorana exotic fermions to illustrate the broader range of possible interactions.

We analyzed how the double beta decay spectrum is modified by the presence of mediator scalar
S
and the exotic fermion species
\chi
, highlighting the dependence of the spectral shape on their masses and couplings. For massless
\chi
, we showed that when
S
is sufficiently light to be produced on-shell, the resulting
2\chi_{S}\beta\beta
spectrum closely follows the scalar emission shape, with only minor deviations arising from finite-width effects of the mediator scalar. As
m_{S}
approaches the kinematic threshold or becomes too heavy to be produced on-shell, the spectrum shifts toward lower electron energies or enters an off-shell regime in which the dominant contributions move to larger
T
, eventually matching the SM
2\nu\beta\beta
distribution in the
m_{S}\gg Q
limit. Introducing non-zero
\chi
masses further restricts on-shell production of the mediator and suppresses visible distortions in the electron spectrum, making heavier
\chi
scenarios increasingly challenging to probe. Finally, in the case where
S
couples exclusively to neutrinos, we demonstrated that the full double beta decay spectrum receives both on-shell and off-shell scalar-mediated contributions, including destructive interference with the SM amplitude, leading to characteristic spectral suppression near threshold. These results collectively illustrate the diverse range of spectral signatures that light scalars and exotic fermions can imprint on double beta decay observables.

Appendix A
Calculation of scattering rates

The squared amplitude, summing over final spins and averaging over initial spins, for annihilation channel of DM
\chi
to neutrinos via s-channel scalar mediation,
\chi(p_{1})+\chi(p_{2})\to S\to\nu(p_{3})+\nu(p_{4})
, can be written as,

\displaystyle\sum_{\rm{spins}}|\mathcal{M}_{s}|^{2}=\frac{1}{(s-m_{S}^{2})^{2}+m_{S}^{2}\Gamma_{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\left[(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2})(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})-2m_{\chi}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\chi}b^{*}_{\chi})\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[(|a_{\nu}|^{2}+|b_{\nu}|^{2})(p_{3}\cdot p_{4})-2m_{\nu}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\nu}b^{*}_{\nu})\right],

(65)

where
p_{1}\cdot p_{2}=(s-2m_{\chi}^{2})/2
,
p_{3}\cdot p_{4}=(s-2m_{\nu}^{2})/2
and
\Gamma_{S}=\Gamma(S\to\nu\nu)+\Gamma(S\to\chi\chi)
is the total decay width of scalar
S
. Following the technique developed in
67
;
58
, we have estimated the thermally averaged annihilation cross section
\langle\sigma v_{\rm{rel}}\rangle
which will be constrained by present-day relic density criteria for DM candidates in the universe, where we have considered
v_{\rm{rel}}=10^{-3}\ll c
.

Similarly, the squared amplitude for elastic scattering between DM
\chi
and neutrinos,
\chi(p_{1})+\bar{\nu}(p_{2})\to\bar{\chi}(p_{3})+\nu(p_{4})
via t-channel scalar mediation can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\sum_{\rm{spins}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2(t-m_{S}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle[(p_{3}\cdot p_{1})(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2})+(p_{4}\cdot p_{2})(|a_{\nu}|^{2}+|b_{\nu}|^{2})

\displaystyle-2m_{\chi}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\chi}b_{\chi}^{\ast})-2m_{\nu}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\nu}b_{\nu}^{\ast})]

(66)

where
p_{3}\cdot p_{1}=(t-2m_{\chi}^{2})/2
and
p_{4}\cdot p_{2}=(t-2m_{\nu}^{2})/2
. Using this, the elastic scattering cross section can be estimated as
\sigma_{\rm{el}}=1/(16\pi s)\sum_{\rm{spins}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}|^{2}
and this is constrained by the bounds from collisional damping
18
;
21
;
22
.

Furthermore, we have obtained the scattering cross section for
\nu(p_{1})+\nu(p_{2})\to\chi(p_{3})+\chi(p_{4})
via s-channel scalar mediation can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\sigma(\nu\nu\to S\to\chi\chi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi s\left[(s-m_{S}^{2})^{2}+m_{S}^{2}\Gamma_{S}^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\frac{s-4m_{\chi}^{2}}{s-4m_{\nu}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\times\left[(|a_{\nu}|^{2}+|b_{\nu}|^{2})(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})-2m_{\nu}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\nu}b_{\nu}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[(|a_{\chi}|^{2}+|b_{\chi}|^{2})(p_{3}\cdot p_{4})-2m_{\chi}^{2}\text{Re}(a_{\chi}b_{\chi}^{*})\right],

(67)

where
p_{1}\cdot p_{2}=(s-2m_{\nu}^{2})/2
and
p_{3}\cdot p_{4}=(s-2m_{\chi}^{2})/2
.

Appendix B
Normalization for
\beta\beta
decay widths

The nuclear structure appearing in our processes, i.e.,
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\beta\beta
and Majoron decay, is the same as that involved in neutrinoless double beta decay. Therefore, we can make use of the NMEs of that process. To do so, our normalization must be matched to that used in the literature in order to consistently employ the numerical results for
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
.

Within our approach, it is straightforward to recover the standard results for
0\nu\beta\beta
. The process amplitude can be factorized into leptonic and hadronic contributions:

\mathcal{A}_{0\nu}=\sum_{n}L_{\mu\nu}H^{\mu\nu},

(68)

where
L
and
H
denote the leptonic and hadronic contributions, respectively, and the sum over
n
accounts for the nuclear intermediate states. Assuming the closure approximation both factors decouple and, using, the symmetry under
\mu\leftrightarrow\nu
in the hadronic sector, the leptonic part can be written as

L_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\,m_{\alpha\alpha}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\bar{u}_{1}P_{R}v_{2},

(69)

where
\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}
is a normalization factor chosen to match the standard results in the literature. The corresponding decay width is then expressed as

\Gamma_{0\nu}=\left|\frac{m_{\alpha\alpha}}{m_{e}}\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}\mathcal{G}_{0\nu}\,.

(70)

where
\mathcal{G}_{0\nu}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}
denote the phase space factor and the nuclear matrix element, respectively. The phase space factor can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0\nu}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}\int\prod_{i=1}^{2}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}2\,p_{1}\cdot p_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\delta(Q-T)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{m_{e}}^{Q+m_{e}}|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})dE_{1}\,,

(71)

where
F_{0}(p)
is the Fermi function, and we have used
E_{2}=Q+2m_{e}-E_{1}
and
|\mathbf{p}_{i}|=\sqrt{E_{i}^{2}-m_{i}^{2}}
. Comparing with the standard result in the literature, we obtain

\left|\mathcal{N}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}=\frac{G_{F}^{4}\cos^{4}(\theta_{c})}{4\pi R^{2}}\,,

(72)

in such a way the decay width is given by

\Gamma_{0\nu}=\left|\frac{m_{\alpha\alpha}}{m_{e}}\right|^{2}\frac{G_{F}^{4}\cos^{4}(\theta_{c})m_{e}^{2}}{32\pi^{5}R^{2}}\left|\mathcal{M}_{0\nu}\right|^{2}\int_{m_{e}}^{Q+m_{e}}|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})dE_{1}\,,

(73)

and reproduce the known result.

For the case where
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are antineutrinos, one must also account for the interference with standard
2\nu\beta\beta
. Following a similar approach, the leptonic contribution can be written as

L_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{N}_{2\nu}\,m_{\alpha\alpha}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\bar{u}_{1}P_{R}v_{2}\;\bar{u}_{4}P_{R}v_{3},

(74)

where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
correspond to the electron momenta, while
p_{3}
and
p_{4}
correspond to the neutrinos. The decay width is

\Gamma_{2\nu}=\left|\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\right|^{2}\mathcal{G}_{2\nu},

(75)

with the phase space factor

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2\nu}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathcal{N}_{2\nu}\right|^{2}\int\prod_{i=1}^{4}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{i}}4\,p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\,F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\,p_{3}\cdot p_{4}\,\delta(Q-T-E_{3}-E_{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{4\left|\mathcal{N}_{2\nu}\right|^{2}}{(2\pi)^{8}}\int\frac{(Q-T)^{5}}{30}|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\,dE_{1}\,dE_{2}\,.

(76)

Comparison with the standard literature then gives

\left|\mathcal{N}_{2\nu}\right|^{2}=8\pi\frac{G_{F}^{4}\cos^{4}(\theta_{c})}{m_{e}^{2}}\,.

(77)

Thus, the decay width is given by

\Gamma_{2\nu}=\frac{G_{F}^{4}\cos^{4}(\theta_{c})}{8\pi^{7}m_{e}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{M}_{2\nu}\right|^{2}\int\frac{(Q-T)^{5}}{30}|\mathbf{p}_{1}||\mathbf{p}_{2}|E_{1}E_{2}F_{0}(p_{1})F_{0}(p_{2})\,dE_{1}\,dE_{2}\,,

(78)

recovering the known result.
