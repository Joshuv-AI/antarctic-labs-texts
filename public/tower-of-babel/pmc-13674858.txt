The influence of cell morphology on the dynamics and stability of model bacterial communities
Authors: Ing Xhu Lim, Faisal Halabeya, Joshua N. Milstein, Stefan Klumpp, Tobias Bollenbach, Stefan Klumpp, Tobias Bollenbach, Stefan Klumpp, Tobias Bollenbach, Stefan Klumpp, Tobias Bollenbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how structures arise in microbial communities remains an outstanding challenge in bacterial ecology. When replicating within dense and confined environments, bacterial populations may collectively self-organize as a result of mechanical interactions. We previously observed that model dual-populations of bacteria, colonizing open-ended microchannels, either quickly fixate to a single population or segregate into long-lived, coexisting populations. This latter quasi-stability results from the alignment of bacteria into lanes, forcing cells to grow toward the open ends of the channel, with inter-lane invasion events driving the boundary dynamics between populations. Here we apply agent-based (AB) simulations to explore the boundary dynamics between dual-bacterial populations of varying morphology and division rate. We find that the simulated boundary dynamics are well-described by a simple drift-diffusion model, which enables us to estimate the mean time to fixation within competitive scenarios where the fixation time is difficult to access by AB simulations. Coccus-like cells display a competitive advantage over bacillus cells as they can more effectively invade and ultimately fixate, while coexisting populations of bacillus cells are “effectively” stable. And while faster-dividing cells should have a selective advantage, their morphology determines if this advantage drives fixation or acts as a defensive strategy to maintain their population. These findings highlight the critical role that cell morphology and mechanics play in shaping bacterial communities.

Introduction

Bacteria commonly exist within communities in nature where they compete for limited space and resources. These communities play an essential role in supporting a diversity of critical ecosystems, from regulating metabolism and protecting against pathogens within the human gut, to cycling carbon and nitrogen within Earth’s atmosphere [1,2]. Even initially well-mixed populations of bacteria will tend to segregate and spatially organize as cells grow and divide. This resulting spatial structure may be crucial for cooperation within species [3], the sharing of public goods such as digestive enzymes or metabolites [4], or as a resistance strategy toward antibiotic compounds [5,6]. While the availability of nutrients and the chemical environment both affect the spatial organization of bacterial communities, within dense systems, spatial structure may also emerge spontaneously through mechanical interactions with neighbouring cells and their confines [7,8]. This emergent behaviour may critically impact the evolution and ecology of these populations.

Mechanical interactions can affect both the invasion dynamics [9] and resulting self-organization [10] of bacterial communities. These effects are thought to give rise to much of the structural architecture of biofilms [11,12]. Collective mechanical forces may also directly influence the growth dynamics of bacteria [13], which may lead to to novel ecologies. For instance, while faster growing cells are often assumed to hold a selective advantage, within a number of dense and confined systems it has been observed that slower growing cells are able to either outcompete [14,15] or, at least, stably coexist with their faster growing peers [16].

Cellular morphology, which affects the packing of dense microcolonies, can also influence structural properties of these communities, as has been observed in the stratification of populations within 3-dimensional biofilms [17]. In simple confined monolayer systems, pill shaped E. coli cells will display a global nematic ordering arranging cells into distinct lanes [18]. The mechanisms behind this spontaneous ordering, as well as factors such as buckling instabilities which cause cells to invade neighbouring lanes and, ultimately, destroy such an arrangement, have been explored by numerous groups [19–22].

Synthetic microfluidic systems serve as a controlled test-bed for studying self-organized spatial structuring within bacterial communities [23,24]. We previously reported on the rapid emergence of quasi-stable, coexisting communities within a model competitive system composed of two distinct E. coli strains confined to open-ended, monolayer microchannels [16]. Cells seeded within these channels would either rapidly fixate to a homogeneous population of one cell type (typically in the time it takes for the populations to fill the channel, i.e., ~6−8 hrs), or would organize into distinct, quasi-stable populations. Once established, these segregated populations would coexist for extended times (> 24 hrs) despite significant heterogeneity between strains in terms of growth advantage or cell morphology.

These observations are largely explained by noting that, once the channel fills, the cells primarily self-align into lanes that grow toward the open ends of the channel. This arrangement is only altered by the rare cell invading into a neighboring lane and taking it over. We previously showed that successful invasions are, in fact, exceedingly rare for all attempts except those that occur in the middle of a lane, away from the open ends of the channel [25]. Most invading cells are quickly pushed out of the ends of the channel, which is why heterogeneous communities with a significant difference in growth rate between populations can coexist for extended periods.

Employing both agent-based (AB) simulations and mathematical modelling, we now explore the stability of these segregated communities by studying the dynamics of the boundary between the populations. In particular, we infer the mean first-passage time (MFPT) of the boundary to the channel edges, which indicates the average time it takes for the community to eventually fixate to a single, homogeneous population. The boundary dynamics are found to be well-modeled by a one-dimensional drift-diffusion equation whose parameters are influenced by the morphology and division rate of the bacteria.

Here we focus on competition between two bacterial strains displaying morphologies ranging from bacillus (pill-shaped) to coccus-like (round). These morphological differences can impart a selective advantage to the more coccus-like cells, such that the coccus-like population fixates within a finite period, whereas competition between increasingly elongated bacillus morphologies leads to mean fixation times that appear to diverge. And while an increased cell division rate can sometimes drive fixation, for well-aligned bacillus populations, it can at most stabilize the population by protecting it from invasion. Despite the simplicity of the system under study, our results highlight the role that bacterial morphology can play in the stability or instability of a bacterial community.

Results

Stability and boundary dynamics

We first employ agent-based (AB) simulations to model bacterial organization within dense communities that grow in two-dimensional, open-ended microchannels. AB simulations are a common tool for modelling microbial communities, and they are widely used to study population dynamics, community interactions, and emergent phenomena [26,27]. The AB simulations used here have been described in detail within a previous publication where they were shown to capture much of the behaviour observed in actual experiments [16]. Briefly, the mechanical interactions between bacteria are handled as a soft sphere model where the overlap of two soft spheres causes a repulsive Hertzian force. To mimic a monolayer where bacteria are confined, a damping coefficient is added to account for the medium and surface friction (see Methods).

While this previous work focused on understanding the relatively rapid dynamics of the populations either fixating to a single species or establishing segregated communities, here we focus on the longer-term dynamics of the latter, quasi-stable communities (states we will refer to as “coexistence”). All simulations are initialized with one cell of each of the two competitor strains randomly seeded within a microchannel (Fig 1A) where they are allowed to grow and divide (Fig 1B). We then ignore all populations that fixate to a single strain within 24 hrs (Fig 1C). The remaining systems will all have reached coexistence with the vast majority segregated into two populations separated by a boundary that runs along the long axis of the channel (Fig 1D). Bacillus cells will tend to organize into aligned lanes that span the channel, while more coccus-like cells will display a less ordered arrangement. Occasionally, we observe segregation into 3 distinct populations (< 10% of simulations) or into 4 or more populations (< 1% of simulations), but we will neglect these configurations in our initial analysis.

We simulated competition between populations of cells with morphologies ranging from bacillus (pill-shaped) to coccus-like (round). The cells were modelled as rectangular segments of finite length l and 1 μm width with half-circular end caps 1 μm in diameter (see S1 Fig). This results in an initial length L of l + 1 μm that, for convenience, will be indicated by Ll+1. By increasing l, we move from coccus-like, to increasingly elongated bacillus cells. As an example, Fig 2A illustrates competition between coccus-like cells and bacillus cells (L1.0 and L2.5, respectively). Figure 2B and 2C display the rapid fixation of the systems to either a pure bacillus or coccus-like population, respectively, while Fig 2D displays the comparatively slower dynamics of the two populations within the coexistence state. Throughout, we will employ the fractional area, which is the total area of a population divided by the area of the microchannel, as a convenient measure of the population abundance. Note, however, that due to edge effects at the channel boundaries, this number can fluctuate above 1.

Fast versus slow fixation of dual competitive populations.

A) During coexistence, the boundary between segregated populations evolves through repeated invasion attempts by cells at the boundary. Coccus-like cells (blue), with less of a propensity to self-align, are more likely to push into the domain of bacillus cells (red). We can separate the competitive outcomes into B) and C) fast (< 24 hrs) and D) slow (> 24 hrs) fixation events. Fast fixations occur when one population out-competes the other in an effort to fill the chamber, while slow fixations result from the boundary dynamics between segregated populations. For this example, the bacillus cells never out-compete the coccus-like cells once coexistence is reached.

Here we are primarily interested in the dynamical process of fixation from the coexistence state, which is driven by the motion of the boundary separating the two populations. To illustrate this, Fig 3A displays a kymograph of the relative position of the boundary over time showing a series of discrete jumps that correspond to successful invasions into one or more lanes of the neighbouring bacillus population. These discrete jumps are also observable in a plot of the fractional area versus time. The takeover of the bacillus cells by the coccus-like cells occurs in a predictable way as illustrated in Fig 3B. First, coccus-like cells invade into the neighbouring lane of bacillus cells with successful invasions almost always occurring near the centre of the chamber. Then, as the invading cells divide, their progeny slowly push the bacillus cells towards the open ends of the chamber until all bacillus cells in that lane have been expelled. These steps repeat, interspersed with many unsuccessful invasion attempts, until the simulation ends or the coccus-like cells take over the entire chamber.

Since the timescale to fixation of a single lane, post invasion, is typically much faster than the translational motion of the boundary (Fig 3B), we may ignore the intra-lane fixation dynamics and approximate the boundary as moving in discrete jumps between lanes. Within this approximation, as we will show, the translational dynamics of the boundary may be modelled as simple drift-diffusive motion. By employing AB simulations to extract both the diffusion constants and drift velocities, for boundaries between cells of varying morphology, we first show that the model predictions agree well with AB simulations of systems that reach fixation from coexistence within about a week (corresponding, roughly, with the actual run-time of the simulations). However, our analytical model also enables us to predict the time to fixation for competitive systems when the fixation time becomes extremely long, making it difficult to assess through AB simulations alone.

Figure 4C summarizes the drift velocities (μ) and diffusion constants (D) extracted from simulations as a function of relative morphology (i.e., length ratios LX/L2.5), but only for those cases where we observed a significant number of lane changes, thus allowing us to reliably extract the diffusive parameters (see S2 Fig and S1 Table). Morphology is seen to provide a strong competitive advantage, with the drift velocity–-which largely determines the dynamics–-increasing by orders of magnitude as bacillus cells compete with increasingly coccus-like cells. Under these conditions, with the drift always promoting invasion into the bacillus population, the more coccus-like cells dominantly fixate (see S4 Fig). Conversely, any dynamics between comparable bacilli populations is exceedingly slow, effectively jamming the two populations into a coexisting state. For competition between dual coccus-like populations, as expected, the dynamics of the boundary are largely diffusive (for L1.0/L1.0, the drift and diffusion constants were 0.0066±0.0084 μm/hr and 0.0328±0.0030 μm2/hr, respectively). Note, very little dynamics were observed for more elongated, neutral systems, which is why we only present the results for L1.0/L1.0. Similar trends are observed if we maintain one strain as coccus-like and vary the morphology of its competitor by elongating those cells (see S7 Fig and S4 Table).

We next considered the effects of varying the relative division time between populations. We analyzed AB simulations of competition between elongated bacillus and coccus-like cells (L1.0/L2.5) keeping the mean division time of the coccus-like populations constant (60±6 minutes) while ramping up the division time of the bacillus cells and maintaining Gaussian noise of ±10% about the mean. In Fig 4D, we display the resulting drift-diffusion parameters as we decreased the relative division time (i.e., growth ratio tdcoccus/tdbacillus) by a factor of 2 (see S3 Fig and S2 Table).

While the drift velocity of the boundary decreased by orders of magnitude as the division time of the bacillus population doubled, the drift remained into the bacillus population, meaning that the coccus-like cells were still the population which dominantly fixated. A faster division rate, while not contributing to an increase in self-fixation, instead protected the bacillus cells for longer from colonization and eventual fixation by the coccus-like population. Coccus-like cells, attempting to colonize the bacillus population, are instead rapidly pushed to the boundary and ejected from the population before they are able to take over a new lane. In our simulations, we observed an exponential dependence of the drift of the coccus-like population as a function of division time (exponent α=−8), which illustrates how the rate of cell division can significantly alter population dynamics.

Discussion

In this study, employing agent-based simulations and mathematical modelling, we investigated the role of bacterial morphology in determining the stability of spatially structured microbial communities confined within a 2D monolayer. Within these dual-population systems, cells tend to spatially order into distinct lanes of a given cell type with competition driven by successful inter-lane invasions.

We find that rounder, more coccus-like cells consistently display an advantage over elongated, pill-shaped bacillus cells due to an increased affinity for invading neighbouring lanes, which more effectively drives the system toward fixation. This competitive advantage can be mitigated by increasing the growth rate of the bacillus cells. However, doing so does not promote fixation of the bacillus population; rather, it only protects the population from invasion and eventual fixation by the coccus-like competitor strain.

A simple one-dimensional drift-diffusion model for the boundary between populations provides an estimate for the long-term fixation dynamics. Here we employ this model to predict the fixation time for systems whose boundary dynamics become exceedingly slow, such that the populations form a jammed or quasi-stable equilibrium, which is the case for competition between similarly elongated bacillus populations. These slow dynamics are difficult to access through AB simulations alone.

One could argue that more sophisticated algorithms than those we employed might provide access to longer time scales. Still, we have shown that both morphology and division dynamics can greatly stabilize these mixed communities. The question remains, however, as to whether these competitive systems are ever truly stable such that the fixation time approaches infinity.

Likewise, while it should be possible to extract the drift and diffusion constants for these systems from a microscopic model alone, here we have relied on phenomenological AB simulations to infer these dynamical values. Indeed, when we vary the parameters in our AB simulations, these values quantitatively change. For instance, if we hinder the rotation of cells upon interaction with neighbouring cells, the boundaries are less dynamic and the systems tend to take longer to fixate (see S6 Fig and S3 Table). However, we do not expect these effects to change our qualitative observations or the trends we have discussed so much as the absolute values of the fixation times. More realistic AB simulations, that incorporate the microscopic interactions between cells and the environment, and which go beyond our simple soft-sphere hydrodynamic model, may provide further insight into these dynamical systems.

Collectively, our findings highlight the influence of cell morphology on the stability of competing bacterial communities. Morphological traits, through purely mechanical interactions, can strongly influence the long-term competitive outcomes and ecological trajectories of bacterial populations. Morphology can give rise to dynamic and spontaneous structure within bacterial communities, which can affect competitive dynamics in unexpected ways. Within these structures, seemingly obvious advantages–-such as being able to replicate faster–-may not always lead to the dominance of a population. Similarly, phenotypic traits such as cell morphology, in certain settings, may play a role in eradicating persistent populations resistant to other forms of treatment. These findings deepen our understanding of microbial ecology, provide valuable insight into how microbial communities form collective structure, and may lead to more predictive tools for exploring and managing bacterial community dynamics across natural and engineered ecosystems.

Methods

The AB model was primarily used to simulate a 2D rectangular environment with dimensions of 44 μm × 12 μm where cells are grown within a monolayer. The bacterial cells are modeled as agents of spherocylindrical shape (although, technically, only 2D) that grow to a maximal length at a given growth rate. Cells grow by exponentially increasing the length of the rectangular portion of the sphero-cylinder until the total length of the cell is twice the initial length, at which point the cell is split into two equal sized new sphero-cylinders (see S1 Fig). Physical contact between the cells happens when cells overlap with each other causing a repulsive Hertzian force (i.e., a soft sphere model) within a confined, damped environment. A damping coefficient ξ is is added to account for the liquid medium and surface friction from the confinement. This results in a set of equations for the translational and rotational motion of each agent (bacteria) within our simulation.

The full set of equations and specific parameters implemented in our AB model are provided in Ma et al. [16]. Each cell persists in the simulation until its centre exits the microchannel boundaries through either of the open ends. At this point, the cell is deemed to have exited the channel and is consequently eliminated from the simulation. In Ma et al. [16], the maximal length was drawn from a normal distribution of final lengths centered around twice the initial length. Here, we instead fix the final length; however, to make the cells asynchronous, we instead draw the growth rates from a normal distribution with a standard deviation of 10% of the cells growth rate around the defined growth rate. The agent-based model ignores the directional flow of the medium, which is consistent with other simulations in the field.

Comments to the Authors:

Reviewer #1: The work by Xhu Lim et al studies the influence of bacterial cell morphology and growth rate on the long-term competition between strains in wide 2D channels that are open on both ends. This particular confining geometry has been used by the same authors to study short-term dynamics of bacterial competition in previous works, both experimentally and theoretically. Here, they use agent-based simulations to study time scales not easily accessible experimentally. They previously demonstrated that rod-shaped bacteria tend to form lanes in the confining geometry that stabilizes their coexistence. Here, they demonstrate that rod-shaped cells are displaced by coccoid cells at long times, and they also show that increasing growth of one population increases its lifetime in the channel. Simulation results are coarse-grained using a one-dimensional drift-diffusion model that is then validated in terms of first-passage (population extinction) times.

Together this is a well written manuscript. All the conclusions are well demonstrated.

I only have one minor comment:

The AB simulation described in a previous paper (Ma et al PNAS 2024) contains a few parameters that might affect drift and diffusion, notably the randomization parameter for angular orientations of daughter cells. Can the authors show, for a selected combination of morphologies, how this parameter affects the experiment? And can they include an argument why they choose a particular parameter in the current simulation?

Reviewer #2: The manuscript by Lim et al. investigates two-strain competition of rod-shaped cells with different lengths in open-ended monolayer microchannels using agent-based simulations and an effective stochastic description of the boundary between competing populations. After an initial transient, many realizations enter a long-lived coexistence regime in which cells segregate into lanes, and eventual fixation proceeds through rare successful invasions of neighboring lanes. The authors coarse-grain this process into a one-dimensional drift-diffusion model for the population boundary, infer drift and diffusion coefficients from simulation data, and use mean-first-passage theory to estimate fixation times. Their main conclusion is that shorter, more rounded cells invade more effectively than elongated cells, whereas increasing the growth rate of the elongated population mainly acts defensively by slowing invasion rather than reversing the fixation bias. The manuscript is clearly written and the reduction to an effective boundary dynamics is potentially useful.

The topic is timely and relevant. There is substantial current interest in how cell shape, growth, steric interactions, confinement, and mechanics shape ordering, stresses, buckling, and competition in dense microbial populations. In that sense, this is in principle a nice and well-written paper. However, the scientific substance presently feels rather lightweight. The analysis is demonstrated for one fairly specific setup, and the generality of the proposed effective description remains unclear. The manuscript would also benefit from being placed more carefully in the context of both the mechanical-ordering literature and the literature on competition/fixation in spatially structured proliferating populations. These connections are currently underdeveloped and are important for interpreting the results. See below for details.

Major points:

1. If I understand correctly, the manuscript analyzes essentially one channel geometry supporting up to 12 lanes. This immediately raises the question what is meant to be universal here. Is the drift-diffusion description intended as a local effective model for interactions between neighboring columns, with coefficients that should be largely independent of the global channel width? Or are the inferred parameters expected to depend on the total number of lanes and thus on the global mechanical state of the system? Related statements in the paper, such as the distinction between fast and slow fixation or the possibility of subdivision into more than two domains, are also likely to depend on system width. The broader literature on proliferating rods in confinement (see point 4 below) suggests that ordering, buckling, and stress organization can be geometry-dependent, so some explicit exploration of width dependence, even at a modest level, would substantially strengthen the paper.

2. At present, the reduction to a drift-diffusion description remains largely phenomenological. However, in this class of systems there is already a substantial body of literature connecting orientational order, its breakdown, and the underlying mechanics of confined proliferating rods. In channels and related confined geometries, nematic ordering has been linked to stress anisotropy and geometric confinement, while free and weakly confined colonies exhibit microdomain formation and (dis)ordering through the interplay of steric alignment and growth-induced extensile stresses. More recent work has further explored the role of strain-rate and subtle particle-shape effects for the stability of ordered states (see point 4). This raises the question how the effective lane-invasion dynamics described here are related to these previously identified mechanical ingredients (buckling, stresses, etc.). Even if the authors cannot address all of these points quantitatively within the present manuscript, they should at least discuss them explicitly and, ideally, probe some aspects directly.

3. I am not sure that plotting the results as a function of a length ratio is the most natural representation. For growth-rate effects, a ratio is plausible because one indeed expects relative exponential growth to be the key control parameter. For cell geometry, that is less obvious. The current x-axis of Fig. 4C suggests that only the ratio matters, but do the authors really expect the absolute initial lengths to not matter? Considering the importance of alignment, steric torques, and packing, this seems unlikely to me. Maybe it would be clearer to show the actual absolute lengths on one axis, or add explicit tests where one population is fixed at 1.0 µm and the other is varied. The inset for the neutral 1.0 µm versus 1.0 µm case is also hard to compare quantitatively with anything else (the data point for 0.4 in the main panel is already an additional "vs. 1.0µm" case, correct?). Moreover, I was surprised that the inferred drift for the nominally neutral 1.0 µm versus 1.0 µm case still appears appreciable and with relatively small error bars; I would have expected zero drift to lie within the error bars if positive and negative drifts are accounted for correctly.

4. The relevant literature is currently a bit underrepresented/narrow, both on competition in spatially structured populations and on mechanically driven ordering in proliferating bacterial systems. The manuscript would benefit from being placed more carefully in the broader context of the field, showing that competition outcomes in dense microbial populations are shaped by mechanics, confinement, and geometry across a range of systems. Some (relatively random, but all not-yet-cited) examples include Karita et al., PNAS, 2022, in confined crypt-like chambers; Postek et al., PNAS, 2024, also on drift and selection channels; Coyte et al., PNAS, 2017, on spatial structure in porous environments; Nadell et al., eLife, 2017, on spatial competition in Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms; Giometto et al., PNAS, 2018, on physical interactions shaping selection; van den Berg et al., Nat. Comm., 2025 on the role of nematic alignment at expanding fronts. As background for complementary systems/mechanisms, there is also Hallatschek et al., PNAS, 2007, on genetic drift and sector formation at expanding frontiers, and Farrell et al., Journal of the Royal Society Interface, 2017, on how cell shape, friction, and colony mechanics affect surfing probabilities. Effective models have also been a topic in some of these publications. Of course it is up to the authors to decide what they want to include/highlight, but if overlaps/interactions/distinctions with respect to the literature were discussed in more detail (what does the discovered mechanism add/contribute?), this would also sharpen the focus of the manuscript.

At the same time, the paper also crucially relies on mechanically induced orientational order and its destabilization in monolayers of growing spherocylinders, and that side of the field should definitely be more thoroughly discussed. Relevant are, for example, Volfson et al., PNAS, 2008 on ordering; Boyer et al., Physical Biology, 2011 on the buckling instability; You et al., Physical Review X, 2018 on the geometry and mechanics of nematic microdomains; You et al., Science Advances, 2021 on confinement-induced self-organization; Isensee et al., Journal of the Royal Society Interface, 2022 specifically on the perfect ordering in confined populations of growing rods that is the basis for this study; Langeslay and Juarez, Soft Matter, 2024 on strain-rate-controlled alignment; and Isensee and Bittihn, Soft Matter, 2025 on the sensitive dependence on particle shape. This is of course a subjective selection and some might be more relevant than others, but I think mentioning/discussing some literature more thoroughly would be very helpful to provide both background and concrete mechanistic connections (see also points 1 and 2).

5. The drift-diffusion reduction is validated only rather lightly. On the model building side, it is not shown how well the fits used to extract drift and diffusion parameters actually fit the data (and whether deviations are simply finite size effects or physically meaningful). Fixation time predictions are directly compared with theory only for one specific parameter set, while slower cases are extrapolated from fitted coefficients. That is not unreasonable given the long simulations required, but then the paper should be more careful in how strongly it states that the model “works.” There are several additional quantities that could be compared without requiring prohibitively long simulations: fixation probability within a fixed observation window, the distribution rather than only the mean of fixation times, the probability that the “wrong” population fixates when drift is small, etc. Some deeper validation would be very helpful, and if discrepancies emerge, that would actually improve the paper by clarifying the limits of the effective description.

6. The authors refer readers to previous work and supporting information for the AB model, but given that the central claims concern mechanical mechanisms, the main text should briefly state the essential ingredients: Overdamped dynamics, steric force law, friction/mobility model, etc.. Without that, it is hard to judge which aspects of the conclusions are robust and which may depend on modeling details, a question which should also at least be discussed in the manuscript (for example, is more successful lane invasion of shorter cells exclusively a geometric phenomenon, or does relative mobility also play a role? That might depend on how these mobilities are chosen.).

Minor points:

1. Line 53: since the shapes differ mainly by length rather than width, “elongated” seems more suitable than “extended.”

2. Line 81: the geometry appears inconsistent as written; the end caps seem to have a *diameter* of 1 µm, not a radius of 1 µm, if they are meant to match a 1 µm-wide rectangular body.

3. Line 146: to eliminate ambiguity, “promoting invasion *into* the bacillus population” would read more clearly.

4. While it is mentioned in the model description that cell lengths are given in terms of their initial length just after division, it would help to repeat this in the relevant figure captions, since different conventions exist in the literature.

5. Fig. 4C: the information that one length is kept constant at 2.5 µm while the other is varied is missing from the plot/caption and currently only appears in the text.

6. Fig. 4D: the caption says “size ratios,” but should probably be growth-rate ratios.

7. Throughout the manuscript, the use of the word “coccus” is somewhat unusual and potentially misleading. In the model, all particles appear to be rodlike or capsule-like objects with varying aspect ratio; the shortest ones are not actual cocci in the biological sense (which could potentially go through different geometries during elongation/division). In many places it would be clearer to write “shorter cells,” “lower-aspect-ratio cells,” or “cells with shorter initial/division length.” instead of using "coccus" as an adjective like "more coccus", etc. ("coccus-like" seems fine to me).

* line 67 there appears to be a stray quotation mark in “dynamics of‘ the latter”;
