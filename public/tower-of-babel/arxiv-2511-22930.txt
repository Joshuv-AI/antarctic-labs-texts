Evidence for unexpectedly low quasiparticle generation rates across Josephson junctions of driven superconducting qubits
Byoung-moo Ann, Sang-Jun Choi, Hee Chul Park, Sercan Deve, Robin Dekker, Gary A. Steele, Jaseung Ku, Seung-Bo Shim, Junho Suh

Abstract
Microwave drives applied to superconducting qubits (SCQs) are central to high-fidelity control and fast readout. However, recent studies find that even drives far below the superconducting gap frequency may cause drive-induced quasiparticle generation (QPG) across Josephson junctions (JJs), posing a serious concern for fault-tolerant superconducting quantum computing. Here, we find experimental evidence that the actual QPG rates in strongly driven SCQs are remarkably lower than expected. We apply intense drive fields through readout resonators, reaching effective qubit drive amplitudes up to 300 GHz. The nonlinear response of the resonators enables quantification of the energy loss from SCQs into their environments, including the contribution from QPG. Even when conservatively attributing all measured dissipation to QPG, the observed energy dissipation rates are far lower than expected from the ideal QPG model. Meanwhile, calculations incorporating high-frequency cutoffs (HFCs) near 17–20 GHz in the QPG conductance can explain the experiments. These HFCs yield QPG rates a few orders of magnitude smaller than those without HFCs, providing evidence that the QPG rates are lower than predicted by the ideal model. Our findings prevent overestimation of drive-induced QPG and provide crucial guidance for operating superconducting quantum processors. Identifying the microscopic origin of the discrepancy opens new material and device opportunities to further mitigate QPG.

In this article, we experimentally investigate drive-induced QPG using superconducting transmon qubits

When a readout resonator coupled to a transmon is strongly probed, a prominent feature in its response is the reappearance of the bare resonator frequency.
We observe that the dissipation associated with this bare response decreases markedly as the probe power increases, over probe photon numbers ranging from 400 to
5\times 10^{5}

This behavior cannot be explained by existing nonlinear dissipation (ND) models.
This ND allows us to extract the energy loss occurring at the transmon, and any loss arising from QPG must be contained within this.

Comparing the experimentally observed energy loss with numerical results obtained using the Floquet–Markov formalism leads to a striking conclusion.
Even under the conservative assumption that all dissipation originates from QPG, the measured loss is far smaller than predicted by the ideal QPG model. Meanwhile, numerical simulations that include high-frequency cutoffs (HFCs) in the QPG conductance around 17–20 GHz explain the data. This indicates that the actual QPG rates are lower by a few orders of magnitude than those expected from the ideal QPG model without cutoffs.
We provide an interpretation of this discrepancy and discuss its practical implications in the last section.

Drive-induced energy loss in the transmon

None of the existing ND models for a superconducting resonator capture the observed ND presented in Fig.

Nonlinear dissipation in superconducting resonators typically arises due to the interactions with two-level system baths or kinetic inductance. However, the scale and tendency of the observed ND in Fig.
1

Moreover, both mechanisms are known to contribute negligibly to ND over the explored range of
N_{r}

Discussions

Our data, supported by both theoretical and numerical analyses, strongly suggest the actuall
J^{(\textbf{QPG})}(\omega)
is far less than expected
If the HFC model is correct, it indicates that QPG processes are not instantaneous but instead exhibit finite response times. Remarkably, to the best of our knowledge, this feature has not been explicitly addressed despite the long history of Josephson physics and decades of studies on dissipation in superconducting circuits.

Our findings prevent overestimation of drive-induced QPG rates and provide crucial guidance for the safe and efficient operation of superconducting quantum processors.
Furthermore, understanding the microscopic origin of these cutoffs could inform new fabrication strategies and device designs aimed at further suppressing QPG. This opens a pathway toward engineering superconducting circuits with intrinsically lower error rates and improved coherence for scalable quantum computing.

Several previous studies
Graaf-PRL-2013
;
Diamond-PRXQ-2023
;
Liu-PRL-2024
present experiments on QPG. Nonetheless, these studies focus on largely different directions rather than quantifying

See

for more discussion.

Methods

Device fabrication

The circuit consists of a transmon and co-planar waveguide resonators (CPWRs). Only the fundamental mode of one of the CPWRs is utilized and considered in the model. The base layer was patterned in a 100 nm niobium titanium nitride (NbTiN) film on a 525
\mu
m thick high-resistance silicon substrate. The Josephson junctions of the transmons are an Al-AlOx-Al structure fabricated using double-angle shadow evaporation. The overlapped areas of the constituent Josephson junctions are approximately
100\times 200~nm^{2}
. See
Supplementary Text 7
for detailed descriptions.

Theoretical methods

Floquet formalism

We define
\ket{\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(t)}=e^{i\alpha\omega_{d}t}\ket{\phi_{i}^{0}(t)}
and
\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha}=\epsilon_{i}^{0}+\alpha\hbar\omega_{d}
(
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
) as Floquet modes and corresponding quasi-eigenenergies of
\hat{H}_{q}(t)
with an Floquet order of
\alpha
.
Here,
\ket{\phi_{i}^{0}(t)}
and
\epsilon_{i}^{0}
are Floquet modes and corresponding quasi-eigenenergies in the first Brillouin zone.
We define
\ket{\phi_{i}(t)}=\ket{\phi_{i}^{0}(t)}
and
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{i}^{0}
in the main text.
For a Floquet mode
\ket{\phi_{i}(t)}
, we define
\overline{H}_{q}

\begin{split}\overline{H}_{q}=\frac{\omega_{d}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega_{d}}dt\bra{\phi_{i}(t)}\hat{H}_{q}(t)\ket{\phi_{i}(t)}.\end{split}

(5)

In the main text, we arrange Floquet modes such that
\ket{\phi_{i}(t)}
yielding small
\overline{H}_{q}
possess lower indexes
i
.
Detail explanations are provided in
Supplementary Text 2
.

Floquet-Markov master equation

We symbolize a specific transmon dissipation mechanism as ‘
\Psi
’ when the associated dimensionless operators describing the transmon–bath interaction are
\hat{\Psi}^{(l)}
.
We label each operators with
l
. We introduce
J^{(l)}(\omega)
, the normalized bath spectra (NBS) of the corresponding operators.
Then, Floquet-Markov formalism yields

\begin{split}\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\Psi)}=\sum_{l}{J^{(l)}(\Delta_{ij,k})|\hat{\Psi}^{(l)}_{ij,k}|^{2}},\end{split}

(6)

where

\begin{split}\hat{\Psi}_{ij,k}^{(l)}=\frac{\omega_{d}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega_{d}}dt\bra{\phi_{i}(t)}\hat{\Psi}^{(l)}\ket{\phi_{j}(t)}e^{-ik\omega_{d}t}.\end{split}

(7)

We take the zero-temperature limit (
\hbar|\Delta_{ij,k}|\gg k_{B}\Theta
,
k_{B}
and
\Theta
respectively refer to Boltzmann constant and the system temperature) in Eq
6
.
Cooperating all the mechanisms into consideration,
\Gamma_{ij,k}
and
\Gamma_{ij}
are given by

\sum_{\textup{$\Psi$}}\Gamma_{ij,k}^{\textup{($\Psi$)}}
and

\sum_{\textup{$\Psi$}}\Gamma_{ij}^{\textup{($\Psi$)}}
, respectively.
More detailed descriptions on the above are provided in
Supplementary Text 2
.

Below, we write the explicit forms of
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\Psi)}
for
\Psi=\textbf{rad},\textbf{diel}
, and
QPG
,

\begin{split}\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{rad})}~=&J^{\textbf{(rad)}}(\Delta_{ij,k})|\hat{n}_{ij,k}|^{2},\\
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{diel})}~=&J^{\textbf{(diel)}}(\Delta_{ij,k})|\hat{\varphi}_{ij,k}|^{2},\\
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{QPG})}=&J^{(\textbf{QPG}+)}(\Delta_{ij,k})|\sin{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|^{2}\\
+&J^{(\textbf{QPG}-)}(\Delta_{ij,k})|\cos{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|^{2},\end{split}

(8)

where the normalized bath spectra (NBS) therein are given as

\begin{split}&J^{\textbf{(rad)}}(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{Q_{\textbf{rad}}},\\
&J^{\textbf{(diel)}}(\omega)=\frac{\hbar\omega^{2}}{4E_{C}Q_{\textbf{diel}}(\omega)},\\
&J^{(\textbf{QPG}+)}(\omega)=\frac{16E_{J}}{h}\frac{S^{+}(\omega)}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega^{c}_{\textbf{QPG}+}}\right)^{2}\right)},\\
&J^{(\textbf{QPG}-)}(\omega)=\frac{16E_{J}}{h}\frac{S^{-}(\omega)}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega^{c}_{\textbf{QPG}-}}\right)^{2}\right)},\end{split}

(9)

and

\begin{split}&S^{\pm}(\omega)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}dxdy\frac{xy\pm 1}{\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}\delta(\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta_{\textup{Al}}}-x-y).\end{split}

(10)

In
J^{(\textbf{QPG}\pm)}(\omega)
, we introduce cutoff factors with cutoff frequencies
\omega_{\textbf{QPG}}^{c}
.
Please also recall that we hypothetically set
1/Q_{\textbf{diel}}(\omega)=(1/Q_{\textbf{diel}})e^{-\omega/\omega_{\textbf{diel}}^{c}}
.
Here,
\delta(x)
is a dirac-delta function and
\Delta_{\textup{Al}}
is the superconducting gap energy of aluminum.

S^{\pm}(\omega)
can be approximated as below

\begin{split}S^{\pm}(\omega)=0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~&(\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta_{\textup{Al}}}<2),\\
S^{+}(\omega)\approx\pi[1+(\omega+2)/4]~~&(2<\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta_{\textup{Al}}}\ll 4),\\
S^{-}(\omega)\approx(\pi/2)(\omega-2)~~~~~~~&(2<\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta_{\textup{Al}}}\ll 4),\\
S^{\pm}(\omega)\approx\omega~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~&(\frac{\hbar\omega}{\Delta_{\textup{Al}}}\gg 4).\end{split}

(11)

For calculating matrix elements
|\hat{n}_{ij,k}|
,
|\hat{\varphi}_{ij,k}|
,
|\sin{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|
, and
|\cos{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|
in
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{({\Psi})}
, we use the following relations

\begin{split}\bra{C_{n}}\hat{n}\ket{C_{m}}&=\delta_{nm},\\
\bra{C_{n}}\hat{\varphi}\ket{C_{m}}&=i\frac{(-1)^{(n-m+1)}}{n-m}~~(n\neq m),\\
&=0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~(n=m),\\
\bra{C_{n}}\sin{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}\ket{C_{m}}&=\frac{i}{\pi}\frac{(-1)^{(n-m+1)}(n-m)}{(n-m)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}},\\
\bra{C_{n}}\cos{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}\ket{C_{m}}&=\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{(-1)^{(n-m)}}{(n-m)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}},\end{split}

(12)

where
\delta_{nm}
is a Kronecker-delta symbol and
\ket{C_{m}}
is an eigenstate of
\hat{n}
with an eigenvalue
m
(
0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots
). The
i
in Eq.
12
is the imaginary unit.
Supporting discussions on the above statements are provided in
Supplementary Text 3
.

From the numerical simulation, we confirm
|\cos{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|^{2}\ll|\sin{(\hat{\varphi}/2)}_{ij,k}|^{2}
is satisfied except when
i=j
and
k=0
are satisfied.
However, these components never contribute to the
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{QPG})}
.
Therefore,
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{QPG})}
should be limited by
J^{(\textbf{QPG}+)}(\omega)
.
In the main text and other parts of this work, we only consider
J^{(\textbf{QPG}+)}(\omega)
to calculate
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\textbf{QPG})}
unless there are separate remarks. We define
J^{(\textbf{QPG}+)}(\omega)
and
\omega_{\textbf{QPG}+}^{c}
as
J^{(\textbf{QPG})}(\omega)
and

Experimental methods

Cryogenic configuration

The device is mounted on the mixing-chamber plate of a dilution refrigerator (Bluefors LD-400).
Input and output signals are sent through separate lines, with 10/10/10/20 dB attenuators installed at the 4 K, 700 mK, 100 mK, and MXC stages of the input line, and a 40 dB isolator (LNF-ISISC) placed at the MXC stage of the output line.
A HEMT amplifier (LNF-LNC) is positioned at the 4 K stage of the output line.
The device is enclosed in radiation and magnetic shieldings made of copper and cryoperm. Details are provided in
Supplementary Text 6
.

Measurement

Transmission spectroscopy is performed using a two-port network analyzer (Keysight E5071C).
We characterize the transmon with an integrated quantum control platform (Quantum Machines OPX-plus and Octave).
For power spectrum measurements, we employ a signal generator (Keysight E8257D) and a spectrum analyzer (Signal Hound SA-124AB).
The output signals from the refrigerator are amplified with a 30 dB power amplifier (Narda MITEQ LNA-30-04000800) before being sent to the room-temperature instruments.
Further details are provided in
Supplementary Text 6
.
