Phenotypic Spectrum and Diagnostic Challenges in Klippel‐Trenaunay Syndrome: A Case Series
Authors: Marya Hameed, Tooba Ali, Md Ariful Haque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKlippel‐Trénaunay syndrome (KTS) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by the triad of capillary malformations, venous varicosities, and soft tissue or bony hypertrophy. This case series highlights rare and atypical presentations of KTS, emphasizing the importance of a multidisciplinary diagnostic approach.Keywords: copper‐beaten skull, craniofacial asymmetry, hemifacial hypertrophy, Klippel‐Trénaunay syndrome, macrodactyly, preaxial polydactyly, vascular malformations

Introduction

Klippel‐Trénaunay syndrome (KTS) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by the clinical triad of capillary malformations (port‐wine stains), venous varicosities, and soft tissue or bony hypertrophy [1]. However, these features do not necessarily occur together in every patient, and the extent or combination of findings can vary substantially, which is particularly relevant to the phenotypic diversity described in this case series. First described by French physicians Klippel and Trénaunay in 1900, this condition was initially defined by its classic clinical triad. With advances in imaging and molecular genetics, particularly the discovery of somatic PIK3CA mutations, the understanding of KTS has evolved from a purely descriptive entity to part of the broader PIK3CA‐related overgrowth spectrum, refining both diagnosis and management [2]. In terms of demographic considerations, KTS affects both sexes, and most studies report no consistent gender predilection. A few reports have noted a slight male predominance; however, this has not been uniformly observed across cohorts [3]. It is a rare condition, with an estimated incidence between 2 and 5 in 100,000 live births, and has been reported across all racial and ethnic groups, with no specific racial predilection [4]. Although the exact etiology remains unclear, recent studies have identified somatic mutations in the PIK3CA gene, which regulates cell growth and proliferation [5]. KTS is now recognized as part of the PIK3CA‐related overgrowth spectrum (PROS), a group of disorders characterized by segmental overgrowth and vascular or lymphatic malformations, providing a molecular framework for its diverse phenotypic presentations.

KTS is often diagnosed in childhood, with clinical manifestations ranging from mild cutaneous lesions to severe limb asymmetry and functional impairment [6]. The syndrome is frequently associated with complications such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, chronic pain, and orthopedic deformities, necessitating early interventions and multidisciplinary care [7]. Recognition can be particularly challenging when patients present with incomplete or atypical features, such as craniofacial involvement, polydactyly, or prominent lymphatic malformations, which may mimic other overgrowth or vascular syndromes. Highlighting these uncommon presentations is therefore crucial to improving timely diagnosis and tailored management, which forms the rationale for this case series. Differential diagnoses include Parkes‐Weber syndrome (PKWS), CLOVES syndrome, Proteus syndrome, and NF1, each distinguished by unique clinical and radiological features [8].

Given that prognosis, consequences, and treatment approaches differ across different illnesses, it is imperative to clarify these distinctions.

Given the rarity of atypical phenotypes, we present three cases that underscore the heterogeneity of KTS and highlight the importance of tailored diagnostic approaches to improve recognition and management.

CASE: 1

Case Presentation

A 7‐year‐old male presented with a history of left‐sided hemihypertrophy of the body (Figure 1A) and right‐hand preaxial polydactyly (Figure 1B), with swelling of both hands and the left arm. Right‐sided hemifacial hypertrophy was also noted (Figure 1C). Additionally, there was a notable hemangiomatous lesion in the inguinal region (Figure 1D).

(A) Clinical photograph showing right‐sided hemihypertrophy of the left side of the body and limb asymmetry. (B) Preaxial polydactyly. (C) Right‐sided hemifacial hypertrophy. (D) Hemangiomatous lesion at the inguinal region. (E) Radiograph showing soft tissue swelling of the left arm with no bony abnormality. (F) Skull radiograph showing asymmetrical hemiatrophy of the left hemiface.

The patient exhibited no systemic complaints such as fever, weight loss, or neurological deficits. His medical history was unremarkable, and there was no known family history of similar conditions. Heart sounds were normal with no murmur, and there were no respiratory or intra‐abdominal abnormalities. Neurological examination was intact. On physical examination, asymmetrical hypertrophy of the right hemiface was evident. The right upper limb displayed macrodactyly involving the thumb, index, and middle fingers. At the same time, similar enlargement was noted in the thumb and index finger of the left hand (Figure 1B). A rudimentary sixth digit was present on the radial side of the right hand, consistent with preaxial polydactyly. The lower limbs were unaffected. Ophthalmologic examination revealed left‐sided slight ptosis, a myopic fundus on the left, and bilateral dysplastic optic discs, with more severe involvement on the right.

Laboratory investigations showed vitamin D deficiency, while insulin levels, thyroid function, and most lipid parameters were within normal limits. HDL cholesterol was below the reference range, while LDL cholesterol was slightly lower than average but not clinically significant. A skeletal survey demonstrated hemiatrophy of the left hemiface (Figure 1F), mandibular hypoplasia, and dentigerous abnormalities. Wormian bones were seen along the coronal, sagittal, and lambdoid sutures. Prominent convolutional markings diffusely present in the skull suggested a ‘copper‐beaten’ appearance (Figure 1F). In the absence of clinical or radiological signs of raised intracranial pressure, our team favored this finding to represent a normal anatomical variant rather than a pathological change. MRI brain imaging revealed significant asymmetry of the cerebral hemispheres, with hypertrophy of the left hemisphere. There was extensive dural venous sinus thrombosis involving the superior sagittal, transverse, and sigmoid sinuses (Figure 2).

Illustrating: MRI brain showing axial, coronal, and sagittal images, including post‐contrast, showing gross asymmetry in both cerebral hemispheres' volume, showing hypertrophy of the left hemisphere with enophthalmos and incidental coloboma. Additionally, there is extensive dural venous sinus thrombosis in the superior sagittal, transverse, and sigmoid deep venous sinuses.

Spinal imaging showed no significant abnormalities, with normal vertebral alignment and no evidence of scoliosis or segmentation defects. The pelvic bones and hip joints appeared well‐developed and symmetrical. The extremities exhibited asymmetric limb enlargement and macrodactyly, with associated small bony outgrowths, particularly in the bilateral index and middle fingers, more prominent on the left. Bone age assessment revealed delayed maturation, corresponding to a 5‐year‐9‐month‐old male child per the Greulich and Pyle method. A radiograph of the left arm (Figure 1E) showed soft tissue swelling without underlying bony abnormalities. An abdominal ultrasound showed bilaterally small‐for‐age kidneys but was otherwise unremarkable. The cardiac silhouette was normally positioned with a left‐sided apex, and the lung fields appeared clear. No signs of visceral enlargement or bowel obstruction were noted.

Given the constellation of findings, hemihypertrophy, soft tissue hemangiomas, macrodactyly, and preaxial polydactyly, a diagnosis of Klippel‐Trénaunay Syndrome (KTS) was strongly suspected. However, PKWS, which presents with true AVMs, remained a differential consideration. However, no clinical bruit was detected, and initial Doppler studies showed no evidence of arteriovenous shunting. Further angiographic evaluation was planned to definitively exclude AVMs.

CASE: 2

Case Presentation

A 2.5‐year‐old male was brought in for evaluation. The patient had a history of unequal leg lengths, first noted at four months of age, which resulted in a limping gait. His right foot also had misaligned toes. There was no relevant past medical or family history.

On examination, a small pigmented lesion was present on the left side of the face (Figure 3A). Multiple non‐tender swellings were observed on the head, right epigastrium, and left dorsal lumbar region (Figure 3B). Patchy areas of hyperpigmentation were also noted over the right lower limb and head (Figure 3C). The child showed right‐sided hemihypertrophy (Figure 3E) and mild soft tissue asymmetry of the right lower limb (Figure 3F).

(A) small pigmented lesion on the left side of the face. (B) Multiple non‐tender swellings were noted on the head, right epigastrium, and left dorsal lumbar region. (C) Patchy large hyperpigmentation. (D) Radiological findings from the skeletal survey revealed a normal skull shape. (E) Right‐sided hemihypertrophy. (F) Mild soft tissue asymmetry was noted in the right lower limb.

A skeletal survey revealed a normal skull shape (Figure 3D) and preserved skeletal alignment. To improve readability, the repetitive details of normal vertebral height, disc spaces, and absence of scoliosis were condensed as all parameters were within normal limits. Increased soft tissue shadows in the abdomen suggested possible hepatosplenomegaly, for which ultrasound correlation was recommended. Pelvic bones and hip joints appeared normal. Bone age was delayed, corresponding to 1 year and 3 months despite a chronological age of 2.5 years. Linear sclerotic metaphyseal bands were noted in the long bones, suggestive of a metabolic or hematopoietic disorder.

X‐rays of both legs showed mild expansion and cortical thinning of the right femoral mid‐shaft with slight lateral angulation, without periosteal reaction or fracture. MRI of the brain demonstrated mild leptomeningeal enhancement with no other abnormalities. Doppler ultrasound of the right leg showed normal vascular flow with no AVMs or thrombosis; though mild soft tissue hypertrophy was noted.

Laboratory results revealed hemoglobin 10 g/dL (reference range for age: 11–14 g/dL), indicating mild anemia, most consistent with a nutritional etiology. Other hematological and biochemical parameters were within reference ranges.

The differential diagnosis included NF1, Proteus syndrome, and KTS. NF1 was excluded due to the absence of café‐au‐lait spots and neurofibromas, which are hallmark features. Proteus syndrome was considered but ruled out, given the lack of progressive, disproportionate overgrowth and absence of cerebriform connective tissue nevi. The presence of hemihypertrophy, pigmented lesions, and soft tissue swellings favored a diagnosis of Klippel‐Trénaunay syndrome. Further vascular imaging and orthopedic follow‐up were advised.

CASE: 3

Case Presentation

A 1.5‐year‐old female child was brought to our facility with complaints of multiple body swellings and progressive enlargement of the left side of the body. There was no history of trauma, infection, or prior medical conditions.

On physical examination, the child had left‐sided hemihypertrophy and multiple soft‐tissue swellings. A port‐wine stain was observed, extending across the mid and lower left posterior thorax, covering nearly half of the back, without ulceration or secondary infection.

Contrast‐enhanced CT imaging revealed extensive multiloculated cystic abnormalities in the left paraspinal region, with extension into the ipsilateral hemipelvis (Figure 4). The cystic lesions demonstrated peripheral enhancement with fluid–blood levels.

Klippel‐Trenaunay syndrome: 1.5‐year‐old girl with multiple body swellings, left hemi‐hypertrophy, and port wine stain on the back. CT scan reveals extensive multi‐loculated peripherally enhancing cystic abnormalities in the left paraspinal region, showing fluid–blood levels consistent with lymphatic malformations with extension into the ipsilateral hemipelvis.

Histopathological analysis of biopsied tissue confirmed macrocystic, multiseptated lesions consistent with lymphatic malformations. Although immunohistochemical markers such as D2‐40 or Prox‐1 can strengthen diagnostic certainty, these were not performed in this case.

Taken together, the findings of hemihypertrophy, a large port‐wine stain, and extensive lymphatic malformations were most consistent with KTS. This case illustrates a lymphatic‐predominant phenotype, highlighting the wide phenotypic spectrum of KTS and underscoring the diagnostic challenges it poses in distinguishing it from other overgrowth and vascular malformation syndromes.

Discussion

KTS is a rare congenital vascular disorder with highly variable clinical presentations, ranging from mild cutaneous lesions to extensive limb or craniofacial involvement, including hemihypertrophy, preaxial polydactyly, craniofacial involvement, and vascular malformations. These features highlight the diagnostic challenges posed by KTS, particularly in differentiating it from other syndromes such as PKWS, CLOVES syndrome, Proteus syndrome, and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Case 1 further emphasizes the significance of a comprehensive approach to diagnosis and treatment, particularly in distinguishing KTS from other vascular malformation syndromes such as PKWS [6]. The patient's presentation with right‐sided hemifacial hypertrophy, mandibular hypoplasia, and dental anomalies aligns with previous reports of craniofacial involvement in KTS [9]. Craniofacial asymmetry in KTS is thought to result from asymmetric growth of bones and soft tissues, often accompanied by vascular malformations [10]. The presence of left‐sided ptosis and dysplastic optic discs further emphasizes the need for ophthalmologic evaluation in KTS patients, as ocular manifestations can significantly impact visual function and quality of life [11]. Ocular abnormalities are reported in approximately 10%–15% of patients, most commonly including choroidal hemangiomas, glaucoma, strabismus, and optic disc anomalies. Although infrequent, such findings can lead to significant visual impairment if not recognized early, reinforcing the need for routine ophthalmic screening in affected individuals. The preaxial polydactyly and macrodactyly observed in this case are rare but well‐documented features of KTS [12]. These anomalies likely arise from disrupted limb bud development during embryogenesis, potentially linked to somatic mutations in the PIK3CA gene, which regulates cell growth and proliferation [13]. Although specific molecular confirmation was not available in our patient, recent studies have demonstrated somatic PIK3CA mutations in a significant proportion of KTS cases, supporting its inclusion within the PIK3CA‐related overgrowth spectrum (PROS) [5, 13]. Thus, new molecular data support the hypothesis that limb abnormalities are linked to dysfunction of the PIK3CA pathway. Although complications such as venous thrombosis and functional impairment can arise over time [4].

Radiological findings, including the “copper‐beaten” appearance of the skull and delayed bone age, suggest possible intracranial hypertension or a normal variant, necessitating further neurological evaluation [14]. Given the patient's lack of neurological symptoms, the latter explanation seems more plausible, but close monitoring for signs of increased intracranial pressure is essential.

Although renal involvement is uncommon in KTS, the presence of bilaterally small‐for‐age kidneys in our patient is noteworthy. Previous reports have described isolated cases of renal hypoplasia and vascular anomalies associated with KTS [11], suggesting that renal manifestations, while rare, may occur secondary to vascular compromise or developmental asymmetry. In our patient, abdominal Doppler ultrasound was performed and did not demonstrate significant renal artery stenosis or abnormal flow patterns, though further contrast‐enhanced vascular imaging was recommended to rule out subtle anomalies.

The differential diagnosis of PKWS was considered due to the presence of limb hypertrophy and hemangiomatous lesions. However, the absence of AVMs on initial imaging supports a diagnosis of KTS [15]. The presence of AVMs can lead to cardiac failure—a complication not typically seen in KTS [16]. Further vascular imaging is warranted to confirm the absence of AVMs and rule out PKWS, which carries a higher risk of high‐output cardiac failure.

Patients with KTS may develop complications such as chronic venous insufficiency, thromboembolism, gastrointestinal bleeding due to visceral hemangiomas, and lymphatic malformations [17, 18, 19]. These complications were reflected within our series: the first case exhibited dural venous sinus thrombosis, illustrating the predisposition to venous thromboembolic events, while the third case demonstrated extensive lymphatic malformations with macrocystic morphology, representing another recognized but less common complication of KTS. Although none of our patients exhibited gastrointestinal or significant venous insufficiency, the spectrum of complications observed aligns with those previously reported in the literature. Early diagnosis and management are critical in KTS to address potential complications such as venous malformations, thrombosis, and functional impairment [17]. Additionally, the presence of low‐flow vascular malformations, such as port‐wine stains and venous varicosities, further supported the diagnosis of KTS. CLOVES syndrome, another important differential, is characterized by congenital lipomatous overgrowth, vascular malformations, epidermal nevi, and skeletal or spinal anomalies. While both KTS and CLOVES syndrome can present with asymmetric overgrowth and vascular malformations, the absence of truncal lipomatous masses and spinal anomalies in these cases favored a diagnosis of KTS [18]. Proteus syndrome, which shares features such as asymmetric overgrowth and vascular malformations, was also considered. However, the progressive nature of Proteus syndrome and the presence of cerebriform connective tissue nevi, which were absent in these cases, helped exclude this diagnosis [19]. NF1 was ruled out due to the lack of neurofibromas which is the hallmark feature of NF1 [20]. Complications such as chronic venous insufficiency, recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE), and gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding due to vascular malformations in the bowel are common in KTS and underscore the importance of early diagnosis and intervention [1]. Lymphatic involvement, though recognized as part of the Klippel‐Trénaunay spectrum, is often under‐reported and less well understood. Our third case demonstrated a lymphatic‐predominant phenotype characterized by macrocystic, multiseptated malformations—an atypical presentation that broadens the classical understanding of KTS as primarily venous‐capillary in origin [21]. Recent literature suggests that such variants fall within the continuum of PIK3CA‐related overgrowth spectrum (PROS) disorders, where lymphatic dysregulation represents a distinct pathogenic subset [22]. By documenting this lymphatic‐dominant form, our series contributes to the expanding phenotypic spectrum of KTS and underscores the diagnostic challenges of distinguishing it from isolated lymphatic malformation syndromes. These malformations can lead to complications such as recurrent infections, pain, and functional limitations, necessitating early intervention and long‐term monitoring [6]. The use of imaging modalities, such as contrast‐enhanced CT and MRI, is critical for delineating the extent of vascular and lymphatic involvement, involvement of neurovascular bundles and guiding treatment decisions [23]. Orthopedic, vascular, and ophthalmologic evaluations should be integrated into the care plan to optimize outcomes and improve quality of life.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case series highlights the phenotypic diversity of KTS and the importance of a thorough diagnostic workup to differentiate it from other vascular malformation syndromes. Accurate diagnosis of Klippel‐Trénaunay Syndrome requires a multimodal imaging approach that comprehensively evaluates vascular, soft‐tissue, and skeletal involvement. Such an integrated strategy is crucial for distinguishing KTS from overlapping overgrowth and vascular malformation syndromes. It raises important questions about the embryological and genetic mechanisms underlying KTS, which warrant further investigation. The pathogenesis of Klippel‐Trénaunay Syndrome is now believed to involve somatic activating mutations in the PIK3CA gene, which result in abnormal vascular and soft‐tissue development during embryogenesis. Recognition of this association has led to the inclusion of KTS within the PIK3CA‐related overgrowth spectrum (PROS), providing a clearer genetic explanation for its diverse phenotypic presentations. By integrating clinical findings with advances in molecular genetics, we can improve our understanding of KTS and develop more targeted therapies for affected individuals. In summary, the present series highlights diagnostically challenging and phenotypically diverse forms of Klippel‐Trénaunay Syndrome, including craniofacial involvement with venous sinus thrombosis, overlapping neurocutaneous features, and a lymphatic‐predominant variant. These findings broaden the recognized clinical spectrum of KTS and reinforce the need for heightened clinical awareness and careful differentiation from other overgrowth and vascular malformation syndromes.
