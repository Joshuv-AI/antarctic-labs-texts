Molecular neurobiological insights on integrative role of Trk neurotrophic receptors and their small-molecule agonists in neurodegenerative conditions
Authors: Megha Sanjai, Jeyaram Bharathi Jeyabalan, Vignesh Pandi Alagesan, Shruthi Laya Saravanan, James Premdoss Clement, Antony Justin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurodegenerative conditions signify an irreversible, progressive loss of neurons eventually resulting in a wide array of symptoms including cognitive decline, gradual loss of memory, compromised motor functions. Conventional therapies available principally attempt to either restore neurotransmitter levels, or activate its subordinate receptors; while underlying disease pathology of neurodegeneration being overlooked. This narrowed therapeutic approach ultimately results in irreversible, augmented neurodegeneration, leading to hastened disease progression, resulting in long-term complications, and impaired quality of life. This poses an imperative need for newer treatments to curb the progression of the disease. Neurotrophins; NGF and BDNF are proteins that are classically acknowledged during the development of vertebrate nervous system. Proneurotrophins and Mature neurotrophins activate their specific p75NTR and Trk receptors, which initiate intracellular neuronal survival signaling cascades that play an imperative role in maintaining survival of neurons, apoptosis, and synaptic plasticity. Such neuroprotective effects, native neurotrophins could mainly be a potent strategy in the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders. Due to their deprived drugability, neurotrophins failed to pass on further. Hence, small molecule neurotrophin mimetics that corresponds to its receptor domains might show promising results by increasing receptor-induced neuronal survival, differentiation, and initiation of downstream signalling pathways. Prospects that these small molecule mimetics possesses influential neurotrophic effects might as well lead to the application of such compounds that might be crucial to treat the underlying pathology of neurodegeneration that extends beyond the standard symptomatic treatments of neurodegenerative disorders.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) are multifactorial, age-related disorders that are primarily characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction and loss, leading to substantial global health and socioeconomic burdens. These conditions commonly manifest as cognitive decline, impaired motor function, and a spectrum of additional neurological deficits that vary according to the specific brain regions affected. According to the estimates of Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021 (1990–2021), neurological disorders continue to represent one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide (GBD 2021 Nervous System Disorders Collaborators, 2024). The global burden has progressively increased over the past decades, largely driven by age-related neurodegenerative conditions, while several communicable neurological disorders have shown a significant decline. The 2024 GBD analysis highlights that disorders affecting the nervous system collectively contributed to substantial morbidity and mortality, reinforcing the growing public health importance of understanding neurodegenerative mechanisms and developing effective therapeutic strategies (GBD 2021 Nervous System Disorders Collaborators, 2024).

Neurological disorders rank among the leading causes of both death and disability globally, constituting about 11.6% of total DALYs. Most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases are Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, Huntington’s, and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis representing a major global challenge (Pathak et al. 2022). These disorders impose high healthcare costs, increased patient suffering, and diminished quality of life due to worsening symptoms and associated progressive neurodegeneration associated with these disorders. Moreover, currently available therapeutics provide more symptomatic relief rather than addressing progressive neuronal loss. Despite the advancements in current research and numerous evaluations, many aspects of NDs remain obscure, that has made the drug discovery and development and biomarkers identification for early diagnosis challenging. The complexity of these diseases that combines both environmental and genetic predispositions, has also been a significant hindrance to develop effective treatment strategies (Magalingam et al. 2018). Interestingly, these neurodegenerative conditions, share common molecular pathologies that results in neuroinflammation, neuronal dysfunction and eventually leading to neurodegeneration, and cell death (Vassar and Zheng 2014).Current trends in research majorly focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms commonly involved in neurodegeneration, opening possible therapeutic interventions. Common targets include NMDA receptors, Sigma receptors, Voltage-gated Calcium Channels, and Neuronal Nitric Oxide Synthase; that are a part of complex signaling cascades within the neurodegenerative brain regions (Trippier et al. 2013). Though these advancements have proven to have promising effects in the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders, these options also majorly fail to address the underlying cause of neurodegeneration and fails to provide trophic support and a survival environment to the degenerative neurons.

Recent evidence suggest that Neurotrophin-based therapies might hold a significant promise for addressing the underlying mechanisms of neurodegeneration, offering a better disease modification and improved patient outcomes. To minimize the total illness load, improve patient quality of life and lessen suffering, the current publication discusses the possible function of neutrophins and neurotrophin mimetics as a treatment method that might be used in conjunction with the current therapy. The existence of neurotrophins, the regulators of survival of motor and sensory neurons was first established in 1949 and led to the characterization of Nerve Growth Factor (NGF), the earliest identified growth factor, and was also known as the “classical neurotrophin” family. Since the discovery, the potential role of neurotrophins is being extensively explored. The neurotrophin family comprehends Brain Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), Neurotrophin3 (NT3), Neurotrophin4/5(NT4/5). Neurotrophins, initially synthesized as pre-proproteins in the Endoplasmic Reticulum, where the signal peptides convert these into pro-neurotrophins. Dimerization and proteolytic processing of pro-neurotrophins through PCSK enzymes occurs at the trans Golgi network and secretory vesicles, respectively, before leaving the cell in their mature forms. These pro-neurotrophins bind and modulates two major neurotrophic receptors: p75 NTR and the Tropomyosin Receptor Kinase (Trk), which is also known as Neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptors. p75NTR, unselectively binds all the neurotrophins while it lacks known intrinsic enzymatic activity but recruits signaling adaptors that modulate molecular signaling via Trk receptors.

Meanwhile, Trk receptors are crucial for the neuronal cell biology and moderates several aspects that are neuronal development and function, neuronal differentiation, neuronal cell outgrowth and synaptic plasticity through neurotrophin-mediated signalling cascades. There are three neurotrophin receptors; TrkA, TrkB, and TrkC, that are largely confined to the body of neurons and plasma membranes at the axon terminals. These receptors are mainly activated through the specific neurotrophins’ binding; NGF-TrkA, BDNF, and NT4 -TrkB and NT3-TrkC resulting in the promotion of pro-neuronal survival cascades like Phosphatidylinositol-3-OH kinase (PI3K)/Akt kinase pathway, Ras/extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK) protein kinase pathway, and Phospholipase C-γ1 (PLC-γ1) pathway. Neurotrophin signaling is mediated not only by Trk receptors but also by the p75NTR, which plays a crucial modulatory role in determining neuronal fate. While mature neurotrophins preferentially activate Trk receptors to promote neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity, their precursor forms (proNGF and proBDNF) exhibit higher affinity for p75NTR, often inducing apoptotic and neurodegenerative signaling pathways. The balance between Trk-mediated pro-survival signaling and p75NTR-mediated pro-apoptotic signaling is therefore a critical determinant in the progression of neurodegenerative disorders (Lu et al. 2005).

Neurotrophins, receptors & structures

Nerve growth factor

Nerve growth factor (NGF) was discovered by Rita Levi-Montalcini through pioneering studies demonstrating that certain tumors release a diffusible factor that stimulates the growth and differentiation of sensory and sympathetic neurons. This groundbreaking work, conducted in collaboration with Stanley Cohen, led to the identification and biochemical characterization of NGF, earning them the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1986 (Cohen and Levi-Montalcini 1956).

NGF is a dimeric protein with complex structure, featuring three cysteine disulfides and two extended β-hairpins depicted in (Fig. 1A). Structurally, NGF has significant homology with insulins and proinsulin, suggesting an evolutionary relationship (Fahnestock and Shekari 2019). This is structural complexity underlies NGF’s varied physiological roles in survival, differentiation, and maintenance of cells. Its tertiary structure is held together by interactions between aromatic residues, whereas specific surface lysine residues are involved in receptor binding. NGF can bind lipids at its dimeric interface with residues such as Lys88, Tyr52, and Arg50 implicated in recognizing inositol group containing lipids (Canu et al. 2017). This dimeric nature allows simultaneous binding to TrkA receptors, inducing receptor dimerization and activation of downstream signaling pathways. The NGF receptor is a 427 amino acid protein with a highly ordered extracellular domain that contains four 40-amino acid repeats and six conserved cysteine residues. This motif confers high thermal and proteolytic stability, ensuring that NGF maintains its integrity and functional conformation in the extracellular environment. Flexible loop regions facilitate receptor adaptation, while disulfide bonds confer structural stability. The processing of pro-NGF to mature NGF further modulates receptor specificity and activity (Fahnestock and Shekari 2019).

Brain derived neurotrophic factor

BDNF is a neurotrophin which is very important for neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and neuroprotection (Schirò et al. 2022). Brain Derived Neurotrophic Factor gene structure is complex since it has multiple 5’ non coding exons and one 3’ coding exon, which enables tissue specific as well as stimulus -responsive expression of the gene. BDNF also exists as a tightly associated dimer with approximately 70% beta sheet and 20% beta turn content, which is crucial for its stability and bioactivity as depicted in (Fig. 1B). It dimerizes as homodimers or heterodimers with other neurotrophins, such as NT-3, which influences receptor binding and signaling(Schirò et al. 2022). BDNF mainly binds and activates TrkB receptor and thereby activates the downstreaming signaling pathways; MAPK, PLCγ and PI3K (Schirò et al. 2022; Toader et al. 2025). In addition, specific hydrophobic and polar interactions in BDNF’s receptor-binding regions ensure high-affinity binding to TrkB; interactions with p75 NTR modulate the activity of BDNF, balancing survival and apoptosis pathways. Flexible loop regions enhance receptor engagement adaptability, while disulfide bonds provide structural integrity. Expression of BDNF transcripts is controlled by factors such as neuronal activity, DNA methylation, and histone deacetylation.

NT-3,4,5

Neurotrophins, among which are NGF, BDNF, NT-3 and NT-4/5 are a family of structurally related proteins known to promote neuronal survival and differentiation. The NT-3 crystal structure was found to be a homodimer with a twisted four stranded beta sheet and interposed disulfide bonds as in other neurotrophins which is crucial for stability and biological function depicted in (Fig. 1C). The flexible loop regions and specific hydrophobic and polar interactions in these neurotrophins enable precise receptor binding, while their interactions with p75NTR modulate survival versus apoptotic pathways. These proteins interact specifically with high affinity receptors and with a common low affinity receptor that affects the survival and morphology of neurons (Rodríguez-Tébar et al. 1991). In vitro studies show that NT-4/5, BDNF, and NT-3 enhance the morphological differentiation of dopaminergic neurons, with NT-4/5 having the most pronounced effects on neuritic growth and complexity (Studer et al. 1995).

Neurotrophin mediated signaling pathways play a central role in maintaining neuronal integrity, and disruptions in these pathways are dependably associated with the progression of neurodegeneration. Growing scientific evidence suggests that the neuroprotective actions of Trk receptors may offer a valuable therapeutic avenue for conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis (MS), and Huntington’s disease. Functions of Trk receptors in neurodegenerative disorders and their molecular mechanism

The p75 neurotrophin receptor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{p}{75}^{\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{R}}$$\end{document}): a central nexus in neurodegeneration

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}is a multifunctional receptor belonging to the tumour necrosis factor receptor superfamily, distinguished by its ability to act as a context-dependent signalling hub rather than a linear mediator of cellular responses (Siddi et al. 2026). Unlike Trk receptors, which predominantly promote survival and differentiation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}integrates a broad spectrum of extracellular cues, including mature neurotrophins, pro-neurotrophins, and pathological ligands such as amyloid-β (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:A\beta\:$$\end{document}) to regulate divergent cellular outcomes such as neurite extension, synaptic remodelling, or apoptosis (Kraemer et al. 2014). Importantly, its signalling is not intrinsic but emerges from a highly dynamic interplay among ligand type, co-receptor availability, and intracellular adaptor recruitment. In the healthy adult nervous system, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}expression remains low and spatially restricted; however, it is markedly upregulated under conditions of neuronal stress, injury, and chronic inflammation. Structural architecture and molecular composition

The functional plasticity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}arises from its modular structural organisation, which enables it to serve as a versatile signalling platform. The p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) is a single-pass transmembrane protein characterized by a modular structure consisting of extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular regions (Almeida and Duarte 2014) depicted on Fig. 2. The extracellular domain contains four conserved cysteine-rich domains (CRD1–CRD4), which are stabilized by disulfide bonds and form a compact, folded architecture that provides structural stability and a defined ligand-binding surface (Lin et al. 2015).The receptor is anchored in the membrane by a single hydrophobic α-helical transmembrane domain, which also contributes to receptor dimerization and structural integrity. On the cytoplasmic side, the intracellular domain includes a well-defined death domain composed of six α-helices arranged in a conserved fold. Overall, p75NTR displays structural asymmetry, with a rigid extracellular ligand-binding region and a more flexible intracellular domain, supporting its ability to form different receptor complexes.

Differential signaling: the pro-neurotrophin and mature neurotrophin dichotomy

The signaling output of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}is critically determined by the molecular state of its ligands, establishing a functional dichotomy between pro-neurotrophins and mature neurotrophins. Under pathological conditions, impaired extracellular proteolysis leads to the accumulation of pro-neurotrophins, which preferentially bind to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}–Sortilin complex and initiate a robust apoptotic signaling cascade (Sun et al. 2013). This pathway involves the recruitment of adaptor proteins such as NRIF and TRAF6, followed by activation of upstream kinases including ASK1 and MKK4, ultimately leading to the activation of stress kinases JNK and p38 (Guo et al. 2017). These kinases activate transcription factors such as c-Jun, driving the expression of pro-apoptotic genes and culminating in neuronal death. Oxidative stress further amplifies this pathway by enhancing ASK1 activation, thereby establishing a feed-forward loop that accelerates neurodegeneration (Meeker and Williams 2015).

In contrast, mature neurotrophins engage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}in a manner that can promote survival and neuronal plasticity, although these effects are highly dependent on cellular context and receptor composition. Activation of TRAF6 and RIP2 can stimulate NF-κB signaling, leading to the transcription of anti-apoptotic and pro-survival genes, while recruitment of RhoGDI inhibits the small GTPase RhoA, facilitating neurite outgrowth and cytoskeletal remodeling (Bothwell 2016). When co-expressed with Trk receptors, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}enhances ligand affinity and signaling specificity, acting as a co-receptor that amplifies survival pathways. Thus, rather than functioning as a simple binary switch, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}operates as a finely tuned integrator that modulates signaling intensity and direction based on environmental and molecular cues (Franco et al. 2021).

Functional roles in neurodegeneration

p75NTR serves as a critical modulatory receptor across multiple neurodegenerative disorders, with a particularly prominent pathogenic role in Alzheimer’s disease. In Alzheimer’s disease, it plays a prominent pathogenic role by interacting with amyloid-β and proneurotrophins, leading to neuronal apoptosis, synaptic loss, and cholinergic degeneration (Perini et al. 2002). In Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, its expression is upregulated in injured motor neurons and Schwann cells, serving mainly as a marker of degeneration and regeneration rather than a primary cause (Moss and Saxena 2025; Copray et al. 2003). In Multiple sclerosis, p75NTR is associated with oligodendrocyte injury and remyelination, reflecting a role in repair processes (Zhang et al. 2011). In Parkinson’s disease, it contributes to dopaminergic neuron vulnerability under stress conditions, acting as a modulatory factor (Ali et al. 2024). Similarly, in Huntington’s disease, dysregulated neurotrophin signaling involving p75NTR contributes to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration (Brito et al. 2014). (Simmons et al. 2016) A key feature of its involvement is its interaction with amyloid-β peptides, which act as non-canonical ligands capable of directly binding to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}and mimicking pro-neurotrophin signalling. This interaction activates JNK-dependent apoptotic pathways, thereby providing a direct mechanistic link between extracellular amyloid accumulation and intracellular neuronal death. Beyond its role in apoptosis, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}significantly contributes to synaptic dysfunction by mediating proBDNF-dependent synaptic pruning, enhancing long-term depression, and promoting dendritic spine loss, all of which are strongly correlated with cognitive decline (Yang et al. 2014). Additionally, the receptor is deeply involved in neuroinflammatory crosstalk, as inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β upregulate its expression, while activated microglia increase the release of pro-neurotrophins (Danelon et al. 2023). This establishes a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and degeneration. Furthermore, through its interaction with myelin-associated inhibitors such as Nogo-A, MAG, and OMgp, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}mediates growth cone collapse and inhibits axonal regeneration, thereby contributing to the failure of neural repair mechanisms (Akbik et al. 2012; Lee and Zheng 2012). Collectively, these roles position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}as a central integrator of amyloid toxicity, synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and impaired regeneration in neurodegenerative diseases.

Translational potential and therapeutic perspectives

Given its central role in orchestrating neurodegenerative signaling, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic target with multiple translational applications. One important avenue lies in biomarker development, as the extracellular domain of the receptor is shed following proteolytic cleavage and can be detected in cerebrospinal fluid, plasma, and urine. Elevated levels of this fragment, along with altered ratios of pro-neurotrophins to mature neurotrophins, have been correlated with disease progression, highlighting their potential as non-invasive indicators of pathological signaling activity (Wu et al. 2026). From a therapeutic standpoint, current strategies are increasingly focused on selective modulation rather than complete inhibition of receptor function. The development of biased ligands aims to block the binding of pro-neurotrophins and amyloid-β while preserving or enhancing survival-promoting pathways such as NF-κB activation and RhoA inhibition (Greenwood et al. 2018). Targeting co-receptor interactions, particularly the disruption of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}–Sortilin complex, offers a means to selectively inhibit apoptotic signaling without affecting beneficial pathways. Modulation of receptor proteolysis, including the regulation of α- and γ-secretase activity, represents another promising strategy, although it requires careful optimization to avoid off-target effects associated with these enzymes (MacLeod et al. 2015). Additionally, targeting intracellular adaptor proteins such as NRIF or TRAF6 provides an opportunity to fine-tune downstream signaling responses (Akyol et al. 2018). Emerging therapeutic approaches are also exploring combination strategies, integrating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}modulation with anti-amyloid and anti-inflammatory therapies, as well as advanced approaches such as AI-guided ligand design to achieve pathway-specific modulation. In summary, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p{75}^{NTR}$$\end{document}functions not merely as a receptor but as a molecular decision-making node that integrates neurotrophic, inflammatory, and proteotoxic signals. In neurodegenerative diseases, this balance is shifted toward degeneration; however, targeted and selective modulation of its signaling pathways offers a compelling strategy to redirect cellular responses toward neuronal survival, plasticity, and repair, thereby opening new avenues for disease-modifying therapies.

Trk receptors

The proximal immunoglobulin (Ig) domain of each Trk receptor has been identified as the most significant location for interactions with neurotrophins (Huang and Reichardt 2001a). The neurotrophins binding might initiate a wide range of cellular survival responses. The type of response is determined by the combination of neurotrophic receptors (TrkA and B) present on the cell membrane and distinctive set of intracellular effectors, upon activation leading to phosphorylation of evolutionarily conserved tyrosine present in the cytoplasmic domains of each receptor.

TrkA is activated by phosphorylation at Y490 as it recruits the adaptor protein Shc and Frs2 to activate Ras/MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways. Tyrosine residue phosphorylation of Y785 activates PLC-γ, causing the elevation of intracellular calcium leading to PKC activation and subsequent neurite outgrowth and differentiation. TrkB, activated by BDNF and NT-4/5, undergoes autophosphorylation at residues Y706, Y710, Y713, and Y716, enhancing kinase activity. Y515 recruits Shc and Grb2 for Ras/MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling, and Y816 activates PLC-γ, critical for synaptic plasticity. NT-3 activates TrkC, phosphorylating at Y674, Y676, and Y679 to enhance catalytic activity; Y516 recruits’ adaptors like Shc for Ras/MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling, and PLC-γ activation promotes differentiation and survival, particularly in proprioceptive sensory neurons. Such pathways underscore the neuroprotective roles of Trk receptors in conditions such as Alzheimer’s, ALS, Parkinson’s, multiple sclerosis, and Huntington’s disease.

The crystal structures of the ligand binding domains of Trk A, Trk Band Trk C receptors exhibit an immunoglobulin- like fold characteristic of the I-set within the immunoglobulin superfamily. These domains essential for neurotrophins binding, feature distinctive structural elements, including an exposed disulfide bond connecting B and E strands, as well as variability in loop regions, particularly the DE loop, which plays a key role in determining ligand specificity. Structurally, Trk A-d5, Trk B-d5, Trk C- d5 display a β-sandwich arrangements of β-strands, with residues in the AB, EF and CD loops forming the ligand – binding interface. Although isolated Trk receptor D5 domains have been observed to crystallize as symmetric dimers, these crystallographic dimers should be interpreted cautiously (Banfield et al. 2001; Wiesmann et al. 1999) Such dimer arrangements often arise from crystal-packing forces and therefore do not, by themselves, represent the physiologically relevant dimeric state of the receptor. This does not contradict the established requirement for ligand-induced dimerization in Trk receptor activation. Physiological activation of Trk receptors occurs when dimeric neurotrophins such as NGF, BDNF, NT-3, and NT-4/5 cross-link two Trk ectodomains in a defined geometry that promotes downstream signaling (Deng and Li 2015). Structural and biochemical studies demonstrate that neurotrophin binding induces the proper orientation of extracellular domains, which is then coupled to conformational rearrangements across the juxta-membrane and transmembrane regions, ultimately enabling the intracellular kinase domains to form an active, signaling-competent dimer (Hernández-del Caño et al. 2024; Huang and Reichardt 2001b; Lewin and Barde 1996); Huang and Reichardt 2001; Lewin and Barde 1996). Therefore, while D5 dimers observed in crystal structures may suggest potential receptor–receptor interfaces, definitive evidence for functional dimerization requires ligand-bound full-length receptor data, biophysical validation, and signaling assays. This clarification has been incorporated to reconcile structural observations with the well-defined mechanism of ligand-driven Trk receptor activation. Pathways and residues

Neurotrophin after binding to Trk receptors triggers a cascade of intracellular signaling pathways that collectively protect neurons from diverse neurodegenerative insults. The PI3K/Akt pathway is a principal mediator of neuroprotection; Akt activation inhibits glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β), thereby reducing tau hyperphosphorylation and mitigating neurofibrillary tangle formation, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease pathology. Beyond GSK3β, Akt phosphorylates pro-apoptotic factors such as BAD, caspase-9, and FOXO family transcription factors, suppressing apoptosis triggered by reactive oxygen species (ROS). Furthermore, Akt enhances mitochondrial resilience by promoting hexokinase-2 association with the mitochondrial membrane, which stabilizes mitochondrial function, and by upregulating antioxidant enzymes including superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and catalase, thus attenuating oxidative stress-induced damage (Liu et al. 2014; Komiyama et al. 2002; Song et al. 2005).

Parallel activation of the ERK1/2 (extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2) pathway supports synaptic maintenance and neuronal survival. ERK signaling increases expression of glutamate transporters such as EAAT2 (also known as GLT-1) in astrocytes, facilitating glutamate clearance and preventing excitotoxic accumulation. It also modulates the composition of NMDA receptor subunits at synapses, favoring NR2A-dominant configurations associated with pro-survival signaling and reduced calcium overload. The PLCγ (phospholipase C gamma) pathway, activated downstream of Trk receptors, regulates intracellular calcium levels by modulating release from internal stores and buffering calcium influx, attenuating the excessive Ca2+ entry that is a key driver of excitotoxic cell death.

Together, these signaling events provide an integrated defense by limiting oxidative stress, stabilizing mitochondrial function, fine-tuning glutamatergic neurotransmission, and modulating tau phosphorylation dynamics. This multifaceted neurotrophic network highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting neurotrophin pathways to slow or prevent neurodegenerative progression.

Ras pathway

The activation of the Ras pathway is initiated by the phosphorylation of Y490 tyrosine residue in TrkA and Y515 in TrkB receptors, creating a binding site for the Phosphotyrosine-binding domain of the adaptor protein. Upon phosphorylation, Shc binds Grb2 (adaptor protein), which is associated with SOS, a Ras exchanger. Ras is subsequently triggered by various extracellular stimuli, which in turn triggers the RAF-MEK-ERK cytokine kinase cascade. Phosphorylated Erk1/2 subsequently engages in a minimum of two signaling cascades; it may phosphorylate the kinase Rsk or Erk1/2 may translocate to the nucleus to phosphorylate the transcription factor Elk-1. This phosphorylation enables interaction with auxiliary transcription factor SRF, which then binds to SRE (serum response element) in c-fos promoter region, facilitating the commencement of transcription. The phosphorylation of Rsk results in translocation to the nucleus and the subsequent phosphorylation of CREB at serine 133. Phosphorylated CREB associates with CPB, a transcriptional co-activator protein which concurrently interacts with the SRF-Elk complex, therefore forming an extensive transcription factor complex that facilitates c-fos gene transcription. Consequently, this leads to the preservation of synaptic plasticity, neuronal differentiation, and proliferation (Komiyama et al. 2002).

PI3K pathway

Binding of neurotrophins to the Trk receptors results in the phosphorylation of Y490 and Y515 present in the juxta membrane region of the receptors. This phosphorylation further results in the activation of two adaptor proteins Shc and frs2. These activated proteins will then recruit PI3K protein which phosphorylates Phosphoinositol 3 phosphate (PIP3) followed by phosphorylation of Akt at Ser 473, which further phosphorylates GSK3β to inactivate the GSK3β proteins at Ser9. Activated GSK3β acts as a critical kinase for tau protein phosphorylation hence, inactivation becomes crucial to impede further tau phosphorylation. In addition to diminished tau phosphorylation, the production of reactive oxygen species within mitochondrial complex due NFT formation is also attenuated ultimately resulting in scaling down of neuroinflammation. Similarly, inhibition of GSK3β results in the suppression of NF-kβ’s translocation into nucleus resulting in the nullification of NF-κβ-dependent pro-inflammatory cytokine genes transcription. Alongside, activation of PI3K/Akt pathway also induces the expression of IL-10 and IL-1 receptor antagonist, promoting a comprehensive anti-inflammatory effect (Minnone et al. 2017).

PLC-γ pathway

The phosphorylation of tyrosines Y785 and Y816 on TrkA and TrkB activates PLC-1, which hydrolyses Prostaglandin (4,5)- di phosphate, resulting in the production of inositol tris-phosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). IP3, then facilitates the release of Ca2+ from intracellular reserves, leading to the activation of enzymes, including Ca2+ regulated isoforms of protein kinase-C and Ca2+ calmodulin regulated protein kinases. DAG subsequently activates DAG-regulated Protein Kinase C-γ, which is essential for neurite outgrowth and the activation of Erk1/2 (Niewiadomska et al. 2011). TRK receptors agonism and neurodegenerative diseases

Currently available therapies for neurodegenerative disorders provide symptomatic relief and rather do not focus on neuronal loss due to various underlying pathologies leading to neurodegeneration. Activation of Trk receptors is reportedly found to be effective in various neurodegenerative disorders like, Alzheimer’s, Parkinsons’s, Huntington’s disease, Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, and Multiple Sclerosis. Targeting multiple events associated with neurodegeneration could positively be regulated via small molecule Trk ligands. Notable role of Trk agonism in various neurodegenerative condition are emphasized below.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

AD is the most reported neurodegenerative disorder, affecting the elderly population that accounts for 60–80% of dementia worldwide (Mast and Yochim 2017; Sahab and Ashraf 2019). AD is primarily characterized by progressive memory loss and cognitive impairment due to persistent neurodegeneration marked by the presence of amyloid plaques (Aβ) and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) within the brain (Sahab and Ashraf 2019). These lesions are accompanied by neuronal and synaptic loss, cerebral amyloid angiopathy and neuroinflammation (Jellinger 2020; Serrano-Pozo et al. 2011). Other pathological mechanisms include cholinergic impairment, oxidative stress, and glutamate mediated excitotoxicity. Clinicopathological correlations suggest that the formation of amyloid plaques occurs before cognitive deficits, whereas neurofibrillary tangles and synaptic loss parallel to cognitive decline. As the disease progresses, the symptoms worsen, with loss of basic memory and cognitive functions resulting in severe cognitive decline and eventually resulting in death while the accumulation of amyloid-beta peptide is believed to initiate a cascade of events leading to neuronal dysfunction. Moreover, altered expressions of TrkA isoforms were observed in the striatum of AD patients where the decrease in TrkA expression was quite heterogenous (Jellinger 2020; Perl 2010; Finder 2010). These findings collectively suggest that dysregulation of Trk receptors may contribute to the pathogenesis of AD, possibly through compromised neurotrophin signaling and reduced neurotrophic and protective effects on the cholinergic projections of AD affected brain.

Recent studies are investigating the therapeutic potential of small molecule TrkA and TrkB agonists in AD. Selective TrkA agonists have shown to restore cholinergic function, decrease amyloid pathology, and increase hippocampal neurogenesis in AD mouse model (Xhima et al. 2020, 2022), Similarly, TrkB agonists and its optimized derivative have shown to enhance spatial memory, increase thin spine density and reduce AD progression in validated in vivo studies (Castello et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2021). These findings suggest that modulating TrkA and TrkB receptors through small molecule neurotrophin mimetics might be a promising method to enlighten new disease modifying treatments for AD.

Parkinson’s disease (PD)

PD is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by its cardinal motor symptoms; bradykinesia, muscle rigidity, resting tremors, and postural instability. The etiology of PD is majorly multifaceted, with genetics, autoimmune, traumatic brain injury and environmental factors contributing to the development of the disorder. In addition, to its cardinal motor symptoms, non-motor features like olfactory dysfunction, impaired cognition, mood disturbances and REM sleep behavior disorders are also observed in PD patients together affecting their quality of life. The pathophysiology of PD is multiple molecular mechanisms, involving disparity in neurotransmission systems (Cholinergic, Serotonergic) mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, protein misfolding, presence of lewy bodies, augmented α-Syn expression and neuroinflammation. However, loss of dopamine and dopaminergic neurotransmission within the Substantia Nigra pars Compacts (SNpC) brain region is the principal hallmark pathology of the disease resulting in motor and non-motor clinical manifestations (Kaur et al. 2019; Moore et al. 2005; Smith et al. 2012). α-Syn protein encoded by the SNCA gene causes the formation of lewy bodies and interferes with normal neuronal signaling (Kaur et al. 2019). Deficiency in dopamine storage and metabolism results in the production of free radicals and reactive oxygen species that results in the worsening of the disease progression. While, the mutations in genes, such as DJ-1, LRRK2, PARKIN and PINK1contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and neurotoxicity (Kaur et al. 2019).

Currently the existing therapies are symptomatic but no therapies available are known to have neuroprotective properties and that could be capable of slowing down or reversing disease progression. Alongside, several major challenges remain in place for a defined management of the non-motor symptoms associated (Smith et al. 2012). Current trends in neurodegenerative research focuses on finding biomarkers for the early diagnosis and developing more potent therapeutic alternatives (Albin 2022).The involvement of neurotrophins, neurotrophic receptors (TrkA, B) and small molecule neurotrophin mimetics are now being investigated for their therapeutic potency to slow down or reverse the disease progression. It is identified that the BDNF-Trk B pathway is involved in neuroprotection and neuronal maintenance and it has also been reported that this neuronal survival pathway is fundamentally downregulated in the SNpC brain region in PD patients. Similarly, activation of TrkA in the Basal Forebrain region might mitigate dementia like symptoms and other non-motor complicated associated to PD; Jeyaram and Antony 2022). Modulating Trk receptors’ signaling cascade through the development of small molecule agonists represents a convincing therapeutic alternative towards the attenuation of disease progression and mitigating the pathological outcomes (Palathoti et al. 2023). Hence, this therapeutic approach might possess the potential to intensify neuroprotection, and promote neuronal survival, thereby offering significant therapeutic benefits in PD like neurodegenerative and other debilitating conditions.

Huntington’s disease (HD)

HD is a rare neurodegenerative disorder characterized by psychiatric disturbance, chorea, and progressive cognitive decline(McColgan and Tabrizi 2018); Roos 2010) (McColgan and Tabrizi 2018; Roos 2010). It is caused by an expanded CAG repeat in the Huntington Gene (HTT gene) on chromosome 4, with longer repeats this disease is associated with earlier onse t(Roos 2010). The pathophysiology of the disease involves multiple mechanisms such as excitotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress (Gil and Rego 2008).Understanding these complex pathogenic pathways is important for the development of effective therapeutic strategies for HD. Alterations in synaptic and receptor activity in striatal and cortical neurons are time-dependent and region specific. Predominantly mutant HTT gene has multiple effects on cellular processes through disturbing proteostasis, transcription, and mitochondrial function (McColgan and Tabrizi 2018). While the macroscopic changes start in the striatum and spread to the cortex resulting in the irreversible progression of the disease condition. At the same time, HD, being associated with severely impaired neurotrophic support, especially BDNF and NT-3 majorly contributes to the anchored pathogenesis of the disease(Simmons et al. 2023).

At present, there are no disease-modifying therapy available that enhance the neuronal survival; however, several promising therapies targeting mutant huntingtin lowering (antisense oligonucleotides), are currently under clinical trials. However, a promising therapeutic approach for HD can be achieved through selectively targeting TrkB, and TrkC, receptors. Small molecule Trk receptor agonists and selective antibodies (Simmons et al. 2023) capable of activating TrkB and TrkC receptors have shown promising effects in the management of HD and such related neurodegenerative changes and motor dysfunction in suitable in-vivo rodent models (Forsell et al. 2024). These strategies are intended to promote pro-neuronal survival, signaling and block neurodegenerative cascades associated with HD.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

Amyotrophic lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease having the loss of both upper and lower motor neurons, eventually leading to muscle weakness, paralysis, and eventual death in patients (Grad et al. 2017) With a prevalence of 5 in 100,000 and an incidence of 1.7 per 100,000, ALS typically afflicts people between 50 and 60 years old (Oskarsson et al. 2018). The pathophysiology of ALS involves a complex interaction between genetic and molecular factors leading to oxidative stress, neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction. The functions of BDNF-TrkB are complex in ALS; although TrkB expression is reported to increase in the spinal cords of patients, receptor activation, as indicated by autophosphorylation, is significantly reduced, suggesting dysregulated BDNF/TrkB signaling that may exacerbate motor neuron degeneration. Simultaneously, motor neurons in ALS have shown to upregulate TrkA expression, which may suggest a shift in neurotrophic dependence from BDNF to NGF. This adaptive response might be an attempt to survive the neurogenerative environment (Brahimi et al. 2016). The TrkA is the NGF receptor and necessary for survival of sensory and sympathetic neurons; it could prove neuroprotective for the motor neuron in ALS. Enhancing survival and growth-promoting pathways, TrkA agonists may slow the neurodegeneration. Both TrkA and TrkB agonists hold hope in combination therapies with neurotrophic factors such as GDNF or CNTF, providing a more comprehensive, multi-faceted approach to enhance survival of motor neurons.

Research on Trk C receptors in ALS demonstrated intricate interactions and therapeutic possibilities. Spinal cord motor neurons of ALS patients were found to over express p75NGFR and p145TrkB mRNA. Trk C receptor has two isoforms (TrkC-FL and Trk C-T1) possessing opposite functions: former supporting the health of the motor neuron, and the latter generating neurotoxic TNF-α. Activation of the agonistic antibody, which selectively acts upon the Trk C-FL might enhance the motor neuron function and extended survival(Brahimi et al. 2016) Preclinical research on modulating Trk signaling holds promise for therapeutic efficacy that certainly comes with challenges, concerning the specificity of Trk receptors to avoid off- target effects. However, optimization of specific activation could offer an overall strategy for the treatment of ALS.

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Multiple sclerosis is a chronic, progressive disease mediated by the immune system that affects the central nervous system. It is characterized by axonal demyelination and axonal degradation which leads to loss of sensation or strength in an arm or leg and blindness of the eyes (Terracina et al. 2023). The immune cells such as Th1, Th2 (Lafaille et al. 1997) and Th17 (Kebir et al. 2007), CD8+ T cells (Loma and Heyman 2011) and B cells (Genain et al. 1999) have been implicated in the underlying pathology of MS. Patients with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) especially in the early stages go through periods of neurological symptoms that are followed by intensive remissions. Axonal damage and neurodegeneration eventually replace chronic inflammation and demyelination, frequently leading to secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS), a condition in which ongoing neuronal loss results in permanent impairment.

BDNF and TrkB have complex roles in MS. BDNF, which is produced by immune cells and astrocytes in MS lesions, has been considered to exert neuroprotective effects and participate in tissue repair. The BDNF-TrkB pathway is involved in the CNS health and might be a potential therapeutic target for MS. Through ERK and PI3K signaling, BDNF levels and oligodendrocyte myelin expression were shown to be positively correlated in the cuprizone induced animal model of demyelination. Targeting Trk receptors and creating selective agonists to strengthen neuroprotection and encourage myelin repair is a promising therapeutic plan for multiple sclerosis. The modulation of the BDNF-TrkB pathway may alleviate axonal injury, enable remyelination, and might affect disease progression. In addition, exploring Trk receptor agonists might offer new opportunities for therapeutic intervention in MS, targeting both neurodegeneration and functional recovery. Limitations of neurotrophins therapeutic application and ways to overcome

Neurotrophins, being critically important for neuronal survival and function, encounter considerable limitations when therapeutically applied in neurodegenerative diseases. Although mature neurons are post-mitotic and do not undergo cell division, neurotrophins support neuronal survival, neurite outgrowth, synaptic plasticity, and functional recovery, and may enhance endogenous repair processes such as axonal and dendritic remodeling as well as limited neurogenesis from neural progenitor cells in specific regions like the hippocampus and subventricular zone. Their clinical translation is stalled primarily by their inability to cross BBB, which drastically limits their CNS bioavailability. Additionally, neurotrophins exhibit poor pharmacokinetic properties, including rapid enzymatic degradation and short systemic half-lives, necessitating frequent dosing or invasive delivery methods to maintain effective CNS concentration. Systemic administration also poses challenges due to the risk of off-target effects in peripheral tissues, potentially leading to adverse outcomes such as pain sensitization, weight loss, or unwanted cell proliferation outside the nervous system (Jang et al. 2009). Another particularly important concern for Trk receptor-based therapies is their potential activation in non-target tissues, where Trk receptors are expressed but where neurotrophin stimulation may cause deleterious proliferative or metabolic effects. For example, systemic TrkA activation has been associated with increased nociceptive signaling and hyperalgesia, complicating therapeutic use. Moreover, uncontrolled activation of TrkB or TrkC receptors in peripheral tissues may influence oncogenic processes or disrupt normal cellular homeostasis. Hence, therapeutic strategies must carefully balance sufficient CNS target engagement with minimal peripheral exposure to avoid toxicity.

To overcome these limitations, various novel delivery and molecular engineering approaches have been pursued. Direct intra-cerebroventricular (ICV) injections, while enabling CNS access, are invasive and prone to limited diffusion, exemplified by the restricted spreading of BDNF due to high TrkB receptor expression in ependymal cells, limiting therapeutic efficacy (Senanayake et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2023).Viral vector-mediated gene therapies offer long-term, localized neurotrophin expression and have demonstrated safety and potential efficacy in clinical trials for PD, circumventing issues related to repeated dosing but raising concerns about immune responses and vector-associated risks. Nanotechnology-based delivery platforms represent a promising frontier, with nanoparticle carriers and biomaterial scaffolds enabling sustained, targeted release and enhanced BBB penetration. These systems can be functionally modified to preferentially accumulate in neural tissue, reducing systemic exposure and thus the risk of non-target adverse effects. Small molecule neurotrophin mimetics designed to selectively activate Trk receptors with improved stability and CNS penetration further circumvent limitations related to protein therapeutics. Collectively, these advances aim to maximize neuroprotective efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity, addressing critical hurdles in translating neurotrophin biology into safe and effective therapies for neurodegenerative disorders (Ahlawat et al. 2020; Thompson et al. 2025).

Conclusion

Neurodegenerative diseases reflect an imbalance between pro-survival neurotrophic signaling and multiple converging neurotoxic processes, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, and chronic glial activation. In the healthy CNS, neurotrophins such as NGF, BDNF, NT-3, and NT-4/5 support neuronal viability, synaptic plasticity, and metabolic homeostasis through high-affinity Trk receptors and context-dependent p75NTR signaling. In disease states, upregulation of p75NTR together with reduced Trk signaling and sustained inflammatory milieu shifts neurotrophin actions from trophic to degenerative, thereby promoting progressive neuronal vulnerability.Importantly, the interplay between Trk receptors and p75NTR represents a critical regulatory axis in neurotrophin biology. While Trk receptor activation supports neuronal survival and repair, p75NTR, particularly when engaged by pro-neurotrophins such as proBDNF, can mediate opposing effects, including apoptosis and synaptic degeneration. Therefore, future therapeutic strategies should aim not only to enhance Trk receptor signaling but also to modulate p75NTR-associated pathways, thereby restoring the balance between neuroprotection and neurodegeneration.

Disease-specific alterations in neurotrophin pathways have been mapped across major neurodegenerative disorders, supporting a mechanistic rather than merely correlative role. In Alzheimer’s disease, increased pro-NGF with decreased TrkA and relative p75NTR dominance drive basal forebrain cholinergic neuron loss, while BDNF reduction in cortex and hippocampus further compromises synaptic integrity. Parkinson’s disease exhibits reduced neurotrophic support for dopaminergic neurons and glia-driven inflammatory and oxidative stress signals that intersect with p75NTR-mediated degenerative cascades. In Huntington’s disease, mutant huntingtin impairs cortical BDNF production and axonal transport, depriving striatal neurons of essential trophic input, whereas in multiple sclerosis and ALS, deficits in neurotrophic signaling contribute to demyelination, motor neuron stress, and failure of endogenous repair mechanisms.

Pre-clinical work demonstrates that restoring or enhancing neurotrophin signaling can mitigate native neurotoxin-induced (ROS, Excitotoxic Glutamate, Aβ Peptides, Mutant Huntingtin Protein and Inflammatory Cytokines) mediated injury and disease-like pathology. Administration of neurotrophins or gene-based upregulation of BDNF and NGF improves neuronal survival, synaptic function, and behavioral outcomes in rodent models of Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s disease. However, native neurotrophins are limited by short half-life, poor blood–brain barrier penetration, and nonselective activation of p75NTR, which can trigger apoptosis under conditions of Trk downregulation or oxidative stress.

To overcome these barriers, several therapeutic strategies are being pursued, including small-molecule Trk agonists, biased ligands, and p75NTR modulators. Rationally designed TrkB/TrkC modulators and p75NTR ligands such as LM11A-series compounds have shown the ability to prevent cholinergic neuron atrophy, reduce neurite degeneration, and improve cognitive or motor outcomes in advanced-stage Alzheimer’s and other neurodegeneration models. Parallel efforts in nanomedicine and biomimetic carriers are improving CNS delivery of neurotrophins and neurotrophin-mimetic agents, with engineered nanoparticles and cell membrane–coated systems achieving enhanced blood–brain barrier crossing and targeted brain accumulation in experimental settings (Chavez et al. 2015; Kochman et al. 2026; Konermann et al. 2015). These converging lines of evidence establish neurotrophin and neurotrophin-receptor pathways as central regulators of neuronal resilience and attractive targets for disease modification rather than solely symptomatic relief. Systematic characterization of temporal and region-specific changes in Trk and p75NTR signaling across disease stages, coupled with optimized delivery platforms and receptor-selective ligands, is expected to guide precision strategies that restore neurotrophic tone, rebalance pro-survival versus pro-death signaling, and ultimately slow or halt neurodegenerative progression.

Future directions

Therapeutic strategies that target Trk receptors while modulating neurotrophin signaling pathways offer a cutting-edge approach to neuroprotection and possible regeneration in neurodegenerative diseases. Importantly, because mature neurons are post-mitotic and do not undergo cell division, the “regenerative” effects attributed to neurotrophin signaling largely reflect enhancement of neuronal survival, neurite extension, synaptic plasticity, and axonal or dendritic remodeling, as well as stimulation of limited neurogenesis from neural progenitor cells in specialized brain regions such as the hippocampus and subventricular zone. Identification and quantification of reliable biomarkers such as levels of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) or the activation states of Trk receptors are instrumental for guiding and assessing the efficacy of Trk-targeted therapies. Because BDNF serves as a principal ligand for TrkB and a surrogate marker of neurotrophic activity and synaptic health, carefully monitoring its levels along with the phosphorylation status of Trk receptors or downstream effectors like phosphorylated Akt or MAPK/ERK in cerebrospinal or peripheral fluids provides crucial insights for clinicians. Biomarker analysis enables titration of neurotrophic treatments to optimal ranges, early detection of therapeutic response, and the identification of possible adverse effects such as excessive Trk activation, which can lead to excitotoxicity or apoptosis if not tightly regulated. Integrating biomarker analysis into clinical paradigms advances the precision and safety of neuroprotective interventions.

Single-agent neurotrophin-based therapies have often shown limited efficacy in complex neurodegenerative diseases, where the underlying pathophysiology is intrinsically multifactorial. To overcome this, combination therapies, such as pairing TrkB agonists with anti-inflammatory agents, offer enhanced neuroprotection through synergistic mechanisms. In diseases like multiple sclerosis, where demyelination results from both a loss of trophic support and immune-mediated neuroinflammation, the combined amplification of endogenous repair and the attenuation of inflammatory injury maximize therapeutic benefit. Selective small molecules that boost Trk signaling, when combined with inhibitors of p75NTR or truncated Trk isoforms, further favor neuronal survival and regeneration while suppressing detrimental apoptotic processes. Thus, rationally designed combination regimens provide a nuanced and adaptable toolkit for addressing neurodegenerative disorders with greater efficacy. Gene therapy techniques, notably the use of adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors encoding full-length, functional Trk receptors, represent an innovative approach for achieving sustained and targeted neurotrophin signaling in affected neuronal circuits. Unlike transient effects seen with soluble agonists and peptides, AAV-mediated gene delivery ensures prolonged upregulation of the neurotrophic axis within specific neuronal populations.

Preclinical studies show that persistent TrkB expression via AAV vectors confers increased neuronal resilience, fosters axonal regrowth, and promotes synaptic repair, effectively counteracting the negative effects of truncated Trk isoforms or aberrant p75NTR signaling. Advances in genome-editing tools and engineered regulatory elements substantially increase the precision and safety of AAV-based strategies to restore Trk signaling. Rather than constitutive overexpression, contemporary approaches enable activation of endogenous NTRK loci using CRISPR-activation (CRISPRa) systems (dCas9 fused to transcriptional activators), which upregulate native Trk expression while preserving endogenous splicing and regulatory control; inducible transcriptional control (for example Tet-On/Tet-Off systems or drug-responsive splice switches) that permit temporal tuning of Trk expression in response to an exogenous small molecule, thereby limiting prolonged or excessive receptor activation; activity-dependent promoters (e.g., c-Fos, Arc, or other immediate-early gene regulatory elements) that bias transgene expression toward active neuronal ensembles and reduce untargeted expression in quiescent cells; cell-type targeting and detargeting strategies combining cell-specific promoters/enhancers or Cre-dependent AAV architectures with microRNA target sites in the vector 3′-UTR to suppress off-target expression in peripheral tissues, improving tissue selectivity; post-translational safety switches such as destabilizing domains or ligand-dependent stabilizers and proteolysis tags that allow rapid, reversible control of protein levels; and synthetic gene circuits (AND/NOT logic, feedback control) and inducible degrons that permit closed-loop regulation to maintain Trk activity within a therapeutic window. Collectively, these strategies reduce the risks of continuous over-activation (which can provoke excitotoxicity, aberrant growth signaling, or maladaptive plasticity), permit stage- or activity-dependent augmentation of neurotrophic tone, and enable reversible shut-off if adverse effects appear. Proof-of-concept studies show that neuron-optimized CRISPRa architectures and CRISPR-based activation of neurotrophic factors produce robust, long-lasting upregulation of target genes in neuronal contexts, while single-AAV Tet-On designs achieve robust, doxycycline-regulated transgene expression in brain in vivo; miRNA-regulated AAVs and Cre-dependent designs are established methods to limit peripheral or off-target expression. These modular tools when combined with modern AAV serotype selection and improved promoters/enhancers therefore offer a feasible path toward inducible, cell-selective, and safer Trk-based gene therapies for neurodegenerative disease.
