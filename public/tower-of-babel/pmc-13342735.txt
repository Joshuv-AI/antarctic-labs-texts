INTRODUCTION

Tonsilloliths or tonsil stones are singular or multiple calculi found in tonsillar crypts that are located unilaterally or bilaterally. A review report determined the exact location of the tonsillolith, showing that in 69.7% of cases they are located in the tonsillar tissue, 21.2% in the tonsillar fossa, and 9% in the soft palate.[1] The earliest known description of concretions in the oropharynx is thought to have been recorded by Lang in 1560. Large calculi are clinically seen as a hard mass in the tonsil. Some of them are also found to mimic the symptoms of peritonsillar abscess. A lateral radiograph of the neck shows a radio-opaque mass overlapping the ramus of the mandible and this can be mistaken for a foreign body, a displaced tooth, or a calcified blood vessel.[2] It is not mandatory for a patient having tonsil stones to show any symptoms. Clinically, the superficial tonsillitis may be seen as a white or yellowish hard mass within the tonsillar crypt. However, a tonsillolith may also have a deeper location and present as an enlarged or calcified mass within the tonsil.[3] The largest tonsillolith that has been removed, weighing about 420 mg was noted by Hiranandani, in 1967.[4] Our research aims to document a case of unilateral tonsillolith that we removed under general anesthesia.

CASE REPORT

A 27-year-old patient reported to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery with the chief complaint of having a “tooth-like structure close to his throat.” He mentioned feeling foreign body sensations while deglutition. There was no pain, irritation, or halitosis involved. The patient was afebrile and gave no history of chronic tonsilitis, peritonsillar abscess, or any other disease although he did mention consuming one cigarette a day for 3 years. His orthopantomography revealed a large radiopacity with respect to the anterior border of the left ramus and the angle area, measuring about 23.1 mm × 21.5 mm × 15.2 mm [Figures 1-3]. An appropriate diagnosis of tonsillolith in the aforementioned area was made and the patient was scheduled for surgery under general anesthesia. During the operation, a vertical incision was placed along the left tonsillar region and the area was slightly dilated. A curved artery forceps was employed and the stone was removed in multiple fragments. The concretions were hard, irregular, and pale yellow, weighing approximately 31 mg [Figure 4]. Our patient showed no discomfort or harbored any other complications post-surgically. He was prescribed a prophylactic dose of antibiotics and analgesics and was discharged [Figure 5]. Histopathological analysis showed the presence of magnesium salts calcium phosphate and carbonate in the given samples.

DISCUSSION

Accumulation of calcium salts is common in the bones. A heterotrophic calcification occurs when the same minerals deposit themselves in the soft tissue in an irregular manner. Various authors have classified this type of calcification into three broad categories, namely idiopathic, metastatic, and dystrophic. Our patient conformed to the first type because he neither exhibited any abnormal levels of serum calcium or renal disease as seen in the metastatic variant nor did his concretions occur in “degenerative and dead tissues,” which is a common finding of dystrophic calcification.[3456789101112131415]

The etiopathology of tonsillolith can lead to several theories of its formation. The most common one deals with the build-up of organic and inorganic debris, sloughed epithelium, and saliva which provides a suitable medium for bacterial action and infection.[1] Another theory by Mishenkin and Shtil states that the aberrations in the salivary gland ducts may lead to a “stasis of saliva in the efferent ducts of the small accessory salivary glands in the oral cavity, owing to their mechanical obstruction caused by post tonsillectomy scar or chronic inflammation.”[16] Gaur et al.[17] have further confirmed this theory in their findings where there were three tributaries of Wharton’s duct, individually opening into the floor of the mouth. In many texts, bilateral tonsillar concretions have been observed, as opposed to our study where the calcified mass lay unilaterally on the left side.

CONCLUSION

Although it is acceptable for a patient to remove a small mass of tonsil stone from the crypts, it is strictly advised that any structure that is difficult to remove or is large in size must not be tampered with by the patient as it warrants proper surgical intervention. An early diagnosis and timely treatment can save the patient from an aggravated infection and prevent dyspnoea, choking due to dislodgement, otalgia, and the likes. If there are multiple recurrences of tonsilloliths then the correct treatment option would be to go for a tonsillectomy.
