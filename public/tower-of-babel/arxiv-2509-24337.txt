Canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization on the unit circle: matching subspaces versus Riccati equations Thanks: This work is based on the research supported in part by the National Research Foundation of South Africa (Grant Numbers 127364 and 145688) and the Magnus Ehrnrooth Foundation.
S. ter Horst, M. Kurula, A.C.M. Ran

Abstract
. Wiener-Hopf factorization is an important tool in the study of block Toeplitz and block Wiener-Hopf operators, and many applications involving these operators. In this paper we compare two approaches to Wiener-Hopf factorization, namely, the more classical approach based on matching invariant subspaces and a more recent approach based on solutions to a non-symmetric Riccati equation. The latter approach is extended to the case of Hilbert space operator-valued functions that are analytic on a neighborhood of the unit disc 𝕋 {\mathbb{T}} , but need not be rational. In both approaches, existence of canonical right Wiener-Hopf factorization is characterized by existence of a stabilizing solution to a Riccati equation, however, the Riccati equations are not the same. We analyse the solution sets of the Riccati equations and show that they indeed are not the same, but they do have the same stabilizing solution.

1.
Introduction

Let
R(z)
be a function whose values are bounded linear operators on a given Hilbert space, which is analytic and invertible on an annulus containing the unit circle. A factorization
R(z)=V_{-}(z)V_{+}(z)
is called a
right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
when
V_{-}(z)
and
V_{+}(z)
are also operator-valued functions, on the same Hilbert space, such that
V_{+}(z)
is analytic and invertible on the closed unit disc
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
with an inverse that is also analytic on
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, while
V_{-}(z)
is analytic and invertible on the exterior of the open disc
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{D}}
with an inverse that is also analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{D}}
. When the properties of the factors are reversed, so
R(z)=W_{+}(z)W_{-}(z)
, with
W_{+}(z)
and
W_{-}(z)
corresponding to the interior and the exterior, respectively, of the unit circle, we say that this is a
left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
.

Canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization of matrix- and operator-valued functions has been studied extensively in the last seventy years. It appeared for instance in
[
15
]
in connection to applications to systems of Wiener-Hopf integral equations, and in
[
11
]
in connection to applications to (block) Toeplitz operators. These sources focussed on the matrix-valued case. The technically much more difficult case of operator-valued functions was treated in
[
16
]
.
For an extensive overview see
[
14
]
.

Having some form of a state space realization for the function
R(z)
turns out to be extremely helpful in determining the Wiener-Hopf factorization. In many sources it is assumed that
R(z)
has a realization as the transfer function of a continuous time system, with the additional assumption that the value at infinity is an invertible operator (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
]
and the references give there). The latter assumption is weakened in
[
8
]
, while in
[
13
]

R(z)
is assumed to be the transfer function of a continuous time differential-algebraic system (i.e., the state equation is a system of differential-algebraic equations). A related type of realization was used in
[
17
]
.
A rather different representation of
R(z)
, more adapted to Toeplitz operators, was used in
[
10
]
; see (
2.1
) below. While earlier realization formulas for
R(z)
lead to conditions for canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization in terms of a matching pair of invariant subspaces for two operators, the main result in
[
10
]
expresses existence of canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization in terms of existence of a stabilizing solution of a (discrete) algebraic Riccati equation. In this approach the function does not need to be invertible at zero or at infinity, in fact, it does not even need to be defined at zero or at infinity. The disadvantage of the representation of
R(z)
in (
2.1
) is that an immediate formula for the inverse of
R(z)
is lacking. Algebraic Riccati equations were used in earlier sources as well, mainly to discuss factorizations of functions with symmetry, see e.g.,
[
20
]
. In
[
10
]
the non-symmetric case is the focus of attention. Recently, in
[
18
]
, we used an approach based on dichotomous systems (see
[
7
]
) to return to the factorization of operator-valued functions whose values on the unit circle are of the form identity plus a strict contraction. It is known that such functions admit both right and left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
5
]
and the references given there). In
[
18
]
, we provided explicit formulas for the factors in terms of the operators appearing in a realization of the transfer function of a dichotomous system.

The goal of the present paper is twofold. We expand the results of
[
10
]
to the infinite dimensional situation in Theorem
2.1
below, and we shall discuss the connections between the approaches using a matching pair of invariant subspaces as in
[
4
,
5
]
and the approach using a non-symmetric algebraic Riccati equation from
[
10
]
. For the latter we shall restrict to operator-valued transfer functions of dichotomous systems.

In Subsection
2.1
we expand the result of
[
10
]
to the infinite dimensional situation, while the Wiener-Hopf factorization for dichotomous realizations in terms of invariant subspaces is discussed in Subsection
2.2
. The connection between dichotomous realizations and stable representations from
[
10
]
is clarified in Subsection
2.3
. The two approaches described in Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
lead to two algebraic Riccati equations, which are strongly related, but may have different sets of solutions. The connection between these two algebraic Riccati equations is analysed in Section
3
. Finally, in Section
4
we assume a right Wiener-Hopf factorization exists, and then give a necessary and sufficient condition for existence of a left Wiener-Hopf factorization (compare
[
2
,
5
]
).

The following notation and terminology will be used throughout the paper. For Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
denotes the Banach space of bounded linear operators mapping

into
{\mathcal{Y}}
, abbreviated to

in case

. For a subspace

of a Hilbert space

2.
Canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization: Two approaches

In this section we discuss two state space approaches to canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization for operator-valued functions, namely the one from
[
10
]
based on non-symmetric algebraic Riccati equations (which we extend here to the non-rational Hilbert space operator-valued case) and the one from Chapters 7 and 12 of
[
5
]
based on matching invariant subspaces. Since the method from
[
10
]
works for a more general class of functions, we shall discuss that approach first.

2.1.
Canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization in terms of stable representations

Theorem 2.1

For a stable representation

The function

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{\circ}

) are obtained using such a sign swap in (

), (
2.4
) and (

), respectively.

Now, applying Thm
2.1
to

, we get the result, observing that
\widetilde{R}(z)={\widetilde{V_{-}}}(z){\widetilde{V_{+}}}(z)
is a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization for

if and only if

∎

2.2.
Canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization for dichotomous realizations

In the second approach to canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization, we assume that the
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}})
valued function
R(z)
is given by a transfer function formula of the form

(2.13)

R(z)=D+zC(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA)^{-1}B,

with
{\mathcal{X}}
another Hilbert space and
A,B,C
and
D
operators acting between appropriate Hilbert spaces, and here we assume that
\sigma(A)\cap{\mathbb{T}}=\emptyset
. Such functions are analytic in a neighborhood of 0, in addition to a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{T}}
. The right-hand side in (
2.13
) is the transfer function of the discrete-time input-state-output system
\Sigma
over the nonnegative integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
given by

(2.14)

\Sigma:\left\{\begin{aligned} x(n+1)&=Ax(n)+Bu(n)\\
y(n)&=Cx(n)+Du(n),\quad n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}},\quad x(0)=x_{0}~\text{given},\end{aligned}\right.

which is called
dichotomous
since we assume
\sigma(A)\cap{\mathbb{T}}=\emptyset
; see
[
7
]
for more on linear discrete-time input-state-output dichotomous systems, and see
[
18
]
for Wiener-Hopf factorization in case
G(z)
has the form
G(z)=I+K(z)
with
K(z)
strictly contractive on
{\mathbb{T}}
. The dichotomy assumption is equivalent to the existence of a direct sum decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
of the state space
{\mathcal{X}}
, such that with respect to this decomposition,
A
has the form

(2.15)

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{-}&0\\
0&A_{+}\end{bmatrix}:{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}\to{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+},

with
A_{-}
invertible, and
A_{+}
and
A_{-}^{-1}
both exponentially stable. The spaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
are in fact uniquely determined by
A
, and correspond to the spectral subspaces of
A
with respect to
{\mathbb{D}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, respectively. Next we decompose
C
and
B
with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
as

(2.16)

C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{-}&C_{+}\end{bmatrix}:{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}\to{\mathcal{U}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{-}\\
B_{+}\end{bmatrix}:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}.

In order to apply the matching invariant subspace approach, we shall assume that the feedthrough operator
D
is invertible, and define

(2.17)

A^{\times}:=A-BD^{-1}C.

In that case
R(z)
is invertible on the set
\{0\}\cup(\rho(A)^{-1}\cap\rho(A^{\times})^{-1})
, with inverse given by

R(z)^{-1}=D^{-1}-zD^{-1}C(I-zA^{\times})^{-1}BD^{-1},\quad z\in\{0\}\cup(\rho(A)^{-1}\cap\rho(A^{\times})^{-1}).

When
A^{\times}
is also dichotomous, then for
A^{\times}
we also have a decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
, with
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
the spectral subspaces of
A^{\times}
with respect to
{\mathbb{D}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, respectively, such that
A^{\times}
decomposes as

(2.18)

A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{\times}_{-}&0\\
0&A^{\times}_{+}\end{bmatrix}:{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}\to{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}.

Now assume that
D
is invertible and factor
D=D_{+}D_{-}
with
D_{\pm}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{U}})
invertible. Set

\widehat{R}(z):=D_{+}^{-1}R\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)D_{-}^{-1}=I+D_{+}^{-1}C(zI_{\mathcal{X}}-A)^{-1}BD_{-}^{-1},

so that a canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization of
R
can easily be obtained from such a factorization of
\widehat{R}
using the formula
R(z)=D_{+}\widehat{R}(\frac{1}{z})D_{-}
. Note, however, that a right canonical factorization of the operator-valued function
\widehat{R}
translates to a left canonical factorization of
R(z)
(and vice versa), due to the change of
\frac{1}{z}
to
z
. Applying Theorem 7.1 in
[
5
]
to
\widehat{R}
, with the Cauchy contour
\Gamma:={\mathbb{T}}
, we thus get the following result:

Corollary 2.3
.

The function
R
in (
2.13
) admits a left canonical factorization if and only if both
A
and
A^{\times}
are dichotomous and with respect to the state space decompositions
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}={\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
above, we have the
matching decomposition

{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
; then write
P
for the projection of
{\mathcal{X}}
along
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
onto
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
.

When the above conditions hold, for every factorization
D=D_{+}D_{-}
with
D_{\pm}
invertible,
R(z)=W_{+}(z)W_{-}(z)
is a left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization, where

(2.19)

\displaystyle W_{+}(z):=

\displaystyle D_{+}+zC(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA)^{-1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-P)BD_{-}^{-1},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z):=

\displaystyle D_{-}+zD_{+}^{-1}CP(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA)^{-1}B,\quad z\in{\mathbb{T}},

with inverses given by

(2.20)

\displaystyle W_{+}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{+}^{-1}-zD_{+}^{-1}C(I_{\mathcal{X}}-P)(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA^{\times})^{-1}BD^{-1},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{-}^{-1}-zD^{-1}C(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA^{\times})^{-1}PBD_{-}^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{T}}.

Note that the invariances of
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
under
A
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
under

imply that, with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
, we have the decompositions
P=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
A_{21}&A_{+}\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{-}^{\times}&A_{12}^{\times}\\
0&A_{22}^{\times}\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{-}^{\times}\\
\widetilde{B}_{+}\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{-}^{\times}&\widetilde{C}_{+}\end{bmatrix}

with
A_{+}
as in (
2.15
) and
A_{-}^{\times}
as in (
2.18
). Note that
\widetilde{C}_{+}=C_{+}
. It then follows from
[
4

that
\sigma(A_{11})\subset{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
and
\sigma(A_{22}^{\times})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
, and that

and their inverses are also given by

(2.21)

\displaystyle W_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{+}+zC_{+}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}}-zA_{+})^{-1}\widetilde{B}_{+}D_{-}^{-1},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}},

\displaystyle W_{+}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{+}^{-1}-zD_{+}^{-1}C_{+}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}}-zA_{22}^{\times})^{-1}\widetilde{B}_{+}D^{-1},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{-}+zD_{+}^{-1}C_{-}^{\times}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}}-zA_{11})^{-1}B_{-}^{\times},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{D}},

\displaystyle W_{-}(z)^{-1}

confirming that

and

(that

and

) have analytic extensions to open neighborhoods of

(of

). Hence,

In order to obtain a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization of a function

given by a dichotomous realization (

) it is necessary and sufficient for

and

to be dichotomous and for

to decompose as
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
. In that case, write

for the projection on
{\mathcal{X}}
along
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
onto
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
. As above, we get a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
R(z)=V_{-}(z)V_{+}(z)
of

with the factors and their inverses obtained by replacing

by
P^{\times}
in (

) and (
2.20
), in such a way that

is obtained from the formula for

, and swapping

and

:

(2.22)

\displaystyle{V_{-}}(z)

\displaystyle:=D_{-}+zC(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA)^{-1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-P^{\times})BD_{+}^{-1},

\displaystyle{V_{+}}(z)

\displaystyle:=D_{+}+zD_{-}^{-1}CP^{\times}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA)^{-1}B,

with inverses given by

(2.23)

\displaystyle{V_{-}}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{-}^{-1}-zD_{-}^{-1}C(I_{\mathcal{X}}-P^{\times})(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA^{\times})^{-1}BD^{-1},

\displaystyle{V_{+}}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{+}^{-1}+zD^{-1}C(I_{\mathcal{X}}-zA^{\times})^{-1}P^{\times}BD_{+}^{-1}.

Again, using the invariance of
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
, it follows that with respect to the matching decomposition

,

0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,

(2.24)

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{-}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}^{\times}&0\\
A_{21}^{\times}&A_{+}^{\times}\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{B}_{-}\\

with

,

and

) and (

). As before,

and

, and the right canonical version of (

(2.25)

\displaystyle V_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{-}+zC_{-}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{-}}-zA_{-})^{-1}\widetilde{B}_{-}D_{+}^{-1},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}},

\displaystyle V_{-}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{-}^{-1}-zD_{-}^{-1}C_{-}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{+}}-zA_{11}^{\times})^{-1}\widetilde{B}_{-}D^{-1},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}},

\displaystyle V_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{+}+zD_{-}^{-1}C_{+}^{\times}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}}-zA_{22})^{-1}B_{+}^{\times},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{D}},

\displaystyle V_{+}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=D_{+}^{-1}-zD^{-1}C_{+}^{\times}(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}}-zA_{+}^{\times})^{-1}B_{+}^{\times}D_{+}^{-1},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{D}}.

Under the assumptions made in this paragraph, the formulas in (
2.25
) give a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization
R(z)=V_{-}(z)V_{+}(z)
, for all factorizations
D=D_{-}D_{+}
with

invertible.

Following Section 12.3 in
[
5
]
we can also associate a Riccati equation with the above paired invariant subspace approach. We include here the details for the right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization.
We start from the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{-}\dotplus{\mathcal{X}}_{+}
and the corresponding decompositions of
A,

B
and
C
in (
2.15
)–(
2.16
). Then

(2.26)

A^{\times}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{-}-B_{-}D^{-1}C_{-}&-B_{-}D^{-1}C_{+}\\
-B_{+}D^{-1}C_{-}&A_{+}-B_{+}D^{-1}C_{+}\end{bmatrix}.

We next apply Theorem 12.5 (and its proof) in
[
5
]
to
R(\frac{1}{z})
in (
2.13
), adjusted to the left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization setting. It follows that

admits a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization if and only if

, see also Theorem 6.1 in

for some bounded operator

. Invariance of

being a solution to the Riccati equation

\widehat{Q}B_{+}D^{-1}C_{-}\widehat{Q}-\widehat{Q}(A_{+}-B_{+}D^{-1}C_{+})+(A_{-}-B_{-}D^{-1}C_{-})\widehat{Q}-B_{-}D^{-1}C_{+}=0.

Setting
S=\begin{bmatrix}I&\widehat{Q}\\

and computing

using the Riccati equation, one sees that

is the spectral subspace of

corresponding to the open unit disc if and only if the following two spectral inclusions hold

\sigma(A_{+}-B_{+}D^{-1}(C_{+}+C_{-}\widehat{Q}))\subset{\mathbb{D}}\mbox{ and }\sigma(A_{-}-(B_{-}-\widehat{Q}B_{+})D^{-1}C_{-})\subset{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}.

For later purposes, we point out that the Riccati equation (

A_{-}\widehat{Q}-\widehat{Q}A_{+}+(\widehat{Q}B_{+}-B_{-})D^{-1}(C_{-}\widehat{Q}+C_{+})=0.

2.3.
Dichotomous realizations versus stable representations

In order to make a connection between the two approaches to canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization, we need to assume that
R(z)
is defined and analytic in a neighborhood of
0
. To achieve this in the stable representation (
2.1
), we shall assume that
\alpha_{-}
is invertible, in which case
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

An operator-valued function
R(z)
is the transfer function of a dichotomous system if and only if it has a stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible. In case
R(z)
is given by (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{+}
and
\alpha_{-}
exponentially stable and
\alpha_{-}
invertible, then
R(z)
is the transfer function of the dichotomous system (
2.14
) with

(2.30)

\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}A_{-}&0&B_{-}\\
0&A_{+}&B_{+}\\
\hline\cr C_{-}&C_{+}&D\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&0&\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}\\
0&\alpha_{+}&\beta_{+}\\
\hline\cr-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&\gamma_{+}&\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}\end{array}\right].

In particular,
R(z)
does not have a pole at
0
. Conversely, if
R(z)
is the transfer function of the dichotomous system (
2.14
) with
A
decomposing as in (
2.15
) and
B
and
C
as in (
2.16
), then

R(z)
is given by (
2.1
) with

(2.31)

\displaystyle\delta:=D-C_{-}A_{-}^{-1}B_{-},\quad\gamma_{+}:=

\displaystyle C_{+},\quad\alpha_{+}:=A_{+},\quad\beta_{+}:=B_{+},

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}:=-C_{-}A_{-}^{-1},\quad\alpha_{-}:=

\displaystyle A_{-}^{-1},\quad\beta_{-}:=A_{-}^{-1}B_{-}.

Proof.

The conversions in (
2.30
) and (
2.31
) between the operators
A,B,C,D
and
\alpha_{+},\beta_{+},\gamma_{+},\delta
,
\alpha_{-},\beta_{-},\gamma_{-}
, with
\alpha_{-}
and
A_{-}
invertible, are each others inverses, as is easily checked. Assume that
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
,
\alpha_{+}
,
\beta_{+}
,
\gamma_{+}
,
\delta
,
\alpha_{-}
,
\beta_{-}
and
\gamma_{-}
, with
\alpha_{-}
and
A_{-}
invertible, are related as in (
2.30
) and (
2.31
); then

\displaystyle D+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B

\displaystyle=D+zC_{+}(I-zA_{+})^{-1}B_{+}+zC_{-}(I-zA_{-})^{-1}B_{-}

\displaystyle=D+z\gamma_{+}(I-z\alpha_{+})^{-1}\beta_{+}-z\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}(I-z\alpha_{-}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}

\displaystyle=D+\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}+z\gamma_{+}(I-z\alpha_{+})^{-1}\beta_{+}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{-}(I-z\alpha_{-}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}

\displaystyle=\delta+z\gamma_{+}(I-z\alpha_{+})^{-1}\beta_{+}-\gamma_{-}(\alpha_{-}-zI)^{-1}\beta_{-}

\displaystyle=\delta+z\gamma_{+}(I-z\alpha_{+})^{-1}\beta_{+}+\gamma_{-}(zI-\alpha_{-})^{-1}\beta_{-}.

This calculation shows that the transfer function (
2.13
) of the dichotomous system (
2.14
) and the stable representation (
2.1
) are the same function. Finally,
A_{+}=\alpha_{+}
and
A_{-}^{-1}=\alpha_{-}
obviously have the required matching stability properties.
∎

3.
Analysis of the associated Riccati equations

In this section we compare the Riccati equations that appear in the two approaches that were discussed in the previous section, for right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization. These are (
2.3
) in the setting of stable representations and (
2.29
) in the setting of dichotomous realizations. In order to compare the two Riccati equations, we work in the setting of a stable representation (
2.1
) with the additional assumption that
\alpha_{-}
is an invertible operator, so that we can also work in the setting of dichotomous realizations via the connection provided by Proposition
2.4
. In that case, the Riccati equation (
2.29
), in terms of the stable representation operators, after multiplying with
\alpha_{-}
on the left, translates to

(3.1)

Q=\alpha_{-}Q\alpha_{+}+(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q).

Conforming to (
2.28
) and (
2.30
), we call a solution
Q
of (
3.1
)
stabilizing
if the operators

(3.2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}-\beta_{+}(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\alpha_{-}^{-1}+\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1})^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}

are stable, and anti-stable, respectively.

It turns out that in general the solution sets of (
2.3
) and (
3.1
) need not coincide unless we restrict to stabilizing solutions. Indeed, for
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
to satisfy (
2.3
), the operator
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
needs to be invertible, which is not a requirement in (
3.1
). On the other hand, for (
3.1
) necessarily
\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}=R(0)
should be invertible, while one might be able to solve (
2.3
) without this condition. We illustrate this in the following example.

Example 3.1
.

We show that solution sets of the two Riccati equations need not coincide; in fact, neither solution set needs to be contained in the other.

The first example will show that it is possible that (
2.3
) has a stabilizing solution while (
3.1
) does not, because
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
is not invertible. Choose
\alpha_{+}:=0
,
\beta_{+}:=\gamma_{+}:=1
, let
\alpha_{-}:=a\in(0,1)
,
\beta_{-}:=\gamma_{-}:=1
, and choose
\delta:=\tfrac{1}{a}
. Then (
2.3
) becomes the equation
q=(1-aq)(\tfrac{1}{a}-q)^{-1}
. One easily checks that
q=a
is a solution, for which we have
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}=0
(compare also Corollary
3.3
below) and
\alpha_{+}^{\circ}=(a-\tfrac{1}{a})^{-1}
. Note that for
a
small enough
\alpha_{+}^{\circ}
will be stable, but
R(0)=0
is not invertible.

The second example will show that it is possible that (
3.1
) has two solutions while (
2.3
) has only one solution. Take
\beta_{+}:=1,\gamma_{-}:=1
,
\alpha_{+}
and
\alpha_{-}
scalar and non-zero, choose
\delta:=\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}
, and take
\beta_{-},\gamma_{+}
such that
\gamma_{+}\alpha_{-}-\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}\not=0
. Then (
2.3
) becomes

q=\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q+(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}q)(\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}-q)^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-q\alpha_{+}).

This is equivalent to
q=\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q+\alpha_{+}\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q=\alpha_{+}\beta_{-}
. So (
2.3
) only has one solution. On the other hand, (
3.1
) becomes

q=\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q+(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}q)(\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}-\tfrac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha_{-}})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{-}}q).

Multiply this equality by
\tfrac{\gamma_{+}\alpha_{-}-\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}}{\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}}
in order to obtain

\tfrac{\gamma_{+}\alpha_{-}-\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}}{\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}}(1-\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+})q=(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}q)(\gamma_{+}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{-}}q),

or equivalently,

\left(\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}-\tfrac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha_{-}}-\gamma_{+}\alpha_{-}+\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}\right)q=q^{2}-\alpha_{-}\gamma_{+}q-\tfrac{\beta_{-}}{\alpha_{-}}q+\beta_{-}\gamma_{+},

which in turn equivalent is to

0=q^{2}-\left(\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}+\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}\right)q+\beta_{-}\gamma_{+}=\left(q-\tfrac{\gamma_{+}}{\alpha_{+}}\right)\left(q-\beta_{-}\alpha_{+}\right).

This shows that there are two solutions to (
3.1
), of which one coincides with the unique solution to (
2.3
), in line with Proposition
3.7
below.
\Box

In the next two lemmas we investigate the two invertibility requirements, starting with the invertibility of
R(0)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R(z)
be given by the stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible. Then the following two implications hold:

(1)

If
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
is invertible, then
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
is invertible for all
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible, and we have the inversion formula

(3.3)

(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}=\alpha_{-}^{-1}+\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}.

(2)

If there exists a
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
and
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
are invertible, then
R(0)
is invertible.

Proof.

For
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
define

M_{Q}:=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+}\\
\gamma_{-}&\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}\end{bmatrix}.

Then the Schur complement of
M_{Q}
with respect to
\alpha_{-}
is equal to

\displaystyle M_{Q}/\alpha_{-}

\displaystyle=\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}=R(0).

In particular,
M_{Q}
is invertible if and only if
R(0)
is invertible. Hence, invertibility of
M_{Q}
is independent of the choice of
Q
.

Now assume that
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
is such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible. Then the Schur complement of
M_{Q}
with respect to
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
works out as

\displaystyle M_{Q}/(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-}-(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}\gamma_{-}=\alpha_{-}^{\circ},

and it follows that
M_{Q}
is invertible if and only if
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
), for this choice of
Q
, is invertible. However, invertibility of
M_{Q}
is also equivalent to invertibility of
R(0)
, and hence independent of
Q
. Therefore,
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is either invertible for all
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible, precisely when
R(0)
is invertible, or invertible for no
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible, precisely when
R(0)
is not invertible. The formula for
(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}
follows from the standard Schur complement inverse formula, cf., pages 28–29 in
[
4
]
.
∎

The proof above makes extensive use of Schur complements. The following formulas for
M_{Q}
may also have been used to prove part of the lemma. One easily checks that

M_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&0\\
\gamma_{-}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-Q\beta_{+}\\
0&\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}\end{bmatrix},\qquad M_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&\beta_{-}\\
\gamma_{-}&\delta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&-Q\beta_{+}\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}.

Negating the implications in Lemma
3.2
, we obtain the following implications:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
R(z)
be given by the stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible. Then:

(1)

If there exists a
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible but
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is not invertible, then
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
is not invertible.

(2)

If
R(0)
is not invertible, then for each
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
with
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
invertible,
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is not invertible.

The following example will show that
M_{Q}
can be invertible without there existing a
Q
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible. In fact,
R(0)
is invertible, and hence
M_{Q}
, but
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is not invertible for any
Q
. In this case (
2.3
) cannot have solutions, but (
3.1
) can.

Example 3.4
.

Take

\delta:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\ \gamma_{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\ \beta_{+}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix},\ \beta_{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix},\ \mbox{$\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}\in{\mathbb{D}}$ with $\alpha_{-}\not=0$.}

We determine
\gamma_{+}
at a later stage. For each
q\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have

\delta-\gamma_{-}q\beta_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-q\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

which is not invertible, while

R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha_{-}^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}

is invertible. Moreover, the matrix
M_{Q}
in the proof of Lemma
3.2
is given by

M_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-q\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

which is invertible for every
q\in{\mathbb{C}}
. However, since
\delta-\gamma_{-}q\beta_{+}
is not invertible,
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
and
\alpha_{+}^{\circ}
cannot be defined using (
2.4
). Moreover, an easy computation shows that the right hand side of (
3.3
) becomes 0, as it should be according to Lemma
3.5
below.

The Riccati equation (
3.1
) becomes

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q+\begin{bmatrix}1&-\alpha_{-}q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha_{-}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\left(\gamma_{+}-\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}^{-1}q\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-}\alpha_{+}q+q+\begin{bmatrix}1&-\alpha_{-}q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha_{-}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\gamma_{+}.

Taking
\gamma_{+}:=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
will yield
q=\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{+}}
as the unique solution. On the other hand, taking
\gamma_{+}:=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, equation (
3.1
) becomes trivial, so that every
q\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a solution. Nonetheless, equation (
3.1
) does not have a stabilizing solution, since (
2.28
) in particular requires the right hand side of (
3.3
) to be anti-stable; see also (
2.30
).
\Box

Even if
R(0)
is invertible, and hence
M_{Q}
in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, it can happen that
Q
is such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is not invertible, and hence
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is not defined. In the next lemma we characterize when
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible, when we know in advance that
R(0)
is invertible.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
R(z)
be given by the stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible. Assume that
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
is invertible. Let
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
. Then
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible if and only if
the right-hand side of (
3.3
) is invertible. In that case,
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is invertible with
(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}
given by (
3.3
).

Proof.

For
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
we now define

N_{Q}:=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+}\\
-\gamma_{-}&\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}&\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+}\\
-\gamma_{-}&R(0)\end{bmatrix}.

Then the Schur complement of
N_{Q}
with respect to
\alpha_{-}
is

N_{Q}/\alpha_{-}=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}+\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})=\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+},

while the Schur complement of
N_{Q}
with respect to
R(0)
is given by

\displaystyle N_{Q}/R(0)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-}+(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}\gamma_{-}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{-}(\alpha_{-}^{-1}+\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1})\alpha_{-}.

We can then conclude that
N_{Q}
is invertible if and only if
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible if and only if
\alpha_{-}^{-1}+\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}
is invertible. In this case, since
R(0)
is assumed to be invertible, we know from Lemma
3.2
that
\alpha_{-}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is invertible with inverse given by (
3.3
).
∎

Much of the above relies on the existence of a
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible. However, such a
Q
need not exist. In the case that
{\mathcal{U}}
is finite dimensional, we characterize when this happens in the next general lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
U\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
,
V\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
and
W\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{n},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
, with
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
possibly infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces. Then there exists a
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{X}}_{1})
such that
U-VQW
is invertible if and only if
\operatorname{Im}P_{(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}}U=(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}
and
\operatorname{Ker}U|_{\operatorname{Ker}W}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Set
{\mathcal{V}}:=\operatorname{Im}V
and
{\mathcal{W}}:=(\operatorname{Ker}W)^{\perp}
. Assume that
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{1})
is such that
U-VQW
is invertible. Then necessarily
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}=\operatorname{Im}P_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}(U-VQW)=\operatorname{Im}P_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}U
and
\{0\}=\operatorname{Ker}(U-VQW)|_{{\mathcal{W}}^{\perp}}=\operatorname{Ker}U|_{{\mathcal{W}}^{\perp}}
. Hence the conditions
\operatorname{Im}P_{(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}}U=(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}
and
\operatorname{Ker}U|_{\operatorname{Ker}W}=\{0\}
are necessary.

For the converse claim, assume that
\operatorname{Im}P_{(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}}U=(\operatorname{Im}V)^{\perp}
and
\operatorname{Ker}U|_{\operatorname{Ker}W}=\{0\}
. Decompose
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}={\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}={\mathcal{W}}^{\perp}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
, and write out the operators with respect to this decomposition:

U=\begin{bmatrix}U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&U_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad V=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
V_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad W=\begin{bmatrix}0&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

By definition if
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
, then
\operatorname{Im}V_{2}={\mathcal{V}}
and
\operatorname{Ker}W_{2}=\{0\}
. Since
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
are finite dimensional, it the follows that
V_{2}
has a right inverse
V_{2}^{+}
and
W_{2}
has a left inverse
W_{2}^{+}
. It then follows that for any
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{W}},{\mathcal{V}})
, if we take
Q:=V_{2}^{+}(U_{22}-T)W_{2}^{+}
, then
U_{22}-V_{2}QW_{2}=T
, and hence

U-VQW=\begin{bmatrix}U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&U_{22}-V_{2}QW_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&T\end{bmatrix}.

The question thus becomes whether we can replace the
(2,2)
entry in
U
in such a way that the resulting matrix in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
becomes invertible. Now further decompose

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}=(\operatorname{Ker}U_{11})^{\perp}\oplus\operatorname{Ker}U_{11},\ \

\displaystyle\ \ {\mathcal{W}}_{\perp}=\operatorname{Im}U_{11}\oplus(\operatorname{Im}U_{11})^{\perp},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}=\operatorname{Im}U_{21}|_{\operatorname{Ker}U_{11}}\oplus(\operatorname{Im}U_{21}|_{\operatorname{Ker}U_{11}})^{\perp},\

\displaystyle\ {\mathcal{W}}=(\operatorname{Ker}P_{\operatorname{Im}U_{11}^{\perp}}U_{12})^{\perp}\oplus\operatorname{Ker}P_{\operatorname{Im}U_{11}^{\perp}}U_{12},

and note that with respect to this further decomposition we have

U-VQW=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{U}_{11}&0&U^{\prime}_{12}&U^{\prime\prime}_{12}\\
0&0&\widetilde{U}_{12}&0\\
U^{\prime}_{21}&\widetilde{U}_{21}&T_{11}&T_{12}\\
U^{\prime\prime}_{21}&0&T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}

with
\widetilde{U}_{11}
invertible, and with
\widetilde{U}_{12}
and
\widetilde{U}_{21}
invertible by our assumption. Then
U-VQW
is invertible if and only if the Schur complement of
U-VQW
with respect to
\widetilde{U}_{11}
is invertible, and this Schur complement works out as

U-VQW/\widetilde{U}_{11}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\widetilde{U}_{12}&0\\
\widetilde{U}_{21}&T_{11}&T_{12}\\
0&T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0\\
U^{\prime}_{21}\\
U^{\prime\prime}_{21}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{U}_{11}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}0&U^{\prime}_{12}&U^{\prime\prime}_{12}\end{bmatrix}.

If we now take

T:=\begin{bmatrix}U^{\prime}_{21}\\
U^{\prime\prime}_{21}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{U}_{11}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}U^{\prime}_{12}&U^{\prime\prime}_{12}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&T_{0}\end{bmatrix}

with
T_{0}\in{\mathcal{B}}(\operatorname{Ker}P_{\operatorname{Im}U_{11}^{\perp}}U_{12},(\operatorname{Im}U_{21}|_{\operatorname{Ker}U_{11}})^{\perp})
invertible, then

U-VQW/\widetilde{U}_{11}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\widetilde{U}_{12}&0\\
\widetilde{U}_{21}&0&0\\
0&0&T_{0}\end{bmatrix}

is invertible. That one can take
T_{0}
invertible, follows since
\dim\operatorname{Ker}P_{\operatorname{Im}U_{11}^{\perp}}U_{12}=\dim(\operatorname{Im}U_{21}|_{\operatorname{Ker}U_{11}})^{\perp}
, which in turn follows from a dimension count.
∎

Next we consider what can happen to the solution sets of the two Riccati equations. For this purpose, given a stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible, we define the following sets:

\displaystyle\textup{Ricc}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})\colon\mbox{$Q$ solves \eqref{RiccFKR}}\},

\displaystyle\textup{Ricc}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})\colon\mbox{$Q$ solves \eqref{RiccFKR2}}\},

\displaystyle\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})\colon\mbox{$Q$ solves}\mbox{ \eqref{RiccFKR2} and $\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}$ is invertible}\}.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
R(z)
be given by the stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
invertible. If
R(0)
is not invertible, then
\textup{Ricc}_{2}
and
\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}
are empty, while
\textup{Ricc}_{1}
might not be empty.
If
R(0)
is invertible, then
\textup{Ricc}_{1}=\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}\subset\textup{Ricc}_{2}
, and the latter inclusion might be strict.

Proof.

If
R(0)
is not invertible, then the Riccati equation (
3.1
) can never be solved, hence
\textup{Ricc}_{2}
and
\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}
are empty. That
\textup{Ricc}_{1}
need not be empty in this case, follows from Example
3.1
. Now assume that
R(0)
is invertible. The inclusion
\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}\subset\textup{Ricc}_{2}
is obvious, and Example
3.1
again shows that it can be strict.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that whenever
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
is such that
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible, then
Q
is a solution to (
2.3
) if and only if it is a solution to (
3.1
). To show that this is the case, we follow the argument from
[
19
]
, where a similar result is proved in the Hermitian case.

Let
Q
be a solution to (
2.3
). Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\alpha_{-}Q\alpha_{+}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q-(\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}).

Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q=

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}-(\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+})

\displaystyle\qquad=(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}+\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+})

\displaystyle\qquad=(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}).

Thus, since
\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}=R(0)
is invertible, we see that

(3.4)

(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+})=(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q).

From this identity it is immediately clear that
Q
solves (
3.1
), since it solves (
2.3
).

Conversely, assume that
Q
solves (
3.1
). Again, it suffices to show that (
3.4
) holds. In this case we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q=

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\alpha_{-}Q\alpha_{+}+\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}(\beta_{-}-\alpha_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q)

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}+(\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q),

so that

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}Q\alpha_{+}=

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q+(\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q)

\displaystyle\qquad=(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}+\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q)

\displaystyle\qquad=(\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+})(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q),

again leading to (
3.4
), since
\delta-\gamma_{-}Q\beta_{+}
is invertible.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
R(z)
be given by the stable representation (
2.1
) with
\alpha_{-}
and
R(0)
invertible. If
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{+},{\mathcal{X}}_{-})
solves (
2.3
), then
\alpha_{+}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) is also given by

(3.5)

\alpha_{+}^{\circ}=\alpha_{+}-\beta_{+}(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q).

In this case, the (unique) stabilizing solutions in
\textup{Ricc}_{1}
and
\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}
coincide.

Proof.

It follows from the proof of Proposition
3.7
that (
3.4
) holds whenever
Q\in\textup{Ricc}_{1}\cup\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}
. From (
3.4
), it in turn follows immediately that
\alpha_{+}^{\circ}
in (
2.4
) equals (
3.5
), the first operator in (
3.2
).
By (
3.3
), the second operator in (
3.2
) is equal to
(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}
. This shows that the stabilizing solutions in
\textup{Ricc}_{1}
and
\textup{Ricc}_{2}^{\prime}
coincide, and we already know from Theorem
2.1
that any stabilizing solutions in
\textup{Ricc}_{1}
are unique.
∎

Note that the uniqueness of the stabilizing solution for both Riccati equations is well-known; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
.

4.
Left versus right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization

In this section our starting point is a dichotomous realization (
2.30
). We shall also assume that
R(0)=\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}
is invertible. Consider

\displaystyle A^{\times}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&0\\
0&\alpha_{+}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}\\
\beta_{+}\end{bmatrix}(\delta-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&\gamma_{+}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{-}^{-1}+\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&-\alpha_{-}^{-1}\beta_{-}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{+}\\
\beta_{+}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&\alpha_{+}-\beta_{+}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{+}\end{bmatrix}.

We assume that
R(z)
has a right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization, so that there is a unique stabilizing solution
Q
to (
2.3
) by Theorem
2.1
. The same
Q
is the unique stabilizing solution to (
2.29
) by Corollary
3.8
. Then
Q
is also the angular operator for the space
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}^{\times}
in the sense that the spectral subspace of
A^{\times}
corresponding to
{\mathbb{D}}
is equal to
{\rm Im\,}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}Q\\
I\end{smallmatrix}\right]
; indeed, by Corollary
3.8
and (
2.29
), we have

A^{\times}\begin{bmatrix}Q\\
I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Q\\
I\end{bmatrix}\big(\alpha_{+}-\beta_{+}R(0)^{-1}(\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}Q)\big)=\begin{bmatrix}Q\\
I\end{bmatrix}\alpha_{+}^{\circ}.

Further, by (
3.3
) we have

A^{\times}\begin{bmatrix}I&Q\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I&Q\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}&0\\
\beta_{+}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1}&\alpha_{+}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix}.

To find the spectral subspace
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
of
A^{\times}
corresponding to
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
we block-diagonalize
A^{\times}
as follows. Let
Z
be the solution of the Lyapunov equation

(4.1)

Z(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}-\alpha_{+}^{\circ}Z=-\beta_{+}R(0)^{-1}\gamma_{-}\alpha_{-}^{-1},

which exists and is unique since
\sigma((\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1})\cap\sigma(\alpha_{+}^{\circ})=\emptyset
; see, e.g., Theorem I.4.1 in
[
12
]
.
Then

\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
Z&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&-Q\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}A^{\times}\begin{bmatrix}I&Q\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
-Z&I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha_{-}^{\circ})^{-1}&0\\
0&\alpha_{+}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix}.

So, the spectral subspace
{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}
of
A^{\times}
corresponding to
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
is given by

{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}I-QZ\\
-Z\end{bmatrix}.

This can now be applied to determine when there will be a left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization of
R(z)
. Indeed, this will be the case when
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}\dot{+}{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}={\mathcal{X}}
, see, e.g., Theorem 6.1 in
[
4
]
. Since
{\mathcal{X}}_{+}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I\end{bmatrix}
we see that this is the case precisely when
I-QZ
is invertible, and in that case

{\mathcal{X}}_{-}^{\times}={\rm Im\,}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
-Z(I-QZ)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

We arrive at the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
R(z)
be given by (
2.1
), with
R(z)
analytic and invertible at zero, and assume that
R(z)
admits right canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization. Let
Q
be the stabilizing solution to (
2.3
). Also let
Z
be the unique solution of (
4.1
). Then
R(z)
admits left canonical Wiener-Hopf factorization if and only if
I-QZ
is invertible.

Compare Theorem
4.1
to
[
2
]
and Theorem 12.6 in
[
5
]
, where the same question was studied, but from a different starting point.
