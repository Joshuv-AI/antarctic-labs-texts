Bidifferentials, Lagrangian projections and the Virasoro extension
Eduard Looijenga

Abstract
. Let C C be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field k k of characteristic zero. We prove that a Lagrangian supplement of H 0 ​ ( C , Ω C ) H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}) in the de Rham cohomology group H d ​ R 1 ​ ( C ) H^{1}_{dR}(C) determines and is determined by a particular type of symmetric bidifferential on C 2 C^{2} (its polar divisor must be twice the diagonal and have biresidue one along it). When k = ℂ k={\mathbb{C}} , a natural choice of such supplement is H 0 , 1 ​ ( C ) H^{0,1}(C) and we show that this corresponds with the bidifferential that after a twist is the rational 2 2 -form on C 2 C^{2} found by Biswas-Colombo-Frediani-Pirola. We determine the cohomology class carried by that 2 2 -form and define an analogue of this form as rational n n -form on C n C^{n} that is regular on the n n -point configuration space of C C . The proof relies on a local version of the above correspondence, which can be stated in terms of a complete discrete valuation ring. We use this local version also to construct in a natural manner the Virasoro extension of the Lie algebra of derivations of a local field.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this article is to show that the three items mentioned in the title are intertwined (and that we might have included a fourth, namely a curious de Rham cohomology class on
C^{2}
, but the title is already too long).
It is a spin-off of our approach to WZW theory, but as the results in question have a semi-classical (if not classical) nature—these are rather basic properties of Riemann surfaces—they may have an appeal beyond the
WZW community. This also means that applications particular to WZW theory will not be discussed here. It will however serve as a foundation for one or more subsequent papers that will be devoted to it.

To explain what the paper is about, let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. Recall that we have a short exact sequence involving its first de Rham cohomology group

0\to H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\to H^{1}_{dR}(C)\to H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C})\to 0

and that the middle term comes with a de Rham intersection pairing
H^{1}_{dR}(C)\times H^{1}_{dR}(C)\to k
.
It is nondegenerate and alternating and has
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
as a Lagrangian subspace.
It is not hard to see that the Lagrangian supplements of
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
in
H^{1}_{dR}(C)
form an affine
space (torsor) whose translation space can be identified with the vector space
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
.
We show that this affine space is canonically isomorphic with a space of bidifferentials, as defined below.

A
bidifferential

\eta
of

is an object on
C^{2}
: at

it is what looks like a
2
-form on
C^{2}
at

,
but is not quite that: if

is a local coordinate of

at

, then

is of the form
fdz_{1}dz_{2}

with

, but where we regard

as equal to
dz_{2}dz_{1}
, that is,

\eta
is considered as an element of
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C^{2},p}}\Omega_{C^{2},p}
. These bidifferentials form a

of

-modules of rank one. The bidifferentials which have a pole of order 2 along the diagonal embedding

have there what is called a biresidue; this is an element of
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
.
The component exchange

and preserves the biresidue.
The

-invariant sections

0\to\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\to H^{0}(C^{2},\Omega^{(2)}_{C}(2\Delta_{C}))^{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Bires}}k\to 0.

The preimage of

We arrive at this identification via a local version, which concerns a complete discrete valuation ring
{\mathcal{O}}
with residue field

(think of the

-adic completion of

). Let

stand for the universal continuous

-derivation and extend this to its field of fractions

for the module bidifferentials for which we have similarly defined extension

(1.1)

0\to(\Omega^{(2)})^{\sigma}\to(\Omega^{(2)})^{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Bires}}k\to 0.

Proposition

{\mathcal{O}}/k
in

.

In the next two sections we take

can be made into a

We show that then the Lagrangian supplement of

.
We express this class in terms of the Hodge decomposition of

In the last section we return to the setting of Section

2.
Bidifferentials and Lagrangian projections

In this section,
k
is a field of characteristic zero. As of Subsection
2.2
, this field is algebraically closed and

is a connected projective smooth curve over
k
whose genus we denote by

2.1.
Biresidues and projective structures

Let
{\mathcal{O}}
be a complete discrete valuation ring whose residue field
k
is of characteristic zero.
We write
{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathcal{O}}
for its maximal ideal and
K
for its field of fractions
K
.
We regard
{\mathcal{O}}
and associated
{\mathcal{O}}
-modules (such as
K
) as endowed with the
{\mathfrak{m}}
-adic topology.
The example to keep in mind is the formal completion of a local ring of a smooth curve.
We denote by
\theta
the
{\mathcal{O}}
-module of continuous
k
-derivations
{\mathcal{O}}\to{\mathcal{O}}
and by

d:{\mathcal{O}}\to\Omega
the universal continuous
k
-derivation. Both
\theta
and
\Omega
are
{\mathcal{O}}
-modules of rank one and each others
{\mathcal{O}}
-dual. We use
K
as a subscript when we tensor up over
{\mathcal{O}}
with
K
. For example,
\Omega_{K}:=K\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}}\Omega
. A uniformizer
t\in{\mathfrak{m}}
identifies
{\mathcal{O}}
with
k[[t]]
,
K
with
k((t))
,
\Omega
with
{\mathcal{O}}dt
and
\theta
with
{\mathcal{O}}\frac{d}{dt}
.

We may sometimes describe a concept in terms of a uniformizer (always denoted
t
), but it is then understood that the notion is independent of this choice. For example, there is a naturally defined residue map
\operatorname{Res}\colon\Omega_{K}\to k
, which assigns to
\alpha=\sum_{i}a_{i}t^{i-1}dt\in\Omega_{K}
the coefficient
a_{0}
. The residue pairing

K\times\Omega_{K}\to k,\quad(f,\alpha)\mapsto\operatorname{Res}(f\alpha)

is a perfect pairing of topological
k
-vector spaces. It gives rise to an antisymmetric
k
-bilinear pairing

(f,g)\in K\times K\mapsto\langle f|g\rangle:=\operatorname{Res}(f\,dg)\in k

whose kernel is
k\subset K
. For example,
\langle t^{p}|t^{q}\rangle=q\delta_{p+q,0}
. We regard the quotient
k
-vector space
K^{\prime}:=K/k
endowed with the residue pairing as a topological symplectic space. The derivation
d
maps
K^{\prime}
isomorphically onto the subspace
\Omega^{\prime}_{K}\subset\Omega_{K}
of differentials with zero residue. The space
K^{\prime}
contains the image
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathfrak{m}}
in
K^{\prime}
(this is also the image of
{\mathcal{O}}
) as a Lagrangian subspace; note that this image is isomorphically mapped by
d
onto
\Omega
.

Remark 2.1
.

For every
D\in\theta_{K}
,
f,g\in K
, we have
\operatorname{Res}(Df.dg)+\operatorname{Res}(Dg.df)=0
, for

Df.dg+f.dDg=Df.dg-df.Dg+d(f.Dg)=d(f.Dg)

(the first two terms cancel: if
D=u\frac{d}{dt}
, then
Df.dg=uf^{\prime}g^{\prime}dt=df.Dg
). In other words,
D
infinitesimally preserves the simplectic form on
K^{\prime}
. This suggests that we may regard
\theta_{K}
as the (topological) Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{sp}}(K^{\prime})
. We shall however find that for this interpretation to be useful, it needs to be modified.

We denote the
{\mathfrak{m}}\otimes_{k}{\mathfrak{m}}
-adic completion of
{\mathcal{O}}\otimes_{k}{\mathcal{O}}
resp.
K\otimes_{k}K
by
{\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}
resp.
K^{(2)}
. So these consist of the formal power series

\sum_{i,j}a_{k_{1},k_{2}}t_{1}^{k_{1}}t_{2}^{k_{2}}
with
a_{k_{1},k_{2}}=0
when
\min{\{k_{1},k_{2}\}}
is smaller
than zero resp. some integer. The diagonal defines a restriction map
\Delta^{*}\colon{\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}\to{\mathcal{O}}
which takes

f_{1}\otimes f_{2}
to
f_{1}f_{2}
. Its kernel, which we denote by
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}\subset{\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}
, is the ideal generated by
t_{1}-t_{2}
and hence principal.

The
{\mathcal{O}}^{(2)}
-module
\Omega^{(2)}:=\Omega\hat{\otimes}_{k}\Omega
resp.

K^{(2)}
-module
\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}:=\Omega_{K}\hat{\otimes}_{k}\Omega_{K}
of
bidifferentials
is free of rank one and generated by
dt_{1}dt_{2}
. We here regard
dt_{1}dt_{2}
as a symmetric tensor, so that it is invariant under the transposition
\sigma
which exchanges the two factors:
dt_{1}dt_{2}=dt_{2}dt_{1}
. This makes
\sigma
act on
\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}
.

Let
\Omega^{\otimes 2}:=\Omega\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}\Omega
stand for the
{\mathcal{O}}
-module of quadratic differentials
(so this has
(dt)^{2}
as a generator). Restriction to the diagonal defines a map

\Delta^{*}\colon\Omega^{(2)}\to\Omega^{\otimes 2}
(which takes
dt_{1}dt_{2}
to
(dt)^{2}
) whose kernel is
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}\Omega^{(2)}
.
There is a canonically defined ‘biresidue map’

\operatorname{Bires}\colon{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}\to K,

where
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}
stands for
\cup_{n>0}{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-n}
. In terms of our uniformizer: if we expand
\eta\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}
as a series

\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}(t_{1}-t_{2})^{n}f_{n}(t_{2})dt_{1}dt_{2}
(with
f_{n}\in K
), then
\operatorname{Bires}(\eta)=f_{-2}(t)
.
We could here have exchanged the roles of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
: the biresidue is
\sigma
-invariant.

We also have the somewhat more conventional looking residue map

\operatorname{Res}_{1}\colon{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}\to K

which assumes
t_{1}
to be small compared with
t_{2}
: it is obtained by expanding

(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-1}
as a series
t_{2}^{-1}(t_{1}/t_{2}-1)^{-1}=-\sum_{i\geq 0}t_{1}^{i}t_{2}^{-1-i}
and then
take the ordinary residue in zero with respect to the
t_{1}
-variable.
The residue
\operatorname{Res}_{2}\colon{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}\to K
is similarly defined (we then expand

(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-1}
as
t_{1}^{-1}(1-t_{2}/t_{1})^{-1}=\sum_{i\geq 0}t_{1}^{-1-i}t_{2}^{i}
).
We also have a residue for
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}
along the diagonal, but here the ordering matters because
\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}
consists of bidifferentials, not of
2
-forms. We define

\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}:{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}\to\Omega_{K},

the notation suggesting that the second variable is fixed and it the first one that moves. So if we write

\eta\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}\Omega_{K^{(2)}}^{(2)}
as
\eta_{1}dt_{2}
, where
\eta_{1}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-\infty}K^{(2)}dt_{1}
(we just split off
dt_{2}
), then
\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}(\eta)
is obtained by taking the standard residue of
\eta_{1}
along
\Delta
and multiply the result with
dt_{2}
. In terms of the above expansion,
\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}(\eta)=f_{1}(t)dt
.
Note that
\operatorname{Res}_{2\to 1}(\eta)=-\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}(\eta)
.

The biresidue restricted to
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega^{(2)}
takes values in
{\mathcal{O}}

and has kernel
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-1}\Omega^{(2)}
. Consider the quotient
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega^{(2)}/\Omega^{(2)}
.
It inherits an action of
\sigma
. Its
\sigma
-anti-invariant part is
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-1}\Omega^{(2)}/\Omega^{(2)}
, which is also the kernel of the biresidue map
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega^{(2)}/\Omega^{(2)}\to{\mathcal{O}}
.
We denote by
\hat{\Omega}^{\otimes 2}
the
\sigma
-invariant part of
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega^{(2)}/\Omega^{(2)}
on which the biresidue is constant, i.e., takes its values in
k
. So we have an exact sequence of
k
-vector spaces

(2.1)

0\to\Omega^{\otimes 2}\to\hat{\Omega}^{\otimes 2}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Bires}}k\to 0.

Remark 2.2
.

If
\eta\in\hat{\Omega}^{\otimes 2}
has biresidue constant equal to
1
(and so splits the above sequence), then
it has the form
(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-2}dt_{1}dt_{2}+f(\tau)dt_{1}dt_{2}
(
\tau
uniformizes the diagonal: we can substitute
t_{1}
or
t_{2}
for it). It is
well known that there is uniformizer

of
{\mathcal{O}}
such that the second term becomes zero and that such a

is unique up to a fractional linear transformation: any other uniformizer

with this property is of the
form
t/(ct+d)
with

and

(for an arbitrary uniformizer the second term is

times the Schwarzian derivative). So to give

is
equivalent to giving

a projective structure (that is, a
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(k)
-orbit of isomorphisms of

with a completed local ring of

). Since

, the polynomial subalgebra of
K

generated by

only depends on

. This subalgebra defines a supplement of
{\mathfrak{m}}
in
K
and is Lagrangian for the residue pairing. We will however be interested in another Lagrangian supplement
(which need not be multiplicatively closed) namely the one obtained in Proposition

below.

We shall consider several variations on the short exact sequence (
2.1
). The one that is relevant here is
the preimage of
k\subset K
under the biresidue map
{\mathcal{I}}_{\Delta}^{-2}\Omega^{(2)}\to K
. We denote this preimage
\hat{\Omega}^{(2)}
, so that we have an exact sequence

. The transposition

acts on

nontrivially, but its anti-invariants lie of course in

. We focus on the
\sigma
-invariant part

(2.2)

0\to(\Omega^{(2)})^{\sigma}\to(\hat{\Omega}^{(2)})^{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Bires}}k\to 0.

We recall that

and
\Omega^{\prime}_{K}:=\ker(\operatorname{Res}\colon\Omega_{K}\to k)

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\eta\in(\hat{\Omega}^{(2)})^{\sigma}
have biresidue constant

.

Then
\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}\pi_{1}^{*}(f)\eta=df
for every

. Furthermore, the map

S_{\eta}\colon K\to\Omega_{K}\quad f\mapsto\operatorname{Res}_{1}\pi_{1}^{*}(f)\eta,

is zero on

and its image is a Lagrangian supplement of

. If we use the universal derivation to identify

with
\Omega^{\prime}_{K}
, so that

factors though an endomorphism

is a projector with kernel

and image a Lagrangian supplement

of

in

.

In particular,

factors through a Lagrangian

\Pi_{\zeta}
of

onto

with kernel

.

The assignment

establishes an isomorphism between the elements of

with biresidu
1

{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
in

. This is an isomorphism of
\Omega^{(2)}
-torsors.

Proof.

By definition
\eta=(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-2}dt_{1}dt_{2}+\eta_{0}
for some symmetric

\eta_{0}\in\Omega^{(2)}
.
The first assertion follows from the fact that

\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}\pi_{1}^{*}(f)\eta=\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}f(t_{1})(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-2}dt_{1}dt_{2}=f^{\prime}(t_{2})dt_{2},

where for the last equality we used a classical residue property.

In order to check the assertions regarding

\textstyle\operatorname{Res}_{1}t_{1}^{n}(t_{1}-t_{2})^{-2}dt_{1}dt_{2}=\operatorname{Res}_{1}t_{1}^{n}t_{2}^{-2}(t_{1}/t_{2}-1)^{-2}dt_{1}dt_{2}=\\
=\textstyle\operatorname{Res}_{1}t_{1}^{n}t_{2}^{-2}\sum_{i\geq 0}(i+1)(t_{1}/t_{2})^{i}dt_{1}dt_{2}=\operatorname{Res}_{1}\sum_{i\geq 0}(i+1)t_{1}^{n+i}t_{2}^{-2-i}dt_{1}dt_{2}.

, or equivalently,

(otherwise the residue is zero) and then gives

.

We next expand

with

. Then

\textstyle\operatorname{Res}_{1}(t_{1}^{n}\eta_{0})=\sum_{i\geq 1}\operatorname{Res}_{1}t_{1}^{i-1+n}dt_{1}.\pi_{2}^{*}(df_{i})

and this is zero unless

.
It follows that

vanishes on

and takes

(

The

of

clearly supplements

in

and we just proved that

is the image of

Finally, any supplement

2.2.
The sheaf of projective structures

As of now,

is algebraically closed (and of characteristic zero) and

a connected projective smooth curve over
k
of genus by

. This subsection as well as the next contain little that is new; they merely recall known material.

The involution of

which exchanges factors will be denoted by

. We write

\Omega_{C}^{(2)}:=\pi_{1}^{*}\Omega_{C}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C^{2}}}\pi_{2}^{*}\Omega_{C}

for the sheaf of bidifferentials on

. We regard this however as a subsheaf of

(in terms of a pair of local coordinates

on

with

), so that
\Omega_{C}^{(2)}
comes with an action of

Let

be the diagonal embedding or just stand for its image.
It is clear that
\Omega_{C}^{(2)}(2\Delta_{C})/\Omega_{C}^{(2)}(-\Delta_{C})
is supported by
\Delta_{C}
and that the component exchange
\sigma
acts on this sheaf. The
\sigma
-anti-invariant part maps isomorphically to
\Omega_{C^{2}}^{2}(\Delta_{C})/\Omega_{C^{2}}^{2}
(taking the residue along
\Delta_{C}
identifies with sheaf with
\Omega_{C}
) and the
\sigma
-invariant part is an extension

where the arrow to
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
is given by the biresidue. Let us write
\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{\otimes 2}
for the middle term of the exact sequence above, so that have the exact sequence of abelian sheaves

(2.3)

0\to\Omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}\to\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{\otimes 2}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{C}\to 0,

(NB: the middle term is
not
a sheaf of
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
-modules).
The preimage of
1\in{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
is defines a
\Omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}
-torsor, which we may interpret as the sheaf of
projective structure on
C
. So the following (well-known) lemma is also a consequence of the existence of a projective structure on

.

Lemma 2.4
.

The section sequence of (
2.3
) gives the exact sequence

(2.4)

0\to H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{\otimes 2})\to H^{0}(C,\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{\otimes 2})\to k\to 0.

Proof.

When
g(C)>1
, this is immediate from the vanishing of
H^{1}(C,\Omega_{C}^{\otimes 2})
.
In the two remaining cases, we proceed as follows.

When
g(C)=0
, the space
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C}^{\otimes 2})
is zero and then its is a matter of finding a
nonzero element of
H^{0}(C,\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{\otimes 2})\cong k
. Indeed, in terms of an affine coordinate

z
, the form
(z_{1}-z_{2})^{-2}dz_{1}dz_{2}
does the job.

When
g(C)=1
, then choose a nonzero differential

\alpha
on
C
(which is then necessarily translation invariant). Choose also

and a rational differential
\beta
on
C
whose whose pole divisor is
2(o)
and which is

(for example

, where

is then a rational
2
-form on
C^{2}
whose polar divisor
is
2\Delta_{C}
and which is invariant with respect to the transposition. Its biresidue is nonzero and hence the sequence (
2.4
)is still exact.
∎

The extension (
2.3
) lifts to a corresponding extension over all of
C^{2}

(2.5)

0\to\Omega_{C}^{(2)}\to\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{(2)}\to\Delta_{C*}{\mathcal{O}}_{C}\to 0.

We can interpret a result of Biswas-Raina (Prop. 2.10 of
[
1

) as saying that the associated long exact cohomology sequence splits up in two short exact sequences, the first of which is

(2.6)

0\to H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})^{\otimes 2}\to H^{0}(C^{2},\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{(2)})\to k\to 0

with
\sigma
acting on
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})^{\otimes 2}
by exchanging tensor factors, but acting trivially on

.

Question 2.5
.

The second exact sequence can be written as

0\to H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\oplus H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\to H^{1}(C^{2},\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{(2)})\to k\to 0,

where
\sigma
acts on
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\oplus H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
by exchanging summands (and acting trivially on
k
). Although we shall not use that sequence, we mention it, because its

-invariant part produces a canonical extension of
k
by
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
(or dually, an extension of
H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C})
by
k
). We wonder whether or not that extension is canonically split (we suspect it is not).

2.3.
Brief review of the Rham cohomology of
C

We first fix some notation. For
p\in C
, we denote the local ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{C,p}

completed with respect to its maximal idea by
{\mathcal{O}}_{p}
. This is a complete discrete valuation ring as considered at the beginning of this section. We adopt a notation that agrees with the notation introduced there, so that

{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{p}\subset K_{p}
,
\theta_{p}\subset\theta_{K_{p}}
, and
\Omega_{p}\subset\Omega_{K_{p}}
have the obvious meaning. We extend this to the case of a finite subset
P\subset C
. So

{\mathcal{O}}_{P}:=\prod_{p\in P}{\mathcal{O}}_{p}
and likewise for the other items. We sometimes regard these as (sections of) sheaves over
P
.

We recall the (adelic) description of the first cohomology of a quasi-coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{F}}
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
-modules (as for example explained in Ch. II of Serre’s book
[
6
]
).
Let
P\subset C
be a finite nonempty subset. Then an ‘affine covering’ of
C
consists of the affine curve
C\smallsetminus P\subset C
and a formal neighborhood of
P
. To be precise, if
{\mathcal{F}}
is a coherent
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
-module, then
H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{F}})
can be obtained in these terms as follows. Denote by
{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]
the group of sections of
{\mathcal{F}}
over
C\smallsetminus P
. It is clear that
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]
is the
k
-algebra
k[C\smallsetminus P]
defining the affine curve

C\smallsetminus P
and so
{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]
is a
k[C\smallsetminus P]
-module. We have a natural map

{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]\to K_{P}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}
, where
i_{P}:P\subset C
. If we denote the cokernel of its composite with
K_{P}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}\to(K_{P}/{\mathcal{O}}_{P})\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}
provisionally by
C(P)
, then
it is easy to check that for a nonempty finite
Q\supset P
we have a natural
k
-linear isomorphism

C(P)\xrightarrow{\cong}C(Q)
.
So
C(P)
maps isomorphically the inductive limit
\varinjlim_{Q}C_{Q}
and has therefore an intrinsic meaning. Indeed, this is the adelic description of
H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{F}})
by means of ‘repartitions’. We have in particular an exact sequence

{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]\to(K_{P}/{\mathcal{O}}_{P})\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}\to H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{F}})\to 0.

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is locally free, the maps from
i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}
and

{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]
to
K_{P}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}
are both injective, so if we regard these as inclusions, then

H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{F}})\cong K_{P}\otimes_{{\mathcal{O}}_{C,P}}i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}/\big(i_{P}^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}+{\mathcal{F}}[C\smallsetminus P]\big).

For example, with the notional conventions above,

H^{1}(C,\Omega_{C})\cong\Omega_{K_{P}}/(\Omega_{P}+\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P])
. It is a basic fact
(and a consequence of Riemann-Roch) that a polar part of a
1
-form at
P
, i.e., an element of

\Omega_{K_{P}}/\Omega_{P}
, is realized by an element of
\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]
if and only if
that polar part has zero residue sum. This means that taking the residue sum gives a well-defined isomorphism

\operatorname{Tr}_{C}:H^{1}(C,\Omega_{C})\cong\Omega_{K_{P}}/(\Omega_{P}+\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P])\xrightarrow{\sum_{p\in P}\operatorname{Res}_{p}}k.

This is also called the
trace map
(whence the notation). It is the de Rham analogue of integration, but the value of the latter differs by a factor
2\pi\sqrt{-1}
.

Consider the following filtration of
K_{P}
:

(2.7)

0\subset k[C\smallsetminus P]\subset d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]+k[C\smallsetminus P]\subset d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]\subset K_{P}.

Here
\Omega_{C}[C]=H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
and so
d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]
consists of the
f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{p}
for which
df
is
the restriction of a regular
1
-form. Hence the first subquotient
(d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]+k[C\smallsetminus P])/k[C\smallsetminus P]
maps isomorphically onto
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
. For the second subquotient, we note that

d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]\cap{\mathcal{O}}_{P}=d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]
and hence

\frac{d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]}{d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]+k[C\smallsetminus P]}\cong\frac{d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]+{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}{k[C\smallsetminus P]+{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}\cong\frac{K_{P}}{k[C\smallsetminus P]+{\mathcal{O}}_{P}}\cong H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C}).

By the preceding

d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]/k[C\smallsetminus P]
may be identified with the cokernel of

d:k[C\smallsetminus P]\to\Omega_{C}^{\prime}[C\smallsetminus P]
, where
\Omega_{C}^{\prime}[C\smallsetminus P]\subset\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]
is space of
1
-forms on
C\smallsetminus P
having zero residue sum. This cokernel is the first De Rham cohomology space
H^{1}_{dR}(C)
of
C
. We thus obtain the familiar short exact sequence

(2.8)

0\to H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\to H^{1}_{dR}(C)\to H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C})\to 0.

The residue pairing

\textstyle(f,g)\in K_{P}\times K_{P}\mapsto\langle f|g\rangle:=\sum_{p\in P}\operatorname{Res}_{p}(fdg)\in k

has the property that of the filtration (
2.7
) of
K_{P}
, the subspaces
k[C\smallsetminus P]
and
d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus P]
are each others annihilator. So it induces a perfect pairing

(2.9)

\langle\;|\;\rangle\colon H^{1}_{dR}(C)\times H^{1}_{dR}(C)\to k.

This is the intersection pairing for de Rham cohomology, which in case
k={\mathbb{C}}
differs from its topological analogue by a factor
2\pi\sqrt{-1}
. The middle term of the filtration
d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C]+k[C\smallsetminus P]
is its own annihilator and so

H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\hookrightarrow H^{1}_{dR}(C)
has a Lagrangian image. This implies that
we have a perfect pairing
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\times H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C})\to k
. This is of course just the Serre duality pairing; it can be obtained as the composite of taking the cup product
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})\times H^{1}(C,{\mathcal{O}}_{C})\to H^{1}(C,\Omega_{C})
, followed by the trace map

\operatorname{Tr}_{C}:H^{1}(C,\Omega_{C})\to k
.

2.4.
Lagrangian projections in
H^{1}_{dR}(C)

We shall use a relative version of the above discussion, namely one which concerns the projection
\pi_{2}:C\times C\to C
onto the second factor.

In what follows we fix a symmetric
\eta\in H^{0}(C^{2},\hat{\Omega}_{C}^{(2)})
with biresidue
1
.
There is an associated rational
2
-form on
C^{2}
(with polar divisor
2\Delta_{C}
) that we shall denote by

(p,q)\in C^{2}
and we have local coordinates

at

at

\alpha\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}_{dR}(C)\mapsto\pi_{2*}(\pi_{1}^{*}(\alpha)\cup\zeta)\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}_{dR}(C).

We will however work with

We here focus on the degree 1 part (which is the most interesting anyway). In that case

.
We fix some

, so that we can identify

d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus\{p\}]/k[C\smallsetminus\{p\}]

We then show how the above edomorphism can be understood on the form level as inducing a

which preserves

and

(thereby inducing an endomorphism of

).

We define
\Pi^{dR}_{\eta}(\alpha)\in\Omega[C\smallsetminus\{p\}]
for
\alpha\in\Omega[C\smallsetminus\{p\}]
as follows. Given

, we choose a formal local parameter

at
q
and then write
the coherent restriction of
\eta
to

as
\eta(q)dz_{2}(q)
, where
\eta(q)
is a

1
-form on
C
with a pole of order 2 at

and
dz(q)
denotes the value of

in

(so this is an element of the cotangent space of
C
at

) and stipulate that the value of

is
\langle\alpha|\eta(q)\rangle dz(q)
.
In order to compute

, let us first assume

. The residue formula shows that the intersection product has two contributions coming from

and
q

\alpha
in

(the formal completion of

for some

. So then

\Pi^{dR}_{\eta}(\alpha)(q)=\big(\operatorname{Res}_{q}f_{q}\eta(q)+\operatorname{Res}_{p}f_{p}\eta(q)\big)dz(q)

If

for some

, then by the residue theorem applied to

, the two residues have zero sum and hence
\Pi^{dR}_{\eta}(\alpha)(q)=0
.
This description makes it clear that by regarding

as a variable,
\Pi^{dR}_{\eta}
takes

and kills the exact forms. It therefore induces an endomorphism of

(that we shall also denote by

When

\operatorname{Res}_{1\to 2}f_{p}\eta+\operatorname{Res}_{1}f_{p}\eta,

.

The map

a Lagrangian projection

onto

Proof.

Since
d{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}\cap d^{-1}\Omega_{C}[C\smallsetminus\{p\}]=H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
, it follows that
T_{\eta}
is a
Lagrangian projection onto

.

It is clear from the definition that the symmetric

.

3.
The class of the canonical 2-form

In this section and the next we take the complex field as base field. We adhere to the conventions of Hodge theory, which has a Betti component and a de Rham component. In order to keep the two compatible in the sense that Betti cohomology with complex coefficients is identified with de Rham cohomology, the former will typically appear with Tate twists.

3.1.
Betti and de Rham cohomology

If
M
is a complex-projective manifold of complex dimension
m
, and
i\colon N\subset M
is a closed complex submanifold of complex codimension
d
, then the Gysin map
takes the form

(3.1)

i_{!}\colon H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(N)\to H^{2d+{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}}(M)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}(d).

We use the occasion to recall that this map (
3.1
) is
H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(M)
-linear, if we consider
H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(N)
a
H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(M)
-module via
the ring homomorphism
i^{*}\colon H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(M)\to H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(N)
: if
\beta\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(N)
and
\alpha\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(M)
, then

(3.2)

i_{!}(i^{*}\alpha\cup\beta)=\alpha\cup i_{!}(\beta).

The Gysin map is weight preserving, but the degree increases by
2d
.
In particular, we have defined the
class
,

cl(N):=i_{!}(1)\in H^{2d}(M)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}(d),

which represents the Hodge-Poincaré dual of
N
in
M
. It is
(2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{d}
times its topological Poincaré dual when we use the complex orientation. So here
{\mathbb{Z}}(d)
functions as the (trivial) local system of orientations of the normal bundle of
i
. If we take for
N
a singleton, and
M
is connected, then this gives the orientation class

[M]:=i_{!}(1)\in H^{2m}(M)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}(m).

The resulting map
H_{2m}(M)\to{\mathbb{Z}}(m)
(given by integration over
M
) is an isomorphism.

Let
C
be a nonsingular connected complex-projective curve of genus
g
. As before, we denote by
\sigma
the involution of
C^{2}
which exchanges its factors and hence the two projections
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\colon C^{2}\to C
. So
\sigma
acts on the Künneth component
H^{k}(C)\otimes H^{l}(C)
of
H^{k+l}(C^{2})
by

\sigma^{*}(\alpha\otimes\beta)=\sigma^{*}(\pi_{1}^{*}\alpha\cup\pi_{2}^{*}\beta)=\pi_{2}^{*}\alpha\cup\pi_{1}^{*}\beta=(-1)^{kl}\pi_{1}^{*}\beta\cup\pi_{2}^{*}\alpha=(-1)^{kl}\beta\otimes\alpha.

It follows that the group of

\sigma
-invariants in
H^{2}(C^{2})
is the span of the
antisymmetric
tensors in
H^{1}(C)\otimes H^{1}(C)
and

[C]\otimes 1+1\otimes[C]
, whereas the group of

\sigma
-anti-invariants
in
H^{2}(C^{2})
(the subgroup of
H^{2}(C^{2})
on which
\sigma
as multiplication by
(-1)
), is the span of the
symmetric
tensors
\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{1}(C)\subset H^{1}(C)\otimes H^{1}(C)
and
[C]\otimes 1-1\otimes[C]
.

The intersection pairing on
H_{1}(C)
should be regarded as a bilinear map
H_{1}(C)\times H_{1}(C)\to{\mathbb{Z}}(1)
.
It therefore defines a tensor
\delta\in H^{1}(C)\otimes H^{1}(C)\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}(-1)
. Concretely, if
(\alpha_{\pm 1},\dots,\alpha_{\pm g})
is a basis of
H^{1}(C)
such that
\alpha_{i}\cup\alpha_{-j}=\operatorname{sgn}(i)\delta_{ij}.[C]
, then

(3.3)

\textstyle\delta=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(-\alpha_{i}\otimes\alpha_{-i}+\alpha_{-i}\otimes\alpha_{i}).

So
\sigma^{*}
fixes
\delta
. In fact,

(3.4)

[\Delta_{C}]=[C]\otimes 1+\delta+1\otimes[C].

It follows from the identity (
3.2
) that

\Delta_{!}([C])=[C]\otimes[C]
and that for
\alpha\in H^{1}(C)
,

(3.5)

\Delta_{!}(\alpha)=\alpha\otimes[C]+[C]\otimes\alpha.

It is a little exercise to check that in
H^{2}(C^{3})
we have the following identity

(3.6)

\pi_{1,2}^{*}\delta\cup\pi_{2,3}^{*}\delta=\pi_{1,3}^{*}\delta\cup\pi_{2}^{*}[C],

where the subscripts of
\pi
indicate the projections on the corresponding factors.

3.2.
The canonical
2
-form

The configuration space
\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{2}(C)
is the complement of the image of the diagonal embedding
\Delta_{C}\colon C\to C^{2}
. The map
\Delta_{C!}\colon H^{1}(C)(-1)\to H^{3}(C^{2})
is via duality identified with
\Delta_{C*}\colon H_{1}(C)\to H_{1}(C^{2})
which takes
a\in H_{1}(C)
to
a\otimes 1+1\otimes a
. This map is clearly injective. So the Gysin sequence for
\Delta_{C}
gives the short exact sequence

(3.7)

0\to H^{0}(C)(-1)\xrightarrow{\Delta_{C!}}H^{2}(C^{2})\to H^{2}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{2}(C))\to 0.

Let
\zeta\in H^{0}(C^{2},\Omega_{C}^{2}(2\Delta_{C}))
have biresidue constant equal to one along the diagonal. Since taking the anti-invariant part under the exchange map
\sigma
does not affect the biresidue, we can also assume that
\sigma^{*}\zeta=-\zeta
. It is then unique up an anti-invariant element of
H^{0}(C^{2},\Omega_{C}^{2})\cong H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})^{\otimes 2}
, i.e., an element of

\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
(which has Hodge type
(2,0)
). A priori, it defines an anti-invariant class in
H^{2}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{2}(C);{\mathbb{C}})
, but the short exact sequence (
3.7
) shows that the restriction map
H^{2}(C^{2};{\mathbb{C}})\to H^{2}(\mathscr{C}\!\mathit{onf}\!_{2}(C);{\mathbb{C}})
induces an isomorphism on their anti-invariant parts, and hence defines a class in

H^{2}(C^{2};{\mathbb{C}})
. Biswas-Colombo-Frediani-Pirola
[
2
]
prove that there exists a unique anti-invariant
\zeta_{C}\in H^{0}(C^{2},\Omega_{C}^{2}(2\Delta_{C}))
of pure Hodge type
(1,1)
(their Thm. 5.4).
The theorem below identifies this class, as well as the endomorphism of
H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(C)
it defines via the isomorphism

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}:H^{2}(C^{2})\xrightarrow{\cong}\operatorname{Hom}(H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(C),H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(C)(-1)),\quad\zeta\mapsto{\mathcal{T}}_{\zeta},\text{ with}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\zeta}:\alpha\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(C)\mapsto\pi_{2*}(\pi_{1}^{*}\alpha\cup\xi)\in H^{\scriptscriptstyle\bullet}(C)(-1).

We reprove the characterization of
\zeta_{C}
in
[
2
]
along the way.

Theorem 3.1
.

There is a unique section
\zeta_{C}
of
\Omega^{2}_{C^{2}}(2\Delta_{C})
which is anti-invariant under the exchange map
\sigma
, whose double residue along the diagonal is
1
. Its class lies in
H^{2}(C;{\mathbb{R}}(1))
, is of pure Hodge type
(1,1)
, and is represented by

\textstyle-{\tfrac{1}{2}}[C]\otimes 1+1\otimes{\tfrac{1}{2}}[C]+\sum_{i=1}^{g}(\omega_{i}\otimes\bar{\omega}_{i}+\bar{\omega}_{i}\otimes\omega_{i}),

where
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{g}
is a basis of
H^{0}(C,\Omega_{C})
with the property that

Proposition 3.5

The self-intersection of the (real) class of

4.
A generalization for higher powers of the curve

We will here find a higher order generalization of the

We here identify

{\mathbb{Z}}/n
so that

is thought of as

4.1.
The form
\zeta_{n}

Let
\Delta_{ij}
stand for the diagonal divisor in
C^{n}
defined by
z_{i}=z_{j}
and consider for
n\geq 2
the divisor

\displaystyle\textstyle E_{n}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}/n}\Delta_{i,i+1},

(so that
E_{2}=2\Delta_{0,1}
).
Note that
E_{n}
is a normal crossing divisor away from the main diagonal. Let
\Delta_{pr}\colon C\hookrightarrow C^{n}
be the main diagonal.
By taking successive residues along
\Delta_{0,n-1},\dots\Delta_{2,1}
and finally a biresidue along
\Delta_{1,0}
, we see that the coherent pull-back
\Delta_{pr}^{*}\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(E_{n})
is canonically isomorphic with
{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
. We denote the kernel of the restriction
\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(E_{n})\to{\mathcal{O}}_{C}
by
\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(\log E_{n})
. so that we have a short exact sequence

0\to\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(\log E_{n})\to\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(E_{n})\to\Delta_{pr*}{\mathcal{O}}_{C}\to 0.

The reason for this notation this is that in terms of a local chart
z
at
p
,
the ideal defining the main diagonal at

is generated by
(z_{i}-z_{i+1})
and so

\textstyle\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(\log E_{n})=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}/n}{\mathcal{O}}_{C^{n}}(-\Delta_{i,i+1})\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(E_{n})=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}/n}\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(\sum_{j\not=i}\Delta_{j,j+1}).

Since each sum
D_{n}^{i,i+1}:=\sum_{j\not=i}\Delta_{j,j+1}
is a normal crossing divisor, this sheaf consists of logarithmic forms. In order to give this a Hodge theoretic interpretation, we better first blow up
C^{n}
along its main diagonal.
Let

be this blowup and write
\tilde{E}_{n}
for the strict transform of
E_{n}
and

for the preimage of the main diagonal.
Then
\tilde{E}_{n}+E(f)
is a normal crossing divisor. We have
f^{*}\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(\log E_{n})=\Omega^{n}_{\tilde{C}^{n}}(\tilde{E}_{n}+E(f))
and
f^{*}\Omega^{n}_{C^{n}}(E_{n})=\Omega^{n}_{\tilde{C}^{n}}(\tilde{E}_{n}+2E(f_{n}))
.

Since
\tilde{E}_{n}+E(f)
is a normal crossing divisor, a theorem of Deligne
[
3
]
, Th. (3.2.5) tells us that the natural map

H^{0}(\tilde{C}^{n},\Omega^{n}_{\tilde{C}^{n}}(\tilde{E}_{n}+E(f)))\to H^{n}(\tilde{C}^{n}\smallsetminus\tilde{E}_{n})\cong H^{n}(C^{n}\smallsetminus E_{n})

is injective.

We write
D_{n}
for
D^{0,1}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\Delta_{i,i+1}
, so that
E_{n}=D_{n}+\Delta_{n-1,0}(C^{n-1})
.

Let

stand for the

. We sometimes regard this as the quotient algebra

.
