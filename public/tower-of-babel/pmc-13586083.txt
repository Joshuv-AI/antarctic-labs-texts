Self-report Instruments for Anxiety, Depression, and Stress in Adolescents: A Scoping Review of Psychometric Properties and Cultural Applicability
Authors: Divyani Srivastava, Dimpy Mahanta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of the Review:This scoping review mapped self-report instruments assessing anxiety, depression, and stress among adolescents, focusing on their psychometric properties and cultural applicability in the Indian context. It was anticipated that most instruments would be developed and validated in Western settings, with limited validation in Indian adolescent samples; that multidimensional tools covering all three constructs would be fewer than single-construct measures, and that cultural and linguistic adaptation for Indian adolescents would be insufficient.Methods:Following Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines, a systematic search was conducted across PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Google Scholar on July 28, 2024, to identify studies reporting psychometric evaluations of self-report instruments measuring these constructs in adolescents aged 10–19 years, published in English in peer-reviewed journals.Results:Of 2,812 records identified, 15 instruments were retained after deduplication and screening, and most showed acceptable internal consistency and structural validity per COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN)-informed criteria. Most instruments originated in Western populations; validation in Indian samples remained limited, and none combined all three constructs into a single measure.Conclusions:Existing instruments offer a useful starting point for assessing adolescent anxiety, depression, and stress, but their fit for the Indian context is uneven, with gaps in test–retest evidence, adolescent-specific norms, and cultural adaptation. Progress would benefit from co-development with Indian adolescents, bilingual validation, and evidence gathered mainly from school and clinical settings.Keywords: Adolescents, anxiety, depression, psychometric properties, stress

Adolescence is widely regarded as one of the most important periods of development, marked by rapid hormonal, cognitive, and social change. 1 This crucial stage of development leaves adolescents especially exposed to different intense emotions that could lead to stress, depression, and anxiety. The World Health Organization defines adolescence as the period from 10 to 19 years of age. 2 Adolescence typically begins toward the end of childhood. It gradually gives way to adulthood, a period marked by new, more intense emotions that shift more often than at other stages of life.3,4 When these emotions go unregulated, they are often linked to a range of difficulties, including the onset of psychopathology. 5 This makes adolescence a key window for identifying and measuring symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. Yet these symptoms are frequently dismissed as ordinary growing pains and left unaddressed. 6

Depression involves a defined cluster of symptoms, and its clinical and diagnostic features are largely similar in adolescents and adults. 7 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV (DSM-IV) and the International Classification of Diseases-10 define depression in broadly similar terms. However, DSM-IV makes an exception for children and adolescents by allowing irritable mood, rather than depressed mood, as a core diagnostic criterion. 8 Even so, depression tends to go unrecognized in adolescents more often than in adults. 9 This is particularly true when screening does not rely on a psychological tool with strong psychometric properties and cultural relevance. Much the same applies to anxiety, which involves persistent worry or fear that can interfere with daily functioning and is among the most common mental health problems in adolescents worldwide. 10 Stress, meanwhile, can be understood as the psychological, physiological, and behavioral response that occurs when a person perceives an imbalance between the demands placed on them and their capacity to meet those demands. This state can eventually affect physical health. 11 Among adolescents, stress can have a lasting effect on physical, emotional, and psychological development. Assessing these three constructs separately risks overlooking the overlap between them, since anxiety and depression in particular commonly occur together during adolescence and often share symptoms, 12 which is part of the rationale for treating all three jointly in this review rather than mapping instruments for a single construct at a time.

In both clinical and school settings, a range of self-report instruments are used to screen for these difficulties. The availability of multiple scales has expanded screening and assessment options. Still, the resulting variation in psychometric strength, developmental fit, and cultural relevance can make it difficult for researchers, clinicians, and school mental health professionals to identify which instrument best suits a given context. 13 The COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) framework holds that health-related screening and measurement instruments should be evaluated through careful examination of key psychometric properties, particularly reliability, structural validity, and construct validity. 14 These four dimensions of instrument selection are related but not interchangeable. Screening utility is used here to describe how practical an instrument is to administer and score in a given setting. 13 Psychometric quality describes the reliability and validity evidence supporting the instrument itself, independent of who is using it or where. 14 Developmental appropriateness describes whether an instrument’s content, language, and response format suit the cognitive and emotional maturity of the age group being assessed. 13 Cultural fit describes whether the constructs, wording, and normative assumptions built into an instrument reflect the lived experience of the population it is used with, rather than assuming a construct behaves the same way across cultural contexts. 15 An instrument can score well on one of these dimensions while remaining weak on another.

Although narrative and systematic reviews have examined specific instruments for depression or anxiety in adolescents, no scoping review to date has mapped the full range of self-report scales assessing anxiety, depression, and stress together, with attention to both their cultural context and their psychometric properties. Based on the existing literature, this review was guided by the expectation that the majority of available self-report instruments measuring depression, anxiety, and stress among adolescents would have been developed and validated in Western contexts, with limited psychometric validation specific to Indian adolescents, that multidimensional tools assessing all three constructs together would be fewer in number compared to single-construct measures, and that cultural and linguistic adaptation of existing tools for the Indian adolescent population would be insufficient across the identified instruments.

These gaps demand an updated scoping review grounded in the COSMIN framework evaluation to help guide better scale selection for screening depression, anxiety, and stress in the Indian adolescent population in particular. The research question guiding this review was: What self-report measures currently exist to assess anxiety, depression, and stress among adolescents, and how do they compare in terms of psychometric properties, constructs, practical utility, and cultural applicability, with particular reference to the Indian adolescent context? Given the breadth of available instruments and the need to map current psychometric assessments rather than compare their effectiveness, a scoping review design was adopted.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), 16 and the completed checklist is provided as supplementary material. The review was not pre-registered. The first and second authors developed the review protocol. The framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley 17 was adopted, following five stages: Identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, study selection, charting the data, and collating and summarizing the findings. Language refinement of the manuscript was supported using AI-assisted writing tools. These tools were not involved in study design, data extraction, synthesis, or interpretation, and all analytic decisions and conclusions were made independently by the authors.

Selection Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

The eligible participants were adolescents aged 10–19 years, consistent with the World Health Organization definition of adolescence, including instruments validated across broader age ranges that still encompassed adolescence, drawn from community, school-based, or clinical settings. Eligible studies reported on self-report instruments measuring at least one of three constructs, anxiety, depression, or stress; no comparator was applied to this review. The primary outcomes of interest were the psychometric properties of each instrument, including internal consistency, structural validity, and test–retest reliability, assessed using COSMIN-informed criteria. In contrast, secondary outcomes covered practical characteristics such as the number of items, response format, age range, language availability, and cultural context of development. Eligible study designs included instrument development, validation, and psychometric evaluation studies, reported in peer-reviewed journal articles and published in English.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they involved only adults aged 19 or older or children aged 10 or younger, unless adolescent-specific validation data were available. Non-self-report measures, clinician-administered instruments, and observational tools were also excluded, as were studies that did not report any psychometric evaluation of the instrument in question; no comparator or secondary outcomes met this criterion. Conference abstracts, dissertations, book chapters, narrative reviews, and gray literature were also excluded, along with non-peer-reviewed publications and studies not published in English.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed by the first author in consultation with the second author. A systematic search was conducted across four databases: PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Google Scholar, and no filters for species, age, or article type were applied at the database level, since these were addressed during screening instead. The searches were run on July 28, 2024, at the data collection stage of this review, and the months that followed were spent on screening, extraction, COSMIN-informed rating, and the writing, and internal review of the manuscript before submission; this is a common gap between when a review’s searches are completed and when it is finally submitted, though it does mean that studies published after this date are not reflected here. Eligibility was restricted to English-language publications, largely because the majority of peer-reviewed work on developing and validating these instruments appears in English, and keeping to this restriction made it easier to extract statistical detail consistently for COSMIN-informed ratings across studies; it is possible that instruments validated mainly in Hindi or other Indian languages were missed as a result, a point we return to in the Limitations section. Gray literature and unpublished studies were not searched, as the review focused on psychometrically evaluated instruments reported in peer-reviewed publications. The concept terms and the full PubMed search history are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

The search combined the PICO-D framework with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords. The main terms included: Adolescent, anxiety, depression, stress, psychological, psychometrics, surveys, and questionnaires. The Boolean operators AND and OR were used to combine population, construct, and measurement-related terms. The Google Scholar search was handled differently from the other three databases, since it does not support the same kind of structured Boolean or MeSH-based filtering, and a single search string can return several thousand results. Therefore, the first 300 results, sorted by relevance, were screened rather than the entire set. This follows earlier work on using Google Scholar for evidence reviews, which found that most useful records tend to sit within this initial range, after which genuinely new eligible studies become rare while duplicates and non-peer-reviewed material start to take over, meaning that screening the full result set was unlikely to add much beyond what this cutoff already captured. 18

Study Screening and Selection

Records identified from database searches were exported and managed using a structured Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Duplicate records were identified and removed manually before screening. Full-text articles were accessed through institutional library subscriptions, PubMed Central, and, where necessary, through ResearchGate or direct author requests.

Title and abstract screening was carried out by one author, then checked by the second author; full-text screening followed the same process, with the second author verifying the decisions made at that stage as well. Disagreements at each screening stage were resolved through discussion between the two authors until consensus was reached. A formal pilot screening round was not conducted before full screening began, and this is acknowledged as a limitation of the screening process. Consistent with the Arksey and O’Malley 17 scoping review framework and JBI guidance, 19 formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not required.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed systematically across all included instruments, guided by the COSMIN framework 14 and JBI scoping review methodology. 19 For each instrument, the following information was extracted: Author(s) and year of publication, instrument name, and acronym, construct(s) measured (anxiety, depression, stress, or combined), target age group, number of items, response format, sample characteristics of the validation population, country, or cultural context of development, study design, language availability, and psychometric properties including internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha), structural validity (factor analytic evidence), and test–retest reliability, along with construct, or criterion validity where this was reported, such as correlations with other established measures of the same construct. Any additional information relevant to clinical or research utility, such as administration time, cost, and availability of translated versions, was also recorded where reported. Data extraction was performed by both the first and second authors and again verified by the same to ensure accuracy and completeness before synthesis. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Risk of Bias Assessment

As is standard for scoping reviews, a formal risk of bias assessment was not conducted. Although methodological appraisal is not a standard component of scoping reviews, the psychometric quality of the included instruments was summarized descriptively using COSMIN-informed criteria to support interpretive clarity rather than to exclude studies. These ratings involve a degree of judgment about measurement quality. In that sense, they go beyond purely neutral description. Still, they should be read as a structured descriptive summary of the available evidence rather than as a formal appraisal of study quality. They were not intended to carry the same weight as a systematic risk of bias assessment.

Synthesis of Results

Due to the heterogeneous nature of the included instruments, a quantitative meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate, and a narrative synthesis was adopted. Each measurement property was rated according to the following criteria, adapted from Mokkink et al. 14 These ratings were assigned jointly by both authors, working together rather than independently, and were compared afterward; any differences in judgment were resolved through discussion at the time of rating. A rating of very good was assigned when the instrument showed strong evidence across the relevant property, typically reflected by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80 or above, a well-supported factor structure through confirmatory or exploratory factor analysis, and adequate test–retest reliability with an ICC or r of 0.70 or above. Adequate was assigned when evidence was present, but only partially. Doubtful was assigned when evidence was insufficient or based on a single study with notable limitations. These ratings were applied descriptively to map the relative psychometric strength of each instrument and were not used as exclusion criteria.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 2,812 records were identified across four databases (PubMed: 1,311; PsycINFO: 101; Scopus: 1,100; Google Scholar: 300). After duplicate removal, 350 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following screening, 72 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 57 were excluded, primarily because they were not in English or did not demonstrate COSMIN-aligned psychometric evaluation. The remaining 15 instruments were included in the final review. Overview and Psychometric Properties of Included Scales

This scoping review identified 15 self-report measures assessing anxiety, depression, and stress among adolescents. The included instruments varied across their psychometric approaches, with internal consistency estimates generally ranging from 0.70 to 0.95. Measures such as the Children’s Depression Inventory 20 (CDI), the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire 21 (MFQ), and the Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale 22 (SCAS) reported alpha values above 0.80. Multidimensional tools, including the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 23 (DASS-21), the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-Youth 24 (DASS-Y), and the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS), showed stable factor structures, and subscale internal consistency generally ranging from 0.70 to 0.90. While multidimensional tools such as the DASS and RCADS assess overlapping constructs, none of the instruments identified through this search was developed and validated specifically for Indian adolescents as a combined measure of all three constructs. However, this observation is necessarily bounded by the scope of the search strategy described earlier.

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) shows test–retest reliability ranging from 0.55 to 0.91, and although the total score generally meets the COSMIN-recommended ICC threshold of >0.70, published test–retest evidence in adolescent samples specifically remains limited, 14 resulting in a doubtful rating in the present review. The DSM-5 Severity Measure for Depression, Child Age 11–17 23 (DSM-5 Depression Severity Measure Child) has limited independent test–retest data outside the original DSM-5 field trials, resulting in a doubtful rating.14,23,24 The Student Stress Inventory (SSI) similarly lacks published test–retest data beyond its original development study, warranting a doubtful rating.14,25 The characteristics of the instruments are presented in Table 2.

Depression Measures

Several of the included instruments were specifically intended to assess depressive symptoms in children and adolescents. These included the CDI, 20 the MFQ 21 , the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale for Children 26 (CES-DC), and the DSM-5 Depression Severity Measure Child. 23 Most of these measures use Likert-type response formats and have demonstrated satisfactory to strong internal consistency. Although the DASS-21 was originally developed and normed for adult populations, 27 subsequent validation studies have supported its use with adolescents aged 12 years and above, 12 and it is included on that basis. Variations were seen in the targeted age ranges, number of items, and the depth of screening.

Anxiety Measures

The review identified several instruments focusing on anxiety-related symptoms. These included the SCAS, 22 the anxiety subscales of the RCADS, 28 the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders 29 (SCARED), and the Revised Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale Second Edition 30 (RCMAS-2). These tools assess multiple domains of anxiety and have generally reported strong internal consistency along with acceptable factorial support. The SCARED in particular has shown good internal consistency and test–retest reliability across its five factors in the original validation sample, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.70 to 0.90. 29 Most were developed in Western cultural contexts, and cross-cultural validation within Indian school-based populations remains limited.

Stress Measures

Compared to depression and anxiety, fewer instruments were identified that focus specifically on stress among adolescents. The PSS-10 31 and the SSI 25 were the main tools identified. These instruments differ in their focus: The PSS-10 measures general perception of stress, whereas the SSI focuses more on situational and academic stressors. Both have reported acceptable levels of internal consistency in adolescent samples. The limited number of stress-specific measures highlights a gap in the literature.

The 15 instruments in Table 2 covered a wide spread of ages, from as young as 6 years for the MFQ and CES-DC up to 19 for the RCMAS-2, and tools such as the DASS-Y, RCADS, Beck Youth Inventories, Second Edition 32 (BYI-II), and SCARED covered most of the adolescent range in a single form. Only three of them, the DASS-21, DASS-Y, and RCADS, were built to capture more than one construct at once, which fits the relative scarcity of multidimensional measures noted earlier. The COSMIN-informed ratings in Table 1 also clustered unevenly, with the CDI, DASS-21, DASS-Y, SCAS, RCADS, MFQ, RCMAS-2, CES-DC, and SCARED receiving an overall very good rating. At the same time, the PSS-10, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 Modified for Adolescents 33 (PHQ-9 Modified for Teens), Youth Inventory-4 Revised 34 (YI-4R), BYI-II, DSM-5 Depression Severity Measure Child, and SSI stayed at adequate, mostly on account of doubtful test–retest evidence, or, for the BYI-II, more moderate structural validity. Cross-cultural adaptation remains limited but not absent: A Hindi version of the DASS-21 has been validated directly in Indian adolescents, 35 and a Hindi version of the PSS-10 has been validated in an Indian adult sample, 36 though not yet in an adolescent-specific sample; the DASS-Y has emerging adaptations, and the CES-DC has multiple translations, while most other tools exist only in English, and the SSI, developed in Malaysia, is the only instrument here that did not originate in a Western context. Several of the instruments with strong COSMIN ratings still have no meaningful adaptation for Indian adolescents specifically, so psychometric strength and cultural reach do not appear to move together in this set.

Clinical and Research Utility

Most of the instruments included here were built for screening rather than formal diagnosis, which makes them well-suited to early identification of depression, anxiety, and stress in school-based settings. The PSS-10 31 and the CES-DC 26 are both relatively easy to administer and score. At the same time, multidimensional instruments such as the DASS-21, 27 the DASS-Y, 37 and the RCADS 28 offer a broader picture of emotional functioning by assessing several domains at once. Tools designed for younger populations, such as the SCAS 22 and the CDI, 20 tend to use simpler language and symptom descriptions suited to adolescents. Even so, only a small number of the instruments reviewed here have documented translations into Hindi or other Indian languages, and detailed evidence of how they perform with linguistically diverse Indian adolescents specifically remains limited.

Discussion

The present scoping review aimed to map the self-report instruments available for assessing these three constructs among adolescents, with particular attention to their psychometric characteristics and cultural applicability. Fifteen instruments were identified, and the findings indicate that a considerable range of assessment tools is currently available, many of which have demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in their original development and validation settings. The patterns observed across the review were broadly consistent with the expectations that guided it: Most instruments were developed and validated in Western contexts, with limited Indian validation; multidimensional tools were fewer than single-construct measures; and cultural and linguistic adaptation for Indian adolescents remained insufficient across the identified instruments.

A pattern that stood out across the reviewed instruments was the growing use of multidimensional measures, reflecting the fact that depression, anxiety, and stress during adolescence often co-occur rather than present in isolation. The DASS-21, DASS-Y, and RCADS all allow simultaneous assessment of these related domains, providing a broader picture of adolescent emotional functioning and reducing the need to administer multiple separate tools. Domain-specific instruments still have a clear place, though, particularly where focused screening of a single symptom area is needed, as with the PSS-10 for stress or the CDI for depression. The depth of psychometric evaluation was not even across the set, and some measures, especially in adolescent samples, rested on comparatively limited evidence, a point returned to below.

The limited cultural applicability of the identified instruments is especially relevant for the Indian context. Most were originally developed and standardized in Western settings, where emotional expression and help-seeking patterns can differ considerably from what is seen in India; stigma, low mental health literacy, and concerns about confidentiality remain the most consistently reported barriers to Indian adolescents seeking support, and many turn first to friends and peers rather than a counselor or clinician. 38 Adolescent distress is often shaped by academic pressure, family expectations, social conformity, and community-oriented values that individualistic frameworks do not always capture. Recent qualitative work with Indian adolescents themselves points to some of what standard instruments may be missing: Interviews with adolescents diagnosed with depression, their parents, and teachers identified explanatory models centered on family-related difficulties, peer and romantic relationship problems, and academic pressure, alongside culturally salient presentations such as academic disengagement, anger and aggression, and additional somatic symptoms not captured by DSM-5 criteria. 39 A similar pattern turns up in how anxiety gets described in Indian clinical settings, where caregivers lean on everyday relational language rather than clinical terms; one caregiver, for instance, summed up a family member’s anxiety as simply worrying “what his friends will think of him,” a framing closer to social and peer-related concern than to clinical vocabulary. That evidence comes from an adult-patient study rather than an adolescent study, though the caregiver’s own example centered on a child. This suggests the pattern may well extend to younger age groups, even if it has not yet been formally tested in an adolescent sample. 40

Although translated versions exist for several measures, systematic validation within Indian adolescent populations remains limited, and language accessibility adds a further layer to this concern. India’s linguistic diversity means that even when a translated version is available, differences in how terms are understood across communities can shape adolescents’ responses. Psychometric adequacy, on its own, may therefore not be sufficient to make a tool contextually appropriate unless cultural and linguistic adaptation has also been carefully considered. None of the instruments identified through this search had been developed and validated specifically for Indian adolescents as a combined measure of all three constructs, despite these constructs frequently co-occurring during this developmental period. However, this gap should be read alongside the search limitations discussed earlier rather than as a claim about the field as a whole.

The CDI, MFQ, and SCAS stand out for having been repeatedly validated across different populations and settings, which supports their continued use in both research and clinical practice, and the BYI-II, 32 the YI-4R, 34 and the PHQ-9 Modified for Teens 33 have each shown strong psychometric properties within their own original validation contexts. The PSS-10’s evidence base is more mixed: It performs adequately overall, but its test–retest reliability remains questionable, 41 and the symptomatic expression of depression in adolescents can differ meaningfully from adult presentations, 42 underscoring the importance of using age-appropriate and contextually validated instruments rather than assuming that adult-normed properties transfer directly. High internal consistency is often treated as the main marker of a good instrument. Still, alpha values mainly reflect how closely items correlate with each other rather than whether an instrument truly captures the construct it claims to measure. A scale can report a strong alpha while still having weaker structural or convergent validity. Strong psychometric properties alone are therefore not enough to make an instrument appropriate for a given context, particularly once language, cultural relevance, and other contextual factors are taken into account. The review followed a systematic and transparent process, though its scope introduced some necessary limitations. The search was restricted to four databases, with Google Scholar limited to the first 300 records sorted by relevance, which may mean some relevant instruments were not captured. 18 Gray literature was not searched, and the review was restricted to English-language peer-reviewed publications, which may not capture the full breadth of available instruments. Several included instruments were developed and validated across broader age ranges extending below the WHO adolescent threshold of 10 years, such as the CDI (7–17), SCAS (8–15), CES-DC (6–17), and RCMAS-2 (6–19); while evidence supports their applicability within the adolescent range, the absence of adolescent-specific norms for the younger boundary should be considered when interpreting their suitability. Furthermore, the absence of Indian-specific normative data across most instruments limits the interpretability of scores in this context; for example, the only available Hindi validation of the PSS-10 was conducted in an adult sample rather than an adolescent one, so its psychometric performance in Indian adolescents specifically has not yet been established. The review was not pre-registered, which should be considered when evaluating its reproducibility.

Conclusions

This scoping review mapped the self-report instruments available for assessing these three constructs among adolescents, with attention to their psychometric characteristics and contextual applicability. Fifteen instruments met the inclusion criteria, and most demonstrated acceptable to strong psychometric properties within their original development contexts. What the review points to, though, is not simply an absence of options but an unevenness in how well those options fit the Indian adolescent population: Instruments with strong internal consistency often had weaker test–retest evidence, tools built for broader age ranges lacked adolescent-specific norms at the younger boundary, and even the instruments with the strongest COSMIN ratings largely lacked meaningful linguistic or cultural adaptation for India. None of the instruments identified here had been developed and validated specifically for Indian adolescents as a combined measure of all three constructs, and the qualitative evidence reviewed above suggests that Indian adolescents’ distress can present in ways that existing tools, built largely around Western frameworks, may not fully capture. Addressing this would benefit from rigorous co-development work involving Indian adolescents directly rather than translation alone, alongside bilingual validation in Hindi and other major Indian languages, dedicated test–retest reliability testing in adolescent samples, and evidence gathered mainly from school settings, as well as from clinical settings. Progress on these fronts would do more to improve mental health screening for Indian adolescents than the addition of another single instrument on its own.
