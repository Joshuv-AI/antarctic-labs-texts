Final year oral hygiene students’ perceptions and reflections on experiential learning in a special needs oral health care program
Authors: Magandhree Naidoo, Priscilla Brijlal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health care programs offer a platform for experiential learning that include prospects for skills development; opportunities for knowledge application and for the creation of an awareness of community responsibilities. Gaining experience in the care for special needs patients is ideally achieved through exposure in special needs settings such as the school community.AimThis study investigated the perceptions and reflections of experiential learning by final year oral hygiene students in a special need’s oral health care program at the University of Western Cape.MethodsThis study incorporated a concurrent triangulation study design that included final year Oral Hygiene students who participated in a special need’s oral health care program. Students completed an on-line self-administered questionnaire, a reflective journal and participated in a focus group discussion.ResultsAll respondents understood the concept of experiential learning. The predominant dispositions that emerged included student adaptation, awareness, empathy, and experience which were regarded as particularly important for personal growth. With regards to academic growth, facets of confidence, theory translation, clinical skill development and improved communication were identified as critical elements in development. Challenges included lack of cooperation from the learners and lack of kills in non-verbal communication. An increased awareness and a strong willingness to participate in community projects was also reported amongst the student respondents.ConclusionThe experiential learning opportunity in the school-based special needs oral health care program was invaluable to the children as well as the students. This community -based program supported personal and academic growth and it provided authentic learning opportunities; facilitated professional development, improved clinical skills and competence, enhanced problem solving and communication skills and raised awareness on social responsibility. Challenges experienced pertained to cooperation and communication with the children. Suggestions included maintaining access to care for the children through oral care program initiatives, as well as ongoing rotation of Oral Hygiene students who are ideally positioned to provide promotive and preventive oral care to special needs individuals. Schools for special needs children provide an ideal platform to facilitate access to care and to provide experiential learning opportunities in authentic settings.Keywords: Oral health care program, Experiential learning, Reflection, Perception, Skills

Introduction

Higher education organizations are engaging the mindful paradigm shifts towards learning methods and experiences that will foster and encourage active involvement and holistic development, as intended within Experiential Learning (EL) [1]. Experiential Learning (EL) is a teaching and learning approach founded on constructivist principles and, is a learner centred pedagogy contributing to the improvement of educational experience. The focus of this approach is on skills development and the promotion of real-life experiences which is frequently achieved through community-based placements [2]. The EL approach has not only been poised as a facilitator, transferring academic content into applicable and real-life experiences [3] but, holistic graduate competencies that reflect, knowledge application, problem solving, critical thinking and the demonstration of applicable skills including being socially and culturally alert [4]. Furthermore, EL platforms also facilitate the achievement of self-sufficiency and improved accountability both personally and academically within the context of real-life experiences [5]. In health sciences programs, knowledge acquisition and application within a real-life setting such as a school-based oral health programs provides invaluable opportunities. Knowledge and skills transfer from the traditional educational platform into community health settings can provide the students with authentic experiential learning opportunities that considers social and cultural contexts of ill-health and health care provision. An important aspect of providing new learning opportunities and contexts is the evaluation of such interventions to establish how the learning experience has impacted student development and how future endeavours may be improved. This study focused on experiential learning of undergraduate Oral Hygiene students who participated in a mobile oral care program at a special needs school. The main objectives included the assessment of the effectiveness of school-based oral care program on experiential learning for the undergraduate oral hygiene students and to investigate the students’ perceptions and reflection on experiential learning and skill application within a real-life context. Research questions encompassed aspects of; how did the participation in mobile oral care program provide a valuable experiential learning opportunity; and how was the experiential learning opportunity perceived by the undergraduate final year students?

School-based programs and experimental learning

Community-based interventions and programs for undergraduate Dentistry, Dental Therapy and Oral Hygiene students have been established as ideal platforms to understand community responsibilities, increase an awareness on the diversity of patients and to improve the application of clinical skills [6]. Community based learning such as school health programs has been poised as a resourceful endeavour, improving clinical skills and personal attitudes especially during service provision to special needs patients [7]. Earlier studies on teaching within outreach programs indicated that students valued clinical outreach regarding it as a useful and positive experience and especially for developing their personal, academic and civic responsibility as well as the ‘real-life’ context of oral health care service delivery to special needs patients [8–10]. The latter study further states that additional research is required to evaluate the learning experiences of students in community-based programs. Later studies evaluated experiential learning of students involved in an oral health promotion initiative indicating that students reported positively on knowledge application, knowledge gain and sharing as well as community responsibility [11]. It has been indicated that community-based learning yielded positive attitudes regarding the needs of the community and the provision of care [12].

Studies on the impact of school-based oral health programs regarding experiential learning for Oral Hygienists could not be located hence making the current study relevant to informing future initiatives. Since Oral Hygienists’ primary philosophy of training and scope of profession rest in prevention of diseases and promotion of oral health, they spend a substantial proportion of the curriculum in community-based settings providing care to all categories of the population. Hence these professionals must be adequately trained and skilled to improve oral health. One of the ways to evaluate their learning in these settings is through an evaluation using reflective journals.

Reflective journals for experiential learning

Reflection exists as an indispensable facit of the education approach intending to provoke deeper learning. The healthcare context incorporates reflection within experiential initiatives aiming for knowledge production that in turn impacts the student clinician positively. Thought provoking reflection permits the exploration of emotions, learning challenges and experiences [13]. Reflective methods incorporated in dentistry has been dominated by written reflections [14]. A study exploring structured written reflections implemented to assess the learning provided by a placement in conscious sedation put forth that reflection allowed the students to identify learning challenges and corresponding problem-solving strategies [15]. Whilst another study on clinical rotation showed deep reflection in the journal writing and suggested that reflective journaling can assist as a beneficial activity to encourage improved self-development during clinical rotations [16].

“Reflective journal writing” has been proposed as an education implement in higher education to foster continued learning skills [17]. Another study with medical students concluded that the practice of reflective writing stimulated experiential learning and initiated discussion in these students [18]. Additionally, the provision of guiding questions prior to engaging in the activity has proven to be beneficial to the value of the student’s reflection [19]. However, a qualitative study with undergraduate dental students undertaking in paediatric clinics yielded that a greater emphasis is placed by students and educators on learning through experience rather than on reflection [20]. Student voice in education is a valuable way of estimating the quality of education from the perspective of the recipient.

Student perceptions

Student perceptions are fundamental tools serving a dual purpose of evaluating quality and identifying future improvements in learning programmes. The learning environment has a strong impact on students’ learning experiences and outcomes. Research lends support to the value of the students’ perceptions in relation to academic success and their insights of the learning context [21]. Student perceptions also contribute as an evaluative tool of their learning environment [22]. Research has also alluded to the importance of students’ perceptions with regards to the impact of the design of teaching and learning implements [23]. Empirical conclusions have also validated student perceptions as a foundation upon which teaching quality can be assessed for a variety of education structures [24].

This study accordingly combined the strength of reflections and perceptions to evaluate experiential learning within a school-based special needs oral health care program.

Methodology

Study design

This study implemented a concurrent triangulation research design to enable the collection and analysis of data from multiple sources simultaneously. Sources included a questionnaire, reflective journal, and a focus group interview. The combined data provided insights into the perceptions and reflections of experiential learning. The fundamental foundation of using a concurrent triangulation research design for this study was to ensure the credibility and validity of research findings [25]. Methodological triangulation in this study involved using more than one type of data collection method to study the experiential learning opportunity. This proved to be beneficial in augmenting findings and delivering comprehensive data to improve the understanding of this studied phenomena.

Setting of the study

This study was located within the Department of Oral Hygiene, Faculty of Dentistry, at the University of the Western Cape.

Participants

All final year undergraduate Oral Hygiene students from the University of Western Cape participating in the special needs oral health care program were invited to participate. The study period was between June and October 2023.

Data Collection

The final year undergraduate oral hygiene students as a part of the clinical practice module participated in the special need’s oral health care program as a component of their clinical rotations. Students were rotated weekly in groups of four. Each group participated in the special needs mobile oral care program in six clinic al rotation during the academic year of 2023. This community-based program provided an experiential learning opportunity for skill application and knowledge acquisition within a real-life setting in the special needs community. At the school, students conducted an extra and intra-oral examination on leaners who had parental consent and that provided assent. A treatment plan was drawn up between the student and the lead researcher based on the clinical findings. Treatment was thereafter implemented accordingly. The students’ perception and reflection of this experiential learning opportunity was collected by means of a survey questionnaire, reflective journal, and a focus group discussion. The online self-administered questionnaire sought to obtain information on the impact of this program on the student’s involvement in the planning of this program, understanding their requirements, skill development, critical thinking skills, awareness of community responsibility, understanding of the special needs’ learners and their needs and hands-on application of their skills. Students that consented to participate in the research were requested to complete the questionnaire after completing their final outreach clinical rotation at the special needs school. This questionnaire was effective in measuring perspectives and opinions using both open and closed-ended questions to provide a wealth of comprehensive data.

Additional data was gathered using a reflective journal and a focus group discussion. Each student was provided with a digital reflective journal with cues to comment on their preparations for the program; experiences and challenges; their personal and academic learning experiences and social responsibility. This journal was electronic, and the student has continuous access to it. The student was encouraged to reflect in their journal after each clinical rotation and update their reflections until their last clinical rotation. Using a reflective journal approach intended to obtain a deeper understanding of the experiential learning experiences and challenges of the students. These reflective journals offered an efficient means with a similar focus and intent as that of an observational study to inform the researcher’s understanding of learning experiences [26].

A post-reflective focus group discussion included semi-structured focus group questions. The focus group discussion was implemented to enable the achievement of more in-depth information on student perceptions, experiences, and challenges [27]. This approach also enabled the collection of additional date to gain further insights of school programs as an experiential learning platform. Consenting final year students who had completed their clinical rotation in the special needs school were randomly selected to participate in the focus group discussion. A single discussion was facilitated by one of the researchers in the faculty and was conducted at the end of semester two. The participants of the focus group discussion included eight final year oral hygiene students. The meeting time and venue was pre-arranged, and the scope of the discussion was reiterated to the group. Discussions were recorded and transcribed for analysis purposes.

Data analysis

Quantitative data was analysed by the main researcher using the statistical program IBM SPSS Version 27. Analysis was done according to frequencies, means and averages. Cross-tabulations were applied accordingly. Open-ended questions were analysed according to emerging themes to provide a healthy balance of narrative and explanatory inferences. Data from the reflective journals and focus group discussion were analysed using the ATLAS.ti software which is a research tool that facilitates the analysis of qualitative data. Reflective journal data and recorded transcripts were managed using this this software. This data was thereafter analysed thematically by the main researcher following the guidelines of (Braun & Clark, 2006) [28]. The multiple sources of data collected in this study are not intended for comparison. The data gathered using different strategies served to add depth, verification where necessary and to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena under investigation. Discussion

The response rate for this study was (n = 16), of the 22 students who participated in the special needs oral health care program. Reasons for non-response could be related to inadequate computer access, absent internet connections and power outages. This was echoed in in research study regarding the digital divide and the gap between access and technology in South Africa [29]. All students were aware of the requirements of the program and were calibrated on the use of the WHO survey 5th Edition. The importance of calibration when planning and conducting surveys cannot be overstated [30]. The purpose of student calibration in this study was to enable the students to fully understand the recorded clinical findings to facilitate the development of a collaborative treatment plan. Outreach has been put forth as a vital component in the students' ability to formulate a diagnosis and treatment plans [31]. All students being aware of the requirements of the program were in line with the recommendations for science outreach programs, indicating the importance of “Effective communication is essential, both within and outside of the team” [32]. Accordingly, all requirements and including them in the developing of visual schedules were ensured.

Student accounts regarding the benefits of participating in the community-based special needs oral care program emphasised personal growth and academic growth. Personal growth attributes included improvements in adaptation to the needs of the children, an increased awareness and empathy. Academic growth embroidered on enhanced learning experience, skills development, and good exposure.

Personal growth

The ability of students to adjust to different communities with diverse backgrounds and special needs children and alternate community-based settings such as schools, necessitates adaptations to regular care approaches used in traditional clinical settings. An increased awareness and adaptation to the diverse needs of different individuals and different populations were also highlighted in a similar community service-learning intervention [33].

Empathy is a vital component in the adaptation process within functional social constructs, influencing understanding and acceptance. This resonates with the model to empower improved patient care [34]. The empathy that students displayed and felt in the current study was a positive realization as it was contrary to a study that concluded that despite their program improving students' emotional reactions, it was incapable of reducing the social distance and barriers to treatment by these students [35].

Academic growth

Academic growth encompassed improvements in attributes such as, professionalism and clinical skills development, confidence development and an increased understanding of special patient management. Professionalism is an attribute that all students aspire towards in preparation for the transition into the real world. Placement in the special needs school program, afforded students the opportunity to apply classroom-based theory in practice in an authentic setting thereby enhancing their understanding, approach and confidence in patient management and the provision of oral care which will support their future endeavours. he development of clinical skills was enhanced and are implicit in reported dispositions of confidence; enhancement in understanding; problem solving and creativity in approaching special needs patients. Confidence was built and competence improved because the opportunity allowed for sufficient practical experience by way in the number of allocated rotations as opposed to once of inoculations of exposure [36].Increased confidence was also reported in a previous study where students who had expressed their lack of self-confidence prior to the intervention relayed more positive levels of confidence after the experience with special needs population [37]. Regular exposures also enabled patient follow up and monitoring, relationship building and an overall holistic experience for both the special needs children and the students. The intervention outcomes supported the notion of community-based programs as being effective and that rotations that are form part of the formal curriculum complements meaningfully to institutional-based dental education [38]. These findings concur with reports indicating that the exposure to diverse patients in community-based settings together with the application of adapted and alternate approaches enhanced the experience [9]. Students in the current program also showed an ardent desire to engage in future community-based projects echoing a previous study which reported on the awareness of social responsibility in the students [9].

Reflective Journal and Focus Group

Students expressed their expectations when participating in community-based interventions highlighting that improved educational experiences, confidence and effective oral health care provision should be achieved. The provision of adaptable community-based outreach learning opportunities as a component of the undergraduate program is a major contributor promoting the alignment of education, with the community needs to be advantageous to both students and identified community engagement to vulnerable groups [39]. Communication was another attribute that was foregrounded. This was an important highlight as better patient and community interactions have been recognised in previous studies [40, 41]. Implicit in communication is the ability to enact and display patience, which was another attribute mentioned. Patience is an emotion intricately linked to that of empathy and was apparent in the questionnaire responses as well. Students’ achievement of this trait is positive and has been reported in other outreach programs where favourable mindsets and attitudes were noted [42]. Confidence and application of knowledge resounded the data from the questionnaires and other research where students reported increased confidence, improved understanding, and applicability in managing patients [43]. Students also expressed gratitude for their experiences in this program with regards to the opportunities provided for learning and working with special needs children. Challenges revolved around cooperation, difficulty in communication with the children and uncertainty as to how the session will unfold as is usually anticipated with this group of special needs learners. Reduced cooperation has been highlighted as one of the numerous difficulties experienced by the special needs population in accessing dental care [44]. Functional verbal communication impacting professional oral health care for children was also highlighted in a previous study in South Africa [45]. Students impressed on the fundamental role and need for Oral Hygienists to provide oral health care education, employ oral health promotion and disease prevention strategies and to improve access to care for vulnerable groups Engaging students in community-based and outreach programs have previously successfully enhanced social responsibilities in students [46].

The translation of knowledge into practice featured strongly in the responses for the understanding of experiential learning during the focus group discussions. Experiential learning according to Kolb [5] is “how people learn through experience.” Allowing students to apply what was learnt in the classroom to a real-life setting, meant the translation of theory into practice as aptly recognised by the students. Students correlated experiential learning with learning opportunities that translates into practical application, the implementation of clinical procedures and demonstrations by supervisors. This further reverberates the experiential learning paradigm of “learning by doing” [47]. Collaboration and peer learning were highlighted as being key drivers for successful learning and skills development. These attributes are also a key feature of teamwork and particularly manifest as important for intersectoral and multisectoral collaboration initiatives in planning and delivering health care.

In terms of the students’ role in social responsibility, concern was raised regarding access for vulnerable special needs groups. This has been raised by previous studies in the Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal [47, 48]. Special needs children are often disadvantaged in terms of access to care because they are reliant on their parents or caregivers to support their health needs who may not fully understand the needs of the children.

Lastly, suggestions from the students on improving care for special needs children included the use of headphones for those sensitive to sound and special goggles for those sensitive to light. Students also stressed on the importance of well-functioning and well-resourced mobile units to support oral care interventions conducted at community-based sites. It is important for the success of interventions and particularly for special needs schools where access to care is often a challenge.

Conclusion

The experiential learning opportunity in the special needs school-based oral health care program was invaluable to the children as well as to the students. This community -based program supported personal and academic growth and it provided authentic learning opportunities; facilitated professional development, improved d clinical skills and competence, enhanced problem solving and communication skills and raised awareness on social responsibility. Challenges experienced pertained to cooperation and communication with the children. Suggestions included maintaining access to care for the children through the program initiatives, as well as ongoing rotation of Oral Hygiene students who are ideally positioned to provide promotive and preventive oral care to special needs individuals. Schools for special needs children provide an ideal platform to facilitate access to care and to provide experiential learning opportunities in authentic settings.

Limitations

The cohort was made up of a small number of final year undergraduate Oral Hygiene students reflecting on only one type of community-based program within their curriculum and for a specific target population. Further research is necessary to obtain insights into the benefits of rotations on other platforms and for other population groups.

Recommendations

Oral Health programs that are implemented in communities have the potential to improve access and care to population groups, especially those with special needs. These opportunities concurrently provide an ideal platform for experiential learning for students and support the development of personal and professional skills and attributes.
