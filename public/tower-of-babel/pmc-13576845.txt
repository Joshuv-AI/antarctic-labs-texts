Agreement Between ICD-10 Code–Only and Composite Definitions of Asthma Exacerbations in Electronic Health Records: A Multicenter Cohort Study in Taiwan, 2017-2023
Authors: Chun-Tse Hung, Chen-Yen An, Chi-Won Suk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate identification of asthma exacerbations in electronic health records (EHRs) is essential for research and clinical decision-making. While composite definitions incorporating healthcare encounters and systemic corticosteroid use are commonly used, ICD-10 code-only definitions are often applied when detailed data are unavailable. However, their agreement remains unclear. This study aimed to (1) compare an ICD-10 code–only definition of asthma exacerbation with a composite definition, and (2) identify factors associated with concordance between both definitions in EHR data.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study using the Taipei Medical University Clinical Research Database (2017–2023). Asthma exacerbations were identified using (1) an ICD-10 code–only definition and (2) a composite definition incorporating asthma-related emergency department visits, hospitalization, or systemic corticosteroid use. Episodes were defined using a seven-day gap rule. Agreement was assessed using overall percent agreement (OPA), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive percent agreement (PPA), and negative percent agreement (NPA), with generalized estimating equations accounting for within-patient clustering.ResultsAmong 1,486,449 episodes, 40,180 (2.7%) met the code-only definition and 35,875 (2.4%) met the composite definition. After covariate adjustment, OPA was 0.930 (95% CI, 0.926–0.933). PPV and PPA were 0.313 (95% CI, 0.293–0.334) and 0.309 (95% CI: 0.291–0.328), respectively, while NPV and NPA were high (0.968 and 0.962). Comorbidity burden, hospital, asthma severity, and index year were associated with concordance.ConclusionICD-10 code–only definition demonstrated high overall agreement with the composite definition, driven primarily by agreement on non-events, but limited agreement for identified exacerbations. These findings suggest that the two definitions may capture different levels of exacerbations. Several factors were associated with concordance between both definitions, highlighting the importance of accounting for these factors when analyzing EHR data. Code-only definitions may be used cautiously in EHR-based studies, given potential heterogeneity and misclassification.Keywords: asthma, asthma exacerbation, agreement, ICD-10, electronic health records

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease that affects millions of people worldwide and is associated with substantial healthcare utilization and morbidity.1–3 It is characterized by airway inflammation and recurrent episodes of worsening symptoms, often referred to as exacerbations.1,3 Asthma exacerbations are a major contributor to this burden and may require urgent treatment, emergency care or hospitalization.1–4 Therefore, accurate identification of asthma exacerbations is critical for clinical care and research, yet remains challenging in real-world electronic health record (EHR) databases.5,6

Several operational definitions have been used in prior studies, reflecting differences in data availability and research objectives.4,7 Composite definitions that incorporate visit types (eg, emergency department [ED] visits or hospitalizations) and systemic corticosteroids (SCS) use are commonly employed to improve accuracy for clinically meaningful exacerbations.1–4 These approaches are often considered closer proxies to true exacerbations but require access to detailed medication and encounter data, which may not always be available.4,7 In contrast, code-only definitions based solely on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes are more readily implementable but may capture a broader range of clinical events.7–10 For example, large-scale studies using EHR-based database, such as TriNetX, have relied on ICD-10 code–only definitions of asthma exacerbation due to data structure constraints.8–11 However, the validity of code-only definitions relative to composite definitions remains uncertain, which raises concerns about potential misclassification.8,9 Outcome misclassification can affect the validity and interpretation of findings, as it may attenuate observed associations or introduce bias in effect estimates.12 Without a clear understanding of how code-only definitions perform relative to commonly used composite definitions, it is difficult to interpret findings derived from databases that lack detailed clinical or treatment information.

To address this gap, this study aimed to (1) compare an ICD-10 code–only definitions of asthma exacerbation with a composite definition incorporating visit types and SCS use, and (2) identify factors associated with concordance between both definitions in EHR data.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from the Taipei Medical University Clinical Research Database (TMUCRD).13 TMUCRD contains EHR data from three affiliated hospitals: Taipei Medical University Hospital (TMUH), Wan Fang Hospital (WFH), and Shuang Ho Hospital (SHH).13 TMUCRD includes information on patient demographics, diagnoses, prescriptions, and laboratory results.13 As of 2023, the database covers approximately 4.4 million patients in Taiwan.13 To account for environmental factors, we linked TMUCRD data to the Air Quality Monitoring Network Database maintained by the Ministry of Environment in Taiwan using patients’ residential ZIP codes.14 Air Quality Index (AQI) values were extracted and included as a covariate. Data accessed complied with relevant data protection and privacy regulations.

Study Population

We included adults aged at least 18 years with asthma between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2023. Asthma was defined as at least one inpatient diagnosis or two outpatient asthma diagnoses of ICD-10 code J45.x. The study cohort comprised all patients who met these eligibility criteria during the study period. The study period began in 2017 to ensure stable use of the ICD-10 coding system following its implementation in Taiwan in 2016.15

Measures

Two operational definitions were used to identify asthma exacerbation events: a code-only definition and a composite definition. The code-only definition required a diagnosis of asthma exacerbation based on specific ICD-10 codes (J45.21, J45.31, J45.41, J45.51, J45.901) during any encounters.8–10 The composite definition was met when an asthma-related encounter (J45.x) was accompanied by at least one of the following: an ED visit, hospitalization, or a prescription for SCS. The composite definition was used as the reference standard for alignment with standard diagnostic evaluation reporting, reflecting commonly adopted approaches in prior EHR and claims-based studies rather than an absolute gold standard.1,4

To avoid counting multiple healthcare encounters related to the same clinical worsening as separate events, encounters were consolidated into exacerbation episodes. The unit of analysis was the episode. Episodes were defined using a seven-day gap rule, whereby any asthma-related encounters occurring within seven days of a prior encounter were considered part of the same episode.16–18 Because the choice of episode window may affect the number and interpretation of identified exacerbations, sensitivity analyses were further conducted using alternative 14-day and 21-day gap windows.19 Asthma-related encounters were defined as healthcare encounters with asthma diagnoses.

Covariates

Covariates were assessed during the one-year period prior to the index episode, including demographics, comorbidities, and asthma severity. Demographics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), AQI, index year, index season, and hospital. Comorbidities included Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), type 2 inflammatory diseases (urticaria, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, eosinophilic esophagitis, prurigo nodularis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]), and IgE-mediated allergy. IgE-mediated allergy was defined by the presence of a serum total IgE level ≥100 IU/mL and a positive allergen-specific IgE test.20,21 Asthma severity was classified based on treatment steps in GINA guidelines.1 Patients whose latest prescription order during the one-year look-back period consisted of biologics, long-acting muscarinic antagonists, high-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), or maintenance SCS (GINA Step 5) were classified as severe asthma, while all others were classified as having mild/moderate asthma.1 Diseases were identified using ICD-10 codes (Table S1). Medications were identified using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes (Table S2).

Statistical Analysis

Agreement between the code-only definition and the composite definition (reference definition) was evaluated using overall percent agreement (OPA), positive percent agreement (PPA), negative percent agreement (NPA), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).22 OPA was defined as the proportion of episodes for which the two definitions yielded the same classification, including both positive and negative classifications. Because individual patients could contribute multiple exacerbation episodes, all estimates accounted for within-patient clustering using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure. Each agreement metric was estimated within the subset of episodes using binomial models with GEE. Specifically, OPA was estimated across the full cohort, PPV was estimated among episodes identified by the code-only definition, NPV among episodes not identified by the code-only definition, PPA among comparator-positive episodes, and NPA among comparator-negative episodes. For these analyses, a binomial distribution with an identity link function was used, such that the model intercept directly represented the marginal probability corresponding to the agreement metric of interest. Unadjusted metrics were obtained using intercept-only models, and adjusted estimates were derived from multivariable models including all covariates. To evaluate temporal variation in agreement, agreement metrics were estimated separately for each calendar year from 2017 to 2023 using the same GEE approach. Calendar year was additionally modeled as a continuous variable to assess linear temporal trends in agreement metrics. Lastly, to evaluate factors associated with agreement between both definitions, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from the multivariable model were reported. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4.

Sensitivity Analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of results. First, the episode definition was varied using alternative gap windows of 14 days and 21 days.19 Second, patients with COPD were excluded to reduce potential misclassification.1,7

Results

A total of 1,486,449 asthma-related episodes were identified during the study period. Of these, 40,180 (2.7%) met the code-only definition for asthma exacerbation and 35,875 (2.4%) met the composite definition, with 10,360 episodes (0.7%) that met both definitions (Figure 1). Discussion

In this study, we found that the code-only definition yielded low PPV and PPA, with consistently high NPV and NPA. Although high overall agreement between both definitions was observed, it was driven primarily by agreement on non-events, with limited positive agreement for identified exacerbations. These findings were robust across alternative episode definitions and after excluding patients with COPD. Agreement between both definitions was associated with asthma severity, hospital, comorbidity burden, and index year.

The partial overlap between definitions, reflected in low PPV and PPA, likely arises from differences in how each definition captured exacerbation events rather than solely from misclassification. Several explanations may account for this pattern. First, episodes identified by the code-only definition may represent true but milder exacerbations, which prompted clinicians to document an exacerbation code during outpatient encounters but did not result in ED visits, hospitalization, or SCS use. Second, some episodes may reflect coding variability, whereby exacerbation-specific ICD-10 codes are applied during routine encounters without a clinically distinct worsening event. Furthermore, the composite definition itself is subject to misclassification. While an SCS prescription alongside an asthma diagnosis code is a common proxy for an acute exacerbation, this combination can capture events other than acute asthma worsening in real-world data.1,23,24 Because a large proportion of our composite-positive episodes occurred among patients with severe asthma, these events may occasionally reflect routine refills for maintenance oral corticosteroids rather than acute bursts.23,24 We cannot distinguish between these explanations, as our data lack direct clinical measures such as symptom scores, spirometry, or peak flow data to validate individual events. Future studies incorporating such measures are needed to clarify whether discordant episodes reflect differences in exacerbation severity, coding practices, or non-specific treatment patterns. Taken together, these findings suggest that the two definitions captured potentially different levels of exacerbation severity rather than simply differing in accuracy.

The high OPA observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of the very low event prevalence, with approximately 0.7% of episodes meeting both definitions. Because most episodes were classified as non-events by both definitions, the high OPA of 93.0% was driven largely by agreement on non-events. Similarly, the high NPV is expected under low event prevalence, whereas the high NPA reflects strong agreement between definitions in identifying non-events.25 Although PPV is also influenced by prevalence,25 it remains more informative than NPV in this setting because it reflects the proportion of code-identified events that were also identified by the comparator definition.

Several patient- and institution-level characteristics were associated with agreement between the two definitions. Episodes among patients with severe asthma had lower odds of agreement than those with mild/moderate asthma. One possible explanation is that patients with severe asthma have more frequent healthcare contact and closer follow-up for chronic disease management, exacerbation-specific ICD-10 codes may be applied more routinely in this group even without a corresponding ED visit, hospitalization, or SCS prescription.1,2,4 This finding is notable given that prior database studies using code-only definitions were largely conducted in patients with severe asthma.8–10 Our results suggest that code-defined events in this population may not be reliably confirmed by the composite definition, and researchers should remain cautious about potential misclassification when applying code-only definitions in similar cohorts. Agreement also varied by hospital, with WFH and SHH showing higher odds of agreement than TMUH. This institutional heterogeneity likely reflects differences in coding practices, care delivery patterns, and prescribing behaviors across sites.4,5,7,26 This finding highlights the importance of accounting for hospital-level variation in multi-center databases and suggests that analyses using code-only definitions should consider adjustment or stratified analyses where feasible.

Agreement was also associated with comorbidity burden and calendar time. Higher comorbidity burden was associated with lower agreement. This may reflect documentation constraints in multimorbid patients, where multiple conditions compete for limited diagnosis code fields, potentially leading to under-documentation of asthma exacerbation.27,28 Agreement also declined steadily across the study period, reflected by the decreasing PPV from 2017 to 2023 and the corresponding decline in odds of agreement (from OR 0.83 in 2018 to OR 0.50 in 2023 relative to 2017). This finding may reflect evolving coding practices over time, such as broader application of exacerbation-specific codes to a wider spectrum of encounter severity,29 as well as potential disruptions in care-seeking and diagnostic patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic.30,31 These findings underscore that misclassification from code-only definitions is not uniform across patient, institutional, or temporal contexts. If the rate of misclassification is the same across comparison groups (non-differential misclassification), it typically biases effect estimates toward the null.12,32 However, our findings suggest that misclassification in EHRs is likely not uniform. Therefore, studies using code-only definitions should carefully consider whether comparison groups are balanced with respect to these characteristics, and sensitivity or stratified analyses may be warranted.

This study has important implications for future research using EHR-based databases, particularly when access to detailed medication or encounter data is limited. Our findings suggest that while code-only definitions are not suitable for estimating the true absolute incidence of asthma exacerbations due to severe under-ascertainment, they remain methodologically feasible for evaluating relative associations, such as comparative effectiveness or safety analyses. A diagnostic proxy characterized by low sensitivity (PPA) but high specificity (NPA) preserves the validity of relative effect measures, such as odds ratios or hazard ratios, moving estimates conservatively toward the null, provided that the misclassification is non-differential between comparison groups.32 Therefore, code-only definitions remain an acceptable alternative when detailed medication data are unavailable. However, researchers may need to demonstrate that comparison groups are balanced with respect to institutional coding practices, asthma severity, comorbidity burden, and calendar periods to justify against differential bias.12,32

This study has several strengths. First, we leveraged a large, multi-center EHR database and applied a rigorous episode-based approach to reduce double-counting of related encounters, thereby improving the accuracy of event ascertainment. Second, the use of GEE allowed us to account for within-patient clustering of exacerbation episodes, reducing bias from correlated observations and providing valid population-averaged estimates. Third, we incorporated covariate adjustment when estimating agreement metrics, enabling the derivation of marginal probabilities that account for differences in patient and clinical characteristics. However, some limitations should be considered. First, the composite definition is not a gold standard and may misclassify events, particularly milder exacerbations.23,24 Future studies incorporating detailed clinical measures, such as symptom scores or lung function, could further validate these findings.1,4 Second, as this study was conducted within three hospitals in Taiwan, it may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings. The extent of agreement between ICD-10 code-only and composite definitions may vary across countries because of differences in coding practices, healthcare utilization patterns, reimbursement systems, and availability of medication and encounter data.26 However, the underlying methodological concern is likely relevant to other healthcare systems. Future studies in other countries and healthcare settings are needed to assess whether the observed patterns of agreement are reproducible across different coding and clinical practice environments.

Conclusion

The ICD-10 code–only definition of asthma exacerbation demonstrated high overall agreement with the composite definition, driven primarily by agreement in non-events, but limited agreement in identified exacerbations. Concordance varied by asthma severity, comorbidity burden, hospital, and calendar year, suggesting that analyses should account for these factors in EHR-based research. Although the composite definition is not a gold standard, these findings suggest that code-only and composite definitions may capture different subsets of exacerbation events. Future studies using detailed clinical measures or external datasets are needed to validate these definitions and evaluate their generalizability across healthcare settings.
