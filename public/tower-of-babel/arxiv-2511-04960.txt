Phase space volume preserving dynamics for non-Hamiltonian systems
Swetamber Das, Jason R. Green

Abstract
Infinitesimal volumes stretch and contract as they coevolve with classical phase space trajectories according to a linearized dynamics. Unless these tangent space dynamics are modified, the underlying chaotic dynamics will cause the volume to vanish as tangent vectors collapse on the most expanding direction. Here, we propose an alternative linearized dynamics and rectify the generalized Liouville equation to preserve phase space volume, even for non-Hamiltonian systems. Within a classical density matrix theory, we define the time evolution operator from the anti-symmetric part of the stability matrix so that phase space volume is time-invariant. The operator generates orthogonal transformations without distorting volume elements, providing an invariant measure for dissipative dynamics and a evolution equation for the density matrix akin to the quantum mechanical Liouville-von Neumann equation. The compressibility of volume elements is determined by a non-orthogonal operator made from the symmetric part of the stability matrix. We analyze complete sets of basis vectors for the tangent space dynamics of chaotic systems, which may be dissipative, transient or driven, without re-orthogonalization of tangent vectors. The linear harmonic oscillator, the Lorenz-Fetter model, and the Hénon-Heiles system demonstrate the computation of the instantaneous Lyapunov exponent spectrum and the local Gibbs entropy flow rate using these bases and show that it is numerically convenient.



I
Introduction

Phase space volume conservation is a defining feature of Hamiltonian dynamics
Gibbs 2010
.
This time invariance of phase space volume is central to the Liouville’s theorem for divergence-free phase space flows.
A more rigorous statement considers the symplectic geometry of the Hamiltonian flow which leads to the incompressiblity of phase space volumes
Arnol’d 1989
.
It is usually understood as the preservation of an infinitesimal volume element evolving in time despite its distortions (expansion, contraction, and rotation) due to the governing flow
Tolman 1979
.
However, many dynamical systems of interest are characterized by phase space
variability
.
Systems that are dissipative, driven, or constrained do not conserve phase space volume.
The statistical mechanics of these non-Hamiltonian systems must then be based on generalizations of Liouville’s theorem and Liouville’s equation.
The most straightforward generalization
Steeb 1979
;
Andrey 1985
;
Andrey 1986
of Liouville’s equation for an arbitrary deterministic system invokes the continuity equation in the phase space
\partial_{t}\rho+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{f})=0
.
This equation governs the time evolution of the phase space density
\rho({\bm{x}},t)
for an ensemble of systems which themselves evolve according to a dynamical flow of the form
\dot{{\bm{x}}}=\bm{f}({\bm{x}})
.
Because Liouville’s equation also holds for out of equilibrium systems, its solution i.e., the phase space probability density
\rho({\bm{x}},t)
is used to compute mean phase space observables such as Gibbs entropy rate.

For dissipative systems, the rate at which the phase space probability density
\rho(t)
grows with time is determined by the divergence of the flow
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\bm{x}}
.
Equivalently, the flow divergence also measures the compressibility of the phase space which is the contraction rate of phase space volume element
\delta V(t)
.
For non-equilibrium steady states,
\rho
diverges as
\delta V
collapses onto a structure of fractal dimension
Hoover and Posch 1995
;
Klages 2007
.
As a consequence, irreversible-thermodynamics concepts such as nonequilibrium Gibbs entropy rate becomes undefined.
The non-smoothness of the
\rho
in non-Hamiltonian systems due to phase space variability remains a long-standing concern
Ramshaw 1986
;
Ramshaw 2002
;
Tuckerman, Mundy, and Martyna 1999
;
Tuckerman et al. 2001
;
Ezra 2004
.
This ambiguity may be resolved either by course-graining of the phase space or by determining a suitable invariant measure
Ramshaw 1986
.
The presence of an appropriate invariant measure implies that the phase space average of a specific property can be correlated with the time-average of that same property along a trajectory (assuming ergodicity). To derive an appropriate measure, a typical way is to modify the phase space probability density by including either a geometric factor or a “measure function” to normalize the probability density
\rho({\bm{x}},t)

Tuckerman, Mundy, and Klein 1997
;
Tuckerman, Mundy, and Martyna 1999
;
Tuckerman et al. 2001
;
Ezra 2004
;
Ramshaw 2019
;
Ramshaw 2020
;
Ramshaw 2022
.
Obtaining such smooth invariant measures that satisfy the Liouville equation is a mathematically nontrivial and often subtle for non-Hamiltonian systems
Holian et al. 1989
;
Ruelle 1999
;
Dorfman 1999
.

Despite significant theoretical progress, computing the phase space density for large systems remains inherently challenging due to dynamical chaos. When phase space trajectories diverge at an exponential rate, the phase space volume develops intricate fine structures, causing
\rho
to become non-smooth and rendering Liouville’s equation inapplicable. This challenge persists even in chaotic Hamiltonian systems, where the conservation of phase space volume does not guarantee a smooth probability density due to continuous stretching and folding of phase space volume. As a result, direct numerical computation to obtain well-behaved phase space density is computationally intensive because chaos causes nearby trajectories to separate exponentially. This leads to a rapid alignment of tangent vectors with the most expanding direction, making phase-space volume computation difficult
Skokos and Manos 2016
. The issue is conventionally addressed via Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization
Ott 2002
, but its iterative nature is computationally expensive and suffers from loss of orthogonality due to round-off errors
Leon, Björck, and Gander 2013
;
Ford 2015
. This challenge is even more pronounced in non-ergodic chaotic systems, where complex phase-space structures and high-dimensional dynamics further complicate computations
Guralnik 2008
;
Hahn et al. 2021
.

Here, we address two questions in the context of classical chaotic dynamics — (1) how to generalize Liouville’s equation to obtain a well-defined invariant measure and (2) how local Lyapunov exponents, in various bases, contribute to the local Gibbs entropy flow rate.
To do that, we use the emerging framework of classical density matrix theory
Das and Green 2022
which is akin to quantum density matrix formulation in its mathematical treatment.
In Sec.
II
, we summarize the theory and show how quantities like the instantaneous Lyapunov exponents and local phase space volume contraction rates appear.
In Sec.
III
, we obtain a set of evolution operators for a modified tangent space dynamics.
Based on the mathematical properties of these operators we derive, in Sec.
IV
, a set of generalized Liouville’s theorem and equations.
To describe local rates of stretching and contraction of phase space volume elements, we introduce several “basis states” in Sec.
V
and show how to derive those local rates for Hamiltonian and non-Hamiltonian systems.
We compute these rates for a several model systems including the damped harmonic oscillator, Lorenz-Fetter model, and Hénon-Heiles system in Sec.

II
Density matrix theory

We have recently introduced a classical density matrix theory for deterministic systems to obtain upper and lower bounds on finite time Lyapunov exponents and a family of classical speed limits on its time evolution
Das and Green 2022
;
Das and Green 2023
;
Das and Green 2024a
;
Das and Green 2024b
. The formalism is based on a projection operator
{\bm{\varrho}}
defined as the outer product of unit tangent vectors:
{\bm{\varrho}}:=\outerproduct{\delta\yu(t)}{\delta\yu(t)}
. Its norm-preserving evolution is

\displaystyle d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}=\{\bar{\bm{A}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}\}+[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}].

(1)

Here,
\bar{\bm{A}}_{+}={\bm{A}}_{+}-\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle={\bm{A}}_{+}-\Tr(\stability_+\brho)
and
{\bm{A}}_{\pm}
represent the symmetric and anti-symmetric parts of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
appearing in the anti-commutator
\{{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}\}={\bm{X}}{\bm{Y}}+{\bm{Y}}{\bm{X}}
and the commutator
[{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}]={\bm{X}}{\bm{Y}}-{\bm{Y}}{\bm{X}}
, respectively.
We refer to
{\bm{\varrho}}
as a pure state because it is constructed from a single tangent vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
.
The norm preserving property of the dynamics manifests as the time invariance of the trace of
{\bm{\varrho}}
:
\Tr\dot{\bm{\varrho}}=0
.
Equation
1
also shows that the dynamics of
{\bm{\varrho}}
is a sum of two norm preserving dynamics:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\{\bar{\bm{A}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}\}}_{\text{incoherent}},\quad\text{and}\quad\underbrace{[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}]}_{\text{unitary}}.

(2)

The symmetric
{\bm{A}}_{+}
and antisymmetric
{\bm{A}}_{-}
parts of the stability matrix generate the dynamics of tangent space with different features.
The antisymmetric part is a unitary (orthogonal) evolution of the tangent vectors.
The incoherent part stretches/contracts tangent vectors.

To see that the incoherent part stretches and contracts tangent vectors, we identify the “expectation value”
\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle
appearing in the anti-commutator in Eq.
1
, as the local Lyapunov exponent corresponding to the density matrix
{\bm{\varrho}}
:

\displaystyle r=\Tr(\stability_+\brho)=\bra{\delta\yu}{\bm{A}}_{+}\ket{\delta\yu}.

(3)

The time average of this local stretching rate of a tangent vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
is

\displaystyle\lambda(t):=\lambda(t,t_{0})=|t-t_{0}|^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}r(t)\,dt,

(4)

the finite-time Lyapunov exponent.
The usual phase-space invariant Lyapunov exponent is computed by taking an asymptotic limit:
\lambda=\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{i}(t)
.

Together the incoherent and unitary parts compose the tangent space dynamics in a deterministic system.
We will discuss these parts individually.

III
Evolution operators

The solution of Eq.
1

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}(t)=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}{\bm{\varrho}}(t_{0})\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}

(5)

evolves the density matrix from time
t_{0}
to time
t
with the operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}(t,t_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{-}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\langle\mathcal{T}e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle^{1/2}}

(6)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time ordering operator.
Figure
1
shows a schematic illustrating the action of
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
on the perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
.

This evolution operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
is reminiscent of the Dyson series that appears in the interaction picture in quantum mechanics
Joachain 1983
.
It has several properties, proven in Appendix
A
:

1.

Norm-preserving:
\bra{\delta\yu(t_0)}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\ket{\delta\yu(t_0)}=\bra{\delta\yu(t)}\ket{\delta\yu(t)}=1

2.

Normal :
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}

3.

Non-unitary:
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\neq\mathbb{1}

The operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
can be decomposed into two normal operators:

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{-}=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{-}dt^{\prime}},\quad\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\langle\mathcal{T}e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle^{1/2}},

(7)

so that
{\bm{M}}_{-}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
. Here, the tilde in
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}
indicates their dependence on the tangent vector (appearing in the denominator), whereas
{\bm{M}}_{-}
is independent of any tangent vector.
Any stretching effect on
\ket{\delta\yu}
originates in
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}
, which involves the symmetric part of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}_{+}
,
and evolves a pure state as

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}_{+}(t)=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}{\bm{\varrho}}(t_{0})\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}.

(8)

For example, a natural choice of basis vectors for expressing
{\bm{\varrho}}
is the eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
(Sec.
V
).

Another important property of both operators
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}_{+}}
are state dependent because their denominator includes an expectation value over a given the tangent vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
which we refer to as a pure state.
Both these operators are pure-state specific and tailored to preserve the norm of the state.

Because the operators
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}_{+}}
define norm-preserving dynamics of tangent vectors, they lead to phase space volumes that remain conserved in time even for non-Hamiltonian systems.
However, as these evolution operators are not unitary (orthogonal) which means that they do not preserve the initial angle between any two tangent vectors.
Without repeated reorthogonalization, the tangent vectors eventually collapse to the most expanding direction along any chaotic trajectory and phase space volume vanishes for both Hamiltonian and non-Hamiltonian systems
Wolf et al. 1985
.
As we show next, the operator
{\bm{M}}_{-}
prevents this collapse.

Unitary evolution of a pure state

Unlike
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}_{+}}
, the operator
{\bm{M}}_{-}
generates a unitary evolution — it preserves the inner product of two pure states:

\displaystyle\bra{\delta\yu_i(t)}\ket{\delta\yu_j(t)}

\displaystyle=\bra{\delta\yu_i(t_0)}{\bm{M}}_{-}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{-}\ket{\delta\yu_j(t)}

(9)

\displaystyle=\bra{\delta\yu_i(t_0)}\ket{\delta\yu_j(t_0)},

(10)

as
{\bm{M}}_{-}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{-}=\mathbb{1}
.
The operator belongs to
SU(n)
as its determinant is one.
Figure
2
shows the action of
{\bm{M}}_{-}
on a set of three pure states.

In dynamical systems with a time-independent
{\bm{A}}
, for instance, the linear harmonic oscillator or Chua’s circuit
Matsumoto, Chua, and Komuro 1985
, the operator becomes
{\bm{M}}_{-}=e^{{\bm{A}}_{-}t}
.
It is a classical analog of the evolution operator
\hat{\bm{U}}=e^{-i\hat{\bm{H}}t}
in quantum mechanics producing unitary evolution of a quantum state for a time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{\bm{H}}
. With this analogy, the equation of motion of a pure state evolving under
{\bm{M}}_{-}
is

\displaystyle d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}_{-}=[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}_{-}],

(11)

which has the solution

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}_{-}(t)={\bm{M}}_{-}^{\top}{\bm{\varrho}}(t_{0}){\bm{M}}_{-}.

(12)

For
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics, a natural basis to write
{\bm{\varrho}}_{-}
is the eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}_{-}
which are complex. This suggests that dynamics take place on a complex tangent space. We discuss this case in Sec.
V
.

The preserved inner products under
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics implies that volumes are invariant under time evolution; that is, volumes defined by
k
linearly independent vectors in a subspace of an
n
-dimensional tangent space, where
k\leq n
.
We use this feature of
{\bm{M}}_{-}
to arrive at a generalization of Liouville’s theorem, which is free from the undesirable collapse of the tangent vectors in chaotic systems.

IV
A generalized Liouville’s theorem and equation

Let us first observe, from Eq.
1
that the normalized evolution preserves the determinant of the density matrix
{\bm{\varrho}}
:

\displaystyle d_{t}\ln|{\bm{\varrho}}|

\displaystyle=\Tr(d_t\ln\brho)=\Tr(\brho^{-1}d_t\brho)=0,

(13)

provided
{\bm{\varrho}}
is invertible. The invertibility requirement excludes pure states whose determinant vanishes. The evolution law of the determinant
|{\bm{\varrho}}|
holds for
mixed
state density matrix which represents an ensemble of phase space points in the neighborhood of a point on the reference trajectory (for details, see
Sahbani, Das, and Green 2025
).

The determinant
|{\bm{\varrho}}|
represents phase space volume and its vanishing equation of motion Eq.
13
represents a generalization the Liouville’s equation. To see this geometrically,

spanning an

{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}=\outerproduct{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}.

(14)

Here

\bm{V}=[\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_1}\,\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_2}\,\cdots\quad\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_n}]

|{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}|=n^{-n}|\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}|=n^{-n}|\bm{V}|^{2}.
Rewriting, we obtain the geometric relation
(d\mathcal{V})^{2}=n^{n}|{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}|,
which connects the phase space volume element
d\mathcal{V}
to determinant
|{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}|

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=n\Tr(\stability_+\brho_\text{max})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Tr(\stability_+\brho_j).

(15)

For Hamiltonian dynamics,
\Lambda(t)=0
and for dissipative systems,
\Lambda(t)<0
. A set of basis states, therefore, provides the full spectrum of instantaneous or local Lyapunov exponents at a given time
t
for an arbitrary dynamical system (Sec.
V

To track how the phase space volume
d\mathcal{V}
changes over time, we express the dynamics of
{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}
using the operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}(t)=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}(t_{0})\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime\top}.

(16)

This operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}
is constructed as
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[\delta_{ij}]\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{i}
. In the Appendix
B

|\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}|=1
which indicates that it is a volume-preserving operator that belongs on
SL(n)
and is generally non-orthogonal. The determinant of
{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}
remains unchanged over time:

\displaystyle|{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}(t)|=|{\bm{\varrho}}_{\text{max}}(t_{0})|.

(17)

Thus, we have shown that the operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}
generates a coordinate transformation that preserves the phase volume element between time
t_{0}
and
t
:

\displaystyle d\mathcal{V}(t)=d\mathcal{V}(t_{0}).

(18)

For a general mixed state
{\bm{\varrho}}
, its non-vanishing determinant
|{\bm{\varrho}}|
defines a phase-space volume spanned by its column vectors, which form a complete set of linearly independent basis vectors. The generalized Liouville equation (Eq.
13
) ensures that this determinant and hence the volume remains preserved over time. This equation is applicable to arbitrary deterministic dynamics including non-Hamiltonian systems.
The invariance of
|{\bm{\varrho}}|
also implies that a complete set of linearly independent vectors remains so during time evolution because these vectors define a non-zero determinant:
|{\bm{\varrho}}|\neq 0
.

However, the generalization of Liouville’s equation under
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
reveals a fundamental computational challenge on chaotic trajectories.
In such systems, tangent vectors align exponentially fast with the most expanding direction, causing any initial volume defined by these vectors to collapse under
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
dynamics — any initial volume defined by those tangent vectors and preserved by
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
dynamics vanishes during computation
Benettin et al. 1980
.
This collapse of the tangent vectors on the most expanding direction is a known technical bottleneck in computing the full Lyapunov spectrum for chaotic systems, where numerical methods impose orthogonality among tangent vectors.
This issue is typically addressed by periodic orthonormalization of the tangent vectors using the Gram-Schmidt procedure.
However, the method is prone to numerical instability due to the accumulation of round-off errors during repeated reorthogonalization, which can artificially inflate or suppress estimated growth rates over long trajectories
Geist, Parlitz, and Lauterborn 1990
.
Furthermore, loss of orthogonality in finite-precision arithmetic results in unreliable perturbation vector norms, leading to spurious positive Lyapunov exponents
Gencay and Dechert 1996
or convergence failures
Hassanaly and Raman 2019
.

The dynamics generated by
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics is free from the orthonormalization issue because it generates a unitary (orthogonal) evolution. An initial complete set of orthonormal basis states remain so for all times (see Fig.
2
) even on a chaotic trajectory. Under
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics, a density matrix (pure or mixed) evolves as:

\displaystyle d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}=[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}]\quad\quad({\bm{M}}_{-}\,\text{dynamics}).

(19)

This is classical analog of the von-Neumann equation for isolated quantum systems. Like the quantum evolution operator
\hat{U}
, the unitary (orthogonal) operator
{\bm{M}}_{-}
preserves the angle between any two pure states (tangent vectors) for all times. Consequently, the evolving perturbation vectors no longer collapse onto the most expanding direction. Also, because trace
\Tr{\bm{A}}_{-}=0
, the corresponding Liouville’s equation remains unchanged:
d_{t}\ln|{\bm{\varrho}}|=0
. The phase space volume given by the determinant
|{\bm{\varrho}}|
is time invariant.

To illustrate the volume preserving dynamics due to
{\bm{M}}_{-}
, we consider the Lorenz-Fetter model

. In Fig.

{\bm{M}}_{-}
. These vectors define a unit 2-sphere of phase space volume that remains invariant as they evolve on the attractor. These vectors rotate but preserve the mutual angles between them along the trajectory. Enlarged spheres show these vectors computed at their respective phase space point.

In general, at every phase point in an
(n+1)

n

\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1)

This sphere is a classical analog of the Bloch sphere

The surface of the classical Bloch sphere contains phase space points local to the reference point at the center.
In this picture, the local expansion/contraction rates at any point

\bra{\delta\yu}{\bm{A}}_{+}\ket{\delta\yu}=\Tr(\stability_+\brho)

V
Basis states and their stretching rates

Local rates for stretching and contractions are important to characterize the evolution and determine the stability of dynamical systems.
The sum of these rates is the local phase space contraction rate, which contributes to the local Gibbs entropy
flow
rate
Andrey 1985
;
Andrey 1986
;
Tuckerman et al. 2001
;
Patra et al. 2016
;
Das and Green 2024b
.
These local or instantaneous Lyapunov exponents depend on local directions in phase space.
While asymptotically the local tangent vectors converge to the most stable direction, they rapidly fluctuate temporally
Eckmann and Ruelle 1985
;
Hoover, Posch, and Hoover 2001
.
The set of these rates computed for the complete set of orthogonal directions provides the entire spectrum of local Lyapunov exponents.
In our density matrix theory, these rates are expectation values of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
over a pure state.

To compute the full spectrum of these exponents, we can choose an arbitrary set of basis states. Consider a
pure
state formed by the basis vector
\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}\in T\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{\delta\bm{\phi}_i(t)}{\delta\bm{\phi}_i(t)}.

(20)

There are uncountably many sets of linearly independent vectors that span an
n
-dimensional phase space.
A simple choice is the Cartesian coordinates fixed on the given trajectory,
\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}=\delta_{ij}\ket{\mathbb{1}}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
\ket{\mathbb{1}}
represents an
n\times 1
unit matrix.
The instantaneous Lyapunov exponents for these basis states are the diagonal elements of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
:

\displaystyle r_{i}=\bra{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}{\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\bm{\phi}_i}=\Tr(\stability_+\brho_i)=(\bm{A})_{ii}.

(21)

Other coordinates systems can be chosen to represent the basis states that are time-independent or time-dependent and comoving with the phase point.

The time evolution of a set of complete basis states at an initial time
t_{0}
is governed by the operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
(or
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
or
{\bm{M}}_{-}
). Alternatively, a set of basis states can be chosen at each phase space point (we give some examples below). These two sets of basis states are not necessarily identical, but for
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics, differ by an overall phase factor or an orthogonal rotation.

These basis vectors can be used to write the corresponding maximally mixed state at time
t
using Eq.
14
. One can construct another mixed state at the same time
t
, say
{\bm{\varrho}}^{d}
using a set of basis states defined by the original dynamics. These two states
{\bm{\varrho}}
and
{\bm{\varrho}}^{d}
are related by a similarity transformation:

\displaystyle{\bm{\varrho}}^{d}=\bm{P}{\bm{\varrho}}\bm{P}^{-1},

(22)

where
\bm{P}
is a change of basis matrix.
Therefore, all such maximally mixed states are
similar
to each other and give the local phase space dissipation rate when
{\bm{A}}_{+}
is averaged over any of them (see Eq.
15
).

Next we analyze several flow-dependent basis vectors to demonstrate the computation of the entire spectrum of instantaneous Lyapunov exponents in this framework.

a.
{\bm{A}}_{+}
basis.–
Eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
,
\sigma^{{\bm{A}}_{+}}_{i}
, satisfy

{\bm{A}}_{+}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}=\sigma^{{\bm{A}}_{+}}_{i}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}
.
The instantaneous Lyapunov exponents are tangent space averages:

\displaystyle r_{i}^{{\bm{A}}_{+}}

\displaystyle=\Tr(\stability_+\brho_i)=\sigma^{{\bm{A}}_{+}}_{i}.

(23)

Due to the symmetric nature of
{\bm{A}}_{+}
, its eigenvalues are always real.
Also, notice that in the equation of motion for
{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}
(Eq.
1
) for the general
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
dynamics, the first and the third terms cancel out and the equation simplifies:

\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}}{dt}=[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}].

(24)

The same equation of motion holds for the
{\bm{M}}_{-}
dynamics. However, for
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}
, the corresponding equation reduces to
d_{t}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}=\mathbb{0}
. This means that in the
{\bm{A}}_{+}
eigenbasis, the operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}
generated dynamics preserves the basis state
{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}
.

A discussion on other sets of possible basis states needs a generalization of the formalism to finite-dimensional complex vector spaces. Consider a generalized basis state given by a complex vector
\ket{\delta\yu}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and a Hermitian density matrix
{\bm{\varrho}}
. In this case, we can update our usage of Dirac’s notation to now represent a finite-dimensional column (row) vector with the ket (complex conjugate bra). However, the operators
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{A}}_{\pm}
are still given by real matrices. The local Lyapunov exponents are
\Tr(\stability_+\brho)
also real for a Hermitian
{\bm{\varrho}}
as we will see next.

b.
{\bm{A}}_{-}
basis.–

The eigenvectors of the anti-symmetric matrix
{\bm{A}}_{-}
form a complete basis but are not necessarily orthogonal.
The eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}_{-}
are purely imaginary, so:
[{\bm{A}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}]={\bm{A}}_{-}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}-{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}{\bm{A}}_{-}=\mathbb{0}.
Eq.
1
then becomes

\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}}{dt}=\{{\bm{A}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}_{i}\}-2r_{i}^{{\bm{A}}_{-}}{\bm{\varrho}}_{i},

(25)

with the instantaneous Lyapunov exponent
r_{i}^{{\bm{A}}_{-}}
in the eigenbasis of
{\bm{A}}_{-}
. The generalized Liouville equation for the maximally mixed state
{\bm{\varrho}}
in this basis is then

\frac{d|{\bm{\varrho}}|}{dt}=\bigl\rvert\{{\bm{A}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}\}-2r{\bm{\varrho}}\bigr\rvert=0.

(26)

c.
{\bm{A}}
basis.–
The eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}
form a complete set of basis but not mutually orthogonal. If the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
are denoted by
\sigma^{{\bm{A}}}_{i}
, then the instantaneous Lyapunov exponents for these basis states are derived as follows

\displaystyle r_{i}^{{\bm{A}}}

\displaystyle=\Tr(\stability_+\brho_i)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\stability\brho_i + \stability^\top\brho_i)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{{\bm{A}}}_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{\dagger{\bm{A}}})=\real(\sigma^{{\bm{A}}}_{i}),

where
\dagger
denotes the complex conjugate and

gives the real part.

Hamiltonian systems

Given the Liouville’s theorem is a statement about volume conservation, we a natural example is Hamiltonian dynamics.
For Hamiltonian systems, the components of a Lyapunov vector are the first variations of position and momentum
(\delta\bm{q},\delta\bm{p})
and the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
becomes:

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{\bm{q}}\partial_{\bm{p}}H&\partial_{\bm{p}}^{2}H\\
-\partial_{\bm{q}}^{2}H&-\partial_{\bm{p}}\partial_{\bm{q}}^{2}H\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{0}&\partial_{\bm{p}}^{2}H\\
-\partial_{\bm{q}}^{2}H&\mathbb{0}\\
\end{array}\right).

The trace of the Jacobian gives the traditional form of Liouville’s theorem.
In the last equality, we assume the Hamiltonian is
H(\bm{q},\bm{p})=T(\bm{p})+V(\bm{q})
and the stability matrix is the product of its Hessian and the Poisson matrix:

\bm{\Omega}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{I}\\
-\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\end{array}\right).

The identity matrices
\mathbb{I}
are
n/2\times n/2
.

The symmetric and anti-symmetric parts of the stability matrix are:

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\bm{p}}^{2}H-\partial_{\bm{q}}^{2}H)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\bm{p}}^{2}H+\partial_{\bm{q}}^{2}H)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{0}&\mathbb{I}\\
-\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\end{array}\right),

respectively. Equation
1
then becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{\varrho}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{+},{\bm{\varrho}}\}+[{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{-},{\bm{\varrho}}]-2r{\bm{\varrho}}.

(37)

{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{+}

The eigenvectors of

n

and

VII
Conclusions

A classical density matrix theory provides a useful alternative framework to generalize Liouville’s theorem and equation for arbitrary deterministic systems (Hamiltonian or non-Hamiltonian). Our construction leads to time invariant phase space volumes and decouples the stretching/contraction effect from the tangent space dynamics. To this end, we identified the role of symmetric and anti-symmetric part of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}
obtained from linearizing a dynamical system. We show that the growth or shrinking of a perturbation vector is governed exclusively by
{\bm{A}}_{+}
while the anti-symmetric part
{\bm{A}}_{-}
generates only rotations in the tangent space. Based on this partition, we have introduced two time evolution operators
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
and
{\bm{M}}_{-}
, both of which preserve the norm of a perturbation vector evolving along a phase space trajectory. Both time evolution operators then yield generalized Liouville equations like equations producing norm-preserving dynamics of
{\bm{\varrho}}
. The mathematical form of these equations are similar to the quantum von-Neumann-Liouville’s equation.

We have also shown that the generator
{\bm{A}}_{-}
produces an evolution in the form of a “classical Bloch sphere” of phase space volume that is time invariant.
In addition, we showed that the framework for this
{\bm{M}}_{-}
based evolution is particularly useful for chaotic systems in which tangent vectors evolving under the linearized dynamics collapse on to the most expanding direction at a give phase point.
Because
{\bm{M}}_{-}
generates a unitary (orthogonal) evolution, the inner product of tangent vectors remains preserved.
This modified dynamics then offers an computationally convenient method for computing the Lyapunov spectrum as the periodic GS orthogonalization procedure is not required to be performed.
Any orthogonal set of basis vectors remains so throughout the evolution, by construction, and the instantaneous Lyapunov exponents are computed using the symmetric part of the stability matrix
{\bm{A}}_{+}
.
We have also discussed several set of basis states and computed those exponents for Hamiltonian and non-Hamiltonian systems as examples.

Appendix

A
Properties of the evolution matrix
\widetilde{\bm{M}}

Norm-preserving

Along a phase space trajectory, a unit perturbation vector
\ket{\delta\yu}
evolves in the tangent space according to:

\displaystyle d_{t}\ket{\delta\yu}={\bm{A}}\ket{\delta\yu}-\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle\ket{\delta\yu},

(A1)

where
{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}_{+}+{\bm{A}}_{-}
and
\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle=\bra{\delta\yu}{\bm{A}}_{+}\ket{\delta\yu}
. The solution of this equation of motion is given by:

\displaystyle\ket{\delta\yu(t)}=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\ket{\delta\yu(t_0)}.

(A2)

Here,
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
is a the norm-preserving operator given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}(t,t_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{-}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{T}\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle_{t_{0}}^{1/2}}.

(A3)

where
\mathcal{T}
stands for time-ordered integration indicating the continuum limit of successive multiplications.

To see that
\widetilde{\bm{M}}
recovers the equation of motion for
\ket{\delta\yu}
, we differentiate it with respect to
t
:

\displaystyle d_{t}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}={\bm{A}}_{-}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}+{\bm{A}}_{+}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}-\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}.

(A4)

This operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
is norm-preserving as it preserves the unit norm for
\ket{\delta\yu(t_0)}
as it evolves with time.

Normal but non-orthogonal

The operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
is normal
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
=
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}
. To see this, we write these matrix product below:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{T}\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle_{t_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,2{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{T}\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle}=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}.

(A5)

The exponential terms with
{\bm{A}}_{-}
cancel out as time-ordering is taken into account.
Next, the expectation value

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\rangle=1,

(A6)

which indicates the norm-preserving property of
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
.

However,
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
is non-orthogonal in general:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}=\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\top}\neq\mathbbm{1}.

(A7)

B
Volume preserving operator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}^{\prime}

The operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}
(or
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}_{+}}
) is norm-preserving and specific to a pure state. However, for maximally mixed state which is given by a equally weighted sum of pure states formed using the complete set of
n
basis vectors, we would need a set of
n
operators
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{i}
.

To derive the Generalized Liouville’s equation in the main text, we construct a volume preserving operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}
given by:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{i}\right)^{1/n},

(B8)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{i}(t,t_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{-}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\langle\mathcal{T}e^{2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle_{i}^{1/2}}.

(B9)

Moreover, we see that for
\delta t\to 0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\langle e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}\rangle_{t_{0}}\approx\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}2\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle dt^{\prime}},

which leads to:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}(t,t_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{-}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle_{i}dt^{\prime}}}.

Thus, the determinant of volume preserving
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}
becomes:

\displaystyle|\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}|^{1/n}=\mathcal{T}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle_{i}dt^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,\Tr{\bm{A}}_{+}dt^{\prime}},

where we have used the relation
|e^{\bm{X}}|=e^{\Tr\bm{X}}
for a square matrix
\bm{X}
and the identity
\Tr{\bm{A}}_{-}=0
as
{\bm{A}}_{-}
is anti-symmetric.

But
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle_{i}
is the sum of instantaneous Lyapunov exponents which is the phase space volume contraction rate
\Lambda=\Tr{\bm{A}}_{+}
. So, the terms
-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{\bm{A}}_{+}\rangle_{i}
and
\Tr{\bm{A}}_{+}
in argument of the resulting exponential precisely cancel out and we find:

\displaystyle|\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}^{\prime}|=1.

(B10)

The same treatment for the symmetric operator
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}^{\prime}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[\delta_{ij}]\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+i}
leads to
|\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}_{+}^{\prime}|=1
. Therefore, both of these volume preserving operators belong to
SL(n)
.
