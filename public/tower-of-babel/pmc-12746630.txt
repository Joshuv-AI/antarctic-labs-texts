Assessing preventive dental care in Italy: insights from a KAP survey
Authors: Demetrio Lamloum, Fabio Scaffidi, Thomas Gerhard Wolf, Maddalena Gaeta, Anna Odone, Guglielmo Campus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreventive dental care, despite its well-established benefits, is underutilized in many jurisdictions, including Italy, where a curative approach often prevails. An international KAP survey (Italy, Switzerland, Serbia, India, Argentina, Spain) was planned to assess the dentists’ knowledge, attitudes, and barriers to preventive care, exploring the influence of age and gender, alongside factors like revenues and time. The present study specifically reports on the data collected from the Italian cohort, serving as a pilot within this broader international endeavour.MethodsA cross-sectional survey on Italian dentists was conducted. The questionnaire, originally in English, underwent back-translation to ensure linguistic accuracy before evaluating knowledge, attitudes, and barriers. The data collected via Google Forms were analysed using STATA® 18.0, with descriptive statistics, contingency table analysis, Cuzick’s test and multinomial logistic regression being used.ResultsThree-hundred-twenty-two questionnaires were completed (65.8% male, 34.2% female), with the 46–65 age group being the largest (50%). The distribution of participants across dental specialties varied significantly by gender: female dentists predominated in Pediatric Dentistry (84.6%) and Orthodontics (61.1%), while males were more prevalent in Oral Surgery (87.18%). Age significantly impacted knowledge of sugar intake (p = 0.04) and fluoride use (p < 0.01), with younger dentists showing higher knowledge. Knowledge on sealant application was significantly higher among males compared to females (p = 0.04). Age affected attitudes toward smoking cessation and providing oral hygiene instruction (p < 0.01). Males reported higher remuneration concerns (p = 0.03). Multinomial logistic regression identified female gender, first visit age, sugar consumption knowledge, and perceived knowledge deficit as significant predictors of the age-knowledge interaction.ConclusionsStatistically significant age and gender disparities exist in knowledge, attitudes, and barriers to preventive dental care among Italian dentists. Younger dentists demonstrated superior preventive knowledge, while older dentists perceived greater knowledge deficits. Gender influenced knowledge and remuneration concerns. Educational interventions and policy changes are needed to integrate preventive practices, addressing age and gender-specific needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07184-8.

Background

The global recognition of the importance of dental public health and preventive care has been ramping up, especially since the late century with a severe resource constrains in national healthcare budgets [1–3]. Many countries implemented oral health promotion programs targeting different population groups, alongside fluoridation initiatives aimed at reducing the burden of oral diseases such as dental caries and improving quality of life [1, 4–6]. Despite the robust evidence supporting the efficacy of preventive clinical-based dental interventions, their adoption is consistently limited across various jurisdictions and the integration into routine dental practice remains suboptimal [7]. Traditionally, dental care has been characterized by a surgical, curative, and technology-focused approach, which prioritizes treatment over prevention [8]. This discrepancy can be attributed, in part, to a confluence of factors, including financial disincentives, time constraints, perceived impacts on professional quality of life, complexities associated with payment systems, and the ambiguity of clinical guidelines [9]. Consequently, interventions aimed at modifying provider attitudes, addressing knowledge deficits, and resolving practical implementation barriers are crucial to enhance the delivery of primary preventive dental care [10]. A comprehensive understanding of healthcare providers’ perceptions towards preventive oral care remains limited. While some studies have explored this area via Knowledge Attitudes Practice (KAP) surveys, a systematic evaluation across diverse world regions is lacking [11]. KAP surveys, developed in the 1950 s within family planning and population research, have become a widely recognized methodology for investigating health-related behaviours and professional practices. KAP surveys when employed to assess dentists’ understanding, perceptions, and implementation of dental preventive practices aim to elicit their knowledge regarding current preventive guidelines, attitudes towards delivering preventive interventions, and their reported practices and perceived barriers in daily clinical settings. Such approach allows for generating both quantitative and qualitative insights into the factors influencing the delivery of preventive care [10].

Italy’s preventive oral care system remains limited in accessibility [12]. Public dental services coverage in Italy inconsistent across regions, is leading to disparities in preventive care access [13]. It lacks a systematic school-based preventive interventions, public subsidies or copayments systems, and coverage of specialized public pediatric care in remote areas, resulting in a reliance on private dental care for preventive treatments [14]. Moreover, financial and organizational barriers hinder the integration of prevention into routine dental practice, with fee-for-service models incentivizing curative rather than preventive approaches [15]. The limited role of preventive care in oral health promotion further exacerbates the issue, as general dental practitioners receive minimal training in dental prevention, reducing opportunities for early intervention [16]. This challenge is further compounded by barriers to accessing dental care, such as workforce shortages and skewed preferences in the types of care delivered based on gender [17]. For example, female practitioners often exhibit a greater inclination toward paediatric and preventive dentistry, which may be influenced by sex socialization and stereotyped interests, perceived patient rapport, and the nurturing aspects often associated with working with children [18, 19]. In contrast, older dentists might tend to show less proactivity in managing children’s oral health, potentially due to generational differences in training, preferences for specific patient demographics, or a focus on curative treatments developed over years of clinical practice [20–22]. Providers may lack up-to-date training on evidence-based preventive approaches, particularly if continuing education is not systematically encouraged within this dental branch [23]. Without sufficient awareness of the long-term benefits and cost-effectiveness of preventive care, providers may prioritize treatments that align more closely with their existing skill sets or perceived patient demands [23]. Additionally, providers’ personal preferences and professional biases can shape the delivery of preventive care. Some dentists may feel more comfortable or confident in delivering operative treatments due to their familiarity with advanced technology and procedural techniques [24]. Others might perceive preventive care as less prestigious or impactful compared to restorative or surgical interventions, which are often associated with visible and immediate patient outcomes. Understanding these provider-specific factors is essential to bridge gaps in care delivery [25]. Therefore, shifting from treatment-cantered approaches to prevention requires not only systemic policy changes but also a fundamental reorientation in the professional practices of oral health providers.

Providers play a pivotal role in shaping the delivery of preventive services, and their willingness, understanding, and practical application of preventive approaches directly influence patient outcomes [23]. Since research indicates significant variability in how dental professionals perceive and implement preventive care. Sex, age, and cultural factors are among the determinants shaping a KAP survey [26].

Given such premises, the gap between the recognized importance of prevention and its practical implementation underscores the necessity of identifying obstacles to the adoption of preventive care and formulating strategies that integrate oral health as an essential element of overall health and well-being [27]. Hence, an international survey was planned with the main purpose of collecting data on prevention, barriers, and other relevant factors impacting the delivery of dental care and discovering global trend, common features, and targeted needs.

The objective of this paper is to evaluate the knowledge of Italian dentists regarding dental prevention and to correlate their socio-demographic variables with their clinical practices and perceived barriers in implementing preventive measures in a pilot study.

Methods

This is a pilot study conducted in Italy. A structured questionnaire was utilized to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of dental professionals regarding preventive care. Data collection is also currently ongoing in five more countries so far: Switzerland, Serbia, India, Argentina, and Spain. Only data about Italy will be provided here. The same methodology was systematically applied in each jurisdiction.

To ensure a comprehensive and diverse representation of dental professionals, the authors partnered with local unions and key dental associations. Specifically, the National Association of Italian Dentists (ANDI) facilitated the distribution of the questionnaire to their members in Italy. Inclusion criteria for participants were limited to dentists currently enrolled in ANDI, with a major requirement being active professional practice as stipulated by the ANDI statute. Based on the number of dentists members of the associations (27,000), considering a 50% response rate and a 5% confidence limits, 323 were required.

The self-administered standardized questionnaire was developed based on prior research on prevention in general dental practice [22], addressing four key domains with a total of 20 questions: (1) Vital statistics; (2) Knowledge of preventive care; (3) Attitudes toward delivering preventive interventions; and 4). Perceived barriers to performing preventive care (Supplementary Table 4). The first domain included questions specifically designed to capture local nuances and challenges within each participating country. The knowledge section of the questionnaire was dichotomous, with correct answers based on established guidelines and scientific evidence. Additionally, Likert scales with four scale points were employed to assess attitudes and perceptions, using a scale ranging from “totally agree” to “disagree” and from “always” to “never”. Results from the Likert scale responses are presented both as numerical values and percentages for each scale point [28].

The initial version of the questionnaire was in English and underwent a forward and then back-translation process to ensure linguistic accuracy in the participants’ native languages: Italian. The forward translation procedure involved two translators with full English proficiency: one dental professional and one non-oral health professional. This translator’s role was to ensure the clinical and technical accuracy of the terminology. An independent, native English-speaking non-oral health with no prior knowledge of the original English version translated the Italian version back into English. Eventually, the original English questionnaire and the back-translated English version were then compared. Discrepancies were identified and resolved through discussion, ensuring the equivalence of the Italian version.

This pilot phase involved a two-round cognitive debriefing to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness of the questions. Participants provided feedback on ambiguous phrasing, potential misunderstandings, and any questions that did not resonate with the local context. The final Italian version of the questionnaire was refined based on this feedback to ensure it was well-understood and appropriate for the target population. This approach ensured that the final instrument was culturally relevant, linguistically accurate, and aligned with the objectives of the study.

The present study did not require ethical approval for its observational design according to Italian law (Gazzetta Ufficiale no. 76 dated 31 March 2008). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection was carried out using Google Forms, an online survey tool, which allowed us to efficiently gather responses from a wide geographical area. The questionnaires were distributed via email by ANDI in multiple waves from January to June 2024. Participation was voluntary, with respondents required to complete a privacy consent form prior to participation. All responses were anonymous to ensure privacy and confidentiality for all participants, and each respondent was allowed to submit only one response. Data were automatically transferred into Excel (Microsoft Office, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) via Google Form for organization and subsequent analysis. Data analysis was performed using STATA® 18.0 statistical software (StatCorp., Austin, TX, USA) at a statistically significant level of p < 0.05. This approach facilitated large-scale data collection while maintaining the ethical standards of the study.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the study participants. This included presenting the frequency and percentage distributions of participants by age group and gender. To examine the relationships between categorical variables, specifically perceived knowledge, attitudes, and barriers to dental practice and demographic factors (age and gender), contingency table analysis was employed. To ensure statistical validity, when a response category contained in more than one variable fewer than 4 observations, it was merged with the closest response category. To assess the association between age groups and the degree of knowledge, Cuzick’s non-parametric test for trend was performed to test the presence of a statistically significant trend across ordered groups. An interaction variable, designed to reflect the interplay between age and knowledge, was constructed to categorize participants into distinct levels. These levels were derived from a combination of age and knowledge scores, where knowledge scores were calculated as the sum of correct responses to knowledge-based questions. The interaction variable categorized participants as follows: Level 1: Youngest, least knowledge; Level 2: Young with medium/high knowledge OR older with low knowledge; Level 3: Middle-aged with moderate knowledge; Level 4: Middle-aged with high knowledge OR older with low/moderate knowledge; Level 6: Older with high knowledge; and Level 9: Oldest, most knowledge. A multinomial logistic regression model was subsequently employed to determine the predictors of this interaction variable.

The multinomial logistic regression analysis to examine the factors associated with the composite measure was significantly associated with several predictors. Female dentists exhibited significantly lower odds of being associated with higher interaction values (p < 0.001). The recommended age for the first visit showed a consistent and significant negative association with the interaction outcome. Knowledge of maximum consumption of sugar was significantly associated with the interaction variable (p = 0.14). Discussion

The primary objective of this pilot study was to identify dental professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers concerning preventive dental practices, with a particular emphasis on the influence of age and gender, while testing the design to determine its feasibility for a more extensive future extension in other countries.

The demographic distribution of the sample revealed a predominance of males, particularly in the 46–65 age group, and a notable gender-based variation in dental specialty representation. This imbalance may reflect broader trends in the dental profession, where certain specialties, such as oral surgery, continue to be male-dominated, while others, like Paediatric Dentistry, attract a higher proportion of female practitioners [30].

The analysis of knowledge-based questions highlighted significant age-related differences in key areas. Younger professionals (24–45) demonstrated superior knowledge regarding recommended sugar intake and fluoride, suggesting that recent educational initiatives may have effectively disseminated this information. In contrast, older dentists were more likely to perceive a lack of knowledge as a barrier when delivering preventive care. The significance level for the recommended age for the first dental visit and sealant application indicates a potential shift in knowledge acquisition, influenced by evolving guidelines and increased emphasis on preventive care in younger cohorts. The absence of age-related differences in other knowledge domains, such as Early Childhood Caries (ECC) and oral health risk factors, suggests a more consistent understanding across age groups or potentially, a need for targeted interventions to reinforce these areas [31]. This aligns with existing literature that emphasizes the need for comprehensive continuing education programs to equip dental professionals with the necessary skills to deliver effective preventive care [32]. These programs should be targeted to address specific knowledge gaps and learning preferences for specific age-groups, such as older dentists, ultimately empowering practitioners to integrate preventive measures into their routine care [33].

Gender differences were less pronounced in knowledge but emerged significantly in the perception of sealant application. This finding, with males demonstrating higher knowledge, tends to go in opposition to common perception. It could reflect variations in training or practice focus, even gender-specific learning preferences, or selection bias [34]. Furthermore, male practitioners showed higher agreement in their reported application of fluoride varnish and providing dietary advice, suggesting a broader gender-based divergence in the adoption of preventive services. Additionally, a notable difference in professional consensus or practice patterns between the specialty groups concerning the timing of initial dental intervention for children was observed. Such findings point to a need for broader dissemination of these recommendations among general dental practitioners and other specialists to ensure optimal early preventive care.

Perceived barriers to practice also exhibited demographic variations. The significant age-related difference in perceived lack of time suggests that younger professionals may face greater time constraints, possibly due to practice establishment or higher patient loads within a fee-for service setting [12, 15]. The trend towards significance for lack of remuneration across age groups indicates a broader concern within the profession [35, 36]. Importantly, the significant gender difference in perceived lack of remuneration, with males reporting higher agreement, warrants attention [37]. Disparities in income or practice management strategies across age and gender groups may contribute to these findings. Younger dentists, facing potentially lower revenues, might prioritize revenue-generating procedures over preventive care, while male dentists may perceive preventive care as less financially appealing [30].

Attitudinal analyses revealed significant age-related differences in the perceived role of dentists in smoking cessation and the provision of oral hygiene instructions. Younger professionals were more likely to acknowledge their role in smoking cessation, possibly reflecting a greater awareness of the broader health impact of dental practice. Older professionals, conversely, showed higher agreement regarding oral hygiene instruction, which might stem from their accumulated clinical experience and a focus on traditional preventive measures [38].

Such findings underscored the impact of practice environments and financial models on the adoption of preventive care. In Italy, the fee-for-service payment system prioritizes time-efficient, high-revenue treatments, within the constraints of medical ethics and clinical guidelines, posing a substantial barrier to the integration of preventive practices [12, 39]. Furthermore, the significant gap in the literature regarding dental patients’ perspectives on prevention in oral health care, specifically their understanding of “prevention” and their perceived role in it, further complicates efforts to promote preventive approaches it [11]. Pay for performance systems (P4P) might pave the path for a potential avenue to address these challenges, by turning the focus from volume-based reimbursement to value-based care [15]. These models can incentivize dental professionals to prioritize preventive services. For example, specific performance metrics could be established for the delivery of preventive interventions and their long-term benefit, such as oral hygiene instruction, fluoride varnish application, or smoking cessation counselling [15, 40]. Furthermore, P4P could reward dentists for reducing the necessity of curative dental treatments, generating better health outcomes, or a high patient satisfaction rate in relation to preventive care [40].

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. The sample, while representative in some respects, may not fully reflect the broader dental professional population. While our sample size calculation indicated that 323 dentists were required, a larger sample could have produced more reliable results. Additionally, the voluntary nature of the questionnaire makes the study’s exact replication difficult. However, this approach was chosen to reach a broad range of specialists and minimize the selection bias that a more targeted recruitment strategy would have introduced. This pilot study elicits that an all-in-one recipe has yet to come. Taken together, these factors, time constraints, financial incentives, knowledge gaps, and personal preferences, create a complex interplay of barriers that limit the prioritization of preventive care within dental practices.

Conclusions

These barriers reflect longstanding professional norms within the dental field, where curative treatments are often seen as more urgent and rewarding than preventive care. The results suggest that overcoming these barriers will require a multifaceted approach, from cultural shift within the profession, to prevention prioritization as an integral component of oral health care.
