Influence of coal liquefaction residues as fine aggregate substitution on asphalt mixture performance and adsorption characteristics
Authors: Zhe Wang, Zhen Wang, Shuangfeng Guo, Chuan Sha, Yansheng Yang, Minghao Zhang, Xiaoguang Zhou, Peng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effects of partially replacing fine limestone aggregates in asphalt mixtures with indirect coal liquefaction residue (ICLR) and direct coal liquefaction residue (DCLR). Through equal-volume substitution based on the Marshall mix design method, the influence of ICLR and DCLR on pavement performance and asphalt-aggregate interfacial behavior was systematically evaluated. Performance assessments included high-temperature rutting resistance, low-temperature bending, freeze–thaw splitting, and immersion Marshall tests. Adsorption characteristics and asphalt film thickness were also analyzed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. The results indicate that single-size substitution with ICLR (1.18–2.36 mm or 2.36–4.75 mm) significantly enhances rutting resistance, whereas combined (dual or triple) substitutions result in reduced performance. For DCLR, single-size substitution (0.6–1.18 mm) provided the greatest improvement in high-temperature stability. While ICLR had negligible effects on low-temperature flexibility, DCLR slightly reduced the mixture’s low-temperature ductility. Both residues markedly improved moisture resistance, with the most substantial benefits observed in single-size substitutions. Limestone aggregates showed the highest total asphalt adsorption capacity, followed by ICLR and DCLR. However, DCLR exhibited faster adsorption kinetics. Increased asphalt film thickness was negatively correlated with rutting resistance and low-temperature strain but had limited influence on moisture stability. Overall, the study recommends using single-size ICLR (1.18–2.36 mm) or DCLR (0.6–1.18 mm) at substitution rates not exceeding 11% by volume to optimally balance high-temperature performance, moisture resistance, and mechanical durability. These findings support the sustainable application of coal liquefaction residues in asphalt pavement engineering.Keywords: Coal liquefaction residue, Fine aggregate, Asphalt mixture, Adsorption behavior, Pavement performanceSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Coal plays a fundamental role in China’s energy consumption structure. In 2022, China’s coal consumption reached 5.4 billion tons, accounting for over 50% of the global total1. To achieve cleaner and more efficient utilization of coal, coal liquefaction technology has emerged as a key approach. This advanced clean coal technology converts solid coal into liquid fuels, chemical feedstocks, and other valuable products through chemical processes, primarily via direct liquefaction and indirect liquefaction2.However, both liquefaction processes generate significant amounts of by-products: direct coal liquefaction residue (DCLR) and indirect coal liquefaction residue (ICLR), which account for approximately 30% of the original coal material)3,4. Currently, the management of DCLR and ICLR in China remains limited; these residues are often treated as waste or incinerated directly, leading to substantial resource wastage as well as negative ecological and social impacts5,6.Studies indicate that ICLR exhibits physical properties similar to natural sand and gravel—it is hard, dense, and stable at high temperatures—suggesting potential use as an aggregate or blending agent in construction materials7,8. On the other hand, DCLR contains 20–30% heavy oil, 20–40% asphaltene, 15–30% pre-asphaltene, and approximately 45% tetrahydrofuran-insoluble matter. Its composition makes it a promising candidate for use as a modifier in road asphalt9,10.

Currently, international research on DCLR-modified asphalt and corresponding mixtures is limited, with almost no established background. Domestic scholars have only recently begun relevant research, which remains in its preliminary stages. Wang Zhaixia utilized different mass fractions of DCLR instead of TLA, so that the modified asphalt obtained the needle penetration of 40–55 levels11. Luo et al. conducted experimental studies on asphalt before and after DCLR modification using a dynamic shear rheometer, and found that the temperature sensitivity of the modified asphalt was reduced, and there was a significant improvement in resistance to fatigue cracking, deformation resistance and deformation recovery12. Shi and Wu modified asphalt using tetrahydrofuran soluble substance (THFS) in DCLR, revealed the mechanism of THFS modification of asphalt, and used five representative asphalt to evaluate the compatibility with DCLR, proposed Shell-90 and SK-90 asphalt is the best compatibility with DCLR, and suggested that the dosage of DCLR should not be more than 12%13,14. Ji et al. found that DCLR can improve the high-temperature performance of asphalt but has some damage to the low-temperature performance, when the addition of the appropriate amount of capacitance enhancers can enhance the low-temperature cracking resistance of DCLR-modified asphalt, and after the second compound modification found that the dosage of 3% dioctyl maleate (DOM) to enhance the low-temperature performance of the DCLR-modified asphalt is more obvious15–17. Ji et al. found that the addition of DCLR can improve the adhesion between asphalt and limestone and the water stability of the mixture, and has better resistance to high temperatures and heavy loads, and can significantly reduce the rutting depth of the pavement18–20.

So far, few studies have applied ICLR in road engineering, and its application as a fine aggregate in asphalt pavements has not been reported. Alexander et al.21 found that the addition of ICLR can lead to better mechanical properties of the mixture. Zhang Yiluo et al.22 added ICLR to asphalt stabilized aggregates and found that ICLR asphalt stabilized aggregates have better fatigue resistance at low stress levels. Ren23 concluded that replacing part of the mineral powder with ICLR can lead to better high temperature performance of asphalt pavement and enhance the snow and ice removal effect of the pavement. Lei24 found that ICLR coarse slag is suitable to be applied to semi-rigid base materials, while ICLR fine slag is suitable for asphalt surface materials. Wang et al.25 found that 0–10 mm ICLR can improve the permeability coefficient, internal friction angle and compression modulus of the mixture, which is conducive to making the mixture obtain good mechanical properties. Gao et al.26 found that 0–4.75 mm ICLR can make the 7d unconfined compressive strength of pavement base material reach 2.2–6.8 MPa, and the addition of fly ash on the basis of this can improve the mechanical properties and frost resistance of the base material. Hu et al.27 and Qin et al.28 believed that 0–4.75 mm ICLR doping in the vicinity of 10% improves the low temperature performance of the mix significantly. Liu et al.29 concluded that 0–9.5 mm ICLR doped with 10–20% is beneficial to improve the mix’s internal friction angle, water loss resistance, fatigue performance, and to a certain extent high temperature stability. 0–4.75 mm ICLR doped with 10–20% is beneficial to improve the internal friction angle, water loss resistance, fatigue performance, and to a certain extent high temperature stability. 0–4.75 mm ICLR doped with 10–20% is beneficial to improve the mix’s internal friction angle, water loss resistance, fatigue performance, and to a certain extent high temperature stability. 4.75 mm ICLR replaces part of the aggregate, which can make the mix obtain a certain structural strength and improve the frost resistance and skid resistance of the pavement30–33, of which the substitution rate of 10% or less is obvious for the mix low-temperature performance enhancement, and there is an improvement in the water stability performance of the mix34. When all the mineral powder is replaced, the high temperature rutting resistance of asphalt mixtures can be improved without affecting the low temperature cracking resistance and water damage resistance. When 10–20% is added, the mixture can obtain higher internal friction angle and compression modulus, and the high temperature deformation resistance is greatly improved, and good water stability and fatigue properties can be maintained22,25,35.

In summary, existing research on coal liquefaction residues has primarily focused on utilizing DCLR as an asphalt modifier, with limited exploration into its application as a fine aggregate. In contrast, studies on ICLR have commonly employed it as an aggregate through simplified gradation designs, with emphasis placed on macroscopic pavement performance. However, the influence of specific particle sizes and replacement ratios on the asphalt-aggregate adsorption behavior and the resulting mechanical properties of mixtures remains poorly understood, and the underlying mechanisms have not been thoroughly investigated. To address these research gaps and promote the sustainable utilization of coal liquefaction residues in road engineering, this study systematically investigates the effects of equal-volume, same-particle-size replacements of fine aggregates in AC-20 mixtures with DCLR or ICLR. The objective is to elucidate their impact on asphalt-aggregate interfacial interactions and mixture performance, thereby providing theoretical support and practical guidance for future applications.

Objectives

This work presents significant advancements over previous studies by systematically investigating the influence of controlled particle size substitutions of ICLR and DCLR as fine aggregates on asphalt mixture performance. Unlike prior research, which often employed coarse or mixed particle sizes, our study precisely matches the particle size distribution of limestone fine aggregates, enabling a direct comparison of performance impacts. Furthermore, we establish a novel correlation between the adsorption characteristics of coal liquefaction residues and the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures, providing deeper insights into the mechanisms governing high-temperature stability, low-temperature flexibility, and water resistance. These findings offer a scientific basis for optimizing the sustainable utilization of coal liquefaction residues in pavement engineering while addressing gaps in understanding their adsorption behavior and performance relationships.

DCLR is characterized by four main constituent elements: carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S), with carbon and hydrogen being relatively abundant. The high C/H ratio in DCLR suggests a prevalence of aromatic ring structures within its molecular composition. Polar heteroatom structures, including sulfur (S), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O), in DCLR are encapsulated by condensed aromatic compounds through intermolecular forces such as hydrogen bonds, π-H bonds, and π-π bonds. Carbon atoms predominantly exist in the form of benzene rings, methyl groups, and cyclic methylene, while oxygen is primarily present as carbonyl and ester groups, and nitrogen is mainly found in pyrrole structures.

Pore size.

The pore size distribution of 0.6–1.18 mm, 1.18–2.36 mm, and 2.36–4.75 mm particles of ICLR, DCLR, and limestone fine aggregate were examined by using a fully automatic specific surface and pore analyzer of the Japanese BELSORP-max type, as shown in Fig. 6.

Pore size distribution of ICLR and limestone fine aggregate.

Figure 6 provides that the most probable pore diameter of ICLR is larger than that of limestone fine aggregate. Among the three particle sizes of the same material, the pore diameter distributions of ICLR and limestone fine aggregate do not differ much. The pores of both materials are mainly mesopores with a size of 2–50 nm, and the number of micropores smaller than 2 nm and macropores larger than 50 nm is small. The most probable pore diameter of DCLR is larger than that of limestone fine aggregate and also larger than that of ICLR. This indicates that compared with ICLR and limestone fine aggregate, the most developed pore diameter range of DCLR is higher.

Among the three different particle sizes of ICLR, the number and volume of micropore structures do not differ much. The maximum pore volume of the micropore structure is concentrated between 0.000025 and 0.000045 cm3/g·nm. The difference is that the mesopore and macropore structures of the 1.18–2.36 mm and 2.36–4.75 mm particle sizes are higher than those of the 0.6–1.18 mm particle size, and there is an increasing trend overall. This indicates that as the particle size of ICLR increases, the volume and number of its mesopore and macropore structures increase. It also indicates that, relatively speaking, the larger the particle size of ICLR, the greater the amount of asphalt that its pores can accommodate. Comparing the three particle sizes of limestone fine aggregate, it can be seen that the maximum void volume of the micropore structure is between 0.002–0.004 cm3/g·nm. The mesopore and macropore structures of the 0.6–1.18 mm and 1.18–2.36 mm particle sizes are relatively close and both are higher than those of the 2.36–4.95 mm particle size. From a quantitative perspective, the overall pore volume of limestone fine aggregate is larger than that of ICLR.

The pore diameter distributions of the three particle sizes of DCLR are all above 2 nm, indicating that DCLR does not contain a micropore structure and only contains mesopores and macropores. The maximum pore volume of the pore diameter distribution of the three particle sizes of DCLR is similar to that of ICLR, concentrated between 0.000025–0.000045 cm3/g·nm. For the mesopores of 2–50 nm and macropores larger than 50 nm, the pore diameter distributions of the 1.18–2.36 mm and 2.36–4.75 mm particle sizes of DCLR are almost the same, and the corresponding void volumes do not differ much. This indicates that the pore diameter distribution differences of the three particle sizes of DCLR are not obvious. Comparing with the three particle sizes of limestone fine aggregate, it can be seen that from a quantitative perspective, the overall pore volume of limestone fine aggregate is still larger than that of DCLR.

ICLR asphalt mixture.

ICLR asphalt mixture.

Dynamic stability and rutting depth of different asphalt mixtures36.

It can be read from Fig. 6, by the pre-study basis36, overall, single-particle-size substitution of ICLR yields better performance than double-particle-size substitution, which in turn is better than triple-particle-size substitution. The dynamic stability (DS) of the mixture with 1.18–2.36 mm ICLR substitution (Group C) reached 3247 times/mm, which is 123.2% higher than that of the control group (1455 times/mm). The rutting resistance of the mixes with ICLR replacing 0.6–4.75 mm fine aggregate (Group H) is the worst, with a DS of only 1615 times/mm, representing a modest improvement of 11.0% over the control. 2.36–4.75 mm and 1.18–2.36 mm ICLR replacing fine aggregate are favorable to improve the high temperature rutting performance, and 0.6–1.18 mm ICLR has a negative effect on the high-temperature performance has a negative impact, and the preferred way of ICLR substitution for fine aggregates is single-particle -size substitution.

ICLR can improve the high temperature rutting resistance of the mixture is because it contains high SiO2, the content of which is more than 50%, and it is also rich in metal oxides such as Fe, Mg, Al, etc., which has high modulus and strong hardness. And limestone fine aggregate mainly contains CaCO3, hardness grade is low, ICLR to a certain extent than limestone fine aggregate baked oil treasure stronger resistance to deformation.

Figure 18 illustrates that for different ICLR asphalt mixtures, as the asphalt film thickness increases, the asphalt film thickness has a strong negative correlation with TSR and low temperature damage strain, and a weaker correlation with residual stability and dynamic stability.

The dynamic stability, maximum bending strain, residual stability and TSR as a whole show a decreasing trend with the increase of combined particle size and substitution amount as well as with the spiral increase of asphalt film thickness. In terms of high temperature performance, the dynamic stability curve change is basically consistent with the change of asphalt membrane thickness, i.e., dynamic stability increases or decreases correspondingly with the asphalt film thickness, which is due to the asphalt membrane thickness is small, the structural asphalt membrane adhesion to the mineral material is small, and its strength is mainly derived from aggregate cohesion and internal friction, and with the increase in the thickness of asphalt membrane, its adhesion with the mineral material between the gradual increase, and when the asphalt membrane is too thick When the asphalt film is too thick, the mineral particles will be pushed away from each other, the aggregate surface formed a surplus of “free asphalt”, at this time the adhesion of the asphalt slurry will also be reduced, so with the increase of the combined particle size and the amount of substitution, when the thickness of asphalt film is too thick, the high temperature performance of the mixture shows a downward trend.

In terms of low-temperature performance, the maximum bending tensile strain of the eight mixtures decreased with the increase of asphalt film thickness, on the one hand, this may be due to the fact that asphalt in the low-temperature environment, showing hard and brittle, the strength increases, while the deformation capacity decreases, prone to brittle fracture, so the gradual thickening of the asphalt film accelerates the asphalt-aggregate structure as a whole, “brittle fracture”. Therefore, the gradual thickening of the asphalt film will accelerate the “brittle fracture” of the whole asphalt-aggregate structure, leading to cracks in asphalt mixtures at low temperature. On the other hand, perhaps related to the ICLR material properties, with the increase of combined particle size and substitution amount, a large number of ICLR is blended into the mixture, the adhesion between ICLR and asphalt is not as strong as that between asphalt and limestone fine aggregate, and even the embedded extrusion and mechanical occlusion between ICLR and limestone fine aggregate is not as strong as that of the limestone fine aggregate, resulting in the low-temperature cracking of the mixture after ICLR substitution of limestone fine aggregate. The low-temperature cracking resistance of the mix continues to decrease after the substitution of limestone fine aggregate by ICLR.

In terms of water stability performance, the Marshall stability, residual stability and TSR before and after immersion in water generally decrease with the increase of asphalt film thickness, although the residual stability reaches 93.5% when ICLR replaces 2.36/1.18/0.6 mm at the same time, the stability of 5kN can be seen from the above, which does not meet the specification requirements anymore. This is because the increasing thickness of the asphalt film will make the proportion of structural asphalt on the surface of the particles decreased, the increase of free asphalt between the minerals to fill some of the original voids in the mixture, reducing the void ratio of the mixture, although this is to some extent conducive to the improvement of the water stability of the mixture, but the existence of too much free asphalt between the minerals will also make the mechanical properties of the mixture decreased, although the reduction of the void ratio makes the impact of water on its itself, its mechanical properties are weakened, thus ultimately leading to a decrease in the water stability performance.

For ICLR mixtures, asphalt film thickness exhibits a strong negative correlation with TSR and low-temperature failure strain, and a weaker but significant negative correlation with dynamic stability. This underscores that increasing film thickness, primarily resulting from the lower pore volume and adsorption capacity of ICLR compared to limestone (Section “Materials”, 3.3.1), is detrimental to key performance metrics. The link between pore structure and adsorption kinetics is critical. ICLR’s mesoporous structure (2–50 nm, Sect. 3.1.2) facilitates capillary action and surface diffusion, leading to a moderately fast initial adsorption rate. However, its overall lower pore volume limits total asphalt uptake. Consequently, when ICLR replaces limestone, less asphalt is adsorbed into the pores as structural asphalt, leading to an excess of free asphalt that increases the overall film thickness (Sect. 3.3.2). This thicker, weaker film of free asphalt reduces the cohesive strength of the mastic and the mechanical interlock between aggregates, explaining the pronounced negative impact on rutting resistance (decreased dynamic stability) and low-temperature cracking resistance (decreased failure strain). The negative correlation with TSR arises because while reduced void ratio (from free asphalt filling voids) can initially benefit water resistance, the concomitant reduction in mechanical strength and potential for moisture-induced adhesive failure at the thicker, weaker asphalt-aggregate interface ultimately compromises water stability.

DCLR’s pore structure is dominated by mesopores and macropores (> 2 nm, Sect. 3.1.2) and lacks micropores. This structure results in a very fast initial adsorption rate (the highest among the three materials, Sect. 3.3.1) due to rapid surface wetting and filling of larger pores. However, similar to ICLR, its total pore volume is lower than limestone, limiting ultimate adsorption capacity. The critical difference is DCLR’s composition. The molten asphaltenes and heavy oils from DCLR (Sect. 3.2.1) significantly increase the effective asphalt binder content, which is the primary driver of the substantial increase in film thickness (Sect. 3.3.2). This process dominates the film formation mechanism compared to physical adsorption. Therefore, for DCLR, the performance is less about the physical adsorption capacity of its pores and more about the chemical modification of the binder and the kinetics of the initial interaction. The fast adsorption kinetics ensure good initial coating and adhesion, while the melting component dictates the final film thickness and binder properties. The optimal performance observed for single-size substitutions, particularly with smaller particles (0.6–1.18 mm), arises because smaller particles have a higher dissolution rate and specific surface area, leading to more efficient modification and a more homogeneous blend, preventing the agglomeration and incomplete dissolution that plague multi-size substitutions and lead to erratic property changes.

Future research perspectives

While this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the pavement performance and adsorption mechanisms of asphalt mixtures incorporating ICLR and DCLR as fine aggregate substitutes, several promising avenues for future research remain to further advance their sustainable application in road engineering.

Long-Term Field Performance and Durability Monitoring: The current findings are based on laboratory tests which, while accelerated and controlled, cannot fully replicate the complex and long-term effects of real-world environmental conditions (e.g., oxidative aging, ultraviolet radiation, freeze–thaw cycles, and traffic loading). Establishing full-scale test sections and implementing long-term performance monitoring (LTPP) are crucial next steps. This would validate the laboratory results, provide data on long-term durability, service life, and potential maintenance requirements, and build confidence among road authorities.

Multi-Functional Pavement Applications: The unique composition and surface properties of these residues present opportunities beyond structural performance. Research could explore their potential in developing multi-functional pavements. For instance, the high carbon content and porous structure of DCLR might be leveraged for photocatalytic degradation of vehicle emissions or as a component in electrically conductive asphalt for self-healing or snow-melting applications. The dark color and thermal properties could also be studied in the context of urban heat island mitigation.
