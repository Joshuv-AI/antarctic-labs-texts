Low-Molecular-Weight Phenolic Adhesives Tailored for Bonding Flattened Moso Bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis)
Authors: Rihan Hai, Shanghuan Feng, Yamei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Commercial phenol-formaldehyde (PF) adhesives adapted from wood-composite applications are not specifically designed for bamboo, whose relatively high density, low porosity, poor surface wettability, and restricted transverse transport pathways hinder resin spreading and penetration. In this study, four PF adhesives with different molecular weights and viscosities were synthesized by optimizing the reaction temperature and time for bonding flattened Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis). The developed adhesives showed enhanced wettability to bamboo compared with the commercial phenolic resin. The hydroxymethyl groups in the developed adhesives were determined by NMR, FTIR, and wet chemical methods to be present at high levels, demonstrating high reactivity during the hot-pressing process. When applied for laminating bamboo, the developed adhesives performed better than the commercial one from the perspective of penetration depth and bonding performance. Powered by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), the “bamboo–adhesive–bamboo” was clearly mapped, and it was clearly observed that the developed phenolic resins penetrated deeper into bamboo than the commercial one. Also, the developed phenolic adhesives contributed higher tensile shear strength to the laminated bamboo products than the commercial phenolic resin, regardless of dry or wet strength. These findings demonstrate that coordinated regulation of molecular weight and viscosity can improve resin penetration and curing reactivity, providing a bamboo-oriented PF adhesive design strategy for bamboo composites.

Introduction

Bamboo has increasingly been used as an alternative to wood because of the depletion of natural forests and the growing dependence of the forest-products industry on imported timber and plantation forest resources in China. , As a highly sustainable natural resource, bamboo grows rapidly, generally matures within 3–5 years, and possesses high strength, biodegradability, and strong carbon-storage capacity. − Significant efforts have contributed to a wide range of bamboo products, such as plybamboo, bamboo scrimber, − bamboo fiber composites, laminated bamboo lumber (LBL), bamboo-winding composite products, and bamboo-based container flooring. However, bamboo is hollow in the core, and a pronounced density gradient exists across the culm thickness. − Processing bamboo for target products unavoidably refers to machining it into bamboo units and integrating the units into the target products. In most cases, integrating the units into the target products involves the application of adhesives. Different from the wood species commonly used for forest products, bamboo displays higher density, lower porosity, and poor surface wettability, which severely restrict the penetration of the adhesive into bamboo and thus adversely influence bamboo bonding. Moreover, the anatomical pathways available for transverse resin transport in bamboo are relatively limited, making it difficult for conventional adhesive systems to achieve both sufficient penetration and effective retention at the bonding interface. Therefore, the formation of a reliable bamboo–adhesive interface remains a key challenge in the manufacture of engineered bamboo products.

Bamboo bonding can be enhanced via several pathways. Numerous studies have focused on improving the performance of bamboo bonding interfaces via processing and surface modifications. Bamboo flattening and surface grooving to open up and normalize the pores of the bamboo surface can increase the gluing area and material utilization. − Sand blasting modification has been used to obtain bamboo surface roughness and characteristics more suitable for resin penetration by removing bamboo green and yellow. In addition, ultrasonic, plasma, and alkali treatments were employed for surface modification and bamboo bonding properties enhancement. Although these solutions have obtained good treatment results, the preparation process is relatively complex, expensive, and lacks versatility, which makes it hard to achieve considerable economic effects. Furthermore, these methods mainly improve bonding by modifying the bamboo substrate and generally require additional treatment procedures. Therefore, regulating the adhesive itself represents a potentially more direct and adaptable approach for overcoming the restricted wettability and penetration characteristics of bamboo.

Phenolic resin as the first synthetic polymer material has been applied in various fields, including insulation materials, fiber-reinforced composites, and forest products. − Globally, the forest-products industry consumes the biggest volume of phenolic resins. Particularly, because of its well-known advantages in bonding strength, durability, manufacturing techniques, and cost, phenolic resin is widely used and remains difficult to replace in engineered bamboo products, including bamboo scrimber, bamboo-based container flooring boards, coach flooring boards, bamboo-based oriented strand boards, and bamboo-winding composite products. , However, most commercial PF systems are still adapted from wood-composite adhesive systems rather than designed specifically for the anatomical and surface characteristics of bamboo. Therefore, the bamboo-oriented PF resin industry is practically established, but it still faces technical challenges related to resin viscosity, molecular-weight consistency, free formaldehyde control, and stable penetration into bamboo units. One feasible adhesive-side strategy is to control the molecular weight (Mw) and viscosity of PF resins and thereby prepare specialty adhesives tailored to bamboo bonding.

Penetration depth and diffusion degree of phenolic resins in the cell walls of timber or bamboo are highly dependent on the molecular weight of phenolic resins. − Comparatively high molecular weight and high viscosity of phenolic resins lead to unsatisfactory bamboo bonding performance due to the limited penetration of phenolic resins into bamboo, while phenolic resins with low molecular weight excessively penetrate into bamboo cells, which results in waste and bondline starvation. Thus, trace adhesive penetration into bamboo or adhesive filtration into the microstructure in bamboo is essentially important for selecting or even synthesizing adhesives. However, it was not easy to trace the adhesives, especially the penetration depth. Integrating Br into phenolic resins for better observation on the bondline and interaction between phenolic resins and the substrates has been reported. Br-substituted phenolic resins with an Mw of 945 exhibited excellent performance in bonding bamboo scrimber. In a study by Huang, low-Mw phenolic adhesive was applied onto bamboo; subsequently high-Mw phenolic adhesive was applied. The bamboo was laminated by hot pressing. Compared with lamination with one single adhesive, the bonding strength was higher. This demonstrated that molecular-weight distribution of phenolic resins could somehow influence the bonding performance of bamboo.

To address these bamboo-specific interfacial challenges and clarify the effects of molecular weight and viscosity on bamboo–adhesive interaction, several phenolic resins with various Mw values and viscosities were developed and characterized (Figure ). Their basic physicochemical properties, molecular-weight characteristics, wettability, chemical structures, curing behavior, and thermal stability were systematically evaluated. Two-ply plybamboo was prepared using phenolic resin and flattened moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis), and the resin penetration and bonding performance were investigated. To better trace the penetration of phenolic resins into bamboo, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to observe the distribution of cured resin in the bamboo–adhesive–bamboo interfaces.

Preparation of phenolic resin.

Experimental Section

Materials

Flattened Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis) was purchased from a local bamboo company in Jiangxi Province, China. Solid phenol, 37 wt % formaldehyde aqueous solution, and sodium hydroxide were all analytical-grade reagents provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All of the chemicals were used as received without any further purification. Commercial phenol-formaldehyde (CPF) resin was purchased from a resin manufacturer in Guangdong Province, China.

Phenolic Resin Synthesis

Phenolic resins were synthesized using an F/P molar ratio of 1:1.8. During the synthesis, 100 g of phenol was loaded into a 500 mL three-necked reactor, then 73.1 g of 32% NaOH solution was added to the reactor, and the mixture was stirred by a magnetic stirrer at 200 rpm. Subsequently, 155.3 g of 37% formaldehyde solution was slowly added into the reactor. After the addition of formaldehyde, the reactor was slowly heated to 55–60 °C and maintained at this temperature for a period of time, then further heated to 85–90 °C and held at 85–90 °C for 60, 120, 180, and 240 min. Finally, the reactor was cooled to room temperature in 10 min, to yield adhesives denoted as PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4, respectively. All synthesized phenolic resins were collectively designated as SPF resins.

Phenolic Resin Characterizations

Basic Characterizations

The pH value, solid content, viscosity, free formaldehyde content, and hydroxymethyl group content (−CH2OH) of the PF adhesive were tested according to China National Standard GB/T 14074-2017. In solid content measurement, 4.0 g of adhesive was poured into an aluminum dish and then placed in a drying oven at 120 ± 1 °C for 2 h. The solid content was calculated by the weight percentage of nonvolatile in the liquid adhesive. Each solid content was repeated three times and the average values were recorded. Viscosity of the adhesives was determined by a DV-III Ultra rotary viscometer (Brookfield, USA) at 22 °C.

Molecular Weight

Molecular weight of the adhesives was tested via a gel permeation chromatography (GPC, LC20AD, Shimadzu, Japan) system equipped with a Shodex KD-806 M gel column at 35 °C. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) was used as the eluent at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Before GPC tests, the adhesive sample was dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) with a concentration of 5 mg/mL. The dissolved adhesive was shaken until uniform and then it was injected into the GPC column.

Wetting Properties

Adhesives wettability to bamboo was determined by measuring the contact angles of the adhesive to the bamboo surface. The dynamic contact angle was tested with the OCA20 instrument (Dataphysics Ltd., Germany) with a droplet volume of 3 μL. Each contact angle was repeated three times and the average values were reported.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nexus 670, Thermo Electron Corporation, USA) was employed to investigate the chemical structure of the adhesives. Adhesive samples before and after curing at 120 °C for 120 min were scanned at a resolution of 4 cm–1 in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm–1.

1H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (1H NMR)

The liquid 1H NMR spectra of the adhesives were collected using a Bruker AVANCE III 600 MHz spectrometer (1H NMR, Bruker 600M, Germany). Before NMR tests, 10 mg of adhesive sample was dissolved in 0.5 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d 6) and shaken uniformly.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, 3500Sirius, Netzsch, Germany) was applied to test the curing properties of the resins using the multiple heating rate method. Before DSC tests, the PF resin samples were first freeze-dried at −80 °C for 24 h, then vacuum-dried at 35 °C for 24 h. In each DSC test, 5.0 mg of adhesive powder was loaded into an aluminum crucible and heated from 40 to 200 °C under a N2 flow of 50 mL/min. Four heating rates of 5 °C/min, 10 °C/min, 15 °C/min, and 20 °C/min were employed for each adhesive. The kinetic parameters of resin curing were calculated from the Kissinger equation and Crane equation 1 Kis⁢singerequation:dln(β/Tp2)d(1/Tp)=−ER Crane⁢ equation:dlnβd(1/Tp)=−EnR 2 where β is the heating rate (K/min), T p is the peak temperature (K), E is the activation energy (kJ/mol), R is the gas constant, and n is the reaction order. E/R values were directly acquired as the slope of the regression line of the linear plot of ln­(β/T p 2) vs 1000/T p, and n was calculated from the slope of the regression line of ln β vs 1000/T p.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

The thermal stability of the adhesives was analyzed with a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA4000, PerkinElmer, USA). Before the TGA tests, the adhesives were cured at 120 °C for 120 min and then ground into a fine powder. The powder samples were tested in a nitrogen atmosphere with a gas flow rate of 20 mL/min and heated from 30 to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.

Applications of Phenolic Resins for Bamboo Bonding

Preparation of Two-Ply Plybamboo

The bonding performance and applicability of the adhesives were evaluated by laminating flattened Moso bamboo boards with a thickness of 6.0 mm. Each resin was applied to the bamboo surfaces at a spread rate of 180 g/m2. The bamboo was then laminated into two-ply plybamboo under 1.6 MPa at 135 °C for 6 min.

Micromorphology of the Bonding Interface

The micromorphology of the bonding interface was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, GeminiSEM 360, Zeiss, Germany) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Stellaris 5, Leica, Germany). SEM samples were sputtered with a gold coating before SEM scanning. The glueline thickness of resins was measured by ImageJ software, and the images were recorded digitally.

The CLSM was used to detect the penetration and distribution of phenolic resins at the bonding interface after hot press. For the CLSM samples, transverse sections with a thickness of 15 μm were prepared using a sliding slicer and stained with 0.5% toluidine blue solution for 3 h. The imaging was performed at the 488 nm excitation wavelength, and 20× images were used for penetration depth measurements. The average penetration depth (AP) was selected as the quantitative evaluation index for the penetration performance of phenolic resins into bamboo. The AP of resins was measured by ImageJ software, and the images were recorded digitally. Specifically, the CLSM image was measured with 10 individual maximum penetration depths on both sides of the glueline. Twenty penetration depth data were obtained, and the average value was reported.

Bonding Performance

The two-ply plybamboo boards were cut into specimens with the size of 100 (length) × 25 (width) mm and then slotted with an overlapping area of 25 × 25 mm. After conditioning at 20 °C and 65 RH% for 72 h, the laminated bamboo was tested with a universal testing machine (Instron 5582, USA) for the bonding strength via tensile shear tests at a loading speed of 3 mm/min. While for wet strength tests, the specimens were boiled in water for 180 min. After cooling, the specimens were mechanically tested. Dry and wet strength tests on plybamboo bonded with each type of phenolic resin were separately measured for at least 20 specimens.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to analyze the collected data using the software SPSS (Version 27.0, IBM, New York, USA) based on the normality and homogeneity values of variances. The least significant difference (LSD) and Duncan’s test were applied after rejecting the null hypothesis. Differences between means were regarded as statistically significant when the confidence interval was >95% (p < 0.05).

Results and Discussion

Primary Characteristics

The primary characteristics of the resins are as shown in Figure a–c. pH values of the SPF adhesives vary in 10.87–11.13, higher than that of the CPF adhesive (pH 9.64). The solid content of the SPF resins was 46.82–47.80%. The viscosity of the PF resins ranged in 32.0–126.9 cPs at room temperature, which was lower than the CPF resin viscosity value of 154.8 cP. The hydroxymethyl group content of the PF resin is another important parameter. It indicates the degree of reaction polymerization and cross-linking. Figure d shows that as the reaction continued, the hydroxymethyl group content of PF-1 to PF-4 gradually decreased, with the maximum and minimum values reaching 35.64% and 27.13%, respectively. The free formaldehyde content range was 0.39%, while for PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4, no free formaldehyde was detected.

Molecular Weight and Distribution

Molecular weight and distribution were analyzed by GPC. As illustrated in Figure e, the weight-average molecular weight (M w) of PF-1 was 882, and the number-average molecular weight (M n) was 783. The M w of PF-4 was 1759, and M n was 1573. As the synthesis proceeded, both M n and M w of the resin increased. This resulted from the continuous addition of formaldehyde into phenol or phenol derivatives (hydroxylated phenol) and condensation of phenolic prepolymer or phenol. Moreover, the polydispersity index (PDI) of all the SPF resins ranges 1.12–1.22 and was less than the polydispersity index (PDI = 1.38) of the CPF resin, indicating that the molecular weights of the SPF resins were more homogeneous. −

Wettability

Figure f shows the adhesive contact angle on the bamboo surface over time. Smaller contact angle suggests better surface wettability. , The contact angle of PF-1 at 0 s was 121.6°, while at 20 s, the contact angle decreased to 18.2°. The adhesive droplet contact angle gradually decreased over time, showing increased wettability. With the increased molecular weight, the contact angle of the PF adhesive at 0 and 20 s increased. While PF-2 and PF-3 displayed the contact angles of 33.8° and 36.2° at 20 s, respectively, PF-4 displayed dramatically higher contact angle of 49.3° at 20 s. The CPF adhesive demonstrated a higher contact angle of 62.5° at 20 s. All SPF adhesives showed lower contact angles than CPF adhesives, which is directly associated with the molecular weight and viscosity. This exhibited superior wettability and facilitated the penetration of the resin into bamboo.

At 300–600 °C, the DTG curves of PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4 almost overlapped, indicating comparable degradation rates for all SPF adhesives. The second and third DTG of all SPF adhesives peak at around 0.04%/°C and 0.12%/°C, respectively. The degradation rates tended to slow down and then accelerate. The degradation in this temperature range is caused by breakage of the methylene bridges, loss of water, volatiles, and oligomers, and then methylene scission. Above 700 °C, the degradation of all SPF adhesives is significantly accelerated, due to the disruption of the adhesive network structure and adhesive carbonization. The residual weights of PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4 adhesives at 800 °C are 60.4%, 61.5%, 63.8%, and 60.4%, respectively, which are higher than that of the CPF adhesive.

Chemical structure

FTIR Spectra

Figure a displays the FTIR spectra of synthetic and commercial PF adhesives before curing. Transmittance peak at 3630 cm–1 was attributed to the typical phenolic −OH stretching and vibration. The infrared fingerprint areas of adhesives before curing over the range of 1800–600 cm–1 are shown in Figure b. The peaks at 1665 cm–1 and 1604 cm–1 are the result of CC stretching vibrations of the aromatic structure. Due to the presence of methylene (−CH2−), IR transmittance in the range of 1480–1450 cm–1 the spectrum of SPF and CPF adhesives appeared. This confirms that the condensation reactions occurred between phenol and hydroxymethylated phenol or between two hydroxymethylated phenols during the resin synthesis. Compared with the CPF, all SPFs exhibit stronger transmittance peaks at 1480–1450 cm–1, which may be due to the greater cross-linking density produced by the condensation reaction. The broad absorption band around 1264 cm–1 corresponded to the C–O–C stretching vibration, and C–O–C was formed by a reaction between two hydroxymethyl (−CH2OH) phenols. Moreover, the characteristic peak at 1020 cm–1 can be assigned to the hydroxymethyl groups (−CH2OH), which is convincing evidence of the indispensable hydroxymethylation reaction during the resin synthesis. However, CPF shows a strong absorption peak at 1020 cm–1, indicating a high −CH2OH content. This is consistent with the data collected by the wet chemical method (Figure d).

FTIR spectra of SPF and CPF adhesives (a, b) before and (c, d) after curing. (e) 1H NMR spectra of SPF and CPF adhesives.

The IR spectra of the cured adhesives are shown in Figure c,d. The C–O–C stretching vibration of the broad absorption band at 1264 cm–1 was dramatically decreased, which was associated with the massive scission of the ether bonds (C–O–C) losing formaldehyde to methylene (−CH2−). At the same time, the characteristic peak at 1020 cm–1 for −CH2OH almost vanished. This confirms that the hydroxymethyl has condensed during the curing process, forming a more robust cross-linking network structure. Furthermore, the typical peaks at 1665 cm–1 and 1593 cm–1 are attributed to CC stretching vibrations of the aromatic structure.

NMR Spectra

Figure e shows the liquid 1H NMR spectra of the adhesives. Aromatic protons were observed at 6.30–7.25 ppm in all adhesives. Strong proton signals at 3.85–4.60 ppm indicated the presence and abundance of methylated phenols in the adhesives. The proton signals around 4.43 ppm were attributed to the hydroxymethyl groups (−CH2OH) on the ortho position of phenolic hydroxyl groups, and the proton signals around 4.19 ppm can be assigned to the −CH2OH on the para position of phenolic hydroxyl groups. The chemical shifts at 3.42–3.78 ppm were caused by the methylene (−CH2−) bridge connecting two benzene rings, including three types of methylene bridges, namely, ortho–ortho, ortho–para, and para–para methylene bridges. PF-1 to PF-4 exhibit strong proton signals at 4.43 and 4.19 ppm, with the intensity gradually decreasing, indicating a high content of hydroxymethyl groups that decreases as the polymerization reaction proceeds. The high content of hydroxymethyl groups in the adhesive ensures a high reactivity and cross-linking density during the hot-pressing process.

Performance of the Adhesives in Bonding Bamboo

Micromorphological Properties of the Bonding Interface

SEM was used to characterize the glueline in the laminated bamboo. As shown in Figure , the glueline bamboo was thin and adhesive somehow penetrated into the bamboo (Figure a). The bamboo is innately devoid of horizontal organization (rays), and the adhesive can only penetrate into the bamboo cell walls and cell lumens through the pit channels (Figure b,c). , As shown in Figure d–i, most SPF and CPF adhesives retained in the glueline and some penetrated into the cell lumen. The glueline thicknesses of PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, PF-4, and CPF were 26 μm, 31 μm, 34 μm, 39 μm, and 33 μm, respectively. With the increase in molecular weight and viscosity of the adhesive, the spreading and flowability of the resin in the bamboo decreased, and the glueline became gradually thicker.

(a) Cross section of glued bamboo. (b, c) SEM images of bonded bamboo cell walls. (d–i) SEM images of the bonding interface of SPF and CPF.

To further characterize the glueline and penetration characteristics of the adhesives into bamboo, CLSM was applied, and CLSM demonstrated powerful efficiency and generated clear “bamboo–adhesive–bamboo” interface images in this study (Figure ). After pressing at 135 °C for 6 min, the adhesive penetration depth into bamboo was highly dependent on the adhesive properties. Compared to the CPF resin, the SPF resin showed dramatically higher penetration depth. The average penetration depths (AP) of PF-2 and PF-3 into bamboo reached 189 and 200 μm, respectively, which were 3.7 and 3.9 times that of CPF. The low viscosity, low molecular weight, as well as improved wettability of the SPF resins enhanced its fluidity and facilitated their penetration into bamboo. As a result of increased molecular weight and viscosity of SPF, the penetration depth of adhesives into bamboo decreased. With the lowest molecular weight and viscosity, the PF-1 resin may partially flow out from both sides of the bamboo during hot pressing, resulting in thin bondlines. In addition, as the molecular weight of the SPF resin increases, the large resin molecules are unable to pass through the pits in the bamboo cell wall, more resin accumulates near the bondline, and the bondline becomes gradually thickened. These results also indicate that the penetration depth should be optimized rather than maximized. PF-3 showed the highest AP, whereas PF-2 presented the best bonding strength (Figure b,c), suggesting that adequate resin retention in the bondline and sufficient penetration into bamboo must be balanced.

Bonding Performance in Bamboo Lamination

Figure shows the dry and wet tensile shear strengths of the laminated bamboo. Dry shear strengths of PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4 were 9.30, 9.90, 9.23, and 9.88 MPa, respectively, all higher than that of CPF at 8.92 MPa. SPF penetrated effectively into the bamboo cell walls and interacted with each other, forming micromechanical interlocking. Consistent with the penetration results, the PF-2 resin demonstrated the maximum bonding strength in laminating bamboo, reaching 9.90 MPa. It seems that from the perspectives of penetration and bonding capability, PF-2 with a viscosity of 47.5 cP (room temperature) and an Mw of 1013 worked best. The tensile shear strength was also statistically evaluated by the ANOVA test at a level of confidence of 95%. As Figure b,c illustrates, the dry shear strength of bamboo products laminated with PF-2 and PF-4 adhesives was significantly higher than that laminated with CPF adhesive. The wet shear strengths of the bamboo specimens laminated with PF-1, PF-2, PF-3, and PF-4 were 7.20, 7.98, 7.53, and 7.94 MPa, respectively. The wet shear strengths of PF-2 and PF-4 adhesives were significantly higher than that of PF-1 or CPF adhesives. Furthermore, Figure d displays obvious bamboo failure of the laminated bamboo. After the mechanical tests, failure occurred within the bamboo matrix rather than at the glueline itself. This suggests that the strength of the glueline is higher than the bamboo strength. It can be also concluded here that by controlling the molecular weight and viscosity, adhesives performance in bonding bamboo can be enhanced. The strength improvement should be interpreted together with the failure mode. Although the dry shear strength increased by about 11.0%, the wet shear strength of PF-2 was 15.3% higher than that of CPF, and SPF-bonded specimens showed 100% bamboo failure. Once the bonding interface becomes stronger than the bamboo substrate, the measured shear strength is partly limited by the intrinsic strength of bamboo. Therefore, the improved wet strength and higher bamboo failure ratio are important indicators of interfacial reliability.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed four phenolic adhesives for bamboo bonding with low molecular weight, controllable viscosity, excellent wettability, and high strength through optimizing reaction conditions. Hydroxymethyl content tests and 1H NMR results indicated that the developed adhesive contained a high content of −CH2OH groups, demonstrating high reactivity during the hot-pressing process. When applied for laminating bamboo, the developed adhesives performed better than the commercial ones from the perspective of penetration depth and bonding performance. Powered by CLSM, the “bamboo–adhesive–bamboo” was clearly mapped, and it was clearly observed that the developed phenolic resins penetrated deeper into bamboo than the commercial ones. Moreover, the developed phenolic adhesives provided higher tensile shear strength for laminated bamboo, with dry bonding strength (9.90 MPa) and wet bonding strength (7.98 MPa) exceeding commercial phenolic adhesives by 11.0% and 15.3%, respectively. Optimal bonding performance requires achieving the best balance between molecular weight, viscosity, penetration, and curing reactivity. The developed adhesives demonstrated high applicability in bonding bamboo products and holds great potential for industrial applications.
