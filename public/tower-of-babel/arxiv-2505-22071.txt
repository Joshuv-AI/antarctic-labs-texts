Ocean-E2E: Hybrid Physics-Based and Data-Driven Global Forecasting of Extreme Marine Heatwaves with End-to-End Neural Assimilation
Ruiqi Shu, Yuan Gao, Hao Wu, Ruijian Gou, Yanfei Xiang, Fan Xu, Qingsong Wen, Xian Wu, Xiaomeng Huang

Abstract
This work focuses on the end-to-end forecast of global extreme marine heatwaves (MHWs), which are unusually warm sea surface temperature events with profound impacts on marine ecosystems. Accurate prediction of extreme MHWs has significant scientific and financial worth. However, existing methods still have certain limitations, especially in the most extreme MHWs. In this study, to address these issues, based on the physical nature of MHWs, we created a novel hybrid data-driven and numerical MHWs forecast framework Ocean-E2E, which is capable of 40-day accurate MHW forecasting with end-to-end data assimilation. Our framework significantly improves the forecast ability of extreme MHWs by explicitly modeling the effect of oceanic mesoscale advection and air-sea interaction based on a differentiable dynamic kernel. Furthermore, Ocean-E2E is capable of end-to-end MHWs forecast and regional high-resolution prediction using neural data assimilation approaches, allowing our framework to operate completely independently of numerical models while demonstrating high assimilation stability and accuracy, outperforming the current state-of-the-art ocean numerical forecasting-assimilation models. Experimental results show that the proposed framework performs excellently on global-to-regional scales and short-to-long-term forecasts, especially in those most extreme MHWs. Overall, our model provides a framework for forecasting and understanding MHWs and other climate extremes. Our codes are available at https://github.com/ChiyodaMomo01/Ocean-E2E .

Introduction

Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are abnormally warm seawater events that significantly damage marine ecosystems
oliver2021marine
;
pearce2011marine
. In other words, MHWs are extreme
Sea Surface Temperature Anomaly (SSTA)
events. MHWs, particularly extreme ones, can cause coral bleaching
hughes2017global
;
hughes2018spatial
and widespread mortality of marine organisms
garrabou2009mass
;
thomson2015extreme
. As a result, precise forecasting of extreme MHWs has significant scientific and economic implications. For example, synoptic scale MHWs forecasting can help sea food production and management planning, such as feed cycles, at 1-7 day timescales, while subseasonal to seasonal forecasting can further support proactive decision-making for the blue economy
hobday2016seasonal
;
malick2020environmentally
;
mills2017forecasting
;
payne2022skilful
. In this study, we will mainly focus on the subseasonal-to-seasonal forecast (i.e., 1-40 day).

Traditional MHW forecasting predominantly relies on numerical models solving oceanic primitive equations, which generally bifurcate into two categories:
seasonal
forecasts characterizing large-scale patterns of MHW onset and intensification
jacox2022global
;
brodie2023ecological
, and
sub-seasonal
predictions resolving finer-scale variability with reduced lead times
benthuysen2021subseasonal
;
yu2024assessing
. While these physics-based approaches achieve operational utility, they demand substantial computational resources
xiong2023ai
;
wang2024xihe
and exhibit limited skill in predicting extreme MHW events
jacox2022global
. Recent advances in deep learning have demonstrated potential for efficient global ocean forecasting
bi2022pangu
;
chen2023fengwu
;
kurth2023fourcastnet
, with emerging applications to MHW prediction
lin2023rossby
;
sun2023artificial
. However, existing implementations face dual limitations: 1) insufficient encoding of
MHW-specific physics
—particularly
air-sea coupling mechanisms
driving extreme events and
mesoscale eddy dynamics
governing local intensification—resulting in non-physical smoothing of SST gradients during autoregressive prediction
giamalaki2022assessing
; 2)
dependence on numerical model initialization
that prevent operational end-to-end forecasting from raw observational inputs
xiong2023ai
. These architectural constraints—shared across mainstream deep learning paradigms—fundamentally limit their capacity to preserve
extreme SST anomalies
and
long-term physical consistency
, as error accumulation from neural networks and initialization biases during long-term rollouts
wang2024xihe
.

By addressing the two shortcomings of the data-driven methods mentioned above, in this study, we designed Ocean-E2E, a global data-driven framework that significantly improves the forecasts of extreme MHWs inspired by numerical models. The contribution of this paper can be summarized as follows: (1)
Global MHWs Forecasting Framework
. We propose a hybrid physics-based data-driven method that supports both global scale and regional high-resolution forecasting, achieving high-accuracy results for extreme MHWs forecasts. (2)
Global MHWs Neural Assmilation Framework
. We designed an end-to-end MHWs neural system that, when combined with the forecasting model, can directly obtain the complete initial field and forecasting results of MHWs from observation data. (3)
Long-Term Forecast
. By combining phyiscal laws into our framework, our model has achieved state-of-the-art results in long-term (subseasonal-to-seasonal) forecasting with higher physical consistency.

The method most similar to us is ClimODE
verma2024climode
. Similarly, it also uses advection partial differential equations to design the forecasting framework. However, our work has many differences and improvements compared to ClimODE: (1)
Differences in theoretical framework.
From the perspective of the theoretical framework, in their work, the ‘speed’ of the flow field is essentially an abstract mathematical definition, whose core function is to maintain the conservation of passive tracers. In our work, the flow field speed strictly corresponds to the realistic ocean flow field and models the additional transport effect of the flow field on passive tracers at sub-grid scales through mesoscale ‘bolus’ velocity. (2)
Differences in model design.
ClimODE uses an emission model to simulate the unclosed and uncertain portions of the real system, while our model further relates this part to the ocean-atmosphere interaction and is designed based on physical theory to form the structure of the source term. (3)
Differences in application.
ClimODE requires solving the initial velocity field as the initial condition for the model, which involves solving a physics-informed neural network (PINNs) for each training sample. This makes the ClimODE framework difficult to scale up. What’s more, our experiments (Section
4.3
) show that our method achieves greater stability and accuracy in MHWs forecasting compared to ClimODE.

Problem Setup

In this study, the problem of the end-to-end MHWs forecast is
essentially forecasting SSTA
, which can be separated into two parts. First, estimating the current state of SSTA
T_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
(a global two-dimensional field, where
H
and
W
correspond to latitude and longitude) based on the observation
T_{0}^{obs}
and the background field
T_{0}^{bg}
. Second, predicting the future state of mixed layer SSTA
T_{t}
based on the current state
T_{0}
under atmospheric forcing
A_{0:t}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times H\times W}
. Here the underscore
0:t
denotes the atmosphere state from time
0
to
t
. Formally speaking, we are going to model the conditional distribution
p(T_{t}|T_{0}^{obs},T_{0}^{bg},A_{0:t},)
in two stages:

p(T_{t}|T_{0}^{obs},T_{0}^{bg},A_{0:t})=\underbrace{p^{f}(T_{t}|T_{0},A_{0:t})}_{\text{Forecast model}}\cdot\underbrace{p^{a}(T_{0}|T_{0}^{obs},T_{0}^{bg})}_{\text{Assimilation model}}

(1)

where
p^{f}
and
p^{a}
denote forecast and assimilation process, respectively. In our end-to-end framework, we model these two processes through two specifically designed neural network
\phi^{f}_{\theta}
and
\phi^{a}_{\theta}
:

T_{t}=\phi^{f}_{\theta}(T_{0},A_{0:t})

(2)

T_{0}=\phi^{a}_{\theta}(T_{0}^{obs},T_{0}^{bg})

(3)

In summary, our end-to-end framework aims to forecast the furture state of MHWs
T_{t}
based on the sparse observation
T_{0}^{obs}
and the initial background field
T_{0}^{bg}
under the forcing of atmosphere boundary conditions
A_{0:t}
. The parameters of these two neural networks
\theta_{f},\theta_{a}
are optimized separately by minizing the mean square error (MSE) between the model’s output and the groundtruth:

\theta_{f}={\rm argmin}\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}(T_{t}^{(i)}-\phi^{f}_{\theta_{f}}(T_{0}^{(i)},A_{0:t}^{(i)}))^{2}\}

(4)

\theta_{a}={\rm argmin}\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}(T_{t}^{(i)}-\phi^{a}_{\theta_{a}}(T_{0}^{(i)},A_{0:t}^{(i)}))^{2}\}

(5)

3
Methods

3.1
Motivation and Framework Overview

Our framework is summarized in Figure
1
. As established in the problem formulation, forecasting MHWs fundamentally reduces to predicting extreme sea surface temperature anomalies (SSTA). To effectively embed physical principles into our modeling framework, we must rigorously account for the governing laws of SSTA evolution. We approach this challenge through a generalized geophysical fluid dynamics perspective, wherein seawater temperature constitutes a
passive tracer

C
advected by oceanic flow fields. As shown in Figure
2
, the spatiotemporal evolution of
C
is governed by two primary mechanisms: 1)
advective transport
by background currents that redistribute thermal energy, 2)
mixing and external sink/source
encompassing convective mixing and external forcing through surface boundary interactions. Formally speaking, the governing equation of
C
can be written as:

\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}+{\bf u}\nabla C+w\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}=\dot{\rm S}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla C)

(6)

Where
t
is time,
({\bf u},w)
are horizontal and vertical flow velocity,
\nabla
is the horizontal derivation,
\dot{\rm S}
is the sink/source term and
\kappa
is a
2\times 2
matrix corresponding to horizontal anistropic diffusion coeffiecient
smith2004anisotropic
. By integrating the above equation, the state of
C
at time
t
can be expressed in the form of the initial condition
C_{0}
:

C_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}-{\bf u}\nabla C+(-w\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}+\dot{\rm S}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla C))dt+C_{0}

(7)

Figure 2:
Governing Mechanisms of MHWs

Where
-{\bf u}\nabla C
represents the advective transport while
-w\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}+\dot{\rm S}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla C)
corresponds to mixing and external sink/source. Next, we will combine physical theory and deep learning methods to simulate these two mechanisms respectively.

Advective transport
. The key factor of advective transport is the ocean surface current
{\bf u}
, where the flow field at the smaller scale (mesoscale) plays an important role in the distribution of MHWs and other oceanic passive tracers. However, in most models and data, due to limited resolution, the model cannot distinguish these small-scale processes, so the advection processes caused by the flow field below the model’s finest grid (i.e., subgrid processes):

\overline{{\bf u}\nabla C}=\overline{{\bf u}}\overline{\nabla C}+\overline{{\bf u}^{\prime}{\nabla C}^{\prime}}

(8)

are not fully simulated. Here the overline
\overline{\cdot}
represents the downsampling operator. In other words, the variables with overline denote the low-resolution data. In the traditional numerical model, Gent and McWilliams
gent1990isopycnal
found that this subgrid process can be fitted by ‘bolus velocity’ (also called GM90 parameterization):

\overline{{\bf u}^{\prime}{\nabla C}^{\prime}}\sim{\bf u}_{gm}\overline{\nabla C}

(9)

According to the GM90 parameterization, these bolus velocity can be expressed as
gent1990isopycnal
:

{\bf u}_{gm}=(\kappa\nabla\rho/\rho_{z})_{z}

(10)

where
\rho=\rho(T)
is the density. It should be noticed that this expression requires subsurface ocean data, which is often extremely difficult to obtain. What’s more, the choice of coefficient
\kappa
requires empirical formula which is always inaccurate. Alternatively, we seek for a neural network
{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u},C)={\bf u}_{gm}
to approximate the bolus velocity and the subgrid advection effect. In summary, the advection term can be expressed as:

{\bf u}\nabla C=({\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u},C)+{\bf u})\nabla C

(11)

Mixing and external sink/source
. This part consists of three components: verical mixing, external source, and dissipation. Vertical mixing and dissipation depend more on the instrinic variations of the ocean while the external source
\dot{\rm S}
is strongly related to the air-sea interaction along the ocean’s surface. To be specific, for SSTA, the source term consists of four parts:

\dot{\rm S}\propto Q_{{\rm net}}^{\prime}=Q_{{\rm SW}}^{\prime}+Q_{{\rm LW}}^{\prime}+Q_{{\rm sh}}^{\prime}+Q_{{\rm lh}}^{\prime}

(12)

where
Q_{{\rm SW}},Q_{{\rm LW}},Q_{{\rm sens}},Q_{{\rm lat}}
is the shortwave radiation, longwave thermal radiation, sensible heat flux, and latent heat flux respectively. Of the four air-sea heat fluxes, the sensible and latent flux
Q_{{\rm sens}}^{\prime}+Q_{{\rm lat}}^{\prime}
determines the majority of the SSTA’s variations
holbrook2019global
. According to the bulk formula, these two terms are significantly related to surface wind speed
U_{10},V_{10}
, near-surface temperature of the atmosphere
T_{2m}
, and surface specific humidity
q_{a}
. Based on the observation above, we assume that mixing and external sink/source can be well approximated by the surface variables of the atmosphere
{\bf A}=(U_{10},V_{10},T_{2m},q_{a})
and
C
itself. Specifically,

\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}({\bf A},C)=-w\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}+\dot{\rm S}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla C)

(13)

In summary, the disscusions above illustrate that the governing processes of SSTA can be efficiently modelled by neural networks. In the following part, we will demonstrate how to incorporate this idea into a hybrid physics-AI model.
Specifically, for the bolus velocity approximation
{\bf u}_{\theta}
, we employ a U-Net like architecture. The core of this network, particularly in its deeper layers or bottleneck, utilizes
GABlock
s (composed of
GASubBlock
units which integrate
SpatialAttention
and
Mlp
modules) to capture multi-scale spatial dependencies from the input ocean current
{\bf u}
and SSTA
C
. This structure allows the model to learn complex, non-local relationships crucial for representing subgrid advection effects.
For the mixing and external sink/source term
\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}
, we utilize a network primarily built upon a stack of
GABlock
s. This network takes atmospheric variables
{\bf A}
and the current SSTA
C
as input, enabling it to effectively model the intricate air-sea interactions and other relevant source/sink processes.
The
GABlock
, with its
SpatialAttention
mechanism (itself using depth-wise and dilated convolutions as seen in
AttentionModule
) and
Mlp
(Multilayer Perceptron with depth-wise convolution,
DWConv
), is designed to efficiently process and integrate these diverse spatio-temporal inputs for both
{\bf u}_{\theta}
and
\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}
.
The details structure of
{\bf u}_{\theta}
and
\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}
can be found in Appendix D.

3.2
Hybrid physics-AI forecast model

As shown in the section above, the furture state of
C
at time
t
can be modelled as:

C_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}-({\bf u}(t)+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}(t),C)\nabla C+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}({\bf A}(t),C)dt+C_{0}

(14)

This indicates that if we are given the values of the ocean currents and atmospheric boundary conditions between the time interval
[0,t]
, we can predict
C_{t}
in a hybrid physics-AI way.

In our study, the ocean current is obtained from surface geostrophic current inferred from satellite observations. The temporal evolution of this geostrophic current is mainly driven by the ocean instrinic mesoscale dynamics, which can be rigorously described by the 1.5-layer nonlinear RGO model
capotondi2001rossby
. In other words, the evolution of
{\bf u}
can be modelled based on an auto-regressive manner:

\frac{\partial{\bf u}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{M}_{\theta}({\bf u})

(15)

What’s more, the atmosphere variables between time interval
[0,t]
is also acquired through a large AI-driven weather forecast model
gao2025oneforecast
:

\frac{\partial{\bf A}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}({\bf A})

(16)

Here
\mathcal{M}_{\theta},\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
are two pretrained neural networks. Details of these neural networks can be found in Appendix D. In all, the forecast model can be expressed as:

C_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}-\Bigg[\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}({\bf u})ds+{\bf u}_{\theta}(\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}({\bf u})ds,C)\Bigg]\nabla C+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}({\bf A})ds,C)ds

(17)

In the training stage, we first pretrain these two neural networks
\mathcal{M}_{\theta},\mathcal{N_{\theta}}
. Then the parameters of these networks are frozen and we utilize the forecast model to optimize the parameters of
{\bf u}_{\theta},\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}
based on the MSE loss, which can be written as:

\mathcal{L}=||\hat{C_{t}}-\int_{0}^{t}-({\bf u}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u},C))\nabla C+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A,C)dt||^{2}

(18)

Where
\hat{C_{t}}
is the groundtruth data of
C_{t}
. In practice, by separating the time interval
[0,t]
into
N
subintervals
[i\Delta t,(i+1)\Delta t],1\leq i\leq N-1
, equation (
18
) is approximated via the second-order Runge-Kutta method:

\displaystyle\tiny C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star}

\displaystyle=C_{i\Delta t}+\left[-\left({\bf u}_{i\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{i\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right)\nabla C_{i\Delta t}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{i\Delta t:(i+1)\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right]\Delta t

(19)

\displaystyle C_{(i+1)\Delta t}

\displaystyle=C_{i\Delta t}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\{\left[-\left({\bf u}_{i\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{i\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right)\nabla C_{i\Delta t}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{i\Delta t:(i+1)\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right]\Delta t

\displaystyle\quad+\left[-\left({\bf u}_{(i+1)\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{(i+1)\Delta t},C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star})\right)\nabla C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{(i+1)\Delta t:(i+2)\Delta t},C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star})\right]\Bigg\}

(20)

3.3
End-to-end Neural Data Assimilation

Under realistic operational forecasting scenarios, the full initial ocean state
T_{0}
(analysis field) cannot be directly observed. This necessitates a data assimilation (DA) process that optimally combines sparse observational data
T_{0}^{obs}
with prior background estimates
T_{0}^{bg}
from numerical models. Traditional DA methodologies require large computational resources, which imposes an inherent trade off between solution accuracy and operational feasibility
boudier2023data
.

Our framework addresses these limitations by establishing a direct mapping from observational inputs
T_{0}^{obs}
to assimilated states
T_{0}
through deep learning. Instead of solving complex Bayesian inverse problems, we train neural networks to optimally combine sparse observations with background fields in one step. Specifically, we begin with the SSTA state at time
-t
(
T_{-t}
), defining
[-t,0]
as the assimilation window. We generate background fields (
T_{0}^{bg}
) by adding structured noise to
T_{-t}
:

T_{0}^{bg}=\phi^{f}_{\theta}(T_{-t}+\varepsilon,A_{0:t})

(21)

where
\varepsilon
denotes Perlin noises. The final analysis field is then produced by merging observations with background data:

T_{0}=\phi^{a}_{\theta}(T_{0}^{obs},T_{0}^{bg})

(22)

Where
\phi^{a}_{\theta}
is an edge-reparameterization and attention-guided network with Kirsch-guided reparameterization, which is capable of reconstructing SSTA fields from sparse observations
liu2024real
. The details of
\phi^{a}_{\theta}
can be found in Appendix D.

Before introducing our experiments, there are two key concepts that should be clarified:
ocean simulation
and
end-to-end ocean forecast
. As pointed out in previous studies
shu2025advanced
;
cui2025forecasting
, the ocean simulation utilizes the current state of SSTA
T_{0}
to forecast future state
T_{t}
under the realistic atmosphere forcing
{\bf A}_{0:t}
. In contrast, the end-to-end forecast task includes assimilating the initial analysis field of SSTA
T_{0}
and forecasting only based on the current atmosphere state
{\bf A}_{0}
.

We compare the simulation performance of NeuralOM with 3 data-driven models (Fourcastnet
kurth2023fourcastnet
, CirT
liu2025cirt
, and
Wenhai
cui2025forecasting
). Fourcastnet is an atmosphere backbone model with robust performance, CirT is a SOTA subseasonal-to-seasonal atmosphere prediction model, and Wenhai is the superior data-driven ocean model that outperforms traditional numerical models and data-driven models.

We also compare the end-to-end operational forecast ability against traditional numerical operational forecast system: ECWMF sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction (S2S). S2S is current superior subseasonal-to-seasonal weather forecast model that provides the forecast of atmosphere and ocean surface physics at a lead time of 40 days. The original resolution is 1.5° and we upsample the data to 1/2° for comparison.

What’s more, we utilize assimilation bias (BIAS) to assess the systematic bias induced by our neural assimilation system. More details can be found in Appendix E.

4.3
Evalution of ocean simulation performance

As shown in
1
, it can be found that in long-term ocean simulations (40-60 days), our framework performs better compared to the baseline. On day 60, the RMSE decreased by an average of 11.6
\%
and the CSI increased by an average of 9.2
\%
. In shorter simulations (20 days), our model was slightly lower than WenHai, but significantly higher than other baselines. We believe this is because WenHai used raw high-resolution (1/12°) data for training, thus capturing more small-scale local details, maintaining relatively high accuracy on shorter time scales. In contrast, our model uses downsampled data (1/2°), which limits our ability to capture small-scale features. To further illustrate our simulation ability, we select a snapshot of our 60 day simulation, as shown in Figure
3
. It can be found that a strong MHW is growing in the West Pacifc Ocean (red solid box in Figure
3
). Comparing to the ground truth and our framework, WenHai significantly overestimates the magnitude of this MHW while ClimODE severely underestimates the MHW. What is more, WenHai and ClimODE generate many physical inconsistencies after long-term simulation. For example, in the red dashed box of WenHai’s forecast, some abnormally warm water can be observed in the tropical ocean, which is unrealistic. In contrast, although our Ocean-E2E encounters a certain degree of oversmoothing, our framework still shows a better consistency with ground truth.

4.4
Evaluation of the end-to-end neural assimilation and forecast performance

We conducted two sets of assimilation experiments using analysis fields from January 1, 2020 and January 1, 2021 as initial conditions, respectively. Both experiments employed a 6-day assimilation window to assimilate SSTA fields over a full year. As shown in Figure
4
, the RMSE and BIAS evolution reveal that assimilation errors rapidly increase and stabilize during the assimilation process, demonstrating the stability of our assimilation framework. Notably, a persistent negative BIAS exists in our framework, indicating that our assimilation model systematically underestimates the intensity of marine heatwaves compared to GLORYS12V1 reanalysis data, as also evidenced in Figure
4
a.

Using these assimilated fields as initial conditions and GLORYS12V1 reanalysis as ground truth, we evaluated the forecasting performance of Ocean-E2E against the S2S system. To ensure experimental fairness, results in Table

In all experimental configurations, Ocean-E2E demonstrates superior performance relative to S2S, confirming its robust competitiveness.

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we perform an ablation analysis by sequentially removing two core components: the advection term

5
Conclusion

In this study, we present Ocean-E2E, a novel hybrid framework integrating data-driven and numerical approaches for end-to-end marine heatwave (MHW) forecasting.
By explicitly resolving oceanic mesoscale advection and air-sea interactions through a differentiable dynamic kernel, the framework achieves stable 40-day global-to-regional MHW predictions.
Notably, in 60-day global ocean simulations, Ocean-E2E demonstrated a significant improvement, reducing the RMSE by an average of 11.6% and increasing the CSI for extreme MHWs by an average of 9.2% compared to leading baseline models.
Furthermore, in end-to-end operational forecast tasks, our framework outperformed the state-of-the-art S2S system, achieving an average RMSE reduction of approximately 10% over a 40-day forecast horizon using its own assimilated initial fields.
Neural data assimilation further enables high-resolution forecasting independent of traditional numerical systems, while maintaining superior accuracy and stability, as evidenced by the rapid stabilization of assimilation errors.
Experimental results comprehensively demonstrate its robustness across spatiotemporal scales, offering a promising tool for advancing MHW prediction and understanding climate extremes.

Appendix C
Details of Dynamical Core of Ocean-E2E

In this section, we are going to introduce the design of our hybrid physics data-driven model. As shown in Section 3.2, the evolution equation of our hybrid physics data-driven framework can be written as:

C_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}-({\bf u}(t)+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}(t),C))\nabla C+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}({\bf A}(t),C)dt+C_{0}

(24)

Spatial Discretization
. In this study, we utilized two-order center discretization to approximate
\nabla C
. To be sepcific,

\nabla C(i,j)=({\bf D_{x}}*C(i,j),{\bf D_{y}}*C(i,j))

(25)

Where and
*
is convolution operator,
{\bf D_{x}}
and
{\bf D_{y}}
are horizontal gradients, which has the form

{\bf D_{x}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-1/d_{i,j}&0&1/d_{i,j}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},{\bf D_{y}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/s_{i,j}&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-1/s_{i,j}&0\end{pmatrix},

(26)

d_{i,j}
and
s_{i,j}
are longtitudal and latitdual grid scale at
(i,j)
.

What’s more, unlike atmosphere variables, the ocean MHWs have coastline boundary, which should be treated properly to aviod any numerical instability. In our study, we mitigate this boundary effect through a boundart mask
\mathbf{M}
, where

\mathbf{M}(i,j)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the grid locates on ocean}\\
0,&\text{else}\end{cases}

(27)

Furthermore, a scaling factor
\varepsilon_{gm}=0.1
is employed on
{\bf u}_{gm}
to avoid the instability of training. The equation
24
then becomes

C_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}[-({\bf u}(t)+\varepsilon_{gm}{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}(t),C))\nabla C]\mathbf{M}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}({\bf A}(t),C)dt+C_{0}

(28)

Time Integration
.
As mentioned in Section 3.2, we use second-order Runge-Kutta method to approximate the time integration:

\displaystyle\tiny C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star}

\displaystyle=C_{i\Delta t}+\left[-\left({\bf u}_{i\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{i\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right)\nabla C_{i\Delta t}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{i\Delta t:(i+1)\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right]\Delta t

(29)

\displaystyle C_{(i+1)\Delta t}

\displaystyle=C_{i\Delta t}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\{\left[-\left({\bf u}_{i\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{i\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right)\nabla C_{i\Delta t}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{i\Delta t:(i+1)\Delta t},C_{i\Delta t})\right]\Delta t

\displaystyle\quad+\left[-\left({\bf u}_{(i+1)\Delta t}+{\bf u}_{\theta}({\bf u}_{(i+1)\Delta t},C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star})\right)\nabla C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star}+\dot{\rm S}_{\theta}(A_{(i+1)\Delta t:(i+2)\Delta t},C_{(i+1)\Delta t}^{\star})\right]\Bigg\}

(30)

In this study, we set
\Delta t=24
hours and the whole integration time is
t=4
days.

D.1
Multi-scale Convolution

Inspired by

0

\mathbf{F}_{g}=\text{Conv}_{1\times 1}\left(\text{DWDConv}_{\text{spatial}}\left(\text{DWConv}_{0}\left(\sigma_{\text{act}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{\text{norm1}}\right)\right)\right)\right)\right)

(31)

\mathbf{F}_{x},\mathbf{G}_{x}=\text{split}\left(\mathbf{F}_{g},\text{dim}=1\right)

The module for approximating the bolus velocity,

These architectures, centered around the versatile

\mathrm{ConvL}(x_{in})=\mathcal{PR}(\mathcal{LN}(Conv(x_{in}))),

D.2.2
Attention Mechanism

The attention mechanism can realize the efficient allocation of information processing resources and can give more attention to key area of observatiuon while temporarily ignoring the missing locations. Therefore, the attention mechanism is used to focus on important information with high weights, ignore irrelevant information with low weights, and continuously adjust the weights during the network learning process. Therefore, a single model can extract more valuable feature information in different imaging environments. In this work, both channel attention and spatial attention are jointly exploited to further improve the assimilation performance.

The channel attention mechanism is thus exploited to reconstruct the relationship between target analysis features and the input of different observation fields. The channel attention module (
\mathrm{CAM}

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{CAM}&=\sigma(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Avg}(x_{in}^{c}))+\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Max}(x_{in}^{c})))\\

\displaystyle\operatorname{SAM}

(36)

where

is the input of

,
\left[~;~\right]

D.2.3
Kirsch-guided Reparameterization Module

To facilitate efficient assimilation in real-world MHWs forecasting, we employ the Kirsch-guided reparameterization module (KRM) with shared parameters, as proposed by Liu et al.
[
39
]
. The KRM is composed of three principal elements: (1) a standard convolutional layer that captures local spatial features; (2) an expand–squeeze convolutional layer that adaptively adjusts the receptive field; and (3) a family of eight directional edge-detection operators that guide the learning and inference of the convolutional kernels. Specifically, the standard convolutional and expand–squeeze operations can be formulated as follows:

\begin{gathered}F_{n}=W_{n}*x_{in}^{k}+B_{n},\end{gathered}

(37)

\begin{gathered}F_{es}=W_{s}*\left(W_{e}*x_{in}^{k}+B_{e}\right)+B_{s},\end{gathered}

(38)

where
x_{in}^{k}
denotes the input of KRM.
W_{n}
,
W_{s}
,
W_{e}
,
B_{n}
,
B_{s}
,
B_{e}
are the weights and bias of convolution operation.
F_{n}
represents the output of the normal convolutional layer.
F_{es}
denotes the output of the expanding-and-squeezing convolutional layer. We first reparameterize the Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
). Subsequently, we merge them into a single normal convolution with parameters
W_{es}
and
B_{es}
:

\displaystyle W_{es}=\mathrm{perm}\left(W_{e}\right)*W_{s},

(39)

\displaystyle B_{es}=W_{s}*\mathrm{rep}\left(B_{e}\right)+B_{s},

(40)

where
\operatorname{perm}(\cdot)
denotes the permute operation which exchanges the
1
st and
2
nd dimensions of a tensor,
\operatorname{rep}(\cdot)
denotes the spatial broadcasting operation. And the predefined eight-direction Kirsch edge filters
K_{i}
are incorporated into the reparameterization module. To memorize the edge features, the input feature
x_{in}^{k}
will first be processed by
C\times C\times 1\times 1
convolution and then use a custom Kirsch filter to extract the feature map gradients in eight different directions. Therefore, the edge information in eight directions can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle F_{K}^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(S_{K}^{i}\odot K_{i}\right)\otimes\left(W_{i}*x_{in}^{k}+B_{i}\right)+B_{K_{i}}

(41)

\displaystyle=W_{K}^{i}\otimes\left(W_{i}*x_{in}^{k}+B_{i}\right)+B_{K_{i}},

where
W_{i}
and
B_{i}
are the weights and bias of
1\times 1
convolution for branches in eight directions,
S_{K}^{i}
and
B_{K_{i}}
are the scaling parameters and bias with the shape of
C\times 1\times 1\times 1
,
\odot
indicates the channel-wise broadcasting multiplication. The combined edge information, extracted by the scaled Kirsch filters, is given by:

F_{\mathrm{K}}=\sum_{i=1}^{8}F_{K}^{i}.

(42)

Therefore, the final weights and bias after reparameterization can be expressed as:

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{rep}}

\displaystyle=W_{n}+W_{es}+\sum_{i=1}^{8}\left(\mathrm{perm}\left(W_{i}\right)*W_{\mathrm{K}}^{i}\right),

(43)

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{rep}}

\displaystyle=B_{n}+B_{es}+\sum_{i=1}^{8}\left(\mathrm{perm}\left(B_{i}\right)*B_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\right).

(44)

Finally, the output can be obtained using a single normal convolution in the inference stage:

F=W_{\mathrm{rep}}*x_{in}^{k}+B_{\mathrm{rep}}.

(45)

(51)

Therefore, for the obsevation data

Appendix E
Experiments Details

E.1
Evaluation Metric

Definition of MHWs
. The
[
25
]
definition of MHW categories is applied to detect MHWs from original SST field. More precisely, any interval in which the SST anomalies surpass the 90th percentile for a minimum of five days in a row is classified as an MHW event. Subsequently, the MHWs are divided into three severity categories: moderate, strong, and severe/extreme. These categories are determined by the 90th, 92.5th, and 95th percentiles of the SST, respectively
[
20
]
. Note that our results are unaffected by different definitions of the MHW category.

Metrics
. In this study, to evaluate the performance of our model, we use three metrics: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Critical Success Index (CSI) and Bias (BIAS). RMSE represents the overall performance of our SST anomaly forecast, which is calculated as:

\displaystyle{\rm RMSE}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{H\times W}\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i,j}(T_{i,j}(t)-\tilde{T}_{i,j}(t))^{2}}

(58)

Where
T_{i,j}(t)
and
\tilde{T}_{i,j}(t)
is the ground truth and prediction of SST anomaly at lead time
t
,
\omega_{i,j}
is the weighting coefficient and the subscript
(i,j)
indicates the data at grid point
(i,j)
while
H
and
W
follow the same definition in Section 2. CSI is the metric that evaluate the forecast ability of extreme events and can be expressed as:

\displaystyle{\rm CSI}(t)=\frac{{\rm TP}}{{\rm TP}+{\rm FP}+{\rm FN}}

(59)

Where TP (True Positive) denotes the number of cases that a MHW event is accurately predicted. FP (False Positive), FN (False Negative), and TN (True Negative) follow a similar definition.

BIAS is used to evaluate the systematic difference between the analysis field and the ground truth:

\displaystyle{\rm BIAS}(t)=\frac{1}{H\times W}\sum_{i,j}\omega_{i,j}(T_{i,j}(t)-\tilde{T}_{i,j}(t))

(60)

E.2
Model Training

All baseline models and Ocean-E2E were trained under identical experimental configurations to ensure fair comparison. The training process employed 100 epochs with an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
, utilizing a step-wise learning rate scheduler to dynamically adjust training parameters until convergence was achieved. For objective evaluation, model checkpoints demonstrating optimal performance on the validation set were selected as final candidates for comparative analysis.
