Metabolic effects and clinical outcomes of olanzapine in schizophrenia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Weifeng Jin, Shuzi Chen, Dan Li, Qing Chen, Mengyuan zhu, Mengxia Wang, Xiaomei Fu, Ping Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the metabolic changes of patients with schizophrenia treated with olanzapine and analyze the correlation between the dosage of the drug, the blood concentration and the clinical response, so as to provide a reference for the clinical diagnosis and treatment.MethodsPubMed, web of science, Cochrane Library, MEDLINE databases were used to search for studies on olanzapine treatment in patients with schizophrenia, to extract changes in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, cholesterol, PANSS (The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale), SAPS (The Positive Syndrome Scale), SANS (The Negative Syndrome Scale), BPRS (Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale), CGI (Clinical Global Impressions Ratings), and so on for meta-analysis, and to analyze the correlation of medication dose, blood concentration, and clinical response.ResultsTwenty clinical trials (1839 participants, 1058 male and 781 female patients) were included in this meta-analysis. The results showed that patients receiving olanzapine had significant weight gain (WMD = 0.25, 95 % CI: 0.06–0.44) and no significant changes in HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides, or total cholesterol. They were significantly decreased in PANSS (WMD = −2.05, 95 % CI: −2.83, - 1.26), SAPS (WMD = −1.62, 95 % CI: −2.15, - 1.09), sans (WMD = −0.80, 95 % CI: −0.93, - 0.66), BPRS (WMD = −2.79, 95 % CI: −4.11, - 1.46), and CGI (WMD = −1.81, 95 % CI: −2.25, - 1.36). The dose of medication was positively associated with clinical response, with higher plasma concentrations at the recommended dose associated with better clinical outcomes (or = 2.91, 95 % CI: 1.36–6.27). When the body blood concentration is > 10 μg/L, the clinical response rate reaches about 80 %.ConclusionThe clinic can make an individualized medication regimen based on adjusting the dosage of drugs based on the results of the detection of blood concentration in the body under the premise of avoiding metabolic side effects to ensure the maximization of efficacy.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a serious neurological and psychiatric disorder that threatens approximately 1 % of the global population [1]. Its long-term impact on the psychological condition of patients involves cognitive, emotional symptoms, behavior, and emotional dysfunction [2]. Symptoms are usually divided into any changes in behavior or thoughts, as well as ruthless or dull emotions, and lack of interest in daily social interactions. Recent studies have shown that many factors, such as genetics, nutrition, environmental factors, and stress, play a role in causing mental illness [3,4]. Although second-generation antipsychotic drugs (SGA) have become the preferred drug in some countries such as the United States, the metabolic changes and side effects caused by multiple factors often vary among different patients [[5], [6], [7]].

Clozapine represents the second generation of so-called atypical antipsychotic drugs and has shown positive effects in previously refractory psychiatric cases [8]. It is worth noting that it was still limited by metabolic and side effects. Compared with clozapine, olanzapine does not cause severe agranulocytosis during treatment [9]. This makes the application of olanzapine more worthy of trust and expectation. The results of multiple randomized controlled trials indicate that compared to placebo, patients receiving long-term injectable antipsychotic drugs have improved their condition and are the best treatment method for preventing the recurrence of schizophrenia [10]. However, the correlation of schizophrenia and serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) are still widely controversial.

The recommended dose of olanzapine for patients under routine circumstances is 5–20 mg per day [11]. Because this can maintain the serum concentration of olanzapine in patients at 20–80 ng/mL, based on past experience, it may be more conducive to improving the patient's condition. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of published literature to determine the metabolic changes and side effects of olanzapine on schizophrenia and analyze its correlation with dose or blood drug concentration.

Materials and method

Search strategy

We follow the Preferred Report Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses statement to perform the meta-analysis. Electronic databases including PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Medline were used to retrieve relevant research published during 2000–2023. The search terms are mainly: ((("Olanzapine"[Mesh]) AND ((("Schizophrenias"[Mesh]) OR ("Schizophrenic Disorders"[Mesh])) OR ("Schizophrenic Disorder"[Mesh]))) AND ((("Drug Therapy"[Mesh]) or ("Pharmacotherapy"[Mesh])) OR ("Pharmacotherapies"[Mesh]))). There were no language restrictions during the search. In addition, the reference list of applicable studies was manually checked for inclusion in other articles. Two researchers jointly complete this search process. The original authors of the study were asked for their opinions if there were any disputes during the search. We'll ask a third expert for professional advice if it doesn't work out.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Research was considered available if the publication meets all of the following criteria.1)Confirm the diagnosis as schizophrenia or mental disorders and identify similar diagnoses;2)Scientific clinical experiments;3)A detailed and accurate description of the experiment participants;4)A complete and appropriate result description;5)Follow-up time was long enough for the expected outcome.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers independently extracted relevant information from each study: first author’s name, publication year, sample size, mean patient age, gender, course of disease, olanzapine dosage, follow-up and main outcmes(including weight, waist circumference, BMI, total cholesterol, as well as PANSS, BPRS, CGI, discontinuation of medication, adverse reactions, etc.). We downloaded the full text in order to get enough information. If in doubt, asked the original author for help. The method recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies.

Statistical analysis

Based on the recommendations of the Cochrane collaboration, quantitative synthesis of the indicators included in the study. The data was pooled by conducting meta-analysis, if data allowed, by using Stata 16.0 software. We extracted the mean (Mean), standard deviation (SD), odds ratio (OR), and their 95 % confidence interval (CI) from various research results for meta-analysis. The random-effects model was used for meta-analysis considering potential sources of clinical heterogeneity. When I2 ˃ 50 %, subgroup analysis based on baseline, intervention and/or sensitivity analysis eliminate studies one by one would be conducted to explore the source of heterogeneity [12,13]. Small sample effect and publication bias were detected by funnel plots and statistical tests, respectively.

This study included a total of 1839 participants, including 1058 male and 781 female patients. The average age ranges from 15 to 55 years old, and also covers a disease course of 1–24 years. According to statistics, the follow-up period of each study included is more than 8 weeks, which is enough time to achieve ideal results. The recommended oral dose is 5–20 mg, and the dosage given to participants in the study design meets this requirement. Metabolic parameters

The evaluation of weight changes in five studies was included in a meta-analysis. Due to the lack of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 10.7 %, P > 0.05), a fixed effects model was used to fit the final results (WMD = 0.25, 95 % CI: 0.06,0.44, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). This indicates that patients may gain weight during treatment, regardless of any recommended dose received. Of course, only one study result clearly supports this conclusion (Serge Sevy, 15±6 mg/d, WMD = 0.79, 95 % CI: 0.25,1.34).Fig. 2Body weight change in schizophrenia patients treated with olanzapine.Fig. 2

Body weight change in schizophrenia patients treated with olanzapine.

However, it seems that the changes in triglycerides (WMD = 0.06, 95 % CI: −0.2,0.37, P > 0.05) are different from body weight. Only two studies (David C Henderson, Alastair J Flint) have explained the changes in triglycerides before and after treatment with olanzapine, which may cause statistical fluctuations. From the results of this study, HDL-C (WMD = −0.21, 95 % CI: −0.52,0.10, P > 0.05), LDL-C (WMD = −0.02, 95 % CI: −0.34,0.29, P > 0.05), Total cholesterol (WMD = −0.02, 95 % CI: −0.33,0.29, P > 0.05) did not show significant differences during olanzapine treatment. This trend has been observed in most lipid observation indicators (Supplemental Figs. 1–4).

Psychopathology scale

Eight studies have elucidated the changes in total PANSS scores in patients with schizophrenia after receiving olanzapine treatment. The results of each study tend to be consistent, and the total PANSS score of patients after treatment decreases. Due to heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 97.4 %, P < 0.05), a random effects model was used for analysis. WMD = −2.05 (95 % CI: −2.83, −1.26, P > 0.05, Fig. 3). It is worth mentioning that studies with higher average doses (Tomihisa Niitsu, 20 mg/d, WMD = −0.71, 95 % CI: −1.27, −0.15) did not show better results. We noticed that the situation seems to differ in terms of positive and negative scores. The corresponding nine studies elaborated on the changes in SAPS scores of patients after receiving olanzapine treatment. There is significant heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 92.1 %, P < 0.05), WMD = −1.62 (95 % CI: −2.15, −1.09, P < 0.05, Fig. 4). Only one study (Muard Atmaca, WMD = −0.50,95 % CI: −1.25,0.26) showed no difference in SAPS scores before and after treatment. More importantly, the treatment effect of the highest average dose (Muard Atmaca, WMD = −0.50,95 % CI: −1.25,0.26) was not entirely satisfactory. On the contrary, patients who received an average dose of 9.7 mg/g responded to the best psychopathological changes (Olalla Robles, WMD = −3.11,95 % CI: −3.95, −2.28). Similarly, the negative portion (SANS) showed the same results. And due to low inter study heterogeneity (I2 = 40.5 %, P＞0.05), a fixed effects model was used to fit the final results (WMD = −0.80, 95 % CI: −0.93, −0.66, P < 0.05, Fig. 5). Only Muard Atmaca's study showed no difference in SANS scores before and after treatment (WMD = −0.50, 95 % CI: −1.25,0.26). Other results

We found that at the recommended dose of 5–20 mg/d, the treatment effect also improved (OR = 2.91, 95 % CI: 1.36–6.27, Fig. 8) as the serum concentration increased in MeiYuan et al.'s study. It is important to note that the treatment effect is good in the short term (1–3 weeks) and highest in the third week (OR = 7.13, 95 % CI: 3.34–15.23). In the analysis of the relationship between blood drug concentration and clinical effects, maintaining a blood drug concentration of over 10 μg/L to maintain a sufficiently high effective rate (70 %–80 %). In addition, we also analyzed the correlation between medication duration and clinical reactions. Undoubtedly, there was a significant positive correlation between the first to eighth week after medication and clinical reactions. Discussion

We conducted a meta-analysis of the metabolic changes caused by any recommended dose of olanzapine in this study, and the results showed that patients had a certain degree of weight increase during treatment (WMD = 0.25, 95 % CI: 0.06–0.44). To illustrate the results of our main outcome, weight change, we identified both short-term (<12 weeks) and long-term outcomes. The results showed that weight gain was rapid in the first few weeks, then gradually decreased until a few months later when the weight gain reached a plateau. Studies have shown that this time period is 4–9 months, which is significantly shorter than that of olanzapine (42–46 months) [[34], [35], [36]]. Our study explains this because longer treatment periods result in more weight gain than shorter treatment periods. While the changes in HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides, and total cholesterol were not significant. And due to the limited number of studies, it is impossible to make absolute conclusions. In the long run, absolute changes will increase, but this is not an absolute estimate of these results, but a relative estimate.

Long term antipsychotic treatment often brings significant long-term consequences, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD). The increase in weight and cholesterol indicates the early characteristics of these metabolic syndromes [37]. Research has found that olanzapine causes patients to achieve the highest cholesterol elevation during treatment compared to other antipsychotic drugs [38]. This indicates that olanzapine may cause higher metabolic side effects. However, our research suggests that the changes in these metabolic parameters are not significant. The reason is that clinical doctors are more cautious in the application of doses. In the studies we included, they often adjust doses based on the clinical reactions of patients.

It is reported that olanzapine has a better effect in improving neurological symptoms such as anxiety, depression, low spirits, and thinking disorders [39]. Olanzapine mainly acts through 5-HT2 receptors and D2 receptors, with an affinity of up to 60 %–80 %, demonstrating excellent effects in controlling extracellular system reactions [40,41]. Research has shown that olanzapine is the only second-generation antipsychotic drug that has shown acute efficacy in psychiatric symptoms in randomized controlled trials [42]. In this meta-analysis, we noticed that all studies had follow-up periods exceeding 8 weeks, which helps clinical doctors adjust the actual dosage of olanzapine based on the condition and side effects. There were significant differences in the average changes in PANSS, SANS, SAPS, BPRS, and CGI relative to baseline among patients receiving recommended doses. Previous meta-analyses have also confirmed that [[43], [44], [45]]. However, only six studies have reported relevant results and used different measurement tools. Therefore, we did not include QoL and general functional results in our analysis.

As an antipsychotic drug, the same dosage of olanzapine has individual differences in blood drug concentration in the human body. More importantly, there may be a certain relationship between serum concentration and clinical efficacy. Under the control of clinical doctors, there is a significant correlation between the patient's medication dosage and clinical response in the short term. This means that under the premise of considering safety, clinical doctors will adjust the dosage of medication and then adjust the blood drug concentration to maintain sufficient clinical effects. In order to truly achieve personalized medication, reduce or avoid adverse reactions of patients, and use medication reasonably, monitoring the blood concentration of antipsychotic drugs is essential in clinical medication guidance [[46], [47], [48]]. We found that the higher the blood drug concentration in patients, the better their clinical efficacy (OR = 2.91,95 % CI: 1.36–6.27). When the blood drug concentration range reaches<10 μg/L, 10–20 μg/L, and>20 μg/L, the corresponding clinical response effective rates are 42.11 %, 80.95 %, and 71.43 %, respectively. Therefore, when taking olanzapine for a long time, clinical doctors should advise patients to regularly monitor the blood concentration of olanzapine and adjust the dosage to a level that is efficient and avoids adverse reactions.

However, this study still has some shortcomings. Firstly, due to the use of various outcome scales for patients with schizophrenia, the data included in some of the scales in the study is limited (for example, only one study provided recurrence rate data), making it difficult to conduct a better meta-analysis of these scales. Secondly, although most of the included studies have low and moderate risk of bias, the quality of the included Chinese studies is lower than that of foreign studies, especially in the generation of random sequence, allocation concealment and blindness. High quality research is needed on Chinese patients to evaluate clinical practices and support guideline recommendations. Thirdly, the heterogeneity of the population included in the trial (such as gender and age differences) may affect the efficacy and safety outcomes. Given that this study does not constitute a large-scale randomized controlled trial, identifying definitive results may pose significant challenges. While MINORS serves as a valuable tool for assessing the quality of non-randomized experimental studies, its effectiveness merely fulfills the essential criteria, leaving room for further refinement. Consequently, the anticipation for the incorporation of future large-scale randomized controlled trials in this field is highly warranted and eagerly awaited.
