Corneal endothelial layer changes in the meibomian gland dysfunction cases in comparison to healthy controls: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rana Sorkhabi, Ali Mostafaie, Farhad Najafzadeh, Mohammad Hossein Ahoor, Saba Asghari, Milad Sheikhian, Mohammad Mahdi Nemati, Alireza Akbari Aghdam, Amin Arasteh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the corneal endothelial layer characteristics in the meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) cases and compare them to those of the healthy controls.Study designCross-sectional.Methods100 eyes from 50 MGD cases and 66 eyes from 33 healthy controls were evaluated in this study. The ocular surface disease index (OSDI), Schirmer’s test, tear break-up time (TBUT), tear meniscus height, and MGD staging were evaluated as the subjective and objective parameters of dry eye disease (DED) and MGD. The corneal endothelial cell density (ECD), coefficient of variation in endothelial cell size (CV), and hexagonal cells proportion (HA) were evaluated using the corneal specular microscopy. Linear mixed-effect models (LMMs) were used to compare endothelial parameters between groups.ResultsThe mean age in the MGD group was 56.9 years, and in the controls, 44.3 years. Although the age difference was significant, it was treated as a covariate in the LMM to adjust for age differences across groups. The adjusted mean ECD was 183 cells/mm2 lower in the MGD cases than in the controls. However, the difference was marginally statistically insignificant (95%CI: -371 to + 3, p = 0.055). On the other hand, within-group analysis showed that the more advanced stages of MGD have lower ECD (Mean difference for stage 4 vs. 1: -745 cells/mm2, and stage 3 vs. 1: -610 cells/mm2). The CV showed no significant difference between the groups. Adjusted HA means were 40.97% in MGD versus 36.04% in controls (mean difference = + 4.93, 95% CI: +1.31 to + 8.55). However, the HA difference does not appear clinically significant and may be due to inter-measurement variability in specular microscopy.ConclusionOur findings suggest a potential association between MGD severity and corneal endothelial parameters, particularly endothelial cell density, although this relationship is modest and influenced strongly by age.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is primarily categorized into two subgroups based on physiopathology: aqueous deficiency and meibomian gland dysfunction/disease (MGD). The MGD is the most prevalent cause of DED, leading to eyelid and conjunctival inflammation and tear film instability [1]. Based on estimates, about 35% of the general population has MGD; however, prevalence can vary by ethnicity, ranging from 21% to 71% [2]. In addition to changes in the tear-secreting organs of the eye, the cornea and conjunctiva may also be altered in DED.

It has been demonstrated that corneal sensitivity in DED cases is lower than in the normal population, and the corneal subepithelial nerves may be thicker than in the normal population [3]. The presence of inflammatory cells, such as Langerhans cells and leukocytes, in the corneal epithelium is also increased in DED cases compared to controls [4]. Another study showed that corneal subbasal nerve density and length are decreased in DED cases and are negatively correlated with the number of Langerhans cells [5].

Interestingly, DED cases have been shown to have lower corneal endothelial cell density (ECD) and a higher rate of ECD loss. It has been reported that DED cases exhibit a mean annual cell density loss of 2.1% and that this value is negatively correlated with subbasal nerve density [6]. Another study has demonstrated that the ECD and the severity of DED are negatively correlated: the more severe the DED, the lower the ECD [7]. The ECD loss in DED and other ocular surface inflammatory conditions may result from reduced levels of neurotrophic factors, increased levels of cytotoxic factors, and heightened immune responses in the cornea [8].

Most studies in the literature have focused on corneal endothelial changes in DED cases as a whole, or on aqueous-deficiency subtypes such as Sjögren’s disease. In this cross-sectional study, we aim to investigate the corneal endothelial profile in MGD cases compared with controls and its correlation with subjective and objective MGD staging.

Methods

Target population

It is a cross-sectional study of 50 MGD cases and 33 standard controls conducted at Nikookari Eye Hospital, Tabriz, Iran, from April 2025 to October 2025. Patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in the study after providing informed consent. All patients aged 18 or older without a medical history of autoimmune or inflammatory disease and without ocular conditions other than DED were included in the study. Patients with a history of ocular trauma, any ocular surgery, or regular use of any topical medications except for artificial tears were excluded. Also, patients with a history of contact lens use were excluded from the study. Both eyes of each patient were included in the study. The demographic, habitual, and medical histories of all patients were recorded before ophthalmic examinations. Based on the previous studies [7], a standard deviation of 350 was assumed for the endothelial cell density, and an expected 220 cells/mm2 density differences between the MGD group and the controls, a statistical power of 80% and alpha error of 0.05, a sample size of 40 was needed for each group. Although the group sizes were slightly unbalanced, the total number of participants was similar to the required sample size, and the anticipated effect size was in the medium–large range (Cohen’s d = 0.63). Because both eyes were included in the analysis using a linear mixed-effects model to account for within-subject correlation, and considering the medium-large difference between the groups, effective precision is expected to be maintained, and the actual statistical power is expected to be close to the originally targeted 80%.

DED and MGD evaluations

The subjective status of DED in patients was evaluated using the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire. The OSDI score was divided into four categories: normal ocular surface (0–12), mild (13–22), moderate (23–32), and severe ocular surface disease (33–100). First, during the ophthalmic examination with slit lamp biomicroscopy, the tear meniscus height was measured to estimate tear volume. One mm was considered the threshold for dividing the tear meniscus height into two subgroups. The Schirmer’s test (without anesthesia) was then performed to assess aqueous secretion. The Schirmer’s test is reported in three categories: less than 7 mm, 7–10 mm, and more than 10 mm. Next, the tear break-up time (TBUT) was measured using a moist fluorescein-impregnated strip and cobalt blue light to evaluate tear film stability. Ten seconds was considered the threshold for dividing TBUT into two subgroups. DED grading was assessed based on the criteria published in the report of the Definition and Classification Subcommittee of the International Dry Eye Workshop (2007) (Because the newer version of reports does not provide a separate severity-grading scheme.) [9]. MGD staging was determined according to the detailed criteria presented in the fourth Table of the International Workshop on Meibomian Gland Dysfunction report published in 2011 [10]. The MGD staging included eyelid margin features, meibomian quality, meibomian expressibility, and corneal staining. Meibography was not used in the MGD staging of our study. Controls were carefully screened and excluded if any clinical signs of MGD, dry eye disease, or ocular surface abnormality were present during slit-lamp examination and TBUT/Schirmer evaluation.

Endothelial evaluation

The corneal endothelial layer was evaluated in all cases using the EM-3000 corneal specular microscope (Tomey, USA). The ECD (cells/mm²), coefficient of variation in endothelial cell size (CV, %), and the hexagonal cells proportion (HA, 6A%) were evaluated by specular microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Skewed continuous data are reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. All tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Given the inclusion of data from both eyes per subject, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed to account for inter-eye correlation using the compound symmetry model. The primary fixed effect of interest was group (MGD vs. control), with age and sex entered as covariates. Estimated marginal means (EMMeans) with 95% CI were derived from the models. Results were reported as mean differences between groups with corresponding p-values. The mean difference values were reported as mean and 95% confidence interval (95%CI). The predictive power of the covariates is reported as regression coefficients β and 95% CIs. Given differences among groups in diabetes status, smoking status, and menopausal status, the parameters were reanalyzed, adding these factors as covariates. Also, due to 0% prevalence of menopause in the control group, another re-analysis without considering menopause was conducted. Also, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing 28 MGD eyes (14 cases) with 28 healthy eyes (14 controls), matched by age (a tolerance of 5 years), diabetes status, menopausal status, and smoking status. SPSS automatically conducted the matching process.

Extended multivariable models were constructed within the MGD group to examine the associations between endothelial parameters and clinical covariates, including MGD stage, DED grade, Schirmer’s test category, tear meniscus height, cigarette smoking, and diabetes status. Pairwise comparisons between levels were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Subgroup analyses were performed in women only, with menopause status included as a fixed factor to assess its relationship with endothelial outcomes.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the research ethics committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences under approval ID IR.TBZMED.REC.1404.064. All patient evaluations were conducted after informed consent was obtained. The study also adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki during its conduct.

Results

A total of 166 eyes from 83 subjects (100 eyes from MGD patients and 66 eyes from controls) were included in the analysis. The mean age of patients in the MGD group was 56.9, while in the control group, it was 44.3, with a statistically significant difference. The prevalence of smoking, diabetes, and menopause was significantly higher in the MGD group. Endothelial layer characteristics

For ECD, the MGD group had marginally lower values compared to controls (p = 0.055). Adjusted means were 2214 cells/mm² in MGD versus 2398 cells/mm² in controls (mean difference = -183; 95% CI: -371 to + 3). Age remained a strong predictor (β = -16.47, 95% CI: -22.35 to -10.60, p < 0.001), as expected, with higher age associated with lower ECD. The detailed data on the comparisons are available in Table 3. Due to the significant difference between the groups regarding the prevalence of DM, smoking, and menopause, these factors entered the re-analysis as the covariates. The estimated adjusted mean of ECD in the MGD group was 2225 cells/mm², in contrast to the 2384 cells/mm² in controls (mean difference: -159; 95% CI: -365 to + 46, p = 0.127). None of the covariates, except age, was a significant predictor of the ECD. Excluding menopause as the covariate also did not change the results (mean difference: -157; 95% CI: -354 to + 39, p = 0.116). The decrease in the mean difference and the increase in p-values indicate that including the covariates reduces the difference between the groups.

For the CV, no significant difference was found between the MGD and control groups (p = 0.246). Adjusted means were 37.60% in the MGD group and 39.81% in the control group (mean difference = -2.00, 95% CI: -5.41 to + 1.40). CV was significantly positively associated with age (β = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.32, p < 0.001), but not with gender. Considering the DM, smoking, and menopause as the covariates, there was no significant difference either (38.20% vs. 38.94% in MGD and controls, p = 0.722). None of the covariates, except age, was a significant predictor of the CV. Excluding menopause as the covariate also did not change the results (37.92% vs. 39.50% in MGD and controls, p = 0.382).

For the hexagonal cell proportion, MGD patients showed significantly higher values than the control group (p = 0.008). Adjusted means were 40.97% in MGD versus 36.04% in controls (mean difference = + 4.93, 95% CI: +1.31 to + 8.55). Age (β = -0.40, 95% CI: -0.54 to -0.27, p < 0.001) was a significant predictor. Female patients had marginally lower HA values than males (36.91% vs. 40.10%; mean difference = -3.18; 95% CI: -6.41 to + 0.57; p = 0.054). Considering DM, smoking, and menopause as covariates, MGD cases had a higher hexagonal cell proportion than controls (40.68% vs. 34.06%; mean difference: 6.62; 95% CI: 2.75–10.49; p = 0.001). None of the covariates, except age and diabetes, were significant predictors of HA. The β for diabetes is -5.89 (95% CI: -10.36 to -1.43, p = 0.010), and the presence of diabetes was associated with lower hexagonal cell proportion. Excluding menopause as a covariate yielded results similar to those with menopause included, with a higher HA in the MGD group (40.78% vs. 33.91%; mean difference: 6.86; 95% CI: 3.15–10.58; p < 0.001). Despite the ECD and CV parameters, considering the covariates makes between-groups differences larger for the HA.

In summary, compared with controls, MGD eyes showed lower endothelial cell density (without a statistically significant difference) and a higher proportion of hexagonal cells, whereas CV did not differ significantly. Age consistently influenced all parameters.

The sensitivity analysis resulted in an estimated adjusted mean ECD of 2342 cells/mm² in the MGD and 2418 cells/mm² in the controls, with no significant difference (p = 0.555). The CV also showed no significant difference, 38.00% in MGD cases and 38.53% in controls (p = 0.838). The proportion of hexagonal cells was also higher in the MGD cases, but the difference was not statistically significant (40.32% vs. 38.10%, p = 0.488).

Within-group analysis of covariates

In further analyses, including MGD stage, DED grade, Schirmer’s test, tear meniscus height, cigarette smoking, diabetes, and menopause in women, distinct patterns emerged across the endothelial parameters.

For ECD, the MGD stage (p = 0.013) was a significant predictor. Estimated marginal means decreased from 2663 cells/mm² in stage 1 to 1919 cells/mm² in stage 4, with substantial pairwise reductions in stage 3 versus stage 1 (mean difference − 610, 95% CI − 1137 to − 84, p = 0.014) and stage 4 versus stage 1 (mean difference − 745, 95% CI − 1399 to − 90, p = 0.017). DED grade, Schirmer’s test, tear meniscus height, smoking, and diabetes were not significant predictors.

For CV, the MGD stage was the only significant predictor (p = 0.035). Adjusted means were 38.3% for stage 1, 44.7% for stage 2, 42.0% for stage 3, and 37.8% for stage 4. Despite the overall significance, none of the pairwise comparisons between stages were significant after Bonferroni adjustment. TBUT, age, sex, Schirmer’s test, DED grade, cigarette smoking, diabetes, and tear meniscus height were not significant.

For HA, neither MGD nor DED severity, Schirmer’s test, nor systemic covariates were significant.

In analyses restricted to the female population, menopause status was not significantly associated with any of the outcomes: CV (p = 0.560), ECD (p = 0.953), and HA (p = 0.815).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we evaluated corneal endothelial parameters in patients with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) compared with age- and sex-adjusted controls using linear mixed-effects modeling. Our findings suggest that MGD may be associated with subtle alterations in corneal endothelial cell characteristics, particularly endothelial cell density (ECD). However, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance in the primary analysis. Importantly, age remained the strongest and most consistent determinant of all endothelial parameters, underscoring its dominant role as a physiological confounder in corneal endothelial assessment.

Various factors have been recognized as affecting endothelial layer characteristics, including systemic conditions (pseudoexfoliation syndrome, diabetes mellitus, or cardiovascular disease), ocular conditions (DED, uveitis, or glaucoma), and ocular surgeries (cataract surgery or glaucoma drainage device implantation) [11]. The ocular surface condition has previously been shown to correlate with the endothelial cell layer. Studies have demonstrated that long-term use of contact lenses, whether soft or hard, can alter endothelial cell parameters, such as ECD and CV [12, 13]. The exact mechanism behind this correlation has not yet been investigated, but changes in tear film inflammatory mediators have been suggested as a possible mechanism [13, 14]. These tear film inflammatory mediators have also been shown to affect stromal keratocyte density [14].

Previous studies have primarily focused on corneal endothelial changes in DED as a whole or in aqueous-deficient subtypes such as Sjögren syndrome. These studies have reported reduced ECD and increased endothelial cell loss rates in DED patients, suggesting a potential relationship between ocular surface inflammation and endothelial health [6, 7]. However, our findings indicate that within the spectrum of dry eye disease, MGD-specific staging may be more closely associated with endothelial variability than global dry eye severity indices such as OSDI, TBUT, or Schirmer’s test. This observation should be interpreted cautiously, as it may also reflect differences in disease classification sensitivity rather than a direct pathophysiological effect.

As in other ocular surface diseases, one possible mechanism underlying endothelial changes in MGD cases could be the disease’s significant inflammatory component. It has been demonstrated that Interleukin (IL)-8, IL-6, Interferon-γ, and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 levels are higher in the tear film of MGD eyes [15]. However, direct evidence linking these inflammatory changes to endothelial cell damage remains limited, and our study was not designed to investigate causality.

On the other hand, meibomian gland secretions primarily contribute to the lipid layer, the most superficial layer of the tear film. The lipid layer thickness could be an indicator of MGD severity; the more advanced the disease, the thinner the lipid layer. A decrease in lipid layer thickness leads to instability of the tear film [16]. Interestingly, it has been proposed that a reduction in the tear film lipid layer could expose the corneal tissue to a higher oxygen content (21% atmospheric oxygen), which normal tissues are not adapted to. This increased permeability could subject corneal tissue to oxidative stress, leading to an overproduction of inflammatory mediators [17]. This mechanism could be the other arm of the MGD, altering the corneal endothelial layer. While biologically plausible, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires validation in experimental or longitudinal studies.

Analysis of endothelial morphology revealed a statistically significant difference in the proportion of hexagonal cells between groups; however, this difference may lack clinical significance. When systemic confounding factors were included as covariates, the between-group difference in HA increased. In contrast, matched analysis, which balanced cases and controls on key confounders, showed that the between-group difference in HA was no longer statistically significant. Collectively, these inconsistent findings indicate that the HA signal likely reflects both underlying biological variation, including the effects of systemic factors such as diabetes, and residual measurement or model-related variability. Consequently, although the adjusted association is reported, changes in HA should be interpreted with caution, and no definitive mechanistic role for endothelial hexagonality in MGD pathophysiology can be established based on this study alone.

A notable finding of this study is the lack of significant association between conventional dry eye parameters (OSDI, TBUT, Schirmer’s test) and corneal endothelial outcomes after adjustment. The finding suggests that classical dry eye severity indices may not adequately capture structural corneal changes. In contrast, MGD staging may reflect a more relevant dimension of ocular surface disease in relation to endothelial health. However, this interpretation remains exploratory and requires confirmation in prospective studies, especially given the smaller sample sizes in stages 1 and 4 of MGD, which have led to wide confidence intervals for the ECD mean difference.

From a clinical perspective, the potential association between advanced MGD stages and reduced endothelial cell density may be relevant in the preoperative assessment of patients undergoing intraocular surgery. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design and baseline differences between groups, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of increased surgical risk but rather as a hypothesis-generating observation.

The methodology and statistical model of our study, using mixed-effect models to account for inter-eye correlation and to evaluate other covariates, are among the strengths of our research. Although our study is the first to specifically examine the corneal endothelial layer in MGD cases, some limitations could be addressed in future studies.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, there were significant baseline differences between groups, particularly in age and systemic comorbidities, which may introduce residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Third, the absence of inflammatory biomarkers, tear osmolarity measurements, and in vivo confocal microscopy limits mechanistic interpretation. Fourth, simultaneous evaluation of central corneal thickness (CCT) could have strengthened the interpretation of the endothelial findings. Fifth, although the OSDI score is a subjective measure, the distribution in our controls was somewhat skewed toward the upper end of the normal range, which might have confounded the study. Finally, the sample size, while adequate for primary comparisons, may not be sufficient to detect subtle differences in secondary endothelial parameters, particularly given the unbalanced case-control distribution.

Simultaneous evaluations of tear film concentrations of inflammatory mediators and osmolarity could have robustly supported the hypothesis suggested by our findings. Concurrent corneal confocal microscopy to evaluate stromal keratocytes, corneal neurons, subbasal nerve density, and dendritic cell density could also have been more informative regarding other layers of the cornea and the neuroepithelial network, which might affect the endothelium. Also, the duration of the MGD was unknown in our study, which limits the accuracy of interpreting the results. Further cohort studies could further elaborate on the role of chronicity of MGD on endothelial parameters. On the other hand, future clinical trials targeting MGD treatments could investigate the effects of MGD treatment on endothelial parameters and determine whether these endothelial changes are reversible.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings suggest a potential association between MGD severity and corneal endothelial parameters, particularly endothelial cell density, although this relationship is modest and influenced strongly by age. Further prospective longitudinal studies incorporating detailed inflammatory and ocular surface biomarkers are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and clinical relevance of these observations.
