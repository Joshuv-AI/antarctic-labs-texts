1.
Introduction

The evolution equation

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,u(t,y)\,d\,y-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(y,x)\,u(t,x)\,d\,y\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

where
K:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a regular integrable kernel, has been proposed as a nonlocal analogous of the diffusion equation

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)&=\Delta u(t,x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

(see
[
3
]
).

In population models,
K(x,y)
can be interpreted as the probability density of a species moving from site
y
to site
x
, which makes it reasonable to assume the hypotheses

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,d\,x=1,\hskip 14.40004pt\textrm{for any }\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.1)

since this gives the probability of going from
y
to an arbitry location.
In general, we won’t expect

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,d\,y=1,

(1.2)

since this measures the influx at the site
x
, from all other points, and this may vary (that is, some sites may be ‘more attractive’ than others).
If hypothesis (
1.1
) is assumed, equation (
1
) becomes:

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,u(t,y)\,d\,y-u(t,x)\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

which has been considered by many authors (see
[
6
]
and references therein).

Now, if we consider the problem in a bounded domain
\Omega
, some ‘boundary condition’ must be added for the problem to be well posed.
If we suppose a hostile environment, the species dies if it goes to a site outside
\Omega
. We then impose the solutions to be
identically zero outside the domain
\Omega
, obtaining a nonlocal version of the Dirichlet problem. The evolution equation is the same, except that the integration is restricted to the domain
\Omega
, that is

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)&=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,u(t,y)\,d\,y-u(t,x),\textrm{ if }x\in\Omega,\\
u(t,x)&=0,\textrm{ if }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

If we impose that the interactions occur only inside the domain, we should obtain a version of the Neumann problem. It is, however, not clear how exactly to formulate the problem in this case. A possible choice (see, for example
[
2
]
and
[
6
]
) is to restrict both integrals in (
1
) to the domain
\Omega
, leading to the problem

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)&=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,u(t,y)\,d\,y-\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,u(t,x)\,d\,y\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

This formulation has some nice features. In particular, if

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,d\,y=\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,d\,y
, the constants are equilibrium solutions, which also happens in the local diffusion Neumann problem. This hypothesis obviously holds if
K(x,y)
is symmetric and, in particular, if
K(x,y)=J(x-y)
is of convolution type and
J
is even.

What seems somewhat unsatisfactory with this formulation is that the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,d\,y
cannot expected to be identically equal to
1
, as
required by its interpretation as the probability of transition from
x
to any point in the domain, since the transition to points outside the domain is now forbidden.

We may assume that the kernell
\displaystyle K(x,y)
, instead of condition (
1.1
), now satisfy:

\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,d\,y=1,\hskip 14.40004pt\textrm{for any }\ x\in\Omega.

(1.3)

In fact, starting with a kernell
\displaystyle K(x,y)
satisfying (
1.1
) we can
modify it in various ways in order to obtain a new kernell

\displaystyle\tilde{K(x,y)}
satisfying (
1.3
). For instance, we may define

\tilde{K}(y,x):=\frac{1}{h(x)}K(y,x)\textrm{ with }h(x):=\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,d\,y.

(1.4)

which is well defined if
h(x)\neq 0
.
However, besides the artificiality and lack of motivation for this definition, the kernel thus defined does not in general satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\,d\,y=1
, so the constants are not equilibria. Also,
\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x,y)
is not symmetric, even if

\displaystyle{K}(x,y)
is symmetric. Ideally, one would like the kernell
to satisfy besides the hypothesis (
1.3
), also the identity:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,d\,y

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K(y,x)\,d\,y,\hskip 14.40004pt\textrm{for any }\ x\in\Omega.

(1.5)

This hypothesis is obviously fulfilled if
K
is symmetric.

In dimension one, we can construct a kernel satisfying (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) by ‘reflecting at the boundaries’.
(see
[
5
]
and
[
1
]
).
Starting with a kernel of the form
K(x,y)=J(x-y)
, where
J
is even, has compact support in the interval
[-T,T]
, with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}J(z)\,d\,z=1
,
we define the Neumann kernel in the interval
]0,T[
by

K^{N}(x,y):=J(x-y)+J(x-R(y))+J(x-L(y))

where
R(y)=T+(T-y)=2T-y
is the reflection of
y
with respect to the right end of
I
and

L(y)=-y
is the reflection of
y
with respect to the left end of
I
.

This construction is more natural than the one proposed in
(
1.4
) above and, more importantly, the kernel
K^{N}

is still symmetric
and satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}K^{N}(x,y)\,d\,y=\int_{-T}^{T}J(z)\,d\,z=1.

for any
x\in[0,T]
, so (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) are met.

The purpose of this note is to show that a similar construction is possible in higher dimensions, with the resulting kernel satisfying condition (
1.3
) and also (
1.5
) in the case of some special domains, when it is also symmetric.

2.
Reflecting kernel for
\mathcal{C}^{2}
domains

Suppose
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain, and the kernel
K(x,y))
vanishes outside a neighborhood of the diagonal. More precisely
K(x,y)=0
if
\|x-y\|>\delta
, for some
\delta>0
.
If
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\cdots\varphi_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

are integrable maps from
\Omega
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we may define a new kernel
\tilde{K}(x,y)
by:

\tilde{K}(x,y)=K(x,y)+K(\varphi_{1}(x),y)+K(\varphi_{2}(x),y)+\cdots+K(\varphi_{n}(x),y).

(2.1)

We now investigate some properties of
\tilde{K}
, under appropriate conditions on the maps
\varphi_{k}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
y\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(y)=\{y+x:x\in B_{\delta}(0)\}
be the ball of radius
\delta
around
y
.
Suppose
U_{1},U_{2},\cdots,U_{n}
are open subsets of
\Omega

and

{\varphi_{1}}_{|U_{1}},{\varphi_{2}}_{|U_{2}},\cdots,{\varphi_{n}}_{|U_{n}}
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
injective maps, satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\varphi_{k}(U_{k})\subset\Omega^{c}
and

\varphi_{k}(\Omega\setminus U_{k})\subset\left(B_{\delta}(y)\right)^{c}
,
for
k=1,2,\cdots,n
.

(2)

\Omega^{c}\cap B_{r}(y)\subset\cup_{k=1}^{n}\overline{\varphi_{k}(U_{k})}
, for all
y\in\Omega
and the the sets
\phi_{k}(U_{k})
are disjoint.

(3)

|J\varphi_{k}^{-1}(x)|=1
, for
x\in\varphi_{k}(U_{k})\cap B_{y}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,n
.

Then

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\,d\,x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,d\,x=1,\textrm{\ for any }y\in\Omega.

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{K}(x,y)\,d\,x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}{K}(\varphi_{i}(x),y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{K}(x,y)\,d\,x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{U_{i}}{K}(\varphi_{i}(x),y)\,d\,x+\int_{\Omega\setminus U_{i}}{K}(\varphi_{i}(x),y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,d\,x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\varphi_{i}(U_{i})}K(x,y)\,\,|J\varphi_{i}^{-1}(x)|\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,d\,x+\int_{\cup_{i}\left(\varphi_{i}(U_{i}))\right)}K(x,y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\,d\,x+\int_{\Omega^{c}\cap B_{\delta}(y)}K(x,y)\,d\,x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,d\,x=1

where we have used hypothesis (1) in the third line, hypotheses (2) and (3) in the
fourth line and hypothesis (2) in the fifth line.

{\square}

Remark 2.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, the points
\varphi(x)
can be interpreted as a forbiden location in
\Omega^{c}
for

which then goes to

x

The natural question now is whether one can find reasonable assumptions on the domain
\Omega

so that the conditions of Theorem

We consider first a class of domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the domain in the plane given in polar coordinates by

R:=\{(r,\theta)\ :\ 0\leq r\leq\rho(\theta),0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\},

where
\rho:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a positive
\mathcal{C}^{1}

Let

be the polar transformation of coordinates. Define a map in the

\Psi(\theta,r)=(\theta,\rho(\theta)+\varphi(\rho(\theta)-r))
, where
\varphi
is a real function to be defined later, and

\Phi(x,y)=P\circ\Psi\circ P^{-1}(x,y).

Since
JP(\theta,r)=r
, we have
JP^{-1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{r(x,y)}
, where
r(x,y)=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
.

Also
J\Psi(\theta,r)=-\varphi^{\prime}(\rho(\theta)-r)

Therefore, we obtain

J\Phi(x,y)=-\varphi^{\prime}(\rho(\theta)-r)\frac{\rho(\theta)+\varphi(\rho(\theta)-r)}{r},\ (\theta=\theta(x,y),\ r=r(x,y)).

So
J\Phi(x,y)=-1\Leftrightarrow\varphi^{\prime}(\rho(\theta)-r)=\frac{r}{\rho(\theta)+\varphi(\rho(\theta)-r)}.

Writing

, we arrive at the differential equation:

y_{\theta}^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{r}{\rho(\theta)+y_{\theta}(r)},\hskip 14.40004pty_{\theta}(\rho(\theta))=0,

whose solution is given by

y_{\theta}(r)=-\rho(\theta)+\sqrt{2\rho(\theta)^{2}-r^{2}}
. Thus

\Psi(\theta,r)=(\theta,\sqrt{2\rho(\theta)^{2}-r^{2}}.

The map
\Phi(x,y)=(\sqrt{2\rho(\theta)^{2}-r^{2}}\cos\theta,\sqrt{2\rho(\theta)^{2}-r^{2}}\sin\theta)
is a kind of reflection of each point
(\theta,r)\in\Omega
with respect to the point
(\theta,\rho(\theta))
, composed with a squeezing in the radial direction in order to make it area preserving. It
has

, by construction (and can also be checked by a straightforward, though somewhat lenghty computation).
Also,
\Phi
maps the annulus inside
R

A=R\setminus\{0\}=\{(r,\theta)\ :\ 0<r\leq\rho(\theta),0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\},

onto the annulus outside
R

A^{\prime}=\{(r,\theta):\rho(\theta)\leq r<\sqrt{2}\rho(\theta),0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\},

and reciprocally.
It can be extended to the whole domain
R
by mapping the origin to any point outside
B_{\delta}(y)

Then, if
\delta<\rho_{\min}=\min\{\rho(\theta):0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}
,
the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
are met with
k=1
and
\varphi_{1}=\Phi
.
{\square}

3.
Symmetric kernels

The kernel defined by (
2.1
) is not symmetric in general, even if
K(x,y)
is symmetric, unless the identity:
K(\varphi_{k}(x),y)=K(x,\varphi_{k}(y))
holds, for alll
x,y\in\Omega
and
k=1,2,\cdots,n
.

We now consider some special cases, where it is possible to obtain this property.

Suppose
J:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
has compact support say,
\textrm{supp\,}(J)\subset[-r,r]
and
K(x,y)=J(\|x-y\|)
is of convolution type and define the modified kernel as in (
2.1
), which now reads:

\tilde{K}(x,y)=J(\|x-y\|)+J(\|\varphi_{1}(x)-y\|)+\cdots+J(\|\varphi_{n}(x)-y\|).

(3.1)

Then, one can prove the following result:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and suppose there exist maps

{\varphi_{1}},{\varphi_{2}},\cdots,{\varphi_{n}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(1)

\varphi_{k}(\Omega)\subset\Omega^{c}
, for
k=1,2,\cdots,n
.

(2)

\Omega^{c}\cap B_{r}(y)\subset\cup_{k=1}^{n}\overline{\varphi_{k}(\Omega)}
, for any
y\in\Omega
, for some

r>0
and the the sets
\varphi_{k}(\Omega)
are disjoint.

(3)

\varphi_{k},

k=1,2,\cdots,n
are idempotent isometries

Then, the kernel defined by (
2.1
)
is symmetric and

\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\,d\,x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)\,d\,x=1,

for any
y\in\Omega
.

Proof.

The last assertion was proved in Lemma
2.1
under the hypothesis that the
\varphi_{k}
are area preserving, which is now an immediate consequence of our hypothesis (3). It remains to prove the symmetry. We have

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=J(\|x-y\|)+J(\|\varphi_{1}(x)-y\|)+\cdots+J(\|\varphi_{n}(x)-y\|)

\displaystyle=J(\|x-y\|)+J(\|\varphi_{1}^{2}(x)-\varphi_{1}(y)\|)+\cdots+J(\|\varphi_{n}^{2}(x)-\varphi_{n}(y)\|)

\displaystyle=J(\|x-y\|)+J(\|x-\varphi_{1}(y)\|)+\cdots+J(\|x-\varphi_{n}(y)\|)

\displaystyle=\tilde{K}(y,x).

{\square}
