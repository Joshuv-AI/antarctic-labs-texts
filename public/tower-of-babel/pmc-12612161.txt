Optimization of micro tool geometry for ballpoint pen tip production using finite element simulation and orthogonal experiments
Authors: Yaxuan Bi, Liangjie Tu, Xin Li, Shuai Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicro-drilling in ballpoint pen tip production faces persistent challenges of tool wear and premature breakage, which limit manufacturing efficiency and dimensional accuracy. In this study, an integrated framework combining finite element simulation (FEM) and orthogonal experimental design was developed to optimize the geometry of micro twist drills. A three-dimensional FEM model was established in Deform‑3D to analyze the effects of apex angle, helix angle, chisel edge length, and chisel edge angle on cutting force and torque. Range analysis of the orthogonal design revealed that chisel edge length and apex angle were the most influential parameters. The optimal configuration (b = 0.05 mm, 2Φ = 135°, Ψ = 76.5°, β = 3°) reduced average torque by 22.2% and extended tool life by 43.9% compared with the original design, while maintaining hole dimensional accuracy within ± 0.01 mm. These results confirm FEM as a reliable predictive tool and demonstrate that the proposed FEM–orthogonal framework provides a structured and cost-effective strategy for micro-tool geometry optimization, with direct industrial applicability to precision manufacturing of ballpoint pen tips.Keywords: Micro-drilling, Twist drill optimization, Tool geometry design, Finite element simulation, Deform-3D, Orthogonal experimental design, Ballpoint pen tip manufacturingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, the demand for high-precision micro drills has grown rapidly across industries such as electronics, aerospace, PCB manufacturing, and precision instruments1–3. Micro-hole drilling, especially for holes with diameters less than 1 mm, plays a vital role in producing components like nozzle plates, medical device parts, and ballpoint pen tips4–6. However, the micro-scale nature of these tools introduces unique manufacturing and operational challenges—namely, diminished stiffness, limited torque capacity, and ineffective coolant delivery in deep-hole drilling environments7,8.

Among various microfabrication technologies, micro twist drilling remains one of the most widely adopted methods due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness9–11. Nevertheless, the small size of micro twist drills makes them prone to issues such as uneven cutting edges, asymmetrical geometry, and unstable chip removal12,13. These problems often lead to tool deflection, excessive torque, and premature tool breakage, thereby limiting productivity and compromising hole quality14,15.

The geometry of micro twist drills—including rake angle, point angle, flute shape, web thickness, and helix angle—has a significant impact on cutting force, torque, chip evacuation, tool wear, and fracture cycles16–18. Optimizing these parameters is essential for enhancing machining efficiency and ensuring tool longevity. For example, Yang et al. employed orthogonal cutting simulations combined with an entropy-weight method to determine that rake angles between 19° and 27° effectively reduce stress concentrations at cutting edges, enhancing tool durability19. Ma et al. designed a micro-drilling with an H-shaped chisel edge that introduced a more favorable rake angle distribution and enabled positive cutting action20. This design led to a significant reduction in thrust force and yielded improvements in hole roundness and geometric consistency, demonstrating the engineering advantages of targeted geometry modifications. Similarly, Liang et al. showed that optimizing web thickness and helix angle in helical-point drills reduced thrust force, minimized burr formation, and improved hole dimensional quality21.

In parallel with experimental investigations, the finite element method (FEM) has emerged as a powerful and versatile tool for analyzing and optimizing cutting processes, owing to significant advances in computational capability and modeling platforms22–24. Recent studies have successfully applied FEM to simulate a range of drilling scenarios with high fidelity. For instance, Zhu et al. (2022) utilized a 3D FEM model in Abaqus/Explicit to simulate Ti‑6Al‑4 V drilling, accurately capturing tool temperature distribution and chip morphology25. Hu et al. developed a validated 3D model for the peck drilling of SiCp/Al composites, incorporating realistic tool geometry and chip separation dynamics, which showed strong agreement with experimental torque and thrust data26. Chai et al. (2022) integrated FEM with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to examine the thermal–mechanical effects of cooling strategies in micro-drilling of Ti alloys, revealing the sensitivity of torque and tool wear to lubrication conditions27. Additionally, Dobrotă et al. modeled the turning of AISI 304 stainless steel using DEFORM‑3D, demonstrating how cutting speed and feed influence force and temperature, with simulation errors under 2% compared to experiments28.

These works demonstrate FEM’s capability in simulating cutting phenomena across various materials and drilling configurations. However, despite its proven predictive accuracy, FEM has rarely been applied for the systematic optimization of micro-drilling geometries, especially when multiple interacting design variables must be considered simultaneously. Most prior studies have focused on single-factor analyses or relied on trial-and-error experimentation, which limits efficiency and scalability in industrial applications.

Therefore, this study develops a finite element–based orthogonal experiment framework to optimize the geometry of micro twist drills used in ballpoint pen tip manufacturing. The objective is to reduce cutting force and torque while extending tool life and preserving hole dimensional accuracy. This work not only confirms the predictive capability of FEM in micro-drilling but also introduces a novel integration of FEM with orthogonal experimental design for systematic multi-parameter optimization. Unlike prior studies that mainly relied on single-factor analyses or empirical trial-and-error, the proposed framework establishes a structured and cost-effective methodology that bridges simulation with experimental validation, providing both theoretical guidance and practical relevance for precision manufacturing, particularly in ballpoint pen tip production.

Experiments and methods

Establishment and simulation of finite element model

To investigate the influence of tool geometry on cutting performance, a three-dimensional finite element model of the micro-drilling process was developed using Deform-3D. Figure 1 illustrates the geometric model of the micro twist drill, the detailed view of its key geometric parameters, and the schematic representation of the cylindrical workpiece used in the drilling simulation. The corresponding meshed model is shown in Fig. 2.

(b) Cylindrical workpiece model with an outer diameter of 2.00 mm, an inner hole diameter of 1.25 mm, and a drilling depth of 6.00 mm used in the finite element simulations.

Finite element model after mesh generation.

The twist drill was modeled as a rigid body, which neglects elastic deflection of micro tools. This simplification is widely adopted in short-duration drilling simulations and is acceptable when the objective is to compare geometric effects rather than to capture tool wear or fatigue-induced failure29. The workpiece was modeled as a plastically deformable body using the Johnson–Cook constitutive model, which accounts for strain-rate and temperature dependence of material flow stress30, although it does not explicitly incorporate microstructural effects at the microscale. At the tool–chip interface, a shear friction model with a fixed friction factor (m = 0.5) was applied, following common practice in micro-machining FEM studies. This choice is supported by previous work, where m = 0.5 has been widely adopted and demonstrated to yield stable predictions within the typical range of 0.1–0.9 tested for micro-cutting simulations31. These assumptions may affect the absolute magnitudes of predicted forces and torque but provide a robust basis for comparative analysis across different tool geometries.

Material properties for tungsten carbide (WC) and 316 H stainless steel were obtained from Deform’s material library and cross-verified with literature sources to ensure physical fidelity32. These include density, Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, thermal conductivity, and specific heat. Table 1 summarizes the mechanical and thermal properties used. The chemical composition of 316 H stainless steel is shown in Table 2 and served as the basis for parameter selection in the Johnson–Cook model.

Orthogonal simulation test design

In this study, four key geometric parameters—apex angle, helix angle, chisel edge length, chisel edge angle—were selected because they can be independently controlled during tool grinding and have been shown to exert direct and significant influence on thrust force, torque, and tool life.Other parameters such as rake angle and web thickness were not included in the current design space because they are often coupled with apex angle or chisel edge length in micro-drills, making them difficult to adjust independently without altering multiple geometric features simultaneously.To ensure both experimental feasibility and interpretability, we therefore focused on four key parameters that represent the most direct and practically adjustable factors in micro-drill design. Drilling experiment

To validate the simulation predictions, micro-drilling experiments were conducted. Drill geometry was manually adjusted using a Swiss universal tool grinder (Fig. 3), and each geometry configuration was reproduced in batches of ten tools. The drilling tests were performed using a custom-built high-speed micro-drilling platform designed for ballpoint pen tip fabrication (Fig. 4). The setup ensured precise alignment and stable feed control.

Swiss universal tool grinder used for geometric parameter adjustment.

Drilling test setup (a) Clamped workpiece (b) Micro twist drill tool.

Drilling conditions in the experiments mirrored those used in simulations: spindle speed was 6000 r/min and feed rate was 0.02 mm/rev. Due to the absence of a dynamometer, thrust force and torque could not be directly measured. Instead, tool performance was indirectly assessed by recording the number of holes successfully drilled before tool failure. Failure was defined as either catastrophic breakage or the onset of significant wear that caused drilling instability (e.g., excessive runout, chip evacuation failure, or thermal discoloration). Hole count averages were computed over ten samples per configuration.

In addition to hole count, post-drilling dimensional accuracy was evaluated as a supplementary indicator of hole quality. The nominal hole diameter was D0 = 1.25 mm, and measurements were performed using a calibrated optical microscope. The diameter deviation was defined as ΔD =∣Di−D0∣, with an acceptance threshold of ΔD ≤ 0.01 mm (± 10 μm). For each tool, three holes were randomly selected at early, mid, and near end-of-life stages for measurement. The proportion of holes meeting the dimensional accuracy threshold, as well as the mean ± standard deviation of ΔD, were reported for each parameter configuration.

This experimental approach provided qualitative and semi-quantitative validation of simulation outcomes, reinforcing the predictive utility of FEM-based performance indicators in micro-tool optimization.

Result

Simulation results

Upon completion of each simulation case, the cutting force (Fz), torque (M), and temperature distribution were extracted using the post-processing module in Deform-3D. The data correspond to the stable cutting stage (typically after 600–800 steps), using consistent process settings (n = 6000 r/min, f = 0.02 mm/rev).

As shown in Fig. 5, both Fz and M rapidly increased during initial tool engagement, reaching peak values before gradually stabilizing. This behavior mirrors the actual drilling process, where the onset of chip formation and material deformation leads to initial load spikes followed by a steady-state phase.

Variation of cutting force (Fz) and torque (M) during the cutting process (n = 6000 r/min, f = 0.02 mm/rev).

The cutting force Fz represents the thrust force in the Z-axis direction, while M reflects the torque required to overcome material resistance. These indicators serve as primary evaluation metrics for tool performance in micro-drilling.

Figure 6 illustrates the simulated temperature field on the drill surface. The highest temperatures are concentrated near the chisel edge and cutting lip, where plastic deformation and chip–tool friction are most intense. Although direct experimental measurement of tool temperature was not feasible due to platform limitations, the simulation provides valuable qualitative insight into thermal loading zones, which can guide future cooling or coating strategies.

Temperature distribution of the micro twist drill during the cutting process.

It should be noted that experimental force and torque signals were not recorded in real-time, as the drilling setup lacked in-situ sensing capability. Therefore, Fz and M are used as simulation-based indicators, and their effectiveness is validated indirectly via performance differences observed in drilling experiments.

To further illustrate the results, main effects plots and range plots were generated (Fig. 7). The main effects plots clearly show the influence of different factor levels on mean values of cutting force and torque, making it straightforward to identify the optimal levels (b = 0.05 mm, 2Φ = 135°, Ψ = 76.5°, β = 3°). Together, these graphical results are consistent with the tabular analysis, provide a more intuitive and robust basis for selecting the optimal configuration, and emphasize the importance of optimizing chisel geometry in micro-drilling design.

The number of holes successfully drilled before tool failure was recorded for each tool under identical cutting conditions. Failure was defined as either complete tool breakage or the appearance of significant wear-induced drilling instability (e.g., visible deviation, poor chip evacuation, burning). Each configuration was tested on 10 identical drills, and the average hole count was calculated.

As shown in Fig. 8, the original tool achieved 58.8 ± 3.5 holes. In comparison, C1A1D1B1 reached 73.4 ± 2.3 holes, C1A3D3B2 achieved 80.1 ± 2.9, and the optimal configuration C1A2D2B2 reached 84.6 ± 2.4, showing a 43.9% increase in drilling endurance relative to the original design.

In addition to hole count, post-drilling dimensional accuracy was assessed as a supplementary quality metric. Table 8 summarizes the combined results for tool life and hole dimensional accuracy. The original tool exhibited an average diameter deviation of 0.014 ± 0.004 mm, with 72% of holes meeting the ± 0.01 mm threshold. The optimized configuration (C1A2D2B2) not only produced the longest tool life but also maintained the smallest average deviation (0.009 ± 0.003 mm), with over 90% of holes satisfying the tolerance. These results demonstrate that the optimized drill geometry improved both endurance and hole quality.

Discussion

As shown in Fig. 5, both cutting force (Fz) and torque (M) exhibit a typical trend during micro-drilling: a rapid increase during initial tool–workpiece engagement, followed by a steady-state stage. This behavior is attributed to the progressive establishment of chip formation and stable contact between the tool and workpiece, which is consistent with findings from previous studies17.

The simulated temperature field (Fig. 6) indicates that the highest temperature occurs near the chisel edge and cutting lip, where severe plastic deformation and chip–tool friction are concentrated. This thermal concentration aligns with Guo et al.13, who reported that over 95% of cutting energy is converted into heat, primarily accumulating in these critical regions. These results highlight the importance of tool material selection and potential benefits of coatings or optimized cooling to mitigate localized thermal loading.

Range analysis (Tables 6 and 7) reveals that chisel edge length (b) is the most influential factor affecting both cutting force and torque, followed by apex angle (2Φ), chisel edge angle (Ψ), and helix angle (β). To further illustrate these results, main effects plots and range plots were generated (Fig. 7). The graphical results provide an intuitive view of factor-level influences and confirm the dominance of chisel edge length, emphasizing the importance of optimizing chisel geometry in micro-drilling design. Reducing the chisel edge length decreases the “ploughing effect” at the drill center, thereby reducing thrust force and torque, which is in agreement with prior studies18.

Three parameter combinations were examined:

C1A1D1B1: minimized cutting force (Fz)

C1A1D1B1: minimized cutting force (Fz)

C1A3D3B2: minimized torque (M)

C1A3D3B2: minimized torque (M)

C1A2D2B2: balanced performance

C1A2D2B2: balanced performance

Although reducing thrust force improves hole quality, torque is more critical to tool failure in micro-drilling due to its relationship with tool wear and breakage. Therefore, the balanced configuration (C1A2D2B2) was identified as optimal, demonstrating a 43.9% increase in tool life compared to the original design.

Beyond tool endurance, the experimental results further demonstrated that the optimized configuration (C1A2D2B2) preserved hole dimensional quality. As summarized in Table 8, this configuration not only produced the longest tool life but also maintained the smallest diameter deviation (0.009 ± 0.003 mm) with over 90% of holes meeting the ± 0.01 mm tolerance. In contrast, the original tool showed larger deviations and lower pass rates. This dual improvement—extended tool life and enhanced dimensional accuracy—highlights the robustness of the FEM–orthogonal design framework. More importantly, it underscores the practical industrial relevance of the proposed approach, as both productivity (through longer tool life) and precision (through consistent hole quality) are critical requirements in high-volume applications such as ballpoint pen tip manufacturing.

From an industrial perspective, the optimized geometry (b = 0.05 mm, 2Φ = 135°, Ψ = 76.5°, β = 3°) was successfully reproduced using a universal tool grinder, confirming that such parameters are within the capabilities of conventional tool fabrication. While microscale production tolerances (± 0.005–0.01 mm for linear dimensions and ± 1–2° for angles) may introduce slight variations, these are acceptable in practice and do not alter the relative advantage of the optimized configuration. Moreover, the optimization results are directly applicable to 316 H stainless steel, the standard material for ballpoint pen tips. The observed influence trends of geometric parameters—particularly the dominant roles of chisel edge length and apex angle—are expected to be qualitatively transferable to other stainless steels and softer metals, although the exact optimal combination may shift depending on hardness, thermal conductivity, and strain-hardening behavior. Finally, the 43.9% increase in tool life achieved with the optimized configuration carries clear economic and productivity benefits, including fewer tool changes, reduced machine downtime, and lower tooling costs. While a detailed cost–benefit analysis was not conducted in this study, future work should incorporate such assessment to quantitatively evaluate the economic impact of micro-drill geometry optimization in large-scale production.

These results validate FEM as a reliable tool for predicting drilling performance and guiding geometry optimization. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The present FEM model assumes a rigid tool, which neglects elastic deflection and bending, potentially underestimating stress concentrations and deflection-induced failure. It also neglects progressive tool wear and employs the Johnson–Cook constitutive law, which may not fully capture microscale thermal–mechanical effects. In addition, the orthogonal design used here is efficient for screening main effects but inherently assumes factor independence, thereby limiting its ability to capture higher-order interactions. As a result, certain parameter combinations outside the tested levels could yield even better performance. Future studies should therefore incorporate elastic tool behavior or rigid–flexible coupling, tool wear modeling, direct experimental force/torque measurements, and advanced optimization approaches such as response surface methodology (RSM) or machine learning–based algorithms, which are capable of modeling nonlinear interactions and achieving more comprehensive multi-objective optimization.

Conclusion

In this study, a simulation-driven optimization framework integrating finite element modeling and orthogonal experimental design was proposed to improve the geometric parameters of micro twist drills for ballpoint pen tip manufacturing. By systematically evaluating the effects of apex angle, helix angle, chisel edge length, and chisel edge angle on cutting force and torque, the study demonstrated that chisel edge length and apex angle were the dominant factors influencing drilling performance.

The optimal parameter combination—b = 0.05 mm, 2Φ = 135°, Ψ = 76.5°, and β = 3°—reduced average torque by 22.2% and extended tool life by 43.9% compared to the original design. These findings confirm the reliability of FEM-based predictions for micro-drilling applications and validate the proposed approach as a cost-effective and practical tool design methodology.

Beyond improving tool performance, this research highlights the potential of combining FEM with structured experimental design to accelerate tool development cycles, reduce material waste, and lower experimental costs. Future work will focus on integrating tool wear modeling, temperature effects, and advanced multi-objective optimization techniques such as response surface methodology or machine learning-based approaches to further enhance tool performance and extend the applicability of the proposed framework to other precision manufacturing domains.
