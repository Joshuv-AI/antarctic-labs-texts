Combating antimicrobial resistance in aquaculture: the stakeholders survey in Nigeria
Authors: Bamidele Tolulope Odumosu, Tajudeen Akanji Bamidele, Olanike Maria Buraimoh, Bosede Romiluyi, Babatunde Eniola Emmanuel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aquaculture practices have contributed immensely to the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly in developing countries where antibiotic use is often poorly regulated. This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to assess the perceptions, knowledge, and practices among stakeholders in Nigerian aquaculture regarding AMR, with a particular focus on biosecurity measures. A one-day stakeholder engagement was conducted at the University of Lagos, Nigeria, involving 97 participants, including 40 researchers, 30 aquaculture farmers, 19 government officials, and 8 others. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics including weighted averages, standard deviations, and Pearson’s Chi-square (X2) tests of independence to evaluate associations between stakeholder categories and AMR-related variables. The study revealed a notable reliance on antibiotics for prophylactic disease control, rather than use as growth promoting agent. A significant number of farmers (83.3%) implemented some form of biosecurity, primarily through disinfection procedures, feed checks, and regular health monitoring of stocks. However, the perceived effectiveness of these measures varied among respondents, highlighting challenges in implementing truly effective biosecurity protocols. The study underscores the need for a comprehensive approach to address the growing antimicrobial resistance threat in the Nigerian aquaculture sector, including enhancing understanding of responsible antimicrobial use, promoting alternative disease control strategies, and strengthening surveillance and monitoring systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05567-9.Keywords: Fish farming, Antimicrobial resistance, Disease management, Stakeholder engagement, Public health

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global problem, and it represents a multifaceted threat to the One Health concept, particularly within the fast-growing aquaculture sectors of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Nigerian aquaculture, the drive for rapid production expansion, and insufficient biosecurity infrastructure creates a high-risk environment for the selection and dissemination of resistant pathogens. Compared to the terrestrial livestock system, the aquatic environment serves as a superior hotspot for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of antibiotic resistant genes across diverse bacterial populations [1, 2]. Recognizing the environmental link to AMR, the European Union has emphasized a One Health approach in its Action Plans [3] in parallel, Nigeria’s second National Action Plan (NAP 2.0) on AMR [4] explicitly included aquaculture, underscoring the growing recognition of aquatic systems in national and international AMR strategies.

The aquaculture sector is rapidly expanding worldwide, serving as a critical source of protein and contributing to food security. According to data released by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), aquacultural products account for 17% of the total proteins obtained from food-producing animals for human consumption globally [5]. In Nigeria, the value of aquaculture contribution to the economy is estimated at approximately $2.6 billion annually, making aquaculture one of the fastest-growing agricultural business in the country [6]. However, this growth is accompanied by peculiar AMR challenges, as studies show that antibiotic misuse is prevalent among the practitioners in Nigeria, thus threatening both its productivity and sustainability. Reports from Eastern and Northern part of Nigeria, for example, highlight frequent antibiotic use without adequate knowledge of AMR risks [7, 8]. Despite the sector’s economic relevance, national-level data on AMR in aquaculture remain scarce, and systematic surveillance is limited.

Evidence from other African countries emphasizes the urgency of addressing AMR in aquaculture. Comparative studies in Egypt, and Ghana revealed similar trends of antibiotic misuse, weak biosecurity practices, and limited regulatory enforcement [9, 10]. A recent systematic review of antimicrobial use and resistance in African aquaculture concluded that data gaps and poor monitoring systems are common across the continent, posing significant risks to public health [11]. These findings underscore the need for Nigeria to generate data- based evidence to create more awareness, inform policy and strengthen its aquaculture practices.

To bridge this knowledge gap, this study convened a stakeholder engagement workshop in Lagos, Nigeria, involving relevant government officials, medical researchers, active aquaculture farmers, and other stakeholders. The workshop aimed to evaluate perceptions, knowledge, and practices related to antimicrobial use and biosecurity in aquaculture. Specifically, this research sought to determine if significant disparities exist in AMR awareness levels across different stakeholder categories and to identify the specific farm-level practices that drive the continued reliance on prophylactic antimicrobial use. We hypothesized that while general awareness of AMR as a public health threat would be high and uniform across all stakeholder groups, the practical implementation of biosecurity measures would remain inconsistent and heavily influenced by the specific challenges of individual farming operations. By analyzing data collected through questionnaires and discussions, this study provides insights into stakeholder priorities and identifies opportunities for improving AMR management in Nigeria’s aquaculture sector.

Methodology

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted during a workshop comprising of 97 participants recruited using a purposive sampling technique to ensure that all respondents are directly involved or have expertise in the Nigerian aquaculture value chain. While priority was given to farmers participations, inclusion criteria required that farmers be actively engaged in commercial fish production, while researchers and government officials were selected based on their professional roles in health, microbiology, or agricultural policy regulation. Recruitment was conducted through an open call and professional networks, with formal invitations extended via email and digital communication platforms. This targeted approach was designed to capture a diverse range of perspectives from stakeholders with the technical capacity to provide informed responses regarding antimicrobial administration and biosecurity implementation. The workshop was designed to cover topics related to AMR in aquaculture, including the current state of AMR, risk factors, prevention and control measures, and policy frameworks. Interactive exercises such as expository talks, case studies, and scenario-based discussions were conducted to stimulate participants’ interest and awareness of the workshop’s purpose.

Participants completed the questionnaire independently, either manually or electronically, and submitted responses to the organizers at the University of Lagos.

The total population size (120 invited stakeholders) was disclosed to ensure representativeness. The final sample size of 97 was justified based on purposive sampling, prioritizing active fish farmers to balance academic and practical perspectives. While pre-workshop learning activities were included to enhance engagement, this may have introduced bias, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 for descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations). To determine the association between disease occurrence frequency and the adopted treatment methods, a Pearson’s Chi-square (X2) test was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants demographics

A total of 97 participants were recruited, including 40 researchers (41.24%), 30 aquaculture farmers (30.93%), 19 government officials (19.60%), and 8 others (8.30%; private consultants (Fig. 1). The average years of experience in the aquaculture industry among the participating fish farmers was 12.5 years (SD = 5.2).

Disease occurrence and treatment and source of antibiotic use

Table 2 summarizes farmers’ responses on disease occurrences in their farms, and were asked to provide information on how diseases were treated and the method adopted for treatment if antibiotics were used or not. Among the respondents, 40% reported rarely or occasionally encountering disease outbreaks in their farms, despite this, antibiotic treatment was the predominant response when disease occurred. Of the 30 farmers surveyed, 60% reported using commercial antibiotics, while non-antibiotic methods (e.g., herbal remedies, probiotics, water quality management) were rarely used. However, the weighted average antibiotic usage across all different treatment methods was 19.57% at a 95% confidence interval [13.82%, 25.31%], while the standard deviation was 28.13. There was a statistically significant association between disease occurrence frequency and the adopted treatment practices (χ² = 80.32, df = 4, p < 0.001). Majority of the farmers responding no disease occurrence (n = 30) did not administer treatment, whereas those who responded rare or occasional outbreaks (80% and 60%, respectively) were more likely to use antibiotics. The common reason for antibiotic use among the farmers was prophylactic treatment (disease prevention in farming), with 60% of farmers reporting prophylactic as the primary source of antibiotics use on the farm, 50% of farmers use antibiotics for treatment of disease while 16.7% use antibiotics for growth promotion in aquaculture. Although the survey captured data on specific antibiotic types and recommendation sources, these variables were excluded from the primary analysis because of high variability and incomplete responses in the ‘specific antibiotic types’ of categories to maintain the study’s focus on the main ‘knowledge-action gap’ and biosecurity implementation.

Biosecurity practices in aquaculture

Table 4 presents farmers on biosecurity measures implemented on their farms; a majority of the farmers (83.3%) reported implementing biosecurity practices including disinfection, feed checks, visitor control, and regular health checks. However, while participation in these individual activities was high, the mean implementation rate across the five core protocols was 49.34% (SD = 29.28%). This composite metric, calculated by averaging the adoption frequencies of the specific measures surveyed, indicates that many participants only utilize a subset of recommended protocols rather than a comprehensive system. Regarding the perceived efficacy of these actions, a low percentage of the farmers (16.7%) considered their implemented biosecurity measures as “very effective,” while 50% responded “effective” and 26.7% reported “moderately effective.” Only 16.7% of the cohort reported carrying out no biosecurity procedures. A Pearson’s Chi-square test revealed a significant association between the number of measures implemented and the perceived level of effectiveness (p < 0.05), suggesting that farmers who adopt a more diverse suite of protocols have higher confidence in their farm’s biosecurity status.

Discussion

Mitigating AMR in aquaculture requires a One Health strategy that encompasses diverse interventions. These include adopting alternative disease control methods and biosecurity protocols, alongside strengthening surveillance systems and educational outreach among stakeholders. In Nigeria, the situation pertaining to antibiotic prescription and irrational use is worrisome, this is complicated by poor policy framework to tackle the menace. In a rational use survey conducted in twelve (12) developing countries in 1993, the country represented the highest average number of prescription (3.8 drugs/encounter) and 3rd highest percentage (48%) of antibiotics prescription [12]. These alarming metrics against the country prompted the institution of National Action Plan (NAP) which set up an AMR surveillance system using a One Health approach [4]. In all these, however, there is no such intervention policy tailored specifically towards the aquaculture sector but rather it is lumped up with the Ministry of Agriculture.

In this study, our findings highlight various critical concerns regarding AMR in the Nigerian aquaculture sector. Two notable findings from the present study is the widespread perception among stakeholders that AMR poses a significant threat to the aquaculture industry and a moderate implementation of biosecurity level. This high level of awareness underscores the importance of addressing AMR within the sector. Food production environments in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have been recognized as one of the major contributors to the increasing AMR threat to global public health and food security systems [13]. These findings provide timely empirical support for the second phase of NAP 2.0 on AMR, which explicitly integrates aquaculture into the national One Health surveillance framework. Given a high-level awareness (92.8%) among the stakeholders in the aquaculture value chain, the present findings on AMR risks, our data validates the strategic priorities of the National One Health Strategic Plan, confirming that the social readiness for intervention exists across the aquaculture sector. However, the present study identified a disconnect between this high awareness and the moderate implementation of biosecurity (49.34%) which highlights a critical area for policy focus.

One of the major findings in the present study is the irony between low reported disease occurrence and high reliance on antibiotics. Despite 60–80% of farmers reporting ‘rare’ or ‘occasional’ disease outbreaks, antibiotic treatment remained the predominant response suggesting heavy use of antibiotics prophylactically rather than therapeutically, a practice that accelerates the selection pressure and spreads of resistant bacteria in the environment [14].This observation corresponds with findings from previous studies in other climes, which have documented the misuse and overuse of antibiotics in aquaculture [15–17]. This paradox suggests that antimicrobial administration is being utilized as a primary risk mitigation strategy to compensate for perceived vulnerabilities in pond-level biosecurity. Given that the mean biosecurity implementation rate was moderate (49.34%), it means farmers may likely adopt a preventive approach to health management, by prioritizing the suppression of subclinical pathogens to protect their total fish biomass for profit taking. This behaviour suggests that in climes such as Nigeria, where diagnostic infrastructure is limited and the economic cost of stock mortality is high, farmers may rationalize the use of antimicrobials as a replacement for robust preventative hygiene.

Alternative treatment methods such as probiotics, vaccines and herbal remedies to lessen reliance on antibiotics use were observed to be lacking or underutilized among the farmers in this study, suggesting a knowledge gap and/or limited access to these approaches. Thus, there is a need for more aggressive awareness and educational program which will facilitate their understanding and stimulate their interest in sustainable aquacultural practices and the widespread promotion of alternative disease control strategies such as vaccines, probiotics and biosecurity measures. We also investigated antibiotic use among farmers and the mean percentage of use across different methods was 20% with a standard deviation of 24.64 suggesting a significant variation in patterns among the participants. The variation in the methods suggests different factors such farm size and practices, production systems, access to resources and individual farmer practices may have a significant influence in decision whether to use antibiotics or not.

We also examined the implementation of biosecurity measures among the farmers. It was interesting to know that a large percentage (83.3%) of the farmers reported implementing some form of biosecurity such as disinfection procedures, feed and water source control, visitors control, waste management control, and regular health checks as their common biosecurity practices. However, the perceived effectiveness of these measures varied among all the farmers, while 16.7% of farmers considered the biosecurity measures “very effective,” 50% felt the measures are ‘effective’ while 6.7% among the farmers felt the measures are ‘Not very effective’.

Global best practices, such as Norway’s strict biosecurity protocols and Vietnam’s adoption of vaccination programs [18], demonstrate that consistent and well-supported measures can reduce antibiotic dependence. Nigeria can adapt these models by providing farmers with training, resources, and clear guidelines. The findings also underscore gaps in awareness. While most stakeholders recognized AMR as a threat, awareness did not translate into responsible practices. This highlights the need to move beyond general awareness campaigns toward capacity-building initiatives that equip farmers with practical alternatives, such as probiotics, vaccines, and improved water management.

Unlike previous studies that has extensively documented the prevalence of resistant pathogens in Nigerian aquaculture, the present findings contribute to the existing knowledge of AMR in West African aquaculture by providing a multi-sectoral analysis of the ‘knowledge-action gap.’ This study also reports significant impact for the Nigerian aquacultural sector, particularly the continued reliance on antibiotics for prophylaxis purpose during disease outbreak in the farm. Effective biosecurity implementation will prevent disease outbreak and thus minimize the reliance on antibiotics either for prevention or treatment. The continuous use of antibiotics poses great danger to the sector, to both animal and human health, and to a larger extent, the environment and this can lead to the emergence and dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. This is due to the amount of unmetabolized antibiotics residues that are consumed from aquaculture products and from those that are released into the environment from the effluents, which have detrimental effects on aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.

Recommendations

To address these concerns, we recommend an interdisciplinary approach to promote responsible antimicrobial use through the formal implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs and the enforcement of stricter regulations to ensure the judicious use of antibiotics across Nigerian food production systems. This regulatory framework should specifically prioritize transitioning away from unregulated over-the-counter antibiotic access toward a system requiring professional clinical oversight to manage the high rates of prophylactic use identified in this study. By integrating these stewardship protocols into the broader ‘farm-to-plate’ safety chain, policymakers can better monitor antimicrobial consumption and mitigate the risk of resistance within the aquaculture sector.

Furthermore, we strongly recommend that government move beyond awareness-building via NAP and prioritize improvement of “veterinary advisory extension” and “antimicrobial stewardship” protocols necessary to translate national policy into standardized farm-level practices. The national veterinary advisory and extension system is essential to provide standardized training and educational programs for farmers on alternative disease control strategies, such as vaccination, probiotics, and herbal solutions. This advisory system would bridge the gap in biosecurity effectiveness by providing farm-level audits and health monitoring, thereby reducing the reliance on reactive treatment methods that necessitate antibiotic intervention. Consequently, reinforcing such a robust surveillance system and monitoring network for antimicrobial use in aquaculture must be prioritized to ensure the long-term sustainability of the industry and the safety of food for human consumption in Nigeria.

Limitations

The present study acknowledges certain limitations in which should be stated clearly. Firstly, even though the stakeholder engagement focused on diverse representatives, researchers constituted a significant portion (n = 40; 41.24%) well above the farmers (n = 30; 30.93%). This population, even though not a majority but, may have introduced a potential academic bias in responses for Table 1, which focuses on the general perception of AMR as a threat. However, it is important to note that Tables 2, 3 and 4 specifically present data collected from the farmers (n = 30) thereby alleviating the potential for self-reporting bias response from participants. Secondly, while the survey intends to gather diverse opinions of the stakeholders, the sample size of farmers (n = 30; 30.93%) and government officials (n = 19; 19.59%) representation is relatively low and this was due to low turnout and willingness to participate in the survey. We also acknowledged the instructional nature of the workshop format, which included academic sessions on AMR and biosecurity prior to questionnaire completion which may likely form bias for response, this carried out to provide a common technical vocabulary for stakeholders with varying levels of formal training.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a substantial reliance on antibiotics in Nigerian aquaculture, often used prophylactically despite relatively low disease occurrence. Such practices contribute to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance, posing risks to animal health, human health, and the environment. While most farmers reported implementing biosecurity measures, their perceived effectiveness varied, highlighting gaps in consistency and enforcement.

By engaging diverse stakeholders, this study provides the first structured evidence of perceptions, practices, and priorities regarding AMR in Nigerian aquaculture. The findings emphasize the urgent need for targeted interventions that move beyond general awareness to practical, sustainable solutions. Future studies will focus on capacity-building initiatives, training programs, or evaluation of alternative disease management strategies.
