Comparative efficacy of non-pharmacological management for chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome: a systematic review and network meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Zongshi Qin, Chao Zhang, Xinyao Wei, Jiaming Cui, Yanlan Yu, Ran Pang, Xiao Li, Joey S W Kwong, R. Christopher Doiron, J. Curtis Nickel, Jiani Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) has posed a significant burden on affected individuals and healthcare systems. While pharmacological treatments are commonly used, non-pharmacological management strategies have gained attention for their potential benefits in improving CP/CPPS symptoms. However, the comparative efficacy of these non-pharmacological interventions remains unclear. The aim of this study is to assess the comparative effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions for CP/CPPS regarding prostatic symptoms.MethodThis systematic review and network meta-analysis will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. A comprehensive search will be conducted in electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science, to identify relevant studies. Eligible studies will include randomised controlled trials investigating non-pharmacological management strategies for CP/CPPS. Two independent reviewers will screen the retrieved citations, extract data and assess the risk of bias. Data synthesis will involve performing a network meta-analysis to compare the efficacy of different non-pharmacological interventions while considering both direct and indirect evidence.Ethics and disseminationThe review does not require ethical approval. The findings of the review will be disseminated through publication in an academic journal, presentations at conferences and various media outlets.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024506143.

Introduction

Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) presents a substantial burden to affected individuals and healthcare systems worldwide.1 The exact prevalence of CP/CPPS is unknown due to variations in diagnostic criteria, evaluation tools and symptom overlap with other conditions such as benign prostate hyperplasia and primary bladder pain syndrome. It was reported from a minimum of 1.8% in population-based prevalence of prostatitis symptoms.26 Characterised by persistent pelvic pain and discomfort, urinary symptoms and sexual dysfunction, CP/CPPS significantly impacts the quality of life of those afflicted.7 Despite its prevalence and clinical significance, the optimal management of CP/CPPS remains elusive.8 9

Pharmacological interventions have traditionally been the management for CP/CPPS.10 A previous systematic review and network meta-analysis concluded that that alpha blockers, antibiotics and combinations of these therapies appear to achieve the greatest improvement in terms of clinical symptom scores compared with a placebo.11 While the findings from a systematic review and network meta-analysis included 25 studies (26 treatments, 3514 patients) that have shown pharmacological treatments have little evidence supporting efficacy in CP/CPPS, all the oral drug monotherapies were not superior to the placebo regarding the patient-reported outcome measurements.12 Before the publication of the network meta-analysis, the story of ineffectiveness of oral pharmacological treatment for CP/CPPS is an old news. For many years, the largest National Institutes of Health (NIH)-sponsored randomised controlled trials (RCT) have shown no support for the use of the alpha blockers, such as alfuzosin or tamsulosin, or the antibiotic ciprofloxacin, either alone or in combination with tamsulosin.13 14 There is growing recognition of the importance of non-pharmacological approaches in addressing the multifaceted nature of this condition for many years.101517 Non-pharmacological interventions encompass a broad spectrum of strategies, including acupuncture,18 physical therapy,19 psychotherapy,20 neuromodulation21 and lifestyle modifications, such as exercise, diet and avoiding a sedentary lifestyle.22 23 However, the clinical practice guidelines do not provide clear recommendations about non-pharmacological management,24 and the comparative efficacy of these interventions remains unclear.2527

A comprehensive Cochrane systematic review was conducted in 2017 which included 38 studies with 3290 patients across 23 comparisons. It found that acupuncture and extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) are likely to result in a decrease in prostatitis symptoms and may not be associated with a greater incidence of adverse events.28 Some systematic reviews have also found that a certain non-pharmacological management is more effective than others. Acupuncture may lead to a clinically meaningful reduction in prostatitis symptoms compared with sham controls, standard medical therapy or medication.18 28 ESWT has shown similar results to acupuncture.16 However, the main limitation of traditional pairwise meta-analysis is that the results were qualitatively summarised, augmenting findings with quantitative analyses only if head-to-head data were sufficient. Thus, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses of non-pharmacological treatments for CP/CPPS do not address how other interventions, such as neuromodulation, compared with placebo, beyond acupuncture and ESWT. Also, the question of who might have the most efficacy of all non-pharmacological treatments is needed to be answered. To address this gap in knowledge, we will conduct an updated systematic review and network meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the comparative efficacy of non-pharmacological management in CP/CPPS regarding prostatic symptoms. Network meta-analysis is a statistical technique that allows both direct and indirect comparisons to be undertaken, even when two of the treatments have not been directly compared.29 By synthesising evidence from existing RCTs and employing mixed statistical methods, we aim to provide insights into the relative effectiveness of various non-pharmacological interventions, thereby informing clinical decision-making and optimising patient outcomes. To our knowledge, this will be the first network meta-analysis on individual non-pharmacological treatment for CP/CPPS. The current study will extend the findings of previous systematic review and pairwise meta-analysis.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This protocol is written in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocol statement (supplemental file 1).30 Any amendments to this protocol will be recorded in an updated version of the PROSPERO registration (CRD42024506143). The study has started the literature searching; the full project is anticipated to be finished in October 2025.

Patient and public involvement

No patient will be involved in the design and conduct of this research.

Types of studies

We will include RCTs comparing different non-pharmacological interventions as monotherapy or combination or sham/placebo non-pharmacological intervention (such as sham stimulation, sham acupuncture, etc) for the treatment of CP/CPPS. Trials with more than a two-arm design will be considered only if the available data meet the criteria for intervention (acupuncture, biofeedback, cryotherapy, ESWT, electromagnetic therapy, neuromodulation, psychotherapy, physical activity and their corresponding sham therapy/placebo). For trials with a cross-over design, data will only be extracted from the first randomisation period to avoid the carryover effects. Because transitivity and similarity are a vital methodology in network meta-analysis, the comparison between pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments may introduce potential bias into the results, thus this study will not include studies that compared pharmacological with non-pharmacological treatment.

Types of participants

Only trials that enrolled participants with a diagnosis of CP/CPPS or non-bacterial chronic prostatitis according to standardised criteria will be sought.27 31 32 In detail, diagnosed from a history of pain perceived in the region of the prostate, and absence of other lower urinary tract pathology, for a minimum of 3 months. Specific disease-associated pelvic pain will be excluded. For studies using other extensive criteria for the diagnosis, detailed diagnostic criteria should be reported.

Types of interventions

This systematic review and meta-analysis will include a maximum range of non-pharmacological interventions for CP/CPPS. Acupuncture involves the insertion of needles into specific points on the body, which are manipulated manually by the practitioner. Electroacupuncture adds the application of electric currents through these needles.18 Electronerve stimulation techniques, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (PENS), differ in their method of delivery, with TENS using non-penetrating electrodes and PENS involving needles inserted through the skin to deliver electric currents.21 Biofeedback techniques use electronic monitoring to help patients regulate physiological functions such as heart rate, muscle tension and skin temperature.19 ESWT, an acoustic wave therapy, delivers shockwaves that can be focused non-invasively on a targeted anatomical region, typically used for treating deep tissue conditions.33 Manual stimulation, such as prostatic massage, is employed to alleviate symptoms and improve circulation in patients with prostate issues. Additionally, manual therapy techniques aimed at releasing tension in the myofascial tissues will also be included.34 Psychotherapy interventions, including cognitive–behavioural therapy, interpersonal therapy, problem-solving therapy, play therapy, supportive counselling, psychodynamic therapy and family therapy, will be considered.20 Structured exercise programmes, designed to enhance overall physical fitness, include specific exercises targeting the pelvic floor muscles.23 Electromagnetic therapy uses electromagnetic fields to promote healing and pain relief in targeted areas, functioning as a form of neuromodulation by influencing pain pathways.35 Laser therapy involves the application of low-level lasers to reduce pain and inflammation while promoting tissue healing.36 The integration of multiple non-pharmacological interventions, such as combining acupuncture with physical activity, will also be explored.

Types of outcome measures

The primary outcome is the NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI) total score, which ranges from 0 to 43 points; a higher score indicates a more severity symptom. As a validated, self-reported questionnaire, NIH-CPSI is widely used to assess CP/CPPS symptoms. NIH-CPSI consists of nine items divided into three discrete domains: pain (0–21 points), urinary symptoms (0–10 points) and quality of life (0–12 points), with a total score of 43 points.37 The follow-up data will also be extracted if available. The data analysis will use the data at the end of treatment.

Secondary outcomes include (1) response to treatment, defined as the proportion of patients who showed 4–6 points or 30–50% reduction in the NIH-CPSI total score after 4 weeks of treatment38; (2) NIH-CPSI subscores; (3) mental health-related outcomes such as Self-Rating Anxiety Scale, Self-Rating Depression Scale, Hamilton Anxiety Scale, Hamilton Depression Scale and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; and (4) sexual dysfunction such as International Index of Erectile Function-5.

Information sources and search strategy

We will identify RCTs through a comprehensive, systematic literature search primarily using the PubMed, Embase, PEDro (Physiotherapy Evidence Database), Cochrane Library and Web of Science databases from their inception to 11 August 2024 without language restriction. Also, the ClinicalTrials.gov will be searched for potential eligible study. We will use Medical Subject Headings and text words related to ‘chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome’ and ‘randomized controlled trial’ for the literature search. In addition, the reference lists of previous systematic reviews will be examined to ensure the quantity and accuracy of the included studies.12 28 Search strategies were described in detail in online supplemental file 2.

Study selection and data extraction

Two persons will scan the titles and abstracts of the trials after duplicated records have been excluded using EndNote V.X7 (Thomson Reuters, New York City, New York). The scanning will be performed using EndNote, and all trials will be allocated to the following five groups: inclusion group (the potential eligible studies), non-patient group (studies without patients with CP/CPPS), non-intervention group (studies without target interventions), non-outcome group (studies without target outcomes) and awaiting group (studies waiting for the full-text screening). A prior data collection process will be conducted using an electrotable created with Microsoft Excel 365 software (Microsoft, Seattle, USA), which has been used in our previous study.12 Disagreements will be resolved through a team discussion. All original data will be submitted as an attachment.

Dealing with missing data

For missing data, we will attempt to obtain more information from the original authors. In the absence of a reply, we will try to calculate the data through the available coefficients according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews.39 For continuous outcomes, SDs will be estimated by SEs, p values or CIs, depending on how the original research is provided. Otherwise, SDs will be evaluated based on the median or IQR. If studies did not provide change-from-baseline data, we will estimate the change value using the baseline and end-of-treatment data. When continuous variables were missing, we will use SDs borrowed from other similar RCTs to calculate the missing SDs. Whenever necessary, we will approximate means and measures of dispersion from plots. The potential impact of these missing data will be assessed by sensitivity analysis. We will not exclude the studies with insufficient data; we will describe these studies in the supplementary file and explain the reasons why they will not be included in the meta-analysis.

Assessment of quality

Two reviewers will independently assess the quality and risk of bias of the included studies by using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool in the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting of results and other sources of bias. Two reviewers will complete the RoB assessment independently, and any differences will be resolved by a consensus.

Assessment of inconsistency

If there is no inconsistency in comparison of node, we will use a consistency model to analyse the data. For cases of significant incoherence, we will initially look for data extraction errors in comparison of node that present inconsistency and in comparisons with large heterogeneity. After the data have been scrutinised, we will investigate possible sources of inconsistency within clinical and methodological variables suspected of being potential sources of either heterogeneity or incoherence in each comparison-specific group of trials. If an important inconsistency cannot be explained, we will consider avoiding the synthesis of the related network.

Assessment of transitivity and similarity

we will investigate similarity based on clinical characteristics, such as the period of treatment, severity of symptoms at baseline, as well as according to methodological characteristics such as study quality. All these effect modifiers will be judged and reported before the network meta-analysis is conducted.

Data synthesis and analysis

The method used for data synthesis will be based on mixed treatment meta-analysis. To examine comparisons, we will use Stata (V.15.0; Stata, College Station, Texas, USA) to synthesise data and will present the comparison results if the included studies are sufficient for each pairwise comparison. We will use a random effects model to combine the data in indirect and network comparisons, and the outcomes of continuous and binary variables will be presented as weighted mean differences or relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs as appropriate. For indirect comparisons, a random effects model network meta-analysis will be employed. This will enable us to estimate the best probability for each intervention for each positive outcome, given the results of the multiple-treatment meta-analysis. To describe relationships among different treatments, a network plot will be created to show direct comparisons between arms based on different outcomes. In addition, the effectiveness of each treatment among all available treatments will be ranked by calculating the effect sizes in order, and plots of the surfaces under the cumulative ranking curves will be generated to rank the various treatments for each outcome. To test the heterogeneity of each pairwise comparison, we will use the I² statistic, which will be deemed as significant heterogeneity if p<0.05 or I2>40%. The qualitative analysis will be performed if the data are insufficient.
