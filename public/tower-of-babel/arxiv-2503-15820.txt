The Deligne Complex for the B 3 B_{3} Artin Group
Katherine Goldman, Amy Herron

Abstract
. We show that the piecewise Euclidean Moussong metric on the Deligne complex of the Artin group of type B 3 B_{3} is CAT ⁡ ( 0 ) \mathrm{CAT}(0) . We do this by establishing a criteria for a complex made of B 3 B_{3} simplices to be CAT ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{CAT}(1) in terms of embedded edge paths, which in particular applies to the spherical Deligne complex of type B 3 B_{3} . This provides one more step to showing that the Moussong metric is CAT ⁡ ( 0 ) \mathrm{CAT}(0) for any 3-dimensional Artin group.



1.
Introduction

Artin groups are closely tied to Coxeter groups.
While Coxeter groups are largely well-understood, there are still a plethora of open questions regarding the Artin groups.
One of the most prominent of these is the
K(\pi,1)
conjecture, which essentially asks if a specific space is an Eilenberg-MacLane space for a given Artin group.
This turns out to be equivalent to asking if an object called the “(modified) Deligne complex” is contractible
[
CD95
]
.
One common technique in geometric group theory and metric geometry for showing spaces are contractible is to show that they possess some notion of non-positive curvature.
Recent success has been had using various new ideas of non-positive curvature
[
PS21
,
Hae23
,
Hua24
]
.
But it has long been conjectured that Deligne complexes are
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
under a specific metric, typically called the Moussong metric, defined in terms of the Davis complex of the associated Coxeter group
[
CD95
, Conj. 3]
. Very few Artin groups are known to satisfy this; in fact, it is only known when the group is 2-dimensional
[
CD95
, Thm. 4]
or if the only “spherical-type” subdiagrams of its Coxeter-Dynkin diagram are either edges or
A_{3}

[
Cha04
]
.

It is notoriously difficult to show that piecewise spherical cell complexes of dimension
\geq 2
are
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
.
Broadly, this is because it requires establishing that any loop of length
<2\pi
is not a (local) geodesic. In
[
EM02
]
, a method is presented for showing arbitrary 2-dimensional piecewise spherical simplicial complexes are
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
, via excluding certain “galleries”. Although an explicit list of galleries is given, it is quite large, and generally unfeasible to apply to Artin groups directly.
In
[
Cha04
]
, this is overcome by cleverly using the geometry of
A_{3}
simplices and the relationship of the Deligne complex with the Coxeter complex to reduce the study of all galleries to just a few which stay in the 1-skeleton. This is the inspiration for our current work.

Our method for simplifying the study of general loops to edge paths is made somewhat more simple by using a slightly altered criteria developed by Bowditch for a locally
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
space to be
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
. This criteria says that in order for a locally
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
space to be
\mathrm{CAT}(1)
, all short loops must be “shrinkable” to the trivial loop. Since shrinkability is an equivalence relation, we are able to easily reduce to the edge paths by showing that an arbitrary short loop is shrinkable to a short closed edge path.

While we perform a similar reduction to edge paths as in the
A_{3}
case, there are a number of other paths that now must be ruled out.
To show that the spherical Deligne complex for the braid group on four strands is CAT(1), Charney needed to essentially consider two configurations of geodesic edge paths; one of edge length 4, and one of edge length 6.
For the spherical Deligne complex of type
B_{3}
, one must consider paths of edge length 4, 6, 8, and 10.
The geometry of the spherical Deligne complex of type
B_{3}
also becomes much less friendly than type
A_{3}
, which requires the implementation of new tools to analyze these edge paths.
We are able to confirm that all edge paths are shrinkable, and therefore, we add
B_{3}
to the collection of Artin groups with
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
Deligne complex:

3.
Background on Artin groups and Coxeter groups

We can now focus on the main application of this result: the Deligne complex of an Artin group of type
B_{3}
. In this section, we introduce the notation that will be used throughout the rest of the paper.

A
Coxeter-Dynkin diagram
is a finite simplicial graph
\Gamma
equipped with a labeling
m:E(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 3}\cup\{\infty\}
of edges. Since
\Gamma
is simplicial, every edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
can be written as an unordered pair of vertices
\{s,t\}
, and sometimes we write
m(s,t)=m(t,s)=m(\{s,t\})=m(e)
. If
\{s,t\}\not\in E(\Gamma)
, define
m(s,t)=m(t,s)=2
. When illustrating a Coxeter-Dynkin diagram, it is common to omit labels on edges
e
with
m(e)=3
.

Such a diagram defines a Coxeter group

W(\Gamma)=\langle\,s\in V(\Gamma)\mid(st)^{m(s,t)}=1\text{ if }m(s,t)\not=\infty,s^{2}=1\,\rangle

and an Artin group

A(\Gamma)=\langle\,s\in V(\Gamma)\mid\underbrace{sts\ldots}_{m(s,t)\text{ letters}}=\underbrace{tst\ldots}_{m(s,t)\text{ letters}}\text{ if }m(s,t)\not=\infty\,\rangle.

We call a Coxeter-Dynkin diagram
\Gamma

spherical
and an Artin group
A(\Gamma)

spherical-type
if
W(\Gamma)
is finite.

We will write
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
to denote a full subgraph
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
(i.e., if two vertices
x,y\in V(\Lambda)
are adjacent in
\Gamma
, then they are also adjacent in
\Lambda
) which inherits the edge labeling of
\Gamma
. Then
A(\Lambda)
is an Artin group which is isomorphic to the subgroup of
\Gamma
generated by
V(\Lambda)
by sending the generators of
A(\Lambda)
to the generators coming from the same vertex viewed inside
\Gamma

[
vdL83
]
. By abuse of notation, we will identify these two groups. A similar statement holds for the Coxeter groups
W(\Lambda)
and
W(\Gamma)
.
A particular type of full subgraphs are the
maximal
full subgraphs. If
s\in V(\Gamma)
, then we let
\hat{s}
denote the full subgraph of
\Gamma
on vertex set
V(\Gamma)\setminus s
. By abusing notation, we sometimes let
\hat{s}=A(\hat{s})
or
\hat{s}=W(\hat{s})
; the meaning will be obvious by context.

For any Coxeter-Dynkin diagram
\Gamma
, we may associate a number of relevant cell complexes, two of which are the
Deligne complex
and the
Davis-Moussong complex
. Let
G=A(\Gamma)
or
W(\Gamma)
.
We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{f}

\displaystyle=\{\,\Lambda\leq\Gamma:\Lambda\text{ is spherical}\,\},and

\displaystyle G\mathcal{S}^{f}

\displaystyle=\{\,gA(\Lambda):g\in G,\Lambda\in\mathcal{S}^{f}\,\},

both partially ordered by standard inclusion. Since
G\mathcal{S}^{f}
is a poset, we can form its derived poset
(G\mathcal{S}^{f})^{\prime}
, the set of linearly ordered subsets (or
chains
) of
G\mathcal{S}^{f}
, itself ordered by inclusion. Then
(G\mathcal{S}^{f})^{\prime}
is an abstract simplicial complex, so we may form its geometric realization
|(G\mathcal{S}^{f})^{\prime}|
. When
G=W(\Gamma)
, we call this the
Davis-Moussong complex

\Sigma(\Gamma)
, and when
G=A(\Gamma)
, we call this the
Deligne complex

\Phi(\Gamma)
. (Note that in some literature,
\Phi(\Gamma)
is called the
modified Deligne complex
.) In either case,
G
acts on
|(G\mathcal{S}^{f})^{\prime}|
with strict fundamental domain isomorphic to
|(\mathcal{S}^{f})^{\prime}|
. A discussion of various natural metrics on
\Phi(\Gamma)
and
\Sigma(\Gamma)
are discussed in
[
CD95
]
. We will be interested in the

, which we will not define explicitly here for reasons that will become evident. (The full definition can be found in
[

, §4.4]
.)

The next of the complexes are the
Artin complex
and the
Coxeter complex
. Again, let
G
denote either
A(\Gamma)
or
W(\Gamma)
, and define a simplicial complex
\Delta=\Delta(\Gamma)
as follows. The vertex set of

is

\{\,g\hat{s}:g\in G,s\text{ a vertex of }\Gamma\,\},

which is the collection of cosets of maximal standard parabolic subgroups of
G
. Note that every vertex
v
has a well-defined “type”, that is, a
unique
vertex
s\in V(\Gamma)
such that
v=g\hat{s}
; when this equality holds, we will say that

is
type
\hat{s}
.
A collection

of vertices span an

if and only if

\bigcap g_{i}\hat{s}_{i}\not=\varnothing.

When

, we call
\Delta
the

and denote it by

. (Note that in some literature,

is called the

, or simply the

. However, we reserve the latter name for our

and denote it by

The group

(for

This action has a strict fundamental domain of a

-simplex with exactly one vertex of each type.
If
\Gamma
is spherical, then

has a metric which makes it isometric to a standard round sphere in

(hence the terminology “spherical” Coxeter-Dynkin diagram). Since the fundamental domains of the actions on
