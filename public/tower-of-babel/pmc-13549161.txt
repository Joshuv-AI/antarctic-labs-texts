“Abductor Sparing”: A New Selective Involvement in ALS
Authors: Amuro Kondo, Keiichi Hokkoku, Takamichi Kanbayashi, Takahiro Nakayama, Takuya Sasaki, Hiromasa Matsuno, Yasuyuki Iguchi, Toshio Fukutake, Tetsuo Ando, Tsutomu Kamiyama, Pyou Kim, Takahiro Hasegawa, Yuki Hatanaka, Shunsuke Kobayashi, Masahiro Sonoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsVarious signs of selective muscle involvement have been reported in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) but such studies for the lower limbs are scarce. We formed a preliminary impression that hip abductors (Ab) are often preserved in ALS. We named this phenomenon “abductor sparing”, and this study aimed to verify our findings.MethodsPatients with a confirmed diagnosis of ALS (ALS group) and patients with pyramidal weakness other than ALS (pyramidal group) were retrospectively identified. Medical Research Council (MRC) scores of 10 muscle groups in the lower limbs were evaluated. The proportion of patients with weakness (MRC score 4 or less) was compared between different groups.ResultsWe enrolled 61 patients in the ALS group and 27 patients in the pyramidal group. The most frequently weak muscle groups in both groups were big toe extensors and hip flexors. Ab was the third (70%) in the pyramidal group, whereas it was weak only in 30% of patients with ALS. This held true also for patients with ALS with shorter duration or less severity. “The lower limb flexor pattern”, i.e., flexor muscles being weaker than extensor muscles, was observed both in ALS and pyramidal groups.DiscussionPatients with ALS generally showed similar muscle weakness patterns to those with pyramidal syndrome, except for abductor sparing. The reason for the latter phenomenon is unclear. Abductor sparing may be useful for early diagnosis of ALS, although larger studies with blinded evaluators are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, hip abductors, pyramidal weakness, selectivity, split phenomena

Introduction

Various signs of selective muscle involvement in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) have been reported, termed as “split phenomena” [1]. They are expected to be useful for differential diagnosis and early diagnosis of ALS. For the upper limbs, several signs have been described starting with the “split hand” [2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

Regarding the lower limbs, several authors have stated that flexor muscles are weaker than their corresponding extensors in ALS [7, 8, 9]. Notably, this distribution resembles the classical pattern of pyramidal weakness [10, 11], i.e., the “lower limb flexor pattern” [12]. Hip adductors (Ad) are another muscle group that is commonly affected by spasticity [13] and therefore tends to be preserved in pyramidal weakness. In contrast, their antagonists, hip abductors (Ab), are frequently involved in pyramidal weakness [14, 15]. In our clinical experiences, we also noticed that patients with ALS showed a similar distribution to pyramidal weakness in which flexor muscles are more affected than extensor muscles. However, we formed a preliminary impression that Ab tends to be preserved in ALS, unlike pyramidal weakness. We named this phenomenon “abductor sparing” in ALS. In this study, we compared the strength of various lower limb muscle groups between patients with ALS and those presenting with pyramidal weakness, and tried to verify the “abductor sparing” phenomenon.

Methods

Subjects

Patients with ALS were retrospectively identified from the outpatient and EMG databases at Teikyo University Hospital and related institutions between January 2023 and February 2025 using the keyword ALS. Medical records of the identified patients were reviewed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) The first evaluation of the patient was within the above study period; (2) age ≥ 20 years old; (3) a diagnosis of ALS was made based on clinical and electrodiagnostic evaluations, and was later confirmed by follow‐up when necessary; (4) exclusion of alternative diagnoses based on appropriate ancillary testing and absence of other disorders that may affect lower limb strength; (5) Medical Research Council (MRC) scores were evaluated by a board‐certified neurologist (MS) with expertise in manual muscle testing (MMT) for the following 10 lower‐limb muscle groups: hip flexors (HF), hip extensors (HE), hip abductors (Ab), hip adductors (Ad), knee flexors (KF), knee extensors (KE), ankle dorsiflexors (ADF), ankle plantarflexors (APF), big toe extensors (BTE), and toe extensors (TE); and (6) at least one of the above 10 scores was less than 5. All patients were categorized according to the Gold Coast criteria (GCC) [16] at the time of initial evaluation, although patients who did not satisfy GCC were included if the diagnosis of ALS was later confirmed during follow‐up based on progression. Patients lacking upper motor neuron (UMN) signs, i.e., progressive muscular atrophy (PMA), were also identified.

ALS group was divided into two subgroups: ALS pyramidal (−) and ALS pyramidal (+) groups depending on the absence or presence of the UMN signs, that is pyramidal signs, in the lower limbs. Pyramidal signs include hyperactive patellar or Achilles tendon reflexes, disproportionately preserved reflex in a weak and atrophic muscle, sustained or unsustained ankle clonus, and the presence of Babinski, Chaddock, or reverse Chaddock signs [17]. Patients with PMA and others (non‐PMA) were also separately considered. The duration of illness and the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS‐R) [18] at the initial evaluation were identified, and the ALS subgroup with a duration of illness less than the median value and that with an ALSFRS‐R score greater than the median value were also considered as well as the remaining subgroups.

Patients having pyramidal signs due to disorders other than ALS (named “pyramidal group”) were identified from the same databases used for the ALS group as well as from the medical records of patients hospitalized in the Teikyo University Hospital between March 2021 and February 2025. Keywords used for the extraction were typical disorders that can present with pyramidal signs, such as “CSM (cervical spondylotic myelopathy)”, “myelitis”, “spastic”, or “stroke”. Medical records of the extracted patients were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria for this group were: (1) The first evaluation of the patient was within the above study period; (2) age ≥ 20 years old; (3) confirmed diagnosis that can cause pyramidal signs in the lower limbs; (4) absence of concurrent peripheral disorder that may affect the lower‐limb muscle strength, including prior lumbar spine surgery; (5) MRC scores of the 10 muscle groups listed previously were evaluated by the same neurologist (MS); and (6) at least one of the above 10 scores was less than 5.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Teikyo University School of Medicine (approval number: 19‐157‐5).

Evaluated Parameters and the Statistical Analyses

MRC scores of all muscle groups were assessed in the supine position [19]. Details of the MMT technique for each muscle group are illustrated in the Figure. The score was dichotomized into normal (MRC score 5) or weak (MRC score 4 or less). The difference in scores between weak muscle groups was not considered since it does not represent the same level of strength across different muscle groups. The percentage of patients showing weakness for a given muscle group was calculated for each subject group and was compared between different groups. The main comparison was done between the ALS and pyramidal groups. Various subgroups of ALS were also compared with the pyramidal group.

Among the pyramidal group, CSM comprised the largest group. Therefore, patients with CSM were compared with the ALS group.

“Abductor sparing”, which we expected to be a feature of ALS, indicates that Ab is normal while other muscles are weak. Therefore, we evaluated the percentage of patients in whom Ab was normal while the relevant muscle group was weak for every examined muscle group. Here, we preliminarily felt that among muscle groups other than Ab, HF and BTE are the two most frequently weak muscle groups, followed by TE, both in ALS and pyramidal groups. Therefore, we also analyzed the percentage of patients in whom Ab was normal while any one of the other six muscle groups, i.e., HE, KF, KE, ADF, APF, and Ad that were expected to be usually preserved in both groups, was weak. Additionally, we further searched for other combinations of muscle groups that achieved high sensitivity and specificity.

We also investigated the reverse sign, “predominant abductor involvement”, which we expected to be characteristic for the pyramidal group. We evaluated the percentage of patients in whom Ab was weak while the relevant muscle group was normal for every examined muscle group. For the same reason as above, we also analyzed the percentage of patients in whom Ab was weak while all of the 6 muscles, HE, KF, KE, ADF, APF, and Ad were normal, together with other possible combinations.

The sensitivity and specificity for these signs were defined in a usual manner, i.e., those for “abductor sparing” signs were calculated for diagnosing ALS in comparison with the pyramidal group, whereas those for “predominant abductor involvement” were calculated for diagnosing the pyramidal group in comparison with ALS.

The “lower limb flexor pattern” [12], i.e., the flexor weakness, was investigated in the pyramidal group as well as in the ALS group by comparing HF vs. HE, KF vs. KE, and ADF vs. APF. We also compared Ab vs. Ad between the two groups.

Two proportions of independent groups were compared using the χ 2 test for independence or Fisher's exact probability test. The latter was used when any one of the expected frequencies of the individual cells was five or under. Two proportions for the same population were compared using McNemar's test. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Our study confirmed that the “abductor sparing” pattern is observed in ALS. This feature was preserved irrespective of the duration of illness and the ALSFRS‐R score, indicating that it is useful in the early diagnosis of ALS. We defined a number of signs of “abductor sparing” and those of “predominant abductor involvement” as a combination of findings in different muscle groups. Some of these combinations were highly specific for ALS or non‐ALS and would be useful in suspecting or doubting ALS from the clinical signs.

“Abductor sparing” was noted in both the ALS pyramidal (−) group and the ALS pyramidal (+) group. The latter in particular indicates that such an “abductor sparing” is specifically attributed to ALS since both groups had pyramidal signs in the lower limbs. “Abductor sparing” was not significant in the PMA group. This is paradoxical since patients with PMA are supposed to be the most purely representative of ALS without UMN signs (more restricted than the ALS pyramidal (−) group since the latter allows UMN signs in parts of the body other than the lower limbs). Investigations using a larger sample size would be necessary to confirm this puzzling result.

The mechanism underlying the “abductor sparing” phenomenon is unknown. Recently, the selective muscle involvement in ALS has been explained by the difference in direct corticomotoneuronal (CM) projections that are responsible for fine motor control. Muscles with rich CM projections are postulated to be more vulnerable in ALS, based on the corticofugal axonal spread hypothesis in ALS degeneration [8, 9, 20, 21, 22]. This implies that the relative preservation of Ab is due to sparse direct CM projections. However, preservation of anti‐gravity muscles in pyramidal weakness is also explained by scarcity of direct CM projections [9, 23], and therefore Ab that weakens in pyramidal weakness [14, 15] is supposed to be rich in direct CM projections, an opposite conclusion from the above speculation. It has been argued that apparent Ab weakness in pyramidal tract dysfunction is due to pathological associated activation of Ad when hip abduction is attempted [14, 15]. However, this hypothesis cannot explain the fact that Ab was frequently preserved even in the ALS pyramidal (+) group.

Other than the “abductor sparing” in ALS, the present study gave further insights into the distribution of weakness in patients with pyramidal involvement or ALS. Classically, pyramidal weakness has been believed to predominantly affect flexor muscles in the lower limbs [10, 11, 12] although this conventional view has been challenged by more recent studies [24, 25, 26]. Our study supported the classical “lower‐limb flexor pattern” [12] in the pyramidal weakness, at least for HF vs. HE and KF vs. KE. The weaker Ab than Ad in the pyramidal weakness also coincides with previous descriptions [13, 14, 15]. These results suggest that the classical concept of the selectivity in the pyramidal weakness is valid and has diagnostic value. Our study revealed that patients with ALS generally showed similar distributions to the pyramidal weakness, such as “lower‐limb flexor pattern” and weaker Ab than Ad, except for minor differences including the “abductor sparing” pattern.

The “distal pattern” has been described both in pyramidal weakness [12, 24, 27] and in ALS [7, 9]. In this study, however, HF and Ab, both being proximal muscles, were among the weakest muscle groups in the pyramidal group. HF was also frequently weak in ALS. This calls into question the “distal pattern” and warrants additional investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a small retrospective study and the examiner was not blinded to the diagnosis of the patient. Second, all patients were evaluated by a single expert. Third, the MRC score remains a subjective measure. A large‐scale, multi‐center study using more objective measures is necessary to confirm the present findings. Fourth, the pyramidal group was heterogeneously composed of patients with various disorders. A control group composed of a uniform disorder may be ideal, and the present results for CSM would be important in this regard. Despite these limitations, the results obtained in this study might help early diagnosis of ALS, and may also cast new light on the mechanism of selective muscle involvement in ALS.
