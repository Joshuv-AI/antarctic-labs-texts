Comparable 2-year functional outcomes following vertebroplasty for thoracic and lumbar osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yong Ng, Jeremy Tze En Lim, Lei Jiang, Yee Gen Lim, Zhixing Marcus Ling, Chee Cheong Reuben Soh, Christian Hwee Yee Heng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy Design Retrospective cohort study.Purpose To compare short- and medium-term patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) between thoracic and lumbar percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) cohorts for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs).Overview of Literature PVP is well-established for pain relief in OVCF; however, whether clinical outcomes vary by the anatomical location of the treated vertebrae, remains unclear.Methods Ninety patients who underwent PVP between January 2010 and August 2022 with complete 2-year PROM follow-up were included and stratified into thoracic (n=33) and lumbar (n=57) cohorts. Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) are common in older adults and can cause acute pain, reduced mobility, progressive kyphosis, and impaired health-related quality of life [1,2]. When conservative management is unsuccessful, percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is a widely employed minimally invasive intervention aimed at stabilizing the fractured vertebral body and providing rapid pain relief [3-5]. Although the efficacy of PVP is well-established, the influence of fracture location on patient outcomes remains unclear. Most clinical studies on vertebral augmentation have analyzed thoracic and lumbar fractures collectively, without stratifying outcomes by vertebral region [6], despite the distinct biomechanical characteristics of these regions that may affect postoperative recovery.

The thoracic spine (T1–T12) is anatomically configured for structural stability and protection of the intrathoracic organs, rather than segmental mobility [7,8]. Following PVP, an augmented thoracic vertebra may be exposed to lower kinematic stress, potentially providing a more stable biomechanical environment that facilitates pain resolution and reduces construct-related mechanical strain. However, the thoracic spine is also characterized by its physiological sagittal kyphosis, and OVCFs in this region may exacerbate kyphotic deformity, thereby contributing to pain and functional impairment [9-11].

In contrast, the lumbar spine (L1–L5) is adapted for greater mobility and bears a substantial proportion of the body’s axial load. Its physiological lordotic curvature is crucial for sagittal balance. Accordingly, an OVCF in the lumbar region can lead to anterior vertebral height loss and a reduction in lumbar lordosis. This disruption can alter the load distribution across the vertebral column, resulting in abnormal transfer of compressive forces to the posterior ligamentous complex and adjacent intervertebral discs [9].

Given these anatomical and biomechanical distinctions between the thoracic and lumbar spines, the frequent pooling of thoracic and lumbar fractures in the vertebroplasty literature represents an important gap in clinical knowledge. Therefore, this study compared the short- and medium-term patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) between thoracic and lumbar vertebroplasty cohorts. We hypothesized that pain relief and functional outcomes would not differ significantly between the two groups.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This retrospective single-center study included consecutive patients who underwent isolated PVP at a tertiary referral center between January 2010 and August 2022. Eligible patients were those treated for symptomatic vertebral compression fractures with complete PROM data available preoperatively and at both 6-month and 2-year follow-up. Patients who underwent kyphoplasty, revision vertebral augmentation, concomitant spinal fusion, or thoracolumbar procedures, as well as those with vertebral fracture secondary to malignancy, were excluded. Thoracic fractures were defined as those involving T1–T12, whereas lumbar fractures involved L1–L5. Patients treated across thoracic and lumbar regions (thoracolumbar procedures) were excluded to avoid misclassification of fracture location. Of the 141 patients treated during the study period, 90 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1), comprising 33 in the thoracic cohort and 57 in the lumbar cohort.

Vertebroplasty was performed based on clinical symptoms, radiographic evidence of OVCF, and the treating surgeon’s judgment. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained where clinically indicated to assess fracture acuity and identify the symptomatic level, although MRI-confirmed edema was not uniformly required throughout the study period. Osteoporosis treatment was provided based on standard clinical practice and was not standardized across cohorts.

Ethics statement

Approval was obtained from the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB No. 2023/2214) for this retrospective study, which was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent from individual patients was omitted because of the retrospective design of this study.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by a single fellowship-trained spine surgeon. Patients were positioned prone, and PVP was performed under fluoroscopic guidance. The target vertebral level was identified using biplanar fluoroscopy, and a vertebroplasty needle was introduced into the vertebral body through a transpedicular approach. After confirming appropriate needle placement on anteroposterior and lateral views, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement was injected under continuous fluoroscopic monitoring until adequate vertebral filling was achieved.

Variables and outcome measures

Preoperative demographic data (age, sex, race), baseline clinical scores, and procedural variables were recorded. Procedural variables included the number of treated vertebral levels, categorized as single- or multi-level procedures. Outcome measures included the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), North American Spine Society (NASS) Neurogenic Symptom severity score, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for back and leg pain, and health-related quality of life evaluated using the Short Form-36 (SF-36). These PROMs were collected preoperatively and at 6 months and 2 years postoperatively. SF-36 domain scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status, whereas lower ODI, NASS, and VAS scores reflected lower symptom burden. Fracture morphology, vertebral height loss, local kyphosis, cement distribution, fracture chronicity, and adjacent-level degeneration were not consistently available and therefore not included in the analysis.

Clinical significance was assessed using established anchor-based minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds [12]. A clinically meaningful improvement was defined as a reduction of ≥12.8 points for ODI, ≥1.2 points for VAS Back Pain, and ≥1.6 points for VAS Leg Pain. MCID analysis was not performed for NASS Neurogenic Symptom or SF-36 because validated thresholds for these measures are not available in the vertebroplasty population.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were evaluated for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test. As PROMs demonstrated non-normal distributions (all p<0.05), between-group comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and categorical outcomes were analyzed using chi-square tests. Within-group change across preoperative, 6-month, and 2-year timepoints was assessed using the Friedman tests. Within-group change across preoperative, 6-month, and 2-year timepoints was assessed using the Friedman test. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all outcomes. Differences in the MCID achievement rates between the thoracic and lumbar cohorts were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To evaluate whether the vertebral region of treatment (thoracic versus lumbar) independently predicted postoperative PROMs, separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed for each outcome. The models were adjusted for age, sex, race (dichotomized to Chinese vs. non-Chinese), number of vertebral levels treated (single versus multiple), and the corresponding baseline score when available. Regression analyses were performed using ordinary least squares with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) robust standard errors, and results were reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% CIs. Although PROMs were non-normally distributed, ordinary least squares regression was retained to estimate adjusted mean differences. HC3 robust standard errors and bootstrap CIs were applied to minimize reliance on normality and homoskedasticity assumptions.

Model diagnostics were performed for all regression models and included variance inflation factors to assess multicollinearity, Cook’s distance, leverage, differences in β for the vertebral region coefficient to identify influential observations, and the Breusch–Pagan and Ramsey regression equation specification error tests to evaluate heteroskedasticity and model specification. Model diagnostics were satisfactory, with all variance inflation factors ≤1.26, no observation demonstrating a Cook’s distance exceeding 1 (maximum 0.62), and exclusion of the most influential observation yielding no change in the conclusions regarding the vertebral region (Supplement 1).

Given the number of outcomes assessed, the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) was applied across the 12 PROM outcomes at each follow-up timepoint in the multivariable regression analyses. Both unadjusted and adjusted p-values are reported. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python ver. 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA).

Results

Discussion

Principal findings and clinical implications

The principal finding of this study was that PVP produced comparable clinical outcomes in thoracic and lumbar OVCFs, with no significant between-group differences observed in any assessed PROM. Both cohorts demonstrated substantial and sustained postoperative improvements in pain, disability measures (ODI), and health-related quality of life (SF-36) over the 2-year follow-up. These findings suggest that thoracic and lumbar vertebroplasty are associated with broadly similar PROM trajectories.

These findings are broadly consistent with the vertebral augmentation literature. While most previous studies have not differentiated outcomes by vertebral region, existing evidence suggests that thoracic and lumbar fractures achieve comparable postoperative improvement following vertebral augmentation [6]. Our study extends this understanding to PVP, demonstrating comparable patient-reported recovery trajectories across both regions. This was further supported by our MCID analysis, which exhibited high rates of MCID achievement for ODI and VAS Back Pain (>87%) both cohorts at 6 months and 2 years. Conversely, MCID achievement for VAS Leg Pain was low (<20%) in both groups, likely reflecting a floor effect due to minimal baseline leg pain scores in many patients. These findings are consistent with prior literature suggesting that fracture location alone may not be a major determinant of pain, disability, or quality-of-life recovery after OVCFs [13-15].

Several plausible biomechanical considerations suggest that fracture location could influence postoperative recovery. The lumbar spine is a highly mobile, load-bearing segment, and fractures in this region may be associated with greater functional impairment. In contrast, thoracic fractures may exacerbate kyphotic deformity, adversely affecting posture and comfort. Thoracic PVP can also be technically more demanding because of smaller pedicles and the proximity of the spinal cord [16]. Biomechanical studies have shown that the rib cage provides substantial stability to the thoracic spine and may restrict motion under physiological loading [17-19], whereas the lumbar spine is subjected to greater dynamic loads during daily activities [9]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in this study.

Although thoracic and lumbar cohorts demonstrated substantial improvements across most PROMs, these gains do not necessarily reflect patient satisfaction, which is influenced by multiple domains, including pain relief, functional recovery, expectations, health status, interpersonal care, and postoperative therapy [20]. Since this study did not directly evaluate postoperative rehabilitation or patient expectations, these considerations remain speculative. Importantly, the anatomical region alone may be an inadequate surrogate for OVCF severity, as recovery is likely influenced more directly by factors such as fracture morphology, vertebral height loss, kyphotic deformity, cement distribution, and adjacent-level degeneration.

From a clinical perspective, the absence of statistically significant differences in pain, disability, and health-related quality of life at 2 years should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical equivalence between thoracic and lumbar PVP. Rather, within the limitations of this cohort, no clear difference in medium-term patientreported recovery was detected between groups. This finding may aid perioperative counseling by suggesting that recovery trajectories are likely to be similar regardless of fracture location.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the 2-year follow-up period limits our ability to assess potential long-term outcome differences between thoracic and lumbar PVP. Moreover, the study focused exclusively on subjective functional and pain-related PROMs and did not evaluate or correlate these findings with long-term objective radiological outcomes, such as vertebral height maintenance, cement leakage, or the incidence of new adjacent-level fractures over the 2-year period [6]. Although the models adjusted for age, sex, race, number of treated vertebral levels, and baseline PROMs, residual confounding remains possible. Important fracture-specific and radiological variables, including bone quality, fracture severity, vertebral height loss, kyphotic deformity, fracture chronicity, cement distribution, and adjacent-level degeneration, were not consistently available across the study period and, therefore, could not be included in the adjusted models. Accordingly, fracture location alone should be interpreted cautiously as an isolated determinant of outcome. In addition, the modest sample size may have limited the precision of effect estimates, reducing the ability to detect small but clinically meaningful differences between the thoracic and lumbar cohorts. Therefore, a type II error cannot be excluded given that several adjusted 95% CIs were relatively wide and encompassed potentially relevant effects in either direction.

Additional limitations include the retrospective, single-center, single-surgeon design, and the inclusion of 90 of 141 screened patients (64%), introducing the potential for selection bias. Although sensitivity analyses demonstrated that included and excluded patients were broadly comparable across demographic characteristics and preoperative PROMs (Supplement 2), similarity in measured variables does not exclude systematic differences in unmeasured factors such as frailty, symptom severity, postoperative complications, health-seeking behavior, or follow-up adherence. Consequently, patients who completed the 2-year follow-up may represent a healthier or more adherent subgroup, potentially influencing the observed magnitude of postoperative improvement and limiting the external validity of the findings. Therefore, prospective multicenter studies incorporating radiographic parameters, osteoporosis treatment, and objective functional measures are warranted to confirm whether these findings are reproducible in larger cohorts. Despite these limitations, a key strength of this study is its focus on patient-centric outcomes and the direct comparison between two spinal regions that had not been well-differentiated in the existing literature.

Conclusions

In this cohort, thoracic and lumbar vertebroplasty yielded comparable 2-year PROMs with no statistically significant differences detected between groups. These findings suggest that the vertebral region may not be a major determinant of patient-reported recovery following vertebroplasty for OVCFs. However, residual confounding, attrition, and absent radiographic variables limit definitive conclusions regarding clinical equivalence.
