Nonlocal anisotropic Riesz interactions with a physical confinement
M.G. Mora, L. Rondi, L. Scardia, E.G. Tolotti

Abstract
. In this work we fully characterize, in any space dimension, the minimizer of a class of nonlocal and anisotropic Riesz energies defined over probability measures supported on ellipsoids. In the super-Coulombic and Coulombic regime, we prove that the minimizer is independent of the anisotropy. In contrast, in the sub-Coulombic regime we show that this property fails: we exhibit an example of anisotropy for which the isotropic minimizer is not optimal. In order to prove our main result, we provide a formula for the potential inside an ellipsoid, valid in any space dimension and involving the hypergeometric function. AMS 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: 31A15 (primary); 49K20 (secondary) Keywords: nonlocal energy, potential theory, anisotropic interaction, Riesz potential



1.
Introduction

Nonlocal energies are ubiquitous: they are the continuum counterpart of discrete, pairwise, long-range interactions, and are used to model aggregation phenomena for large numbers of agents of different nature and size, from animals to vehicles, charges, and defects in materials.
A vast literature is focused on attractive-repulsive energies, where repulsion dominates at small distances, and attraction dominates at large distances.
Depending on the mutual strength of the attraction and the repulsion, energy minimizers exhibit a variety of patterns, from concentration on points and lower dimensional sets to diffusion.

In this work we characterize the minimizers of a class of attractive-repulsive energies where the repulsion is driven by an anisotropic Riesz potential, and the attraction is forced by the constraint that minimizers must be compactly supported.
More precisely, let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
.
We consider the repulsive interaction energy

I_{s}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W_{\!s}(x-y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)

for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where the interaction kernel
W_{\!s}
, for
s\in(0,d)
, is of the form

(1.1)

W_{\!s}(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{s}}\Phi\bigg(\frac{x}{|x|}\bigg)

for
x\neq 0
, and
W_{s}(0)=+\infty
, and the profile
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous, even, and strictly positive function.

The attraction is modelled in terms of the indicator function of a compact set; namely given a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the confinement potential

(1.2)

V_{E}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in E,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The attractive-repulsive energy we study is then

(1.3)

I_{s}^{E}(\mu)=I_{s}(\mu)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{E}(x)\,d\mu(x)

for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Clearly, minimizing
I_{s}^{E}
over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is equivalent to minimizing
I_{s}
over the set of measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\supp\mu\subset E
.

The special choices
\Phi\equiv 1
in (
1.1
) and
E=B_{1}
in (
1.2
), where
B_{1}
denotes the closed unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at the origin, correspond to a radially symmetric energy
I_{\iso,s}^{B_{1}}
, which has been widely studied in the last century. It is well known that the unique minimizer of
I_{\iso,s}^{B_{1}}
is the measure
\mu_{\iso,s}

defined as

\mu_{\iso,s}=\begin{cases}c_{s,d}(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{s-d}{2}}\mathcal{L}^{d}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}B_{1}\quad&\text{if }d-2<s<d,\\
c_{s,d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial B_{1}\quad&\text{if }0<s\leq d-2,\end{cases}

where
c_{s,d}
is a normalization constant (see (
3.2
) below). The minimality of
\mu_{\iso,s}
for
I_{\iso,s}^{B_{1}}
dates back to Riesz
[
20
,
21
]
.
The cases
d=1,2,3
had already been solved a few years earlier by Pólya and Szegő
[
19
]
.
More recently there has been a renewed interest in this result, and alternative methods of proofs have been provided, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
]
.

We observe that the Coulomb exponent
s=d-2
acts as a threshold for the dimensionality of the minimizer. At the threshold value
s=d-2
the minimizer concentrates on the boundary of the constraint. If the potential is more singular at the origin, then the minimizing measure gets more and more diffuse, with a density depending on the Riesz exponent
s>d-2
. On the other hand, if the potential is less singular than Coulombic, then
\mu_{\iso,s}=\mu_{\iso,d-2}
for every
0<s\leq d-2
. In other words, once the minimizer concentrates at the Coulomb threshold
s=d-2
, it remains the same for all the sub-Coulombic Riesz exponents.

In this work we mainly focus on super-Coulombic Riesz interactions
s\geq d-2
, and confining sets
E
given by ellipsoids.
Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
s\in(0,d)
with
s\geq d-2
, and let
W_{\!s}
be as in (
1.1
) with
\Phi:{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
a continuous, even, and strictly positive function. Assume that
\widehat{W}_{\!s}

is continuous and non-negative on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an ellipsoid of the form
E=RDB_{1}
for some
R\in\SO(d)
and some positive-definite
d\times d
diagonal matrix
D
, and let
T^{E}
be the map
T^{E}(x)=RDx
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the unique minimizer of
I_{s}^{E}
over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the push-forward of the measure
\mu_{\iso,s}
by the map
T^{E}
, that is, the probability measure

\mu_{s}^{E}=\begin{cases}|E|^{-1}\dfrac{\Gamma(1+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{s-d}{2})}(1-|D^{-1}R^{T}x|^{2})^{\frac{s-d}{2}}\mathcal{L}^{d}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}E\quad&\text{if }d-2<s<d,\\
\big(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial B_{1})\det D\big)^{-1}|D^{-2}R^{T}x|^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial E\quad&\text{if }0<s=d-2.\end{cases}

In the statement above we require
s>0
, hence logarithmic interactions in two dimensions, which correspond loosely speaking to
s=0
, are not included in our analysis. However,
the results in
[
17
,
18
]
show that also in this case minimizers are the push-forward of the measure
(2\pi)^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial B_{1}
by
T^{E}
, in agreement with the statement of Theorem
1.1
for the Coulomb scaling
s=d-2
.

Surprisingly, the minimizing measure
\mu_{s}^{E}
is completely independent of the profile
\Phi
, and its support is fully determined by the confinement term.
It is natural to ask whether this phenomenon occurs also in the sub-Coulombic regime
s<d-2
. In Section
4
we give a negative answer to this question:
we consider
E=B_{1}
and we show that, for a suitable profile
\Phi
, the measure
c_{s,d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial B_{1}
does not satisfy the Euler–Lagrange conditions for
I_{s}^{B_{1}}
when
s<d-2
.
Hence, for
0<s<d-2
the anisotropy
\Phi

does
play a role in determining the energy minimizers, unlike for
s\geq d-2
.

The role of the anisotropy in determining the shape and dimension of the minimizer has been the subject of recent attention in the case of quadratic attraction, namely for attractive-repulsive energies as in (
1.3
), with
V_{E}(x)
replaced by
|x|^{2}
. The most general result to date is
[
9
]
, where the authors extend the papers
[
2
,
3
,
16
]

beyond dimensions two and three. Unlike the case of physical confinement treated here, however, the characterization of the minimizer in
[
9
]
has been proved under some restrictions on the Riesz exponent
s
, and consequently on the space dimension
d
, which are stricter than for the isotropic counterpart
\Phi\equiv 1
.
We refer to
[
10
]
for a complete overview of the available results in the isotropic case.

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
we show that, for any ellipsoid
E
and any
s\geq d-2
, the corresponding measure
\mu_{s}^{E}
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange conditions for the energy
I_{s}^{E}
(see (EL1)–(EL3) in Theorem
2.1
). To this aim, the strategy devised in
[
16
]
, and further developed in
[
9
]
, requires rewriting the potential
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{s}^{E}
, for a general ellipsoid
E
, in Fourier form. Due to the lack of integrability of the Fourier transform of
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{s}^{E}
at infinity, however, this can be done only for the regularized potentials
P_{r}=\frac{\chi_{B_{r}}}{|B_{r}|}\ast W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{s}^{E}
, for
r>0
. The representation of
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{s}^{E}
is then obtained in Theorem
3.4
via a careful limit procedure as
r\to 0^{+}
.

We note that Theorem
3.4
provides a new general formula for the potential
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
inside
E
, valid for all
0<s<d
and
q\geq d-2
. In particular, this extends the formula derived in
[
9
]
to the full range of Riesz exponents in any space dimension. A Fourier representation of the potential
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
outside
E
is unfortunately lacking for general
s
and
q
. However, in the present setting of a physical confinement,
only the expression of the potential inside
E
is needed. For this reason the formula provided by Theorem
3.4
is sufficient to establish our result without any restrictions on the space dimension
d
or the Riesz exponent
s\geq d-2
, unlike the case of quadratic attraction considered in
[
9
]
, which remains open in high dimensions and for large Riesz exponents.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we collect some definitions and preliminary results that will be used throughout the paper. Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, where we obtain a characterization of the minimizer for
s\geq d-2
. The case
s<d-2
is discussed in Section
4
, where we provide an explicit example illustrating the role of anisotropy in the sub-Coulombic regime.

2.1.
Some special functions

We recall the definition and the main properties of some special functions that will be used in the rest of the manuscript.
For further details we refer to

,
15
]
.

The Gamma and the Beta functions

The Gamma function is defined as

\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,dt

for
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(z)>0
.
It can be extended by analytic continuation to the whole complex plane, except at non-positive integers.
We will use the following properties of
\Gamma
:

(1)

\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)

for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
z\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
(fundamental property),

(2)

\Gamma(z)\Gamma\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)=2^{1-2z}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(2z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
z\neq 0,-\frac{1}{2},-1,-\frac{3}{2},\dots
(Legendre duplication formula),

(3)

\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sqrt{\pi}
.

The Beta function can be defined in terms of the Gamma function as

(2.1)

B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}

for
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(x)>0
and
\Re(y)>0
.
It easily follows that
B
is symmetric. Moreover, for
\mu,\nu>0
,

(2.2)

\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{\mu-1}(t)\cos^{\nu-1}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}B\left(\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{\nu}{2}\right),

see
[
11
, Formula 3.621–5]
.

The hypergeometric function

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
with
\gamma\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
.
The hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
is defined as the power series

{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}(\beta)_{n}}{(\gamma)_{n}n!}z^{n}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|<1
.
Here
(\lambda)_{n}
denotes the Pochhammer’s symbol, namely

(\lambda)_{0}=1,\qquad(\lambda)_{n}=\lambda(\lambda+1)\cdot\dots\cdot(\lambda+n-1)\quad\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

If
-1<\Re(\gamma-\alpha-\beta)\leq 0
, then the series converges for
|z|\leq 1
, except at the point
z=1
. More precisely,
we have the following behavior of the series at
z=1
.
If
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
,

(2.3)

\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\alpha+\beta;z)}{-\log(1-z)}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)},

while if
\Re(\gamma-\alpha-\beta)<0
,

(2.4)

\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)}{(1-z)^{\gamma-\alpha-\beta}}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta-\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}.

If instead
\Re(\gamma-\alpha-\beta)>0
, the series extends continuously also at
z=1
, and

(2.5)

\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)={}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;1)=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha-\beta)}{\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma-\beta)}.

For more details we refer to
[
15
, Chapter 9]
.

Note that if
\alpha
or
\beta
is a non-positive integer, then the hypergeometric function reduces to a polynomial in
z
.
In particular, it holds that

(2.6)

{}_{2}F_{1}(0,\beta;\gamma;z)=1

and

(2.7)

{}_{2}F_{1}(-1,\beta;\gamma;z)=1-\frac{\beta}{\gamma}z.

The Appell function of the fourth kind

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
.
The Appell function of the fourth kind
F_{4}
is defined as the double power series

F_{4}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma,\gamma^{\prime};x,y)=\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{m+n}(\beta)_{m+n}}{(\gamma)_{m}(\gamma^{\prime})_{n}n!m!}x^{m}y^{n}

for
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
with
\sqrt{|x|}+\sqrt{|y|}<1
.
Since
F_{4}
is analytic in its domain, it follows that

(2.8)

\lim_{x\to 0}F_{4}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma,\gamma^{\prime};x,y)={}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma^{\prime};y)

for every
y\in\mathbb{C}
with
|y|<1
.

The Bessel function of the first kind

Let
\nu\geq 0
.
The Bessel function of the first kind of order
\nu
is defined as

J_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}(x/2)^{\nu+2n}}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}

for
x\geq 0
.
We recall the asymptotic behavior of
J_{\nu}
at
0
and
+\infty
:

(2.9)

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{x^{\nu}}{2^{\nu}\Gamma(1+\nu)},

\displaystyle\text{as }x\to 0^{+},

(2.10)

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{2}\nu-\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

\displaystyle\text{as }x\to+\infty,

see
[
15
, Section 5.16]
.

2.2.
The Fourier transform

Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the Schwartz class of rapidly decreasing functions and let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be its dual space, that is, the space of tempered distributions. Following
[
8
]
,
we define the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
of a function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,dx

for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
f
is a radial function, that is,
f(x)=f_{0}(|x|)
for some
f_{0}
, then so is
\widehat{f}
and we have

(2.11)

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{|\xi|^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{0}(r)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(r|\xi|)r^{\frac{d}{2}}\,dr,

see
[
8
, eq. (7.38)]
.

If
f
is a tempered distribution, its Fourier transform can be defined by duality, namely

\langle\widehat{f},\varphi\rangle=\langle f,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
By the inversion theorem for the Fourier transform, if
f
is a tempered distribution such that
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

f(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,d\xi

for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
see for example
[
8
, p. 244]
.

Let
\mu
be a Radon measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support. It is easy to see that
\mu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
\widehat{\mu}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, if
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the convolution
f\ast\mu
is well-defined as an element of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
\widehat{f\ast\mu}=\widehat{f}\,\widehat{\mu}
, see
[
13
, Theorem 1.7.6]
.

2.3.
The Fourier transform of
W_{\!s}

Let
W_{\!s}
be a kernel as in Theorem
1.1
. Since
W_{\!s}
is locally integrable and with sublinear growth at infinity, it is a tempered distribution.
To compute the Fourier transform of
W_{\!s}
, it is convenient to write the profile
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in terms of spherical harmonics, namely

\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n},

where each
\phi_{n}
is a spherical harmonic of order
n
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Then for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

W_{\!s}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|x|^{s}}\,\phi_{n}\bigg(\frac{x}{|x|}\bigg).

By
[
22
, Chapter V, Lemma 2]
for
k=0
and
[
22
, Chapter III, Theorem 5]
for
k\geq 1
, the Fourier transform of
p_{k}(x)|x|^{-s-k}
with
p_{k}

a homogeneous harmonic polynomial of degree
k
is given by

(2.12)

(-i)^{k}2^{d-s}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k+d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k+s}{2})}\frac{p_{k}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{k+d-s}}.

We infer that for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for suitable constants
b_{n,s,d}
,

(2.13)

\widehat{W_{\!s}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d-s}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n,s,d}\,\phi_{n}(\xi),

provided the series at the right-hand side converges in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
to a function which we denote by
\Psi:=\widehat{W_{\!s}}|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
In Theorem
1.1
such a function
\Psi
is assumed to be continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Note that, since
\Phi
is even,
\Psi
is even and real-valued.

2.4.
Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer

The following result is by now standard.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
W_{\!s}
be a kernel of the form (
1.1
) with
s\in(0,d)
and let
\Phi:{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous, even, and strictly positive function. Assume that
\widehat{W}_{\!s}

is continuous and non-negative.
Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set of positive
(d-s)
-capacity.
Then
the functional
I_{s}^{E}
has a unique minimizer
\mu
over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover,
\mu
is the unique measure in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the following Euler–Lagrange conditions: there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(EL1)

\displaystyle\supp\mu\subset E,

(EL2)

\displaystyle(W_{\!s}\ast\mu)(x)=C\quad\text{ for }\mu{-a.e.\ }x\in\supp\mu,

(EL3)

\displaystyle(W_{\!s}\ast\mu)(x)\geq C\quad\text{ for every }x\in E\setminus N\text{ with }{\rm Cap}_{d-s}(N)=0.

We refer to
[
14
]
for the notion of
(d-s)
-capacity.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

The proof is an adaptation of
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
. Note that the tightness of minimizing sequences, as well as the compactness of the support of the minimizer, are immediate consequences of the presence of the confinement potential
V_{E}
in the functional
I_{s}^{E}
.
∎

3.
Characterization of the minimizer for
s\geq d-2

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.
A general ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at the origin can be described as
E=RDB_{1}
, where
R\in\SO(d)
,
D
is a positive-definite
d\times d
diagonal matrix, and
B_{1}
is the closed unit ball centered at the origin.
Given an ellipsoid
E=RDB_{1}
, we define the linear map
T^{E}(x)=RDx
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For any
q\geq d-2
, we define

(3.1)

\mu_{q}=\begin{cases}c_{q,d}(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\mathcal{L}^{d}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}B_{1}\quad&\text{if }q>d-2,\\
c_{q,d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial B_{1}\quad&\text{if }q=d-2,\end{cases}

where
c_{q,d}
is a normalization constant so that
\mu_{q}
is a probability measure. Namely, using for instance
[
11
, Formula 3.251–1]
,

(3.2)

c_{q,d}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle|B_{1}|^{-1}\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{q}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{q-d}{2})}=\pi^{-\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{q}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{q-d}{2})}\quad&\text{if }q>d-2,\\
\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial B_{1})\big)^{-1}=\pi^{-\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{2}\quad&\text{if }q=d-2.\end{cases}

For
s=q\in[d-2,d)\cap(0,d)
,
\mu_{q}
clearly coincides with
\mu_{\iso,s}
. The push-forward of
\mu_{q}
by the map
T^{E}
is given by

\mu_{q}^{E}=\begin{cases}|E|^{-1}\dfrac{\Gamma(1+\frac{q}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{q-d}{2})}(1-|D^{-1}R^{T}x|^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\mathcal{L}^{d}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}E\quad&\text{if }q>d-2,\\
\big(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial B_{1})\det D\big)^{-1}|D^{-2}R^{T}x|^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial E\quad&\text{if }q=d-2.\end{cases}

We start this section by computing the Fourier transform of the measure
\mu_{q}
for
q\geq d-2
and of its push-forward
\mu_{q}^{E}
by the map
T^{E}
.
Note that
\mu_{q}
and
\mu_{q}^{E}
are Radon measures with compact support, hence they are tempered distributions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
q\geq d-2
, and let
\mu_{q}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be defined as in (
3.1
). Then

\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)=\hat{c}_{q}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{q}{2}}}J_{\frac{q}{2}}(|\xi|),

where

\hat{c}_{q}=2^{\frac{q}{2}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{q}{2}\right).

Moreover, if
E
is an ellipsoid of the form
E=RDB_{1}
with
R\in\SO(d)
and
D
a positive-definite
d\times d
diagonal matrix, then

(3.3)

\widehat{\mu}_{q}^{E}(\xi)=\widehat{\mu}_{q}(DR^{T}\xi).

Proof.

We start with the case
q>d-2
.
Applying the formula (
2.11
) for the Fourier transform of a radial function we get

(3.4)

\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)=c_{q,d}\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{|\xi|^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(r|\xi|)r^{\frac{d}{2}}\,dr.

To compute the integral in (
3.4
) we use the identity

(3.5)

\int_{0}^{1}x^{\nu+1}(1-x^{2})^{\rho}J_{\nu}(bx)\,dx=\frac{2^{\rho}\Gamma(\rho+1)}{b^{\rho+1}}J_{\nu+\rho+1}(b),

which holds for
b>0
,
\Re(\nu)>-1
, and
\Re(\rho)>-1
(see
[
11
, Formula 6.567–1]
).
Applying (
3.5
) with
\nu=\frac{d}{2}-1
,
\rho=\frac{q-d}{2}
, and
b=|\xi|
, we then obtain from (
3.4
) that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)=c_{q,d}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}2^{\frac{q}{2}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{q-d}{2}\right)\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{q}{2}}}J_{\frac{q}{2}}(|\xi|)=2^{\frac{q}{2}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{q}{2}\right)\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{q}{2}}}J_{\frac{q}{2}}(|\xi|),

which proves the first claim in the statement.
For a similar computation see also
[
12
, Appendix B.5]
, where a slightly different definition of the Fourier transform is used.

When
q=d-2
, we follow the computation in
[
12
, Appendix B.4]
and obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{d-2}(\xi)=c_{d-2,d}\int_{\partial B_{1}}e^{-i\xi\cdot\omega}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)=2^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(|\xi|).

Finally, (
3.3
) follows from the linearity and invertibility of the map
T^{E}
.
∎

The key ingredient for the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.1
, is a formula for the expression of the potential function
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{q}

inside
E
when
s\in(0,d)
and
q\in[d-2,+\infty)
.
As a first step, in the next technical lemma we study the regularity of
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{q}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
s\in(0,d)
and
q\in[d-2,+\infty)
. Let
W_{\!s}
be as in (
1.1
), let
E=RDB_{1}
be an ellipsoid, and let
\mu_{q}^{E}
be the push-forward of the measure
\mu_{q}
by the map
T^{E}
.
Then
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}\in L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial E)
. Moreover, if
0<s<\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})
, then
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

We only sketch the proof in the case
E=B_{1}
and
\Phi\equiv 1
, and for convenience we ignore the normalization constant
c_{q,d}
.
It is immediate to show that, for any
0<s<d
and
q>d-2
, we have
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}\in L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial B_{1})
.
The continuity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial B_{1}
also holds for
q=d-2
. We focus on the behavior of the potential across
\partial B_{1}
.
To this aim we consider, with no loss of generality,
(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q})(te_{1})
for
t
close to
1
.
We use the compact notation

(3.6)

(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q})(te_{1})=\begin{cases}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0ptv_{s}(t,1)\quad&\text{if }q=d-2,\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}v_{s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t,r})(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr\quad&\text{if }q>d-2,\end{cases}

where

v_{s}(t,r):=\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{|te_{1}-y|^{s}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(y),

for
t,r>0
and
t\neq r
.
By setting
y=r\omega
and using spherical coordinates on
\partial B_{1}
we have

\displaystyle v_{s}(t,r)

\displaystyle=C(d)r^{d-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{d-2}(\varphi)}{(t^{2}+r^{2}-2rt\cos(\varphi))^{s/2}}\,d\varphi

\displaystyle=\frac{C(d)r^{d-1}}{h^{s}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{d-2}(\varphi)}{(1+\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\cos(\varphi))^{s/2}}\,d\varphi,

where
h=\max(t,r)
,
\alpha=\min(t,r)/h\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(0,1)}
, and
C(d)>0
is a dimensional constant.
The integral above can be computed explicitly (see
[
11
, Formula 3.665–2]
with
\mu=\frac{d-1}{2}
,
\nu=\frac{s}{2}
,
a=\alpha
), and gives

(3.7)

v_{s}(t,r)=\frac{C(d)r^{d-1}}{h^{s}}B\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2};\frac{d}{2};\alpha^{2}\right).

We now treat the cases
0<s<d-1
and
d-1\leq s<d
separately.

Let
0<s<d-1
.
As recalled in Section
2.1
,
{}_{2}F_{1}
is continuous with respect to the last variable in the interval
[0,1]
whenever
\frac{d}{2}-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{s-d+2}{2}>0
, namely for
0<s<d-1
.
Hence, by (
3.7
)
v_{s}
is continuous in
t
.
For
q=d-2
, this immediately provides the continuity of the potential in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
q>d-2
the continuity follows by (
3.6
), (
3.7
), and the Dominated Convergence Theorem.
We note that for
0<s<d-1
and
q\geq d-2
, we have

\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})\geq d-1>s
.

Let now
d-1<s<d
. In this range of Riesz exponents, by (
2.4
),

(3.8)

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{s}{2},\frac{s-d+2}{2};\frac{d}{2};\alpha^{2}\right)\sim(1-\alpha^{2})^{d-s-1}\quad\text{for }\alpha\sim 1.

If
q=d-2
, the asymptotics in (
3.8
), with (
3.6
)–(
3.7
) and the fact that
d-s-1>-1
, implies that
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}
is integrable in a neighborhood of
\partial B_{1}
, hence it is locally integrable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The same asymptotics shows that the potential blows up on
\partial B_{1}
, thus it is not continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that for
d-1<s<d
and
q=d-2
, we have
\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})=d-1<s
.

Let now
q>d-2
. Let
0<\varepsilon<1/4
, and assume that
|t-1|<\varepsilon
. In what follows we consider the integral

(3.9)

\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}v_{s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t,r})(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr,

which is crucial in proving the continuity of the potential.
Since
|t-1|<\varepsilon
,
1-2\varepsilon<r<1
, and
t\neq r
, we have
\frac{1-2\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\leq\alpha<1
. Hence, for
\varepsilon
small enough, we can replace, up to constants, the hypergeometric function in (
3.7
) with its asymptotics given by (
3.8
). Namely,
we can estimate (
3.9
) as follows:

\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}v_{s}(t,r)(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr\sim\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{r^{d-1}}{h^{s}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr=:I.

We distinguish two cases, depending whether
t
is smaller or greater than
1
.

Let
t<1
. We split
I
into
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{t}\frac{r^{d-1}}{h^{s}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr=\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{t}\frac{r^{d-1}}{t^{2d-s-2}}(t^{2}-r^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{1}\frac{r^{d-1}}{h^{s}}(1-\alpha^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr=\int_{t}^{1}r^{s+1-d}(r^{2}-t^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr.

To estimate
I_{2}
it is convenient to further split
I_{2}=I_{3}+I_{4}
, where

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\frac{1+t}{2}}r^{s+1-d}(r^{2}-t^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr,

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1+t}{2}}^{1}r^{s+1-d}(r^{2}-t^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr.

For
q<d
we have

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq(2t)^{d-s-1}(1+t)^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\left(1-\frac{1+t}{2}\right)^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\int_{t}^{\frac{1+t}{2}}(r-t)^{d-s-1}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(1-\frac{1+t}{2}\right)^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\left(\frac{1+t}{2}-t\right)^{d-s}=C_{1}\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s}=C_{2}\left(1-t\right)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s},

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, and we used that
t\leq r\leq\frac{1+t}{2}
and
s+1-d>0
. An analogous reasoning leads to the same estimate for
q\geq d
.
Similarly, since
q>d-2
,

\displaystyle|I_{4}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}(1-t)^{d-s-1}\int_{\frac{1+t}{2}}^{1}(1-r)^{{}^{\frac{q-d}{2}}}\,dr=C_{3}(1-t)^{d-s-1}\left(1-\frac{1+t}{2}\right)^{\frac{q-d+2}{2}}

\displaystyle=C_{4}(1-t)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s},

where
C_{3},C_{4}>0
, and we used that
r-t\geq\frac{1-t}{2}
.
As for the term
I_{1}
, setting
\tau=t-r
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t-1+2\varepsilon}\frac{(t-\tau)^{d-1}}{t^{2d-s-2}}\tau^{d-s-1}(2t-\tau)^{d-s-1}(1-(t-\tau)^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\int_{0}^{t-1+2\varepsilon}\tau^{d-s-1}(1-t+\tau)^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,d\tau\leq C_{6}\int_{0}^{t-1+2\varepsilon}f(t,\tau)\,d\tau,

where
C_{5},C_{6}>0
, and

f(t,\tau)=\begin{cases}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\tau^{d-s-1}\quad&\text{if }\frac{q-d}{2}\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle\tau^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s-1}\quad&\text{if }-1<\frac{q-d}{2}<0.\end{cases}

Hence, we deduce the estimate

|I_{1}|\leq C_{7}\begin{cases}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt(t-1+2\varepsilon)^{d-s}\quad&\text{if }\frac{q-d}{2}\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle(t-1+2\varepsilon)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s}\quad&\text{if }-1<\frac{q-d}{2}<0\end{cases}

for some
C_{7}>0
.
In both cases, if
0<s<\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})
, we can find a positive
\beta
such that

|I_{1}|\leq C_{7}(t-1+2\varepsilon)^{\beta}\leq C_{7}(2\varepsilon)^{\beta}.

For
t>1
, instead, we write

I=\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{r^{d-1}}{t^{2d-s-2}}(t^{2}-r^{2})^{d-s-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr,

and, assuming
\frac{q+d}{2}-s>0
, we obtain

|I|\leq C_{8}\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}(1-r)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s-1}\,dr=C_{8}(2\varepsilon)^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s},

where
C_{8}>0
.

We conclude that, if

d-1<s<\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})
, with
q>d-2
, there exist positive constants
\varepsilon_{0}\leq 1/4
,
C_{9}
, and
\beta
such that
for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and any
|1-t|<\varepsilon
, we have

0\leq\int_{1-2\varepsilon}^{1}v_{s}(t,r)(1-r^{2})^{\frac{q-d}{2}}\,dr\leq C_{9}\left(|1-t|^{\frac{q+d}{2}-s}+\varepsilon^{\beta}\right).

Using this estimate it is easy to show that, under these assumptions, the potential is continuous also on
\partial B_{1}
.

The case
s=d-1
follows analogously, by using (
2.3
) instead of (
2.4
) in the estimate of
{}_{2}F_{1}
for
\alpha\sim 1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

From Lemma
3.2
, we infer that
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in particular when
q=s\in[d-2,d)\cap(0,d)
, and when
q=s+2
, with
s\in[d-4,d)\cap(0,d)
.
Such a continuity result will be relevant to
extend up to the boundary of
E
the formulas (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) of the following Theorem
3.4
.

We are now in a position to prove the Fourier representation of the potential
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{q}
inside
E
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
s\in(0,d)
and
q\in[d-2,+\infty)
. Let
W_{\!s}
be as in (
1.1
), let
E=RDB_{1}
be an ellipsoid, and let
\mu_{q}^{E}
be the push-forward of the measure
\mu_{q}
by the map
T^{E}
.
Then,

(3.10)

(W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{q})(x)=\tilde{c}_{d,s,q}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{1}{2};\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)

for every
x
in the interior of
E
,
where
\Psi=\widehat{W_{\!s}}|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
,

(3.11)

\tilde{c}_{d,s,q}=\frac{2^{s-d-1}\Gamma(1+\frac{q}{2})\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\pi^{d}\Gamma(1+\frac{q-s}{2})}

and

\alpha(x,\omega)=\frac{x\cdot\omega}{|DR^{T}\omega|}.

In particular, for
q=s
the potential function
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{s}
is constant in
E
and is given by

(3.12)

(W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{s})(x)=\frac{2^{s-d-2}}{\pi^{d}}s\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)

for every
x\in E
.
Finally, for
s\in[d-4,d)\cap(0,d)
and
q=s+2
, the potential function
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{s+2}
is, up to an additive constant, a quadratic function in
E
given by

(3.13)

\displaystyle(W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{s+2})(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}_{d,s,s+2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)-s\,\tilde{c}_{d,s,s+2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s+2}}(x\cdot\omega)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)

for every
x\in E
.

Remark 3.5
.

Theorem
3.4
provides an alternative way to derive formula (2.31) in
[
9
]
and in fact extends it to the entire range of
s\in(\max\{d-4,0\},d)
in any space dimension
d
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Formula (
3.10
) is a consequence of the inversion theorem for the Fourier transform.
However, we cannot apply the inversion formula directly to
W_{\!s}\ast\mu^{E}_{q}
, since its Fourier transform fails to be integrable at
\infty
for
2s-q\geq 1
. Indeed, by (
2.13
), Lemma
3.1
, and due to the asymptotic behavior (
2.10
) of the tail of the Bessel function at infinity, we have that
\widehat{W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}}=O(|\xi|^{s-d-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{1}{2}})
as
|\xi|\to+\infty
. We thus proceed by approximation.

For
r>0
let
B_{r}
be the ball of radius
r
centered at the origin and let
\chi_{B_{r}}
denote its characteristic function.
We set
\chi_{r}=|B_{r}|^{-1}\chi_{B_{r}}
and define
P_{r}=\chi_{r}\ast(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E})
.

By Lemma
3.2
,

P_{r}
belongs to
L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

P_{r}
converges pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial E
(thus almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) to
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
, as
r\to 0^{+}
.
If
0<s<\min(d,\frac{q+d}{2})
,
the function
P_{r}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
P_{r}
converges pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
, as
r\to 0^{+}
.

We note that
P_{r}
is a tempered distribution and
\widehat{P}_{r}=\widehat{\chi}_{r}\,\widehat{W}_{\!s}\,\widehat{\mu}_{q}^{E}
. To compute
\widehat{\chi}_{r}
we follow the computation in
[
12
, Appendix B.5]
and obtain

(3.14)

\widehat{\chi}_{r}(\xi)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{|B_{r}||\xi|^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{\frac{d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\rho|\xi|)\,d\rho=\frac{2^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})}{r^{\frac{d}{2}}}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{d}{2}}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(r|\xi|),

where we used that
|B_{r}|=r^{d}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}/\Gamma\big(1+\frac{d}{2}\big)
.

By the homogeneity of
\widehat{W_{\!s}}
, Lemma
3.1
, and the asymptotic behavior of Bessel functions in (
2.9
)–(
2.10
), we deduce that
\widehat{P}_{r}(\xi)=O(|\xi|^{s-d})

as
|\xi|\to 0^{+}
and
\widehat{P}_{r}(\xi)=O(|\xi|^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{3}{2}d-1})
as
|\xi|\to+\infty
.
Since
2s-q<d+2
in our setting, this implies that

\widehat{P}_{r}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Thus, we can apply the inversion theorem for the Fourier transform to
P_{r}
.
By Section
2.3
, Lemma
3.1
, (
3.3
), and (
3.14
) we obtain

(3.15)

P_{r}(x)=\frac{\tilde{c}_{q,d}}{r^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{\frac{3}{2}d-s}|DR^{T}\xi|^{\frac{q}{2}}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(r|\xi|)J_{\frac{q}{2}}(|DR^{T}\xi|)\Psi\left(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)\cos(x\cdot\xi)\,d\xi,

where

\tilde{c}_{q,d}=\frac{2^{\frac{q-d}{2}}}{\pi^{d}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{q}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(1+\frac{d}{2}\right).

In (
3.15
) the imaginary part can be dropped because
\widehat{\chi}_{r}\,\widehat{W}_{\!s}\,\widehat{\mu}_{q}^{E}
is even.
Writing (
3.15
) in polar coordinates yields

P_{r}(x)=\frac{\tilde{c}_{q,d}}{r^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{\frac{q}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-1}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(r\rho)J_{\frac{q}{2}}(\rho|DR^{T}\omega|)\cos(\rho\omega\cdot x)\,d\rho\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega).

Set

(3.16)

t=|DR^{T}\omega|\rho,\quad\beta(r,\omega)=\frac{r}{|DR^{T}\omega|},\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(x,\omega)=\frac{x\cdot\omega}{|DR^{T}\omega|}.

Changing variables in the integral with respect to
\rho
we obtain

P_{r}(x)=\frac{\tilde{c}_{q,d}}{r^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s-\frac{d}{2}}}I_{r}(x,\omega)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega),

where

I_{r}(x,\omega):=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-1}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(t\beta(r,\omega))J_{\frac{q}{2}}(t)\cos(t\alpha(x,\omega))\,dt.

To compute
I_{r}
we recall that

J_{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\cos(z)\qquad\text{ for }z\neq 0,

hence

\cos(t\alpha(x,\omega))=\cos(t|\alpha(x,\omega)|)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,t^{\frac{1}{2}}|\alpha(x,\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}J_{-\frac{1}{2}}(t|\alpha(x,\omega)|),

where the last expression on the right-hand side is extended at
t\alpha(x,\omega)=0
by continuity.
Thus, we can rewrite
I_{r}
as

(3.17)

I_{r}(x,\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}|\alpha(x,\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(t\beta(r,\omega))J_{\frac{q}{2}}(t)J_{-\frac{1}{2}}(t|\alpha(x,\omega)|)\,dt.

For the integral in (
3.17
) we use the formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\lambda-1}J_{\nu}(at)

\displaystyle J_{\mu}(bt)J_{\rho}(ct)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\lambda-1}a^{\nu}b^{\mu}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho))}{c^{\lambda+\mu+\nu}\Gamma(\mu+1)\Gamma(\nu+1)\Gamma(1-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+\mu+\nu-\rho))}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\times F_{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+\mu+\nu-\rho),\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho);\mu+1,\nu+1;\frac{b^{2}}{c^{2}},\frac{a^{2}}{c^{2}}\right),

which holds provided that

(3.18)

\Re(\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho)>0,\quad\Re(\lambda)<\frac{5}{2},\quad c>|a|+|b|,

see
[
1
, Formula 7.1]
.
For a fixed
x
in the interior of
E
,
r>0
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we apply the formula with
\lambda=s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2}
,
\nu=-\frac{1}{2}
,
\mu=\frac{d}{2}
,
\rho=\frac{q}{2}
,
a=|\alpha(x,\omega)|
,
b=\beta(r,\omega)
, and
c=1
.
Hence, conditions (
3.18
) translates into
s>0
,
2s-q<d+4
, and
|\alpha(x,\omega)|<1-\beta(r,\omega)
.
The first two conditions are trivially satisfied for
s
and
q
in the range under consideration. As for the last one, we observe that
x=pRD\eta
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
0\leq p<1
, and thus

|\alpha(x,\omega)|=\frac{|x\cdot\omega|}{|DR^{T}\omega|}=\frac{p|\eta\cdot DR^{T}\omega|}{|DR^{T}\omega|}\leq p<1

for every
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Since
\beta(r,\omega)\to 0
as
r\to 0^{+}
, uniformly with respect to
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exist
\delta\in(0,1)
and
r_{0}>0
such that for
r<r_{0}
we have

|\alpha(x,\omega)|+\beta(r,\omega)\leq\delta
for every
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For
x
in the interior of
E
,
r<r_{0}
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we can then evaluate the integral in (
3.17
), and deduce that

\displaystyle I_{r}(x,\omega)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}|\alpha(x,\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{2^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{\frac{d}{2}}(r,\omega)|\alpha(x,\omega)|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(1+\frac{q-s}{2})}

\displaystyle\times F_{4}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{d}{2}+1,\frac{1}{2};\beta^{2}(r,\omega),\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{s-\frac{q}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-1}\beta^{\frac{d}{2}}(r,\omega)\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)\Gamma(1+\frac{q-s}{2})}F_{4}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{d}{2}+1,\frac{1}{2};\beta^{2}(r,\omega),\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right),

where we have used property (iii) of the Gamma function.
By (
3
) and the definition of
\beta
in (
3.16
) we conclude that, for
x
in the interior of
E
and
r<r_{0}

(3.19)

P_{r}(x)=\tilde{c}_{d,s,q}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\Psi(\omega)}{|DR^{T}\omega|^{s}}F_{4}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{d}{2}+1,\frac{1}{2};\beta^{2}(r,\omega),\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega),

where
\tilde{c}_{d,s,q}
is the constant in (
3.11
).

We now pass to the limit as
r\to 0^{+}
.
By (
2.8
) and by the definition of
\beta
we have

(3.20)

\lim_{r\to 0}F_{4}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{d}{2}+1,\frac{1}{2};\beta^{2}(r,\omega),\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{s-q}{2},\frac{s}{2};\frac{1}{2};\alpha^{2}(x,\omega)\right).

Moreover, since
F_{4}
is analytic in its domain of definition and
|\alpha(x,\omega)|+\beta(r,\omega)\leq\delta<1
for
r<r_{0}
, the convergence (
3.20
) is uniform with respect to

\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Hence, passing to the limit in (
3.19
) and recalling that
P_{r}
converges to
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
pointwise in the interior of
E
, as
r\to 0^{+}
, we obtain (
3.10
) for every
x
in the interior of
E
.

The statement (
3.12
) for
q=s
follows immediately in the interior of
E
from (
3.10
), owing to (
2.6
) and property (i) of the Gamma function. Similarly, the statement (
3.13
) for
q=s+2
follows immediately in the interior of
E
from (
3.10
), owing to (
2.7
). In fact both formulas hold up to the boundary of
E
since in both cases the potential
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{q}^{E}
is continuous, as observed in Remark
3.3
.
∎

We conclude this section by proving Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Theorem
2.1
the minimizer of
I_{s}^{E}
exists, is unique, and is the unique measure satisfying the Euler–Lagrange
conditions (
EL1
)–(
EL3
).
Therefore, to conclude it is enough to show that
\mu_{s}^{E}
satisfies
(
EL1
)–(
EL3
).
Condition (
EL1
) is trivially satisfied. Conditions (
EL2
)–(
EL3
)
follow from Theorem
3.4
with
q=s
.
∎

4.
Deviation from the radially symmetric case for
s<d-2

We recall that in Section
3
we have shown that, for
s\geq d-2
, the minimizer
\mu_{s}^{E}
of
I_{s}^{E}
is insensitive to the anisotropy
\Phi
, and in particular if
E=B_{1}
we have
\mu_{s}^{B_{1}}=\mu_{\iso,s}
.

In this section we show that the equality above may fail for
s<d-2
by providing an explicit example of a kernel
W_{\!s}
of the form (
1.1
) with continuous and non-negative Fourier transform, for which
\mu_{\iso,d-2}
is not the minimizer of
I_{s}^{B_{1}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
s\in(0,d-2)
.
Let
W_{\!s}
be as in (
1.1
) with
\Phi:{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\Phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}x_{i}^{2}\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

with
\alpha_{i}>0
for
i=1,\dots,d
.
Then
\widehat{W_{s}}
is non-negative on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
if and only if

(4.1)

\alpha_{i}\leq\frac{1}{d-s-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}\qquad\text{ for every }i=1,\dots,d.

Proof.

Let
p_{0}
and
p_{2,i}
, for
i=1,\dots,d
, be the homogeneous harmonic polynomials defined as

p_{0}\equiv 1,\quad p_{2,i}(x)=(d-1)x_{i}^{2}-\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}^{2},

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we write
\Phi
as

\Phi(\omega)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}p_{0}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}p_{2,i},

and hence, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x\neq 0
, the kernel
W_{\!s}
can be rewritten as

W_{\!s}(x)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\frac{p_{0}}{|x|^{s}}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\frac{p_{2,i}}{|x|^{s+2}}.

From (
2.12
) we deduce that for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\xi\neq 0
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{\!s}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}2^{d-s}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d-s}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}2^{d-s}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{s}{2})}\frac{p_{2,i}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2+d-s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}2^{d-s}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d-s}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i}\frac{d-s}{s}p_{2,i}\left(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)\right),

where we have used property (i) of the Gamma function.
In particular, for
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i}\frac{d-s}{s}p_{2,i}(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i}\frac{d-s}{s}(d\omega_{i}^{2}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\omega_{j}^{2}-\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}(d-s)\omega_{i}^{2}\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left((1-d+s)\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}\right)\omega_{i}^{2}.

Thus, (
4.2
) can be rewritten as

\widehat{W}_{\!s}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\xi|^{d-s}}2^{d-s}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left((1-d+s)\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}\right)\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}.

Since
1-d+s<0
for
s<d-2
, we conclude that
\widehat{W}_{\!s}\geq 0
if and only if (
4.1
) is satisfied.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
s\in(0,d-2)
. Let
W_{\!s}
be as in (
1.1
) with
\Phi:{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\Phi(x)=\frac{d-1}{d-s-1}x_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}=1+\frac{s}{d-s-1}x_{1}^{2}\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Then
\Phi
is continuous, even, and strictly positive, and
\widehat{W_{\!s}}

is continuous and non-negative on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. However, the measure
\mu_{\iso,d-2}
is not the minimizer of the energy
I_{s}^{B_{1}}
with kernel
W_{\!s}
.

Proof.

All the properties of
\Phi
and
W_{\!s}
are straightforward, except for the sign of
\widehat{W}_{\!s}
.
By Lemma
4.1
the non-negativity of
\widehat{W}_{\!s}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is equivalent to

(4.3)

1\leq\frac{1}{d-s-1}\left(d-2+\frac{d-1}{d-s-1}\right).

A simple computation shows that (
4.3
) reduces to
s^{2}-sd\leq 0
, which is true for
0<s<d-2
.

To prove that the measure
\mu_{\iso,d-2}
is not the minimizer of
I_{s}^{B_{1}}
, it is enough to show that

(4.4)

A:=(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2})(e_{2})-(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2})(e_{1})\neq 0.

Indeed, since
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by Lemma
3.2
, equation (
4.4
) implies that
W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2}
is not constant

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
-a.e. on
\partial B_{1}
, contradicting (
EL2
).

We note that

(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2})(e_{1})=c_{d-2,d}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{|e_{1}-\omega|^{s}}\left(1+\frac{s}{d-s-1}\frac{(1-\omega_{1})^{2}}{|e_{1}-\omega|^{2}}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)

and by the change of variables
\tilde{\omega}:=(\omega_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{3},\dots,\omega_{d})
we can rewrite

\displaystyle(W_{\!s}\ast\mu_{\iso,d-2})(e_{2})

\displaystyle=c_{d-2,d}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{|e_{2}-\omega|^{s}}\left(1+\frac{s}{d-s-1}\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{|e_{2}-\omega|^{2}}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)

\displaystyle=c_{d-2,d}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{|e_{1}-\tilde{\omega}|^{s}}\left(1+\frac{s}{d-s-1}\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{2}^{2}}{|e_{1}-\tilde{\omega}|^{2}}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\tilde{\omega}).

Therefore,

A=c_{d-2,d}\frac{s}{d-s-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\omega_{2}^{2}-(1-\omega_{1})^{2}}{|e_{1}-\omega|^{s+2}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega).

Since
|e_{1}-\omega|^{2}=2-2\omega_{1}
for
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
we have

|e_{1}-\omega|^{s+2}=2^{\frac{s+2}{2}}(1-\omega_{1})^{\frac{s+2}{2}}\quad\text{ for }\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

Hence, the claim (
4.4
) reduces to showing that

(4.5)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\omega_{2}^{2}-(1-\omega_{1})^{2}}{(1-\omega_{1})^{\frac{s+2}{2}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\omega)\neq 0.

We consider separately the two cases
d\geq 4
and
d=3
.
We start with the case
d\geq 4
.
Passing to spherical coordinates, the integral in (
4.5
) can be rewritten as
C(d)(I_{1}-I_{2})
, where
C(d)>0
is a dimensional constant, and

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{d}(\varphi_{1})\cos^{2}(\varphi_{2})\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})}{(1-\cos(\varphi_{1}))^{\frac{s+2}{2}}}\,d\varphi_{1}d\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}(1-\cos(\varphi_{1}))^{\frac{2-s}{2}}\sin^{d-2}(\varphi_{1})\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\,d\varphi_{1}d\varphi_{2}.

To conclude we need to show that
I_{1}-I_{2}\neq 0
. We write
I_{1}=I_{3}I_{4}
, where

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d}(\varphi_{1})(1-\cos(\varphi_{1}))^{\frac{-2-s}{2}}\,d\varphi_{1},

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos^{2}(\varphi_{2})\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\,d\varphi_{2}.

By the sine and the cosine duplication formula and by (
2.2
) we obtain

(4.6)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=2^{d-\frac{s+2}{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-2-s}\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}}{2}\right)\cos^{d}\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}}{2}\right)\,d\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{d+1-\frac{s+2}{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{d-2-s}(\varphi_{1})\cos^{d}(\varphi_{1})\,d\varphi_{1}=2^{d-1-\frac{s}{2}}B\left(\frac{d-s-1}{2},\frac{d+1}{2}\right),

whereas

(4.7)

I_{4}=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos^{2}(\varphi_{2})\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\,d\varphi_{2}=B\left(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right).

Similarly, we write
I_{2}=I_{5}I_{6}
, where

(4.8)

\displaystyle I_{5}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}(1-\cos(\varphi_{1}))^{\frac{2-s}{2}}\sin^{d-2}(\varphi_{1})\,d\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{d-\frac{s}{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{d-s}(\varphi_{1})\cos^{d-2}(\varphi_{1})\,d\varphi_{1}=2^{d-1-\frac{s}{2}}B\left(\frac{d-s+1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)

and

(4.9)

\displaystyle I_{6}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\,d\varphi_{2}=2^{d-2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\cos^{d-3}(\varphi_{2})\,d\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle=2^{d-3}B\left(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right).

Combining (
4.6
)–(
4.9
) yields

\displaystyle I_{1}-I_{2}=I_{3}I_{4}-I_{5}I_{6}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1-\frac{s}{2}}B\left(\frac{d-s-1}{2},\frac{d+1}{2}\right)B\left(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right)

\displaystyle-2^{2d-4-\frac{s}{2}}B\left(\frac{d-s+1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)B\left(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right).

By (
2.1
) the previous equality can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I_{1}-I_{2}=2^{d-1-\frac{s}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-1)}{\Gamma(d-\frac{s}{2})}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{d-s-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)-2^{d-3}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-s+1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-1)}{\Gamma(d-2)}\right).

By properties (i) and (iii) of the Gamma function we have that

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{d-s+1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d-s-1}{2}\right)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d-s-1}{2}\right)

and
\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}
.
Therefore,
I_{1}-I_{2}=0
if and only if

(4.10)

\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\Gamma(d-2)-2^{d-4}(d-s-1)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)=0.

Finally, properties (ii) of the Gamma function gives

\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)=2^{3-d}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(d-2),

hence (
4.10
) reduces to

\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\Gamma(d-2)(2-d+s)=0,

which is never satisfied for
s<d-2
. This concludes the proof for
d\geq 4
.

When
d=3
, the previous computations can be repeated with the only difference being that the integration interval for
\varphi_{2}
is
(0,2\pi)
, instead of
(0,\pi)
.
This change simply introduces an extra factor of 2 in the expressions for both
I_{4}
and
I_{6}
, so the same calculations still lead to the desired conclusion.
∎

A simple continuity argument leads to the following.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
s\in(0,d-2)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we set

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{d-1-\varepsilon}{d-s-1}x_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\quad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

and for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x\neq 0
,

W_{s,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{s}}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{x}{|x|}\bigg)

while
W_{s,\varepsilon}(0)=+\infty
.

Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, depending on
s
, such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}

the profile
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
is continuous, even, and strictly positive,

\widehat{W_{s,\varepsilon}}

is continuous and strictly positive on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, but the measure
\mu_{\iso,d-2}
is not the minimizer of the energy
I_{s}^{B_{1}}
with kernel
W_{\!s}
replaced by
W_{s,\varepsilon}
.
