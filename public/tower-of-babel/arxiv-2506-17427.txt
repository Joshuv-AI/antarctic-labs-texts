Detection of positron in-flight annihilation from the Galaxy
J. Knödlseder, K. Sabri, P. Jean, P. von Ballmoos, G. Skinner, W. Collmar

Abstract
Context. Although the annihilation of positrons towards the Galactic centre was established more than 50 years ago through the detection of a 511 keV γ \gamma -ray line, the origin of the positrons remains unknown. The γ \gamma -ray line should be accompanied by continuum emission from positron in-flight annihilation, which has not until now been detected. Aims. We aim to detect positron in-flight annihilation emission as it provides information on the kinetic energy of the positrons that is key in determining the origin of Galactic positrons. Methods. We analysed archival data obtained by the COMPTEL instrument on the Compton Gamma-Ray Observatory satellite to search for positron in-flight annihilation emission at MeV energies. Results. Our analysis revealed extended MeV emission towards the bulge of the Galaxy, which we attribute to in-flight annihilation of positrons produced with kinetic energies of ∼ 2 \sim 2 MeV. The observed spectrum suggests that positrons are produced quasi mono-energetically, which could occur by the annihilation of dark matter particles with masses of ∼ 3 \sim 3 MeV or through bulk motion in the jet of the microquasar 1E~1740.7–2942 . We furthermore detected a point-like MeV emission component towards the Galactic centre that is the plausible low-energy counterpart of the Fermi/LAT source 4FGL~J1745.6–2859 . The broad band spectrum of the source may be explained by the injection of pair plasma from the supermassive black hole Sgr A$^∗$ into the interstellar medium, which would also explain the point-like 511 keV line emission component that was discovered by INTEGRAL/SPI at the Galactic centre. Conclusions. The observed positron in-flight annihilation spectrum towards the Galactic bulge excludes β + \beta^{+} decays from radioactivities as well as any mechanism producing highly relativistic positrons as origin of the Galactic bulge positrons.



1
Introduction

Observations of the Galactic centre region in the 1970s revealed a 511 keV
\gamma
-ray
line
and continuum emission below 511 keV, which was attributed to two- and three-photon annihilation of
thermalised positrons
(
Johnson et al. 1972
;
Leventhal et al. 1978
)
.
The most recent observations with the Spectrometer on INTEGRAL (SPI) suggest the existence of
several positron annihilation emission components, including extended emission from the Galactic
bulge that is slightly offset from the Galactic centre, a point-like emission component
at the Galactic centre with a characteristic scale no more than a few hundred pc,
and extended emission from the Galactic disk with poorly constrained
morphology
(
Bouchet et al. 2010
;
Skinner et al. 2015
;
Siegert et al. 2016
)
.

Despite the progress on the characterisation of the emission, the origin of the positrons remains an
open question, with many possible channels for positron production being discussed in the
literature
(
Prantzos et al. 2011
;
Churazov et al. 2020
)
.
The initial kinetic energy of positrons varies strongly between these channels, yet observations of the
511 keV line (or the three-photon continuum emission) probe thermalised positrons that have lost
their initial energy due to interactions with ambient medium, erasing the link to their sources.
In addition, during the slow-down, positrons may travel substantial distances from the production
site
(
Jean et al. 2009
)
, blurring the link between source distribution and emission morphology.

Positrons may also annihilate in flight before having slowed down, giving rise to Doppler shifted
two-photon continuum emission in the energy interval from
m_{e}c^{2}/2
to

E_{p}+m_{e}c^{2}\,3/2
, where
m_{e}c^{2}
is the rest energy and
E_{p}
the kinetic energy of the
positrons.
For strongly relativistic positrons, the fraction of in-flight annihilations in the ISM may reach several
tens of percent, implying that a noticeable flux from positron in-flight annihilation is
expected
(
Agaronyan & Atoyan 1981
)
.
Previous attempts to place limits on the positron injection energy include an analysis of
INTEGRAL/SPI data that provided unconstraining upper limits
(
Churazov et al. 2011
)
,
and comparisons of expected in-flight
\gamma
-ray
emission with estimates of observed flux
from within a defined patch of sky that place an upper limit of 3–7.5 MeV
on the injection energy depending on the ionisation state of the ISM
(
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
;
Sizun et al. 2006
)
.

The latter limits were based on published spectra of Galactic diffuse emission obtained using
observations with the Compton Telescope (COMPTEL), an instrument operating on the Compton
Gamma-Ray Observatory (CGRO) during 1991–2000.
Since COMPTEL did not have direct imaging capabilities, data analysis relied on forward folding
of sky distributions with the instrument response function, allowing reconstruction of images and
spectra in the 0.75–30 MeV energy range
(
Schönfelder et al. 1993
)
.
Furthermore, the instrumental background that exceeds by far any celestial signal was
modelled in an iterative approach, introducing some circularity that makes resulting images
and spectra dependent on the assumed celestial intensity distribution
(
Bloemen et al. 1999
)
.
In particular, emission components that were not explicitly searched for will be suppressed,
which implies that upper limits on the positron injection energy that are derived from
published COMPTEL spectra of Galactic ridge emission are not very constraining.

To overcome this limitation, we performed the first dedicated analysis of COMPTEL data that
explicitly searched for the signal from positron in-flight annihilation on top the emission that is
known to be associated with the Galactic ridge.
This analysis was done as part of an effort to reduce the environmental footprint of astrophysical
research by addressing science questions using existing data
(
Knödlseder et al. 2022a
;
Knödlseder et al. 2024
)
.
We find that such an analysis allows the identification of continuum emission, one component of
which has morphology, and spectrum suggesting that it is due to in-flight annihilation from positrons.

2
Observations and analysis methods

2.1
Data preparation

We used for our study COMPTEL data that are publicly available at NASA’s High Energy Astrophysics
Science Archive Research Center (HEASARC).
Only data recorded before the second reboost of the CGRO spacecraft were used because the second
reboost resulted in an important increase of the instrumental background at low energies, substantially
decreasing the instrument sensitivity
(
Weidenspointner 1999
;
Ryan & Collmar 2023
)
.
COMPTEL data are split into so-called viewing periods, and our analysis covered 215 viewing
periods, starting from viewing period 1.0 and ending with viewing period 616.1, spanning dates from
16 May 1991 to 18 March 1997.

COMPTEL observed the sky in the energy range from 0.75 MeV to 30 MeV, and we analysed
the data either using the full energy range or using one of two sub-bands, spanning 0.75–3 MeV and
3–30 MeV, respectively.
The sub-bands were split at 3 MeV since it turned out that around that energy there is a distinct
change in the spatial morphology of the emission from the Galactic bulge.
The data in each energy band were split into logarithmically-spaced energy bins, excluding the
1.7–1.9 MeV energy range that contains signal from the 1.8 MeV radioactive decay line of
26
Al.
In total 16 energy bins were used for the full energy band to accurately determine any spectral cut-offs
in the emission, while 5 and 4 energy bins were used for the low and high sub-bands, respectively
(there is one more energy bin for the low sub-band compared to the high sub-band due to
the exclusion of the 1.7–1.9 MeV interval from the 1.5–2.1 MeV energy bin, splitting this energy bin
into two).

For each energy bin, events were binned per viewing period into standard COMPTEL data cubes
spanned by Compton scatter direction
(\chi,\psi)
and Compton scattering angle
\bar{\varphi}
.
Bin sizes of
\Delta\chi=\Delta\psi=1\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
and
\Delta\bar{\varphi}=2\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
were used throughout the analysis.
For each energy bin, data cubes for individual viewing periods were combined into a single data cube
that was centred on the Galactic centre and that spanned
\pm 120\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
in Galactic longitude and

\pm 70\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
in Galactic latitude.
This large data cube provides a good contrast between the inner Galactic plane region that is rich in
Galactic
\gamma
-ray
photons and high-latitude regions that are essentially free of such photons,
allowing for a clear separation of
\gamma
-ray
point sources, Galactic ridge emission, and any
excess emission at the Galactic centre.
At the same time, the data cube excludes events from the Crab nebula and pulsar which is by far
the brightest MeV
\gamma
-ray
source, avoiding any interference in our analysis by events
from this source.

2.2
Data analysis

All data analysis was performed using version 2.1.0 of the ctools
(
Knödlseder et al. 2016
)
and GammaLib

(
Knödlseder 2011
)
software packages.
Data were analysed by adjusting model components to the observed events using a maximum
likelihood method.
Each celestial model component consists of parametrised spatial and spectral models that were fitted
jointly to the data cubes for all energy bins in a given energy band.
We used the
comlixfit
script for model fitting which derives a model of the instrumental background
event distribution in the data cubes while simultaneously adjusting the parameters of the celestial
model components
(
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
)
.
The most critical parameter of the algorithm is
N_{\rm incl}
which specifies the number of

\bar{\varphi}
layers of the three-dimensional data cubes that is used to estimate the background
model.
As demonstrated by
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
, large values of
N_{\rm incl}
conserve the celestial
source fluxes but may lead to substantial residuals attributed to an inadequate modelling of the
instrumental background event distribution.
Smaller values of
N_{\rm incl}
reduce these residuals, but this goes at the expense of suppressing
some of the source flux and detection significance.
In order to minimise background residuals while still conserving source fluxes we used

N_{\rm incl}=11
in this study.
We used the BGDLIXF algorithm for background modelling which is an evolution of the BGDLIXE
algorithm that is described in
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
and that properly treats background event
rate variations between and within viewing periods (see Appendix
A
).

The celestial
\gamma
-ray
emission was modelled using components for the in-flight positron annihilation
emission, ten point sources that were previously detected in MeV
\gamma
-ray
s in the examined
region (see Appendix
B
), and components for Galactic ridge and cosmic
\gamma
-ray
emission.
The spectra of all point sources and the cosmic
\gamma
-ray
background were modelled using power
laws, more elaborate spectral forms were used for the other components.
Spectral parameters of all components were adjusted during model fitting, with the exception of the
cosmic
\gamma
-ray
background component that is spatially isotropic, and hence cannot be
distinguished from the instrumental background through spatial model fitting.
We therefore fixed this component to the spectrum

I(E)=1.12\times 10^{-4}(E/{5\,{\rm MeV}})^{-2.2}

s
-1

sr

suggested by
Weidenspointner 1999
.

The Galactic ridge emission at MeV energies is believed to originate primarily from
diffuse Inverse Compton emission of cosmic ray electrons, yet contributions from unresolved
source populations cannot be excluded
(
Bloemen et al. 1999
;
Strong & Moskalenko 2000a
)
.
To avoid any biases due to an assumed spatial distribution of Galactic diffuse emission,
and to allow for the possible presence of unresolved point sources,
we implemented an empirical model using 2-dimensional asymmetric Gaussian functions
for the spatial distribution.
For the low- and high-energy band fits, a single 2-dimensional asymmetric Gaussian function
was used as spatial model, with a power-law spectral model for which we adjusted the prefactor
and index.
For the full energy band fits we used two 2-dimensional asymmetric Gaussian functions to
accommodate different spatial morphologies of the Galactic ridge emission at low and high
energies, and we modelled the spectra of both components using log-parabola functions for which
we adjusted the prefactor, the index and the curvature during the fit.
All analysis results are available in numerical form for download at
https://zenodo.org/records/TBD
.

3
Results

3.1
Excess emission maps

We searched for any excess emission towards the Galactic bulge region by generating Test Statistic
(TS) maps of extent
200\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}\times 50\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
in Galactic longitude and latitude around the Galactic centre
following the method described in
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
.
The maps were derived by fitting a point-source model for a
1\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
grid of source locations on top of a
reference model that includes all known
\gamma
-ray
sources in the region of interest, comprising Galactic
ridge emission, point sources and cosmic
\gamma
-ray
background, as well as a description of the instrumental
background.
As the morphology of the excess emission changes within the COMPTEL energy range, we derived
separate maps for the
0.75-3
MeV and
3-30
MeV energy bands that are shown in
Fig.
1
.

The TS map for the
0.75-3
MeV energy band reveals significant excess emission towards the
Galactic centre with a morphology that is clearly extended and an emission peak that appears
slightly offset towards negative longitude.
This is the first time that such MeV excess emission from the Galactic bulge is reported, and the
resemblance of the TS map to the first maps of 511 keV line emission revealed by INTEGRAL/SPI

(
Knödlseder et al. 2005
)
is striking.
The map shows few features outside the Galactic bulge excess, indicating that the reference model
adequately describes the emission from the Galactic ridge and the known point sources within the
analysis region.
Significantly excess emission is also seen in the
3-30
MeV energy band with a morphology that
is point-like and located at the Galactic centre.
Besides the Galactic centre source there is no other significant excess in the map.

Figure 2
:

TS maps for the 0.75–3 MeV (left) and 3–30 MeV (right) energy bands for different Galactic
ridge emission models (see text).

To illustrate that the excess emissions are not artefacts of the Galactic ridge emission model
and that they persist under changes of the model, we show in Fig.
2
TS maps that
were derived using alternative Galactic ridge emission models.
Galactic ridge emission at MeV energies is believed to originate primarily from diffuse Inverse
Compton (IC) emission of cosmic-ray electrons
(
Strong et al. 2000
)
, hence we replaced the
2-dimensional asymmetric Gaussian functions in our reference model by either an IC map employed
for past COMPTEL data analysis
(
Youssefi & Strong 1991
, designated by “COMPASS”;)
or by an IC map
derived using GALPROP
(
Ackermann et al. 2012
)
.
The resulting TS maps confirm extended
0.75-3
MeV excess emission towards the Galactic bulge
and the point-like
3-30
MeV excess at the Galactic centre.
The
0.75-3
MeV excess is detected at significance levels that exceed those for the reference
model, showing now a slightly elongated morphology along the Galactic plane.
This suggests that not all Galactic ridge emission is properly accounted for by the IC maps, which
is confirmed by the presence of significant residual Galactic disk emission in the
3-30
MeV maps
at negative Galactic longitudes.
Obviously, the IC maps do not accurately describe the observed Galactic ridge emission, which was
actually the main reason why we employed for our reference model 2-dimensional asymmetric Gaussian
functions that provide a better description of the Galactic ridge emission morphology.

Past COMPTEL data analyses compensated somewhat these inadequacies by including in addition
a map of Bremsstrahlung emission into the model of Galactic ridge emission, although it is unclear
whether the Bremsstrahlung process actually contributes to the observed emission, or whether it
rather mimics a population of unresolved point sources
(
Bloemen et al. 1999
;
Strong & Moskalenko 2000b
)
.
Including Bremsstrahlung maps in the analysis

(
Strong et al. 1993
;
Ackermann et al. 2012
, using maps derived for COMPTEL or using GALPROP;)

results in TS maps that show less residual features outside the Galactic bulge compared to the maps
derived using only the IC templates, without reaching the low level of residuals that is obtained with
the reference model.
The
0.75-3
MeV excess is confirmed, demonstrating once more that its existence does not depend
on the choice of the Galactic ridge emission model.
The significance of the
3-30
MeV Galactic centre excess is more sensitive to the choice the
Galactic ridge emission model, dropping to below
3\sigma
when a combination of GALPROP IC and
Bremsstrahlung maps is used.
This drop is related to a peak in the Bremsstrahlung map towards the Galactic centre which
reflects the high ISM density in the central molecular zone (CMZ).
The flux in the
3-30
MeV Galactic centre excess may therefore be overestimated for our
reference model.

3.2
Characterisation of excess emissions

To characterise the excess emissions, we added a 2-dimensional Gaussian spatial model with a
power law spectrum to the reference model and fitted its free parameters (position, extension,

7.8\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}\pm 1.8\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}

7.5\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}

3.4
Spectral Energy Distributions

To advance our understanding of the nature of the excess emissions, we derived spectral energy
distributions (SEDs) for the extended and point-like components by performing model fits over the full

0.75-30
MeV COMPTEL energy range.
To separate the SED of the extended component from that of the point-like component we jointly
fitted two models on top of the reference model: a 2-dimensional symmetric Gaussian shape to
model the extended low energy emission (hereafter the “Bulge component”) and a point source
to model the point-like high energy emission (hereafter the “Core component”).
We fixed the spatial parameters of both components to the values found in the analysis of the

0.75-3
MeV and
3-30
MeV energy bands.
The spectra of both components were modelled using so called “bin functions”, where piecewise
power laws with fixed spectral index
\Gamma=2
and free intensity were logarithmically spaced
over the full energy range.
In that way the number of spectral points in the SED becomes independent from the number of
energy bins used for the data.
We chose 8 spectral points for the Bulge component and 3 spectral points for the Core component
to capture as well as possible the spectral evolutions while assuring signal detection over a broad
as possible energy range.
In addition, we also performed fits where the bin functions were replaced by analytical spectral laws
such as power laws, exponentially cut-off power laws and log-parabola spectra to study the spectral
evolution of the excess emissions independently of any spectral binning.
We translated uncertainties in the spectral parameters into uncertainly bands that take the covariance
between the spectral parameters of the Bulge and Core components into account.

3.5
Comparison to expected positron in-flight annihilation spectra

As the morphology of the Bulge component suggests positron in-flight annihilation as the possible
origin of the emission, we investigated whether also the spectrum is compatible with such a
scenario, and whether conclusions can be drawn on the underlying positron sources.
For that purpose, we modelled the
\gamma
-ray
emission from a population of energetic positrons interacting
with electrons in the ISM following the prescriptions in
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
,
Sizun et al. 2006
and

Prantzos et al. 2011
.
While these prescriptions are limited to the case of stationary injection of mono-energetic positrons
into the ISM, we extended the formalism to arbitrary injection spectra.
Following
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
, we normalised the resulting
\gamma
-ray
intensities to be in units of equivalent
511 keV line flux
\Phi_{511}
using the observed positronium fraction of
f_{\rm Ps}
= 0.967

(
Jean et al. 2006
)
, so that when the models are fitted to the COMPTEL data the model normalisation
is given in units of
\Phi_{511}
(see Appendix
C
).

4
Discussion

4.1
Possible origin of the Bulge component

The resemblance of the morphology of the Bulge component to that of the 511 keV line bulge emission
is striking.
Both emissions are extended, with comparable sizes, and an emission centroid that is slightly
offset from the Galactic centre towards negative longitudes.
The characteristics of this emission morphology are unique, having no counterpart in the gas components
or in the stellar populations of the Galaxy
(
Prantzos et al. 2011
)
.
Consequently, we suggest that the Bulge component originates most plausibly from positron in-flight
annihilation.

After production, positrons do not annihilate immediately owing to the relatively low densities of the
ISM
(
Prantzos et al. 2011
)
.
Survival times may reach from
\sim 10^{3}
yr in the dense regions around the Galactic centre
to several times
10^{5}
yr in the Galactic disk, during which time the positrons will propagate away
from their sources.
As positron in-flight annihilation occurs before positron thermalisation, in-flight annihilation emission
should have a more compact morphology compared with the 511 keV line emission.
On the other hand, because in-flight annihilation is most probable after positrons have lost
much of their initial energy, it will tend to occur after positrons have travelled substantial distances,
hence the extension of the emissions may not be too different.
Comparison of the morphology of the Bulge component to morphology models of 511 keV line
emission (cf. Table
2
) suggests that the Bulge component is more compact than
the 511 keV line emission, in line with expectations if the Bulge component originates from
positron in-flight annihilation.

The SED of the Bulge component is difficult to explain with cosmic-ray induced Inverse Compton or
Bremsstrahlung emissions as those have broad-band spectra over many decades in energy and do
not show the observed spectral cut-off
(
Strong et al. 2000
)
.
As illustrated in Fig.
4
, the Bulge component is best fitted by a
quasi-monoenergetic injection of positrons with kinetic energies of
\sim
2 MeV into the ISM.
This excludes radioactive
\beta^{+}
decays as noticeable contributors to
the Bulge component, as their positron spectra are limited to sub-MeV energies, producing
\gamma
-ray
spectra that cut-off below
\sim
1 MeV.
Some reacceleration of
\beta^{+}
decay positrons would be needed to increase the injection energies
up to the observed values, yet it is unclear how such a scenario would produce the quasi-monoenergetic
energy distribution that is observed for the Bulge component.
Scenarios that inject highly relativistic positrons into the ISM, such as cosmic-ray secondaries produced
from
\pi^{+}
decays, normal pulsars, millisecond pulsars, and magnetars are also excluded as positron
source of the Bulge component, since they would lead to a positron in-flight annihilation spectrum
that would extend to much higher energies.

One of the proposed sources that would produce mono-energetic positrons with the required kinetic
energies is the annihilation of light dark matter particles
(
Boehm et al. 2004
)
.
If such particles exist, their annihilation would result in MeV
\gamma
-ray
emission dominated by positron in-flight
annihilation, with additional smaller contributions from internal and external Bremsstrahlung

(
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
;
Sizun et al. 2006
;
Boehm & Uwer 2006
)
.
Fitting the expected spectra to the COMPTEL data suggests a dark matter particle mass of
\sim
3 MeV
and implies a 511 keV line flux of (1.5
\pm
0.3)
\times
10
-3

photons
cm
-2

s
-1
.
For a source at the distance of the Galactic centre this flux corresponds to a positron production
rate of (2.3
\pm
0.5)
\times
10
43

s
-1
.
This is at the upper end of the range of positron annihilation rates that were inferred from INTEGRAL/SPI
511 keV line data for the bulge component, but compatible with estimates for the entire Galaxy, including the
bulge and a putative disk component
(
Churazov et al. 2020
)
.
This may indicate that some of the positrons escape from the bulge into the disk region where they
thermalise and finally annihilate, producing the 511 keV line disk emission.

The offsets of the centroids of the bulge emissions towards negative longitudes present however a challenge
for the dark matter scenario.
Offsets could in principle be explained by ISM overdensities that enhance positron annihilation, yet the
ISM distribution towards the Galactic centre is offset towards positive and not negative
longitudes
(
Ferrière 2008
)
.
This excludes the ISM distribution as an explanation and suggests that the offset is rather related to the
positron injection site.
While it cannot be excluded that the barycentre of the dark matter particles is offset from the dynamical
centre of the Galaxy
(
Kuhlen et al. 2013
)
, tidal forces produced by baryons which dominate the gravitational
potential near the Galactic centre should remove such an offset within
\sim
10 million
years
(
Gorbunov & Tinyakov 2013
)
.

Another issue with the dark matter hypothesis is the high ISM density in the CMZ around the Galactic
centre
(
Ferrière 2008
)
that implies that any positron injected into that zone will annihilate within a
few kyr and will not propagate more than a few tens of pc at most.
Therefore, positron sources located within the CMZ should lead to strongly peaked annihilation
emission towards the Galactic centre with an angular extent that is less than the

\sim 2\mbox{${}^{\circ}$}
size of the CMZ
(
Alexis et al. 2014
)
.
Since the Bulge component does not show such a compact source (cf. Table
2
) it is
unlikely that the source giving rise to the extended Bulge component is located in the immediate vicinity
of the Galactic centre.
This excludes in particular
Sgr A$^∗$
as the main positron source for the Bulge component.

A candidate positron source that is seen near the Galactic centre but that is located at the edge or maybe
even outside the CMZ is the microquasar
1E~1740.7–2942

(
Luque-Escamilla et al. 2015
;
Tetarenko et al. 2020
)
, and
intriguingly its position coincides with the centroid of the 511 keV line bulge emission
(
Skinner et al. 2015
)

as well as with the centroid of the Bulge component.

1E~1740.7–2942
has been dubbed the “Great Annihilator” since claims for transient positron
annihilation features in its spectrum have been reported in the past
(
Bouchet et al. 1991
;
Sunyaev et al. 1991
)
.
Although these observations have been questioned
(
Prantzos et al. 2011
)
,
1E~1740.7–2942
is still
a plausible source of positrons, since microquasars may produce positrons in the inner region of the
accretion disk or the hot corona, which are then channeled into the ISM through their jets
(
Guessoum et al. 2006
)
.
While microquasars are variable sources, the variability occurs on time scales that are much shorter
than the positron lifetime in the ISM, hence irrespectively of whether positrons are produced
during outbursts or rather continuously, microquasars should act as steady sources of positron
annihilation.
The time averaged jet power of
1E~1740.7–2942
is estimated to
(0.7-3.5)\times 10^{37}

erg
s
-1

(
Tetarenko et al. 2020
)
, which is sufficient to produce the observed positron annihilation
luminosity of the Bulge component if the jet is composed of a pure pair plasma.
The plasma’s bulk motion will inject positrons with relatively constant kinetic energies into the
ISM
(
Heinz & Sunyaev 2002
)
, which would explain the quasi-monoenergetic positron spectrum that we inferred
for the Bulge component.
Bulk Lorentz factors of microquasar jets are uncertain, but values of up to
\Gamma\sim
4 are
discussed in the literature
(
Luque-Escamilla et al. 2015
;
Zdziarski et al. 2022
)
.
This would correspond to kinetic energies of
\sim
1.5 MeV, which is close to the value inferred for
the Bulge component.
After injection, the positrons will propagate along magnetic field lines away from the source while slowing
down due to scattering on gas particles and possibly plasma waves
(
Jean et al. 2009
)
, giving
rise to extended positron annihilation emission with morphology depending on the magnetic field
configuration and the density and dynamics of the ISM
(
Alexis et al. 2014
)
.
While some positrons may annihilate in the CMZ, the strong turbulence in the inner Galaxy
combined with gas outflows
(
Sarkar 2024
)
may transport positrons away from the
source, leading to the observed extended emission feature
(
Panther et al. 2018
)
.

The obvious question in this scenario is why no positron annihilation emission is observed
around other microquasars.

1E~1740.7–2942
is unique in the microquasar population, remaining persistently in the bright
outburst state and accreting near peak luminosity most of the time, which are properties that are only
shared with
GRS~1758–258

(
Tetarenko et al. 2016
)
.
However
GRS~1758–258
has a lower jet power
(
Tetarenko et al. 2020
)
and is located further away from the
Galactic centre, placing it in a less dense region of the ISM.
Lower ISM densities imply larger positron propagation distances and higher escape fractions,
reducing positron annihilation emission intensities.
The combination of a powerful positron source that is located in a dense ISM region could explain why

1E~1740.7–2942
is the only currently detectable microquasar source of positrons.

4.2
Possible origin of the Core component

Figure 5
:

The SED of the Core component is shown together with the SED of the Fermi/LAT source

4FGL~J1745.6–2859

(
Abdollahi et al. 2022
)
, upper limits from INTEGRAL/PICsIT
(

)
(black),
and a best-fitting model for a pair plasma injected by
Sgr A

(blue solid line).
Contributions to the model from Inverse Compton (dotted line), positron in-flight annihilation (dashed),
and Bremsstrahlung (dash-dotted) emissions are also shown.

The SED of the Core component differs significantly from that of the Bulge component
(cf. Fig.
3
), suggesting that they have different origins.
It is nevertheless intriguing that the 511 keV line and MeV continuum emissions both have a
compact component at the Galactic centre
(
Skinner et al. 2015
;
Siegert et al. 2016
)
, which raises the question
whether also the Core component relates to positron annihilation.
A central component could in principle be produced by the enhanced gas density in the CMZ
that would lead to enhanced positron annihilation emission, yet in that case, the SEDs of the Core and Bulge
components would be similar.

The missing link is probably the compact GeV source
4FGL~J1745.6–2859
that is observed
by Fermi/LAT at the Galactic centre
(
Malyshev et al. 2015
;
Abdollahi et al. 2022
;
Cafardo et al. 2021
)
, and which is by far
the brightest Fermi/LAT source in the field of the Core component.
As illustrated in Fig.
5
, the SEDs of the Core component and
4FGL~J1745.6–2859
connect
rather well, making the Fermi/LAT source the most plausible high-energy counterpart of the Core component.
The
8.8-30
MeV flux of the Core component is slightly in excess of a smooth spectral connection
but the modelling of the Galactic ridge emission introduces some systematic uncertainties in the flux levels,
which also holds for the Fermi/LAT low-energy fluxes
(
Malyshev et al. 2015
;
Abdollahi et al. 2022
;
Cafardo et al. 2021
)
.
Within these uncertainties, the connection between the two SEDs is satisfactorily.

4FGL~J1745.6–2859
is a non-variable
\gamma
-ray
source of unknown nature, but production of relativistic
hadrons or leptons by
Sgr A
∗
is generally invoked to explain the GeV
emission
(
Cafardo et al. 2021
)
.
In Fig.
5
we show the
\gamma
-ray
spectrum that arises from the continuous injection of a
relativistic pair plasma into the ISM at the Galactic centre with a particle spectrum slope
\Gamma

of 2.1 and maximum energy of 1 TeV.
The model satisfactorily explains the observed SEDs of the Core component and
4FGL~J1745.6–2859

and produces at the same time a 511 keV line emission of
10^{-4}

photons
cm
-2

s
-1
, which corresponds
to the observed flux for the central 511 keV line emission component
(
Skinner et al. 2015
;
Siegert et al. 2016
)
.
In such a model, the
\gamma
-ray
emission would be mainly Inverse Compton emission owing to the high
radiation field at the Galactic centre
(
Kusunose & Takahara 2012
)
, with contributions from positron in-flight
annihilation and Bremsstrahlung at low energies.
The high radiation field and large ISM density at the Galactic centre imply that positrons will thermalise
quickly and propagate less, explaining the compact appearance of the source.
Once thermalised, positron annihilation will lead to 511 keV line emission which produces the central
component observed in the INTEGRAL/SPI data
(
Skinner et al. 2015
;
Siegert et al. 2016
)
.

5
Conclusions

By analysing archival data from the COMPTEL telescope we have established the presence of
MeV excess emission on top of Galactic ridge
\gamma
-ray
emission towards the Galactic centre region.
The morphology of this excess emission changes quite drastically within the COMPTEL
0.75-30
MeV
energy range, switching from an extended emission morphology below
\sim 3
MeV to a point-like
appearance above that energy.
A spectral analysis reveals the presence of two distinct excess emission components towards the
Galactic centre.

We plausibly identify the first component to originate from positron in-flight annihilation and reveal
that its spectrum matches a scenario in which the Galactic bulge positrons are produced
by dark matter annihilation
(
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
;
Sizun et al. 2006
;
Boehm & Uwer 2006
)
.
If this scenario applies, we predict the rest mass of the dark matter particles to be
\sim 3
MeV.
The only other candidate source after dark matter annihilations that may satisfy the observations is
positron injection through the jets of the microquasar
1E~1740.7–2942
, yet it is still unclear whether
that scenario can satisfactorily explain the extended morphology of the emission.
So we still do not know where exactly the positrons in the Galactic bulge come from, but our
observations exclude many sources that have so far been proposed
(
Prantzos et al. 2011
)
.
In particular,
\beta^{+}
decays from radioactive isotopes and sources producing highly relativistic
positrons, such as cosmic-ray secondaries produced from
\pi^{+}
decays, normal pulsars, millisecond
pulsars, and magnetars can be clearly discarded as primary source of Galactic bulge positrons.

We plausibly identify the second component as the low-energy counterpart of the Fermi/LAT
point source
4FGL~J1745.6–2859
which coincides with the location
Sgr A$^∗$
.
While production of relativistic hadrons or leptons by
Sgr A$^∗$
has been invoked as origin of
the emission
(
Cafardo et al. 2021
)
, the detection of a low-energy counterpart by COMPTEL
favours a leptonic scenario.
The broad-band spectrum of the source is well explained by the injection of a relativistic pair plasma
into the ISM, and the high matter density at the Galactic centre would limit the propagation of the
plasma and explain the point-like appearance of the source.
Interestingly, the pair plasma scenario would also explain the point-like component in the
511 keV line emission observed by INTEGRAL/SPI towards the Galactic bulge, which would be
attributed to the annihilation of thermalised positrons in the plasma.

Our work has demonstrated the large discovery potential that resides in the exploitation of archival
data, allowing for science advances with modest detrimental environmental impacts.
We estimate that the computing for this analysis produced about 600 kgCO
2
e of greenhouse
gases, which is about 40 times less than the median per-publication emissions associated with the
analysis of data from an active astronomical observatory
(
Knödlseder et al. 2022a
)
.
Shifting research activities to the exploitation of archival data provides hence a powerful measure
to reduce the carbon footprint of astrophysical research while maintaining the ability to make exciting
discoveries.

Appendix A
Modelling of instrumental background events

COMPTEL data are dominated by instrumental background events that arise from cosmic-ray particles,
geomagnetically trapped radiation-belt particles, albedo neutrons, and
\gamma
-ray
photons that
interact with the spacecraft and detector materials
(
Weidenspointner et al. 2001
)
.
Event rates due to celestial
\gamma
-ray
sources are much lower than instrumental background
event rates, with typical signal-to-noise ratios of a few percent.
Consequently, the accurate modelling of the distribution of instrumental background events in the
three-dimensional COMPTEL data space is crucial for a reliable determination of
\gamma
-ray
source characteristics from the data.

For our analysis we modelled the instrumental background event distribution using an algorithm that
we dubbed BGDLIXF, which is an evolution of the BGDLIXE algorithm that was introduced in
section 3.5.3 of
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
.
While BGDLIXE has been shown to provide an accurate model of the instrumental background event
distribution for individual viewing periods
(
Knödlseder et al. 2022b
)
, the algorithm does not cope with
background event rate variations between viewing periods, which is problematic when viewing periods
are combined for analysis.
Viewing periods have typical durations of two weeks during which the CGRO satellite had a stable
pointing, and for our analysis we combined 215 viewing periods that span a duration of almost 6
years of observations.
During this period the instrumental background event rates varied substantially
(
Weidenspointner 1999
)
,
and ignoring these variations introduces significant features in the TS maps that are attributable to
inadequacies of the instrumental background model.

To introduce the BGDLIXF algorithm, we start by rewriting the BGDLIXE algorithm as

Appendix C
Spectral Modelling

To compare the data to expected spectra for positron in-flight annihilation, we modelled the
\gamma
-ray
emission from a population of energetic positrons interacting with electrons
in the interstellar medium following the prescription in
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
,
Sizun et al. 2006
and
Prantzos et al. 2011
.
Specifically, energy losses through Inverse Compton scattering, synchrotron emission, Bremsstrahlung
emission
(
Ginzburg 1979
, using the prescription of)
,
Coulomb scattering and ionisation were considered.
While these prescriptions are limited to the case of stationary injection of mono-energetic positrons
into the ISM, we extend the formalism to arbitrary injection spectra.
The
\gamma
-ray
emission mechanisms that are included in our modelling are positron in-flight annihilation,
Inverse Compton scattering, and (external) Bremsstrahlung.
For the case of dark matter annihilation, we also included internal Bremsstrahlung emission following
the prescription of
Boehm & Uwer 2006
.
Explicit parameters of our model are the kinetic energy spectrum of the injected positrons, the
hydrogen number density
n_{\rm H}
of the ISM in which the positrons slow down, and its ionisation
fraction
Y_{\rm e}
, with
Y_{\rm e}
= 0 corresponding to the neutral and
Y_{\rm e}
= 1 to the
fully ionised ISM.
Following
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
, we normalised the resulting
\gamma
-ray
intensities in units of
equivalent 511 keV line flux
\Phi_{511}
using the observed positronium fraction of

f_{\rm Ps}
= 0.967
(
Jean et al. 2006
)
, so that when the models are fitted to the COMPTEL data the
model normalisation is given in units of
\Phi_{511}
.
From
\Phi_{511}
, the positron injection rate
R
can be derived using

R=\frac{4\pi d^{2}}{2(1-3/4f_{\rm Ps})}\frac{\Phi_{511}}{P(E_{\rm inj},E_{\rm therm})}

(6)

where
d
is the distance to the positron source and
P(E_{\rm inj},E_{\rm therm})
is the
integrated survival probability of positrons as they lose energy from the injection energy

E_{\rm inj}
to the thermalisation energy
E_{\rm therm}

(
Beacom & Yüksel 2006
)
.
Note that
P(E_{\rm inj},E_{\rm therm})
depends also on
Y_{\rm e}
and
n_{\rm H}
,
and for
E_{\rm inj}=1-10
MeV is in the range within
0.95-0.99

For a source at the distance of the Galactic centre

GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2019

this results in

R\approx 1.5\times 10^{46}\,\Phi_{511}

-1

\Phi_{511}

photons

-2

-1

We furthermore assumed a positron fraction
N_{+}/(N_{+}+N_{-})
which is used in the computation
of the Inverse Compton and (external) Bremsstrahlung emissions to account for emission from
both electrons and positrons.
For a pure pair plasma, the positron fraction is
N_{+}/(N_{+}+N_{-})
= 0.5.

We then pre-computed the expected
\gamma
-ray
intensities for different model parameters and stored
the spectral vectors in so-called “table models”.
The spectral vectors of these models are interpolated when they are used in the model fitting,
allowing to determine, for example, positron injection energies, cut-offs and spectral indices
in a maximum likelihood fit of COMPTEL data.
These models were then used to model the spectrum of either the Bulge or the Core component
and adjusted jointly over the full
0.75-30

the other component.
Specifically, a power law spectrum was used for the Core component when table models were
fitted to the Bulge component, while a log-parabola spectrum was used for the Bulge component when
table models were fitted to the Core component.
In this process, model parameters were fitted and corresponding confidence intervals
assessed, determining in particular positron injection energies and equivalent 511 keV
line fluxes.

This is no longer true for the model spectrum shown for the Core component in Fig.
