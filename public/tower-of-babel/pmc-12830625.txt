Altered PTPN13–β-catenin interaction by pathogenic mutations and involvement of this axis in B-cell receptor signalling
Authors: David A Cabrera-Riofrío, Hongbing Li, Clara García-Calvo, Carmen Sánchez-Bernal, Jesús Sánchez-Yagüe, Yigal Dror, Rubén M Buey, Ángel Hernández-Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 13 (PTPN13) is a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase with context-dependent roles as tumour suppressor or promoter. Its modular structure supports multiple molecular interactions, including a critical one with β-catenin, a regulator of the haematopoietic system. We previously identified three pathogenic PTPN13 mutations in families with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), anaemia, and/or inherited bone marrow failure (IBMF). Our current findings reveal that these mutations impair the PTPN13–β-catenin interaction. β-catenin and PTPN13 are stabilised upon B-cell receptor (BCR) activation, while PTPN13 silencing reduces Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) activation and β-catenin levels, indicating that PTPN13 modulates BCR signalling at multiple points. Together with prior evidence showing that PTPN13 mutations compromise protein stability and decrease β-catenin levels, these data support a role for disrupted lymphoid signalling. Altered expression of key surface markers (CD25 and CD38) upon silencing of either PTPN13 or β-catenin further supports this interpretation. In conclusion, our study identifies the PTPN13–β-catenin axis as a critical regulator of lymphoid cell homeostasis and highlights its disruption as a potential driver of haematological abnormalities in patients carrying PTPN13 mutations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33151-y.Keywords: Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), B-cell receptor (BCR), Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK), β-catenin, Inherited bone marrow failure (IBMF), PTPN13Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Protein tyrosine phosphorylation is a crucial post-translational modification (PTM) involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and development1. The cellular levels of this PTM are determined by the balance between protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) and protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs). Disruption of this equilibrium has been associated with various pathophysiological conditions, including cancer, metabolic disorders, and immune diseases2.

Classical PTPs exist as either receptor or non-receptor-type proteins3. Protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor 13 (PTPN13, also known as PTPL1, PTP-Bas, FAP-1, and hPTP1E) is a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase. It shows a modular structure with the following domains from the N-terminus: a kinase non-catalytic C-lobe (KIND) domain at the N-terminus; a Four-point-one, ezrin, radixin, moesin (FERM) domain, which is structurally similar to the band 4.1 superfamily of cytoskeleton-associated proteins; five PDZ domains that mediate interactions with the plasma membrane and cytoskeletal elements and the catalytic PTP domain at the C-terminus4. The role of this phosphatase is dual, functioning either as a tumour suppressor or a promoter depending on the context5.

Due to its modular structure, PTPN13 sustains interactions with a wide range of protein partners5. One of PTPN13’s interacting partners is β-catenin6, the canonical effector of the Wnt signalling pathway, which is essential for numerous differentiation processes, including haematopoiesis7. The primary structure of β-catenin consists of relatively flexible N- and C-terminal domains (NTD and CTD) of 130 and 110 residues, respectively, and a central region containing 12 imperfect armadillo (ARM) repeats, which serve as a platform for interactions with numerous proteins that regulate its stability and localisation8. The NTD and CTD appear to be required for the signalling functions of β-catenin but are dispensable for its role in cell adhesion. The CTD is particularly important for β-catenin’s transcriptional activity9.

We have previously analysed the role of PTPN13 in the haematopoietic system, demonstrating its importance in differentiation10 and the regulation of haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) quiescence11. Our findings support the notion that the significance of PTPN13 in haematopoiesis is closely linked to its interaction with β-catenin11. Although our results suggest an interaction between these two proteins either directly or indirectly, the precise nature of this interaction has yet to be thoroughly investigated.

Germline mutations in several PTP genes have been linked to the development of various medical disorders12, such as Noonan syndrome, which is caused by PTPN11 mutations13, congenital lymphoedema, which has been associated with PTPN14 mutations14, and sensorineural deafness resulting from PTPRQ mutations15. In line with this, we recently identified PTPN13 mutations in patients from two unrelated families affected by acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), anaemia, and/or inherited bone marrow failure (IBMF)16. In one family, three siblings carried a homozygous missense mutation (p.R1838Q), whereas in the other family, the patient exhibited two heterozygous mutations: a missense mutation (p.W1274C) and an in-frame deletion of a serine residue (p.S1004del). In vitro analyses revealed that these mutations compromised PTPN13 stability and impaired megakaryocytic and erythroid differentiation16.

Building on our previous work, which established a functional link between PTPN13 and β-catenin in the haematopoietic system11, we further investigated the nature of this interaction and the impact of PTPN13 mutations. Our findings indicate that the interaction is primarily mediated by the PDZ domain region of PTPN13 and the C-terminal domain of β-catenin. Notably, disease-associated PTPN13 mutations can either enhance or disrupt this interaction. Furthermore, we demonstrate that both proteins are involved in B-cell receptor (BCR) signalling. Disruption of this pathway—particularly at the level of BTK activation and β-catenin stability—may underlie the lymphoid abnormalities observed in patients carrying PTPN13 mutations.

Results

PTPN13 mutations affect the interaction with β-catenin

We have recently described mutations in PTPN13 in infant patients with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia or bone marrow failure from two unrelated families. In one family, three siblings presented a homozygous missense mutation (p.R1838Q), while in the other family, the patient exhibited two heterozygous mutations: a missense mutation (p.W1274C) and an in-frame deletion of a serine residue (p.S1004del), each on different alleles16.

We have previously described that PTPN13 regulates β-catenin stability and transcriptional activity10, which appears essential for regulating haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) function in vivo11. In vitro analyses in model cell lines show that the mutations mentioned above destabilise PTPN13, consistent with the reduced protein expression observed in patients16. Additionally, we observed a reduced expression of β-catenin levels upon expression of the mutant versions of PTPN13 in K562 cells16. Therefore, we wondered whether β-catenin stability is also compromised in the patients. Our analyses reveal reduced β-catenin expression in the patients (Fig. 1A-B), which, in agreement with our previous data, could be due to the downregulation of PTPN1316.

Therefore, we surmise that the haematological abnormalities observed in the patients likely involve an alteration in the PTPN13-β-catenin axis. We have previously shown that PTPN13 and β-catenin co-localise and co-immunoprecipitate11, strongly suggesting that both proteins are part of the same functional multiprotein complex. To further explore this interaction, we analysed the PTPN13–β-catenin association in vitro. Different MBP–β-catenin fusion proteins were expressed in bacteria and subsequently purified (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1). Equal amounts of the three fusion protein variants were immobilised on agarose resin, as described in the Methods section. Wild-type PTPN13 was overexpressed in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 2), and pull-down assays were performed to assess the binding of the MBP–β-catenin constructs to PTPN13 (Fig. 1E). The results showed that all three β-catenin variants were able to retain PTPN13 from cell extracts, confirming their association within the same protein complex (Fig. 1E). The interaction strength was quantified relative to PTPN13 expression in the input lysate (Fig. 1E, first line). Compared with the full-length β-catenin construct, the C-terminal domain (β-Cter) displayed a stronger interaction. In contrast, a construct lacking this region (β-cat-Δ-C-ter) showed a reduced interaction (Fig. 1E–F), indicating the importance of the C-terminal region of β-catenin for binding to PTPN13.

We next analysed the interaction between full-length β-catenin (MBP–β-catenin) and various PTPN13 constructs (Fig. 1D). Interaction levels were quantified relative to the corresponding input extracts (Fig. 1G, lower panel and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Using full-length PTPN13 (WT-PTPN13) as a reference, we observed that the PTPN13 construct containing the five PDZ domains (PDZs) showed a significantly stronger interaction with β-catenin, while the C-terminal fragment of PTPN13 exhibited a notably weaker association (Fig. 1H).

In summary, although other regions in both proteins may also contribute to the interaction, our data indicate that the C-terminal region of β-catenin and the central portion of PTPN13, containing the PDZ domains, are important. This is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the capacity of the β-catenin C-terminal region to bind PDZ domains17.

We next investigated whether the PTPN13 germline mutations previously reported by our group16 influence the interaction with β-catenin. Wild-type and mutant forms of PTPN13 were expressed in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B). We noticed that the p.W1274C mutant displayed an anomalous migration pattern compared with the wild-type protein (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The introduction of an ectopic cysteine residue may promote the formation of aberrant secondary structures or disulfide bonds, resulting in the observed shift in migration, as has been reported for other proteins18.

The interaction of the different PTPN13 variants with full-length β-catenin was assessed using pull-down assays (Fig. 2A). Taking the interaction with wild-type PTPN13 as a reference, we observed that the mutation identified in family A (p.R1838Q) enhanced the interaction with full-length β-catenin, whereas the p.S1004del mutation identified in family B markedly reduced interaction strength. In contrast, the p.W1274C mutation, also present in family B, showed no significant effect on the interaction (Fig. 2A).

Our results support the notion that the C-terminal region of β-catenin (β-Cter) plays a particularly important role in binding to PTPN13 (Fig. 1E-F). We therefore used this construct as bait to further evaluate the interaction with both wild-type and mutant PTPN13 (Fig. 2B). Compared with wild-type PTPN13, the p.R1838Q mutation enhanced the interaction, whereas both p.S1004del and p.W1274C reduced it.

These three mutations are located within the region of PTPN13 that contains the five PDZ domains, although only one of them (p.R1838Q) lies directly within a PDZ domain. Given that this region appears to be particularly important for the interaction with β-catenin, it is not surprising that the mutations affect binding. Notably, the p.R1838Q mutation—located within PDZ4—enhances the strength of the interaction, which is particularly intriguing and may reflect a gain-of-function effect in this context.

We have previously shown that PTPN13 reduces β-catenin transcriptional activity10. To determine whether PTPN13 mutations alter this regulatory function, we assessed β-catenin transcriptional activity in the presence of wild-type and mutant PTPN13. As previously reported, wild-type PTPN13 significantly suppressed β-catenin-driven transcription. A similar inhibitory effect was observed for the p.S1004del and p.R1838Q mutants, despite the stronger interaction of the latter (Fig. 2A), suggesting that a relatively small amount of PTPN13 or interaction is sufficient to inhibit β-catenin transcriptional activity. In contrast, the p.W1274C mutant failed to inhibit β-catenin transcriptional activity (Fig. 2C). This result is intriguing compared with the other two mutants. As noted above, the p.W1274C mutant displayed an anomalous migration pattern, which may reflect the formation of aberrant secondary structures or disulfide bonds, potentially preventing its ability to interfere with β-catenin transcriptional activity.

The C-terminal domain (CTD) of β-catenin is known to be critical for its transcriptional function9. Our results indicate that this region is also important for the interaction with PTPN13, supporting the hypothesis that PTPN13 may repress β-catenin transcriptional activity through binding to its CTD.

PTPN13, β-catenin, and the BCR signalling pathway

Given the development of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) in patients with PTPN13 mutations, we wondered whether PTPN13 and β-catenin are involved in lymphoid cell signalling. The stabilisation of β-catenin upon activation of the B-cell receptor (BCR) has been previously reported19. Upon stimulation of RCH-ACV cells with IgM F(ab’)₂ FC5µ, we observed a decrease in β-catenin phosphorylation at serine 33—a modification known to target the protein for degradation—accompanied by stabilisation of β-catenin (Fig. 3A–C). Additionally, we also observed a stabilisation of PTPN13 upon stimulation (Fig. 3A and D). These results suggest that the stabilisation of both β-catenin and PTPN13 may contribute to the regulation of the BCR signalling pathway. This raises the possibility that the haematological abnormalities observed in patients carrying PTPN13 mutations within the lymphoid compartment could result from disrupted intracellular signalling dynamics.

Considering that these mutations impair PTPN13 stability16, we hypothesised that silencing PTPN13 might recapitulate the molecular alterations observed in patients. The RCH-ACV cell line, which is arrested at the pre-B stage and derived from a paediatric B-ALL patient20, exhibits high levels of PTPN13 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4). We therefore assessed the impact of PTPN13 knockdown in this context (Fig. 4A–B). Silencing PTPN13 using two different RNAi sequences (RNAi 101 and 103) led to a significant reduction in BTK activation (Fig. 4C–D) and in cellular β-catenin levels (Fig. 4H–I). Moreover, RNAi 101, which achieved a more robust knockdown of PTPN13, also resulted in a marked decrease in PLCγ2 activation (Fig. 4C and F–G). No significant changes were observed in ERK activation (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C). These findings suggest that PTPN13 acts as a positive regulator of BCR signalling. Accordingly, the reduced protein stability caused by the PTPN13 mutations identified in patients16 is likely to impair BCR signalling in the lymphoid compartment.

To further investigate this issue, we also analysed the effects of PTPN13 silencing in MEC-1 cells (Fig. 5A–B), a mature B-cell line that also exhibits high PTPN13 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with the findings in RCH-ACV cells, PTPN13 knockdown significantly reduced BTK activation (Fig. 5C–D) and β-catenin levels (Fig. 5H–I). However, no significant changes were observed in PLCγ2 activation (Fig. 5F–G) or in ERK activation (Supplementary Fig. 5D–F). MEC-1 cells display features of anergy, including elevated MAPK pathway activation21, which may account for the lack of effect on certain signalling pathways upon PTPN13 silencing.

In summary, PTPN13 silencing in two distinct B-cell lines perturbs BCR signalling, underscoring the relevance of this phosphatase in modulating the pathway. These findings suggest that PTPN13 acts as a positive regulator of BCR signalling by facilitating BTK and PLCγ2 activation and stabilising β-catenin. Taken together with the observation that patient cells exhibit reduced PTPN13 expression16, it is reasonable to propose that BCR signalling is compromised in affected individuals.

Silencing of PTPN13 alters CD25 and CD38 expression

Next, we investigated whether silencing PTPN13 could impact lymphoid cell function by examining the expression of three key cell surface markers: CD25, CD38, and CD11c.

CD25 is a glycoprotein that functions as the α-chain of the interleukin-2 (IL-2) receptor and is predominantly expressed on CD4⁺ regulatory T cells22. It is also found on CD20⁺ B cells, where elevated expression has been linked to a reduced capacity for immunoglobulin secretion, while correlating with enhanced antigen-presenting capabilities23. In patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (B-ALL), CD25 expression has been associated with the presence of the Philadelphia chromosome (Ph⁺), making it a useful biomarker for identifying Ph⁺ cases. Furthermore, in Ph⁻ B-ALL patients, CD25 expression is correlated with minimal residual disease detection, thus serving as a prognostic marker of poor outcome24. In addition, CD25 has been proposed to contribute to BCR signalling and to be required for the initiation of B cell leukaemia in vivo25.

In RCH-ACV cells, PTPN13 silencing induced a significant increase in CD25 expression (Fig. 6A–B). Notably, CD25 levels remained elevated in PTPN13-silenced cells even after stimulation with PMA, a stimulus known to enhance CD25 expression (Fig. 6C–D; Supplementary Fig. 6).

Consistent with the findings in RCH-ACV cells, a significant increase in CD25⁺ cells was also observed following PTPN13 silencing in MEC-1 cells (Fig. 6E–F).

Altogether, these findings support the idea that loss of PTPN13 function, as occurs in patients carrying PTPN13 mutations, is associated with an increased expression of CD25. As previously suggested25, this alteration may contribute to B-ALL transformation.

CD38 is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein whose expression is closely associated with the activation status of immune cells. In the B cell lineage, CD38 is expressed during the early stages of differentiation and plays a critical role in B cell activation, proliferation, and maturation26. Its expression increases upon B cell activation and is involved in the regulation of B cell fate27. Functioning as a signalling receptor, CD38 mediates splenic B lymphocyte activation, with Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) being essential for downstream signalling events28.

Notably, it has been recently reported that CD38 expression is reduced in B cells from B-ALL patients compared with healthy donors29. In RCH-ACV cells, PTPN13 silencing resulted in a significant decrease in CD38 expression (Fig. 7A-D), further supporting the notion that loss of PTPN13 function may be involved in B-ALL development. In contrast, no significant changes in CD38 expression were observed in MEC-1 cells following PTPN13 silencing (Fig. 7E–H), which may be attributed to the fact that MEC-1 cells represent a mature B-cell stage.

CD11c has been identified in a subset of memory B cells that co-express the inhibitory receptor Fc receptor-like protein 4 (FcRH4, also known as FcRL4), referred to as CD11c⁺ T-bet⁺ B cells30, and has been implicated in immune maintenance31. We also examined the expression of this marker following PTPN13 silencing. No changes were observed in MEC-1 cells, while a slight decrease was detected in RCH-ACV cells, both under basal conditions and after stimulation with PMA (Supplementary Fig. 7).

In summary, the changes observed in CD25 and CD38 expression upon PTPN13 silencing are significant and support a potential link between PTPN13 loss of function and the pathogenesis of B-ALL.

Discussion

PTPN13 is a high molecular weight intracellular protein tyrosine phosphatase that can exhibit a dual role in cancer, depending on the context, acting either as an oncogene by inhibiting the activation of FAS signalling5 or as a tumour suppressor through the regulation of tumour-promoting signalling pathways, such as the Her2/ErbB2 receptor tyrosine kinase cascade32. Its modular structure allows PTPN13 to sustain multiple molecular interactions5, among which stands out the interaction with β-catenin. The interaction between both proteins is especially relevant in the haematopoietic context as have previously reported10,11.

We had recently reported three PTPN13 mutations present in two unrelated families, which are associated with the development of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), and/or inherited bone marrow failure (IBMF)16. Among the three mutations, in silico analyses classified the p.R1838Q mutation as highly pathogenic due to its homozygosity and the loss of the positive charge of the arginine residue, and in agreement with that, the development of T-ALL was observed in one patient that carried this mutation16. Additionally, although the other two mutations are present in heterozygous form, they could still be potentially damaging to protein function. The p.W1274C mutation represents a radical change, as tryptophan is one of the most highly conserved residues in globular proteins, while cysteine is the only amino acid capable of forming disulphide bonds. Finally, serine is a residue susceptible to various post-translational modifications (PTMs), such as phosphorylation, and its deletion (p.S1004del) could therefore disrupt protein function. A patient displaying these heterozygous mutations developed pre B-ALL16. The three PTPN13 mutations impair protein stability and therefore are consistent with a protein loss of function, which would support the notion of PTPN13 acting as a tumour suppressor in the lymphoid compartment16.

In light of the reported relevance of the PTPN13–β-catenin interaction in the haematopoietic context, as shown by our group10,11 and others6 we set out to examine this interaction in greater detail in the present study. The central region of β-catenin, which contains 12 ARM repeats, serves as a docking site for numerous proteins that regulate its stability and localisation33. The C-terminal domain (CTD) of β-catenin includes a PDZ-binding motif (DTDL)34, and its interaction with PDZ-containing proteins has been previously described35. According to our results, the CTD of β-catenin contributes significantly to the interaction with PTPN13, but it is not essential, as a construct lacking this region still retains a substantial level of binding. Nevertheless, it would be of interest in future studies to investigate, through site-directed mutagenesis36, the relevance of the β-catenin DTDL motif for its interaction with PTPN13, and to determine whether this motif is required for the PTPN13-mediated inhibition of β-catenin transcriptional activity.

Our results show a reduced interaction between β-catenin and the C-terminal PTPN13 construct containing the catalytic domain, which is consistent with its expected functionality. In contrast, the construct comprising the central region with the five PDZ domains exhibited a stronger interaction than the full-length protein. One possible explanation is that, in wild-type PTPN13, the PDZ region may be less accessible for binding, thereby accounting for the lower interaction strength observed with the full-length protein compared with the isolated PDZ-containing construct.

We are aware of the limitation of analysing protein-protein interactions by in vitro pull-down assays. However, our results suggest that the unstructured C-terminal region of β-catenin and the central five PDZ domains region of PTPN13 are important for this interaction.

The PTPN13 mutations we recently identified16 lie within the PDZ-PTPN13 region, therefore, we wondered whether the PTPN13 mutations we recently identified16 may somehow alter the interaction. Our results confirmed this notion, suggesting that a defective PTPN13-β-catenin interaction in the patients may be related with their pathology.

The C-terminal domain (CTD) of β-catenin sustains interactions with transcriptional coactivators such as BCL9 and Pygopus, which are required for β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activation37,38. It has previously been reported that β-catenin’s transcriptional activity can be blocked by the interaction of the CTD with the PDZ domain of TIP-1 (Tax-interacting protein-1) 39. Interestingly, we had previously shown that PTPN13 reduces β-catenin transcriptional activity10. Considering the above, this effect is likely due to the physical interaction between PTPN13 and the β-catenin CTD.

The alterations in the lymphoid compartment observed in the patients could be attributed to disruptions in cellular signalling. Supporting this hypothesis, we show that PTPN13 plays a critical role in BCR signalling by regulating the activation of BTK and also probably PLCγ2. In addition, we observed that β-catenin and PTPN13 are stabilised upon BCR activation, which means that this is part of this signalling pathway, so the ability of PTPN13 to stabilise β-catenin is an additional means to intervene in the BCR signalling. It has long been recognised that other protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs), such as SHP1 40 and SHP2, can either inhibit or enhance BCR signalling, respectively41. To our knowledge, this is the first report implicating PTPN13 in the regulation of B-cell receptor signalling.

We have also analysed whether PTPN13 silencing alters the expression of cell surface markers related with B-ALL development, including CD25 and CD38. CD25 expression has been involved with the development of B cell leukaemia in vivo25, and its expression correlates with B-ALL poor prognosis24. We show that PTPN13 downregulation significantly enhances the expression of this marker in MEC-1 and RCH-ACV lymphoid cell lines. This allow us to propose that PTPN13 loss of function in the patients could increase CD25 expression contributing to leukemic transformation.

CD38 ligation has long been linked with BTK activation42. In line with this, our results show that PTPN13 silencing induces downregulation of CD38 and impairs BTK activation. CD38 plays a crucial role in lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and survival43; therefore, it is likely that the PTPN13 mutations analysed here compromise lymphocyte functionality by reducing CD38 surface expression and hindering BTK activation. In addition, CD38 expression is reduced in B-ALL patients in comparison with healthy donors29, suggesting that the alteration of this marker upon PTPN13 loss of function might be also related with leukemic transformation.

We also observe that patients show a reduced level of β-catenin, consistent with a PTPN13 loss of protein stability. Given that the alteration of the Wnt signalling pathway can give rise to ALL44, the downregulation of β-catenin detected in patient cells must be a key factor sustaining the pathology induced by PTPN13 mutations. Supporting this notion, silencing of β-catenin also resulted in reduced CD38 expression, a fact strongly correlated with B-ALL, as pointed above.

In summary, we have analysed the interaction between β-catenin and PTPN13, demonstrating that the three PTPN13 mutations previously identified as pathogenic in haematological patients16 alter this interaction. Moreover, our findings suggest several mechanisms through which loss of PTPN13 protein in patients could contribute to leukemic transformation, including β-catenin downregulation, disruption of BCR signalling, and altered expression of key surface markers—specifically, increased CD25 and decreased CD38. Together, these results support the pathogenicity of the PTPN13 mutations identified in these patients.

Methods

Cell culture

All cell lines were sourced from the DSMZ collection (Leibniz Institute, Germany) and cultured in RPMI medium with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely checked using the PlasmoTest™ assay (InvivoGen, France; cat. #rep-pt1) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Human samples were obtained with informed consent and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. T cells were isolated from whole blood of healthy donors or patients carrying a PTPN13 mutation using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytona, cat# 17144002). Cells were then cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 30 U/mL recombinant IL-2, and Dynabeads® Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 11161D), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cultures were maintained in 24-well plates to allow expansion to confluence. Cells were subsequently passaged to sustain proliferation and harvested at appropriate time points for downstream analyses.

Immunoblotting

Whole-cell extracts were prepared using MLB buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5), and the expression of proteins of interest was analysed by immunoblotting as previously described10. Commercial primary antibodies were obtained from the following sources: Becton Dickinson – anti-β-catenin (#610154), anti-BTK (#611116), anti-phospho-ERK (pT202/pY204) (#612358); Cell Signaling Technology – anti-phospho-BTK (pY223) (#5082S), anti-phospho-PLCγ2 (pY1217) (#3871S); MERCK – anti-HA (#11867423001); Santa Cruz Biotechnology – anti-ERK (sc-94), anti-PLCγ2 (sc-5283), anti-PTPN13 (sc-67164). Secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes were all obtained from Bio-Rad.

Pull-down assay for detecting protein-protein interactions

Three 6×His-MBP-tagged β-catenin variants—full-length, ΔC-terminal, and the isolated C-terminal domain—were PCR-amplified, cloned into the pSOL-MBP plasmid (Expresso® System, Lucigen), and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (NZYTech). Cells were lysed by sonication on ice (15 min, 9 W, 30 s on/off cycles), and soluble proteins were purified using a 5 mL MBP-Trap column (Cytiva) on an ÄKTA Pure system. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

pHM6-PTPN13 constructs were transfected into HEK-293T cells, and whole-cell extracts were prepared using MLB buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5). These lysates were used as input for the in vitro pull-down interaction assay. 50 µg of 6×His-MBP–tagged β-catenin variants (Supplementary Fig. 1) or MBP alone were immobilised on dextrin agarose resin (6HCL-DEX-10, MBP-TAG Purification), pre-equilibrated in elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, pH 7.5) for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing with buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5), 250 µg of HEK-293T lysates expressing PTPN13 variants (Supplementary Fig. 2) were added to the immobilised 6×His-MBP–tagged β-catenin variants or to immobilised MBP alone (negative control) and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle agitation. The beads were then washed five times with 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5. Following the final wash, bound proteins were analysed by SDS-PAGE. PTPN13 expression was assessed both in the input samples and in the pull-down fractions using an anti-HA primary antibody. Fluorescent secondary antibodies (Bio-Rad) were used for detection. Fluorescent signals were acquired with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad), and signal quantification was performed using Image Lab software. Interaction levels were normalised to the amount of PTPN13 present in the input extracts (Supplementary Fig. 3).

RNA interference

PTPN13 and β-catenin were silenced by lentiviral delivery of two distinct shRNA sequences, as previously described10.

Measurement of transcriptional activity

Transcriptional activity was assessed using the TOP-FLASH luciferase reporter and pCMV-βGal as a transfection control, as previously described10. HEK-293T cells were transfected with TOP-FLASH, pCMV-βGal, and, where indicated, pWPI-β-catenin S33Y and pHM6-PTPN13 plasmids. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured (Promega), and β-galactosidase activity was determined by ONPG (Ortho-Nitrophenyl-β-D-Galactopyranoside) hydrolysis. Luciferase values were normalised to β-galactosidase activity.

Cell surface expression marker analyses

MEC-1 and RCH-ACV cells were seeded at 3.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in 24-well plates under various conditions, with or without PMA (Fisher Scientific), or IgM F(ab’)₂ (Jackson IR). After 48 h, cells were stained with anti-CD25 and CD11c (Immunostep), and CD38 (Miltenyi Biotec) antibodies. Surface marker expression was analysed by flow cytometry, comparing MFI and percentage of positive cells as previously10,11. Data were analysed using Flowing Software 2.5.1 (P. Terho, University of Turku).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Excel and GraphPad Prism 8, with two-tailed Student’s t-tests, ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, depending on the data type. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.
