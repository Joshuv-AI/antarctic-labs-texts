Deep Generative Continual Learning using Functional LoRA: FunLoRA
Victor Enescu, Hichem Sahbi

Abstract
Continual adaptation of deep generative models holds tremendous potential and critical importance, given their rapid and expanding usage in text and vision based applications. Incremental training, however, remains highly challenging due to catastrophic forgetting phenomenon, which makes it difficult for neural networks to effectively incorporate new knowledge. A common strategy consists in retraining the generative model on its own synthetic data in order to mitigate forgetting. Yet, such an approach faces two major limitations: (i) the continually increasing training time eventually becomes intractable, and (ii) reliance on synthetic data inevitably leads to long-term performance degradation, since synthetic samples lack the richness of real training data. In this paper, we attenuate these issues by designing a novel and more expressive conditioning mechanism for generative models based on low rank adaptation (LoRA), that exclusively employs rank 1 matrices, whose reparametrized matrix rank is functionally increased using carefully selected functions — and dubbed functional LoRA: FunLoRA. Using this dynamic conditioning, the generative model is guaranteed to avoid catastrophic forgetting and needs only to be trained on data from the current task. Extensive experiments using flow-matching based models trained from scratch, showcase that our proposed parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method surpasses prior state-of-the-art results based on diffusion models, reaching higher classification accuracy scores, while only requiring a fraction of the memory cost and sampling time.

Introduction

One of the primary objectives of deep learning, consists in developing smart agents, capable of continuously learning and interacting with their surrounding environments — thereby mimicking the adaptive capabilities of humans. Among these approaches, generative models are particularly appealing, as they can — in principle — produce an infinite amount of data via sampling. Early attempts to solve this challenge employ regularization techniques on the generative model

An ideal solution would only allocate a few more extra conditional parameters per class while exclusively training on a new task — as this would ensure the parameter cost remains minimal and there is no forgetting, since the architecture is dynamic.

In order to address these aforementioned issues, we present a novel PEFT method for continual adaptation of conditional generative models. The core of this contribution relies in a LoRA reparametrization exclusively using rank 1 matrices, so as to remain very frugal in terms of memory footprint. Nonetheless to ensure those (LoRA) matrices are expressive, their rank is functionally increased (with an equivalent number of parameters) using carefully selected functions, most notably trigonometric and exponential functions. Indeed, as observed in the experiments, LoRA applied to convolutional layers achieves better results, especially in incremental learning scenarios where there is very little data available to train the attention layers, which cannot learn generalizable patterns across different tasks. Based on this novel PEFT method, we also locate the most important layers to adapt in the U-Net backbone — drastically reducing the parameter cost required for learning new classes. Finally, we specifically focus on incrementally conditioning flow-matching models

Considering all the aforementioned issues, the main contributions of this paper include:

1.

A novel LoRA based PEFT method optimized for adapting generative models in continual learning. This method — dubbed FunLoRA — allocates a minimal number of parameters through LoRA reparametrizations, and then functionally increases the rank of the resulting matrix for increased expressiveness.

2.

An innovative flow-matching based solution for generative continual learning, that is very fast to adapt and does not suffer from any forgetting. This proposed method does not require the generative model to retrain on its own synthetic data, and reaches performances very close to a generative upper bound trained in a multi-task setting — while only allocating up 2.3% extra parameters.

3.

A new approach to identify most important layers when training generative models incrementally, which greatly reduces memory footprint and training time in continual learning scenarios. Most notably, we focus on the convolutional layers of the standard U-Net backbone since it incorporates both class and time information.

4.

Extensive experiments through different benchmarks showing the outperformance of our proposed incremental generative method against state-of-the-art related works — while using only a fraction of their memory cost. Most notably, on the CIFAR100

III
Background

III-A

Conditional Flow Matching

Conditional Flow matching
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
is a generative paradigm inspired from continuous normalizing flows
[
115
]
, that is simulation free and very fast to train, as it does not require solving ordinary differential equations in the forward pass. It consists in learning a vector field
v:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is used to construct a time-dependent diffeomorphic mapping called a flow
\phi:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
between an ambient space
{\mathcal{X}}
and a latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}
. More generally, the neural network
v_{\theta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
parametrized by
\theta
, learns the conditional vector field
u_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}})
by minimizing a mean squared error as shown in Eq
1

[
48
,
51
]
.

{\mathcal{L}}_{CFM}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}({\bf{0},\bf{I}}),q({\mathbf{z}}),p_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}})}\lVert v_{\theta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})-u_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{2}^{2}

(1)

where
q({\mathbf{z}})
is a gaussian prior
{\mathcal{N}}(\bf{0},\bf{I})
, and
p_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{z}})
is
known probability path
that is chosen and defined for each sample in the dataset. In general, the probability path between an image
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
, and a random noise
{\mathbf{z}}
is defined as:

{\mathbf{x}}=(1-t){\mathbf{x}}_{0}+t{\mathbf{z}}

(2)

where
{\mathbf{x}}
is the noisy data sample used in Eq.
1
. It can be noted that this formulation shares similarities with the
”paths”
used in diffusion models, but in this case they are much simpler and straighter, which means they are faster to integrate when sampling than in the case of diffusion models. Indeed, Eq.
2
corresponds to the optimal transport (OT) displacement map
[
116
]
, and this property is very interesting for continual learning where faster sampling is of utmost importance. In the following sections, we will simply refer to conditional flow matching as
”flow matching”
, and the word
”conditional”
will indicate the conditioning on class labels
{\mathbf{y}}
.

III-A
1
LoRA

Given the pre-trained weight
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{{a}\times{b}}
of a fully connected layer, LoRA seeks to learn an extra weight matrix
\Delta{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{a}\times{b}}
that will modify the initial values of
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
for higher expressiveness, and that is reparametrized as the product of two low rank matrices
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{a}\times{r}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{r}\times{b}}
, so that
\Delta{\mathbf{W}}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}
. This reparametrization is advantageous since the number of parameters in
\Delta{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{a}\times{b}}
is much smaller than in
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
. At inference, an input
{\mathbf{x}}
will interact with both weight matrices so that:

{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=({\mathbf{W}}_{0}+\Delta{\mathbf{W}}){\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{W}}_{0}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{x}}

(3)

where the rank
r
is a hyperparameter that must be tuned, and can be seen as the lower dimension in which the data is down-projected (
{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{x}}
), before it is
reconstructed into
its initial dimension
({\mathbf{A}}\cdot({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{x}}))
. Usually, LoRA is applied to fully connected layers in attention blocks, however it can also be extended to convolutional layers with weight parameters denoted as
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}\times{s}\times{s}}
, where
C_{out}
and
C_{in}
respectively denote the number of output and input feature channels, and
{s}
the kernel size. Most notably, by defining the parameters
a_{conv},b_{conv},r_{conv}
as the convolutional equivalents of
a,b,c
where
a_{conv}=C_{out}
and
b_{conv}=C_{in}\cdot{s}\cdot{s}
, the matrices from Eq
3
can be rewritten as
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{conv}\times{r}_{conv}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{r}_{conv}\times b_{conv}}
, and their product is reshaped to match the dimension of the initial weight tensor
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
. Furthermore, the variable
r_{conv}
can be customized, and is often set to
{r}_{conv}={r}\cdot{s}
so as to add a dependency on the kernel size in the bottleneck dimension. In general, the matrix
{\mathbf{B}}
is initialized to zero, and the matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
follows a Kaiming normal initialization. The zero initialization ensures the identity function is initially preserved since the product
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{0}
, and the
Kaiming initialization ensures that non-zero gradients can flow into both matrices during optimization.

IV
Proposed Method

IV-A

Problem Formulation.

We consider a continual learning scenario, where a conditional flow matching model
{v}_{{\theta},{\mathbf{y}}}(.)
parametrized by
{\theta}
and conditioned on labels
{\mathbf{y}}
is trained (using Eq.
1
) on a stream of data divided into
T
distinct groups called tasks. Each task
\mathcal{T}{{}^{t}}
can be seen as a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}{{}^{t}}
=
\{({\mathbf{x}}^{t}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}^{t}_{i})\}_{i}
made of image-label pairs, with several distinct classes constituting a task. We denote
{\mathcal{Y}}^{t}
as the label space of task
t
, verifying the condition
{\mathcal{Y}}{{}^{t}}\cap{\mathcal{Y}}^{t^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for
t\neq t^{\prime}
, meaning there are no overlapping classes between tasks. Additionally, we denote
{\mathcal{Y}}^{1:t}=\cup_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}{\mathcal{Y}}^{t^{\prime}}
as the union of all label spaces up to task
t
. Following this notation, incremental learning consists in updating the parameters
{\theta}
on a current task
t
while minimizing catastrophic forgetting on all the previous classes in
{\mathcal{Y}}^{1:t-1}
.
Furthermore, to assess the performance of this incremental generative model, we consider a classifier network
c_{\phi}(.)
parametrized by
\phi
, more specifically its accuracy on a test set with labels including all classes
\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{1:t}
. This setup, where the classifier network
c_{\phi}(.)
is trained without any constraint on fully synthetic data, is possible thanks to the proposed incremental generative model.

IV-B

LoRA based approach

Concerning the backbone of the generative model, which is a standard convolutional U-Net popularized with diffusion models
—
we only focus on adapting the convolutional layers in subsequent tasks
—
since the conditioning and timestep information is only applied on these portions of the network.
Most notably, given the weight parameters
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}\times{s}\times{s}}
of a convolutional layer, we propose to perform a LoRA decomposition to adapt the magnitude of each filter using two matrices
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times 1}
and
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C_{in}}
so that:

{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}

(4)

is used to modify the weight kernels from
{\mathbf{W}}_{0}
as

{\mathbf{W}}={\mathbf{W}}_{0}\odot{\mathbf{F}}

(5)

where
{\mathbf{F}}
is reshaped to have dimensions
\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}}
. This strategy of multiplying the convolutional filters with a matrix
{\mathbf{F}}
(without using a LoRA based on
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
) is inspired from
[
111
]
, and is called feature modulation, and was historically used for style transfer (prior to the advent of stable diffusion and LoRA).

Afterwards, the matrix
{\mathbf{W}}
which had its kernel rescaled is used when performing a convolution with the input data. Furthermore, since we focus on incremental learning (which benefits from reduced memory cost), the shared dimension between
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
(
i.e
the rank) is chosen to be 1; these matrices are initialized with ones to ensure the identity operation is preserved during the first iteration. Indeed, as will be shown in the following sections, this also makes it possible to directly calculate the importance of each layer in the neural network, and can be more expressive than standard addition when using rank 1 matrices. Furthermore, in order to further facilitate class-incremental learning of our generative model, we also condition each matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
on a label
{\mathbf{y}}
so that

{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}

(6)

and consequently

{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{W}}_{0}\odot{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}

(7)

which is the equivalent of learning a different sub-instance of a neural network for each class. This formulation has the advantage of dissociating the learning of classes from tasks, and is the equivalent to learning a single class per task, which is the most challenging scenario in continual learning. Furthermore, it allows for efficient parameter sharing/reuse if the distribution of a certain class
{\mathbf{y}}
changes as only the parameters associated to the current class would be duplicated / fine-tuned.

IV-C

Functional LoRA

In order to further increase the expressiveness of the proposed method, we consider applying several functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i}
to the matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
(that have a minimal rank of 1), before or after taking their product
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
.
This increases the rank
of a final
”functional”
matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}}
that will be used in the LoRA reparametrization, and defined as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}},{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(8)

where
p
is the total number of distinct functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i}
applied, and
\alpha_{i}
is a learnable ponderation factor associated to each function
f_{i}
. As a result the rank of the matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
increases according to the value of
p
, but also depending on the choice of the functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i}
. Different possibilities are available for those functions, and may exhibit different advantages as will be explained in the following sections. Those functions may include circular shifts, element-wise powers, or trigonometric functions.

Circular shifts.
A right circular shift of a vector
{\mathbf{M}}
by
i
elements, denoted
\operatorname{rshift}(\mathbf{M},i)
, is defined using an
m\times m

permutation matrix

P_{R}^{(i)}
. This matrix multiplication rearranges
\mathbf{M}
’s elements: the last
i
elements move to the front, preserving their order, while the first
m-i
elements shift to the end. The operation is simply
\operatorname{rshift}({\mathbf{M}},i)=P_{R}^{(i)}{\mathbf{M}}
. The matrix
P_{R}^{(i)}
is specifically crafted to select the final
i
elements for the initial positions and shift the remaining elements accordingly. Naturally, this function is trivially extended to the transposed matrix
{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
in which case the transpose function is implicitly applied before and after applying the
\operatorname{rshift}(.,i)
function. Then, we can define a corresponding function
f^{\operatorname{rshift}}_{i}
that applies this circular shift to two matrices
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times 1}
and
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C_{in}}
(used in LoRA) before multiplying them so that:

f^{\operatorname{rshift}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})=\operatorname{rshift}({\mathbf{A}},i)\times\operatorname{rshift}({\mathbf{B}},i)

(9)

outputs a matrix with dimension in
\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}}
. By successively applying this function with different values for the circular shifts
— one may show
that it is possible to increase the rank of the final functional matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{rshift}}
defined as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{rshift}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{\operatorname{rshift}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}},{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(10)

in which case the class conditional matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
are employed for the LoRA parametrization. Theoretically speaking (and as will be seen in the experiments), assuming
C_{out}
,
C_{in}>p
, the rank of final matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{rshift}}
is generally equal to
p
, which confirms that this functional parametrization successfully increases the rank of the subsequent matrix used in LoRA. Nonetheless, as will be confirmed through experiments, the expressiveness is not the highest, and other functional parametrizations described in the following sections may exhibit superior performance.

for each class so that their product gives a matrix
{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}}
which spans all filters, and can be used to modify their values. To further increase the rank of this matrix
{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
(which is one by default), functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i}
are applied to each entry in
{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
or to
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
separately, before taking the product.

Element-wise powers.
Another straightforward choice for the function
f_{i}
consists in applying element-wise powers so as to raise any input value
x
to a power
i
following:

f_{i}^{\operatorname{pow}}(x)=x^{i}

(11)

Then by defining a vector of powers
\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
initialized with values from
1
to
p
:

\bm{\delta}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&\cdots&p\end{bmatrix}

(12)

the functional matrix expression
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{pow}}
can be written as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{pow}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{\bm{\delta}_{i}}^{\operatorname{pow}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(13)

where the function
f_{\bm{\delta}_{i}}^{\operatorname{pow}}
is applied element-wise to the product
{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
=
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
, and the rank of the final matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{pow}}
can also theoretically increase to
p
. By default the hyperparameters of
\bm{\delta}
are frozen and not optimizable. However, for further expressiveness they may also be optimized using gradient descent, and when doing so, a surrogate formulation that works with decimal powers is employed
1
1

1

We experimentally found that the formulation in Eq.
11
is not defined for real numbers with negative values and decimal powers in PyTorch (it outputs NaN: Not a Number).
, and defined as

f_{\bm{\delta}_{i}}^{\operatorname{pow-s}}(x)=\sign(x)\cdot(\abs(x))^{\bm{\delta}_{i}}

(14)

where
\sign(.)
and
\abs(.)
respectively denote the sign and absolute value functions.
Under these conditions, the values in
\bm{\delta}
will become decimal during training and the resulting functional matrix will be rewritten as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{pow,\bm{\delta}}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{\bm{\delta_{i}}}^{\operatorname{pow-s}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(15)

While using exponents can theoretically increase the rank up to
p
, our experiments will show that numerical approximations often yield a rank lower than
p
. This happens because higher powers may lead to infinitesimally small terms.

Trigonometric functions.
Trigonometric functions, like the cosine, offer a powerful way to increase the rank of our functional matrices due to their orthogonality and periodicity. We can define an element-wise cosine function as:

f_{i}^{cos}(x)=\cos(\bm{\omega}_{i}\cdot x)

(16)

We also define a frequency vector
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
, initialized with values from
1
to
p
.

\bm{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\omega}_{1}&\bm{\omega}_{2}&\cdots&\bm{\omega}_{p}\end{bmatrix}

(17)

Hence, the function expression of the matrix
\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{cos}}
can be defined as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{cos}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}^{\operatorname{cos}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(18)

Here
f_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}^{\operatorname{cos}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})
is applied element-wise to the product of
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
. By default, the
\bm{\omega}
hyperparameters are not optimized. However for better expressiveness, they can be optimized, in which case the functional matrix is assigned an
\bm{\omega}
superscript, and is rewritten as:

\mathbf{F}_{{\mathbf{y}},p}^{\operatorname{cos,\bm{\omega}}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}f_{\bm{\omega}_{i}}^{\operatorname{cos}}({\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}})

(19)

A particularly interesting property of the cosine function, which our experiments will confirm, is its ability to increase a matrix’s rank beyond
p
. This makes it potentially more expressive than other functional forms we have discussed.

IV-D

Layer Selection

In order to measure the importance of each neural network layer, we define a metric that quantifies the sensitivity of a given layer to a given task. Since all entries in the weight matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}
are initialized to 1, the importance of a given layer
l
can be defined as

I_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{A}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}-1\rVert_{1}}{\dim(\vecc({\mathbf{A}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}))}+\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{B}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}-1\rVert_{1}}{\dim(\vecc({\mathbf{B}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}})|}\right)

(20)

Here,
\lVert.\rVert_{1}
denotes the L1 norm,
\vecc(.)
represents the vectorization of a matrix, and
\dim(.)
signifies the number of entries in a vector.
{\mathbf{A}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}
are the LoRA matrices for layer
l
and class
\mathbf{y}
. This formulation evaluates the average L1 distance from the initial value of 1. To extend this importance to all classes, we average this measure as

I_{l}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}\sum_{{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}I_{l,{\mathbf{y}}}

(21)

where
|{\mathcal{Y}}|
is the cardinal of the set of labels.

V
Experiments

This section showcases the impact of the proposed incremental generative model, with efficient parameter allocation for each new class. We start by performing an ablation on the functions used to increase the rank, and then we compare it against closely related PEFT methods. Finally, we evaluate it with respect to closely related works involving incremental diffusion models trained from scratch
— and against
methods leveraging pre-trained stable diffusion models for continual learning.

V-A

Datasets and Evaluation Metrics

Dataset.
We evaluate the proposed method on a wide range of challenging datasets commonly used in incremental learning with generative models, most notably:

•

CIFAR10
[
56
]
which contains 50000 image for training and 10000 images for testing, equally split amongst 10 classes. This dataset is split into 5 equal tasks, each with an equal number of classes. This split will also be denoted as
”2-2”
since the first task contains 2 classes, and the following ones also contain 2 classes.

•

CIFAR100
[
56
]
which contains 50000 image for training and 10000 images for testing, equally split amongst 100 classes. This dataset is split into 5 or 10 equal tasks, each with an equal number of classes. This split will also be denoted as
”20-20”
(resp
”10-10”
) since the first task contains 20 classes (resp 10 classes), and the following ones also contain 20 classes (resp 10 classes).

•

ImageNet100
[
117
]
which contains 130000 images for training and 5000 images for testing equally split amongst 100 classes and resized to a dimension of
64\times 64
. This dataset is split into 5 tasks, each with an equal number of classes. This split will also be denoted as
”20-20”
since the first task contains 20 classes, and the following ones also contain 20 classes.

Metrics.
We evaluate all methods using the average incremental accuracy (AIA) and the last accuracy (LA) of the classifier defined as following
[
118
,
16
]
. Given an accuracy score
A_{t}
obtained on a test set
{\mathcal{D}}^{t}_{test}
, the average accuracy (
AA_{T}
) up to an arbitrary task
T
is formally defined as:

AA_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}

(22)

which corresponds to the mean of the accuracies achieved on all tasks encountered thus far. In particular, we also define the last accuracy (LA), as the last score
AA_{T}
obtained after training on the last task. The average incremental accuracy (AIA) is defined as:

AIA_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}AA_{t}

(23)

which is the arithmetic mean of the average accuracies computed across all encountered tasks.

Implementation Details:
During the first task, an Adam optimizer is used to train the flow matching model using a learning rate of
2.0\times 10^{-4}
(resp
1.0\times 10^{-4}
), linearly warmed up for the first 1000 iterations, for a total of 500 epochs (resp 250 epochs) on CIFAR10-100 (resp ImageNet100). During this first task, an exponential moving average (EMA) with a decay factor
\beta_{1}=0.9995
is also used starting from epoch 200 (resp 100) on CIFAR10-100 (resp ImageNet100). During subsequent tasks, the learning rate of the Adam optimizer is set to
1.0\times 10^{-2}
, and the flow matching model is trained for 300 epochs (resp 200 epochs) on CIFAR10-100 (resp ImageNet100) using a decay factor
\beta_{1}=0.995
for the EMA, starting from epoch 100; and the batch size is 128 for all datasets. Concerning the classifier, we utilize a ResNet18 trained for 30 epochs and using a batch size of 100 on all datasets. The optimizer used is SGD with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
, a momentum of
0.9
, a weight decay of
5\cdot 10^{-4}
, and a one cycle scheduler
[
119
]
with a maximum learning rate of
0.1
. The augmentations used are random cropping and horizontal flip. Unless mentioned otherwise, the solver used when sampling the flow matching model is a dopri5 (Dormand-Prince) with error tolerances of
1.0\times 10^{-4}
. In all experiments, we utilize either a single A100 or H100 GPU, with 80GB of RAM.

Baseline.
For the baseline, we consider a LoRA applied for feature modulation on convolutional layers as expressed in Eq

and

]
.

]

Comparative Method 2 - LoRA on attention layers.
This comparative method is the standard LoRA decomposition applied to attention layers as in
[
8
]
. In practice, the rank used is larger than 1 for all layers in the generative model, and the matrices are also conditioned on class labels.

Comparative Method 3 - LoRA on Convolutional Layers v1.
This corresponds to a LoRA decomposition applied to convolutional layers, as implemented in the source code of the official LoRA paper
[
8
]

[

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel LoRA based PEFT method — dubbed FunLoRA — to adapt conditional generative model for continual classification. Most notably, our method leverages a frugal low rank reparametrized matrix (of rank 1), whose rank is functionally increased by applying carefully selected element-wise functions. As extensively corroborated through numerous experiments, the proposed method significantly outperforms related works in terms of accuracy, while requiring much less parameters. Indeed, our proposed selection of important layers ensures only a portion of the backbone is adapted while maintaining competitive performance, and ensuring there is no forgetting. Furthermore it can outperform methods using large pre-trained models such as stable diffusion, while requiring significantly less resources (compute power and data). This proposed method was also further bolstered by the usage of the novel flow matching paradigm, resulting in an accelerated sampling time with respect to diffusion models — and which holds tremendous potential in continual learning.
