A narrative‑informed Intensive Care Unit humanizing intervention: trainee perspectives on the ‘Get to Know Me Board’
Authors: E Mirna Mohanraj, Christine Nguyen, Alexander Davidovich, Jonathan Stoever, Deep Patadia
Published: 2026

Clinician educators in Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine navigate the dual imperative of delivering high-acuity care while cultivating humanistic values in trainees. Educators are tasked with modeling human-centered care and evaluating trainees’ performance on communication-centered metrics (ie, patient- and family-centered communication and complex communication around serious illness).1 Best practice intensive care unit (ICU) bundles, such as the A2F ICU liberation bundle,2 are routinely incorporated into daily ICU rounds. Patients and their family members are frequently engaged as partners in ICU rounds where highly technical, medical information is discussed and manipulated into the daily care plan. What is less commonly centered during ICU rounds is personhood. Beyond the vital signs, laboratory values, and imaging studies, who is this person that we are honored to care for?

The ‘Get to Know Me Board’3 (GTKMB) is a well-described, but underutilized tool designed to build connection between caregivers and their patients. Although GTKMBs have been implemented in various ICU settings, their specific impact on medical trainees has received little attention. In this article, we reflect on a patient encounter that informed our own GTKMB experience in the ICU, review relevant literature, and describe our trainee‑focused implementation during the COVID‑19 pandemic. Within this context, we highlight how routine incorporation of a GTKMB on ICU rounds may support clinician educators in modeling humanistic care and fostering more meaningful engagement between trainees, patients, and families.

Patient narrative

In the fall of 2018, Sunil Jha lay intubated and sedated in an intensive care unit bed. His adult daughter, Sandhya Jha – an ordained pastor and social welfare expert – sat with her mother hoping for his recovery. Sandhya shared social media updates on her father’s condition along with the photo of a hastily scrawled note taped to his glass hospital door (Figure 1). With Sandhya’s permission, I shared her simple but powerful act of advocacy with my critical care colleagues and fellows-in-training. Unfortunately, Sunil did not survive his ICU admission. But the life and story of this stranger impressed upon our team the critical importance of centering compassionate care on the person, rather than on the patient. We were struck by the simplicity of her intervention that allowed healthcare providers to see her father more fully and to consider his psychologic, social, and spiritual needs in parallel with his medical needs.

Literature review

As critical care physicians, we saw in Sunil what we see in our own patients: that admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) places patients at risk for dehumanization – or treating a person as if they do not possess the attributes of other human beings.4 ICUs are often loci where patients’ dignity – or inherent worth as human beings – can be threatened and disrespected.5,6 There are many barriers to providing humanistic – and humanizing – care in the ICU including high workload, time pressures, complex care coordination, cultural differences, and physician burnout.4,7-9 These and other factors can lead to empathy erosion, lapses in communication, and a decreased sense of fulfillment from work. Exacerbating this complex milieu is the ‘ICU patient uniform’ that distorts our ability to recognize and relate to our patients. Endotracheal tubes disrupt our ability to process human facial features. Immobility, hospital gowns, mechanical device circuits, and limited eye contact promote othering behaviors and reduced social interaction.10-14 Sedative use, ICU delirium, loss of bodily control, and loss of autonomy further increase patients’ susceptibility to dehumanizing treatment in the ICU.15

Threats to respect and dignity often arise from structural and environmental aspects of the ICU that can unintentionally foster dehumanization. Experiences with disrespect in the ICU are commonly reported by family members, especially for patients who do not survive their ICU stay.16,17 Disrespect of patients can weaken the trust between patients, families, and providers and can also decrease family satisfaction with ICU care.18 Beyond the harms experienced by patients and families, dehumanizing ICU environments can also erode the interpersonal climate in ways that directly affect clinicians. When disrespect becomes embedded in the clinical environment, studies show it can impair clinicians’ cognitive and procedural performance19,20 and contribute to ‘disrespect contagion’21,22 within teams, undermining collaboration and psychological safety. Furthermore, potentially discriminatory impacts are described by Leyens et al.12 in their studies of infrahumanization where we may deny positive human emotions (eg, compassion, respect, admiration) to individuals who are outside of our own identity groups. In ICU settings, healthcare providers may consciously or subconsciously detach from their patients as a self-protective mechanism to decrease emotional burden and burnout.23,24 Conversely, developing familiarity with the human being behind the disease process can positively influence provider attitudes and personal fulfillment from their role as an ICU caregiver.25

Humanizing interventions in the ICU aim to improve the relational proximity between healthcare providers, patients, and patient families. Dignity-building interventions that improve both the frequency and quality of these interactions can reduce our tendency to dehumanize patients.26,27 In a 2018 systematic review, Galvin et al.28 examine how humanization initiatives in critical care – such as improved communication, family engagement, and attention to emotional needs – affect the psychological well‑being of clinicians and relatives. Across studies, these humanizing interventions, defined as interventions with “substantial potential to increase physical or emotional proximity between the patient/relatives and healthcare professionals,” consistently reduced anxiety, stress, and moral distress among healthcare professionals while enhancing trust, satisfaction, and coping for families.

Humanizing ICU process interventions such as liberal family visitation or family participation in basic care may have positive impacts on provider burnout and family emotions/satisfaction, but quantitative psychologic impacts have not been demonstrated.26,28 Narrative medicine – grounded in eliciting, interpreting, and integrating patient stories to foster empathy, reflection, and person‑centered care – provides a framework for a wide range of humanizing interventions in the ICU.29 Although traditionally operationalized through reflective writing or storytelling, narrative medicine encompasses any intervention that brings the patient’s lived experience, values, and identity into the clinical space. Previous ICU studies have demonstrated that narrative medicine approaches can encourage patients to reflect on their critical illness or prompt families to honor a dying patient.30,31 Narrative diaries may mitigate anxiety and reduce risk for post-ICU posttraumatic stress disorder with particular benefits to patients and families at risk for post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and PICS-family.32

Perhaps unbeknownst to Sandhya, the note she taped to Sunil’s ICU door is an example of a narrative-informed intervention known as a GTKMB. Although the GTKMB is not always labeled as a narrative medicine tool, it functions as one by eliciting key elements of a patient’s life story and integrating them into the daily clinical experience. In this way, the GTKMB operationalizes core narrative medicine principles – bearing witness to patient identity, honoring personhood, and using narrative to guide humanized care. The first implementation of a GTKMB in the ICU setting was described by Billings et al.33 in 2006. As one element of a broader initiative to enhance humanizing approaches within the ICU, the authors engaged family members in sharing personal information about the patient, including background, hobbies, and interests, to support a more person‑centered understanding of each individual. This personal information was then displayed on a posterboard in the patient’s room and used to set the tone for ongoing care discussions. The authors reported bilateral positive impacts of the intervention. Family observed that the patient was cared for as a human being, not just as a disease process. ICU caregivers developed familiarity with their patient’s preillness narrative and ‘acceptable’ quality of life.

In the ensuing decades, many endeavors – ranging from patient-centered care models to person-centered care models – have attempted to facilitate deeper connections between providers, patients, and families. Most recently, Ahmad et al.15 reported on the humanizing impact of the GTKMB among multiprofessional ICU caregivers. Using a qualitative approach, the study gathered surveys and interviews from patients, families, and ICU caregivers to evaluate their experiences with a GTKMB. Healthcare professional participants reported that the GTKMB eased connection with family members, contextualized goals of care, and provided an increased sense of empathy and purpose to the administration of ICU care. While some patients and family members reported inconsistent use and limited accessibility of the GTKMB, others reported that the information served as an ‘icebreaker’ of sorts, facilitating connection and ultimately humanizing the patient experience.

Institutional experience

Inspired by Sandhya and Sunil’s story and noting that prior studies had not specifically focused on the medical trainee experience with GTKMBs, we introduced our version of the intervention (Figure 2) in a New York City academic hospital during the second surge of the COVID-19 pandemic. The intervention was designed and conducted by Pulmonary & Critical Care Fellows, approved by the IRB (19-01225), and sponsored by The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Back to Bedside Program. Patients admitted to the Mount Sinai Morningside ICU (New York, NY, United States) with an anticipated ICU stay of greater than 48 hours were enrolled. Patients or a legally authorized representative assented to providing patient biographic information, which was then shared on daily inter-professional rounds (Figure 3) and posted in the patient’s room. This qualitative study involved the development of a trainee‑focused survey which included Likert‑style items assessing changes in behavior, emotional engagement, and professional attitudes before and after exposure to the GTKMB intervention. Internal survey validation was conducted with a sample of 10 faculty members, who completed the survey and contributed data used to assess item performance and refine question wording.

Mount Sinai Morningside Hospital ICU Get to Know Me Board (GTKMB). Patient biographic information was obtained and shared via a GTKMB mounted on each ICU patient door.

A pulmonary and critical care medicine fellow shares patient biographic information from a GTKMB during daily interprofessional rounds. The GTKMB featured in this image shows a version designed for a prior feasibility assessment.

After 2 to 4 weeks of exposure to the intervention, resident physicians completed the questionnaire and participated in semi-structured interviews conducted by trained pulmonary and critical care fellows using an interview guide. Interviews were audio‑recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed to refine questioning and ensure depth of inquiry. Using an iterative, inductive approach, 4 fellows independently coded transcripts, meeting regularly to compare interpretations, resolve discrepancies, and refine the codebook. Coding continued until thematic saturation was reached, at which point 5 overarching themes were identified through consensus‑based thematic analysis. In line with Sponsor guidelines, fellows were responsible for all aspects of study design and implementation including survey and interview design, conducting semi-structured interviews, analyzing transcriptions, and coding data.

The study was conducted for 8 weeks. Twenty patient biographies were completed and shared with the multidisciplinary ICU team. Twenty-nine Internal Medicine and Emergency Medicine resident physicians on ICU rotation were enrolled. Post-intervention, residents perceived that they spent more time with patients eliciting personal information, that they spent more time at the bedside and with family members, and that they established better rapport with family members. Residents reported feeling more enthusiastic and deriving more meaning from ICU work. Residents also reported feeling an increased sense of responsibility for patient welfare and a compulsion to monitor patient progress beyond routine duties. The 5 thematic categories with representative comments are detailed in Table 1. All residents indicated that the intervention was fun, worthwhile, and a welcomed interruption to ICU rounds.

The timing of this intervention was particularly poignant due to the severe isolation inflicted by visitor limitations and COVID-19-associated contact precautions. ICU nurses and later family members became accustomed to seeing the boards and at times pre-empted the study team by completing the board themselves. Ancillary service teams and staff members from clinical consultants to respiratory therapists to environmental services were noted to pause and review the boards before entering a room. Both for individuals entering a patient room and for providers on rounds, reading a patient’s GTKMB provided a respite moment, resetting minds to center on the human behind the illness, alarms, and electronic medical record.

Residents consistently perceived that the GTKMB helped them connect more deeply with patients and families, increased the meaning they derived from ICU work, and strengthened their sense of responsibility for patient welfare. These findings reflect self‑reported perceptions and reflections, rather than observed behavioral changes or measured patient‑ or provider‑level outcomes and should be interpreted within that context. Even so, the consistency of these reflections suggests that structured opportunities to engage with patient narratives may help counteract the dehumanizing forces inherent to critical care.

Our qualitative design and single‑center setting introduce several limitations. The sample size was small. Resident reports in the semi-structured interviews may have been skewed by recall bias, social desirability bias, or a desire to support a fellow-led initiative. Without a comparator group or longitudinal follow‑up, we cannot draw conclusions about confounding effects or sustained impacts on trainee perceptions. While informal staff feedback uniformly supported the intervention as influential to the ICU moral climate, GTKMB implementation required daily and intentional coordination. The greatest barrier was ensuring that daily personnel were designated to consistently obtain and post patient biographical information. During the COVID‑19 surge, this was particularly difficult given restricted visitation, competing clinical demands, staffing limitations, and the emotional weight borne by staff. Beyond the study period, threats to intervention sustenance included identifying a reliable ‘GTKMB champion’ to obtain patient information and variable faculty enthusiasm for incorporating GTKMBs during ICU rounds.

Systematizing humanistic care in the ICU – via GTKMBs or similar approaches – aligns with existing priorities in critical care to promote dignity, communication, and person‑centered practice. While our study does not evaluate patient‑ or provider‑level outcomes, the intervention was feasible, well‑received, and perceived by trainees as enhancing connection and meaning in their work. These findings suggest that humanizing tools such as the GTKMB warrant further exploration and thoughtful consideration by critical care educators and professional societies as they develop tailored humanizing strategies that can be adapted to local institutional practices and community characteristics. Calls have been made to integrate the GTKMB – an affordable and easily reproducible tool – into the best practice A2F ICU liberation bundle endorsed by the Society of Critical Care Medicine.2 The intervention can be completed by the patient, family member, or any member of the ICU team and would foster a humanistic and holistic wrap-up to the standard of care A2F bundle review.

The GTKMB offers a low-cost, feasible, narrative-informed intervention to teach and model humanistic care. It can be integrated into ICU rotations and academic teaching sessions. The intervention supports core competencies in professionalism, communication, and systems-based practice, and provides a tangible method for trainees to engage with the psychosocial dimensions of critical illness. The GTKMB intervention also aligns with competency-based medical education frameworks via opportunities for direct observation and feedback in domains such as patient-centered communication and interprofessional collaboration. Educators may consider pairing GTKMB use with reflective writing exercises or bedside teaching rounds focused on dignity and respect.

Future directions include exploring the impact of GTKMBs on reducing trainee burnout, facilitating patient- and family-centered communication, and easing rapport-building to enhance complex communication around serious illness. Depending on the culture and challenges of individual ICUs, humanizing interventions can be paired with relevant scales to demonstrate improvement in challenging realms such as honoring patient dignity,34 respecting patients and families,16 or improving family satisfaction with ICU care.35 Principles of effective change management must be thoughtfully strategized to ensure passionate and sustained engagement from leadership champions, ICU stakeholders, patients, and their families.

The rationale for humanizing ICU care is compelling: patients and families can experience emotional distress when their personhood is overlooked in high‑acuity settings. While our study does not evaluate long‑term outcomes, existing literature suggests that humanizing practices may help mitigate some of these harms by fostering connection, dignity, and a more trusting therapeutic relationship. Integrating such practices into daily ICU workflows offers a feasible, low‑cost way to support more person‑centered care.

Conclusion

The GTKMB offered trainees a simple, structured way to re‑center personhood in the ICU and reconnect with the human being behind the illness. In this single‑center qualitative study, residents perceived that engaging with patient biographies deepened their sense of connection, increased the meaning they derived from ICU work, and strengthened their sense of responsibility for patient welfare. Narrative‑based tools like the GTKMB can meaningfully shape how trainees experience ICU caregiving, even when effects are self‑reported. Successful and minimally disruptive implementation hinges on workflow integration and a reliable champion to gather patient stories. Clinician educators are uniquely positioned to champion humanizing interventions like the GTKMB. By embedding such practices into ICU education, we can cultivate a culture of dignity and respect that benefits patients, families, trainees, and the broader healthcare team. As we train the next generation of pulmonary and critical care physicians, ensuring that humanism remains central to our educational mission is both necessary and achievable.
