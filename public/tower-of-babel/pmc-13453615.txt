Aortic valve–preserving reconstruction of the aortomitral curtain in extensive prosthetic mitral endocarditis
Authors: Joon Young Kim, Joon Bum Kim
Published: 2026

AV-preserving aortomitral curtain reconstruction in prosthetic mitral endocarditis.

Central Message

In selected patients with prosthetic mitral endocarditis involving the aorto-mitral curtain, SVC division and commissurotomy enable radical debridement while preserving the native aortic valve.

Extensive mitral infective endocarditis (IE) involving the aortomitral curtain (mitral-aortic intervalvular fibrosa) typically necessitates double-valve replacement with reconstruction of the intervalvular fibrous body.1,2 When the native aortic valve (AV) cusps remain uninvolved, however, an AV-preserving approach may be considered as an alternative. We describe a strategy using superior vena cava (SVC) division and longitudinal commissurotomy to achieve radical debridement and AV-preserving reconstruction of the aortomitral curtain in a complex redo case of prosthetic mitral IE.

Case Presentation

This case report was approved by the institutional review board of Asan Medical Center (no. 2026-0512; approved April 14, 2026), which waived informed consent. A 36-year-old man was transferred to our hospital with persistent fever and profound weight loss (20 kg; body mass index, 13.5 kg/m2) after 2 months of intensive care. He had undergone mechanical mitral valve replacement (MVR) for Streptococcus anginosus IE 2 months earlier and permanent pacemaker implantation for complete atrioventricular block 1 month earlier. Preoperative computed tomography and echocardiography demonstrated IE involving the aortomitral curtain, moderate aortic regurgitation, severe mitral regurgitation, severe tricuspid regurgitation (grade 4, tricuspid regurgitation Vmax 4.1 m/s), and severe resting pulmonary hypertension (estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure 75.2 mm Hg) (Video 1).

Operative Strategy

After redo median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass was established. A right atriotomy was performed, cardiac arrest was achieved with retrograde cardioplegia, and the mitral prosthesis was removed. Intraoperative inspection confirmed that the AV cusps were free of disease; accordingly, an AV-preserving strategy was planned.

Two maneuvers were used to optimize exposure. First, the SVC was snared and divided to facilitate visualization of the aortomitral curtain. Before division of the SVC, exposure of the aortomitral curtain and left atrial roof remained limited (Figure 1, A). After SVC division, the operative view of the aortomitral curtain improved, allowing easier extension of the left atriotomy to the left atrial roof (Figure 1, B). Second, the aortotomy was extended longitudinally across the commissure between the noncoronary and left coronary cusps, thereby dividing the aortomitral curtain. This maneuver allowed direct visualization of the infected aortomitral curtain and adjacent subcommissural tissue (Figure 2, A) and enabled radical debridement of all infected tissue.

Exposure and reconstruction of the aortomitral curtain after superior vena cava (SVC) division and longitudinal commissurotomy. A, Exposure of the infected aortomitral curtain after SVC division and longitudinal commissurotomy through the noncoronary–left coronary commissure. B, Direct approximation of the debrided aortomitral curtain and adjacent subcommissural tissue before mitral valve replacement.

After debridement, the aortomitral curtain and adjacent subcommissural area were directly approximated to restore anterior mitral annular continuity, followed by MVR with a 27-mm mechanical prosthesis (Figure 2, B). After MVR, the height and coaptation of the AV cusps were carefully assessed, and the aortomitral curtain and subcommissural tissue were directly reconstructed to preserve native AV function. Because pacemaker lead vegetation was suspected without tricuspid leaflet destruction, tricuspid annuloplasty was performed using a 28-mm ring. Aortic crossclamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times were 80 and 102 minutes, respectively. Video 2 shows the operative sequence.

Postoperative computed tomography and echocardiography confirmed successful aortomitral curtain reconstruction with trivial aortic regurgitation and preserved AV function (Video 3).

Discussion

This case highlights 2 important technical points. First, SVC division with longitudinal commissurotomy through the noncoronary–left coronary commissure provided excellent exposure of the aortomitral curtain in this complex redo case, facilitating radical debridement. Second, when the AV cusps are intact, direct approximation of the debrided aortomitral curtain may offer a viable alternative to conventional double-valve replacement, preserving native AV function without patch reconstruction.

Careful patient selection is essential. Intraoperative assessment should confirm disease-free AV cups and adequate residual tissue for reconstruction after radical debridement. Inadequate debridement or overly conservative valve preservation may increase the risk of persistent or recurrent infection.3

In conclusion, SVC division combined with longitudinal AV commissurotomy through the noncoronary–left coronary commissure enabled safe exposure, radical debridement, and AV-preserving reconstruction of the aortomitral curtain in complex redo prosthetic mitral IE. This approach may represent a reasonable alternative to double-valve replacement in highly selected patients when the native aortic valve cusps remain free of disease.
