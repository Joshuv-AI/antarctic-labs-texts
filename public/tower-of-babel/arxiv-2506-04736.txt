Gravitational effects on Hong-Ou-Mandel interference in terrestrial laboratory
Xuan Ye, Yang Zhang, Bo Wang

Abstract
In this study, we investigate the relativistic effects of Earth on Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) interference experiments conducted in a terrestrial laboratory. Up to the second order, we calculate the relativistic time delay from the null geodesic equation (particle perspective), and the phase shift, along with the associated effective time delay, from the Klein-Gordon equation (wave perspective). Since gravity influences both the temporal and spatial parts of the phase shift, these time delays differ and predict different coincidence probabilities. The previous HOM experiment conducted on a rotating platform shows that the wave perspective can explain the experimental results. We further explore the frame-dragging and redshift effects within an arbitrarily oriented rectangular interferometer in two distinct cases, finding that both effects can be amplified by increasing the number of light loops. Additionally, we emphasize that the next-leading order Sagnac effect, due to the gravitational acceleration, is comparable to the Thomas precession, geodetic effect, and Lense-Thirring effect. To detect the leading-order Sagnac effect and the redshift effect caused by the gravitational acceleration within the current experimental precision, we determine the number of loops photons should travel in the interferometer. Furthermore, we suggest using the difference between any two HOM patterns with different effective time delays as a probe to detect the influence of relativistic effects on quantum systems.

In Ref.
[

The key quantity in the HOM interference experiments is the coincidence probability, which represents
the interference pattern as a function of the time delay. In the literature,
two perspectives are often
used to encode the relativistic effects of a weak gravitational field into the HOM interference pattern:
the particle perspective and the wave perspective. In the particle perspective
[
19
,
20
,
23
,
22
]
, the coincidence
probability is calculated using the two-photon state, constructed from the relativistic time delays
obtained by solving the null geodesic equation.
However, from the wave perspective, the coincidence probability is calculated using the two-photon state constructed from
the effective time delays, which are derived from the phase shift obtained by solving
the Klein-Gordon equation
[
21
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32

. We calculate the time delays based on these
two perspectives up to the second order and find that they differ by several terms at the
second order and even by a minus sign at the first order. These differences occur because gravity not
only influences the temporal part of the phase shift but also the spatial part.
Nevertheless, in the context of the HOM experiments,
the time delays from these two perspectives have not been carefully distinguished.
The analysis of the HOM experiment conducted on a rotating
platform indicates that the experimental results
can be explained through
the wave perspective
[
23
]
. This suggests that when integrating the general relativity and the quantum theory,
emphasis should be placed on the wave nature of matter rather than its particle nature.

In this paper, we study the relativistic effects of Earth on the HOM interference
patterns in a terrestrial laboratory from the wave perspective.
We discuss the feasibility of
performing canonical quantization on a massless minimally-coupling scalar field in a terrestrial laboratory
and show the connection between operators, mode functions, and quantum states, which has
not been extensively covered in the literature. Moreover, we allow the areal vector and arm vectors
of the interferometer to point in arbitrary directions, thereby generalizing the work of
Ref.
[
21
]
. We examine the time delays within the same interferometer configuration in two specific cases involving different light paths. In the first case, the redshift effect is absent,
and only the frame-dragging effect remains. Conversely, in the second case, the frame-dragging effect
is absent, leaving only the redshift effect. This approach enables us to study the independent
influence of these two effects on the coincidence probability. Additionally, we determine the number
of loops photons should travel in the interferometer for each case to ensure that
the Sagnac effect and the gravitational acceleration-induced redshift effect are comparable to the current
experimental accuracy of

\sim 10^{-18}
s, while keeping the interferometer size remains manageable.
We also introduce a probe, the difference between any two coincidence probabilities with
different effective time delays, to detect the relativistic influences and compare it with that from the other interferometer described in Ref.
[
18
]
.

2.1
Hong-Ou-Mandel interference

In the HOM interference experiment, two photons are emitted
from the same light source and reach the 50:50 beam splitter (BS) at different
times, traveling along separate paths. Subsequently, the photons interfere at
the BS before reaching the detectors. The primary physical quantity measured
in the HOM experiment is the probability of each detector
detecting one photon, which is called the coincidence probability.
We shall derive this quantity in the following.

In the Minkowski spacetime, a general two-photon state in the context of the HOM interference
experiment is given by
[
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
]

\displaystyle\left|\phi\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\right\rangle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}}\mathcal{N}_{\phi}\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\Phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}\right)e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}}e^{i\omega_{2}t_{2}}a_{\bm{\sigma}_{1}}^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})a_{\bm{\sigma}_{2}}^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})|0\rangle,

(1)

where
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
are the central
frequencies of two photons, respectively.
\bm{\sigma}
represents
the degree of freedom of a photon, such as polarization, path, etc.

\Phi
is the joint spectral amplitude (JSA)
function describing the two-photon
system.
t_{1},t_{2}
are the travel times of the two photons from the emission to the interference.

{\cal N}_{\phi}
is the normalization constant ensuring

\langle\phi\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\left|\phi\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\right\rangle=1
.

a_{\bm{\sigma}}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)
is
the creation operator,
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state defined by

a_{\bm{\sigma}}(\omega)|0\rangle=0
, where
a_{\bm{\sigma}}(\omega)

is the annihilation operator. The creation and annihilation operators
satisfy the commutation relation

\displaystyle[a_{\bm{\sigma}}(\omega),a_{\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}}^{{\dagger}}(\omega^{\prime})]=\delta_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(2)

where
\delta_{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}}

equals
\delta_{\bm{\sigma}\bm{\sigma^{\prime}}}

if
\bm{\sigma}
is discrete and equals

\delta(\bm{\sigma}-\bm{\sigma^{\prime}})

if
\bm{\sigma}
is continuous.

Similar to the Glauber’s theory of the
joint detection of two electric fields

[
37
,
38
]
, the probability of the joint detection of two photons is given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)|a_{\bm{\sigma}_{1}}^{{\dagger}}(\tau_{1})a^{{\dagger}}_{\bm{\sigma}_{2}}(\tau_{2})a_{\bm{\sigma}_{2}}(\tau_{2})a_{\bm{\sigma}_{1}}(\tau_{1})|\phi\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\rangle,

(3)

which represents the probability that two detectors detect photons
with
\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}
at times
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
.
The creation operator
a^{{\dagger}}_{\bm{\sigma}}(\tau)
relevant to the detector is
defined as

\displaystyle a_{\bm{\sigma}}^{{\dagger}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega e^{i\omega\tau}a^{{\dagger}}_{\bm{\sigma}}(\omega),

(4)

the annihilation operator
a_{\bm{\sigma}}(\tau)
can
be obtained by taking the Hermitian conjugate of (
4
).
Substituting (
1
) and (
4
)
into (
3
), using
(
2
), and integrating over

\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
in (
3
), one gets
the un-normalized coincidence probability
[
33
,
18
]

\displaystyle P_{\bm{\sigma}_{1},\textbf{}\bm{\sigma}_{2}}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi|\mathcal{N}_{\phi}|^{2}\Big\{\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\Big[|\Phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}\right)|^{2}+|\Phi\left(\omega_{2},\omega_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1}\right)|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~+2\text{ Re}\big[\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\Phi^{*}\left(\omega_{2},\omega_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1}\right)\Phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}\right)e^{i(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})(t_{1}-t_{2})}\big]\Big\},

(5)

which differs from that in Refs.
[
33

18
]
by a factor
2\pi
due to
our use of the identity
\int e^{itx}dt=2\pi\delta(x)

The coincidence probability (
5

depends on
(t_{1}-t_{2})
, the difference of

]
. However,

To calculate the coincidence probability of each detector detecting one photon,
one needs to determine the quantum state of the two photons
after passing through the BS and the corresponding JSA.
Before passing through the BS, the paths of the photons are deterministic,
the corresponding quantum state is

\displaystyle\left|\phi(t_{1},t_{2})\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}}e^{i\omega_{2}t_{2}}a_{\cal CC}^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})a_{\cal C}^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})|0\rangle,

(6)

\bm{\sigma}_{1}={\cal CC}

{\cal CC}
and
{\cal C}
represent the paths of the two photons, respectively,
and

\phi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is the spectral profile determined by the light source.
After passing through the BS,
each photon has a 50

probability of continuing along its original path

\%
probability of diverting onto another path.
The paths of two photons are no longer deterministic.
The two-photon state after passing through the BS can be obtained by replacing
the creation and annihilation operators in (

15

39
]

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\cal C}(\omega)\rightarrow e^{i\eta}\hat{a}_{\cal C}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)-\hat{a}_{\cal CC}^{{\dagger}}(\omega),

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{\cal CC}(\omega)\rightarrow\hat{a}_{\cal C}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)+e^{-i\eta}\hat{a}_{\cal CC}^{{\dagger}}(\omega),

where

\displaystyle\left|\phi(t_{1},t_{2})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}e^{i\omega_{1}t_{1}}e^{i\omega_{2}t_{2}}\phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{a}_{\cal C}^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})&\hat{a}_{\cal CC}^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\eta}&1\\
-1&-e^{i\eta}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{a}_{\cal C}^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})\\

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}\right)=\phi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\eta}&1\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big\{1-\frac{2\text{ Re}\big[\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\phi^{*}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})\phi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})e^{i(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})(t_{1}-t_{2})}\big]}{\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\Big[|\phi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})|^{2}+|\phi(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})|^{2}\Big]}\Big\},

The normalization constant

In the original HOM case,

In the quantum beating case, photons are spectrally indistinguishable and frequency entangled, and the spectral profile is

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{QB }}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left[\phi_{\text{f.d}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})+e^{i\varphi}\phi_{\text{f.d}}(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})\right],

\displaystyle P^{c}_{\text{QB}}(t_{1}-t_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1-2\mu\mathcal{N}^{2}[R_{\mu\zeta}^{\varphi}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)+S_{\mu\zeta}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)]\Big),

where

\displaystyle{\cal N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(2\mu(1+\cos\varphi\operatorname{sinc}(2\mu/\zeta))\Big)^{-1},

(22)

\displaystyle R_{\mu\zeta}^{\varphi}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos(\mu(t_{1}-t_{2})-\varphi)\operatorname{tri}\Big(\frac{\zeta(t_{1}-t_{2})}{2}\Big),

\displaystyle S_{\mu\zeta}\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sin\Big(\frac{2\mu}{\zeta}\operatorname{tri}\big(\frac{\zeta(t_{1}-t_{2})}{2}\big)\Big)/\frac{2\mu}{\zeta},

\displaystyle\operatorname{tri}(x)=\begin{cases}1-|x|&\text{ if }|x|\leq 1\\

3
Relativistic effects in coincidence probability

In this section, we present two distinct approaches to investigate how
the relativistic effects of Earth influence the coincidence probability.
The first approach is from the particle perspective, where
the two-photon state in the gravitational field is
influenced through the relativistic time delay, a method often used in the studies of the

3.1
Particle perspective

Under the particle perspective, light is considered as a massless particle, and
the interaction with the gravitational field is localized.
In the Minkowski spacetime the coincidence probability (
16

depends on the difference of the travel times of the two photons. When considering the relativistic
effects of
Earth, the photon travel time will undergo an additional delay, thereby altering
the coincidence probability.
From the particle perspective, the calculation of this relativistic time delay
is straightforward, which only involves an integration
along the null trajectory of a photon. In what follows, we shall compute the
relativistic time delay up to the second order of the coordinates
x^{\mu}

The null interval of a photon in the laboratory reference frame is

\displaystyle 0=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu},

3.2
Wave perspective

Under the wave perspective, light is considered as a wave, and the interaction
with the gravitational field is non-localized. To apparently exhibt
the differences between the
particle and wave perspectives, we shall calculate the phase shift (and the corresponding

Furthermore, we will calculate the coincidence probability

the effective time delay rather than the relativistic time delay.

Since the indistinguishability
of the two photons is primarily influenced by the phase shift and

7
Conclusions and discussions

In this paper, we investigate the relativistic effects on the HOM interference
experiments conducted in a terrestrial laboratory. We calculate the time delays up to the second order
from two perspectives. For the particle perspective the photon is localized, the relativistic time delays
(
40
) and (
41
)
are obtained by solving the null geodesic equation (
33
) for a massless particle. Nevertheless,
for the wave perspective
the photon is non-localized, the effective time delays
(
67
) and (
68
)
can be derived using the phase shifts (
64
) and (
65
), which are obtained by
solving the massless minimally-coupling
Klein-Gordon equation. We find that the relativistic time delay differs from the effective
time delay by several terms at the second order, and they differ by a minus sign even at the first order,
because both the temporal and spatial parts of the phase shift are influenced by gravity.
We point out in Appendix
D
that
the relativistic time delay cannot be trivially obtained by dividing
(ck_{0})

by the phase shift, they are actually related by a complicated coefficient vector.
By revisiting the
HOM experiment conducted on a rotating platform, we point that the experimental results support the
wave perspective. This suggests that the wave nature is more important than the particle nature when one tries
to integrate
the quantum theory and the general relativity.

Under the wave perspective, the first order effective time delay (
64
) consists of the
frame dragging part contributed by the
g_{0i}
component and the redshift part contributed by the
g_{00}
component,
as classified in Ref.
[
21
]
.
The frame dragging time delay is contributed by the leading and next-leading order Sagnac effects,
Geodetic effect, Lense-Thirring effect, and Thomas precession effect. The next-leading order Sagnac effect,
resulting
from the coupling of rotation and acceleration, was previously overlooked in Refs.
[
21
,
41
]
. However, for consistency, this effect should be taken into account, as its order of magnitude is comparable to that of the rest of effects
except the leading order Sagnac effect.
The redshift time delay is contributed by both the gravitational and centripetal accelerations.
We study the frame dragging and redshift effects within an arbitrarily
oriented closed rectangular
interferometer in two distinct cases.
In the first case, the light paths are
{\cal CC}:ABCDA
and
{\cal CC}:ADCBA
,
where
the frame-dragging effect is present and the
redshift effect vanishes. In the second case, the light paths

{\cal CC}:ABC
and
{\cal CC}:ADC

have been chosen, where both the frame-dragging effect
and redshift effects occur, with the frame-dragging effect being half of that in the
first case. To isolate the redshift effect,
one can set
\hat{\cal A}\cdot\vec{\omega}=0
to ensure the frame-dragging effect is zero.
These two cases allow the frame dragging and redshift effects to be studied separately.

The order of magnitude of the leading order Sagnac effect is
\sim 10^{-15}~\text{s/km}^{2}
,
and the redshift effect caused by the gravitational acceleration is

\sim 10^{-18}~\text{s/km}^{2}
, both of which are most likely to be detected.
To achieve the current
time precision
\sim 10^{-18}
s
[
64
]
within a proper interferometer size

\sim 10^{2}
m
2

[
24
,
25
]
, the
number of light loops should be
10
for the leading order Sagnac effect and
10^{3}
for the redshift effect.
As shown in Sec.
6
, it is difficult to detect the relativistic effects
by directly observing the interference pattern, because the time delay

\sim 10^{-18}~\text{s}
only causes a small shift in the interference pattern.
To tackle this issue, we suggest using the quantity
{\cal P}({\cal T},\Delta T_{1},\Delta T_{2})

to measure relativistic effects. For the original HOM case, the quantity
{\cal P}_{HOM}({\cal T},\Delta T_{1},\Delta T_{2})

displays a peak and a valley around
{\cal T}\sim\pm 1/(\sqrt{2}\zeta)\sim 10^{-12}\text{s}
,
which may be detected by experiments. The amplitude of

{\cal P}_{HOM}
can be enhanced by increasing

(\Delta T_{1}-\Delta T_{2})
and
\zeta
, which differs from Ref.
[
18
]
due to the use of a different interferometer configuration. For the quantum beating case,

{\cal P}_{QB}
oscillates due to the rapid transition between the
photon bunching and anti-bunching, and the oscillation frequency and amplitude can be amplified by
increasing
(\Delta T_{1}-\Delta T_{2})
,
\zeta
and
\mu
. The quantity

{\cal P}_{QB}
is an alternative candidate probe to measure the relativistic effects in the HOM experiments.
For both the original HOM and quantum beating cases,
measuring the change in the coincidence probabilities around their maximal value can
ensure high-precision measurements while reducing the experimental workload.

In the current study, the relativistic effects are encoded into the effective time delay derived from the
phase shift, influencing the HOM pattern. The HOM interference is a distinct quantum phenomenon without
a classical analogue, thus serving as a valuable tool to assess the impact of relativistic effects on quantum
systems. The detection of the peak or the valley in Fig.
4
(d) or the signal in Figs.
5
(c)(f)
could support that using the formulas in Sec.
3.2
to describe the combination of the general
relativity and the quantum theory is reasonable at the low-energy limit, with these formulas potentially
serving as a foundation for developing a more comprehensive theory. Conversely, the absence of a
signal in Fig.
4
(d) or in Figs.
5
(c)(f) could indicate that combining the general
relativity and the quantum theory in these manners is not feasible, implying the need for adjustments
in the theories. Regardless of the outcomes of the HOM interference experiments, the results will provide
fresh perspectives on combining the general relativity and the quantum theory.
