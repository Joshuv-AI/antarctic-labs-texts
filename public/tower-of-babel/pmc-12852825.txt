Evaluating the effectiveness of green roofs and walls in Nigeria’s urban centers: a mixed-methods approach
Authors: H C O Unegbu, D S Yawas, B Dan-asabe, A A Alabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effectiveness of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers, focusing on their environmental, economic, and social benefits. Utilizing a mixed-method research approach, the research integrated quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews to provide a comprehensive analysis. The quantitative strand involved a survey of 1000 residents from Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt, while the qualitative strand included in-depth interviews with 30 urban planners, architects, and environmental scientists. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, and multiple regression analyses for the quantitative data, and thematic analysis for the qualitative data. The findings reveal significant environmental benefits, including reduced urban heat island effects and improved air quality. Economically, green roofs and walls contribute to energy savings and increased property values, although high installation costs and maintenance challenges were noted as barriers. Socially, these infrastructures enhance mental well-being and community engagement, though the degree of support varied. Despite these barriers, the positive perceptions and reported benefits suggest a strong potential for green roofs and walls to contribute to sustainable urban development in Nigeria. Comparative analysis with global case studies revealed that the benefits observed in Nigerian cities are consistent with international findings. The study underscores the need for targeted policy interventions, financial incentives, and public awareness campaigns to overcome the identified barriers. Addressing these challenges can harness the full potential of green roofs and walls, contributing to more sustainable and livable urban environments in Nigerian cities.Keywords: Green roofs, Urban centers, Nigeria, Sustainable construction, Urban development, Social implicationsSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The increasing urbanization of Nigerian cities has resulted in significant environmental challenges, including the heat island effect, reduced air quality, and loss of green spaces. These issues are exacerbated by the rapid growth of urban populations and the corresponding expansion of built-up areas. Traditional urban infrastructure, characterized by extensive impervious surfaces such as concrete and asphalt, contributes to higher temperatures and increased energy consumption. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island effect, leads to elevated temperatures in urban areas compared to their rural surroundings, which can have adverse effects on public health and comfort1–3.

Green roofs and walls, often referred to as living roofs or vertical gardens, offer a promising solution to urban issues by integrating vegetation into building designs. These green infrastructures not only enhance the aesthetic appeal of urban environments but also provide substantial environmental, economic, and social benefits. By replacing traditional roofing materials with vegetated layers, green roofs can significantly reduce surface temperatures, thereby mitigating the urban heat island effect4–6. Furthermore, green walls, which involve the use of climbing plants or modular systems to cover building facades, contribute to improved air quality by filtering pollutants and increasing oxygen levels7–9. Green roofs and walls have been widely adopted in many developed countries as part of sustainable urban development strategies. They help mitigate the urban heat island effect, improve air quality, and contribute to biodiversity by providing habitats for various plant and animal species10–12. Additionally, they offer economic advantages by extending the lifespan of roofing materials, reducing stormwater runoff, and lowering energy consumption for heating and cooling13–15. Studies have shown that buildings with green roofs require less energy for air conditioning in the summer and heating in the winter, leading to significant cost savings over time16–18.

In addition to the urban heat island effect, the lack of green spaces in Nigerian cities contributes to poor air quality and reduced biodiversity. Vegetation plays a crucial role in filtering air pollutants, providing shade, and supporting urban wildlife. However, the expansion of concrete and asphalt surfaces at the expense of green areas has led to significant ecological imbalances19,20. These environmental issues are compounded by socio-economic factors, including inadequate urban planning and limited public awareness about sustainable practices21,22.

Despite the global recognition of green roofs and walls as effective solutions for urban environmental issues, their adoption in Nigeria remains limited. In many developed countries, green roofs and walls have been successfully implemented to mitigate urban heat, improve air quality, and enhance the aesthetic and ecological value of urban spaces1,9,10. However, in Nigeria, there is a noticeable gap in empirical data regarding the performance and benefits of these green infrastructures in the local context21,23. This lack of data hinders the development of effective policies and guidelines for their widespread adoption and implementation24,25.

Moreover, the socio-economic context of Nigeria presents unique challenges and opportunities for the adoption of green roofs and walls. Factors such as the cost of installation, maintenance requirements, and the perceived benefits among stakeholders need to be carefully considered26,27. The absence of localized studies makes it difficult to tailor green infrastructure solutions to the specific needs and conditions of Nigerian urban centers. This study seeks to address these gaps by evaluating the effectiveness of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers. By providing empirical data on their environmental, economic, and social impacts, the study aims to offer valuable insights for policymakers, urban planners, architects, and other stakeholders25,28. The findings are expected to inform the development of strategies and policies that promote the integration of green infrastructure into urban development projects, thereby enhancing the sustainability and livability of Nigerian cities.

Literature review

Overview of green roofs and walls

Green roofs and walls, also known as living roofs and vertical gardens, represent a critical aspect of green infrastructure, integrating vegetation directly into the architectural fabric of buildings. These systems are designed with multiple layers that facilitate drainage, provide a suitable growing medium, and support a diverse range of plant life. Green roofs are generally classified into three categories: extensive, semi-intensive, and intensive, based on the depth of the growing medium and the level of maintenance required. Extensive green roofs feature a shallow substrate layer, typically less than six inches deep, and are planted with drought-resistant vegetation requiring minimal maintenance. Semi-intensive green roofs have a deeper substrate, accommodating a more diverse plant palette and requiring moderate maintenance. Intensive green roofs, akin to rooftop gardens, have the deepest substrate layers, support a wide variety of plants, including shrubs and small trees, and necessitate regular maintenance and irrigation39–42.

Green walls, on the other hand, can be categorized into two primary types: green facades and living walls. Green facades involve the use of climbing plants that grow directly on the building's surface or are supported by a structure such as a trellis or cable system. These plants root in soil at the base of the building or in containers placed at various heights. Living walls, or vertical gardens, incorporate modular panels filled with a growing medium, into which plants are directly inserted. These systems are often equipped with built-in irrigation and fertilization mechanisms to support plant growth43–46. The concept of green roofs is not new and can be traced back to ancient civilizations, such as the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, which showcased an early example of integrating green space into built structures. However, the modern iteration of green roofs and walls has seen significant evolution, particularly over the last few decades, driven by advancements in materials science and horticultural practices. Innovations such as lightweight growing mediums, efficient drainage layers, and integrated irrigation systems have made it feasible to implement green roofs and walls on a wide range of building types, from residential homes to commercial skyscrapers.

Modern green roofs and walls offer a multitude of benefits that are well-documented in academic literature. Environmentally, they contribute to urban sustainability by reducing the urban heat island effect, improving air quality, enhancing urban biodiversity, and managing stormwater. Economically, green roofs and walls can lead to energy savings by improving building insulation, increasing property values, and extending the lifespan of roofing materials. Socially, these green infrastructures enhance the aesthetic appeal of urban environments, provide recreational spaces, and improve the overall well-being of city dwellers16,17,47–50. The integration of vegetation into building design through green roofs and walls represents a holistic approach to urban development, addressing multiple aspects of sustainability simultaneously. As cities around the world face increasing environmental and social challenges due to urbanization and climate change, the adoption of green infrastructure like green roofs and walls becomes ever more crucial. Their potential to transform urban landscapes into greener, more livable environments underscores the importance of continued research and development in this field, particularly in rapidly urbanizing regions such as Nigeria.

Environmental benefits of green infrastructure

Green roofs and walls are pivotal components of urban sustainability, offering a plethora of environmental benefits that address critical urban challenges. These benefits encompass the mitigation of the urban heat island (UHI) effect, enhancement of air quality, promotion of biodiversity, and effective stormwater management. The integration of vegetation into urban infrastructure through green roofs and walls is a multifaceted approach that significantly contributes to the resilience and livability of cities17,47,48.

One of the most pressing environmental issues in urban areas is the urban heat island effect, where temperatures in cities are significantly higher than in their rural counterparts. This phenomenon is largely due to the absorption and retention of heat by buildings, roads, and other infrastructure. Green roofs and walls combat the UHI effect through the processes of evapotranspiration and shading. Evapotranspiration involves the transfer of water from plants and soil to the atmosphere, which cools the surrounding air. Additionally, the vegetation provides shade, further reducing surface temperatures. Empirical studies indicate that green roofs can lower rooftop temperatures by up to 40 °C, which consequently decreases the indoor temperature, leading to substantial reductions in energy consumption for cooling purposes1,15,47.

Green roofs and walls play a significant role in improving urban air quality by acting as natural air filters. The vegetation on these green infrastructures captures airborne pollutants such as particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, and sulfur dioxide. Through the process of photosynthesis, plants absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen, thereby contributing to the reduction of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere7,14,48. Moreover, green roofs and walls can intercept heavy metals and other contaminants from rainwater, enhancing the quality of runoff and reducing pollution in urban water bodies10,11,13.

Urban environments often suffer from a lack of biodiversity due to the dominance of concrete and asphalt surfaces. Green roofs and walls address this issue by creating habitats for a variety of plant and animal species. These green infrastructures provide nesting sites for birds, habitats for insects, and corridors for wildlife movement, thus promoting ecological networks within urban settings8,12,41. The inclusion of diverse plant species on green roofs and walls can significantly enhance urban biodiversity, fostering species interactions and ecological balance.

Effective stormwater management is crucial for urban areas to prevent flooding and manage water resources. Green roofs and walls contribute to this by reducing the volume and peak flow rates of stormwater runoff. The vegetation and growing medium on green roofs absorb and retain rainwater, which is then gradually released through evapotranspiration and drainage systems. This process not only alleviates the pressure on urban drainage systems but also reduces the risk of flooding during heavy rainfall events43,44,48. Additionally, the retention of rainwater by green roofs helps in recharging groundwater levels and maintaining the hydrological cycle in urban areas13,14.

Economic impact of green roofs and walls

Green roofs and walls offer significant economic benefits that extend beyond their initial aesthetic and environmental appeal. These benefits include energy savings, increased property values, and the extended lifespan of roofing materials, making them a cost-effective investment for urban buildings.

One of the most notable economic advantages of green roofs and walls is their contribution to energy savings. The insulating properties of green roofs significantly lower heat transfer through the building envelope, reducing the need for artificial heating and cooling. This is particularly beneficial in regions with extreme temperatures. By acting as a thermal barrier, green roofs help maintain a stable indoor temperature, thereby decreasing reliance on HVAC systems. Recent studies have shown that buildings with green roofs can achieve substantial energy savings, with reductions of up to 30% for cooling and 10% for heating16,49–51. These savings translate into lower utility bills for building owners and occupants, providing a direct economic benefit. Moreover, during warmer months, the cooling effect of green roofs can alleviate the demand on air conditioning systems, potentially reducing the incidence of peak load electricity usage and associated costs52–54.

Green roofs and walls also enhance property values due to their aesthetic and environmental benefits. Properties equipped with green infrastructure are often perceived as more attractive and environmentally friendly, which increases their market value and appeal to potential buyers or tenants55–57. This increased desirability can lead to higher property values and potentially quicker sales or rentals. Additionally, green roofs can create additional usable space on a building, such as recreational areas, gardens, or community spaces, further enhancing the property's functionality and value58,59. These spaces can serve as communal areas for residents or tenants, adding a unique selling point that differentiates the property from others lacking such features.

Another significant economic benefit of green roofs is their ability to extend the lifespan of roofing materials. Green roofs protect underlying roofing materials from direct exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation and extreme temperature fluctuations. This protection reduces the wear and tear that typically shortens the lifespan of conventional roofing materials. The protective layer provided by green roofs minimizes thermal stress and mechanical damage, thereby extending the life expectancy of the roof60–62. This extension leads to reduced maintenance costs and less frequent need for roof replacements, resulting in long-term economic savings for building owners. By reducing the frequency of roof replacements, green roofs not only lower material and labor costs but also decrease the environmental impact associated with manufacturing and disposing of roofing materials63,64.

Social implications of green infrastructure

Green roofs and walls have profound social implications, significantly contributing to improvements in mental well-being, fostering community engagement, and enhancing the overall livability of urban spaces. Access to green spaces has been consistently linked to improved mental health and well-being. The presence of green roofs and walls in urban areas provides residents with vital opportunities for relaxation and recreation, which can reduce stress and enhance the overall quality of life. Natural environments can offer restorative experiences, promoting mental recovery from the stresses of urban living38,65,66. Exposure to greenery has been shown to lower levels of anxiety and depression, as well as improve cognitive functioning67–69. This psychological benefit is particularly significant in densely populated urban centers where traditional green spaces may be limited. The sight of green roofs and walls can provide a visual respite, creating a more calming and pleasant environment.

Green roofs and walls also play a crucial role in fostering community engagement. They provide shared spaces for gardening and social interactions, which can encourage collaboration and interaction among residents. Community gardens on rooftops, for example, not only beautify the urban landscape but also serve as communal spaces where residents can come together to grow plants, share gardening tips, and engage in social activities. This interaction promotes social cohesion and a sense of belonging70–72. Furthermore, involving local communities in the planning, installation, and maintenance of green infrastructure projects increases their sense of ownership and involvement. This participatory approach can enhance the community's connection to their environment and encourage a collective responsibility for the sustainability of urban spaces73–75.

The transformation of barren rooftops and walls into green, vibrant areas significantly enhances the livability of urban spaces. Green roofs and walls contribute to the aesthetic appeal of buildings and neighborhoods, making cities more attractive and enjoyable places to live76–78. The presence of green infrastructure can uplift the visual landscape, creating a more inviting and pleasant environment for residents and visitors alike. In addition to aesthetic benefits, green roofs and walls provide practical advantages by offering shading and cooling, which create more comfortable outdoor environments79–81. These green spaces can mitigate the harsh effects of urban heat, making outdoor areas more usable and enjoyable, especially during hot weather. This improvement in the urban microclimate not only enhances the physical comfort of residents but also supports outdoor recreational activities, thereby contributing to a more active and healthier urban lifestyle9,82,83.

Global versus Nigerian case

The implementation and benefits of green roofs and walls differ significantly between global and Nigerian contexts due to variations in economic conditions, climatic factors, urbanization rates, and governmental policies. While green infrastructure is widely recognized for its environmental, economic, and social benefits on a global scale, the specific challenges and opportunities in Nigeria present a unique case that warrants separate consideration. Globally, green roofs and walls have been embraced as effective strategies for enhancing urban sustainability and resilience. In many developed countries, the adoption of green infrastructure is driven by stringent environmental regulations, substantial government incentives, and a strong public awareness of environmental issues. For instance, cities like Singapore, Tokyo, and various European cities have implemented policies mandating or incentivizing the installation of green roofs and walls9,84,85. These policies have led to significant urban greening, resulting in improved air quality, reduced urban heat island effects, and increased biodiversity10,39.

In addition to environmental benefits, green roofs and walls are economically advantageous in global contexts. They contribute to substantial energy savings by reducing the need for heating and cooling, which is especially critical in regions with extreme temperatures16,66. Furthermore, green roofs enhance property values and can extend the lifespan of roofing materials by providing protection against UV radiation and temperature fluctuations40,86. These factors collectively make green infrastructure a cost-effective investment for urban buildings worldwide. The social implications of green infrastructure on a global scale are also noteworthy. Access to green spaces has been shown to improve mental health and well-being, foster community engagement, and enhance the livability of urban areas68,87. In densely populated cities, green roofs and walls provide much-needed recreational spaces and contribute to social cohesion by encouraging community participation in gardening and maintenance activities88.

In Nigeria, the adoption of green roofs and walls is still in its nascent stages, with limited implementation compared to global counterparts. Several factors contribute to this slower adoption rate, including economic constraints, lack of awareness, and insufficient government policies promoting green infrastructure. Despite these challenges, there is a growing recognition of the potential benefits that green roofs and walls can offer in the Nigerian context. Economically, green roofs and walls could play a significant role in addressing Nigeria's energy challenges. With the country's high temperatures and frequent power outages, the insulating properties of green roofs can help reduce the reliance on air conditioning and lower energy consumption89. This reduction in energy demand can lead to cost savings for building owners and contribute to a more sustainable energy future for Nigeria.

From a social perspective, green roofs and walls have the potential to transform urban areas in Nigeria by providing much-needed green spaces in densely populated cities. These spaces can enhance the quality of life for urban residents by offering areas for relaxation and recreation, thereby improving mental health and well-being22. Moreover, community gardens on rooftops can foster social cohesion and community engagement, which are particularly valuable in urban centers where traditional communal spaces are limited. However, the successful implementation of green roofs and walls in Nigeria requires overcoming several challenges. Economic constraints are a significant barrier, as the initial costs of installing green infrastructure can be high. Additionally, there is a need for greater awareness and education about the benefits of green roofs and walls among the public and policymakers90. Government incentives and supportive policies could play a crucial role in encouraging the adoption of green infrastructure in Nigeria91.

Research gaps

The existing literature extensively documents the environmental benefits of green roofs and walls, including their role in reducing the urban heat island effect, improving air quality, enhancing biodiversity, and managing stormwater. However, several research gaps remain. Firstly, while studies highlight general environmental benefits, there is a lack of empirical data on how these systems perform under the specific climatic and environmental conditions prevalent in different regions, particularly in developing countries like Nigeria. Research should focus on localized performance metrics to understand the effectiveness of green roofs and walls in various urban settings92–94. Secondly, many studies provide short to medium-term data on the environmental benefits of green roofs and walls. There is a need for longitudinal studies that track the long-term environmental impacts of these systems to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their sustainability over time17,95,96. Lastly, comparative studies across different regions and climatic conditions are limited. Research should aim to compare the performance of green roofs and walls in diverse environmental contexts to identify best practices and optimize design and maintenance strategies for different urban areas97–99.

Research on the economic impact of green roofs and walls has highlighted potential energy savings, increased property values, and extended lifespan of roofing materials. However, significant gaps exist in the current body of knowledge. Most economic analyses are based on data from developed countries. There is a need for cost–benefit analyses specific to developing countries, where economic conditions, market dynamics, and construction practices differ significantly40,62,100. Additionally, while studies have identified long-term economic benefits, there is limited research on how initial high installation costs can be mitigated, especially in low-income regions. Research should explore financing models, incentives, and policy interventions that can make green roofs and walls more economically feasible for widespread adoption101–103. Furthermore, the broader economic impact of green roofs and walls on local economies, including job creation and the development of green industries, is underexplored. Future research should investigate how the adoption of green infrastructure can stimulate economic growth and support local communities38,104.

The social implications of green roofs and walls are increasingly recognized, with studies highlighting improvements in mental well-being, community engagement, and urban livability. However, several gaps remain in the current research. Studies focus on the social benefits of green spaces in general but often lack consideration of cultural contexts. Research should examine how cultural differences influence the acceptance, use, and social impact of green roofs and walls in various regions, particularly in African cities105–107. Moreover, much of the existing literature is qualitative, providing descriptive accounts of social benefits. There is a need for quantitative studies that measure the specific social outcomes of green roofs and walls, such as improvements in mental health metrics, social cohesion indices, and quality of life indicators38,104,105. Additionally, research on the role of community participation in the planning, implementation, and maintenance of green roofs and walls is limited. Studies should explore how involving local communities can enhance the social benefits and sustainability of these projects10,106,108.

Case studies from regions like Singapore, Germany, and the United States provide valuable insights into the benefits and challenges of green roofs and walls. However, there is a dearth of localized studies in developing countries, including Nigeria. Existing case studies often focus on cities with well-established green infrastructure policies. There is a need for context-specific case studies in Nigerian urban centers to understand the unique challenges and opportunities in these environments38,103,104. Research should investigate how green roof and wall technologies can be adapted to local climatic, economic, and social conditions in Nigeria. This includes studying the performance of different plant species, materials, and design strategies suited to the Nigerian context48,96,101. Moreover, while there is recognition of the potential benefits, research on the policy and implementation barriers specific to Nigeria is limited. Studies should identify the regulatory, financial, and institutional obstacles that hinder the adoption of green roofs and walls and propose practical solutions to overcome these challenges40,100,103.

Addressing these research gaps is crucial for advancing the understanding and implementation of green roofs and walls, particularly in developing countries like Nigeria. By focusing on localized performance metrics, long-term impacts, economic feasibility, social implications, and context-specific case studies, future research can provide comprehensive insights that support the widespread adoption of green infrastructure and contribute to sustainable urban development.

Methodology

This study employed a mixed-method research (MMR) approach to evaluate the environmental, economic, and social impacts of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers. The MMR approach integrates both quantitative and qualitative research strands to offer a comprehensive understanding of the subject matter, as recommended by Beck et al.109. This integration is particularly beneficial in addressing complex research questions that require both breadth and depth of analysis.

The research design followed an explanatory sequential design, where the quantitative data collection and analysis preceded the qualitative phase. This design ensured that the findings from the initial quantitative phase informed and shaped the subsequent qualitative inquiry109,110. Specifically, the quantitative phase involved surveying a large sample of urban residents to gather broad, generalizable data on their perceptions and experiences with green roofs and walls. This phase utilized structured questionnaires to collect data on various aspects, including environmental benefits (e.g., reduction in urban heat), economic impacts (e.g., energy savings), and social implications (e.g., community engagement and mental well-being).

Following the quantitative phase, the qualitative phase was designed to delve deeper into the findings, exploring the nuances and context behind the statistical trends observed. In-depth interviews and focus group discussions were conducted with key informants such as urban planners, environmental scientists, and community leaders. These qualitative methods allowed for a richer, more detailed exploration of the issues, uncovering insights that quantitative data alone could not provide. For instance, while the survey data might reveal a general agreement on the benefits of green roofs, the qualitative interviews could provide detailed explanations of how these benefits are experienced differently across various socio-economic groups in Nigerian urban centers.

Quantitative strand

Population and sample

The study focused on three major urban centers in Nigeria: Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt. These cities were chosen for their diverse urban landscapes and significant environmental challenges, which make them ideal for studying the impacts of green roofs and walls. The population encompassed residents, property owners, and business operators within these cities. To ensure a representative sample, a stratified random sampling technique was employed, which is known to enhance the generalizability of findings across different demographic groups111. The total sample size consisted of 866 respondents, proportionally distributed among the three cities. This sample size adhered to the guidelines for robust statistical analysis, ensuring the reliability and validity of the results112.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected through a structured questionnaire (“Appendix”) specifically designed to evaluate the environmental, economic, and social impacts of green roofs and walls. The questionnaire included multiple-choice questions and Likert-scale items (ranging from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) to effectively capture the perceptions and experiences of the respondents113. The questionnaire was pre-tested to ensure its clarity and reliability, and necessary adjustments were made based on the feedback received114. This pre-testing phase was crucial to enhance the validity and reliability of the data collection instrument115.

Data analysis

The collected quantitative data were analyzed using advanced statistical techniques to derive both descriptive and inferential insights. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated to provide a summary of the data116. These statistics offered a clear overview of the respondents' characteristics and their perceptions regarding green roofs and walls.

Inferential statistical methods were employed to examine the relationships between variables and test the study’s hypotheses117. The specific inferential techniques used included t-tests, ANOVA, and multiple regression analyses. T-tests were utilized to compare the means of different groups to identify any significant differences118. This test was particularly useful for comparing the perceptions of different demographic groups within the sample. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) was employed to assess differences among multiple groups119. This technique helped determine whether there were significant differences in perceptions across various subgroups within the sample.

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of perceived benefits and assess their relative contributions120. This method allowed for the examination of the influence of various independent variables on the dependent variables, providing a comprehensive understanding of the factors that drive perceptions of green roofs and walls. By employing these inferential statistical methods, the study was able to rigorously test its hypotheses and gain deeper insights into the relationships between key variables.

Qualitative strand

Data collection

Qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, facilitating a comprehensive exploration of participants' perspectives. Purposive sampling was employed to select key informants, including urban planners, environmental scientists, and community leaders, ensuring that the qualitative data provided rich, contextual insights121. This approach allowed for the selection of participants with specific knowledge and experience relevant to the study's focus on green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers. A total of 30 in-depth interviews and 6 focus group discussions, each comprising 8–10 participants, were conducted. This sample size adhered to the guidelines for achieving data saturation and ensuring robust qualitative research outcomes122.

The in-depth interviews were designed to elicit detailed information about participants' experiences, perceptions, and attitudes towards green roofs and walls. Interview questions were semi-structured, allowing for flexibility and the opportunity to probe deeper into specific areas of interest123. Focus group discussions, on the other hand, provided a dynamic environment where participants could interact, discuss, and challenge each other's views, thereby enriching the data collected124.

Data analysis

The qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis, a rigorous method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) within the data125. Thematic analysis is particularly suited for qualitative research as it allows for a detailed examination of complex data and the identification of themes that capture the essence of participants' experiences.

The process of data analysis began with the verbatim transcription of the interviews and focus group discussions. This ensured that the data were accurately captured and preserved in their original form. NVivo software was then utilized for coding and theme identification, providing a systematic and efficient way to manage and analyze large volumes of qualitative data126. The use of NVivo facilitated the organization of data into meaningful categories and the identification of recurring patterns and themes.

The thematic analysis followed a structured process that included familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report127. This iterative process ensured that the analysis was thorough and comprehensive, capturing the nuances and complexities of the data. To enhance the validity of the results, the identified themes were cross-checked with the quantitative findings from the study. This triangulation process helped to ensure consistency and comprehensiveness, providing a more holistic understanding of the impacts of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers128. By integrating the qualitative and quantitative data, the study was able to validate its findings and offer deeper insights into the environmental, economic, and social implications of green infrastructure.

Meta-inference

The integration of quantitative and qualitative data occurred at the interpretation stage, where findings from both strands were compared and synthesized to draw comprehensive conclusions. This process, known as meta-inference, was crucial for validating the results and providing deeper insights into the impacts of green roofs and walls129. By combining the strengths of both quantitative and qualitative approaches, the study achieved a more holistic understanding of the subject matter, effectively addressing the complexity of the research questions130.

At the meta-inference stage, the quantitative and qualitative findings were systematically compared and synthesized. This integrative process involved several key steps to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the research outcomes. First, triangulation was employed to cross-validate findings from both strands, identifying convergent, divergent, and complementary results. This step was crucial in ensuring the credibility and reliability of the research outcomes128. By leveraging the complementary strengths of both quantitative and qualitative data, the study achieved complementarity. Quantitative data provided generalizable insights, while qualitative data offered context-specific details and depth, enriching the overall understanding of the research problem131.

The integration process also allowed for the expansion of the study's scope by addressing new research questions and dimensions that emerged during the qualitative phase. This iterative approach not only enriched the overall analysis but also enhanced the study's explanatory power, allowing for a more nuanced and thorough exploration of the research questions132. Lastly, interpretive consistency was maintained by ensuring that the final interpretations and conclusions were consistent with the data from both strands. This consistency was achieved through regular team discussions, iterative refinement of themes, and careful cross-referencing of quantitative and qualitative findings. This meticulous approach ensured that the study's conclusions were robust and reflective of the integrated data133.

The meta-inference process ultimately led to a set of comprehensive conclusions that captured the multifaceted impacts of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers. By integrating data from both strands, the study provided a holistic and nuanced understanding of the environmental, economic, and social benefits and challenges associated with green infrastructure. This integrative approach addressed the complexity of the research questions and contributed to a more robust and credible body of knowledge110,129.

T-test and ANOVA results

A t-test was conducted to compare the perceived reduction in heat between respondents with and without green infrastructure. The results indicated a significant difference (t(864) = 5.23, p < 0.001), with respondents with green infrastructure reporting higher reductions in heat. The results showed significant differences (F(2, 863) = 3.67, p = 0.026), with Abuja reporting the highest perceived improvement in air quality.

The extended lifespan of roofing materials was another economic benefit, with maintenance costs reduced by 15% on average due to the protective effects of vegetation (Table 3).

Qualitative strand

Environmental benefits

Qualitative data from interviews and focus groups reinforced the quantitative findings, providing a deeper understanding of the environmental benefits of green roofs and walls. Respondents consistently emphasized the noticeable reduction in urban heat, attributing it to the insulating properties of green roofs which mitigate the urban heat island effect. Many participants noted a tangible improvement in air quality, attributing this to the increased vegetation which enhances air filtration and reduces particulate matter. These environmental benefits were frequently highlighted as key reasons for supporting green infrastructure, corroborating findings from global studies that demonstrate similar impacts in various urban settings134,138. Moreover, participants mentioned the aesthetic enhancement and cooling effect provided by green roofs, which contribute to a more pleasant urban environment and potentially mitigate the effects of climate change13,36.

Economic impact

Interviewees and focus group participants acknowledged significant economic benefits associated with green roofs and walls, particularly in terms of energy savings and increased property values. Many respondents reported reduced energy costs due to the improved insulation properties of green roofs, which help maintain indoor temperatures and reduce the need for heating and cooling. Increased property values were also noted, as properties with green roofs are perceived as more desirable due to their environmental benefits and aesthetic appeal. However, participants expressed concerns about the high initial installation costs and ongoing maintenance challenges. These financial barriers were seen as significant obstacles to widespread adoption, especially in regions with limited economic resources. Such concerns echo the economic challenges outlined in previous studies, highlighting the need for financial incentives and supportive policies to promote green infrastructure135,136,139. Additionally, respondents suggested that long-term economic benefits, such as increased longevity of roofing materials and potential tax incentives, could offset the initial costs if adequately supported by government policies60,84.

Social implications

Participants in the qualitative strand extensively discussed the social benefits of green roofs and walls, particularly their positive impact on mental well-being and community engagement. Many respondents noted that green spaces provided a sense of tranquility and reduced stress, contributing to improved mental health. This aligns with studies indicating that exposure to green environments can enhance psychological well-being and reduce the prevalence of mental health issues137,140. Additionally, green roofs and walls were seen as catalysts for social interactions, fostering a sense of community and belonging among residents. Participants highlighted that community gardens and rooftop green spaces served as gathering spots for social activities, thus enhancing social cohesion. These findings are consistent with research indicating that green infrastructure can improve social capital and foster stronger community ties105,141. Furthermore, respondents emphasized the educational value of green roofs, noting that they provide opportunities for environmental education and awareness, particularly in urban areas where natural green spaces are limited.

Meta-inference

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings offered a comprehensive understanding of the impacts of green roofs and walls, providing a nuanced perspective that encompasses environmental, economic, and social dimensions.

Environmental benefits

The quantitative data revealed a strong consensus among respondents regarding the environmental benefits of green roofs and walls. Specifically, there were significant reductions in urban heat and notable improvements in air quality. These benefits were reflected in the high percentage of respondents who agreed or strongly agreed with these statements. Statistical analyses confirmed these perceptions, with t-tests showing significant differences in perceived environmental benefits between different cities (p < 0.05).

Qualitative data reinforced these findings, with interviewees and focus group participants frequently emphasizing the cooling effects of green roofs and the enhancement of air quality. Respondents detailed how the vegetation on green roofs reduced the heat absorption of buildings, leading to cooler indoor and outdoor environments. This aligns with the work of Oberndorfer et al.134 and Speak et al.138, who documented similar environmental benefits in different urban contexts. Participants also noted the aesthetic improvement and the positive psychological impact of greener urban landscapes.

Economic impact

Both quantitative and qualitative data underscored the significant economic benefits of green roofs and walls. The quantitative results indicated substantial energy savings and increases in property values. For instance, a significant portion of respondents agreed that green roofs contributed to lower energy costs by providing natural insulation, thus reducing the need for air conditioning (p < 0.05).

The qualitative strand provided deeper insights into these economic benefits and highlighted challenges such as high initial installation costs and ongoing maintenance requirements. Interviewees expressed concerns about the financial feasibility of green roofs, particularly in the context of the Nigerian economy. They pointed out that while the long-term savings on energy and increased property values were attractive, the initial costs were prohibitive for many property owners and developers. This echoes the findings of Castleton et al.135 and Jaffal et al.139, who noted similar economic barriers in different settings. Moreover, participants suggested that financial incentives and government subsidies could be essential for broader adoption.

Social implications

The social benefits of green roofs and walls were strongly supported by both quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitatively, respondents reported significant improvements in mental well-being and community engagement. These social benefits were statistically significant and were identified as key outcomes of green infrastructure.

Qualitative data added depth to these findings, illustrating how green roofs and walls fostered social interaction and community cohesion. Participants described green roofs as communal spaces that facilitated social activities and provided areas for relaxation and leisure, which contributed to mental health improvements. These findings are consistent with global studies by Hartig et al.137 and Armstrong141, which highlight the positive social impacts of green spaces. Interviewees also mentioned the educational potential of green roofs, where children and adults could learn about sustainability and horticulture, further enhancing community engagement.

Comparative analysis with global case studies

The comparative analysis with global case studies revealed that the benefits observed in Nigerian urban centers are largely in line with international findings. For instance, studies in Europe and North America, such as those by Oberndorfer et al.134 and Speak et al.138, reported similar environmental benefits. However, unlike studies in temperate regions where the economic benefits were sometimes negligible, this study demonstrated substantial economic advantages in the Nigerian context. This discrepancy may be due to the higher baseline energy costs and greater demand for cooling in tropical climates, which amplify the cost savings associated with green roofs and walls. Additionally, cultural differences and urban planning policies may also influence the perceived benefits and adoption rates.

Discussion of findings in relation to existing literature

The findings from this study corroborate existing literature on the multifaceted benefits of green roofs and walls, while also providing a nuanced understanding of the specific challenges in the Nigerian context. The environmental benefits, such as reduced urban heat island effects and improved air quality, are well-documented in studies by Oberndorfer et al.134 and Speak et al.138. These studies have consistently shown that green roofs can significantly lower ambient temperatures in urban areas, mitigate the heat island effect, and improve air quality by filtering pollutants and capturing particulate matter. In line with Oberndorfer et al.134 and Speak et al.138, this study found substantial reductions in heat and improvements in air quality in Nigerian urban centers. This is particularly significant given the tropical climate of Nigeria, where high temperatures and poor air quality are persistent issues. The vegetation on green roofs helps absorb solar radiation and cool the air through evapotranspiration, leading to a cooler microclimate. Furthermore, the plants and growing medium act as a natural filter, trapping dust and pollutants, thus enhancing air quality. These findings reinforce the global consensus on the environmental advantages of green roofs and walls.

The economic impacts observed in this study, including energy savings and increased property values, align with the analyses by Castleton et al.135 and Jaffal et al.139. In the Nigerian context, the energy savings are particularly pronounced due to the high baseline energy costs and the significant demand for cooling. Green roofs provide excellent insulation, reducing the need for air conditioning and thus lowering energy consumption and costs. This economic benefit is critical in Nigeria, where energy efficiency is a major concern due to frequent power outages and high electricity tariffs. The increase in property values associated with green roofs and walls can be attributed to their aesthetic appeal and the enhanced livability they offer. Properties with green roofs are often perceived as more desirable due to the additional green space, which can be used for recreation or urban agriculture, further contributing to the economic value.

The social implications, such as improved mental well-being and community engagement, are consistent with the work of Hartig et al.137 and Armstrong141. Green roofs and walls provide urban residents with access to green spaces, which are known to reduce stress, improve mental health, and foster social interactions. In densely populated Nigerian cities, where access to parks and gardens is limited, green roofs offer a vital respite from the urban environment. The community engagement aspect is also significant, as green roofs can serve as communal spaces for social gatherings, educational activities, and community gardening projects. However, this study also highlights significant challenges that need to be addressed to promote the adoption of green roofs and walls in Nigeria. High initial installation costs and technical maintenance challenges were identified as major barriers. These findings echo the economic obstacles outlined by Oladokun et al.136, who noted that the upfront costs of green infrastructure can be prohibitive, especially in developing countries. The technical maintenance of green roofs, which requires specialized knowledge and skills, further complicates their adoption.

Despite these barriers, the positive perceptions and reported benefits suggest a strong potential for green roofs and walls to contribute to sustainable urban development in Nigerian cities. Addressing these challenges will require targeted strategies, including financial incentives, subsidies, and technical support. Governments and policymakers should consider offering tax breaks, grants, or low-interest loans to offset the initial costs and encourage investment in green infrastructure. Additionally, training programs and capacity-building initiatives can help develop the necessary skills for maintaining green roofs, ensuring their long-term sustainability.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the environmental, economic, and social impacts of green roofs and walls in Nigerian urban centers. The findings underscore the significant benefits of green infrastructure in mitigating urban heat island effects, improving air quality, reducing energy costs, increasing property values, and enhancing mental well-being and community engagement. These benefits align with global evidence and reinforce the potential of green roofs and walls as pivotal elements in sustainable urban development. However, the study also highlights several critical challenges that impede the widespread adoption of green roofs and walls in Nigeria. High initial installation costs, technical maintenance difficulties, and economic instability present substantial barriers. These challenges necessitate targeted interventions, such as financial incentives, subsidies, and technical support programs, to alleviate the economic burden on building owners and facilitate the adoption of green infrastructure.

The positive perceptions and reported benefits from respondents indicate a strong potential for green roofs and walls to transform urban environments in Nigeria. By addressing the identified barriers through strategic policy measures and enhancing public awareness, it is possible to create a supportive framework that encourages the implementation of green roofs and walls. This, in turn, can contribute significantly to urban sustainability, resilience, and livability. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to track the long-term impacts of green roofs and walls and explore innovative financing models to overcome economic obstacles. Additionally, examining the cultural and contextual factors that influence the adoption of green infrastructure in diverse urban settings can provide deeper insights and inform more effective policy interventions. In conclusion, while challenges exist, the adoption of green roofs and walls in Nigeria holds immense promise for sustainable urban development. With concerted efforts from policymakers, urban planners, and the community, green infrastructure can play a transformative role in enhancing the environmental, economic, and social fabric of Nigerian cities.
