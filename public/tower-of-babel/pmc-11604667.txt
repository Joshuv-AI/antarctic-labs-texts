Dual intervention of Boeravinone B and Chebulinic Acid mitigates BHT-Induced toxicity in HepG2 cells: modulating apoptosis and autophagy
Authors: Samidha S. Kakade, Harshad K. Bote, Pankaj K. Pawar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Butylated Hydroxytoluene (BHT) is found to exert cellular toxicity through induction of oxidative stress although being used as antioxidant in many food products. This study investigates the protective effects of two herbal compounds Boeravinone B (BB) and Chebulinic acid (CA) in combination (B4C3 i.e. BB 4 µg/mL and CA 3 µg/mL). Key findings revealed that BHT exerted toxicity through induction of ROS (234.47 a.u.) and RNS (0.042 µM/mL), but B4C3 has significantly reduced it (115.46 a.u. and 0.018 µM/mL respecctively). BHT exposure raised the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD (70.9%), CAT (7.08 units/mL), GPX (1.21 units/mL), levels of protein carbonyls (3.52 units/mg) and lipid peroxides (418.34%). Whereas treatment with B4C3 decreased the levels of SOD (29.92%), CAT (3.12 units/mL), GPX (0.36 units/mL), protein carbonyls (0.91 units/mg of protein) and lipid peroxides (106.67%) during BHT exposure. It was found that 20.56% cells were apoptotic while 73.83% were autophagic during BHT treatment. However, proposed phytotherapy rescued the cells from apoptotic and autophagic death and supported cell growth which was confirmed by RT-PCR and growth analysis. Collectively, B4C3 offered a significant protection against BHT-induced cellular damage, suggesting its potential as therapeutic agents for oxidative stress-related hepatotoxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81203-6.

Introduction

The consumption of fast foods in India has become a significant public health concern. Despite the popularity of fast food due to its convenience; affordability and taste, the lack of specific laws and regulations governing the production; sale and marketing of fast foods in India has contributed to various health issues1. The global fast-food consumption is on a significant upward trend, with the market size reaching approximately USD 797.14 billion in 2023. It is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 4.9% from 2024 to 2032, potentially reaching around USD 1,226.05 billion by 20322. Studies have shown that ultra-processed food industries in India have been growing rapidly, with a compound annual growth rate of 13.37%, leading to increased consumption of unhealthy foods. It is crucial to limit fast food consumption and opt for a balanced diet rich in nutrients to maintain overall well-being and reduce the risk of chronic diseases3. Fast foods often contain Chemical Food Additives (CFAs) that serve various purposes such as preserving flavor, enhancing taste, improving appearance, and extending shelf life. Uncontrolled inclusion of CFAs in fast foods warrants the timely awareness about the ingredients present in these convenient but potentially harmful food choices. The harmful impacts of CFAs can manifest in various ways such as diarrhoea, insomnia, asthma, rhinitis, hives etc. affecting human health negatively4.

Harmful impacts associated with the excessive utilization of CFAs underscore the importance of being cautious about the consumption of foods containing chemical additives and the need for regulatory oversight to ensure the safety of these additives in the food supply3. Oxidation of food(s) is one of the many reasons responsible for its / their deterioration which needs to be avoided or postponed. Use of antioxidants, which work by absorbing and neutralizing free radicals is therefore a preferred choice of food manufacturers to avoid or prolong the oxidation ultimately increasing the storage life of the respective food product5. Natural antioxidants fall short to fulfil the needs of the food sector due to their poor stability and extraction challenges. In contrast to their natural counterparts, synthetic antioxidants find extensive application. Their attributes include a wide range of raw material sources, advanced technology, affordability, strong scavenging activity, and simplicity of procurement6.

Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) is one of the most often utilized antioxidants in foods containing fats and is acknowledged as safe for use in foods7. It has been used as a food additive since the 1940s and has been linked with having a positive effect such as maintaining appealing food odor, color, and flavor. It is insoluble in water but soluble in fats and oils, making it effective in preventing fats in foods from going rancid5. The acceptable daily intake (ADI) of BHT by the World Health Organization (WHO)/Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) is 0–0.3 mg/kg bw/day7. Recent investigations have explored the therapeutic potential of BHT for specific medical conditions such as cold sores, genital herpes and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome suggesting its possible use as a treatment option. Furthermore, BHT is presently permitted in dog food products at concentrations not exceeding 0.015%8.

While BHT is commonly used as a preservative in cosmetics and foods, there are growing health concerns regarding its safety with recommendations to avoid products containing BHT to minimize exposure to its potential harmful effects on various organs and systems in the body7. Researchers have highlighted the toxicity of BHT, indicating that it can cause lung irritation leading to lung injury and promote the development of lung tumors in mice9. BHT and its metabolites may damage nuclear DNA and impair mitochondrial respiration, which could start the carcinogenicity process6. Another study reported that exposing larval zebrafish to BHT at concentrations between 0.1 and 10 µM caused heart rate to drop and anxiety-related behaviours, attributed to dopaminergic signalling disruption and reduced oxidative phosphorylation10. Furthermore, embryonic and larval zebrafish exposed to 0.2 µM BHT developed an endocrine abnormality leading to behavioural changes and impair mitochondrial functions11. Excessive amounts of BHT were observed to be responsible for liver haemorrhage through elevated serum transaminase activity and centrilobular necrosis. Additionally, it has also been shown to induce hepatocarcinogenesis by increasing the hepatocellular mitotic activity in rats and cats12. Studies have shown that BHT exposure can lead to histopathological changes in the liver of mice, rats and chickens which has been associated with increased liver weight and elevated levels of liver enzymes such as epoxide hydrolase and glutathione-S-transferase9.

These reports emphasize the need for more research to fully comprehend the mechanisms and implications of BHT exposure on human health, which will further help in development of novel therapeutic approaches to counteract BHT-induced toxicity. A recent study revealed that Angelica sinensispolysaccharides reversed 5-fluorouracil induced B-lymphocytopenia by enhancing Nrf2 signaling, encouraging IL-7 production in mesenchymal stem and progenitor cells and sustaining the survival, proliferation as well as differentiation of B cell progenitors and may offer a feasible treatment approach13. Yang et al. (2024) reported that IL-11 substantially decreased ROS generation, enhanced cellular antioxidant defences and ultimately inhibited apoptosis induced by H2O2in PC12 cells14. Veronica persicaexhibited hepatoprotective effects on acute liver injury induced by acetaminophen, mainly by inhibiting the phosphorylation of ERK-JNK and NF-κB/STAT3 cascade and by activating AMPK to reduce inflammation and oxidative stress15. In our previous study, Boeravinone B (BB) and Chebulinic acid (CA) have been individually reported for their defensive roles against aspartame-induced toxicity in HepG2 cells16.

Herbal intervention for promoting health and managing chronic conditions seems to be an attractive approach due to their gentle effects on body, greater accessibility, potential to promote self-healing of body and focus on holistic health, addressing the root causes of illnesses17. Boeravinone B (BB) is a rotenoid isolated from the Boerhaavia diffusa (Nyctaginaceae) known for its antioxidant and antiapoptotic effects in Saccharomyces cerevisiae18. It has also exhibited various pharmacological effects for the treatment of acute myoskeletal dysfunctions, spondylosis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, psoriasis and atherosclerosis in rodents19. It has been found to alleviate gut dysbiosis during myocardial infarction-induced cardiotoxicity in rats, which is associated with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. In same study, it has also shown cytoprotective effects, evidenced by its ability to reduce the levels of heart injury markers such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), troponin (TnT), creatine kinase (CK) and creatine kinase isoenzymes MB (CK-MB) in isoproterenol induced MI rats20.

On the other hand, Chebulinic acid (CA), an ellagitannin isolated from the ripened fruits of Terminalia chebula (Combretaceae) has been found to have potential to decrease the levels of lipid peroxidation, which is a marker of oxidative stress and also shown to inhibit H2O2induced ROS generation in the THP-1 human monocytic cell line21. It has been found to exert rejuvenation potential in stressed Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells by preventing apoptotic and necrotic cell death18. According to Wang et al. (2018), CA has been shown to have antitumour effects might be useful in the treatment of human colorectal cancer. Briefly, it has inhibited the proliferation and migration of cancer cells, promoted apoptosis and downregulated the expression of tumour-associated proteins22.

HepG2 cells have traits in common with both tumor cells and normal hepatocytes, despite coming from a liver tumor. These have been regularly used for investigating the efficacy of different drugs due to the presence of drug metabolizing enzymes illustrating numerous hepatic functions with high proliferation rate and epithelial like morphology which makes them an excellent model to investigate the functions of the liver23. Considering aforementioned facts regarding toxicity of BHT, its role in development of various diseases and disorders is clear. As diseases and disorders have closely linked with induction of oxidative stress, in our opinion, root cause behind detrimental consequences of BHT lies within its ability to induce tremendous oxidative stress. Therefore, in present investigation, we evaluated role of plant-based compounds in mitigating oxidative stress and related cellular damage. Present study was designed to investigate whether a combination of two phytomolecules BB and CA could ameliorate the deleterious impacts of BHT by managing oxidative stress, reducing cellular damage & death and boosting cell proliferation using HepG2 cells as model. The purpose of this study is to offer important insights into the possible advantages of using herbal interventions to ensure human health not only during diseased conditions but also in day-to-day life.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All the reagents required for cell culture including RIPA buffer were purchased from HiMedia laboratory, India. 2′7′-Dichlorofluorescin (DCFDA), Greiss reagent, Rhodamine 123, BHT, Monodansylcadaverine, c-DNA synthesis kit along with assay kits for Protein carbonylation, Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Glutathione peroxidase (GPX), Catalase (CAT) were procured from Sigma Aldrich, India. Annexin-V-FLUOS staining kit purchased from Roche, Germany, GeNei™, India supplied RNA isolation and SYBR Green PCR kits while Caspase-Glo® 3/7 assay kit was purchased from Promega, India. Primer pairs necessary for mRNA expression studies were procured from Eurofins Genomics Pvt. Ltd., India. Rest of the chemicals and reagents utilized during experimentation were of analytical grade and highest purity.

Culture maintenance

We obtained HepG2 (Human hepatocellular carcinoma) cell line from NCCS (National Centre for Cell Sciences), Pune. Cells were grown in MEM having 2 mM L-glutamine, 1500 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, Earle’s balanced salt solution in addition with 10% FBS as well as antibiotics solution (Penicillin-Streptomycin) 1% and incubated at 370 C in a humid CO2 incubator by maintaining 5% CO2 in the air. Cells were subcultured at regular intervals when they achieved 80–90% confluency and seeded accordingly.

Determination of combination of BB and CA effective in reduction of ROS in BHT exposed HepG2 cells

In this study, we have taken predetermined concentration of BHT (4 µg/mL) from our previous report which has induced a considerable ROS accumulation in HepG2 cells24. To investigate a combinatorial protective effect of BB and CA on BHT exposed HepG2 cells, we have prepared and screened 25 possible combinations using 1 to 5 µg/mL concentrations of BB and CA (Table S1). All the treated cells were kept for incubation in CO2 incubator for 24 h. Following removal of the growth medium, the cells were washed with PBS twice before being incubated in a CO2 incubator for an additional half an hour with a DCFDA solution (10µM). Upon incubation, excess dye was withdrawn and cells were washed thrice with PBS. The accumulation of ROS in harvested cells was quantified using spectrofluoremeter (Fig.S2).

Experimental setup

For present investigation, we have designed 4 sets of experimentation in triplicate as (i) Control having untreated HepG2 cells; (ii) HepG2 cells exposed to predetermined concentration of BHT (4 µg/mL); (iii) HepG2 cells exposed to BHT in supplementation of effective combination of BB and CA (B4C3) and (iv) HepG2 cells exposed to BHT supplemented with vitamin C (vit. C) as a positive Control. All the sets were incubated at 370 C in CO2 incubator for 24 h.

Quantification of extracellular nitrites (RNS)

Cells were harvested from T25 flask and seeded in a 24-well plate at a seeding density of 5 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere by keeping in CO2 incubator overnight. Treatments were given as mentioned above and all the experimental sets were kept in CO2 incubator for 24 h. Following incubation, cell-free supernatants (culture media) from all experimental sets were taken out separately, mixed with Griess reagent at the proportion of 1:1 and allowed to incubate for 10 min in the dark condition. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Preparation of cell homogenate

Cells were seeded at a seeding density of 3 × 105cells per well in 6-well culture plates, left overnight and then treated accordingly for an additional 24 h. Upon incubation, cells were pulled out from each experimental set and homogenized independently in RIPA buffer followed by centrifugation. The resulting supernatant was used further for the measurement various cellular biochemical parameters. Protein content of each sample was determined using Lowry technique25.

Evaluation of oxidative stress parameters

In order to conduct this investigation, we have measured the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT and GPX), levels of protein carbonylation and lipid peroxidation using homogenates prepared from cells of each experimental set as mentioned above. All the biochemical assays except lipid peroxidation were performed according to the protocol mentioned in product information bulletin supplied with respective assay kits with few modifications. For determination of lipid peroxidation, we have estimated the levels of TBARS (thiobarbituric acid reactive substances) in each experimental set according to a previously described protocol26.

Assessment of Caspase 3/7 enzyme activity

The cell homogenate was obtained as mentioned above and utilized as the source of Caspase 3/7 enzyme. The assay was carried out as specified in the product information bulletin provided with Caspase-Glo© 3/7 assay kit with minor modifications. Luminescence was measured using a fluorescence/luminescence spectrophotometer 8300 (Agilent Technology, USA).

Validation of cell death parameters using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry

Fluorescence microscopic examination was carried out for evaluating cell death parameters such as DNA damage, mitochondrial membrane potential dissipation, apoptosis and necrosis using fluorescent dyes DAPI, Rhodamine 123, Annexin V and Propidium iodide respectively. Upon incubation, cells from all experimental sets were treated with specific fluorescent dyes at corresponding time periods followed by couple of PBS washes. Then fluorescence labelled cells were analysed on fluorescence microscope using appropriate filters and images were captured. This study was outsourced as hiring services from Infinite Biotech Institute of Research and Analytics, Sangli, MS, India.

Further, the effects of BHT on HepG2 cells and subsequent defensive role of B4C3 phytotherapy were validated quantitatively using flow cytometer through examining mitochondrial membrane disintegration, apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy using same dyes mentioned above. Here, Monodancylcadaverine was used additionally for analysing autophagic cell death. For this, cells were extracted following incubation from each experimental set and washed twice with PBS. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and treated with different fluorescent dyes independently for appropriate time intervals and analysed on CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, India).

mRNA isolation, cDNA preparation, and quantitative real-time PCR

The mRNA expression patterns of executioner genes of apoptotic and autophagic cell death pathways were examined using RT-PCR. Following a 24 h incubation, mRNA was extracted from cells of each experimental set independently employing the GeneiPure™ total RNA isolation kit. mRNA isolated was quantified using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, USA) and 1 µg mRNA sample from each experimental set was utilized further to prepare cDNA using the ReadyScript™ cDNA synthesis mix kit. A reaction mixture with a total volume of 25µL was prepared using the corresponding cDNA template and appropriate primer pairs and RT-PCR analysis was performed using GAPDH as internal control. Examining growth pattern and cell proliferation of HepG2 cells as a function of BHT exposure and treatment with B4C3

For this analysis, cells were seeded in 24 well plates at specific and uniform seeding density in different 6 groups in such a way that each group consisted all four experimental sets. Treatments were given as mentioned previously to all 6 groups simultaneously and kept for incubation in CO2 incubator. After 24 h incubation, cells from only one group were trypsinized and counted independently using trypan blue dye on haemocytometer and results were recorded. A day after, cells from another group were harvested and counted. This process was continued till the last plate i.e. till 6th day.

Additionally, 1 × 104 cells were seeded in 96 well culture plate and kept in CO2 incubator for overnight. The day after treatments were given as mentioned above for 24 h. Following incubation, media was replaced with 5 mg/mL MTT solution and plate was again kept in CO2 incubator for additional 4 hrs maintaining dark condition. After this, MTT solution was removed and 100 µL DMSO was added in each well and mixed well to develop a purple colour followed by absorbance measurement at 570 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments carried out in this investigation were performed in triplicate as well as parallelly and the data were expressed as mean ± SD. The presented data was interpreted statistically with the help of Microsoft Excel and analysed using the method of two-way ANOVA followed by unpaired t test. A P value *P < 0.01, **P < 0.001, ***P< 0.0001 was regarded as statistically significant in comparison with control set. The coefficient of variance was measured by a previously reported method for all the experiments27.

Results and discussion

The safety assessment of chemical food additives is a critical component of food safety and public health. It involves a thorough review of all available scientific data and a risk assessment approach to determine the safety of food additives. This assessment is particularly important for vulnerable populations and is an ongoing process to ensure the continued safety of food additives28. Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) values represent the maximum amount of a food additives that can be safely consumed daily over a lifetime without risk. ADI is not a limit on the amount of a food additives that can be present in food products, but rather a guideline for safe consumption levels29. In European Union, BHT is authorized for use in frying oils, fish oils, beef, poultry, sheep fats etc. and permitted in concentrations up to 100 mg/kg fat. In India, the alignment with international standards and practices has likely contributed to the growing utilization of BHT as a food additive30. According to previous reports BHT is observed to be responsible for emergence of various diseases and disorders in different biological systems. Based on these facts, we are of the opinion that understanding the mechanisms underlying the BHT induced toxicity and finding ways to mitigate it is a pressing concern.

Determination of effective combination of Boeravinone B and chebulinic acid in BHT exposed HepG2 cells

In this investigation, we have determined an effective combination of BB and CA which could significantly lower ROS accumulation in BHT exposed HepG2 cells. It was revealed that exposure with BHT at 4 µg/mL has tremendously raised ROS accumulation (234.47 ± 10.1 a.u.) in HepG2 cells (Fig. S1). We observed that combination of BB at 4 µg/mL and CA at 3 µg/mL (B4C3) was the most efficient combination in scavenging the cellular ROS (115.46 ± 9.4 a.u.) and was superior than standard antioxidant vit. C (124.50 ± 10.2 a.u.) (Fig. 1a). Here, 5 µg/mL concentration of vit. C was used as positive control based on previous reports31. We have used all these predetermined concentrations in further all experiments throughout the study. ROS accumulation can have harmful impacts on various cellular components, including DNA, lipids, and proteins. Excessive ROS production or inadequate scavenging by the cell’s antioxidant mechanisms can lead to mitochondrial oxidative damage or cell apoptosis32. Earlier studies have demonstrated that BHT inhibits complex I and uncouples mitochondrial energy transmission, causing a burst of ROS that is greater than quenching potential of antioxidants33. Elevated ROS levels contribute to oxidative stress, which can damage cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA. This oxidative damage is a significant factor in promoting apoptosis and other forms of cell death34. Therefore, neutralization of excess free radicals is very crucial to maintain healthy state and to achieve this purpose there are various reports available on emergence of exogenous antioxidants. By scavenging ROS produced during pathogenic processes, Hydrogen sulfide, as an antioxidant, exhibits therapeutic potential in a number of inflammatory, neurological and cardiovascular disorders. H2S might be able to shield cardiac tissues from cardiovascular disorders by lowering oxidative stress as well as preventing endothelial cell death35. Our findings have suggested that B4C3 phytotherapy could be linked with the regulation of ROS levels by scavenging them and therefore, its use could be promising in mitigating oxidative stress and associated harmful effects on cellular components.

Treatment of B4C3 reduced the rate of extracellular nitrites (RNS) production

The measurement of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) along with reactive oxygen species (ROS) is crucial for understanding the complex molecular mechanisms of oxidative stress in various biological systems. RNS play important roles in cellular signaling and homeostasis, but can also contribute to cellular damage when present in excess36. A disturbed redox state that can result in oxidative damage to various cell components is caused by disruptions in RNS signaling, which causes them to overproduce or have decreased detoxifying levels. Numerous disorders, including stroke, hypertension, diabetes, neurological disorders, cancer, inflammation, and sepsis are linked to the overproduction of RNS37. Therefore, we have investigated the role of B4C3 phytotherapy in minimizing BHT induced increased nitrites production in HepG2 cells. It was noted that BHT triggered the production of extracellular nitrites (0.042 ± 0.004 µM/mL) but subsequently B4C3 intervention lowered it up to normal levels (0.018 ± 0.0007 µM/mL) (Fig. 1b). Several strategies are employed to reduce RNS levels, such as the use of antioxidants like superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, glutathione and vitamin E, which help neutralize RNS and prevent oxidative and nitrosative damage38. Some flavonoids were tested for their ability to scavenge nitrogen monoxide, and the results showed that anthocyanidins, such as pelargonidins and cynadins, were the most successful at achieving so. This finding was associated with the flavonoids’ possible medicinal properties39. It was evidenced from our investigation that, B4C3 supplementation could maintain cellular homeostasis by reducing excess nitrites during BHT exposure and eventually maintained cellular health in HepG2 cells.

B4C3 intervention ameliorated antioxidant enzymes’ status in HepG2 during BHT exposure

Since ROS are constantly produced in a physiological environment, cells have the ability to neutralize them on their own to prevent harmful consequences. These secretions are collectively referred to as cellular antioxidant defense mechanism40. During oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced in excess, leading to cellular damage. In response, cells activate antioxidant enzymes to mitigate this damage. For instance, SOD catalyzes the conversion of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which is then further detoxified by CAT and GPX41. Here, we measured induction of enzymes involved in cellular antioxidant defense (SOD, CAT and GPX) of HepG2 cells during BHT exposure and subsequent treatment with B4C3 in due course of BHT exposure. BHT exposure raised the activities of all antioxidant enzymes studied such as SOD (70.9 ± 3.9% inhibition of superoxide radicals), CAT (7.08 ± 0.07 units/mL) and GPX (1.21 ± 0.12 units/mL). Whereas treatment with B4C3 decreased the levels of SOD (29.92 ± 3.31% inhibition), CAT (3.12 ± 0.86 units/mL) and GPX (0.36 ± 0.04 units/mL) during BHT exposure (Fig. 2a, b and c). During oxidative stress, the balance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses is disrupted which may lead to cellular damage and development of various disorders such as diabetes, atherosclerosis, cancer, cardiovascular diseases and so on42. Intervention with plant derived antioxidants can be an attractive tool to manage oxidative stress which could complement cells’ antioxidant defense system43. Nrf2 is a transcription factor that regulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT and GPX which gets activated by oxidants and electrophiles leading to its translocation to the nucleus and subsequent transcriptional activation of antioxidant genes44. Our data suggest that B4C3 phytotherapy could have modulated Nrf2 pathway which eventually ameliorated the oxidative stress response in HepG2 cells in due course of BHT exposure.

Decrement in Protein Carbonylation, Lipid peroxidation and Caspase 3/7 activity as a function of B4C3 intervention

ROS production is a routine physiological phenomenon and necessary for regulation of several cellular processes at normal level42. However, when produced in excess, they can cause oxidative damage to proteins, lipids and nucleic acids which may trigger cell death pathways45. Protein carbonylation is a marker of irreversible protein oxidation and can lead to changes in the functional properties of proteins which has been implicated in various diseases, including skeletal muscle dysfunctions, cardiac muscle dysfunctions, cancer etc46. Cellular membranes or organelle membranes are especially susceptible to ROS damage due to their high polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) content and can form lipid peroxides45. Here we have estimated the levels of protein carbonyls, lipid peroxides along with activity of caspase 3/7 in HepG2 as a function of BHT exposure and augmentation of B4C3 during BHT exposure. It was observed that BHT was responsible for significant induction in levels of protein carbonyls (3.52 ± 0.53 units/mg of protein) and lipid peroxides (418.34 ± 44.78%). On the other hand, B4C3 phytotherapy has markedly reduced the levels of protein carbonyls (0.91 ± 0.13 units/mg of protein) and lipid peroxides (106.67 ± 9.43%) in HepG2 cells. The effects of B4C3 intervention on BHT induced protein carbonyl content and lipid peroxidation are presented in Fig. 2d and e. Since protein oxidation can result in conformational changes, unfolding, polymerization, aggregation or impairment of structural and functional activity, it is especially concerning. As aggregates of oxidized proteins are not easily broken down by cells, an accumulation of them results in cellular dysfunction and death47. On the other hand, lipid peroxidation products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA) can activate signaling pathways that promote apoptosis. For instance, oxidized lipids can stimulate mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c into the cytosol. Additionally, lipid peroxidation can activate caspases, which are crucial for executing the apoptotic program48.

Expression of Caspase 3/7 is an important marker of activation of apoptotic cell death pathway. In BHT exposed HepG2cells the activity of caspase 3/7 was found to be elevated (0.68 ± 0.04 RLU). However, phytotherapy (B4C3) supplementation in due course of BHT exposure has significantly lowered the activity of Caspase 3/7 (0.28 ± 0.04 RLU) indicating protection of cells from entering apoptosis (Fig. 2f). Elevated levels of ROS can lead to cellular damage and initiate apoptotic signaling pathways. For instance, exposure to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) has been shown to activate caspase-3 through both mitochondrial and cytosolic pathways, leading to apoptosis in N27 cells (dopaminergic neuronal cell line). However, co-treatment with antioxidants like Trolox and MnTBAP can significantly attenuate increases in caspase-3 activity. This suggests that reducing ROS levels can help maintain lower caspase-3 activity and prevent apoptosis49. Another study has demonstrated capsaicin’s protective role against oxidative stress-induced protein carbonylation and lipid peroxidation in human erythrocytes, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target50. Our findings coincide with above study which have suggested that B4C3 phytotherapy may have a potential in protecting cells from apoptosis by lowering elevated levels of protein carbonyls, lipid peroxides and caspase 3/7 during BHT induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells.

Validation of cell death parameters using fluorescent microscopy and flow cytometry as a function of BHT treatment and subsequent herbal intervention (B4C3)

Present study was aimed to thoroughly investigate whether B4C3 phytotherapy could ameliorate cell death parameters in course of BHT exposure in HepG2 cells by using fluorescent microscope and flow cytometer.

Microscopic evaluation of HepG2 cells

ROS can directly interact with DNA, causing base modifications, abasic sites (neither a purine nor a pyrimidine base) and strand breaks. In response to oxidative DNA damage, cells activate complex signaling networks to promote DNA repair and survival or trigger cell death. However, when these mechanisms fail, genomic instability can lead to disease51. We have used DAPI to demonstrate DNA damage in HepG2 cells. DAPI intercalates into the minor groove of dsDNA, with a significant preference for adenine-thymine (A-T) rich regions. This binding is facilitated by the more negative electrostatic potential of these areas, allowing DAPI to form hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with the DNA base pairs. Upon binding, the quantum yield of DAPI increases dramatically by over 20-fold resulting in bright blue fluorescence when excited by ultraviolet light (around 358–360 nm)52. It was observed that BHT exposure led a marked DNA damage wherein huge number of cells showing fragmented DNA. On the other hand, augmentation of B4C3 resulted into reduction in number of cells having fragmented DNA during BHT treatment (Fig. 3a).

Excessive ROS can lead to oxidative damage to mitochondrial components, including the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes. This damage can disrupt the normal functioning of the ETC, affecting the generation of the proton gradient necessary for maintaining the mitochondrial membrane potential53. Rhodamine 123 (RH123), a cell-permeant, cationic, green-fluorescent dye used to assay mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in populations of cells based on the principle that the dye accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix due to the positive charge of the dye and the negative charge of the mitochondrial matrix. The higher the MMP, the more the dye accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix, and the higher the fluorescence intensity. Conversely, a decrease in MMP leads to a decrease in dye accumulation and a decrease in fluorescence intensity54. In present study a reduced fluorescence intensity of RH123 in cells from BHT exposed group was observed because of having disturbed MMP whereas augmentation of B4C3 during BHT exposure was found accountable for maintaining intact MMP in HepG2 cells which were ultimately appeared brighter in fluorescence resembling control ones (Fig. 3b).

Normally, cellular changes especially DNA damage and disturbed MMP trigger apoptotic signalling pathways, leading to programmed cell death as a protective mechanism against cellular damage and dysfunction55. Annexin V (fluorescent protein which can be used to detect apoptosis) binds to phosphatidylserine (PS), a lipid that is normally found on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane but becomes exposed on the outer leaflet in apoptotic cells, where they can be detected by fluorescently labelled annexin V. Whereas, Propidium iodide (PI) is a fluorescent dye (used to detect cellular necrosis) that cannot penetrate intact cell membranes. However, in late apoptotic and necrotic cells, the plasma and nuclear membranes become more permeable, allowing PI to enter the cells and intercalate with nucleic acids which results in red fluorescence56. We observed that a large number of cells have undergone apoptotic cell death as a treatment of BHT. B4C3 phytotherapy has protected cells from undergoing apoptosis and efficiently reversed these numbers alike control group (Fig. 3c). We did not see any necrotic cell death events in BHT exposed HepG2 cells.

Quantitative determination of disturbed MMP, apoptosis and autophagy using flow cytometric evaluation of HepG2 cells

Flow cytometric analysis is an excellent tool to quantitively study different cellular events like disturbed MMP, apoptosis, necrosis etc. In present investigation, while studying the effect of BHT and phytotherapy on MMP we observed that 93.74% cells from control set were found to be healthy and having intact mitochondrial membrane potential whereas during BHT treatment, proportion of healthy cells reduced up to 71.25% and remaining 28.75% cells were having disturbed mitochondrial membrane potential. Treatment with B4C3 efficiently maintained the MMP during BHT exposure that is 92.54% cells were showing intact mitochondrial membrane integrity (Fig. 4a). Moreover, it was observed that BHT was accountable for triggering apoptosis in HepG2 cells wherein percentage of late apoptotic cells was found to be 20.56% followed by 7.63% were observed in early apoptosis. Supplementation of B4C3 could reduce these proportions up to normal levels (1.68% cells were found in late apoptosis while 1.44% cells in early apoptosis). Here also, we did not find any evidence for cells undergoing necrosis during BHT exposure (Fig. 4b).

Deregulation of redox homeostasis results in excessive generation of ROS, leading to oxidative stress, which can further induce autophagy. ROS-induced autophagy is a cellular protective mechanism that alleviates oxidative stress, but it can also be a destructive process when ROS levels are too high57. To further investigate the protective role of proposed phytotherapy in autophagic cell death during BHT treatment, we have used Monodansylcadaverine (MDC), an autofluorescent marker which is incorporated into autophagic vacuoles within cells, making it a valuable tool for studying autophagy58. It was noted that BHT exposure was responsible for tremendous induction of autophagy in HepG2 cells wherein 73.83% cells were found to be undergoing autophagic cell death. But B4C3 phytotherapy has markedly inhibited autophagy by reversing the proportion of autophagic cells up to 3.89% only; during BHT exposure (Fig. 4c).

Considering outcome of above investigations, it is clear that, BHT is a potent inducer of apoptosis and autophagy but not necrosis due to its specific effects on cellular processes and signalling pathways. B4C3 supplementation proved its therapeutic approach in protecting cells from undergoing apoptosis and autophagy which may be due to its antioxidant properties that can neutralize ROS, protect cells from oxidative damage and eventually ensured cell survival.

B4C3 phytotherapy positively modulated the expression of genes involved in apoptosis and autophagy

BHT have been shown to induce cell death, particularly through mechanisms such as apoptosis and autophagy. This can involve the upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins like caspases and the downregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins, ultimately leading to programmed cell death. We have implemented RT-PCR analysis to confirm antiapoptotic as well as antiautophagic potential of B4C3 phytotherapy in HepG2 cells. Apoptosis is a highly regulated process of programmed cell death essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis, development and the elimination of damaged or unwanted cells59. p53, often referred to as the “guardian of the genome,” is a critical tumor suppressor protein that plays a central role in apoptosis. It acts primarily as a transcription factor that regulates the expression of a wide array of genes involved in apoptosis. Upon activation, it binds to specific DNA sequences, leading to the transcription of pro-apoptotic genes such as Bax, PUMA as well as Noxa and downregulate anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL60. Bcl-2 prevents apoptosis by binding to pro-apoptotic proteins (like Bak and Bax) through its BH3-binding pocket, thereby sequestering them and preventing their activation. When p53 is activated, it can inhibit Bcl-2’s protective effects. This interaction is crucial for the apoptotic response to stress signals61. Bak is a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family that plays a direct role in mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), a key step in the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis. Bak works alongside Bax to mediate the effects of p53-induced apoptosis. When p53activates these pro-apoptotic proteins, it enhances their ability to promote cell death by disrupting mitochondrial integrity62. Cas 3activation is a key step in the apoptotic process which is essential for the controlled and regulated cell death that characterizes apoptosis. It is indispensable for orderly dismantling of the cell during apoptosis leading to cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation and the formation of apoptotic bodies63.

On the other hand, autophagy is a crucial biological process that involves the degradation and recycling of damaged cellular components. The term, derived from Greek meaning “self-eating,” describes how cells can break down and repurpose their own parts to maintain homeostasis and promote survival, especially during stress or nutrient deprivation64. Beclin 1is a pivotal protein in the autophagy pathway, primarily functioning as a regulator of the Class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K-III) complex. This complex plays a crucial role in the formation and maturation of autophagosomes, which are essential for the degradation of cellular components65. Whereas, LC3 is a specific marker of autophagy and particularly of autophagosome formation. Moreover, LC3is the only well-characterized protein which is specifically localized to autophagic structures throughout the process from formation to degradation66. For this purpose, we have studied mRNA expression of these key genes such as p53, Bcl2, Bak and Cas 3 associated with apoptosis and Beclin 1 and LC 3 involved in autophagic cell death pathway.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed that BHT exposure upregulated the mRNA expressions of genes associated with apoptosis such as p53, Bak and Cas3 by 1.9 ± 0.11 folds (p53), 1.61 ± 0.16 folds (Bak) and 2.12 ± 0.21 folds (cas3) respectively. Whereas 0.52 ± 0.06 folds downregulation in mRNA expression of Bcl2 gene was observed in BHT exposed HepG2 cells. But augmentation of B4C3 during BHT exposure has positively modulated these mRNA expression patterns of respected genes and proved its antiapoptotic potential in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5a, c, d and b). Moreover, in case of autophagy markers, upregulation of both the genes such as Beclin1 and LC3 by 4.82 ± 0.78 and 4.53 ± 0.54 folds respectively was evidenced as a function of BHT exposure. While implementation of B4C3 has efficiently lowered these expressions back to normal levels in due course of BHT exposure in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5e and f). Previous studies have reported that synthetic phenolic antioxidants BHA and BHT exposure may activate apoptosis and also cause a built-up of misfolded and unfolded proteins, which can encourage autophagy and ultimately cause cell death in the hepatocytes of the grass carp67. Our outcomes are in agreement with above report which stated that BHT might have altered the expression of marker proteins involved in apoptotic and autophagic pathways as a consequence of accumulation of damaged macromolecules and organelles leading to cell death. While defensive role of B4C3 phytotherapy against hyperactivation of autophagy along with apoptosis during BHT exposure in HepG2 cells was plausible.

Implementation of phytotherapy restored disturbed cell growth phases during BHT exposure

Further, we have conducted a growth curve analysis of HepG2 cells during exposure of BHT alone as well as BHT in augmentation with B4C3. The cell growth curve is a graphical representation of how the population of cells changes over time. It is a crucial tool, particularly in the study of cell growth and division, as it provides valuable insights into (i) cell proliferation dynamics; (ii) effect of genomic manipulations; and (iii) impact of chemical compounds on cell growth and behavior68. It was observed that cells from control group have displayed continuous growth showing appropriate growth phases whereas BHT exposure resulted into disturbed cell growth phases from day 1 after seeding. Subsequently, B4C3 supplementation during BHT exposure restored the growth phases of HepG2 cells and expressed growth pattern alike control one (Fig. 6a). Complementary to this, outcomes of cell proliferation assay performed after 24 h of treatments using MTT revealed that BHT exposure was found accountable for reducing cell viability up to 53.03% in HepG2 cells. Whereas supplementation of B4C3 during BHT exposure has restored the percentage of viable cells upto 95.73% (Fig. 6b). At low concentrations, ROS are essential for various cellular functions, including cell proliferation and differentiation. They act as signaling molecules that activate pathways involved in cell cycle progression such as activation of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and other metabolic processes. Conversely, high ROS levels have been shown to induce G1-S phase arrest by activating cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors like p21, thereby halting cell proliferation69.

Research on human maxillary cancer cells and rhesus monkey kidney cells showed that BHT inhibited the growth of these cells in vitro in concentration and time-dependent manner, with varying effects on RNA, protein and DNA synthesis. Additionally, the study highlighted that BHT inhibited the growth of human maxillary cancer cells in vitro decreasing the cellular survival by 50%70. In concurrence with this study, at the end of the experiment (Day 6) we observed that survival rate of HepG2 cells was dropped down by 90% during BHT exposure. On the contrary, B4C3 phytotherapy proved beneficial in restoring proliferation potential of cells by maintaining redox homeostasis and encouraging antioxidant capacity during BHT induced holocaust.

Conclusion

Present study illuminated the detrimental effects of BHT on HepG2 cells, such as the induction of oxidative stress, apoptosis and autophagy. The findings highlighted the potential of BB and CA as a protective combination against the toxic effects of BHT. The combination notably mitigated built-up of free radicals, restored the activities of antioxidant enzymes, reduced protein carbonylation, lipid peroxidation and caspase 3/7 activity, indicative of its protective role against oxidative damage and cell death. The phytotherapy also demonstrated a significant modulatory effect on apoptosis and autophagy by protecting cells from DNA damage, maintaining mitochondrial membrane potential, regulating expression of genes involved in apoptosis and autophagy and eventually restored growth phases in HepG2 cells during BHT exposure. Overall, the comprehensive results offered valuable insights into the protective mechanisms of these natural compounds in combination, highlighting their potential as a therapeutic approach to counteract BHT-induced hepatotoxicity. Further research in this area may open new avenues for developing novel strategies aimed at alleviating oxidative stress and promoting cellular health.
