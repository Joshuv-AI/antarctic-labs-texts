Comprehensive Understanding and Diagnostic Approaches to Acute Right Ventricular Failure in the CCU
Authors: Jung Sun Cho
Published: 2026

As highlighted in a recent study published in the International Journal of Heart Failure (IJHF), acute right ventricular (RV) failure in the Cardiac Care Unit (CCU) arises from diverse etiologies, including myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, sepsis, and respiratory failure.1) Notably, normal RV function was observed in only 24.3% of cases—a sobering statistic that underscores the high prevalence of RV dysfunction in critical care settings. It is now well-established that the presence of RV dysfunction is not merely a clinical finding but a critical determinant of poor patient prognosis.1,2)

The pathophysiology of RV failure in the intensive care unit is multifactorial, involving excess preload, bradycardia, increased pulmonary artery (PA) pressure or pulmonary vascular resistance, concomitant left ventricular (LV) failure, and increased afterload secondary to mechanical ventilation. To understand these complexities, one must consider the unique anatomical and physiological characteristics of the RV. Unlike the LV, the RV is a thin-walled, crescent-shaped chamber. It is designed to pump venous blood from the systemic circulation into the lungs—a low-pressure, low-resistance, and high-capacitance system. Consequently, the RV is highly susceptible to afterload increases due to its lower intrinsic contractility compared to the LV.

In the CCU, clinicians frequently encounter critically ill patients requiring timely volume assessment and monitoring of PA pressure. Such situations necessitate rapid decision-making regarding volume replacement, inotropic support, or the initiation of mechanical circulatory support, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. In this context, Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) plays a pivotal role in the management of RV heart failure. While transthoracic echocardiography remains the gold standard for assessing RV dimensions, function, and hemodynamics, performing a comprehensive evaluation of all guideline-defined parameters at the bedside remains a logistical challenge. This gap highlights the urgent need for simplified, yet effective, evaluative parameters.

Among the currently utilized echocardiographic parameters, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) is the most widely used due to its speed and ease of assessment. However, TAPSE is highly load-dependent and tends to remain preserved in the early stages of precapillary pulmonary hypertension. To overcome these limitations, RV/PA coupling parameters (e.g., RV free-wall longitudinal strain/systolic PA pressure [sPAP], TAPSE/sPAP) have gained traction, with increasing emphasis on their significance as prognostic indicators.2,3) Nevertheless, these parameters primarily assess lateral wall longitudinal motion and may overlook RV volume and circumferential function. Therefore, concurrent evaluation of RV fractional area change is necessary, as its utility as a mortality predictor has been validated in numerous studies.4)

A comprehensive understanding of acute RV dysfunction requires an analysis of RV remodeling, intrinsic function, and the underlying causes of increased afterload (categorized into precapillary, postcapillary, or combined types). Evaluating these multifaceted elements with a single parameter is inherently difficult. Instead, there is a clear need for systematic diagnostic approaches using established algorithms. Although the 2015 American Society of Echocardiography guidelines provided well-defined reference values for individual parameters, they did not reach the point of offering a structured diagnostic algorithm, unlike the comprehensive frameworks established for diastolic function.5) In this regard, the current study offers a pragmatic and intuitive alternative: the expert echocardiographic “eyeball” global RV assessment. This semi-quantitative approach to evaluating RV geometry and function was found to correlate strongly with advanced Doppler-derived hemodynamic parameters. Furthermore, it demonstrated significant power as a prognostic predictor for both in-hospital and one-year mortality, even when stratified by LV function and the presence of cardiogenic shock. While this approach acknowledges limitations such as subjectivity and inter-observer variation, it provides a highly practical solution for the bedside clinician.1)

As the medical community continues to actively discuss the “knowledge gaps” in the diagnosis and management of acute RV failure, this study provides a vital contribution to the standardization of RV assessment via POCUS.3,6) By demonstrating that a pragmatic, expert-led visual assessment can mirror complex Doppler hemodynamics and reliably predict mortality, it bridges the gap between sophisticated guidelines and the immediate needs of the bedside clinician.

However, the transition from intuitive “eyeball” global RV assessment to a truly systematic diagnostic framework remains the next frontier. Further studies are warranted to develop and validate structured diagnostic algorithms that can integrate these multifaceted parameters into a cohesive clinical pathway, ensuring that RV evaluation becomes standardized and reproducible.
