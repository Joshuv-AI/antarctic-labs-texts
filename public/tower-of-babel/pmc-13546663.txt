Referral Patterns, Diagnostic Delay, and Clinical Characteristics of Patients with Axial Spondyloarthritis in a Tertiary Rheumatology Centre in Malaysia: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Ezra Zhe Shen Tan, Federic Yiong Poh Kho, Ahmed Shaheed, Jia Min Foo, Farahnurbain Ghazali, Ravinash Ratnam, Woon Shen Teo, Thayalan Ponniah, Fariz Yahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate diagnostic delay, referral pathways, presenting symptoms, and clinical characteristics among patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) managed at a Malaysian tertiary rheumatology centre.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adults with consultant-confirmed axSpA attending the Rheumatology Clinic, Universiti Malaya Medical Centre. Demographics, axSpA subtype, diagnostic delay, presenting symptoms, laboratory profiles, HLA‑B27 status, referral pathways, and treatments before and 6 months after diagnosis were recorded.ResultsA total of 141 adults were included. This consisted of 87 males (61.7%) and was predominantly Chinese (n=83, 58.9%), followed by Malay (n=45, 31.9%), Indian (n=12, 8.5%), and other ethnicities (n=1, 0.7%). Radiographic axSpA and non‑radiographic axSpA accounted for 103 (73.0%) and 38 (27.0%) patients, respectively. Median age at symptom onset and diagnosis were 28 and 35 years. Median diagnostic delay was 48 months, and radiographic patients experienced longer delays (60 vs 36 months). Back pain was the commonest presenting symptom, with subtype‑specific variations in neck, shoulder, and peripheral joint involvement. External referrals had longer delays than internal referrals. Before diagnosis, 76.5% received NSAIDs, and 31.2% commenced biologic therapy within six months.ConclusionIn this Malaysian tertiary-care cohort, axSpA was associated with a median diagnostic delay of 48 months, with longer delays observed in radiographic disease and external referrals. Delayed recognition may reflect non-classical presentations and pathway-level barriers in referral coordination and specialist access. Greater awareness among primary care and non-rheumatology specialties, supported by structured referral criteria, minimum referral datasets, shared imaging pathways, and streamlined external referral processes, may improve timely rheumatology assessment.Keywords: axial spondyloarthritis, diagnostic delay, referral patterns, non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis, Malaysia

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily involves the sacroiliac joints and spine and may also affect peripheral joints and entheses.1 Previously known as ankylosing spondylitis, this condition encompasses radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA), with structural changes evident on conventional radiograph, and non‑radiographic axSpA (nr-axSpA), in which active inflammation can be detected on MRI in the absence of definite radiographic change, consistent with the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) classification criteria.2 Globally, the epidemiology of axSpA varies substantially across regions; the estimated prevalence is lower in Southeast Asia than in other higher‑latitude populations.3 However, contemporary Southeast Asian epidemiological data remain scarce; a recent national Thai database study reported an ankylosing spondylitis prevalence of 20.4 per 100,000 population and an incidence of approximately 10.4 per 100,000 person-years.4 In Malaysia, low back pain is commonly reported, but prevalence estimates vary widely across selected populations and occupational groups, with reported prevalence ranging from 12.4% to 84.6% in a local scoping review.5 To our knowledge, population-level prevalence estimates for axSpA in Malaysia are not currently available. Current national data are mainly registry-based rather than population-based epidemiological estimates.6

Clinically, patients most often report inflammatory back pain lasting more than three months, which improves with exercise, worsens at night and does not improve with rest.7 Extra‑musculoskeletal features, including acute anterior uveitis, enthesitis, peripheral arthritis, and dactylitis, are common and contribute to heterogeneity in care‑seeking and referral patterns.1 Diagnosis remains challenging as early symptoms may be non-specific, awareness of the condition may vary, and access to appropriate imaging, including MRI, may be uneven across healthcare settings.8–10

Assessment of disease activity typically uses patient-reported Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and the more robust composite score of Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS), which incorporates acute-phase reactants.11,12 Both metrics capture inflammatory burden but provide complementary information for clinical and research use.13 Early and effective treatment beginning with NSAIDs and escalating to biologic agents targeting tumour necrosis factor (TNF) or interleukin-17 (IL‑17) pathways, when indicated, has been proven to improve symptoms, function, and quality of life and may alter the trajectory of structural damage.14 Timely referral is therefore important to enable appropriate assessment and treatment initiation.

In the local public-private healthcare system, patients with suspected axSpA may be assessed through various routes, including primary care clinics, public hospitals, private healthcare providers, and medical or surgical specialties, before a rheumatology assessment. Although local studies have described clinical phenotypes15 and proposed structured referral frameworks, including the Malaysian 3R inflammatory back pain referral algorithm16 for primary care, its implementation and impact in routine clinical practice have not been systematically evaluated. The 3R framework guides clinicians to recognise inflammatory back pain, review relevant clinical features and investigations, and refer suspected axSpA patients to rheumatology. However, real-world data on actual referral trajectories and diagnostic intervals remain limited.

Therefore, this study aims to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with axSpA attending a Malaysian tertiary centre, quantify diagnostic delay overall and across radiographic subtypes, characterize referral patterns and their association with time to diagnosis, summarize baseline disease activity, and the initial treatment patterns, including pre‑diagnosis NSAID exposure and early biologics.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‑centre, retrospective observational study conducted at the Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC), a tertiary referral hospital in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. The study included all consecutive adult patients diagnosed with axSpA who attended the rheumatology clinic at UMMC between January 2005 and July 2025. Clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Patients with axSpA

We included all adults aged ≥18 years with a clinically confirmed diagnosis of axSpA who were evaluated at the UMMC rheumatology clinic during the study period. Diagnosis was established by a consultant rheumatologist after review of clinical, laboratory, and imaging records, and after exclusion of alternative diagnoses. For study classification, the 2009 ASAS axial spondyloarthritis classification criteria were applied using either the imaging arm or the clinical arm, where the required data were available.17,18 Patients were subsequently classified as having r-axSpA or nr-axSpA according to the presence or absence of radiographic sacroiliitis. Patients were excluded if they had an alternative diagnosis to axSpA, an uncertain diagnosis after specialist review, or insufficient clinical or imaging documentation to support axSpA. All eligible patients were identified from the rheumatology patient database, ensuring full capture of confirmed axSpA cases seen during the study period.

Data Sources and Variables

Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. All patient identifiers were removed at the point of data extraction to ensure full anonymization. Information regarding demographic, clinical, laboratory, imaging, referral, and treatment variables was obtained from structured data fields where available and supplemented by a manual review of clinician free-text notes. All variables were analysed on an available-case basis, with no imputation performed for missing data. Due to the retrospective study design, the number of available observations varied across variables, particularly for symptom-onset date, HLA-B27 status, inflammatory markers, imaging, disease activity scores, and treatment variables. Variable-specific denominators are reported where data were incomplete.

Demographic data included age, gender, ethnicity, age at symptom onset, and age at diagnosis. Clinical variables comprised axSpA subtype (radiographic or non-radiographic) and history of inflammatory back pain. Symptom variables included axial (neck, lower back, and pelvic/buttock area) and peripheral (shoulder and peripheral joints) involvement, reflected documentation from the first rheumatology encounter rather than a cumulative history. Additionally, extra-musculoskeletal features were recorded.

Laboratory markers collected at the initial rheumatology assessment included erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP). Inflammatory markers were considered raised if the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was ≥ 21 mm/hr or the C-reactive protein (CRP) was ≥ 5 mg/L.

Disease activity at presentation was assessed using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS).19,20 Disease activity at presentation was defined as active if the BASDAI was ≥ 4 or the ASDAS was ≥ 2.1.

Radiological data were retrieved based on available pelvic radiographs, sacroiliac joint (SIJ) radiographs, and/or MRI findings of the SI joints. Radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA) was identified by the presence of radiographic sacroiliitis meeting the modified New York criteria on pelvic X-ray, as documented in clinical radiology reports.21

Referral Patterns

Referral pattern characteristics included the referring specialty, healthcare sector (public or private), and referral source (internal or external). Internal referrals originated from clinical specialties within UMMC, while external referrals came from outside facilities. The public sector included referrals from UMMC and other government-funded institutions, whereas the private sector comprised those from private healthcare providers. Named referring facilities were extracted where documented, but facility-level identifiers were inconsistently recorded across the retrospective study period and individual facility counts were small. Therefore, referral analyses were conducted by referring specialty, referral source, and healthcare sector rather than by named hospital or private centre. Diagnostic delay was defined as the time interval, in months, between the earliest patient-reported onset of symptoms attributable to axSpA and the index rheumatology visit at UMMC. Using the initial specialist encounter as the operational endpoint standardises measurement across the cohort and avoids variability arising from institutional turnaround times for outpatient investigations, such as waiting times for MRI scheduling or HLA-B27 processing. Diagnosis of axSpA was confirmed by a consultant rheumatologist following review of all relevant clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings from investigations initiated at or prior to the index visit. Treatment variables included pre-diagnosis NSAID exposure, defined as any documented NSAID use prior to the first rheumatology visit, and early biologic initiation, defined as starting therapy within 6 months of diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp). Continuous variables (age at symptom onset, age at diagnosis, diagnostic delay) were summarised as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) after assessing distributional normality. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons between r-axSpA and nr-axSpA used the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Referral‑related comparisons (internal vs external referrals, public vs private sector) used the same approach.

Effect sizes were quantified using Hodges–Lehmann median differences with 95% confidence intervals for continuous outcomes, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for categorical outcomes.

To evaluate potential confounding between gender, axSpA subtype, and selected outcomes, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed on log‑transformed diagnostic‑delay data, with assessment of interaction terms. Model assumptions, including linearity and homoscedasticity of residuals, were checked and met. To further evaluate whether these unadjusted associations were driven by confounding between gender and subtype, we conducted stratified Mantel–Haenszel analyses.

All analyses used an available‑case approach, with no imputation for missing data. All p-values were two‑sided, and given the exploratory nature of multiple subgroup comparisons, no adjustment for multiplicity was applied. A p-value of <0.05 is considered statistically significant.

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

We included 141 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of axSpA, of which 61.7% (n=87) were males. The overall median age of symptom onset and diagnosis were 28 (IQR: 22.0–39.3) and 35 (IQR: 26.5–46.0) years, respectively. Radiographic axSpA comprised 73.0% (n=103) of the cohort. Patients’ HLA‑B27 status was available for 97 patients and was positive in 69.1% (n=67). Pelvic radiographs and MRI of SIJs were performed within 6 months of diagnosis in 74.5% (n=105) and 26.2% (n=37) of patients, respectively (Table 1). The distribution of gender differed by axSpA subtype (χ2 (1) = 23.6, p<0.001; φ=0.41).

Clinical, Laboratory, and Symptom Profiles by Subtype

Positivity for HLA‑B27 and elevated ESR findings were more common in patients with r-axSpA (OR 2.89, 95% CI 1.18–7.05; p=0.018 and OR 3.28, 95% CI 1.32–8.14; p=0.009, respectively). Levels of CRP did not differ meaningfully by subtype. Based on symptom profile, patients with r-axSpA showed a higher frequency of neck symptoms (32.0% vs 7.9%; OR 5.56, 95% CI 1.59–20.00; p=0.004), with lower frequency of shoulder symptoms (8.7% vs 23.7%; OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.11–0.85; p=0.025) and lesser peripheral joint involvement (27.2% vs 60.5%; OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.11–0.53; p<0.001). Lower back and pelvic/buttock symptoms did not differ meaningfully between groups. Extra‑articular features were uncommon overall, with no clear subtype differences (Table 4).

Adjusted Associations Between HLA‑B27, Gender, and axSpA Subtype

After adjusting for axSpA subtype, male patients were more significantly found to be HLA‑B27 positive (common OR 2.97, 95% CI 1.14–7.70; p=0.025), indicating an independent association between gender and HLA‑B27 status. In addition, gender remained independently associated with r-axSpA after adjusting for HLA‑B27 status, with males showing markedly higher odds of radiographic disease (common OR 3.95, 95% CI 1.58–9.90; p=0.003). Collectively, these results suggest that male sex is an important independent determinant of both HLA‑B27 positivity and radiographic disease.

Disease Activity at Initial Clinical Presentation

Using prespecified thresholds (BASDAI ≥ 4 or ASDAS ≥ 2.1), the proportion with active disease at first presentation was similar between radiographic and non‑radiographic axSpA (57% vs 67%; χ2 = 0.19, p = 0.66; OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.11–4.13; n = 41). Overall, disease‑activity status did not differ materially by axSpA subtype.

Pre‑diagnosis NSAID Exposure and Early Biologic Initiation

The use of NSAIDs before diagnosis showed no clear differences by axSpA subtype, gender, HLA‑B27 status, or inflammatory markers. However, patients from private sector referrals had higher recorded prior-NSAID use than those referred from the public sector (OR 5.49, 95% CI 1.28–24.53; p=0.014). Early biologic initiation (within 6 months of diagnosis) did not differ by axSpA subtype, gender, HLA‑B27 status, ESR, or CRP. Early biologic use was more frequent in the private sector than the public sector (OR 2.86, 95% CI 1.27–6.67; p=0.010) and more frequent among external than internal referrals (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.08–4.76; p=0.030). Discussion

In this study of a Malaysian cohort with confirmed axSpA, derived from two decades of documented electronic records, we observed substantial diagnostic delay overall, where patients with r‑axSpA experienced consistently longer delays despite similar ages at symptom onset. Patients with r-axSpA were also diagnosed at an older age and were more frequently associated with HLA‑B27 positivity, elevated ESR, and symptoms affecting the neck, whereas patients with nr-axSpA more often presented with shoulder symptoms and peripheral joint involvement. Differences in time to diagnosis were associated with referral source, as external referrals had significantly longer delays than internal referrals. Disease activity at first review did not differ meaningfully between subtypes, and pre‑diagnosis NSAID exposure and early biologic initiation were more closely associated with the healthcare sector and referral source than with measured clinical phenotype.

Late diagnosis of axSpA has been reported to be associated with structural changes and higher rates of disability.22,23 In this study, diagnostic delay was longer in r-axSpA despite similar onset ages, yielding an older age at diagnosis. The wide distribution of diagnostic delays, including a considerable proportion of patients with delays beyond five years, highlights barriers to timely identification and referral. Our findings are consistent with international evidence that diagnostic delays in axSpA commonly span over several years, with global medians ranging between 2 to 6 years,24 and that delays are often longer in r‑axSpA than nr‑axSpA.25 This pattern is consistent with reports that radiographic change becomes apparent over time2,26 and delays may be exacerbated in systems that rely on plain radiography as the first‑line investigation, given that structural changes typically take years to become detectable. On the contrary, a Spanish cohort27 observed the opposite pattern, with longer delays in nr‑axSpA. While the ASAS‑COMOSPA Asia analysis28 found no significant delay difference by subtype in Asian populations, highlighting heterogeneity across settings. Nevertheless, these differences suggest that local healthcare organisations and referral structures meaningfully shape the observed diagnostic intervals.

Male sex was independently associated with r-axSpA in this cohort, even after accounting for HLA‑B27 status. This finding aligns with prior studies linking male sex to structural progression.29 Although HLA‑B27 positivity increased the likelihood of diagnosis, it did not independently distinguish between radiographic and non‑radiographic disease once gender was considered. Clinically, this underscores the importance of avoiding over‑reliance on HLA‑B27 status or gender when evaluating suspected axSpA, particularly in patients with non‑radiographic disease. This is important given that HLA‑B27‑negative disease accounts for a substantial proportion of the cohort. Likewise, although men constitute a larger proportion of r‑axSpA cases, gender distribution should not shape referral thresholds or investigation intensity. This remains important because female patients with axSpA may experience longer diagnostic delay and distinct unmet diagnostic needs in other settings, potentially related to different clinical presentations, greater peripheral symptom burden, and lower radiographic burden.30 Appropriate use of MRI, alongside structured referral criteria, is needed to avoid under‑recognition of nr‑axSpA in women.

There are several proposed mechanisms for observed gender differences in radiographic classification, including sex‑linked differences in biomechanical loading and entheseal microtrauma, as well as variation in IL-17A-related inflammatory pathways that may influence new bone formation.31–34 These hypotheses warrant prospective investigation but were beyond the scope of this retrospective analysis.

The symptom of inflammatory back pain is one of the most common presenting symptoms in patients with axSpA. In our study, we found that the classic inflammatory back pain alone is insufficient to differentiate r‑axSpA from nr‑axSpA at first contact. In contrast, neck involvement should heighten suspicion for radiographic disease, whereas prominent shoulder or peripheral joint symptoms should not deter consideration of axSpA, especially in younger adults with no radiographic changes. In this study, the higher rates of neck involvement in r‑axSpA align with prior observations of axial symptoms in established r-axSpA.35 Recent MRI-based evidence in patients with r-axSpA and chronic neck pain further supports the clinical relevance of cervical symptoms in radiographic disease.36 Conversely, the greater shoulder and peripheral joint involvement in nr‑axSpA have been reported variably across cohorts, with some series showing similar peripheral involvement in r‑axSpA.37 Recent imaging evidence similarly suggests that peripheral entheseal abnormalities may occur in radiographic disease, with reduced Achilles tendon stiffness demonstrated using shear wave elastography.38 Such discrepancies likely reflect population genetics, ethnicity, and referral patterns rather than a single uniform clinical signature. Nonetheless, incorporating these symptom cues into referral prompts and structured assessment checklists could reduce diversion to non‑rheumatology pathways and accelerate appropriate investigation.

Referral patterns in this cohort highlight system‑level influences on time to diagnosis. Although patients were referred from a broad range of specialities, diagnostic delay did not differ across referring disciplines, suggesting that variability in speciality-specific recognition was not a major contributor to delay in this setting. In contrast, referral source showed a clear association with diagnostic timing, as patients referred from outside the institution experienced substantially longer delays than those referred internally. This pattern may reflect the influence of logistical factors such as inter‑facility coordination, transfer of clinical information, and access to imaging and specialist review, on diagnostic intervals. The absence of a significant difference between public and private sector referrals suggests that funding sector alone may not explain diagnostic timing. Instead, delays may be more closely related to pathway-level factors, particularly coordination between services and timely access to imaging and specialist review.

This interpretation is supported by a recent systematic review of patient journeys in rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases, which identified fragmented care, limited disease awareness, poor care coordination, unclear referral processes, and delayed access to rheumatology as recurring barriers.39 AxSpA-specific qualitative evidence similarly highlights diagnostic complexity, limited awareness, unclear referral pathways, and communication barriers as contributors to diagnostic delay.40 Regional consensus recommendations further support structured referral pathways for primary care and non-rheumatologists, rheumatologist confirmation of diagnosis, adequate clinical information for imaging requests, and appropriate use of HLA-B27 and MRI in suspected axSpA.41 In the Malaysian context, our findings suggest that key bottlenecks may include delayed recognition of non-classical presentations, external referral routes, variable transfer of clinical information, and access to timely specialist assessment.

There are limitations to this study. As a retrospective analysis of routinely collected data, causal inference is limited and residual confounding may remain. Symptom onset and symptom profiles were based on patient-reported history documented in clinical records, including narrative documentation rather than structured inventories, which may introduce recall or recording bias and affect completeness or consistency. Data availability varied across selected variables, including HLA-B27 status, inflammatory markers, imaging, disease activity scores, and symptom-onset timing; analyses were therefore performed using available-case data, with variable-specific denominators reported where data were incomplete. Some referral-specialty categories contained small numbers, so specialty-specific median delay estimates were interpreted descriptively. Finally, the single‑centre study design may constrain generalizability to other healthcare systems.

Effective management of axSpA requires a diagnostic approach that looks beyond inflammatory back pain. Clinicians should recognize that neck symptoms may signal early radiographic disease, while shoulder or peripheral symptoms are often compatible with nr-axSpA, particularly in younger populations. While HLA-B27 remains a valuable tool to raise clinical suspicion, it should not function as an exclusionary gatekeeper. Furthermore, ensuring equitable access to MRI is critical to reducing diagnostic delays and preventing the systemic under-recognition of the disease in women. To optimize the patient pathway, service design should prioritize integrated logistics for external referrals through the implementation of shared imaging protocols, standardized data transfer checklists, and e-referrals with minimum datasets to facilitate fast-track specialist assessment.

Conclusion

In this Malaysian tertiary-care cohort, patients with axSpA experienced a median diagnostic delay of 48 months. Despite similar ages at symptom onset, patients with r-axSpA were diagnosed later and experienced longer diagnostic delays than those with nr-axSpA. Radiographic disease was more frequently associated with HLA-B27 positivity, elevated ESR, and neck involvement, whereas nr-axSpA more often involved shoulder symptoms and peripheral joint involvement. The nominal unadjusted gender difference in diagnostic delay did not persist after adjustment for radiographic subtype, suggesting that subtype partly explained the observed difference. Delayed diagnosis may reflect non-classical presentations and pathway-level barriers, including external referral routes, transfer of clinical information, imaging access, and timely specialist review. Earlier recognition across primary care and non-rheumatology specialities, supported by structured referral criteria, minimum referral datasets, shared imaging pathways, and streamlined external referral processes, may improve care for Malaysian patients with axSpA.
