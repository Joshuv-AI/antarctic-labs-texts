The unexpected impact of the second year of Explo’Santé, a school-based health education programme on life skills development among 5th grade pupils in France: an interventional cohort study
Authors: Corélie Salque, Adeline Darlington-Bernard, Florence Carrouel, Emily Darlington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth inequalities emerge during childhood, shaped by both structural and behavioural determinants, often leading to long-term disadvantages in global health. Schools represent a strategic setting for early and equitable health promotion interventions. This study aimed to assess the impact of the second year of Explo’Santé, a school-based health education programme designed to enhance life skills (LS), among 5th grade pupils.MethodsThis cohort study, embedded within the Explo’Santé interventional research protocol, involved 604 fifth-grade pupils from 43 French primary schools across six districts. A 10-session health education programme, “My day-to-day health in my environment,” was delivered by teachers and French League Against Cancer prevention officers, combining theoretical input with interactive activities. Differences in LS, self-efficacy (SE), life satisfaction (LSa), and health literacy (HL) scores between pre- and post-intervention were assessed using validated French scales. Data were analysed in RStudio using linear mixed-effects models to account for hierarchical structure of the data assessed score variation across time and interactions with gender and district.ResultsLSa declined significantly post-intervention (p = 0.04), particularly in school-related LSa (–2.47, p = 0.04). No significant results were observed for LS, SE or HL. No significant gender × time effects were found, though girls had higher baseline LS (p < 0.001) and SE in French (p < 0.001), but significantly lower in Maths SE (p = 0.01) and self-love (p = 0.01). Notable district disparities were also observed, as well as variations at school- and class-level.ConclusionsThese results underscore the challenges of achieving short-term impact from limited-duration programmes. Thus, to fully assess effectiveness, future research should incorporate longitudinal, mixed-method designs capable of capturing delayed and context-dependent changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27223-5.

Introduction

Worldwide, large health inequalities observed among children and adolescents represent a burden on public health [1]. These inequalities are associated, in Europe at least, with different social determinants of health such as income, education, housing and access to services [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), these inequalities can manifest early in life and lead to lifelong disadvantages, including an increased risk of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), poor mental health and less satisfaction with life [3]. Over 165 million youths are affected by mental disorders worldwide [4] and nearly one third of all young people are projected to be overweight or obese by 2050 [5].

According to several studies, children living in adverse socio-economic conditions are more at risk of exposure to stress, low education, and lack of access to health information and services [6, 7]. Therefore, interventions targeting both structural and behavioural determinants of health and promote equity at an early age are essential [8].

Thus, the school environment appears to be ideal for implementing public health interventions and early, sustainable and contextually sensitive health promotion for all children [9]. Health promotion interventions in schools improve psychosocial and behavioural outcomes in pupils [10, 11]. The concepts of life skills (LS) and health literacy (HL) are central to these interventions [12, 13]. WHO defines LS as a set of psychosocial skills that enable individuals to cope effectively with the demands and challenges of everyday life, and include critical thinking, emotional regulation, interpersonal communication and decision-making [14]. HL refers to the ability to access, understand, assess and apply health information for appropriate decision-making [15].

Higher HL and LS are strongly associated with better self-efficacy (SE), mental well-being, and life satisfaction (LSa) [16, 17]. The integration of HL and LS into school curricula can also lead to long-term benefits, including reduced substance abuse, improved mental health outcomes, and enhanced social integration [16].

However, few studies have examined the joint evolution of LS, HL, SE, and LSa within real-world, large-scale school interventions, and they rarely consider contextual factors such as gender, school, class, or district, that may significantly modulate programme efficacy [7]. Indeed, Gunawardena et al. (2023) highlight the importance of adapting interventions to context, as programme effectiveness varies according to local implementation dynamics [17]. Variables such as school culture, teacher training, parental involvement and neighbourhood safety influence how pupils understand and apply health messages and behavioural strategies. It is therefore crucial to consider these contextual dimensions in order to increase the reach and equity of school curricula [17], and the impact of these interventions at population level. They should also be integrated into institutional policy, teacher training programmes, and national education strategies [18] to ensure sustainability [19, 20].

Thus, Explo’Santé is a school programme inspired by the Health Promoting Schools (HPS) framework, which supports a holistic approach to school, integrating health education into the curriculum, creating a supportive physical and social environment, and actively involving families and communities [21]. It was implemented in 43 primary schools in France. This programme includes 10 health education sessions per school year focused on the development of LS. In addition, the programme encourages teacher, school staff and family involvement to create a healthy school environment.

This study analysed the second year of Explo’Santé, aimed at 5th grade pupils. The primary objective was to assess programme impact on LS, HL, SE, and LSa using validated instruments. The secondary objective was to analyse how contextual variables (gender, school, class, and district) influence these outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational cohort study was part of the Explo’Santé interventional research protocol [22] and followed the STROBE guidelines for cohort studies (Supplementary Table 1). The protocol was approved by the University of Lyon ethics committee (France) on March 1, 2023 (n°2023-01-12-004) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from parents of participating pupils, and verbal assent was given by pupils prior to data collection.

Setting

The study involved 604 pupils (5th grade), from 55 classes from 43 French primary schools. Schools were distributed across 34 towns in 6 administrative districts: 4 towns in District B, 11 in District C, 6 in District A, 11 in District D, 1 in District E and 1 in District F. The distribution of pupils and classes across schools is provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Each school volunteered to take part in the research protocol, after the Explo’Santé project was introduced by local committees of the French League Against Cancer one year prior. This approach was meant to encourage engagement, boost motivation, and maintain participation over the three-year study period [23]. In each town, all participating primary and secondary schools are from the same catchment area. Pupils were recruited in 4th grade, between March 1 and March 13, 2023. In each participating school, the intervention was delivered to all 4th grade classes, and 5th-grade classes the following year. Since pupils generally remain in the same school when progressing from 4th to 5th grade, the large majority of pupils receiving the programme in 5th grade were the same individuals recruited the previous year. Only pupils who changed school did not continue the intervention. The programme therefore followed the pupils’ progression within the same school, resulting in a predominantly longitudinal cohort.

Participants

To be included in the study, pupils had to be (i) in the 5th grade, (ii) enrolled in a school participating in the Explo’Santé programme during the 2023–2024 school year, (iii) fluent in French, (iv) with written parental consent, and (v) to have given oral consent before answering the questionnaires [22].

No exclusion criteria were applied. In particular, pupils with special educational needs (e.g., neurodevelopmental disorders, learning difficulties, or chronic health conditions) were not excluded and were able to participate with the support of their allocated classroom assistant (AESH).

Intervention

A 10-session health education module entitled “My day-to-day health in my environment” (Supplementary Table 2) was developed by the French League Against Cancer. The targeted skills and pedagogical objectives of the intervention were defined by the French League Against Cancer prior to implementation, based on national health promotion guidelines and evidence-based frameworks for LS education [24], and validated internally by the French League Against Cancer. Each session combined a brief theoretical input and activities (games, role-plays, and group discussions) that were predetermined and standardised across classes using a detailed session manual, ensuring consistent delivery while allowing minimal adjustments to local classroom dynamics [22]. The programme followed a structured progression addressing communication skills, self-awareness, critical thinking, responsible decision-making, conflict resolution and emotional coping (see Supplementary Table 2 for full details).

The 10 sessions lasted 60 min each and were delivered once a week throughout the school year, excluding school breaks. They were co-led by classroom teachers and prevention officers from the French League Against Cancer. Teachers were actively involved in facilitating discussions, supporting group work and guiding pupils during activities, while prevention officers provided health promotion expertise and led the structured sessions. The intervention began shortly after the pre-intervention data collection, which took place in November 2023, and continued until spring 2024. Post-intervention data collection was conducted between March and May 2024, after completion of the 10th session.

As the programme did not target discrete, testable behaviours, but aimed to foster a global health-promotion mindset in the school environment, the evaluation focused on broad psychosocial outcomes rather than on direct assessment of session-specific content.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to assess changes in the LS, SE, LSa and HL scores of pupils between the start (pre) and the end (post) of the second school year of implementation of the programme. Secondary outcomes were to explore associations between the LS, SE, LSa and HL scores and potential influencing factors, such as gender, class, school or district.

Variables

The outcomes were assessed through the scores of four French validated scales: the LS scale (30 questions, 5-item Likert scale, range up to 120) assessing social, emotional and cognitive LS [25–27], the SE perception at school scale (17 questions, 6-item Likert scale, range up to 85) assessing SE at school, in French and in Maths [28], the LSa scale (30 questions, 4-item Likert scale, range up to 90), assessing pupils’ subjective wellbeing at school, at home, in their family, with their friends, and with themselves [29], and the HL scale (10 questions, 4-item Likert scale, range up to 30), assessing global HL in pupils [30]. Gender and district were included as covariates, while class and school were modelled as random effects to account for the hierarchical structure of the data.

Data collection and processing

Data were anonymously collected by three researchers. The questionnaires were proposed on tablets to all participants who gave explicit oral consent. Assessments were conducted during the 2023–2024 academic year, pre-intervention (November 2023) and post-intervention (March to May 2024). Intervention sessions and data collection necessarily followed the school calendar, and thus all post-intervention assessments were conducted after the completion of the 10th session, but not necessarily after a standardized time. Only pupils who filled in the questionnaires pre- and post-intervention were included in the analysis.

Data processing was carried out using RStudio (version 2023.06.1 Build 524). All pupils present in class at data collection were invited to participate. Non-participation resulted from absence or direct refusal, and no additional sociodemographic or academic information was available for these pupils, preventing comparative analyses between participants and non-participants.

All scores were normalised on a 100 scale for comparability. Pupil identification codes were stored separately from all personal data. The correspondence table, which linked each identifier to the pupil’s name, was maintained as a dedicated, password-protected database and kept in an encrypted file accessible only to the data protection officer, thereby ensuring strict compliance with confidentiality requirements.

Sample size

The sample size calculation was based on the proportion of pupils expected to present satisfactory life skills at baseline (binary indicator). As no prior data were available for this population, we assumed a conservative prevalence of 50%, which maximises variance in Cochran’s formula. The objective was not to detect a predefined change over time but to ensure adequate precision in estimating the baseline prevalence of satisfactory LS. Using a 95% confidence level and a ± 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was 385 pupils.

Statistical methods

Descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted to examine changes in LS, SE, LSa, and HL scores pre-and post-intervention. The distribution and evolution over time of each score and its sub-scores were explored using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). To take the hierarchical structure of the data into account, random effects for class and school levels were added to the LMMs. Fixed effects included time (pre- vs. post-), gender, and district. District was treated as a fixed effect due to the small number of units (n = 6), enabling direct estimation and comparison of territorial effects. Differential effects of gender and district on programme impact were also explored through interaction models (Time × Gender and Time × District). Significance was assessed using Satterthwaite’s approximation.

Boxplots were used to illustrate each scale and sub-scale evolution over time and gender-based differences in mean score evolution. Forest plots display district-level estimates and their 95% confidence intervals, with District A chosen as the district of reference since it has the most participants. Heatmaps were used to represent the variability between the observations and the values predicted by the model (residuals) at class and school levels. All analyses were performed in RStudio (version 2023.06.1) using the lme4 package.

Results

Study population

The analysis included 604 pupils in the 5th grade (aged 9–11)(Fig. 1), 54.30% of whom were girls. Their schools were located in six French districts: District A (25.66%), District B (10.43%), District C (9.77%), District D (20.03%), District E (19.54%) and District F (14.57%). The exact number of pupils for each district is detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Gender effects on programme impact

Overall, our findings show no statistically significant gender effects on score evolution pre- and post- intervention (Fig. 3).

Gender means score evolution in life skills, self efficacy, life satisfaction and health literacy pre-intervention (T2) and post-intervention (T3)

For LS, pre-intervention, girls achieved significantly higher scores than boys on average (+ 3.38, p < 0.001), mainly driven by social LS (+ 5.50, p < 0.001) and cognitive LS (+ 2.80, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 4).

For SE, pre-intervention, girls scored significantly higher in French on average (+ 5.76, p < 0.001), but significantly lower in Maths on average (-3.04, p = 0.01) than boys (Supplementary Table 4).

For HL, no significant differences were observed between boys and girls (p = 0.10).

For LSa, pre-intervention, girls showed significantly higher scores in LSa related to school (+ 8.70, p < 0.001) but scored significantly lower on self-love (–3.15, p = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 4).

District, school and class effects on programme impact

A substantial heterogeneity between districts was observed (Fig. 4). District B had significantly lower mean LS (β =–4.16, p = 0.05), SE (β =–6.21, p = 0.03) and HL (β =–5.53, p = 0.02) scores than District A. SE (β =–7.28, p = 0.01) and HL (β =–7.10, p < 0.01) scores in District F, and HL (β =–5.08, p = 0.028) scores in District C, were also significantly lower. District E had higher mean scores than District A in LS (β =+0.90, p = 0.61), SE (β =+0.23, p = 0.92) and LSa (β =+2.74, p = 0.18).

Regarding sub-scales, lower scores in general SE were observed in District B (β =–7.01, p = 0.03), District C (β =–6.02, p = 0.03), and District F (β =–8.98, p < 0.01) compared to District A. In the self-love dimension, pupils from District C showed significantly lower scores (β =–11.25, p < 0.01), and those from District F trended similarly (β =–8.54, p = 0.06). District E had significantly higher scores in cognitive LS (β =+4.28, p = 0.04) and LSa related to family (β =+6.71, p = 0.03).

The disparities observed between districts were also found between schools for all outcomes, and between classes specifically for SE and LSa (Fig. 5). For instance, in District C, one school had an LS mean score 14.57 points below the expected value, while another scored 14.24 points above. Differences also appeared within schools: in District A, the LS mean score in one school was slightly above expectations (+ 1.11) (see Supplementary Table 5 for more details), but at class level, variations ranged from − 5.85 to + 5.98.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of the second year of the Explo’Santé programme on LS, SE, LSa, and HL among French 5th-grade pupils. Both increases and decreases in scores were observed across all outcomes (LS, SE, LSa and HL), with a statistically significant decrease in LSa driven by the school aspect. Moreover, our results suggested that district, school and class impact the effects of the programme.

Several hypotheses may explain our findings. Programme intensity and duration might have limited its impact. Indeed, Explo’Santé delivered only ten 75-minute sessions per year, whereas according to Lamboy et al., more than 20 h a year are required to maintain durable benefits [31]. For instance, the Zippy’s friends programme, implemented in Europe, provides 24 weekly sessions, with a significant positive impact on emotional skills [32, 33]. However, Explo’Santé was not designed solely as a sequence of ten sessions, but rather as a catalyst for a broader health-promoting school approach. In this perspective, the classroom sessions constitute one component among others intended to encourage daily practices, teacher–pupil dialogue, and the integration of psychosocial skills into routine pedagogy. While more intensive programmes often demonstrate stronger effects [31], the current intervention aims to initiate, rather than replace, a whole-school developmental process. Nevertheless, reinforcing the programme through additional teacher-led sessions or extended implementation represents a promising direction for strengthening skill acquisition and sustainability, and should be investigated in future iterations of the programme. In addition, extensive teacher support has been identified as a key promoting factor towards programme efficiency [34]. In the case of Explo’Santé, the duration of the training received by teachers ranged between 0.5 and 1.5 days [22], which may have been too brief for effective integration into classroom practices. This variability largely reflected real-world organisational constraints, as participation in the training was subject to validation from the local educational district academy, and to its capacity to find substitute teachers for more than half a day. This is consistent with evidence that teacher engagement and pedagogical alignment are critical factors in programme success [24].

A significant decrease in school-related LSa is observed. Mixed and negative short- and long-term effects were also documented in other behavioural and LS interventions [35–37]. These findings could be explained by a variety of factors including misalignment between pedagogical strategies and children’s developmental readiness [36], teacher stress and time constraints [36] or even study design [38]. Thus, these findings confirm the importance of tailoring interventions not only to age but also to cognitive and emotional maturity [24, 31, 36] and of taking into account broader contextual factors [19, 22, 23].

In addition to programme content, measurement issues may have contributed to the lack of significant findings. LS and related psychosocial constructs remain inconsistently defined and operationalised in the literature [12, 14, 39]. It is important to note that Explo’Santé does not aim to teach specific, testable skills that could be directly assessed. Instead, it seeks to initiate a broader health-promotion process within the school environment, fostering communication, emotional regulation, decision-making and reflective thinking through experiential activities. Accordingly, the instruments used in this study were chosen to capture these broader psychosocial domains rather than the specific content of individual sessions. Despite the use of validated instruments, the tools employed may lack sensitivity to detect subtle, short-term changes, especially when change is diffuse, context-dependent, or cumulative [40, 41]. Indeed, many LS (problem-solving, emotional regulation, or decision-making) develop gradually over time, often requiring longitudinal exposure and reinforcement to consolidate [14, 42, 43]. Although short-term effects could have been expected immediately post-intervention, the absence of significant change suggests that the intervention may require a longer maturation period before measurable effects can be detected [44, 45].

Our study focused on the individual level. Although it modelled class and school clustering, the broader school environment, including teacher–pupil relationships, peer dynamics, and leadership culture, was not directly assessed. Yet, several studies suggest that these structural and relational dimensions mediate how pupils receive and engage with LS content [33, 38]. Furthermore, family involvement, identified as a crucial component in sustaining school-based health promotion, was limited in this programme. Although families received an information letter at the beginning of the school year and pupils were given short take-home activities designed to be completed with a family member, schools reported low engagement with these materials and difficulties maintaining contact with parents over the year. This minimal participation likely reduced opportunities for reinforcing programme messages outside the school setting [33, 46].

Despite these constraints, some differentiated patterns emerged. The higher scores achieved by girls compared to boys, particularly in LS and LSa, are similar to other such findings as Vicary et al.‘s evaluation of Botvin’s LS Training (2006) [47], where girls achieved higher scores than boys coping with anxiety and communication skills. Although these gendered differences did not translate into differential progress over time in our study, they suggest the need for gender-sensitive programme design and evaluation [48].

Pupils in certain districts, particularly District B and District C, reported significantly lower SE and HL scores pre-intervention than those in District A, even after adjusting for clustering. These contrasts align with the marked heterogeneity across the six participating districts. According to the INSEE database (the French national institute for statistics and economic studies) [49], the districts differed substantially in their socioeconomic environments, with population densities ranging from sparsely populated rural areas (8–25 inhabitants/km²) to dense urban centres (up to 1,600 inhabitants/km²). Median household income varied between €18,000 and €24,000, and unemployment rates ranged from 4% to 17%. Such disparities are consistent with the baseline variations observed across districts, where lower SE and HL scores tended to appear in more rural and economically constrained areas (e.g., B, C), compared with more urbanised and economically advantaged districts (e.g., E, F). Disparities associated with school and class were also observed. These differences were visible across multiple outcomes: for example, some schools showed average LS or SE scores substantially above the expected values, while others, sometimes within the same district, displayed lower-than-expected performances. Variations were also present within schools: in several cases, one class showed improvements post-intervention while another class in the same school showed stagnation or decline. Such heterogeneity suggests that factors beyond the intervention itself likely influenced pupils’ trajectories. This aligns with research showing that school culture, teacher commitment, resource availability, local governance structures and family involvement all shape the effectiveness of health education programmes [23, 34]. Successful implementation often depends less on strict adherence to a standardised protocol than on adaptability and local ownership [23, 34]. A more detailed multilevel exploration of these factors, using indicators such as the school-level Socioeconomic Status Index, will be undertaken in future longitudinal analyses.

The literature identifies that evaluating LS remains both complex and non-consensual. For example, Durlak et al. (2011) and Taylor et al. (2017) have highlighted that many social and emotional competencies develop progressively and often require contextual or qualitative insights to be meaningfully assessed [44, 45]. Similarly, several studies identified a twofold research gap in relation to LS evaluation. First, the lack of a clear conceptual definition: if LS are not clearly defined, their evaluation appears challenging [12, 40]. Secondly, the design of qualitative studies and standardised quantitative instruments developed specifically to assess these constructs in youth beyond pupils’ self-reports remains scarce [14, 40, 41]. Thus, a mixed-method design combining quantitative tracking with ethnographic or participatory approaches could offer a more comprehensive understanding of what changes, for whom, and under what conditions. Such approaches are also more likely to capture the formation of enabling environments, those that foster emotional safety, agency, and engagement, especially at local and community level, which are increasingly seen as critical to the success of school-based health promotion [14, 19, 23, 44].

This study presents several strengths. First of all, it offers a comprehensive assessment of four interrelated psychosocial domains (LS, HL, SE, and LSa) rather than focusing on a single outcome, which better reflects the complexity of child wellbeing. Secondly, the study includes a large sample drawn from multiple districts and schools, providing variability across educational contexts. Although the sample is not intended to be nationally representative, this heterogeneity helps capture differences across settings. Additionally, the use of linear mixed-effects modelling appropriately accounted for hierarchical data structures [50]. However, the study also has limitations. First, the reliance on self-reported data may have introduced social bias and is also questioned regarding how participants evaluate their own skills [41]. Secondly, the solely quantitative approach constrains understanding of the contextual mechanisms at play. Thirdly, no control group was established for ethical and practical reasons, as all participating schools required access to the programme. This design constraint limits causal inference: observed changes cannot be unequivocally attributed to the intervention and may also reflect natural developmental trajectories or other external influences. Within-subject designs are nevertheless well suited to the study of psychosocial outcomes, as they minimise inter-individual variability and enhance sensitivity to change [51].

In conclusion, this study aimed to assess the impact on LS, SE, LSa, and HL of the second year of the Explo’Santé programme, a school-based health education programme targeting psychosocial outcomes, on French 5th-grade pupils. Our findings identified limited effects. However, not all changes can be captured through quantitative measures alone. A complementary qualitative approach, sensitive to local school environments, relational dynamics, and pupils’ lived experiences, may be necessary to fully understand programme impact. With stronger integration, extended follow-up, and mixed-method evaluation, interventions like Explo’Santé have the potential to support child wellbeing and reduce health inequalities from an early age.
