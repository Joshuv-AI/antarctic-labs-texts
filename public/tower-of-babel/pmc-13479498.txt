AARS1-mediated lactylation reprograms macrophage lipid metabolism to restrict antitumor immunity
Authors: Zhaofeng Xiao, Lijun Meng, Shengjun Xu, Nan Xu, Ye Li, Jianguo Wang, Jinyi Tong, Hongda Ding, Xiaodong Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-derived lactate has long been regarded as a metabolic waste product. However, accumulating evidence indicates that lactate also functions as a signaling molecule that actively remodels the tumor immune microenvironment. How lactate-driven post-translational modifications in immune cells contribute to immune evasion in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify the immune cell population responsible for lactylation-driven immunosuppression in HCC and to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which lactylation rewires macrophage metabolism to impair CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity.MethodsSelective in vivo immune cell depletion models were employed to define the key immune mediators of lactate-induced immunosuppression. Proteomic screening, site-directed mutagenesis, and lipidomic profiling were used to characterize lactylation targets and lipid metabolic alterations. Functional assays, including signaling pathway analyses, cytokine measurements, and tumor immune profiling, were performed in both in vitro systems and mouse HCC models.ResultsMacrophages were identified as the principal immune cell type mediating lactylation-dependent immunosuppression in HCC. Alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) functioned as a non-canonical lactyltransferase, catalyzing lactylation of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A at lysine 675. This modification impaired long-chain fatty acid transport into mitochondria, leading to cytosolic accumulation of oleic acid (OA). OA directly disrupted cGAS binding to cytosolic DNA, thereby suppressing STING activation and type I interferon (IFN-I) production. Attenuated IFN-I signaling resulted in reduced major histocompatibility complex-I expression on tumor cells and impaired CD8+ T cell-mediated recognition and cytotoxicity.ConclusionThese findings uncover a lactate-lipid metabolism axis that links tumor-derived lactate to innate immune suppression in HCC. Targeting AARS1-mediated lactylation represents a potential therapeutic strategy to restore macrophage immunostimulatory function and enhance antitumor immunity.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Tumor-derived lactate is increasingly recognized as an active regulator of the tumor immune microenvironment rather than a simple metabolic byproduct. Previous studies have shown that lactate can induce lysine lactylation and promote immunosuppressive phenotypes in macrophages, but the key immune cell target, the relevant non-histone substrate, and the downstream mechanism linking lactylation to impaired antitumor immunity in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remained unclear.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study identifies macrophages as the principal immune cell population mediating lactate-driven immunosuppression in HCC. Our findings indicate that alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) functions as a non-canonical lactyltransferase to catalyze carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) lactylation at lysine-to-arginine substitution at residue 675 (K675), thereby impairing long-chain fatty acid transport and metabolism. This process leads to cytosolic oleic acid (OA) accumulation, suppresses cGAS-STING-mediated interferon-I signaling, and ultimately weakens tumor antigen presentation and CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity.

These findings define an AARS1-CPT1A-OA-cGAS axis as a previously unrecognized immunometabolic checkpoint in HCC. The study provides a mechanistic basis for targeting AARS1-mediated lactylation, restoring macrophage innate immune function, or combining this strategy with immune checkpoint blockade to improve antitumor responses. It also broadens the conceptual framework linking lactylation modification, lipid rewiring, and innate immune suppression in HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with limited treatment options and poor prognosis.1 Although immune checkpoint blockade has improved outcomes in a subset of patients, the majority fail to achieve durable responses.2 Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), a dominant myeloid population in HCC, play multifaceted roles in shaping tumor immunity. TAMs can promote immune evasion by suppressing cytotoxic T-cell function, secreting immunoregulatory cytokines, and recruiting immunosuppressive cell populations.3 Meanwhile, intact innate immune signaling in TAMs is essential for initiating and sustaining antitumor immunity within the tumor microenvironment (TME).4 Among these pathways, the cGAS-STING axis serves as a canonical cytosolic DNA-sensing mechanism.5 On recognition of aberrant cytosolic DNA, cGAS catalyzes the production of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which binds to STING and promotes STING oligomerization, TBK1 recruitment, and IRF3 phosphorylation, ultimately inducing type I interferons (IFN-I) and IFN-stimulated genes.6 IFN-I signaling enhances antitumor immune surveillance by promoting antigen presentation, activating dendritic cells and macrophages, and supporting cytotoxic T-cell responses. Conversely, suppression of IFN-I signaling contributes to immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy.7 Therefore, targeting TAMs and identifying key pathways that govern their immunometabolic reprogramming may provide a promising avenue for improving immunotherapeutic responses in patients with HCC.

Tumor-derived metabolites have emerged as key drivers of immunometabolic reprogramming in immune cells within the TME.8 9 Lactate, a hallmark of aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells, accumulates to high levels in solid tumors and profoundly shapes immune cell behavior.10 Beyond serving as a metabolic substrate, lactate can induce lysine lactylation, a recently identified post-translational modification.11 Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are traditionally recognized as housekeeping enzymes that charge amino acids onto their cognate tRNAs to maintain translational fidelity.12 Beyond this canonical function, alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) has recently been identified as a lactyltransferase that catalyzes TP53 lactylation in breast cancer cells, thereby impairing DNA repair and promoting tumorigenesis.13 14 These findings reveal a non-canonical role for AARS1 in protein lactylation and raise the possibility that AARS1-mediated modification of non-histone metabolic proteins may reprogram immune cell function within the TME. Lipid metabolic reprogramming is also a major determinant of TAM phenotype and function.15 Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), the rate-limiting enzyme that transports long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation, is central to macrophage lipid metabolism.16 Although lactylation modification and lipid metabolic reprogramming have largely been investigated as distinct processes, their functional integration in TAMs remains incompletely understood.

In this study, by integrating immune cell depletion strategies, flow cytometric analyses, and in vivo HCC models, we identified macrophages as the principal mediators of lactate-driven immunosuppression. We further demonstrated that AARS1-mediated lactylation of CPT1A rewires macrophage lipid metabolism and suppresses IFN-I signaling in TAMs. This convergence of post-translational modification and metabolic reprogramming ultimately promotes immune evasion and accelerates HCC progression.

Materials and methods

Mouse strains

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were used in this study. Aars1fl/+ mice (NO.TO22801) and Csf1r-cre+ mice (NO. T005640) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Macrophages-specific AARS1 knockdown C57BL/6J mice (Csf1r-cre+Aars1fl/+) were generated by mating Aars1fl/+ mice with Csf1r-cre+ transgenic mice. All the mice were housed at the Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals (Hangzhou, China) in a specific pathogen-free facility.

Human HCC samples

Human HCC tissue was obtained from Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University between 2020 and 2026. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0 and R (V.4.2.1), unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical details are provided in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001; ns indicates no significance. Detailed materials and methods are described in the online supplemental materials.

Results

High lactylation predicts poor prognosis and immunosuppressive remodeling in HCC

Analysis of liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) showed that patients with higher transcriptome-derived lactate-associated signature scores, calculated based on the HP_INCREASED_SERUM_LACTATE gene set, tended to have poorer overall survival (figure 2A). Consistently, these lactate-associated signature scores were significantly lower in patients with stage I tumors than in those with more advanced-stage tumors (figure 2B), suggesting a potential association between lactate-related transcriptional activity and HCC progression. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) enrichment analysis of genes significantly correlated with the lactate-associated signature score in the integrated and batch-corrected TCGA-LIHC and ICGC-LIHC cohort showed that negatively correlated genes were enriched in inflammatory response and fatty acid metabolism pathways, whereas positively correlated genes were enriched in epithelial-mesenchymal transition and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways (figure 2C and online supplemental online supplemental figure 1A,B). These findings suggest that elevated lactate-related transcriptional activity is associated with an immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting transcriptional landscape in HCC. To further delineate the impact of lactate on the HCC immune microenvironment, we analyzed published spatial transcriptomic dataset17 and observed that tumor regions with higher lactate-associated signature scores exhibited increased TAMs infiltration accompanied by reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells, including cytotoxic subsets (figure 2D–F). Consistently, CIBERSORTx18 analysis of TCGA cohort showed a positive correlation between lactate-associated signature scores and M2 macrophage abundance (online supplemental figure 1C and online supplemental table 1).

To validate these findings in clinical samples and further define the spatial organization of the immunosuppressive microenvironment, we measured intratumoral lactate levels of 60 patients with HCC and stratified the cohort into lactate-high and lactate-low groups using the median lactate level as the cut-off. We then evaluated lactylation modification levels in clinical HCC specimens by multiplex immunohistochemistry staining for pan-lysine lactylation (Pan-Kla), which detects global lysine lactylation, together with CD68, CD4, CD8, and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing molecule 3 (TIM3). Stained images were analyzed using QuPath and CytoMAP19 with artificial neural network-based channel classifiers trained on cell-level features to identify immune cell subsets and extract their spatial coordinates (figure 2G). Macrophages were further classified into lactylation-high and lactylation-low macrophages according to their Pan-Kla fluorescence intensity, using the mean Pan-Kla intensity of macrophages as the cut-off. Quantitative analysis showed that lactate-high tumors contained increased proportions of CD4+ T cells and macrophages, but reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 2H). Moreover, TIM3-based analysis revealed a significantly higher proportion of TIM3+CD8+ T cells in lactate-high tumors, whereas TIM3+CD4+ T cells showed only a modest increase (figure 2). We next quantified the precise spatial proximity and local cell-to-cell interactions between lactylation-high macrophages and exhausted CD8+ T cells. Nearest-neighbor distance analysis showed that, compared with lactylation-low macrophages, CD8+ T cells spatially surrounding lactylation-high macrophages exhibited a stronger tendency toward a TIM3+ exhausted phenotype (figure 2J). To further assess local macrophage-CD8+ T-cell interactions, we defined a 50 µm radius neighborhood around each macrophage and calculated the proportion of TIM3+CD8+ T cells among total CD8+ T cells within each neighborhood. This neighborhood-based interaction analysis showed that regions surrounding lactylation-high macrophages contained a significantly higher proportion of TIM3+CD8+ T cells than regions surrounding lactylation-low macrophages (figure 2K). Clinically, patients with lactate-high tumors exhibited significantly worse overall survival than those with lactate-low tumors (figure 2L). Together, these results suggest that elevated lactate levels and lactylation in HCC are associated with macrophage accumulation and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Moreover, lactylation-high macrophages may contribute to the formation of a localized immunosuppressive niche that favors exhaustion of surrounding CD8+ T cells.

Lactate drives macrophage reprogramming to promote tumor progression

Colony formation and invasion assays demonstrated that sodium lactate, the basic form of lactate, had no significant effect on the proliferative or invasive capacity of tumor cells (online supplemental figure 1D,E). To investigate the impact of lactate on immune cells within the TME, we established an orthotopic Hep53.4-luc HCC model and treated mice with sodium lactate or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), together with selective depletion of macrophages, T cells, natural killer (NK)/NK T cell (NKT) cells, B cells, or monocytes using specific depletion antibodies (figure 3A). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the anti-IgG control groups showed that macrophage infiltration was markedly increased after sodium lactate treatment (figure 3B and online supplemental figure 1F). Bioluminescence imaging and quantitative region of interest analysis showed that sodium lactate markedly accelerated orthotopic tumor growth compared with PBS treatment. Notably, among the different immune cell depletion conditions, only macrophage depletion substantially abrogated the tumor-promoting effect of sodium lactate (figure 3C). Moreover, lactylation levels were significantly higher in macrophages from tumor than in macrophages from peripheral blood or normal liver tissues (figure 3D,E). These findings indicate that macrophages are the primary target cells mediating the protumorigenic effects of lactate.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we performed transcriptomic profiling of macrophages isolated from normal liver, PBS-treated tumors, and sodium lactate-treated tumors. Differential expression analysis showed that sodium lactate induced broad transcriptional remodeling of macrophages, affecting multiple immune-related and metabolism-related programs, including, among others, T-cell activation, ERK1/2 signaling, innate immune activation, myeloid leukocyte activation, and lipid catabolic processes (figure 3F, online supplemental figure 2F). In parallel, sodium lactate increased the expression of immunosuppressive molecules, including CD55, CD274, and VTCN1, suggesting a shift toward an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype.20–22 Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further revealed that innate immune activation and lipid catabolic processes were significantly suppressed in sodium lactate-treated macrophages compared with the other groups (figure 3G). Among these altered programs, the T-cell activation module was particularly relevant, as CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity represents a central effector mechanism of antitumor immunity. Its suppression was consistent with the reduced intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration observed in sodium lactate-treated tumors, linking lactate-induced macrophage reprogramming to impaired antitumor adaptive immunity. Meanwhile, alterations in multiple pathways, such as innate immune activation, myeloid cell activation, and lipid metabolism, suggest the existence of potential intermediate regulatory nodes through which lactylation reshapes macrophage phenotypes, thereby contributing to macrophage-mediated suppression of T-cell activation and tumor progression.

Macrophage-specific AARS1 knockdown restrains tumor progression and enhances CD8+ T-cell immunity

To investigate the mechanisms by which lactylation in macrophages promotes tumor progression, we first stratified patients according to their M2 macrophage infiltration levels in the TCGA liver cancer dataset and performed differential expression analysis (online supplemental figure 2A). Subsequently, weighted gene expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed,23 and a brown module was identified that was highly correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration (online supplemental figure 2B–D). Among the four overlapping genes (AARS1, SDHA, PDHA1, and GYS2) identified from the intersection of WGCNA, differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and lactylation-related genes (online supplemental figure 2E), AARS1 exhibited the highest correlation with M2 macrophage infiltration and lactylation-related signaling. Given its known enzymatic potential to catalyze lysine lactylation,14 AARS1 was prioritized for further investigation as a potential macrophage lactyltransferase.

To validate the functional role of AARS1 in TAMs, we generated macrophage-specific AARS1-knockdown mice (Csf1r-cre+Aars1fl/+, CKO) and established both hydrodynamic tail vein injection-induced primary HCC models and Hep53.4-luc orthotopic transplantation HCC models. Compared with control mice (Aars1fl/+, loxp), CKO showed significantly slower tumor progression (figure 4A,B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the overall proportion of intratumoral macrophages remained unchanged in CKO tumors (online supplemental figure 3A). However, these macrophages displayed a marked phenotypic shift, characterized by reduced CD206 and PD-L1 expression (figure 4C,D) and increased expression of CD80, CD86, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I (online supplemental figure 3B–D), suggesting attenuated immunosuppressive features and enhanced antigen-presenting potential. In addition to macrophage phenotypic remodeling, CD8+ T-cell infiltration was markedly increased in CKO tumors (figure 4E). Given that programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) expression on CD8+ T cells can be associated with either activation or dysfunction depending on the biological context, we further evaluated TIM3 expression together with functional readouts, including granzyme B (GZMB) and IFN-γ production. Compared with those from loxp tumors, CD8+ T cells from CKO tumors showed reduced PD-1 and TIM3 expression (figure 4F,G). Importantly, this reduction was accompanied by enhanced GZMB and IFN-γ production (figure 4H), indicating that the decrease in PD-1 expression in CKO tumors did not reflect impaired T-cell activation. Instead, together with reduced TIM3 expression and improved effector function, these findings are more consistent with attenuation of exhaustion-associated inhibitory signaling and restoration of antitumor CD8+ T-cell activity. In contrast, the infiltration of CD4+ T cells, regulatory T cells, NK/NKT cells, B cells, and dendritic cells showed no significant changes (online supplemental figure 3E–I).

Analysis of the TCGA dataset further confirmed that AARS1 expression in tumor-infiltrating macrophages was significantly higher than in adjacent liver tissue and increased with tumor stage (online supplemental figure 3J,K). Patients with elevated macrophage AARS1 expression exhibited poorer survival outcomes (online supplemental figure 3L). CIBERSORTx analysis revealed that tumors with high AARS1 expression contained more M2-like macrophages and fewer CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure 3M). Furthermore, CellChat24 network analysis revealed enhanced intercellular communication among tumor cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages in the AARS1-low macrophage group (figure 4). Ligand-receptor analysis revealed that macrophages with low AARS1 expression had enhanced interactions with other cells via IFN-I (figure 4J,K). These findings demonstrate that AARS1 expression in macrophages promotes an immunosuppressive phenotype and suppresses CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity.

AARS1 suppresses IFN-I signaling and limits CD8+ T-cell immunity

Given the critical role of AARS1 in macrophages, we performed RNA sequencing of peritoneal macrophages from loxp and CKO mice before and after IFN-γ stimulation. DEGs were clustered into four groups based on expression patterns and subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis. Notably, clusters 3 and 4 were upregulated on AARS1 knockdown, and these genes were enriched in pathways related to the regulation of IFN-I production, activation of innate immune responses, and regulation of T cell-mediated immunity. In contrast, cluster 1 genes associated with extracellular matrix organization and ameboidal-type cell migration were markedly downregulated (figure 5A). GO enrichment analysis further highlighted that the most prominent changes occurred in pathways regulating innate immune responses and IFN-I production (figure 5B). To determine how AARS1 affects the cytokine profile of macrophages in the TME, we sorted intratumoral macrophages from loxp and CKO tumors for multiplex cytokine analysis. Among the detected cytokines, IFN-β secretion was markedly increased in CKO macrophages (figure 5C). Consistently, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis further confirmed that Ifnb1, which encodes IFN-β and activates the IFNAR1/IFNAR2 receptor complex, was significantly upregulated in CKO macrophages (online supplemental figure 4A). Considering the pivotal role of IFN-I mediated immunity in HCC immunotherapy,25 we next treated mice with an anti-IFNAR1 antibody to block IFN-I signaling. In an orthotopic transplantation model, blockade of IFN-I completely abrogated the tumor-inhibitory effects of AARS1 knockdown in macrophages, leading to accelerated tumor progression and significantly reduced survival (figure 5D). Flow cytometric analysis yielded consistent results: anti-IFNAR1 treatment fully reversed the increase in total and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells induced by AARS1 knockdown, while further promoting CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and upregulating PD-L1 expression on macrophages (figure 5E–H).

To further dissect the mechanism, we depleted CD8+ T cells with an anti-CD8 antibody. Similar to IFN-I blockade, CD8+ T-cell depletion completely eliminated the tumor-suppressive effects of AARS1 knockdown in macrophages (online supplemental figure 4B). In vitro assays provided additional mechanistic insight: Hep53.4 cells co-cultured with CKO macrophages exhibited elevated MHC-I expression, an effect abolished by anti-IFNAR1 treatment (online supplemental figure 4C,D). Moreover, when Hep53.4 cells engineered to stably express OVA were co-cultured with CKO macrophages, they exhibited increased susceptibility to OT-I CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (online supplemental figure 4E). These findings indicate that AARS1 knockdown in macrophages activates IFN-I signaling, enhances tumor cell antigen presentation, and restores CD8+ T cells mediated antitumor immunity.

Given the enhanced CD8+ T-cell activity observed in tumors from CKO mice, we next evaluated whether macrophage-specific AARS1 knockdown could improve the therapeutic response to immune checkpoint blockade. In the orthotopic HCC model, anti-PD-L1 treatment further suppressed tumor progression and prolonged survival in CKO mice compared with anti-IgG control treatment (online supplemental figure 4F). Flow cytometric analysis showed that anti-PD-L1 treatment further enhanced the cytotoxic function of intratumoral CD8+ T cells, as reflected by increased GZMB and tumor necrosis factor-α production (online supplemental figure 4G). These results suggest that macrophage-specific AARS1 knockdown sensitizes HCC tumors to PD-L1 blockade, supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting AARS1-mediated macrophage immunosuppressive reprogramming in combination with immune checkpoint blockade.

AARS1 interacts with CPT1A to regulate lipid metabolism and IFN-I production

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which AARS1 shapes macrophage function, we performed mass spectrometry analysis of AARS1 binding partners and identified 435 interacting proteins, 25 of which carried lactylation modifications. Among these candidates, CPT1A, a rate-limiting enzyme for the mitochondrial import of long-chain fatty acids, ranked highest in interaction strength (figure 6A). Co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence co-localization further confirmed the interaction between AARS1 and CPT1A (figure 6B,C). Given the critical role of lipid metabolism in regulating innate immunity,26 27 we next examined the activation status of the IFN-I signaling pathway in macrophages from normal liver tissues and tumor tissues. On herring testis DNA (HT-DNA) stimulation, tumor-infiltrating macrophages showed impaired activation of the cGAS-STING pathway compared with macrophages from normal liver tissue, as indicated by reduced phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3. This impairment was largely reversed by AARS1 knockdown (figure 6D). Notably, AARS1 knockdown did not affect poly(I:C)-induced IFN-I pathway activation, indicating that this regulatory axis specifically modulates the DNA-sensing pathway rather than RNA-mediated retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptor signaling (online supplemental figure 5A). In parallel, macrophages from tumor tissues displayed marked lipid droplet accumulation, whereas AARS1 knockdown significantly reduced lipid deposition (figure 6E). Consistent with suppressed cGAS-STING pathway activation, tumor-infiltrating macrophages showed markedly reduced cGAMP levels compared with macrophages from normal liver tissue, whereas AARS1 knockdown significantly restored cGAMP levels. Accordingly, IFN-β production and the expression of IFN-I-related genes, including Ifnb1, Isg15, and Cxcl10, were reduced in tumor-infiltrating macrophages after HT-DNA stimulation, whereas these responses were partially restored by AARS1 knockdown (figure 6F). These findings indicate that AARS1 knockdown limits lipid accumulation in tumor-infiltrating macrophages and is associated with restoration of cGAS enzymatic activity and downstream STING-TBK1-IRF3 signaling. Importantly, inhibition of CPT1A with etomoxir or knockdown of CPT1A partially reversed the effects of AARS1 knockdown on both lipid droplet reduction and enhanced IFN-I pathway activation (figure 6G,H and online supplemental figure 5B). Moreover, herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV1)-green fluorescent protein (GFP) infection further confirmed the functional consequence of restored IFN-I production, as AARS1 knockdown suppressed HSV1-GFP replication in macrophages, whereas CPT1A knockdown reversed this effect (figure 6). These findings suggest that AARS1 promotes lipid accumulation in macrophages through CPT1A, thereby suppressing cGAS-STING mediated IFN-I responses.

AARS1 lactylates CPT1A at lysine 675 to impair long-chain fatty acid transport

We next performed mass spectrometric analysis of CPT1A lactylation in mouse macrophages and human TAMs sorted from resected HCC tumor tissues, and identified lysine 675 (lysine-to-arginine substitution at residue 675 (K675)) as a common lactylation site in both mouse and human CPT1A (figure 7A and online supplemental figure 5C,D). Cross-species sequence alignment showed that K675 is located within the catalytic choline/carnitine acyltransferase domain, which mediates the conjugation of long-chain acyl-coenzyme A (CoA) with carnitine to generate long-chain acyl-carnitine (figure 7B). Notably, K675 was highly conserved across species, suggesting that CPT1A K675 lactylation may represent a conserved modification. Immunoprecipitation assays further confirmed that AARS1 binds to the CPT1A domain at lysine 675 through its N-terminal lactylation catalytic pocket (figure 7C). When AARS1 lactates K675 of CPT1A, the catalytic pocket of AARS1 first binds to the molecule lactyl-adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and the peptide fragment where K675 is located to form a ternary complex. Then, through a chemical reaction, the carbonyl carbon of the lactyl group in lactyl-AMP is connected to the side-chain nitrogen of K675. This results in the transfer of the lactyl group. We used AlphaFold3 assisted by a crystal structure of AARS1 (PDB: 4xem) to predict the complex formed by AARS1, CPT1A aa 670–680 and lactyl-AMP (figure 7D). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were further carried out to investigate the complex. By carefully checking the obtained dynamics trajectory, it is found that lactyl-AMP stays in a state relatively far from the substrate for most of the time. In addition, it will quickly switch to another state close to K675 from time to time. This is clearly demonstrated by the time-dependent distance between the side-chain nitrogen of K675 in CPT1A and the carbonyl carbon of the lactyl group in lactyl-AMP, where the smallest distance obtained within our simulation time is only 0.38 nm (figure 7E,F).

Sodium lactate stimulation increased CPT1A lactylation in mouse peritoneal macrophages, whereas AARS1 knockdown markedly reduced this modification (figure 7G). Mutation of lysine 675 to arginine (K675R) completely abolished mouse CPT1A lactylation (figure 7H). Further immunoprecipitation analysis of human macrophages sorted from resected human HCC tumors and paired adjacent liver tissues showed that CPT1A was lactylated with markedly higher lactylation levels in tumor-derived macrophages than in adjacent liver-derived macrophages (figure 7). Mutation of lysine 675 of human CPT1A to arginine similarly abolished CPT1A lactylation, further supporting K675 as a conserved lactylation site of CPT1A (figure 7J). Functionally, microscale thermophoresis analysis showed that lactylated wild-type CPT1A (CPT1A-WT) exhibited significantly reduced affinity for oleoyl-CoA compared with the K675R mutant (figure 7K), indicating that lactylation at K675 impairs the binding of CPT1A to long-chain fatty acyl-CoA substrates. To directly determine whether AARS1 catalyzes CPT1A lactylation in a biochemical system, we performed an in vitro reconstitution assay using purified proteins. Incubation of purified CPT1A-WT with AARS1 in the presence of sodium lactate and ATP robustly induced CPT1A lactylation, whereas this modification was markedly reduced by β-alanine, which structurally resembles lactate and competitively inhibits AARS1-mediated lactate activation, and was abolished in the CPT1A-K675R mutant (figure 7L). To further investigate whether this reaction proceeds through a lactyl-AMP intermediate, we measured AMP production in the in vitro reaction system. In the presence of AARS1, ATP, and sodium lactate, robust AMP generation was detected only when CPT1A-WT was included as the protein substrate, whereas AMP production was nearly absent in the no-substrate control and CPT1A-K675R (figure 7M). Moreover, β-alanine substantially inhibited AMP production, further supporting that AARS1-catalyzed substrate activation is required for this process. Together, these findings indicate that AARS1 first activates lactate in an ATP-dependent manner to form a lactyl-AMP intermediate, which is subsequently used to transfer the lactyl group onto CPT1A at K675. Based on these data, we propose a working model in which AARS1 binds lactate and ATP to generate lactyl-AMP, and then catalyzes the transfer of the lactyl group to lysine 675 of CPT1A, thereby impairing CPT1A-mediated long-chain fatty acid transport (figure 7N).

Oleic acid accumulation suppresses cGAS-STING signaling and IFN-I responses

Previous experiments demonstrated that AARS1-mediated lactylation of CPT1A remodels lipid metabolism in macrophages. We next investigated how this altered lipid metabolism influences innate immune signaling. Tissue immunostaining showed that tumor-infiltrating macrophages accumulated substantially more lipid droplets than macrophages in normal liver tissue, whereas AARS1 knockdown significantly reduced lipid droplet accumulation (figure 8A). Lipidomic profiling further revealed extensive metabolic reprogramming in tumor-infiltrating macrophages. Compared with normal liver macrophages, 349 lipids were upregulated in tumor-infiltrating macrophages, among which 17 were downregulated on AARS1 knockdown (figure 8B,C). Among these, oleic acid (OA) was the most significantly altered metabolite (figure 8D). Consistent with previous reports, OA was found to suppress cellular innate immunity.28 In the presence of sodium lactate, OA inhibited HT-DNA-induced activation of the macrophage cGAS-STING pathway. AARS1 knockdown rendered macrophages resistant to OA-mediated suppression of cGAS-STING signaling, whereas further CPT1A knockdown restored and enhanced the inhibitory effect of OA on this pathway (figure 8E). Notably, stimulation of macrophages with cGAMP before and after OA treatment or AARS1 knockdown did not alter STING pathway activation (online supplemental figure 5E,F), suggesting that OA acts directly on cGAS rather than on downstream signaling components. Indeed, compared with untreated cells, in which cGAS condensed into discrete puncta, OA treatment caused cGAS to adopt a diffuse distribution (online supplemental figure 5G). Pull-down assays further demonstrated that OA binds to the DNA-binding region (residues 155–345) of cGAS and, in a dose-dependent manner, progressively weakened cGAS-dsDNA binding (online supplemental figure 5H,I). Molecular docking and MD simulations were performed to recover the inhibition mechanism. The molecule of OA is featured by its long hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain. We used Autodock Vina29 to predict its binding modes with cGAS, where one can observe that OA is basically embedded in a long pocket consisting of hydrophobic residues in cGAS (figure 8F). We further employed MD simulations to study the dynamics of the complex. Time evolution of the distance between these two molecules and the number of cGAS atoms that contact with OA were calculated (figure 8G). The relatively stable values both reveal it being a strong binding and OA does not dissociate from cGAS within our simulation time. We present a crystal structure (PDB: 4k96) of the complex formed by cGAS and dsDNA. The protein is in surface representation and residues that contact with OA are rendered in pink (figure 8H). It is obvious that the contact of OA heavily interferes with dsDNA association. Notably, we observed that the distribution of the pink region is discrete, which is due to a drastic change in cGAS conformation on OA binding. This again rationalizes that the binding can block the interaction between cGAS and dsDNA.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which lactate-driven protein lactylation reprograms macrophage lipid metabolism and suppresses antitumor immunity in HCC. Our results demonstrate that AARS1-mediated lactylation of CPT1A at K675 impairs the mitochondrial transport of long-chain fatty acids, resulting in intracellular accumulation of OA. Rather than acting solely as a metabolic substrate, accumulated OA directly inhibits cGAS activation, thereby attenuating STING-TBK1-IRF3 signaling and IFN-I production in TAMs. This pathway ultimately weakens tumor antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity, establishing a direct mechanistic link between post-translational modification, lipid metabolic remodeling, and innate immune dysfunction in TAMs.

Previous studies have shown that lactate promotes immunosuppressive macrophage phenotypes through HIF-1α stabilization, induction of ARG1 and VEGF, checkpoint ligand expression, and histone lactylation-dependent transcriptional programs.30–33 These mechanisms have mainly emphasized transcriptional and epigenetic regulation. In contrast, our study demonstrates that lactate can also reshape TAMs function through direct modification of a metabolic enzyme. The identification of CPT1A as a non-histone substrate of AARS1 is particularly important because CPT1A controls mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acids and thereby influences intracellular lipid utilization. K675 lactylation reduces the affinity of CPT1A for long-chain fatty acyl-CoA substrates, thereby impairing mitochondrial fatty acid transport and promoting intracellular lipid accumulation. These findings suggest that lactylation may function as a metabolic switch that converts excess lactate into sustained lipid-dependent immunosuppression.

A major conceptual advance of this study is the identification of OA as a metabolite checkpoint of macrophage innate immune sensing. Altered fatty acid oxidation and lipid droplet accumulation have been implicated in sustaining the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs.34 35 Our results indicate that specific lipid species may exert direct signaling effects. OA competitively binds to cGAS and occupies its cytosolic DNA-binding interface, thereby impairing DNA recognition and suppressing cGAMP production and downstream STING activation. These findings suggest that lipid remodeling can regulate innate immunity at the level of cytosolic DNA sensing. This expands the current understanding of cGAS-STING regulation, which has largely focused on DNA availability, cGAS modification, STING trafficking, and nucleotide metabolism.36–38 Moreover, the consequences of this pathway extend beyond macrophages. AARS1 knockdown restored macrophage IFN-I production, increased tumor cell MHC-I expression, and enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity. These observations position the AARS1-CPT1A-OA axis as an upstream metabolic checkpoint connecting macrophage-intrinsic DNA sensing with adaptive antitumor immunity. In this framework, impaired cGAS-STING signaling in TAMs not only alters macrophage function but also reduces the inflammatory and antigen-presenting signals required for effective CD8+ T-cell responses. This may help explain why the accumulation of immunosuppressive TAM subsets in HCC is frequently associated with CD8+ T-cell dysfunction and poor responses to immune checkpoint blockade.39 40 The enhanced antitumor effect observed when macrophage-specific AARS1 knockdown was combined with PD-L1 blockade further supports this interpretation. Immune checkpoint inhibitors release inhibitory signals in T cells, but their efficacy depends on adequate antigen presentation, immune cell recruitment, and innate immune support.41 42 Restoring macrophage IFN-I signaling may therefore create a more permissive immune environment for immune checkpoint blockade. These results provide a rationale for combining TAMs-directed metabolic interventions with immune checkpoint blockade in HCC.43

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although OA emerged as a key immunosuppressive metabolite, altered CPT1A activity likely affects a broader lipid network, and additional lipid species may contribute to TAMs-mediated immune suppression. The structural basis of OA-mediated cGAS inhibition also requires further clarification through high-resolution structural studies. In addition, AARS1 may lactylate other immune-regulatory proteins in macrophages or other cell types. Finally, although macrophage-specific AARS1 knockdown enhanced the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade in orthotopic HCC models, the translational feasibility of this strategy will require selective inhibitors and macrophage-targeted delivery approaches. Collectively, our study defines an AARS1-CPT1A-OA-cGAS axis that links lactylation modification to lipid metabolic remodeling and innate immune suppression in TAMs. Therapeutic disruption of this pathway may restore IFN-I signaling, reinvigorate CD8+ T-cell responses, and improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in HCC.
