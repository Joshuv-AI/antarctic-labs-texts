A ( 2 + ε ) (2+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for the general scheduling problem in quasipolynomial time
Alexander Armbruster, Lars Rohwedder, Andreas Wiese

Abstract
We study the general scheduling problem (GSP) which generalizes and unifies several well-studied preemptive single-machine scheduling problems, such as weighted flow time, weighted sum of completion time, and minimizing the total weight of tardy jobs. We are given a set of jobs with their processing times and release times and seek to compute a (possibly preemptive) schedule for them on one machine. Each job incurs a cost that depends on its completion time in the computed schedule, as given by a separate job-dependent cost function for each job, and our objective is to minimize the total resulting cost of all jobs. The best known result for GSP is a polynomial time O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ P ) O(\log\log P) -approximation algorithm [Bansal and Pruhs, FOCS 2010, SICOMP 2014]. We give a quasi-polynomial time ( 2 + ε ) (2+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for GSP, assuming that the jobs’ processing times are quasi-polynomially bounded integers. For the special case of the weighted tardiness objective, we even obtain an improved approximation ratio of 1 + ε 1+\varepsilon . For this case, no better result had been known than the mentioned O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ P ) O(\log\log P) -approximation for the general case of GSP. Our algorithms use a reduction to an auxiliary geometric covering problem. In contrast to a related reduction for the special case of weighted flow time [Rohwedder, Wiese, STOC 2021][Armbruster, Rohwedder, Wiese, STOC 2023] for GSP it seems no longer possible to establish a tree-like structure for the rectangles to guide an algorithm that solves this geometric problem. Despite the lack of structure due to the problem itself, we show that an optimal solution can be transformed into a near-optimal solution that has certain structural properties. Due to those we can guess a substantial part of the solution quickly and partition the remaining problem in an intricate way, such that we can independently solve each part recursively.



1
Introduction

We consider the following general and fundamental scheduling problem:
we are given one machine and a set of jobs
J
where each job
j\in J

is characterized by a processing time
p_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and a release
time
r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. We seek to compute a (possibly preemptive) schedule
for the jobs in
J
, i.e., each job
j\in J
is processed for
p_{j}

time units in total such that
j
is not processed before time
r_{j}

and the machine works on at most one job at a time. Ideally, we would like to
finish each job as early as possible. To quantify this,
it is natural to associate a cost to each job
j\in J
in the computed
schedule which depends on the completion time of
j
; the later
j

completes, the higher should be the cost for
j
.

In practical settings, the given jobs might be highly heterogeneous:
some jobs might be very important or urgent and, hence, need to be
finished quickly. They may even have a hard deadline before which
they must finish in
any
feasible schedule. Other jobs might
be less critical and even large delays may be tolerable for them at
(almost) no cost. Therefore, it makes sense to allow each job
j

to have a separate function that defines its cost, depending
on its completion time. For each job
j
we denote this function by

\mathrm{cost}_{j}:[r_{j},\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}

and we assume it (only) to be non-decreasing; given a schedule we denote by
C_{j}
the completion time of
j
which then
yields a cost of
\mathrm{cost}_{j}(C_{j})
.
We assume that we have access the jobs’ cost functions via suitable oracles
(similarly as in
[
10
]
, see Section
2
for details).
Overall, we seek to
minimize the total resulting cost of our jobs, i.e., we want to minimize

\sum_{j\in J}\mathrm{cost}_{j}(C_{j})
. This defines the General Scheduling
Problem (GSP) as introduced by Bansal and Pruhs
[
10
]
, who
gave a polynomial time

O(\log\log P)
-approximation algorithm for it. Here,
P
denotes the ratio
between the largest and the smallest job processing times in the input.
It is open whether GSP admits a constant factor approximation algorithm, or
even a PTAS. For the approach used in
[
10
]

it seems unlikely that it can yield a constant factor approximation, as argued in
[
11
]
.
There has been no progress for the general case of GSP since the work by Bansal and Pruhs
[
10
]
.

Instead, most research in the context of GSP has focused on special cases of the problem in which the jobs’ cost
functions are very structured. For example, in the weighted sum of
completion times objective each job
j
has a weight
w_{j}>0

and we seek to minimize
\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}C_{j}
. Hence,
\mathrm{cost}_{j}(t)=w_{j}\cdot t

for each job
j
. This setting admits a polynomial time approximation
scheme (PTAS) as shown by Afrati et al.
[
1
]
. Another well-studied
special case is the weighted flow time objective
[
11
,
15
,
21
,
5
]
.
For each job
j
its flow time in a computed schedule is the time
between its release and its completion, i.e.,
C_{j}-r_{j}
, and
we seek to minimize
\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}(C_{j}-r_{j})
.
The difference
to the weighted sum of completion times objective is “only” the
fixed term
\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}r_{j}
and, therefore, the optimal solutions are the
same for both objectives. However, approximation ratios of non-optimal solutions are not preserved and
constructing approximation algorithms for the weighted flow time objective is much more challenging. For example, it had been a long-standing
open question to find even a constant factor approximation algorithm
with polynomial running time (quasi-polynomial time algorithms for
bounded input data had been known earlier though
[
14
]
). In a breakthrough
result Batra, Kumar, and Garg
[
11
]
presented an
O(1)
-approximation
algorithm with pseudopolynomial running time which was subsequently
improved to polynomial time by Feige et al.
[
15
]
. Finally, the approximation
ratio was improved to
1+\varepsilon
by Armbruster, Rohwedder,
and Wiese
[
5
]
.

Intuitively, the mentioned algorithms for weighted flow time work with discretized candidate completion times for the jobs. They crucially
use that slight changes in a job’s flow time change its cost only by a small factor and that this change is proportional for any two jobs with (almost) the same release times.
These properties extend to the objective of minimizing the weighted
\ell_{p}
-norm of the jobs’ flow times for
p\geq 1

[
11
,
6
]
. However, they do not extend to arbitrary instances of GSP,
since there the cost function
\mathrm{cost}_{j}
of a job
j
can increase abruptly by large factors when
its flow time only increases marginally.
Already the weighted tardiness objective suffers from this issue since a job’s cost can stay zero for some time (which may be different for two jobs with the same release time)
and only then start to increase. Therefore, it forms an interesting case to study; moreover, it is well-motivated in its own right.
Formally, in this objective we are given a due date
d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for each job
j
which is intuitively
a soft deadline for
j
. Since we might not be able to finish each
job
j
before its due date, for each job
j
we consider the time
between the completion of
j
and
d_{j}
, i.e.,
C_{j}-d_{j}
,
and seek to minimize
\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}\max\{C_{j}-d_{j},0\}
. No improvements over
the general result of GSP are known for weighted tardiness.

There is another limitation of the mentioned results for special cases of GSP: they assume that

all
jobs’ cost functions have the
same
structure.
However, they no longer work for heterogeneous sets of jobs, e.g., when
each job
j
either has a hard deadline (the cost is zero if
j
is completed before the deadline and
\infty
otherwise)
or its cost in the computed schedule equals its weighted flow time.
Even though both cases individually can be approximated well or even solved exactly in polynomial time, this is
not clear for the combination of both. This motivates searching for algorithms for the general case of GSP which can thus handle
such settings.

1.1
Our contribution

In this paper, we present a quasi-polynomial time
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for (the general case of) GSP, assuming that the jobs’ processing times are quasi-polynomially bounded integers. We denote by
p_{\max}:=\max_{j\in J}p_{j}
the maximum job processing time of a given job.

Theorem 1
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the general scheduling problem with a running time of
2^{\mathrm{poly}((1/\varepsilon)^{1/\varepsilon}\log(n+p_{\max}))}
.

For the special case of weighted tardiness, our approximation ratio improves to
1+\varepsilon
.

Theorem 2
.

For each
\varepsilon>0
there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the weighted tardiness problem with a running time of
2^{\mathrm{poly}((1/\varepsilon)^{1/\varepsilon}\log(n+p_{\max}))}
.

Since weighted tardiness is strongly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard,
this is the best possible approximation ratio in quasi-polynomial running time,
unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{\mathrm{poly}(\log n)})
.
Note that our result also implies that the problem cannot be
\mathsf{APX}
-hard (with bounded input data), unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{\mathrm{poly}(\log n)})
.

Figure 1:
Rectangles and rays in an instance of the rectangle covering problem to which we reduce GSP.

Inspired by the PTAS for weighted flow time
[
5
]
, we reduce GSP and weighted tardiness
to a geometric covering problem in which we need to select a subset
of some given axis-parallel rectangles in the plane in order to cover
the demand of a given set of rays (see Figure
1
). All given rays
are oriented vertically downwards. Each rectangle
R
has a cost
and a value that it contributes to satisfy the demands of the rays
that it intersects with (in case
R
is selected). The rectangles
are pairwise non-overlapping and organized in rows such that from
each row we must select a prefix of its rectangles (or none of them).
Our objective is to satisfy the demand of each ray while minimizing
the total cost of the selected rectangles.
Our reduction loses only a factor of
1+\varepsilon
in the objective function value.

Due to the properties of the weighted flow time objective,
in the algorithm for that case in
[
5
]
it was possible to slightly round the constructed rectangles so that they
have a tree-like structure. This structure then guided a dynamic program (DP) for the geometric covering problem.
However, in GSP the job’s cost function may not have these properties and
it seems impossible to ensure a similar structure for the rectangles.
Despite this, we construct a quasi-polynomial time algorithm that
computes a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for this geometric covering
problem which, due to our reduction, yields a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for GSP as well.
Our strategy is to recursively split the plane vertically in two parts, i.e., into a left and a right subproblem. All rays are vertical and, hence, with
respect to them the two subproblems are independent. However, there can be rows whose rectangles intersect with both subproblems (see Figure
2
).

spanning rows
left-sticking-in rows
right-sticking-in rows
centered rows

Figure 2:
The shaded region indicates a given subproblem. It is split into two parts by the blue dotted line. We lose a factor of
2
for the centered rows crossing this line in this step, but not for the other rows.

To compensate for this, we can sacrifice a factor of 2 for the cost of such rows, and give independent
copies of its rectangles to each subproblem. More precisely, we can restrict each
copy to rectangles contained in the respective subproblem and cut
one rectangle (which intersects both subproblems) into two pieces if necessary.
One core difficulty is that from each rectangle row we need to select a prefix of its rectangles.
Therefore, even if the right subproblem needs to select only one single rectangle from the row, it needs to pay for

all
rectangles in that row, including those that are entirely contained in the left subproblem.
Moreover, when we recurse further we cannot sacrifice another factor of
2
in
each recursion level; doing so would result in a superconstant approximation
ratio. In fact, even if each row contained only one single rectangle it would not be clear
how obtain even a constant approximation ratio.

One key observation is that once we sacrificed a factor of
2
for the cost of a row,
in each subsequent recursive subproblem
the rectangles of the row start on the left of the subproblem, or end on the right of the subproblem, or even both.
We call such rows
left-sticking-in
rows,
right-sticking-in
rows, and
spanning
rows, see again Figure
2
.
We treat these rows differently than the other rows; in this way avoid that
in each recursion level we lose another factor of 2 in our approximation ratio for them.
Intuitively, for each recursive subproblem we consider an optimal solution for this subproblem.
We add certain rectangles to it and in this way, we extend it to a structured near-optimal solution. As a result, for some rays their respective demand is satisfied to a significantly larger extent than necessary, which creates some slack. Also,
the solution’s structure allows us to guess a potentially very large number of rectangles directly.

If a row with right-sticking-in rectangles is entirely contained in the
area of the right subproblem, it is clear that its rectangles should be passed on to the right subproblem.
However, a difficult case is when there is a right-sticking-in row from which some rectangles intersect the area
of the left subproblem and some other rectangles intersect the area of the right subproblem, see e.g., the first (upper) right-sticking-in row in Figure
2
.
To handle this, we argue that we can pass all rectangles of some of these rows to the left subproblem and all rectangles of the remaining rows to the right
subproblem. However, if then a row is passed to the
left
subproblem, then we might need its rectangles also to (partially) cover
the demand of rays in the
right
subproblem. To ensure this we create some artificial rays for the left subproblem which are, maybe counterintuitively, placed outside the area corresponding to that subproblem. A crucial argument is that it is sufficient to create a polylogarithmic number of these artificial rays due to the mentioned slack. Hence, we can guess them in quasi-polynomial time. We use a similar argumentation for the left-sticking-in rays. However, for those we have the additional difficulty that whenever we select one of its rectangles, we need to pay for all rectangles on the left of it, and such rectangles might not even
“belong” to the current subproblem, i.e., they might not even intersect its area. To remedy this, on a high level we argue that we do not lose the mentioned factor of 2 for all rectangles in a row at once in one subproblem. Instead, intuitively we argue that for each rectangle in a row there might be
some
subproblem in which we lose this factor, and for different rectangles these might be different subproblems. In this way, we obtain an approximation ratio of
2+\varepsilon
overall.

Finally, for the special case of the weighted tardiness objective, we argue that
we can adjust our reduction to the geometric covering problem such
that the rectangles’
x
-coordinates are slightly rounded.
This is related to the obtained properties in the corresponding
reduction for weighted flow time
[
21
,
5
]
. However, our obtained properties
are much weaker since weighted tardiness is a more general
cost function. Nevertheless, we show that even these weaker properties
are sufficient to ensure that we do
not
lose a factor of 2 in our approximation ratio as above.
On a high level, our rounding ensures that the rectangles’
x
-coordinates are discretized such
that in each recursive subproblem we need to consider only a polylogarithmic number of different
types
for the rows that do not trivially belong to the left or the right subproblem.
Even more, for each row type we can directly guess up to a factor of

1+\varepsilon
in quasi-polynomial time which of its rectangles are contained in an optimal
solution for the subproblem. This makes the resulting algorithm much simpler and, at the same time,
improves the approximation ratio to
1+\varepsilon
.

Other related work

For GSP, when all release times are equal, Bansal and Pruhs

[

]
.
Moseley
[
20
]
extended the approach to multiple machines (when job migration is allowed) and gave a polynomial time
O(\log\log nP)
-approximation algorithm.
Bansal and Batra
[

]
improved this approximation factor to
O(1)
.
For the objective of weighted sum of completion times (and release times) there is even a PTAS for multiple identical machines
[

]

[

.

.
Another well-studied special case of GSP is
the problem of minimizing the total weight of tardy jobs where for each job

and

2
Algorithmic framework for GSP

Given an instance of GSP, we reduce it to an instance of the rectangle
covering problem (RCP) which we define in the following. This reduction
loses only a factor of
1+\varepsilon
in the objective. Then, we present
a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for RCP which yields a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for GSP.

Formally, RCP is defined as follows. The input consists of a set of
axis-parallel rectangles
\mathcal{R}
and a set of rays
\mathcal{L}
in
the plane with the following properties:

1.

Each rectangle
R\in\mathcal{R}
is of the form
R=[a,b)\times[j,j+1)
for
some values
a,b,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and we define
\mathrm{left}(R):=a
and
\mathrm{right}(R):=b
; it has a
given cost
c(R)>0
and a given value
p(R)>0
.

2.

The rectangles in
\mathcal{R}
are pairwise disjoint.

3.

The rectangles in
\mathcal{R}
are partitioned into a set of
rows

\mathcal{W}
such that two rectangles
R=[a,b)\times[j,j+1)
,
R^{\prime}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\times[j^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)

are in the same row if and only if
j=j^{\prime}
.

4.

For each row
W\in\mathcal{W}
all rectangles in

W
have the same value, i.e., for any
R,R^{\prime}\in W
we have that

p(R)=p(R^{\prime})
, and
the rectangles in
W
are consecutive along the
x
-axis, i.e.,
for each rectangle
R\in W
there is another
rectangle
R^{\prime}\in W
with
\mathrm{right}(R)=\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime})
or there is no rectangle

R^{\prime\prime}\in W
with
\mathrm{right}(R)<\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime\prime})
.

5.

Each ray in
\mathcal{L}
is of the form
\{t\}\times(-\infty,s]=:L(s,t)

for some
s,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and has a given demand
d(L(s,t))\geq 0
. For a clearer visualization, we draw such
a ray as
\{t+1/2\}\times(-\infty,s+1/2]
since then the drawn ray intersects
with the same rectangles as the actual ray but it is not aligned with
the rectangle boundaries.

The goal is to compute a set of rectangles
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
with
the following properties:

•

For each ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}
the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
intersecting

L(s,t)
cover the whole demand of
L(s,t)
, i.e.,
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)\geq d(L(s,t))
.

•

From each row
W\in\mathcal{W}
the set
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
contains a consecutive (possibly
empty) set of rectangles starting with the leftmost rectangle, i.e.,
for each rectangle
R\in W\cap\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
the set
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
contains also each
rectangle
R^{\prime}\in W
with
\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{left}(R)
.

Our objective is to minimize the total cost of the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
,
i.e., to minimize
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}c(R)
. In our reduction, we will
ensure for each row that the cost of all its rectangles together is by at most a bounded
factor larger than the cost of any single one of its rectangles.
Therefore, in a given instance of RCP
we denote by
K
the maximum ratio of these costs in the same row,
i.e.,
K:=\max_{W\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{\sum_{R\in W}c(R)}{\min_{R\in W}c(R)}
. Note that this implies that
each row can have at most
K
rectangles.
For a given instance of RCP we denote by
n
the number of input
bits in binary encoding and, analogously to GSP, we define
p_{\max}:=\max_{R}p(R)
.

We can reduce GSP to RCP while losing only a factor of
1+\varepsilon

in the approximation ratio. To this end, we will prove the following
lemma in Section
3
. When measuring the running
time to solve a given instance of GSP, we denote by
n
the number
of bits needed to encode the processing time
p_{j}
and the release
time
r_{j}
of each job
j\in J
. For the jobs’ cost functions,
we assume (similarly as in
[
10
]
)
that we are given access to an oracle that returns in constant time
for each combination of a job
j\in J
and a value
q>0
the earliest
time
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{cost}_{j}(t)\geq q
.

Lemma 3
.

Given an
\alpha
-approximation
algorithm for RCP with a running time of
f(n,p_{\max},K)
there is an
\alpha(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for GSP with a running time of
f((n\cdot p_{\max})^{O(1)},p_{\max},(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1/\varepsilon^{3})})
.

Due to this reduction, it suffices to construct an algorithm for RCP.
We will present a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for RCP
in Section
4
which will prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

There is a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for RCP with a running time of
2^{(1/\varepsilon\cdot K\log n\log p_{\max})^{O(1)}}
.

Finally, Lemmas
3
and
4

yield Theorem
1
. We will present our
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm
for weighted tardiness (corresponding to Theorem
2
) in Section
5
.

3
Reduction

In this section we prove
Lemma

3
. Recall that our goal is to reduce GSP to RCP, losing only a factor of
1+O(\varepsilon)
. This yields a factor of
1+\varepsilon
by standard rescaling. Throughout the section, we allow our running time to be polynomial in
n
, the size of
the GSP instance and
p_{\max}
.

A standard result in scheduling, see e.g.
[
19
, Chapter 3]

is the following condition on when a set of jobs can be scheduled within their
deadlines on a single machine.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that for each job
j\in J
we are given a deadline
d_{j}\geq r_{j}
. Then there
exists a possibly preemptive schedule on one machine where each job
is completed by
d_{j}
if and only
if

\sum_{j:s\leq r_{j}<d_{j}\leq t}p_{j}\leq t-s\quad\forall\forall s,t\in\mathbb{R}:\min_{j}r_{j}\leq s<t\leq\max_{j}d_{j}

(1)

Such a schedule can be found by scheduling the jobs using earliest-deadline-first (EDF).
If all input numbers are integral
then EDF produces a schedule that completes and preempts jobs only at integral times.

The lemma above implies that to solve GSP, all we need to do is to find job completion times that minimize the cost and
satisfy the condition. Then we can find a schedule using EDF on the completion times.
Let
T=2^{k+1}
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{k}\leq\max_{j\in J}r_{j}+\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}<2^{k+1}
.
Then
T
is an upper bound on
the last completion time in an optimal schedule produced by EDF.
We may assume without loss of generality that the smallest release time is zero and the difference between any two consecutive release times
r_{j}<r_{j^{\prime}}
is
at most
\sum_{j}p_{j}
, since otherwise all jobs released before
r_{j^{\prime}}
will in an EDF solution
be finished before
r_{j^{\prime}}
and therefore the instance would split into two independent instances.
Thus, we can bound
T
by
O(n\sum_{j}p_{j})\leq O(n^{2}p_{\max})
.

Based on the considerations above, the following time-indexed integer linear program (ILP1) exactly models GSP:

\displaystyle\min\sum_{j\in J}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=r_{j}}^{T-1}(\mathrm{cost}_{j}(t+1)-\mathrm{cost}_{j}(t))\cdot x_{j,t}\hskip-85.35826pt

\displaystyle\sum_{j:s\leq r_{j}<t}p_{j}\cdot x_{j,t}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j:s\leq r_{j}<t}p_{j}-(t-s)

\displaystyle\forall s,t\in[T]:s<t

\displaystyle x_{j,t-1}

\displaystyle\geq x_{j,t}

\displaystyle\forall j\in J,t\in\{r_{j}+2,r_{j}+3,\dotsc,T\}

\displaystyle x_{j,t}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall j\in J,t\in\{r_{j}+1,r_{j}+2,\dotsc,T\}

Here
x_{j,t}=1
if and only if
C_{j}>t
.
Note that the size of ILP1 is not necessarily polynomial in the size of the GSP instance
n
, but
polynomial in
n+p_{\max}
.

Lemma 6
.

Given a solution for GSP with some cost
c
, in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,p_{\max})
we can compute a solution
for ILP1 with cost at most
c
.
Conversely, given a solution of cost
c
for ILP1
in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,p_{\max})
we can compute a solution for GSP of cost at most
c
.

Proof.

Let
C_{j}
,
j\in J
, be the completion times in a solution to GSP.
We derive a solution to ILP1 by setting
x_{j,t}=1
for all
t<C_{j}
and
x_{j,t}=0
for
all
t\geq C_{j}

Proof.

•

there is no ray

with

•

there is no rectangle

and

•

there exists a rectangle

with
\mathrm{right}(R)\geq b

Intuitively, we just delete the vertical strip

•

t
for every ray

•

\mathrm{left}(R)
for every rectangle

with
\mathrm{left}(R)\geq b

•

\mathrm{right}(R)
for every rectangle

with
\mathrm{left}(R)\geq b

This operation does not change whether a set of rectangles is a prefix of a row nor does it change whether a certain rectangle and a certain ray intersect, so the feasible and optimal solutions do not change.
We do this operation repeatedly for an interval

of maximal length.
This can only happen
|\mathcal{R}|+|\mathcal{L}|
times.
And after that, we have that for any

L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}

with
\mathrm{left}(R)=t
or there exists a rectangle

with
\mathrm{right}(R)=t
. So
\max_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\mathrm{right}(R)=O(|\mathcal{R}|+|\mathcal{L}|)=O(n)
and thus also

.

Now we guess the rectangle
R_{\max}
with maximal cost in the optimal solution
\mathrm{OPT}
.
We discard all rectangles

with cost larger than

and all rectangles that are in the same row as such an

on the right of it.
Let

denote these rectangles.
Note that the optimal solution cannot select any rectangle from

Next, let

Note that

Let

For

be the smallest power of

and let

.
We call the given algorithm with the instance

with cost

and the original demand rays
\mathcal{L}
, but a ray

has demand

Let
\mathrm{APX}
be the obtained solution. Then we output

Clearly,

is feasible.
Next, we analyze the cost.
The cost of two rectangles in the same row can differ at most by a factor of

.
So for

and

Thus the cost of two rectangles in a row differs by at most

\mathrm{set}\in\{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{right},\mathrm{span}\}
and for each pair
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}

for which

the following holds:
if a ray

intersects with a rectangle from a row

above

and additionally

we have that

Proof.

Consider a group
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})
and let
\operatorname{set}\in\{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{right},\mathrm{span}\}
. Then
c(S\cap\mathcal{R}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}})\geq c^{\prime}\cdot n^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}
as
S
contains rectangles in
n^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}
different rows of
\mathcal{W}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}
and the first rectangle in each such row has a cost of
c^{\prime}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{R}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set},\mathrm{add}}
contains all rectangles from up to
\frac{\varepsilon}{2\cdot K\cdot\log T}n^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}
rows.
Furthermore, the total cost of all rectangles in a row is at most a factor
K
larger than the cost of a single rectangle and the first rectangle in each row has a cost of
c^{\prime}
. Thus, we have that

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{R}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set},\mathrm{add}})

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot c^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2\cdot K\cdot\log T}n^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\cdot\log T}\cdot c(S\cap\mathcal{R}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}W).

Using this for all
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})
and each
\operatorname{set}\in\{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{span},\mathrm{right}\}
, we get
c(S^{+})\leq(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\log T})c(S)
.

From the definition of
W^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set},\mathrm{filled}}
we obtain directly that

contains the rectangles from row
W^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set},\mathrm{filled}}
and every row in
\mathcal{W}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}_{\operatorname{set}}
below

that intersect with

•

the line
{\mathrm{left}(A)}\times\mathbb{R}

•

the line

if
\operatorname{set}\in\{\mathrm{span},\mathrm{right}\}

So it remains to show that

covers substantially more than
S

4.2
Algorithm

Algorithmically, we guess certain properties of
S^{+}
, select some
rectangles from
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
accordingly, and then partition the remaining
problem into two subproblems which we then solve recursively. First,
for each pair
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})
we guess whether

n_{\mathrm{left}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}<\frac{2K\cdot\log T}{\varepsilon}
. If this is the case,
we guess
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{left}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
and all rectangles in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}

that are contained in
S^{+}
and select those. Since
n_{\mathrm{left}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}<\frac{2K\cdot\log T}{\varepsilon}
we
can do this in time
n^{O(K\log T/\varepsilon)}
.
Assume now that
n_{\mathrm{left}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\geq\frac{2K\cdot\log T}{\varepsilon}
.
We guess
W_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{filled}}^{c,p}
and for
each row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{left}}^{c,p}
underneath
W_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{filled}}^{c,p}

we select the rectangle
R\in W
intersecting
\{\mathrm{left}(A)\}\times\mathbb{R}

and all rectangles on the left of
R
. Similarly, for each pair
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})

we guess whether

n_{\mathrm{right}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}<\frac{2K\cdot\log T}{\varepsilon}

and if yes, we guess
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{right}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
and all rectangles in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}

that are contained in
S^{+}
and select them.
Otherwise, we guess
W_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{filled}}^{c,p}

and for each row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{right}}^{c,p}

underneath
W_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{filled}}^{c,p}
we select the rectangle
R\in W

intersecting
\{\mathrm{mid}(A)\}\times\mathbb{R}
and all rectangles on the left
of
R
. We handle the spanning rows in the same way as the right-sticking-in
rows. Let
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
denote the selected rectangles.

We want to split the remaining problem into a left and a right subproblem
for the areas
A_{\mathrm{left}}:=[\mathrm{left}(A),\mathrm{mid}(A))\times[0,\infty)
and
A_{\mathrm{right}}:=[\mathrm{mid}(A),\mathrm{right}(A))\times[0,\infty)

and for sets of rectangles
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
, respectively,
and for certain sets of rays which we will define in the following.

Centered rows.

First, we consider the centered rows
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{center}}
. For each row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{center}}

we do the following. Let
W_{\mathrm{left}}\subseteq W
denote all rectangles

R\in W
with
R\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}
and let
W_{\mathrm{right}}\subseteq W

denote all rectangles
R\in W
with
R\subseteq A_{\mathrm{right}}
. If
W_{\mathrm{left}}=W

then we simply assign all rectangles in
W
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
; similarly,
if
W_{\mathrm{right}}=W
then we add all rectangles in
W
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
.
Suppose that
W_{\mathrm{left}}\neq W\neq W_{\mathrm{right}}
. Assume first that there
is a rectangle
R_{\mathrm{mid}}\in W
with
\mathrm{left}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})<\mathrm{mid}(A)<\mathrm{right}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})
;
note that there can be at most one such rectangle. We divide
R_{\mathrm{mid}}

into a left and a right half defined by
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}:=R_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap A_{\mathrm{left}}

and
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}:=R_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap A_{\mathrm{right}}
. We define
c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}):=c(R_{\mathrm{mid}})

and assign all rectangles in
W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\}
to

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
. Also, we define
c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}):=\sum_{R\in W_{\mathrm{left}}}c(R)+c(R_{\mathrm{mid}})
.
The intuition for this is that if the right subproblem selects
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}

then in our given problem we must also select all rectangles in
W_{\mathrm{left}}

and pay
\sum_{R\in W_{\mathrm{left}}}c(R)
for them. We assign all rectangles
in
W_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}\}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
. If there is no
rectangle
R_{\mathrm{mid}}\in W
with
\mathrm{left}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})<\mathrm{mid}(A)<\mathrm{right}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})

then instead we increase the cost of the leftmost rectangle
R_{\mathrm{leftmost}}\in W_{\mathrm{right}}

by
\sum_{R\in W_{\mathrm{left}}}c(R)
, i.e., we redefine
c(R_{\mathrm{leftmost}}):=c(R_{\mathrm{leftmost}})+\sum_{R\in W_{\mathrm{left}}}c(R)
.
Finally, we assign all rectangles in
W_{\mathrm{left}}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
all rectangles in
W_{\mathrm{right}}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
.

Right-sticking-in and spanning rows.

Consider now the right-sticking-in rows
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{right}}
and let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{right}}
.
A simple case arises if each rectangle
R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}

satisfies that
\mathrm{mid}(A)\leq\mathrm{left}(R)
. In particular, this happens for rows
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{right}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
below
W_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{filled}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}

for some pair
(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})
for which
W_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{filled}}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
is defined. In this case, we assign each rectangle in

W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
. Assume now that there is a rectangle
R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}

intersecting
A_{\mathrm{left}}
. Then, we assign each rectangle in
W
to

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
, i.e., to the rectangles for the
left
subproblem.
In particular, this may include rectangles that intersect
A_{\mathrm{right}}

or that are even contained in
A_{\mathrm{right}}
. However, such rectangles
might be needed to satisfy the demands of rays intersecting with
A_{\mathrm{right}}

which we will assign to the
right
subproblem. Therefore, when
we define the rays for the left subproblem, those will include certain
additional
artificial rays

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}
intersecting with
A_{\mathrm{right}}

which will ensure that the left subproblem selects sufficiently many
rectangles intersecting
A_{\mathrm{right}}
and, therefore, help covering the
demand of rays contained in
A_{\mathrm{right}}
. We treat the spanning rows
in exactly the same way as the right-sticking-in rows.

Left-sticking-in rows.

Finally, we consider the left-sticking-in rows
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{left}}
. Consider
a row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{left}}
. If each rectangle
R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}

is contained in the interior of
A
then, intuitively, we already selected some rectangles in
W
and thus
W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
behaves like a centered
row. Therefore, in this case we treat
W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
in exactly the same way
as we treated the centered rows above. Assume now that there is a
rectangle
R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
with
\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{left}(A)
.
If for each rectangle
R^{\prime}\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
we have that

R^{\prime}\cap A_{\mathrm{right}}=\emptyset
(i.e.,
\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{mid}(A)
) then we
assign each rectangle in
W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
,
i.e., to the left subproblem. On the other hand, if there is a rectangle

R^{\prime}\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
with
R^{\prime}\cap A_{\mathrm{right}}\neq\emptyset

then we assign
all
rectangles in
W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}

to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
, i.e., to the right subproblem. Similarly as for
the right-sticking-in and the spanning rows, we will define artificial
rays in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
for the right subproblem to ensure that from
such rows, the right subproblem selects sufficiently many rectangles
to cover enough demand from rays in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
that intersect with
A_{\mathrm{left}}
.

Reference solutions.

For the left and right subproblem, we define reference solutions
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}
. Intuitively, to define
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}
we restrict
S^{+}
to the rectangles contained in the left subproblem and we define
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}
similarly. Moreover, whenever we cut a rectangle from
S
into two pieces, we assign the left piece to
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}
and the right piece to
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}
.
Formally, we define
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}:=\{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}:\exists R^{\prime}\in S^{+}\text{ with }R\subseteq R^{\prime}\}
and
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}:=\{R\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}:\exists R^{\prime}\in S^{+}\text{ with }R\subseteq R^{\prime}\}
.

Rays and artificial rays for subproblems.

It remains to define the sets of rays for the left and right subproblem,
respectively.
As mentioned above, for a ray
L\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
L\cap A_{\mathrm{left}}\neq\emptyset

we would like that its demand is partially satisfied by rectangles
from
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
, i.e., selected by the left subproblem, and partially
by rectangles from
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
, i.e., selected by the right subproblem.
Therefore, for each ray
L\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
L\cap A_{\mathrm{left}}\neq\emptyset
we
intuitively reduce its demand by a certain value; formally, we introduce
a ray in a set
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
for the left subproblem corresponding
to
L
with reduced demand. On the other hand, we introduce artificial
rays in a set
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
for the right subproblem to compensate
for this reduction. We perform a symmetric operation for the rays
in the right subproblem.

Formally, the rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
with their reduced
demands and the artificial rays
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}

are defined by a function
f:A\rightarrow\{0,\dots,\sum_{R}p(R)\}
which
we will guess; this function is a step-function with only polylogarithmically
many steps. Its steps are defined by a partition
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},...,Q_{k}\}

of
A
such that each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is an axis-parallel rectangle of
the form
Q=[x_{Q}^{L},x_{Q}^{R})\times[y_{Q}^{B},y_{Q}^{T})
for
suitable values
x_{Q}^{L},x_{Q}^{R},y_{Q}^{B}\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{Q}^{T}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

and
either
Q\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}
or
Q\subseteq A_{\mathrm{right}}
. Also, for
any two points
(t,s),(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in Q
we have that
f(t,s)=f(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})
.
For each ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
L\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}
we add a
ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)
to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
with a demand of
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=d(L(s,t))-f(t,s)-p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}^{\mathrm{mid}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})
.
Symmetrically, for each ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
L\subseteq A_{\mathrm{right}}

we add a ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)
to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
with a demand of
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=d(L(s,t))-f(t,s)-p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}^{\mathrm{mid}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})
.
Additionally, we define a polylogarithmic number of artificial rays

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}
. For each “step”
Q\in\mathcal{Q}

of
f
we introduce an artificial ray
L_{Q}
that starts in the
point
(x_{Q}^{R}-1,y_{Q}^{B})

(i.e., at the bottom-right integer point of
Q
) and is oriented vertically downwards.
We define its demand such that
d(L_{Q}):=f(t,s)
for each
(t,s)\in Q
.
For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
Q\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}
we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}

the resulting set of rays, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}=\{L_{Q}:Q\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}\}
;
similarly, we define
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}=\{L_{Q}:Q\subseteq A_{\mathrm{right}}\}
.
Note that all rays in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+},
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}

are uniquely defined from
f
. Therefore, we say that they are
induced

by
f
.

It remains to consider the rays in
{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{out}}
. We introduce sets of rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{out}}
(independent of
f
) for the left and right subproblems, respectively.
Consider a ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{out}}
.
We guess the value
p(\{R\in S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})
by which the reference solution for the left subproblem covers
L(s,t)
. Then we add a ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)
with a demand of
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=p(\{R\in S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})
to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{out}}
and a ray
L^{\prime\prime}(s,t)
with
d(L^{\prime\prime}(s,t)):=\max\{0,d(L(s,t))-d(L^{\prime}(s,t))-p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}^{\mathrm{mid}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})\}
to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{out}}
.

In the next lemma, we show that there exists a function
f
and corresponding
induced rays which admit certain properties. Those will allow us to
partition the remaining problem into the left and the right subproblem.

Lemma 16
.

There exists a step-function
f:A\rightarrow\{0,\dots,\sum_{R}p(R)\}

with only
(K\cdot M\log(T+p_{\max})/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}
steps with the following properties. Let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+},

and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}
be the rays induced by
f
. It holds that

•

|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}|\leq(K\cdot M\cdot\log(T\cdot p_{\max})/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}

•

each ray
L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
is contained in
A_{\mathrm{left}}
and each
ray
L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}
is contained in
A_{\mathrm{right}}
,

•

the solution
S_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}
is a feasible solution
to the (left) subproblem
(A_{\mathrm{left}},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}})
,

•

the solution
S_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}
is a feasible
solution to the (right) subproblem
(A_{\mathrm{right}},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}})
.

We will prove this lemma to Section
4.4
.
Algorithmically, we guess
f
which we can do in time
2^{(K\cdot M\cdot\log(n\cdot T\cdot p_{\max})/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}

since we have that
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq(K\cdot M\cdot\log(T\cdot p_{\max})/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}
and for the value of

f
corresponding to each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
(i.e., the value
f(s,t)
for
each
(s,t)\in Q
) there are only
O(n\cdot p_{\max})
options. We recurse
on the left and right subproblems
(A_{\mathrm{left}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}^{+}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left},\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}})

and
(A_{\mathrm{right}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{left}}^{+}\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right},\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}})
. Let
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}

and
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
denote the obtained solutions for them.
Intuitively, we output
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
. Formally, we need to include also all rectangles on the left of the rectangles in these sets. Therefore, our
output
\mathrm{APX}
is the set of all rectangles
R=[\mathrm{left}(R),\mathrm{right}(R))\times[j,j+1)\in\mathcal{R}
for which there exists a rectangle
R^{\prime}=[\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime}),\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime}))\times[j,j+1)\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
with
\mathrm{left}(R)<\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime})
.

4.3
Analysis

For proving Lemma

g(t^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},s^{\prime})-g(t^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}+1},s^{\prime})\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{p^{\prime}+1}\frac{\bar{n}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}}{X}

, this shows

We have a step
Q=[t^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},t^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}+1})\times[s^{\prime},\projy(W_{\ell+1}))
, i.e. for each
(t,s)
with

at the bottom-right integer point of

It remains to show that
\bar{f}
fulfills the requirements of the lemma. First note that the number of steps is bounded by

Now fix a step

. Let
\ell<\ell^{\prime}

.
As

By construction of

.
Formally, we have
g(t,s)-g(t,y^{B}_{Q})\leq\sum_{(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}}(1+\varepsilon)^{p^{\prime}+1}\lfloor\frac{\bar{n}^{c,p}}{X}\rfloor
as rectangles intersecting with the ray

Altogether, this implies

\displaystyle g(t,s)-\bar{f}(t,s)

\displaystyle=g(t,s)-g(x^{R}_{Q}-1,y^{B}_{Q})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}}(1+\varepsilon)^{p^{\prime}+1}\lfloor\frac{\bar{n}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}}{X}\rfloor

∎

For each

f_{\mathrm{right}}(t,s)+f_{\mathrm{span}}(t,s)&\text{ if }(t,s)\in A_{\mathrm{right}}\end{cases}

So it remains to show that

Lemma 21

For each ray

\displaystyle p(\{R\in S^{+}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle=p(\{R\in S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle+p(\{R\in S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle+p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

Let

be a row, let

\displaystyle p(\{R\in S^{+}\cap W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle+p(\{R\in S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle+p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

And a union bound over all rows

∎

Lemma 22

The set
S_{\mathrm{left}}

Proof.

We start with

.
It follows directly from the construction of

that it contains a prefix in each row.
The other necessary property for feasibility is to show that all demands are satisfied.
For that, consider a ray

If

.
And if

f(s,t)

20

S_{\mathrm{left}}

So suppose that
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}

Using the equality from Lemma

, this can be rearranged to

\displaystyle p(\{R\in S^{+}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})-d(L^{\prime}(s,t))

\displaystyle\geq p(\{R\in S_{\mathrm{right}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})-f(s,t)

(4)

Let
\mathcal{H}:=\{(c^{\prime},p^{\prime}):\exists R\in\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{c,p}_{\mathrm{left},\text{top}}}W\text{ with }R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\}

(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})

, we can upper bound the right hand side of (

\sum_{(c^{\prime},p^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}}(1+\varepsilon)^{p^{\prime}}\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon}{2\cdot K\log T}\bar{n}^{c^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle p(\{R\in S^{+}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

4.5
Proof of Lemma
18

First, we show that the constructed reference solutions
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}
are not too expensive. For that, we use the introduced cost function
c_{\mathrm{APX}}
. Whether a row is centered, left-sticking-in or right-sticking-in depends on the area
A
of the subproblem, so also
c_{\mathrm{APX}}
depends on
A
. Therefore, let

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A^{\prime},S^{\prime}):=\>

\displaystyle 2\cdot c(S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{center}}(A^{\prime}))+c(S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}(A^{\prime}))+c(S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{span}}(A^{\prime}))

\displaystyle+c(\{R\in S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}(A^{\prime}):\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{left}(A^{\prime})\})+2\cdot c(\{R\in S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}(A^{\prime}):\mathrm{left}(R)>\mathrm{left}(A^{\prime})\})

where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{center}}(A^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}(A^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{span}}(A^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}(A^{\prime})
denotes the centered, right-sticking-in, spanning and left-sticking-in rows w.r.t. the area
A^{\prime}
.
During the algorithm, we redefine the cost of some rectangles in centered rows (i.e., the rectangle
\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{leftmost}}
) or left-sticking-in rows
W
(when we treat
W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
as a centered row), therefore we denote by

the cost of a rectangle
R
after redefining the costs.

Lemma 23
.

We have
c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+})\geq c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}})+c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}})
.

Proof.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}
be a row and let
R=[\mathrm{left}(R),\mathrm{right}(R))\times[j,j+1)\in W
be a rectangle in this row. Furthermore let
W^{\prime}:=\{R^{\prime}=[\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime}),\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime}))\times[j^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}:j^{\prime}=j\}
denote the corresponding rectangles in the left and right subproblem and the selected rectangles.
We show that

c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+}\cap W)\geq c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})+c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime}).

(5)

We make a case distinction by the type of row
W
.

First consider a centered row
W
.
If
W_{\mathrm{left}}=W
or
W_{\mathrm{right}}=W
we have
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime}=W
or
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}=W
, which directly yields (
5
).
So now assume that
W_{\mathrm{left}}\neq W\neq W_{\mathrm{right}}
.
Then the row
W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\}
is right-sticking-in in
A_{\mathrm{left}}
and the row
W_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\}
is left-sticking-in in
A_{\mathrm{right}}
.
Suppose that
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
exists and
\mathrm{mid}(W)\in S^{+}
.
Then
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime}=W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\}
and
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}=S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}\}
.
Recall that
c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}})=c(W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\})
and
c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}})=c(R_{\mathrm{mid}})
.
So

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})+c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c(W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\})+c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}})+2c(S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}})

\displaystyle=2c(W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid}}\}\cup(S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}}))=2c(S^{+}\cap W)

\displaystyle=c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+}\cap W)

And if
\mathrm{mid}(W)\not\in S^{+}
, we have
S_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime}=S^{+}\cap W
. This yields
c_{\mathrm{APX}}(S^{+}\cap W)=2c(S^{+}\cap W)\geq c(S^{+}\cap W)=c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}})
and thus yields (
5
) as well.
So suppose that
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
does not exist and

. Recall that

, which yields

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})+c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c(W_{\mathrm{left}})+c^{\prime}(R_{\mathrm{leftmost}})+2c(S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}}\setminus R_{\mathrm{leftmost}})

\displaystyle=2c(W_{\mathrm{left}})+c(R_{\mathrm{leftmost}})+2c(S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}}\setminus R_{\mathrm{leftmost}})

\displaystyle\leq 2c(W_{\mathrm{left}}\cup(S^{+}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}}))=2c(S^{+}\cap W)

\displaystyle=c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+}\cap W)

And if
R_{\mathrm{leftmost}}\in S^{+}
, we again have
S_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime}=S^{+}\cap W
which yields
c_{\mathrm{APX}}(S^{+}\cap W)=2c(S^{+}\cap W)\geq c(S^{+}\cap W)=c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W_{\mathrm{right}})
and thus completes the proof of (
5
) for centered rows.

Next suppose that
W
is a right-sticking-in row.
Furthermore, suppose that each rectangle in

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+}\cap W)

\displaystyle=c(S^{+}\cap W)=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})

So suppose that there is a rectangle

).
So suppose that there is a rectangle

with
\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{left}(A)
. In this case, we either assign all rectangles in

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})+c^{\prime}_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq c(\{R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}:\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{left}(A)\})+2c(\{R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}:\mathrm{left}(R)>\mathrm{left}(A)\})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})

) for left-sticking-in rows.

Now, we show that

\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}

Lemma 24
.

We have
c(\mathrm{APX})\leq c^{\prime}(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}})+c^{\prime}(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}})
.

Proof.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}
be a row and let
R=[\mathrm{left}(R),\mathrm{right}(R))\times[j,j+1)\in W
be a rectangle in this row. Furthermore let
W^{\prime}:=\{R^{\prime}=[\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime}),\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime}))\times[j^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{right}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}:j^{\prime}=j\}
denote the corresponding rectangles in the left and right subproblem and the selected rectangles in
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}
. We show that
c(\mathrm{APX}\cap W)\leq c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)
.
Note that
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W

.

First suppose that the rectangle
R_{\mathrm{mid}}

and
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}

\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}=\emptyset

\mathrm{APX}\cap W=(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)\cup(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})
and thus
c(\mathrm{APX}\cap W)=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})
. And if
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}=\emptyset
and
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
, we have
\mathrm{APX}\cap W=(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)\cup(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid}}\}
and thus
c(\mathrm{APX}\cap W)=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})-c(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}})+c(R_{\mathrm{mid}})=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})
.
And if
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
we have
\mathrm{APX}\cap W=(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)\cup(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})\cup(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}},R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}\}\cup\{R_{\mathrm{mid}}\}
and thus

\displaystyle c(\mathrm{APX}\cap W)

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}\})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}\})+c(R_{\mathrm{mid}})

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c^{\prime}(R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}\})

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W)+c^{\prime}(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})

This yields the lemma in the case where
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
exists and the algorithm splits this rectangle into
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{left}}
and
R_{\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{right}}
.

So suppose that this is not the case. Note that by construction we have
W=(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})\cup(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})\cup(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})
. If
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}=\emptyset
then
\mathrm{APX}\cap W=(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})\cup(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})
, which yields the lemma. So suppose that
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and let
R
be the leftmost rectangle of
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
. Then
R
is also the leftmost rectangle of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime}
and by construction we have
c^{\prime}(R)=c(R)+c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})
. So

\displaystyle c(\mathrm{APX}\cap W)

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})+c(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap W^{\prime})c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}}\cap W^{\prime})+c^{\prime}(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap W^{\prime})

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we can combine Lemmas
15
,
23
and
24
to obtain the following approximation ratio.

Lemma 25
.

Let
S
be any feasible solution for a subproblem with region
A
. Then

c(\mathrm{APX})\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log(\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A))}{\log T}\right)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S)

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
b-a
. If
b-a=1
, the problem can be solved exactly according to Lemma
13
. So suppose
b-a>1
. Then
S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}}
are feasible solutions for the left and right subproblem. By the induction hypothesis, we have

c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}})\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log((b-a)/2)}{\log T}\right)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}})

c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}})\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log((b-a)/2)}{\log T}\right)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}})

Together with Lemmas
24
and
23
we obtain

\displaystyle c(\mathrm{APX})

\displaystyle\leq c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log((b-a)/2)}{\log T}\right)\cdot\left(c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{left}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{left}})+c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A_{\mathrm{right}},S^{+}_{\mathrm{right}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{mid}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log((b-a)/2)}{\log T}\right)\cdot c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S^{+})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon(\log(b-a)-1)}{\log T}\right)\cdot\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\log T}\right)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log(b-a)}{\log T}\right)\cdot c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S)

In the second to last step, we used Lemma
15
. This completes the proof.
∎

This directly the approximation ratio stated in Lemma
18
.

Proof of Lemma
18
.

Recall that for the root subproblem
A=[0,T)\times[0,\infty)
. By Lemma
25
, we know that
c(\mathrm{APX}^{\operatorname{root}})\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\log(\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A))}{\log T}\right)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{APX}}(A,S)\leq(2+2\varepsilon)c(S)
.
∎

5
Weighted Tardiness

In this section prove Theorem
2
,
i.e., we present a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the
weighted tardiness objective for any constant
\varepsilon>0
with a
running time of
2^{\mathrm{poly}(\log(n+p_{\max}))}
. Recall
that in this setting each job has a due date
d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and a weight

w_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and its cost function is
\mathrm{cost}_{j}(t)=w_{j}(t-d_{j})

for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
. First, we reduce the problem to a special case
of RCP in which each instance has certain structural properties. Then
we give a recursive algorithm which is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for this setting of RCP. In particular, due to the additional
structural properties this algorithm is much simpler than the algorithm
for the general case of RCP (see Section
4.2
)
and at the same time achieves an approximation ratio of only
1+\varepsilon
,
rather than
2+\varepsilon
.

Formally, in our special case of RCP, each instance, given by a set
of rectangles
\mathcal{R}
and a set of rays
\mathcal{L}
, is
\delta
-well-structured

(for some
\delta>0
that our running time will depend on) which means that for each rectangle
R=[\mathrm{left}(R),\mathrm{right}(R))\times[j,j+1)\in\mathcal{R}

and the (unique) value
h\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{h}\leq\mathrm{right}(R)-\mathrm{left}(R)<2^{h+1}

we have that
\mathrm{left}(R)
and
\mathrm{right}(R)
are integral multiples of
\delta\cdot 2^{h}
.
We reduce the weighted tardiness problem to this special case of RCP
in the following lemma,
which we will prove in Section
5.1
.

In the following, assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon=2^{-k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 26
.

Given an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm
for
\delta
-well-structured instances of RCP with a running time of
f(n,K,\max_{R}\mathrm{right}(R),p_{\max},\delta)
,
there is a
\alpha(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for minimizing
weighted tardiness in time
f((np_{\max})^{O(1)},(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1/\varepsilon^{3})},(np_{\max})^{O(1)},\varepsilon/32)
.

Using this reduction,
it suffices to solve
\delta
-well-structured instances of RCP. We
will present an algorithm for this task, corresponding to the following
lemma, in Section
5.2
.

Lemma 27
.

There
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for
\delta
-well-structured
instances of RCP with a running time of
2^{(1/\delta\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot\max_{R}\mathrm{right}(R))/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
.

Together,
Lemmas
26
and
27

yield Theorem
2
.

5.1
Reduction

Recall that the
x
-coordinates for the rectangles in RCP are derived from the milestones
from
Lemma

7
. We modify the construction here, achieving the same properties,
but additionally an equivalent of
\delta
-well-structuredness.
Recall also that
T=O(\max_{j\in J}r_{j}+\sum_{j\in J}p_{j})
is an upper bound on the time when the last job is finished.

Lemma 28
.

Consider an instance of weighted tardiness minimization.
For each job
j
we can in polynomial time construct a sequence
m_{0}(j),m_{1}(j),m_{2}(j),\dotsc,m_{f_{j}}(j)\in\mathbb{N}

where

1.

\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i+1}(j))\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1)
for all
0\leq i<f_{j}
;

2.

\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i+1}(j)+1)>(1+\varepsilon/4)\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1)
for all
0\leq i<f_{j}-1
;

3.

m_{0}(j)=r_{j}
;

4.

m_{1}(j)=d_{j}
;

5.

m_{f_{j}}(j)=T
;

6.

let
h\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{h}\leq m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)<2^{h+1}
. Then
m_{i}(j),m_{i+1}(j)\in\delta 2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}
, where
\delta=\varepsilon/32
.

Proof.

We set
m_{0}(j)=r_{j}
and
m_{1}(j)=d_{j}
.
Then for
i\geq 1
suppose we have already defined
m_{i}(j)
.
Let
h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{h^{\prime}}\leq m_{i}(j)-d_{j}<2^{h^{\prime}+1}
. We define
m_{i+1}(j)\in\{m_{i}(j)+1,m_{i}(j)+2,\dotsc,T\}
to be
the minimal number that is at least as large as the
second
smallest
t\in\frac{\varepsilon}{2}2^{h^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{Z}
with
t>m_{i}(j)
;
or
T
if this does not exists (because it would exceed
T
). Let
f_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest index
i
where
m_{i}(j)
equals
T
.

It is easy to check that Properties 1 and 2 hold for
i=0
.
Let
1\leq i<f_{j}
.
Then

\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i+1}(j))=w_{j}(m_{i+1}(j)-d_{j})\leq w_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1+\varepsilon 2^{h^{\prime}}-d_{j})\\
\leq(1+\varepsilon)(m_{i}(j)+1-d_{j})=(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1).

Furthermore, for
1\leq i<f_{j}-1
,

\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i+1}(j)+1)=w_{j}(m_{i+1}(j)+1-d_{j})>w_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1+\varepsilon 2^{h^{\prime}-1}-d_{j})\\
\geq(1+\varepsilon/4)(m_{i}(j)+1-d_{j})=(1+\varepsilon/4)\mathrm{cost}_{j}(m_{i}(j)+1).

Consider now Property 6.
If
m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)\leq 32/\varepsilon
then the claim is trivial, since
\varepsilon/32\cdot 2^{h}\leq 1
,
and
m_{i}(j),m_{i+1}(j)\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Hence, assume otherwise.
Let
h\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{h}\leq m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)<2^{h+1}
.
It holds that that
m_{i}(j)-d_{j}<2^{h}
,
since otherwise
m_{i+1}(j)<m_{i}(j)+\varepsilon 2^{h}
, a contradiction to the definition of
h
.
It follows that
m_{i+1}(j)\in\frac{\varepsilon}{2}2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\frac{\varepsilon}{32}2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}
.

For the discreteness of
m_{i}(j)
, we will argue that the differences
m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)
for
i>1/\varepsilon
do not grow too quickly. Note that we have

m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)>\varepsilon(m_{i}(j)-d_{j})/4.

Furthermore, for all
i>2/\varepsilon
we have

m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)\leq 2\varepsilon(m_{i}(j)-d_{j}).

Assuming that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small it follows that

m_{i+1}(j)-d_{j}\leq 2(m_{i}(j)-d_{j}).

Thus,

m_{i+1}(j)-m_{i}(j)\leq 2\varepsilon(m_{i}(j)-d_{j})\leq 4\varepsilon(m_{i-1}-d_{j})<16(m_{i}(j)-m_{i-1}(j)).

It follows that
2^{h^{\prime\prime}}\leq m_{i}(j)-m_{i-1}(j)
, where
2^{h^{\prime\prime}}\geq 2^{h}/16
.
By the argument used to show
m_{i+1}(j)\in\frac{\varepsilon}{2}2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}

we obtain
m_{i}(j)\in\frac{\varepsilon}{2}2^{h^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\frac{\varepsilon}{32}2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Since this construction satisfies all properties of
Lemma

7
, we can use it
with the reduction in
Section

3
.
Recall that the rectangles
[a,b)\times[j^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)\in\mathcal{R}
all had the form that there
is some
j\in J
and
i
with
a=m_{i}(j)
and
b=m_{i+1}(j)
.
Assuming that
i\geq 1
, the previous lemma ensures that for
h\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{h}\leq b-a<2^{h+1}
we have

a,b\in\frac{\varepsilon}{32}2^{h}\cdot\mathbb{Z}
.
The first rectangle of each job with
x
-coordinates
[m_{0}(j),m_{1}(j))=[r_{j},d_{j})
may be very
wide and
r_{j},d_{j}
cannot be rounded. This rectangle, however, has cost zero, so the reduction in

Section

3
anyway removes it in the preprocessing.
The instance size and
K
are bounded as in
Lemma

3
. The maximum right hand side of
a rectangle, i.e.,
\max_{R}\mathrm{right}(R)
, is bounded by
T
and

is bounded by
(n+p_{\max})^{O(1)}

via the preprocessing in

.
Hence, this implies

5.2
Algorithm

In this section we prove Lemma
27
. Suppose
that we are given a
\delta
-well-structured instance of RCP. Our
approach to solve it is similar to our algorithm for general case of RCP.
As before, let
p_{\max}=\max_{R}p(R)
. We start with a preprocessing step. This time, we argue that it suffices to construct an algorithm for which
C:=\frac{\max_{R\in\mathcal{R}}c(R)}{\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}c(R)}=O(K\cdot n/\varepsilon)
.

Lemma 29
.

Assume that there is an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
\delta
-well-structured instances of RCP
with a running time of
2^{(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot\max_{R}\mathrm{right}(R)))^{O(K)}}
. Then there
is an
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
\delta
-well-structured
instances of RCP with a running time of
2^{(1/\delta\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot\max_{R}\mathrm{right}(R))/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{L}
denote the rectangles and rays of a
\delta
-well-structured instance
I
and let
S
be an optimal solution to this instance.
Then we guess the most expensive rectangle
R_{\max}\in S
in the optimal solution.
Let
\mathcal{W}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
denote all rows in which there exists a rectangle of cost at most
\varepsilon\cdot c(R_{\max})/(n\cdot K)
.
Let
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
denote all rectangles in the rows
\mathcal{W}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
.
We select
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
.
As for each row
W\in\mathcal{W}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
we have
\sum_{R\in W}c(R)\leq K\cdot\varepsilon\cdot c(R_{\max})/(n\cdot K)\leq\varepsilon c(R_{\max})/n
, this implies
c(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}})\leq\varepsilon c(R_{\max})
.

Let
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{discard}}
denote all rectangles
R
with
c(R)>c(R_{\max})
and all rectangles in the same row on the right of such a rectangle. Note that the optimal solution cannot select any rectangle in
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{discard}}
as it contains a prefix from each row.
Next, we define a set of rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. For each ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}
we add a ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with a demand of
d(L^{\prime}(s,t))=\max\{0,d(L(s,t))-p(\{R\in\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})\}
. Then we use the given algorithm for the instance
I^{\prime}
with rectangles
\mathcal{R}\setminus(\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{discard}})
and the rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
Given a solution
\mathrm{APX}
to this instance, we output
\mathrm{APX}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
.
The most expensive rectangle in
I^{\prime}
has a cost of
c(R_{\max})
and the cheapest rectangle has a cost of at least
\varepsilon\cdot c(R_{\max})/(n\cdot K)
, so we have
C\leq K\cdot n/\varepsilon
for the instance
I^{\prime}
.

Clearly
\mathrm{APX}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
is a feasible solution for the instance
I
. Also
S\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}}
is a feasible solution for
I^{\prime}
. So
c(\mathrm{APX})\leq\alpha\cdot c(S\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}})
, which implies
c(\mathrm{APX}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}})\leq\alpha\cdot c(S\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}})+\varepsilon c(R_{\max})\leq c(S\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{cheap}})+\varepsilon c(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\alpha c(S)
.
This shows that we obtain a
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
-approximation.
As we need to call the given algorithm at most
n
times (once for each choice of
R_{\max}
) and
C\leq K\cdot n/\varepsilon
for the instance
I^{\prime}
, we obtain the claimed running time.
∎

Our algorithm is based
on a recursion in which the input of each recursive call consists
of

•

an instance of RCP defined by
a set of rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and a set of rectangles
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
; we denote by
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}

be the partition of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
into rows,

•

an area
A
of the form
A=[\mathrm{left}(A),\mathrm{right}(A))\times[0,\infty)
for
two values
\mathrm{left}(A),\mathrm{right}(A)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A)=2{}^{k}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{-1\}
and
\mathrm{left}(A)
and
\mathrm{right}(A)

are integral multiples of
2^{k}
,

•

each rectangle
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is contained in
A
, i.e.,
R\subseteq A
,

•

each ray
L\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is contained in
[\mathrm{left}(A),\mathrm{right}(A))\times\mathcal{R}
.

The recursive call returns a solution to the RCP instance defined
by
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. At the end of our algorithm, we output the solution
returned by the (main) recursive call in which
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{L}
,

\mathrm{left}(A)=0
, and
\mathrm{right}(A):=T
where
T
is the smallest power of
2 that is at least
\max_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\mathrm{right}(R)
. Any solution to this subproblem
is a solution to our given instance.

Assume we are given a recursive call as defined above. The base case
of our recursion arises when
\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A)=1/2
. Then
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}=\emptyset

since by assumption each rectangle in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
has integer coordinates
and is contained in
A
. Therefore, the subproblem is feasible if
an only if for each ray
L\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
we have that
d(L)=0
. Assume
now that
\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A)\geq 1
. As for the general case of RCP, we
split the problem into a left and a right subproblem at the vertical
line
\mathrm{mid}(A)\times\mathbb{R}
where
\mathrm{mid}(A):=(\mathrm{right}(A)-\mathrm{left}(A))/2
. Formally,
the areas for the left and right subproblem are defined by
A_{\mathrm{left}}:=[\mathrm{left}(A),\mathrm{mid}(A))\times[0,\infty)

and
A_{\mathrm{right}}:=[\mathrm{mid}(A),\mathrm{right}(A))\times[0,\infty)
, respectively. In
the following, we will define the rectangles
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}

for the left and right subproblem, respectively, and select certain
rectangles from
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\setminus(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}})
. Finally
define the rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
for the left and
right subproblem, respectively,

First, we define that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
contains all rectangles from each row

W\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
in which
each
rectangle
R\in W
is contained
in
A_{\mathrm{left}}
, i.e.,
R\subseteq A_{\mathrm{left}}
. Symmetrically, we
define that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
contains all rectangles from each row
W\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}

in which each rectangle
R\in W
is contained in
A_{\mathrm{right}}
, i.e.,

R\subseteq A_{\mathrm{right}}
. Consider now all remaining rows in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
,
i.e., let
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
be the set of all rows

W\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
which contain a rectangle
R\in W
with
R\cap A_{\mathrm{left}}\neq\emptyset

and a rectangle
R^{\prime}\in W
with
R\cap A_{\mathrm{right}}\neq\emptyset
.

We partition the rows in
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}
into groups. Intuitively,
the rectangles of rows in the same group are vertical translates of
each other, have approximately the same cost, and the same value. Formally,
a group
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}
is specified by a tuple

g=(c_{1},\dots c_{k},p,t_{0},\dots,t_{k})
of integers where
k\leq K

(recall that
K
is an upper bound on the number of rectangles in
a row). Consider a row
W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}
and assume that
R_{1},\dots R_{k^{\prime}}

are its rectangles such that
\mathrm{left}(R_{i})<\mathrm{left}(R_{i+1})
for each

i\in[k^{\prime}-1]
. We define that the row
W
is in group
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}

if and only if

•

k=k^{\prime}
,

•

(1+\varepsilon)^{p}\leq p(R_{1})<(1+\varepsilon)^{p+1}
, and

•

for
i\leq k
we have that
(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i}}\leq c(R_{i})\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i}+1}
,

\mathrm{left}(R_{i})=t_{i-1}
and
\mathrm{right}(R_{i})=t_{i}
.

Let
\mathcal{G}:=\{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\cup\mathbb{Z}^{6}\cup...\cup\mathbb{Z}^{2K+2}:\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}\neq\emptyset\}

denote all vectors
g
specifying a non-empty group
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}
.
Next, we show that there is only a quasi-polynomial number of groups
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 30
.

We have that
|\mathcal{G}|\leq(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot\log n\cdot\log p_{\max}\cdot\log T/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathcal{G}
and consider a row
W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g}
. Let
R_{\mathrm{mid}}\in W
be the rectangle intersecting
\mathrm{mid}(A)\times\mathbb{R}
, i.e. with
\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{mid}(A)
and
\mathrm{right}(R)>\mathrm{mid}(A)
.
We can limit the number of options for
g
as follows:

•

There are
K
options for the number
k
of rectangles in row
W
.

•

Recall that
C=\frac{\max_{R\in\mathcal{R}}c(R)}{\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}c(R)}
. So there are only only
O((\log C)/\varepsilon)
different integers
c_{i}
for which there exists a rectangle
R\in\mathcal{R}
with
(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i}}\leq c(R)\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i}+1}
. Thus there are
O(((\log C)/\varepsilon)^{K})
options for
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
.

•

There are
O((\log p_{\max})/\varepsilon)
options for
p
as
p(R)\leq p_{\max}
for all
R\in\mathcal{R}
.

•

The length of any rectangle
R\in W
is at most
T
. So there are
\log T
possible values of
h
such that
2^{h}\leq\mathrm{right}(R)-\mathrm{left}(R)\leq 2^{h+1}
. As the instance is
\delta
-well-structured, there are
O((\log T)/\delta)
possible lengths for any rectangle. So there are
O((\log T)/\delta)
options for the length of
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
.

•

The rectangle
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
fulfills
\mathrm{left}(R)\leq\mathrm{mid}(A)
and
\mathrm{right}(R)>\mathrm{mid}(A)
and as the instance is
\delta
-well-structured, there are only
1/\delta
options for
\mathrm{left}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})
when the length of
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
is fixed.

•

There are
k
options which of the rectangles
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
is
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
.

•

The rectangles in a row are adjacent. So given the exact position for one rectangle (in this case
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
), it suffices to enumerate all possible lengths of the other rectangles, as this determines their position. As there are
O(\log T/\delta)
options for the length of a single rectangle, there are
O((\log T/\delta)^{K-1})
options for the rectangles
W\setminus\{R_{\mathrm{mid}}\}
.

So it suffices to know
k,c_{1},\dots,c_{k},p
, the lengths of the rectangles
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
, the position
\mathrm{left}(R_{\mathrm{mid}})
and the information which of the rectangles
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
is
R_{\mathrm{mid}}
to determine the group. So
|\mathcal{G}|=O(K\cdot((\log C)/\varepsilon)^{K}\cdot(\log p_{\max})/\varepsilon\cdot((\log T)/\delta)^{K}\cdot 1/\delta\cdot K)=(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot\log p_{\max}\cdot(\log T)/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}
.
∎

Let
S
be an optimal solution to the given subproblem. Intuitively,
we want to guess the rectangles in
S
in each row
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}

for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Consider a group
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}

for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Essentially, the main difference of the
rows within a group is their
y
-coordinate, since for the rectangles
in such a row the costs and the values are almost the same by the
definition of the groups. When two rectangles differ only by their

y
-coordinate, every ray
L\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
intersecting with the top rectangle
also intersects with the bottom one. Therefore, intuitively it is
sufficient to select the same number of rectangles from the group
g

as
S
and select them greedily from bottom to top, if we select
by a factor
1+\varepsilon
more rectangles to compensate for the fact
that their values may differ by a factor
1+\varepsilon
.

Formally for the group
g
, we do the following: For each
0\leq i\leq k

let
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{\mathrm{cross}}
denote the rows in
\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{cross}}

from which
S
contains exactly
i
rectangles. Let
n_{g,i}:=|\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{\mathrm{cross}}|

denote the number of such rows. First, we guess the number
n_{g,i}

for each
i\in[K]
. For each
i\in[K]
with
n_{g,i}\leq 1/\varepsilon

we simply guess the corresponding rows
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{\mathrm{cross}}
and
for each row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{\mathrm{cross}}
we select its leftmost
i

rectangles. Let
\mathrm{APX}_{g,i}
denote the resulting rectangles. Let

\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{done}}=\bigcup_{i\in[k]:n_{g,i}\leq 1/\varepsilon}\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{\mathrm{cross}}

denote all rows, from which we already guessed the rectangles in
S
.

We go through the indices
i
with
n_{g,i}\geq 1/\varepsilon
in
decreasing order, i.e., from
K
to 1. Consider an index
i\in[K]
.
Intuitively, in the
\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor
bottom
rows where no rectangle is selected yet, we add the first
i
rectangles
to our solution. Formally, let
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
denote the bottom-most

\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor
rows in
\mathcal{W}_{g}\setminus(\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{done}}\cup\bigcup_{i^{\prime}:i<i^{\prime}\leq K,n_{g,i^{\prime}}>1/\varepsilon}\mathcal{W}_{g,i^{\prime}}^{*})
.
Now let
\mathrm{APX}_{g,i}
denote all rectangles that are within the first

i
rectangles in some row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
. We also select

\mathrm{APX}_{g,i}
. We define
\mathrm{APX}_{g}:=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathrm{APX}_{g,i}

and
S_{g}:=\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{g}}S\cap W
. In the following lemma
we prove that
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
is not much more expensive than
S_{g}
.

Lemma 31
.

We have that
c(\mathrm{APX}_{g})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{g})
.

Proof.

Let
g=(c_{1},\dots c_{k},p,t_{0},\dots,t_{k})\in\mathcal{G}
. By construction we have
\mathrm{APX}_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}=S_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W
. So we can intuitively ignore the rows
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}
.
Furthermore for each
i\leq k
with
n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon
we have that
\mathrm{APX}_{g,i}
contains the first
i
rectangles from
\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor
rows. Due to the definition of
g
, we have that the cost of the first
i
rectangles in
g
is at least
\sum_{i^{\prime}\leq i}(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i^{\prime}}}
and at most
\sum_{i^{\prime}\leq i}(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i^{\prime}}+1}
.
So
c(\mathrm{APX}_{g,i})\leq(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\sum_{i^{\prime}\leq i}(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i^{\prime}}+1}
and
c(S\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i}}W)\geq n_{g,i}\sum_{i^{\prime}\leq i}(1+\varepsilon)^{c_{i^{\prime}}}
.
This implies
c(\mathrm{APX}_{g,i})\leq(1+\varepsilon)(1+2\varepsilon)c(S\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i}}W)\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i}}W)
.
So we obtain

\displaystyle c(\mathrm{APX}_{g})

\displaystyle\leq c(\mathrm{APX}_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}})+\sum_{i\in[k]:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}c(\mathrm{APX}_{g,i})

\displaystyle\leq c(S_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}})+\sum_{i\in[k]:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}(1+5\varepsilon)c(S\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i}}W)

\displaystyle\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(\sum_{i\leq k}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i})

\displaystyle=(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{g})

This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we also show that it covers the same amount on any ray as
S_{g}
, so it is a good substitute for
S_{g}
.

Lemma 32
.

For any ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, we have that
\sum_{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)\geq\sum_{R\in S_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)
.

Proof.

Let
g=(c_{1},\dots c_{k},p,t_{0},\dots,t_{k})\in\mathcal{G}
. By construction we have
\mathrm{APX}_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}=S_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W
. So we can intuitively ignore the rows
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}
.
Let
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
be a ray.
In each row
W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}
the sets
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
and
S_{g}
contain the same rectangles, so they also cover the same amount on
L(s,t)
.
Suppose that there is no row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{g}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{done}}
with a rectangle
R\in W\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{g}
and
R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset
.
Then every rectangle
\{R\in S_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\}
is also in
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
, which yields the lemma.
So suppose now that there is a row
W^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{g}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{done}}
such that there exists a rectangle
R^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}\setminus\mathrm{APX}_{g}
with
R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset
.
Let
i^{\prime}\leq k
such that
t_{i^{\prime}-1}\leq t<t_{i^{\prime}}
.
Then
\mathrm{left}(R^{\prime})=t_{i^{\prime}-1}
and
\mathrm{right}(R^{\prime})=t_{i^{\prime}}
.
For each
i\geq i^{\prime}
, we have
W^{\prime}\not\in\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
as
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
contains the first
i
rectangles from each row in
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
, but not the
i
-th rectangle
R^{\prime}
in row
W^{\prime}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
denotes the bottom-most

\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor
rows in
\mathcal{W}_{g}\setminus(\mathcal{W}_{g}^{\mathrm{done}}\cup\bigcup_{i^{\prime}:i<i^{\prime}\leq K,n_{g,i^{\prime}}>1/\varepsilon}\mathcal{W}_{g,i^{\prime}}^{*})
. This implies that all rows in
\mathcal{W}_{g,i}^{*}
are below
W^{\prime}
.
As the
i^{\prime}
-th rectangle in each such row is in
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
and intersects
L(s,t)
, we have

|\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}\setminus\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\}|\geq\sum_{i\in\{i^{\prime},\dots,k\}:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor
.
By definition of
n_{g,i}
we have
|\{R\in S_{g}\setminus\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\}|\leq\sum_{i\in\{i^{\prime},\dots,k\}:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}n_{g,i}
. Recall that each rectangle
R
in some row
W\in\mathcal{W}_{g}
fulfills
(1+\varepsilon)^{p}\leq p(R)<(1+\varepsilon)^{p+1}
. This implies

\displaystyle p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}\setminus\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\}|)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{p}\sum_{i\in\{i^{\prime},\dots,k\}:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}\lfloor(1+2\varepsilon)n_{g,i}\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{p+1}\sum_{i\in\{i^{\prime},\dots,k\}:n_{g,i}>1/\varepsilon}n_{g,i}

\displaystyle\geq p(\{R\in S_{g}\setminus\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

And as
\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W\}=\{R\in S_{g}\cap\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{done}}_{g}}W\}
, this completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Finally, we define the rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}

for the left and right subproblem, respectively. Intuitively, we define
that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
contain all rays in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
that intersect

A_{\mathrm{left}}
, but we adjust their demands if they intersect some rectangles
in
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
for some groups
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Formally, for each
ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with
(t,s)\in A_{\mathrm{left}}
we
add a
ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)
to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}

with a demand of
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=\max\{0,d(L(s,t))-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)\}
.
We define the rays
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
analogously. We recursively
compute solutions
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
for the left
and right subproblem. If these solutions exist, we obtain the candidate solution
\bigcup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{APX}_{g}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}\cup\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
. We output the minimal cost candidate solution
\mathrm{APX}
among all guesses.
This completes the description of the algorithm.

5.3
Analysis

We show that the computed solution is feasible, has small cost, and
that our algorithm has a running time of
2^{(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot T)/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
.
First, we show that
S
restricted to the rectangles
in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
is a feasible solution for the left subproblem and

S
restricted to the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
is a feasible
solution for the right subproblem, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 33
.

Let
S_{\mathrm{left}}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}\cap S

and
S_{\mathrm{right}}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}\cap S
. Then
S_{\mathrm{left}}
is a feasible
solution for the left subproblem and
S_{\mathrm{right}}
is a feasible solution
for the right subproblem.

Proof.

We prove that
S_{\mathrm{left}}
is a feasible solution for the left subproblem, the proof that
S_{\mathrm{right}}
is a feasible solution for the right subproblem is analogous to the proof for
S_{\mathrm{left}}
.
From the definition of
S_{\mathrm{left}}
and the fact that
S
contains a prefix in each row, it follows that
S_{\mathrm{left}}
contains a prefix in each row as well.
So it suffices to show that
S_{\mathrm{left}}
covers all rays an
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
. Let
L^{\prime}(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
be a ray. Then there is a ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=\max\{0,d(L(s,t))-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)\}
. Note that there is no rectangle
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{right}}
with
R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset
. So

\displaystyle p(\{R\in S_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle=p(\{R\in S:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p(\{R\in S_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle\geq d(L(s,t))-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle=d(L^{\prime}(s,t))

where the inequality follows from Lemma
32
. This show that
S_{\mathrm{left}}
is a feasible solution and thus completes the proof.
∎

So we actually obtain a solution
\mathrm{APX}
.
Now we prove that
\mathrm{APX}
is feasible.

Lemma 34
.

The set
\mathrm{APX}
is a feasible solution.

Proof.

Suppose that we recursively obtained solutions
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
for the left and right subproblem. Note that by Lemma
33
this is at least the case for the correct guesses. Then
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
contain a prefix of the rectangles in each row, as they are feasible solutions to the respective subproblems. For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
the set
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
contains a prefix from each row by construction.
Thus, also
\mathrm{APX}
contains a prefix from each row.
So it remains to show that every ray
L(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is covered. Suppose that
L(s,t)
intersects
A_{\mathrm{left}}
, as the proof for the case when
L(s,t)
intersects
A_{\mathrm{right}}
is analogue. Then there is a ray
L^{\prime}(s,t)\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{left}}
with
d(L^{\prime}(s,t)):=\max\{0,d(L(s,t))-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset}p(R)\}
.
As
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
is a feasible solution for the left subproblem, we have

p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})\geq d(L^{\prime}(s,t))
. And as
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
and the sets
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
for
g\in\mathcal{G}
are disjoint, we have

\displaystyle p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle=p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle\geq d(L^{\prime}(s,t))+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}p(\{R\in\mathrm{APX}_{g}:R\cap L(s,t)\neq\emptyset\})

\displaystyle\geq d(L(s,t))

This shows that
L(s,t)
is covered and thus completes the proof.
∎

Next, we bound the cost of
\mathrm{APX}
. Lemma
31

already bounded the cost of
\mathrm{APX}_{g}
for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Thus, it remains only to
bound the cost of
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}}
and
\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}}
.
By combining Lemmas
31
and
33

we obtain an approximation factor of
1+5\varepsilon
.

Lemma 35
.

We have that
c(\mathrm{APX})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S)
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
\mathrm{left}(A)-\mathrm{right}(A)
. In the base case where
\mathrm{left}(A)-\mathrm{right}(A)=1/2
, we solve the problem optimally as then
\mathrm{APX}=S_{g}=\emptyset
.
So suppose
\mathrm{left}(A)-\mathrm{right}(A)>1/2
. Then by induction and Lemma
33
, we have that
c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{\mathrm{left}})
and
c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{\mathrm{right}})
. By Lemma
31
we have
c(\mathrm{APX}_{g})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{g})
. Altogether, we obtain

\displaystyle c(\mathrm{APX})

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{left}})+c(\mathrm{APX}_{\mathrm{right}})+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}c(\mathrm{APX}_{g})

\displaystyle\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{\mathrm{left}})+(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{\mathrm{right}})+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}(1+5\varepsilon)c(S_{g})

\displaystyle=(1+5\varepsilon)c(S)

∎

Thus it only remains to bound the running time.

Lemma 36
.

The running time of the algorithm can be bounded by
2^{(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot T)/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
.

Proof.

The recursion depth of our algorithm is
O(\log T)
. In each recursive call we have
|\mathcal{G}|\leq(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot\log p_{\max}\cdot\log T/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}
.
For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
and each
i\in[K]
, we need to guess
n_{g,i}
and if
n_{g,i}\leq 1/\varepsilon
we also guess the rows
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cross}}_{g,i}
.
As
n_{g,i}\leq n
and there are at most
n
rows, this can be done in
n^{O(K/\varepsilon)}
per group
g
.
For fixed guesses the remaining computation can be done in time
O(n)
.
Thus, the total running time is
((n^{O(K/\varepsilon)})^{|\mathcal{G}|})^{O(\log T)}
. By Lemma
30
, this can be bounded by
2^{(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot T)/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
.
∎

Altogether, this implies Lemma
27
.

Proof of Lemma
27
.

By Lemma
34
the algorithm computes a feasible solution to every subproblem, so also to the root subproblem which is the output of the algorithm.
By Lemma
35
we have
c(\mathrm{APX})\leq(1+5\varepsilon)c(S)
, we obtain a
(1+5\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm.
By Lemma
36
, the running time is bounded by
2^{(1/\delta\cdot\log C\cdot K\cdot\log(n\cdot p_{\max}\cdot T)/\varepsilon)^{O(K)}}
. So we can apply Lemma
29
, which yields the claimed result by rescaling
\varepsilon
.
∎
