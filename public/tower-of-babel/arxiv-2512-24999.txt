Basic Inequalities for First-Order Optimization with Applications to Statistical Risk Analysis
Seunghoon Paik, Kangjie Zhou, Matus Telgarsky, Ryan J. Tibshirani

Abstract
We introduce basic inequalities for first-order iterative optimization algorithms, forming a simple and versatile framework that connects implicit and explicit regularization. While related inequalities appear in the literature, we isolate and highlight a specific form and develop it as a well-rounded tool for statistical analysis. Let f f denote the objective function to be optimized. Given a first-order iterative algorithm initialized at θ 0 \theta_{0} with current iterate θ T \theta_{T} , the basic inequality upper bounds f ⁡ ( θ T ) − f ⁡ ( z ) f(\theta_{T})-f(z) for any reference point z z in terms of the accumulated step sizes and the distances between θ 0 \theta_{0} , θ T \theta_{T} , and z z . The bound translates the number of iterations into an effective regularization coefficient in the loss function. We demonstrate this framework through analyses of training dynamics and prediction risk bounds. In addition to revisiting and refining known results on gradient descent, we provide new results for mirror descent with Bregman divergence projection, for generalized linear models trained by gradient descent and exponentiated gradient descent, and for randomized predictors. We illustrate and supplement these theoretical findings with experiments on generalized linear models.



1
Introduction

This paper introduces
basic inequalities
for first-order optimization algorithms
that connect implicit and explicit regularization.
We consider an optimization problem
\min_{\theta}f(\theta)
and study the relationship between

\theta_{T}\quad\text{and}\quad{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}},

where
\theta_{T}
is the
T
-th iterate of a first-order iterative optimization algorithm,
and
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}:=\argmin_{\theta}\{f(\theta)+\lambda g(\theta)\}

denotes the explicitly regularized solution with penalty
g

and regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
.
While
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
represents the classical approach for explicit regularization,

\theta_{T}
captures implicit regularization,
where the optimization algorithm enforces an inductive bias and favors specific solutions in the model

(
43
)
.
A popular example of this is early stopping, which focuses on the finite termination time
T
of the algorithm

(
46
)
.
Consequently, one of its central research directions is to relate
the early-stopped estimator
\theta_{T}
to the corresponding explicitly regularized solution
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}

(
54
;
2
)
.

The basic inequality allows us to interpret
the number of iterations as an effective regularization coefficient.
It upper bounds the gap
f(\theta_{T})-f(z)
for any reference point
z

in terms of two quantities:
the distances between
\theta_{0}
,
\theta_{T}
, and
z
;
and the accumulated step sizes, which represent the total elapsed time.
For example, gradient descent initialized at
\theta_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and a constant step size
\eta
achieves the following basic inequality:

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{2\eta T}\Big(\|z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big)

for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T\in\mathbb{N}
.
Meanwhile, in explicit regularization with loss
f
and penalty
g
,
the solution
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
satisfies the standard zeroth order optimality condition:

f({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-f(z)\leq\lambda\Big(g(z)-g({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})\Big).

For example, in the lasso problem with

f(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\|Y-X\theta\|_{2}^{2}
and
g(\theta)=\|\theta\|_{1}

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
rearranging the above inequality yields the so-called basic inequality

(
14
)
,
central to statistical risk analysis of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
:

\frac{1}{2n}\big\|X({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta)\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\big\langle Y-X\theta,X({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta)\big\rangle+\lambda\big(\|\theta\|_{1}-\|{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}\|_{1}\big).

The close structural resemblance between the bounds on

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)
and
f({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-f(z)

suggests a unified way to bridge implicit and explicit regularization through these basic inequalities.

While related inequalities
appear in the optimization literature for convergence analyses

(
42
;
40
;
48
)
,
and have been implicitly used in recent work

(
29
;
28
;
65
;
66
)
,
we isolate and highlight specific forms as the central framework.
By treating the basic inequalities as primary objects,
we provide a well-rounded tool for statistical analysis of implicit regularization.

Summary of contributions.

Our contributions are as follows.

•

A single analytical framework.

We introduce
basic inequalities
for the iterate
\theta_{T}
of first-order algorithms,
including gradient descent and mirror descent.
We demonstrate that they serve as well-rounded tools for understanding implicit regularization,
as detailed in the applications below (Sections
2
and
7
).

•

Training envelopes and dynamics.

Starting from the basic inequality,
we derive a training envelope that provides lower and upper bounds on
the combined loss and penalty of the iterates relative to the explicitly regularized solution.
We also characterize the asymptotic behavior of the algorithms, which converge to specific solutions (Section
2
).

•

Statistical risk bounds.

We establish high-probability prediction risk bounds of the iterates,
demonstrating that they match the optimal rates of their explicit regularization counterparts.
Specifically, for sample size
n
, dimension
d
, and parameter radius
b
,
our framework yields the following results:

–

For gradient descent on generalized linear models,
we obtain a rate of
\tilde{O}(b\sqrt{d/n})
,
matching the rate of ridge regularization (Section
3
).

–

For exponentiated gradient descent on model aggregation,
we obtain a rate of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{(b\log d)/n})
,
matching the rate of KL-divergence regularization (Section
4
).

–

For exponentiated gradient descent on randomized predictors,
noting that the iterates coincide with the KL-regularized solutions in this setting,
we obtain a rate of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{(b\log d)/n)}
(Section
5
).

•

Experiments.

We supplement our theory with experiments
using gradient descent and exponentiated gradient descent
on linear, logistic, and Poisson regression tasks.
Across both underparameterized and overparameterized regimes,
the results show strong empirical alignment between implicit and explicit regularization
in terms of training dynamics, prediction risk curves, and solution paths (Section
6
).

With the exception of the basic inequalities, all proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

1.1
Related work

Implicit regularization.

Implicit regularization
(
43
)

refers to the phenomenon where optimization algorithms favor certain solutions among many candidates.
A canonical example arises in overparameterized linear regression:
gradient descent initialized at the origin converges to the minimum
\ell^{2}
-norm solution
among the infinite set of interpolators
(
21
)
.
While much of this literature focuses on the asymptotics of the iterates where
T\to\infty
,
the characterization of such inductive bias differs across losses and algorithms.

The perspective that an algorithm can enforce proprerties vastly stronger than
those by the loss was studied earlier in the boosting literature.
It was observed that coordinate descent methods like AdaBoost do not merely minimize training error,
but are correlated with increases in the
\ell^{1}
-margin
(
52
;
50
)
.
This max-margin perspective was later formalized with convergence rate analyses

(
69
;
56
)
.

In the context of logistic regression on separable data,
this analysis has been extended to gradient descent,
which is now known to converge to the maximum
\ell^{2}
-margin direction

(
53
;
29
)
.
Analogous results have been established for mirror descent

(
55
;
22
)
.
For broader loss classes for which the infimum of the risk is unattainable,
the gradient descent path and a corresponding explicit regularization path
converge to the same direction
(
28
)
.

In the modern deep learning era,
the generalization capacity of neural networks despite significant overparametrization
has reinvigorated the study of implicit regularization.
Theoretical progress includes
double descent
(
11
)
,
neural tangent kernel
(
27
;
4
)
,
and benign overfitting
(
6
;
24
)
,
alongside recent work on self-attention and transformers
(
20
;
39
)
.

Explicit regularization.

Explicit regularization, achieved by adding a penalty term to the original loss function,
is a standard approach to regularization supported by a mature theoretical toolkit
in both asymptotic and non-asymptotic regimes

(
25
;
61
;
62
)
.
In some parametric models like ridge regression,
the existence of closed form solutions enables precise risk characterization,
with sharp asymptotic results derivable by random matrix theory

(
19
;
24
)
.
For lasso
(
57
)
,
non-asymptotic analysis typically relies on the ‘basic inequality’ derived from zeroth-order optimality conditions,
which yields risk bounds at slow or fast rates depending on the conditions on
X

(
60
;
14
)
.
In classification,
\ell^{2}
-regularized logistic regression
utilizes the self-concordance property to derive risk bounds
(
5
)
.
Non-parametric models also adapt explicit regularization, including

\ell^{2}
-regularized regression in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
(
16
)

and trend filtering with total variation penalties for piecewise polynomial functions
(
58
)
.

Early stopping.

Distinct from the asymptotic behavior of an algorithm,
early stopping is another type of implicit regularization,
which considers the finite number of iterations
T
to terminate the algorithm.
Originally started as a heuristic to prevent overfitting in neural networks
(
46
)
,
this technique has been analyzed in statistical learning theory
(
69
;
68
)
.
For early-stopped gradient descent,
minimax optimal convergence rates have been studied in boosting and kernel regression

(
15
;
47
;
63
)
.
Similarly, in shallow neural networks with the logistic loss,
it has been shown to achieve low calibration error in binary classification
(
28
)
.
More recent work on logistic regression with linearly separable data
derives risk bounds
(
66
)

and shows that large step sizes induce phase transitions during training
(
65
)
.

A significant body of work investigates the connection between early stopping and explicit penalties,
though these connections are typically established within specific loss or algorithmic contexts.
In linear regression,
continuous gradient flow closely tracks ridge regression under the relation

T\approx 1/\lambda

(
2
)
.
This relationship extends to gradient descent on strongly convex functions
(
54
)

and stochastic gradient flow
(
1
)
.
From a spectral perspective,
this equivalence is further supported by analyses in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

(
68
;
63
)
.

KL-divergence regularization.

The use of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence as a penalty term in explicit regularization
is less common than
\ell^{p}
-regularization in modern statistical learning.
Nevertheless, it serves as a powerful tool for certain learning tasks,
particularly those involving the estimation of probability distributions over a collection of candidate models.
Moreover, KL-divergence regularization plays a central role in the
PAC-Bayes
framework;
see
3
and the references therein.

Two prominent applications of KL-divergence regularization considered in this paper are

model aggregation

(
64
;
12
)
and

randomized predictors

(
37
;
70
)
,
both of which involve optimizing probability weights to combine predictors from a given collection of base models.
In model aggregation,
the final predictor is constructed as a convex combination of the base predictors
according to the learned probability vector.
By contrast, a randomized predictor is a single base model,
randomly selected for prediction according to the learned probability vector.
In both settings, the KL penalty regularizes the probability vector by
penalizing its deviation from a prior distribution,
which is typically chosen to be the uniform distribution over the candidate models.

2
Basic inequalities

This section introduces
basic inequalities
and shows their utility
through a direct application to training dynamics.
While similar inequalities have been used in classical and recent optimization literature,
as described in the introduction,
we highlight a specific formulation that translates the number of iterations into
an effective regularization level.
Doing so, we establish a standalone framework for implicit regularization analysis,
with additional applications in later sections.

2.1
Gradient descent

Gradient descent
(
17
)

on a differentiable loss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with initialization
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and step sizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}

generates iterates via

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t}).

(1)

Theorem
1
presents our first basic inequality result for gradient descent,
which bounds the objective value of the iterate
\theta_{T}
with respect to any reference point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 1

(Basic inequality; gradient descent)
.

Let a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be convex, differentiable, and
L
-smooth for some
L>0
.
Consider gradient descent with iterates (
1
) with step sizes
\eta_{t}\in(0,1/L]
.
Then, for any reference point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any stopping time
T\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}\Big(\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

In particular, for a constant step size
\eta_{t}=\eta
, this simplifies to

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2\eta T}\Big(\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

(2)

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof proceeds in three steps.

Step 1
: Bounding the proximity difference between
t
and
t+1
. We measure proximity via the Euclidean distance.
For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|\theta_{t}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{t+1}-z\|_{2}^{2}=\|\theta_{t}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t})-z\|_{2}^{2}=2\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t}-z\rangle-\eta_{t}^{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

Step 2
: Bounding the criterion difference
f(\theta_{t})-f(z)
.
We know
f(\theta_{t})-f(z)\leq\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t}-z\rangle

by convexity of
f
.
Substituting this into the result from Step 1,

2\eta_{t}(f(\theta_{t})-f(z))\leq\eta_{t}^{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\|\theta_{t}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{t+1}-z\|_{2}^{2}.

The
L
-smoothness of
f
and
\eta_{t}\in(0,1/L]
guarantee,
via the descent lemma (Lemma
B1
), that

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta_{t}\big(1-\frac{L}{2}\eta_{t}\big)\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq f(\theta_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

This ensures
f(\theta_{T})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

for any
t<T
.
Using this to lower bound
f(\theta_{t})-f(z)
by
f(\theta_{T})-f(z)
,

2\eta_{t}(f(\theta_{T})-f(z))\leq\|\theta_{t}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{t+1}-z\|_{2}^{2}.

Step 3
: Aggregating bounds over iterations.
Summing the result of Step 2 over
t<T
gives a telescoping sum:

2\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}(f(\theta_{T})-f(z))\leq\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2},

which concludes the proof after rearrangement.
∎

To provide a clear interpretation of the basic inequality,
note that the bound in (
2
) decomposes into two components:
the distances between
\theta_{0}
,
\theta_{T}
, and
z
;
and the accumulated step sizes
\eta T
.
When viewing gradient descent as a discretization of continuous gradient flow,

\eta T
represents the total elapsed time.
Thus, the bound translates the number of iterations into an effective
\ell^{2}
-regularization coefficient.

A subtle distinction remains, however, between this bound and explicit regularization.
While the term
\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2}
in (
2
) entangles
\theta_{T}
and
z
,
the explicit regularization decouples them in penalty terms.
The following corollary resolves this
and yields a bound that exactly matches the structure of explicit regularization,
but with coefficients in a fixed 1:4 ratio.
This result provides compelling evidence of an algorithm-inherent regularization effect.

Corollary 1

(Training envelope; gradient descent)
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1
,
consider gradient descent (
1
) with a constant step size
\eta\in(0,1/L]
.
Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
T\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\lambda_{T}:=1/(\eta T)
,

\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(f(z)+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{4}\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\;\leq\;f(\theta_{T})+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{4}\|\theta_{0}-\theta_{T}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(f(z)+\lambda_{T}\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

(3)

The next corollary derives standard properties of gradient descent’s training dynamics.
While these are not the primary focus of the paper,
they demonstrate a direct application of our framework by recovering classical results for
discrete gradient descent and continuous gradient flow
(
36
)
.
Importantly, all of these statements follow from a single primitive, the basic inequality.

Corollary 2

(Training dynamics; gradient descent)
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1
,
consider gradient descent (
1
) with step sizes
\eta_{t}\in(0,1/L]
.

1.

(Asymptotic training loss.)
Let
\inf f:=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\theta)\in[-\infty,\infty)
.
If
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
, then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}f(\theta_{t})=\inf f.

2.

(Non-increasing distance to solution set.)
Define the solution set
S:=\{\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(\theta^{*})=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\theta)\}
.
Note that
S
is closed and convex, but possibly empty.
Let
{\mathrm{Dist}}_{S}(u):=\min_{s\in S}\|u-s\|_{2}
denote the distance from
u
to
S
.
If
S\neq\emptyset
, then

\forall s\in S,\;\{\|\theta_{t}-s\|_{2}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}\;\text{is non-increasing},\quad\text{and thus},\quad\{{\mathrm{Dist}}_{S}(\theta_{t})\}_{t=1}^{\infty}\;\text{is non-increasing}.

3.

(Limit of iterates.)
If
S\neq\emptyset
and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
, then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\theta_{t}=\theta_{\infty}\in S.

Furthermore, let
{\mathrm{Proj}}_{S}(u):=\argmin_{s\in S}\|u-s\|_{2}
denote
the projection of
u
onto
S
.
Then

\|\theta_{\infty}-{\mathrm{Proj}}_{S}(\theta_{0})\|_{2}\leq{\mathrm{Dist}}_{S}(\theta_{0}),\quad\text{and thus},\quad\|\theta_{\infty}-\theta_{0}\|_{2}\leq 2{\mathrm{Dist}}_{S}(\theta_{0}).

4.

(Minimum norm solution.) If
S
is a non-empty affine subspace and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
, then

\theta_{\infty}={\mathrm{Proj}}_{S}(\theta_{0}).

Whereas Corollary
1
gives a combined envelope,
Corollary
2
describes the individual behavior of

f(\theta_{T})
and
\|\theta_{0}-\theta_{T}\|_{2}
separately.
The first statement establishes the consistency of the algorithm
by showing that it approaches the infimum.
The second and third statements address the stability of the iterates
with respect to the solution set
S
and the projection of the initial point onto
S
.
The last statement characterizes the limit point of the iterates for an affine solution set.
This result generalizes a well-known phenomenon in the overparameterized linear regression,
that gradient descent initialized at the origin converges to the minimum norm solution.
This corollary demonstrates that this limit behavior is not unique to linear regression,
but a consequence of the broader principle captured by the corollary.

2.2
Mirror descent

Mirror descent
(
41
;
10
)
generalizes gradient descent
by replacing the Euclidean distance with a Bregman divergence induced by a function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
:

{D_{\phi}}(u,v):=\phi(u)-\phi(v)-\langle\nabla\phi(v),u-v\rangle,

where
u,v\in\Omega
and
\nabla\phi(v)
is well-defined.
Given a constraint set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\Omega
,
initialization
\theta_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
,
and step sizes
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
,
mirror descent generates iterates as

\theta_{t+1}:=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}}\Big(\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle+D_{\phi}(\theta,\theta_{t})\Big).

(4)

This update has an equivalent two-stage formulation that
first compute
\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}
then performs a Bregman projection onto
{\mathcal{C}}

(
13
)
:

\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle+{D_{\phi}}(\theta,\theta_{t})\Big),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}}{D_{\phi}}(\theta,\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}).

A popular example of mirror descent is the exponentiated gradient descent algorithm,
which is designed for optimization over the probability simplex

\Delta_{d}:=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}=1\}
.
By choosing the negative entropy function
\phi(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\log a_{i}

and
{\mathcal{C}}=\Omega=\Delta_{d}
,
the induced Bregman divergence becomes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence,

{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\log(a_{i}/b_{i})
.
Note that
\phi
is
1
-strongly convex on
\Delta_{d}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{1}
by Pinsker’s inequality.
In this setting, the two-stage mirror descent formulation yields a multiplicative update.
Given
\theta_{t}\in{\mathrm{int}}(\Delta_{d})
,
where
\odot
denotes coordinate-wise multiplication,

\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}=\theta_{t}\odot\exp(-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t})),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}\,/\,\|\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}\|_{1}.

(5)

Mirror descent can also recover
projected gradient descent
over a closed convex set
{\mathcal{C}}
.
In the unconstrained setting
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
it is equivalent to gradient descent.
Taking
\phi=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
,
the two-stage formulation becomes a gradient step followed by a Euclidean projection onto
{\mathcal{C}}
,
since its Bregman divergence is
{D_{\phi}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\|u-v\|_{2}^{2}
:

\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}}\|\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

Below we introduce a basic inequality for mirror descent iterates in (
4
).
Just as mirror descent generalizes gradient descent,
Theorem
2
serves as a direct generalization of Theorem
1
.
In particular, by selecting
\phi
as the squared Euclidean distance,
Theorem
2
reduces exactly to Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2

(Basic inequality; mirror descent)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
and
\Omega
be closed convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\Omega
, and their interiors are not empty.
Suppose that
(i) a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is convex on
{\mathcal{C}}
, and it is differentiable on
{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
; and
(ii) a function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of Legendre type, and it is continuous on
\Omega
.
Further assume that
(iii)
f
is
L
-smooth with respect to
\|\cdot\|
for
L>0
on
{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
; and
(iv)
\phi
is
\alpha
-strongly convex for
\alpha>0
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Consider mirror descent (
4
) with step sizes
\eta_{t}\in(0,\alpha/L]
.
Then, for any reference point
z\in{\mathcal{C}}
and any stopping time
T\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}\Big(D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{0})-D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{T})\Big).

In particular, for a constant step size
\eta_{t}=\eta
, this simplifies to

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{\eta T}\Big(D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{0})-D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{T})\Big).

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The proof parallels that of the gradient descent case, but leverages properties of the Bregman divergence.

Step 1
: Bounding the proximity difference between
t
and
t+1
.
We measure proximity via the Bregman divergence.
The well-known three-point identity for the Bregman divergence states that

{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1})+{D_{\phi}}(\theta_{t+1},\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})=\langle\nabla\phi(\theta_{t})-\nabla\phi(\theta_{t+1}),z-\theta_{t+1}\rangle.

Step 2
: Bounding the criterion difference
f(\theta_{t})-f(z)
.
Convexity of
f
on
{\mathcal{C}}
implies that

f(\theta_{t})-f(z)\leq\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t}-z\rangle=\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}\rangle+\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-z\rangle.

Also Step 1 implies, via Lemma
B5
, that

\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-z\rangle\leq{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1})-D_{\phi}(\theta_{t+1},\theta_{t}).

Combining these two inequalities, we have

\eta_{t}\Big(f(\theta_{t})-f(z)\Big)\leq\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1}\rangle+{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1})-{D_{\phi}}(\theta_{t+1},\theta_{t}).

Meanwhile, the
L
-smoothness of
f
yields

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})+\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\rangle+(L/2)\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|^{2}
,
and the
\alpha
-strong convexity of
\phi
implies that

D_{\phi}(\theta_{t+1},\theta_{t})\geq(\alpha/2)\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|^{2}

(Lemma
B3
).
Substituting these into the above inequality,

\eta_{t}\Big(f(\theta_{t+1})-f(z)\Big)\leq{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1})-\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{L}{2}\eta_{t}\Big)\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|^{2}\leq{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1}),

where
\eta_{t}\leq\alpha/L
is used in the last inequality.
The descent lemma for mirror descent (Lemma
B6
) shows that

f(\theta_{t})
is non-increasing, thus we have

\eta_{t}\Big(f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\Big)\leq{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1}).

Step 3
: Aggregating bounds over iterations.
Summing the result of Step 2 over
t<T
gives a telescoping sum:

\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\Big(f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\Big)\leq D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{0})-D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{T}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

One might wonder about the need to present
Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
separately,
given that one is a special case of the other.
However, this dual presentation illustrates not only the similarity but also
the distinct geometries of each algorithm.
The gradient descent analysis directly leverages the alignment between
its update and the Euclidean inner product,
yielding
\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\rangle=\eta_{t}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}
.
On the other hand, the mirror descent analysis is more delicate,
handling the interplay between its iterate and a more general geometry in the space.
By examining their basic inequalities and proofs side by side,
we gain a richer understanding of how algorithmic choice and corresponding geometry interact.
Consistent with this approach,
we now present the mirror descent analogues of

and
2
from gradient descent,
describing training envelope and training dynamics of the algorithm.

3
Generalized linear models and ridge regularity

We now shift to a statistical perspective to analyze the prediction risk of the iterates.
This section focuses on generalized linear models (GLMs)
and provides a comparative analysis of two regularization schemes:
implicit regularization via gradient descent and explicit ridge regularization.

We adopt a standard GLM setup with an identity sufficient statistic.
A more formal and general definition of GLMs and the underlying exponential families
is provided in the Appendix.
Let
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the feature matrix and the response vector.
The negative log-likelihood loss function
\ell
for the GLM
and its (unregularized) estimator
{\hat{\theta}_{0}}
are defined as

\ell(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\Big(-Y^{\top}X\theta+{A}(X\theta)\Big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\hat{\theta}_{0}}:=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell(\theta),

(7)

where the map
{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
acts componentwise
as
{A}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(v_{i})
.
Here
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the cumulant function of
the univariate exponential family associated with the specific GLM.

We consider a
fixed-
X
data generating process

where
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is fixed.
With independent observations

y_{i}\sim P_{i}
, where each
P_{i}
may depend on
x_{i}
,
we have
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}Y=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})^{\top}
denote the mean vector
and
{\epsilon}:=Y-{\mu}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}^{\top})
the noise.
Model misspecification is allowed in two sense:

P_{i}
is not required to belong to the exponential family used to define the GLM loss;
and the true mean
{\mu}
is not required to satisfy the canonical link.
For example,
{\mu}
in the Gaussian case or
the log-odds
\log(\tfrac{\mu}{1-\mu})
in the Bernoulli case
may not be linear in
X
.

The
prediction risk
of an estimator
\theta=\theta(X,Y)

is evaluated on a new response vector
\tilde{Y}=(\tilde{y}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{y}_{n})^{\top}

generated from the same feature matrix
X
.
In other words,
\tilde{Y}
is an independent copy of
Y
conditional on
X
.
Then the prediction risk is defined as

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Y}}\Big[-\tilde{Y}^{\top}X\theta+{A}(X\theta)\,\big|\,X,Y\Big]=\frac{1}{n}\Big(-{\mu}^{\top}X\theta+{A}(X\theta)\Big).

(8)

An important observation in this GLM setup is that
the prediction risk admits the following decomposition:

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)=\ell(\theta)+\frac{1}{n}\epsilon^{\top}X\theta.

(9)

This expresses the prediction risk as the sum of
the training error and a stochastic term linear in the noise.
This structure will allow us to apply the basic inequality to derive high-probability risk bounds.

3.1
Risk analysis: ridge-regularized GLMs

We begin with ridge regularization, which is a common form of explicit regularization.
The primary purpose of this section is to establish an elementary yet rigorous risk bound
that will be compared with the implicit regularization induced by gradient descent,
which appears in the following subsection.

The ridge-regularized GLM estimator is defined by
augmenting the GLM loss (
7
) with the squared
\ell^{2}
penalty.
For a regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, we define

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}:=\argmin\nolimits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\{\ell(\theta)+\lambda\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\big\}.

(10)

This definition is consistent with
the unregularized estimator
{\hat{\theta}_{0}}
in (
7
) when
\lambda=0
.
Since the cumulant function
A
for the exponential family is convex,
the GLM loss
\ell
is also convex.
Consequently, for any
\lambda>0
,
the objective function in (
10
) is
2\lambda
-strongly convex,
and thus the minimizer
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
exists and is unique.
A formal proof of these properties is provided in the Appendix.

The following proposition upper bounds the prediction risk of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}

relative to an arbitrary reference point
\theta
.
This bound is derived by using the rewrite of prediction risk for GLM in (
9
).

Proposition 1

(Risk bound; ridge-regularized GLM)
.

For any
\lambda>0
and any reference point
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the prediction risk of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
10
)
satisfies

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})\leq{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

The above bound decomposes the risk gap

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)
into two parts:
a stochastic term scaled by
\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}

and a regularization term scaled by
2\lambda
.
Therefore
\lambda
has a central role in balancing two competing sources of error.
Deriving a statistical guarantee from this deterministic bound,
we further assume sub-Gaussian noise,
yielding a high-probability excess risk bound.

Proposition 2

(Risk bound; ridge-regularized GLM; sub-Gaussian noise)
.

Assume each
\epsilon_{i}

is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
Let
\sigma^{2}:=\max_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}
and
{\widehat{\Sigma}}:=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}X
.
Then, for any
\delta>0
and
b>0
, choosing

\lambda=\frac{\sigma}{2b\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}

(11)

yields the following bound with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
:

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-\inf_{\theta:\;\|\theta\|_{2}\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{2b\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}.

(12)

The bound (
12
) is governed by
the spectral properties of the empirical covariance
{\widehat{\Sigma}}
.
In a standard setting with independent sub-Gaussian entries in
X
,
the following concentration results hold
(
61
)
:

{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}=O(d),\quad{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}=O(\sqrt{d}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}=O(1)

(13)

with respect to
d
.
Under these conditions, choosing
\delta=\log n
yields an excess risk bound
of
\tilde{O}(b\sigma\sqrt{d/n})
with probability at least
1-1/n
.

We now present our main result for the ridge-penalized GLM estimator.
Theorem
3
tailors the general oracle inequality in
Proposition
2
to specific GLMs by
identifying the explicit sub-Gaussian parameter for the noise
\epsilon_{i}
.
Notably, Poisson regression requires a truncation argument due to
the exponential nature of the loss fuction,
resulting in a slightly weaker probability guarantee than
that for linear and logistic regression.

Theorem 3

(Risk bound; ridge-regularized GLM; specific cases)
.

Consider the ridge-regularized GLM estimator
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
10
)
with
\lambda>0
.
Suppose one of the following cases holds for all
i\in[n]
:

1.

Gaussian (linear regression):

P_{i}={\mathcal{N}}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
and set
\sigma=\max_{i\in[n]}\sigma_{i}
.

2.

Bernoulli (logistic regression):

P_{i}=\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mu_{i})
and set
\sigma=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

3.

Poisson (Poisson regression):

P_{i}=\mathrm{Pois}(\mu_{i})
and set

\sigma=(2\|{\mu}\|_{\infty}+\tfrac{2}{3})\log n+\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\mu}\|_{\infty}
;
and
n\geq 3
.

Then, for any
\delta>0
and
b>0
, if we choose

\lambda=\frac{\sigma}{2b\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta},

then the following high-probability excess risk bound holds:

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-\inf_{\theta:\;\|\theta\|_{2}\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{2b\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}.

with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
in cases 1 and 2, and at least
1-1/n-e^{-\delta}
in case 3.

This theorem provides a unified prediction risk bound for
ridge regularization on common GLMs.
Under the spectral assumptions of
{\widehat{\Sigma}}
in (
13
),
the result simplifies to a bound of order
\tilde{O}(b\sigma\sqrt{d/n})
.
A key feature of the theorem is its robustness to model misspecification.
In other words,
the true distribution
P_{i}
of
y_{i}
given
x_{i}
need not belong to
the exponential family associated with the GLM loss,
and the true mean
\mu
does not need to satisfy the canonical link with
X
.
The analysis relies solely on the sub-Gaussian tail bounds for
\epsilon_{i}
.
Therefore, the bound is not necessarily limited to the distributions used in the theorem,
but remains valid when
\epsilon_{i}
have compatible tail behavior.
For example, the result holds when applying logistic regression to any bounded
y_{i}
,
or applying linear regression to Poisson
y_{i}
.

Comparison with existing literature.

Our derived bound reflects the standard slow rate
O(\sqrt{d/n})
for risk minimization.
In statistical learning theory, such rates are expected for many function classes,
as captured by Rademacher complexity, Gaussian complexity, and VC dimension

(
7
;
31
;
62
)
.
It is important to note that
while
\sqrt{d/n}
implies a slower asymptotic decay than
d/n
,
the actual finite-sample risk contains problem-dependent constants
such as the signal size
b
and noise level
\sigma
.
In the following discussion, we omit these constants for brevity.

Meanwhile, from a classical asymptotic perspective,
a fast rate of
O(d/n)
is often expected.
The asymptotic theory of
M
-estimators establishes that,
under some regularity conditions, the excess risk has an
O(d/n)
bound
due to the asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimator
governed by Fisher information
(
35
;
44
)
.
However, these analyses rely on the sample size
n
being sufficiently large
to ensure the MLE converges to the normal distribution.
While this provides an asymptotic benchmark,
finding whether such a fast rate holds in finite-sample regimes
depends heavily on the specific problem structure.

For linear and ridge regression, the fast rate is achievable via their closed form solutions.
Exact risk expressions can be derived via the bias–variance decomposition

(
31
;
2
)
.
Naively selecting the ordinary least squares estimator with
\lambda=0
yields
a risk bound of
O(d/n)
.
However, by optimally tuning
\lambda
, a bound of
O(\sqrt{d/n})
can be achieved.
While it is asymptotically slower,
this bound may yield a faster rate in finite-sample regimes
than the ordinary least squares estimator,
due to the omitted problem-dependent constants.
Furthermore, the precise limiting behavior of the prediction risk in proportional asymptotic regimes,
where
d/n\to\gamma
,
has been characterized via random matrix theory

(
24
;
19
;
59
)
.
Meanwhile, we provide a simple analysis in the Appendix
for the prediction risk (
8
) of linear regression estimator,
which achieves
\tilde{O}(d/n)
high-probability bound
via its closed form solution.

For logistic regression, the rate analysis is more nuanced.
The fast rate of
O(d/n)
is achievable in finite samples,
but only under specific conditions.
For misspecified models,
a rate of
O(d/\sqrt{n})
has been established by
leveraging the self-concordance property of the logistic loss
5
.
For well-specified models,
an
O(d/n)
bound has been proven
(
44
)
.
However, this fast bound is conditional on the sample size being sufficiently large,
for example,
n=\Omega(d^{2})
or
n=\Omega(d)

with large constant factors.

For Poisson regression, a rigorous finite-sample
O(d/n)
bound remains open,
to the best of our knowledge.

In summary, Theorem
3
offers a unified perspective
on prediction risk for a range of GLMs.
While the theorem may not strictly recover the fast
O(d/n)
rate in every instance,
it does provide a universal guarantee that holds without restrictive or complicated conditions,
and in certain cases, offers a comparable or tighter bound than existing specialized analyses.

3.2
Risk analysis: gradient descent on GLMs

Shifting from explicit to implicit regularization,

Initializing at

Proposition 3

(Risk bound; (projected) gradient descent on GLM)

Assume the GLM loss

\mathbb{R}^{d}

{\mathrm{Risk}}\big({\theta_{T}}\big)\leq{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{T}}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

Mirroring the development of the ridge regularization case,

Proposition 4

Under the same assumptions as Proposition

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\theta_{T}})-\inf_{\theta:\;\|\theta\|_{2}\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{b\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}.

For general

T=\Big\lceil\frac{1}{\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*}}\Big\rceil,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad T=\argmin_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\{\lambda_{t}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*}\},

the same bound holds with an additional discretization error term

(\sigma^{2}\eta/(2n))\cdot({\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta)
.

Finally, building on this proposition,
we present the risk bounds for gradient descent on specific GLMs.
This result mirrors Theorem
3
for ridge-regularized GLMs,
and remains robust to model misspecification.

Theorem 4

(Risk bound; (projected) gradient descent on GLM; specific cases)
.

Let the distributions
P_{i}
and
\sigma
be defined as in Theorem
3
:
Gaussian, Bernoulli, and Poisson cases.
For each GLM, the loss function
\ell
in (
7
) is
L
-smooth on the specified domain
with
L
given by

1.

Gaussian (linear regression):

L=\|{\widehat{\Sigma}}\|_{\rm op}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

2.

Bernoulli (logistic regression):

L=\frac{1}{4}\|{\widehat{\Sigma}}\|_{\rm op}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

3.

Poisson (Poisson regression):

L=\|{\widehat{\Sigma}}\|_{\rm op}\exp(b\cdot\max\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq n}\|x_{i}\|_{2})
on

{\mathsf{B}}_{d}(b)
for any
b>0
.

Consider gradient descent for cases 1 and 2,
or projected gradient descent on
{\mathsf{B}}_{d}(b)
for case 3,
initialized at
\theta_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a constant step size
\eta\in(0,1/L]
.
For any
\delta>0
and
b>0
,
define the target regularization coefficient

\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*}=\frac{\sigma}{b\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}.

Suppose the stopping time

T=\frac{1}{\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*}}

is an integer, then with probability
at least
1-e^{-\delta}
for cases 1 and 2,
or at least
1-1/n-e^{-\delta}
for case 3,

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\theta_{T}})-\inf_{\theta:\;\|\theta\|_{2}\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{b\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}\sqrt{\delta}+2{\big\|\widehat{\Sigma}\big\|_{\rm op}}\delta}.

For general
T=\lceil 1/(\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*})\rceil
, i.e.,

T=\argmin_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\{\lambda_{t}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{gd}}^{*}\}
,
the same bound holds with an additional discretization error specified
in Proposition
4
.

Theorem
4
shows that early-stopped gradient descent achieves
essentially the same risk bound as optimally tuned ridge regularization in Theorem
3
,
differing only by a factor of
2
.
Therefore, under the spectral assumptions of
{\widehat{\Sigma}}
in (
13
),
one obtains the same bound of
\tilde{O}(b\sigma\sqrt{d/n})
as the ridge regularization.
This result highlights the parallel roles between the number of iterations
T
and
the regularization coefficient
\lambda
in controlling the prediction risk.

Comparison with existing literature.

For linear regression, the relationship between estimators obtained by
gradient descent and ridge regularization has been characterized with high precision.
Leveraging their closed form solutions,
it can be proven that the prediction risk of gradient flow at time
T

is within a constant factor of the ridge solution with
\lambda=1/T

(
2
)
;
moreover, the derived explicit expression demonstrates the necessity of tuning the stopping time
T
.
From a spectral perspective,
particularly in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS),
both early-stopped gradient descent and ridge regularization
behave as low-pass filters,
retaining directional components corresponding to larger eigenvalues
(
68
)
.
In the related context of boosting on RKHS,
more precise characterization of the optimal stopping time
T
is possible
under specific eigenvalue decay conditions of the kernel matrix
(
63
)
.

The analysis of logistic regression is distinct due to the absence of closed form solutions.
In the asymptotic limit, it is well-established that for linearly separable data,
gradient descent converges to the maximum
L_{2}
margin direction,
with the estimator norm diverging to infinity
(
53
;
29
)
.
In the finite-sample regime,
risk bounds for early-stopped gradient descent have been derived recently,
which prove the existence of a beneficial early stopping time, without providing a closed form,
that obtains an
O(\sqrt{d/n})
rate for misspecified model and an
O(d/n)
rate for well-specified model

(
66
)
.
They also suggest a geometric connection between gradient descent and ridge regularization,
with respect to the angle between their estimators.
Meanwhile, the training dynamics with large step sizes in linearly separable data exhibit
phase transitions that occur prior to convergence
(
65
)
.

4
Model aggregation with KL regularization

In this section, we investigate mirror descent
and its explicit regularization counterpart,
Bregman-divergence-regularization.
As a primary application of this, we focus on exponentiated gradient descent
and the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty.
While this section specializes in the KL case,
the general results for Bregman-divergence-regularization and
mirror descent are presented in the Appendix.
We conclude the section by discussing how these results relate to
model aggregation, also known as stacking.

4.1
Risk analysis: KL-regularized GLMs

With data
(X,Y)
under the fixed-design setting described in
Section

3

and the GLM loss
\ell(\theta)
in (
7
),
we define the
KL-regularized GLM estimator
as

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}:=\argmin_{\theta\in{\Delta_{d}}}\big\{\ell(\theta)+\lambda{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\big\},

(14)

where
{\Delta_{d}}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\theta_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}=1\}

is the probability simplex,

z\in{\Delta_{d}}
is an arbitrary anchor point,
and
{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\log(a_{i}/b_{i})

denotes the KL divergence between
two probability vectors
a,b\in{\Delta_{d}}
.
The proposition below establishes a prediction risk bound for

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
relative to any reference parameter
\theta
.

Proposition 5

(Risk bound; KL-regularized GLM)
.

For any
\lambda>0
,
an anchor point
z\in{\Delta_{d}}
,
and a reference point
\theta\in{\Delta_{d}}
,
the prediction risk of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
14
) satisfies

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\hat{\theta}_{\lambda})-{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{\infty}^{2}+2\lambda{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z).

This resembles
Proposition

1
for the ridge-regularized GLM.
Both are in fact instances of a general result for
Bregman-divergence-regularized GLMs (see the Appendix).
Consequently, under a sub-Gaussian noise assumption analogous to

Proposition

2
, optimally tuning
\lambda
yields
an oracle type risk bound for
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
.

Proposition 6

(Risk bound; KL-regularized GLM; sub-Gaussian noise)
.

Fix any
z\in{\Delta_{d}}
.
Assume each
\epsilon_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
Let
\sigma:=\max(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
.
Further assume that
\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|{X_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}j}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}
,
where
{X_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}j}}
denotes the
j
-th column of
X
.
Then, for any
\delta>0
and
b>0
, choosing

\lambda=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2d)+\delta}{nb}},

yields the following bound with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
:

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\hat{\theta}_{\lambda})-\inf_{\theta:\;{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq 4\sigma\sqrt{\frac{b(\log(2d)+\delta)}{n}}.

The column-norm assumption in the above proposition can be relaxed by
tracking an additional factor
C_{n,d}=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|{X_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}j}}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n}
.
In this case, the bound becomes

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\hat{\theta}_{\lambda})-\inf_{\theta:\;{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq 4\sigma C_{n,d}\sqrt{\frac{b(\log(2d)+\delta)}{n}}.

(15)

Finally, we specialize this to several common GLMs,
whose results and proofs parallel Theorem
3
.

Theorem 5

(Risk bound; KL-regularized GLM; specific cases)
.

Under the same assumptions as
Proposition

6
,
consider the KL-regularized GLM estimator in (
14
)
with
\lambda>0
and
the loss function
\ell
for Gaussian, Bernoulli, or Poisson cases.
Further assume that, for each case,
the distribution
P_{i}
and sub-Gaussian parameter
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma
are
as given in
Theorem

3
.
Then, for any
\delta>0
and
b>0
, if we choose

\lambda=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2d)+\delta}{nb}},

then the following high-probability excess risk bound holds:

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-\inf_{\theta:\;{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq 4\sigma\sqrt{\frac{b(\log(2d)+\delta)}{n}}

with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
for the Gaussian and Bernoulli cases,
and at least
1-1/n-e^{-\delta}
for the Poisson case.

4.2
Risk analysis: exponentiated gradient descent on GLMs

We now consider the implicit regularization counterpart of the KL-regularized GLM:
exponentiated gradient descent on the unpenalized GLM loss.
Extensions to general mirror descent,
which corresponds to Bregman-divergence-regularized GLMs,
are presented in the Appendix.

Recall the exponentiated gradient descent iterates
\theta_{t}

in (
5
), initialized at
\theta_{0}\in{\Delta_{d}}
.
Define
\lambda_{T}=\frac{1}{\eta T}
as the effective regularization parameter.
We obtain both data-dependent and oracle type risk bounds for
the exponentiated gradient descent iterate
{\theta_{T}}
,
similar to those for the KL-regularized GLM,
and derive bounds for specific GLMs.

Proposition 7

(Risk bound; exponentiated gradient descent on GLM)
.

Consider exponentiated gradient descent iterates
{\theta_{T}}
in (
5
)
initialized at
\theta_{0}\in{\Delta_{d}}

and with a constant step size
\eta\in(0,1/L]
.
Then, for any reference point
\theta\in{\Delta_{d}}
and

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\theta_{T}})-{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{T}}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{\infty}^{2}+\lambda_{T}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,\theta_{0}).

Proposition 8

(Risk bound; exponentiated gradient descent on GLM; sub-Gaussian noise)
.

Under the same assumptions as
Proposition

7
,
let
\epsilon_{i}
be sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma_{i}^{2}
and

\sigma:=\max(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
.
Further assume that
\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|{X_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}j}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}
,
where

denotes the
j
-th column of

.
For any
\delta>0
and

,
define the target regularization coefficient

\lambda_{\mathrm{egd}}^{*}=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2d)+\delta}{nb}}.

Suppose the stopping time

T=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{egd}}^{*}\eta}

1-e^{-\delta}
:

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\theta_{T}})-\inf_{\theta:\;{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,\theta_{0})\leq b}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq 2\sigma\sqrt{\frac{b(\log(2d)+\delta)}{n}}.

For general

,

T=\argmin_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\{\lambda_{t}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{egd}}^{*}\}
,
the same bound holds with an additional discretization error term

\eta^{2}\sigma^{3}\cdot(\log(2d)+\delta)^{3/2}/(n^{3/2}b^{1/2})
.

Theorem 6

(Risk bound; exponentiated gradient descent on GLM; specific cases)
.

Under the same assumptions as

,
consider the GLM with the loss function

and

.

4.3
Comparison with existing literature

The procedures studied in this section are closely related to

model aggregation
, also known as
stacking
.
Given base predictors
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
,
the goal is to form an aggregated predictor

h_{\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}h_{i}

under certain risk criterion

(
64
;
12
)
.
A large literature studies the statistical properties of such aggregates
and designs efficient algorithms to compute them.
Let
{\hat{\theta}}
denote the aggregation weights produced
by a statistical procedure (e.g., empirical risk minimization)
and computed via an efficient algorithm (e.g., mirror descent).
A central performance measure is the excess risk,

R(\hat{\theta})-\inf_{1\leq i\leq d}R(h_{i}),

where
R
is a generic risk,
not necessarily limited to the risk defined in (
8
).
This compares the aggregated predictor
{\hat{\theta}}
and
the best base predictor.

Another common benchmark compares the aggregated predictor with
the optimal predictor in a certain function class.
Under standard settings,
the corresponding excess risk typically achieves
the canonical parametric rate
\Theta(\sqrt{\log d/n})
.
In particular,
34
prove an

O(\sqrt{\log d/n})
oracle type upper bound for
several estimators, together with a matching lower bound.

Meanwhile, fast rates against the best base predictor are known
under stronger conditions.

30
show that
for exponentially concave losses, mirror averaging algorithms
based on online mirror descent achieve
O(\log d/n)
.

18
and
32
establish
a similar result for the exponentially weighted aggregate algorithm,
under i.i.d. data and bounded or quadratic loss functions.

33
propose Q-aggregation,
whose loss function is a mixture of those used in
model aggregation and randomized predictors (see
Section

5
),
with a weighted
L_{1}
penalty.
They establish that Q-aggregation achieves the fast rate of

O(\log d/n)
under
strong convexity and Lipschitz conditions on the loss function.
These results rely on the
exponential concavity or strong convexity,
which are stronger than standard convexity.

By contrast,
our analysis requires neither exponential concavity nor i.i.d. data.
A reverse-Pinsker-type inequality gives

{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,\pi)\leq\frac{\log d}{2}\|\theta-\pi\|_{1}\leq\log d

(
51
)
.
Thus, from
Proposition

7
and since
b\leq\log d
,

{\mathrm{Risk}}(\hat{\theta})-\inf_{\theta\in{\Delta_{d}}}{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Note that this infimum is over all convex combinations of
the base predictors, not just themselves.
This rate is slower than the fast rate by a factor of
\sqrt{n}
.
We conjecture that exploiting strong convexity of the loss on
{\Delta_{d}}
may recover fast rates for GLMs.
However, we leave this refinement to future work.

5
Randomized predictors

In this section, we study
randomized predictors
,
an alternative meta-learning scheme to model aggregation in

Section

4
.
While model aggregation outputs a convex combination of base models,
a randomized predictor samples a single model
according to a distribution over the base models.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a finite set of base models, and
let
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
denote all probability measures on
{\mathcal{B}}
.
We choose a distribution
\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
,
and draw a model
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}
according to
\theta
.
Since
{\mathcal{B}}
is finite,
\theta
is a probability vector in
the simplex
\Delta_{|{\mathcal{B}}|}
.
Our goal is to select
{\hat{\theta}}

so that a draw
{\hat{\beta}}\in{\mathcal{B}}
according to
{\hat{\theta}}

has small risk.
Given a model
\beta:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}

and loss
r:{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
define the population risk
R
and empirical risk
{\widehat{R}}_{n}

on data
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
by

R(\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}\big[r(\beta(X),Y)\big]\;\text{or}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y|X}\big[r(\beta(X),Y)];\quad\text{and}\quad{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}r(\beta(x_{i}),y_{i}).

Here
R
is a generic risk,
not necessarily limited to the risk defined in (
8
).
Meanwhile, for the GLMs studied in earlier sections,
taking
r(y^{\prime},y)=-yy^{\prime}+A(y^{\prime})
and identifying

\beta
with the model parameter, we recover

{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)=\ell(\beta)
and
R(\beta)={\mathrm{Risk}}(\beta)
.

One popular approach to construct the sampling distribution

{\hat{\theta}}
is exponential weighting,
based on the empirical risk evaluated on each base model:

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}(\mathrm{d}\beta)\propto\exp(-{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)/\lambda)\cdot z(\mathrm{d}\beta)

(16)

where
z
is a base (prior) measure and

\lambda>0
is a tuning parameter.
With no prior information,
one may take the uniform
z=\pi
on
{\mathcal{B}}
,
where
\pi(\beta)=1/|{\mathcal{B}}|
.

The above
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
is called the ‘Gibbs posterior’
in the Bayesian statistics literature,
and can be equivalently derived by
solving the KL-regularized optimization problem

(
3
)
:

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}({\mathcal{B}})}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)\right]+\lambda{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\Big\},

(17)

where the expectation is only over the randomness of
\beta
.
This optimization problem is also referred to as
information risk minimization in the literature

(
70
;
67
)
.

Meanwhile, we can also consider the implicit regularization counterpart of
(
17
) on the unpenalized empirical risk.
As studied in Section
4
, this corresponds to
exponentiated gradient descent iterates
\theta_{t}
in (
5
),
where we set
f(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}[{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)]
.
Given an initialization
z\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})

and a constant step size
\eta>0
,
we note that
\eta
can be arbitrarily large.
This is because the loss
f(\theta)
is linear in
\theta
,
and thus
L
-smooth for any
L>0
.
Interestingly, in this setting, implicit and explicit regularization coincide:
they yield the same exponentially weighted estimator.

Proposition 9

(Equivalence of
\theta_{T}
and
\hat{\theta}_{\lambda_{T}}
)
.

Let
\lambda_{T}=1/\eta T
.
For
\theta_{t}
in (
5
) and

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
17
),

\theta_{T}=\hat{\theta}_{\lambda_{T}}
.

Despite this equivalence,
which allows for a unified analysis of the population risk for both
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
and
\theta_{T}
,
we derive the expected population risk bounds for these estimators
separately to compare the results.

Proposition 10

(Expected population risk bound;
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
)
.

Let
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
be defined as in
Equation

17
.
Then, the following holds for the expected population risk
of a model sampled according to
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
:
for any
\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}}\left[R(\beta)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[R(\beta)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\|{\widehat{R}}_{n}-R\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}})}^{2}+2\lambda{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z),

where the
L^{\infty}
norm is defined as

\Big\|{\widehat{R}}_{n}-R\Big\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}})}:=\sup_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}}\Big|{\widehat{R}}_{n}(\beta)-R(\beta)\Big|.

Proposition 11

(Expected population risk bound;
\theta_{T}
)
.

Let
\lambda_{T}=1/\eta T
.
Then, the following bound holds for the expected population risk
of a model sampled according to
\theta_{T}
:
for any
\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
and stopping time
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta_{T}}\left[R(\beta)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[R(\beta)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{T}}\left\|{\widehat{R}}_{n}-R\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}})}^{2}+\lambda_{T}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z).

While
Proposition

10
stems directly from (
17
),

Proposition

11
is derived from
the basic inequality for mirror descent in
Theorem

2
.
By applying the equivalence from

Proposition

9
,
the bound in
Proposition

10
can also be expressed in terms of
\theta_{T}
and
\lambda_{T}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta_{T}}\left[R(\beta)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[R(\beta)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{T}}\left\|{\widehat{R}}_{n}-R\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}})}^{2}+2\lambda_{T}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z).

This closely aligns with the bound in
Proposition
11
,
differing only by small constant factors in the coefficients.

Crucially, both bounds apply to any pair
({\widehat{R}}_{n},R)

under minimal assumptions,
and only depend on
\|{\widehat{R}}_{n}-R\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{B}})}
.
This quantity represents the uniform convergence rate of
the empirical risk
{\widehat{R}}_{n}
to
the population risk
R
over the model space
{\mathcal{B}}
.
Furthermore, the term
\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}[R(\beta)]

plays a similar role to
{\mathrm{Risk}}(\beta)
discussed in
Sections
3
and
4
.

Finally, we use
Proposition

10
to establish
an oracle type risk bound for
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}

under the assumption of a bounded loss.
We omit the bound for
\theta_{T}
this time
due to the equivalence in
Proposition
9
.

Proposition 12

(Excess expected population risk bound;
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
)
.

Assume the loss function
r
is bounded by
C>0
,
and the training data
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
are i.i.d.
Then, for any
\delta>0
,
with probability at least

,
the following holds:
for all
\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}\left[R(\beta)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[R(\beta)\right]\leq\frac{C^{2}(\log(2|{\mathcal{B}}|)+\delta)}{2n\lambda}+2\lambda{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z).

In particular, for any

, choosing

\lambda=\frac{C}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2|{\mathcal{B}}|)+\delta}{nb}},

ensures that, with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}\left[R(\beta)\right]-\inf_{\theta:\;{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)\leq b}\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}\left[R(\beta)\right]\leq 2C\sqrt{\frac{b(\log(2|{\mathcal{B}}|)+\delta)}{n}}.

Comparison with
3
.

The population risk results for
{\hat{\beta}_{\lambda}}

in
3
are derived under the identical setting as in

Proposition

12
:
the observed data
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d.,
and the loss function

is bounded by an absolute constant

C>0
.
Under these conditions, they show that
the following holds with probability at least
1-e^{-\delta}
:
for any
\theta\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{B}})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}}[R(\beta)]-\mathbb{E}_{\beta\sim\theta}[R(\beta)]\leq\frac{C^{2}}{4n\lambda}+2\lambda\big({D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\theta,z)+\log 2+\delta\big).

While our upper bound in
Proposition

12

includes an additional
\log|{\mathcal{B}}|
factor,
this discrepancy likely stems from our use of
Young’s inequality in the proofs;
on the other hand,
3
uses a more refined analysis
such as Donsker–Varadhan formula.

7
Other iterative algorithms and basic inequalities

We also discuss basic inequalities for two additional first order iterative algorithms,
proximal gradient descent and NoLips.
They were not used in the previous applications in the paper,
yet are included for completeness.

7.1
Proximal gradient descent

Proximal gradient descent minimizes a composite convex function

f=g+h
where
g
is convex and differentiable, and
h
is convex but possibly non-differentiable.
Given initialization
\theta_{0}
and step sizes
(\eta_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
,
its iterates are

\theta_{t+1}={\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta_{t}h}(\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla g(\theta_{t}))\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathrm{Prox}}_{h}(\theta):=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-z\|_{2}^{2}+h(z),

where
{\mathrm{Prox}}_{h}
is called the proximal operator.
It is known that the above iterates can be rewritten as

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}G_{\eta_{t}}(\theta_{t}),\quad\text{where}\quad G_{\eta}(\theta):=\frac{1}{\eta}\Big(\theta-{\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta h}(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta))\Big).

(18)

A basic requirement for using this algorithm is that
{\mathrm{Prox}}_{h}

can be computed in closed form or efficiently approximated.
The following theorem derives the basic inequality for proximal gradient descent.

Theorem 7

(Basic inequality; proximal gradient descent)
.

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex function
of the form
f=g+h
, where
g
is convex and differentiable,
and
h
is convex but possibly non-differentiable,
with the computable proximal operator
{\mathrm{Prox}}_{h}
.
Further assume that
g
is
L
-smooth.
Consider proximal gradient descent with iterates (
18
)
with initialization
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and a constant step size
\eta_{t}\in(0,1/L]
.
Then, for any reference point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and stopping time
T\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}\Big(\|\theta_{0}-z\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta_{T}-z\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

A notable example is iterative soft-thresholding algorithm (ISTA)
for lasso penalty.
Let
f(\theta)=g(\theta)+h(\theta)

with
g(\theta)=\frac{1}{2n}\|Y-X\theta\|_{2}^{2}
and
h(\theta)=\lambda\|\theta\|_{1}
.
Then
{\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta h}(\theta)=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2\eta}\|\theta-z\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|z\|_{1}

has a closed form solution, obtained by a coordinate-wise soft-thresholding operator:
its
j
-th coordinate for
j\in[d]
is

[{\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta h}(\theta)]_{j}={\rm sign}(\theta_{j})\cdot\max(0,|\theta_{j}|-\eta\lambda).

Moreover,
g(\theta)
is
\|{\widehat{\Sigma}}\|_{{\rm op}}
-smooth in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
as mentioned in Theorem
4
.
Thus, choosing an appropriate step size
\eta
, Theorem
7
implies the following for ISTA
with initialization
\theta_{0}=0
: for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{1}{2n}\|Y-X\theta_{T}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\theta_{T}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\eta T}\|\theta_{T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\|Y-Xz\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|z\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\eta T}\|z\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the infimum over
z
in the bound leads to
an elastic net regularization,
where
\lambda
and
T
determine the regularization coefficients separately.
This parametrization of the coefficients differs from
typical packages such as scikit-learn in Python and glmnet in R.
They use one parameter to scale the overall regularization level
and another parameter for the
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
coefficient ratio.

7.2
NoLips algorithm

NoLips algorithm
(
8
)
can be viewed as an instance
of mirror descent but operates under a slightly different set of assumptions.
While its iterates are the same as those of mirror descent in (
4
),
NoLips relaxes the strong convexity of
\phi
and the smoothness of
f
to an alternative condition,
which is explained in the basic inequality statement below.

Theorem 8

(Basic inequality; NoLips algorithm)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
and
\Omega
be closed convex sets in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\Omega
,
whose interiors are not empty.
Suppose
(i) a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is convex on
{\mathcal{C}}
,
and it is differentiable on
{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
;
(ii) a function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is of Legendre type,
and it is continuous on
\Omega
.
Further assume that
(iii) there exists a constant
L>0
such that

L\phi-f
is convex on
{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
.
Consider NoLips iterates with the same update as (
4
),
initialized at
\theta_{0}\in{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)

and step sizes
\eta_{t}\in(0,1/L]
.
Then, for any reference point
z\in{\mathcal{C}}

and stopping time
T\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}\Big(D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{0})-D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{T})\Big).

In particular, for a constant step size
\eta_{t}=\eta
, this simplifies to

f(\theta_{T})-f(z)\leq\frac{1}{\eta T}\Big(D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{0})-D_{\phi}(z,\theta_{T})\Big).

An interesting example where NoLips works but mirror descent doesn’t is the
Poisson linear inverse problem (PLIP).
Unlike Poisson GLM which uses the canonical link function of

\log(\mu)=X\theta
,
PLIP uses the linear link function
\mu=X\theta
,
often assuming that all elements of
X
and
\theta
are positive
so that negative means are prevented.
Thus the maximum likelihood estimator in PLIP becomes

{\hat{\theta}}=\argmin_{\theta\in(0,\infty)^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(-y_{i}\log(x_{i}^{\top}\theta)+x_{i}^{\top}\theta\Big).

Up to the constant term
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}y_{i}\log y_{i}-y_{i}
,
we can rewrite this using a Bregman divergence
{D_{\mathrm{BS}}}

induced by the Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
x\mapsto x\log x
:
where

{D_{\mathrm{BS}}}(u,v)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\log u_{j}-u_{j}-u_{j}\log v_{j}+v_{j}
,

{\hat{\theta}}=\argmin_{\theta\in(0,\infty)^{d}}\frac{1}{n}{D_{\mathrm{BS}}}(Y,X\theta).

Here the map
f(\theta)={D_{\mathrm{BS}}}(Y,X\theta)
is not globally Lipschitz on
(0,\infty)^{d}
.
However, with a convex function
\phi(\theta)=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\theta_{i}
on the same domain,
we can verify that
L\phi-f
is convex for any
L\geq\|Y\|_{1}
.
Therefore, PLIP can be solved via NoLips algorithm.
More details about using NoLips on PLIP can be found in
8
.

8
Discussion

We introduced basic inequalities for first-order optimization algorithms,
establishing a unified framework for the joint statistical and computational analysis of implicit regularization.
By translating the number of iterations into an effective regularization parameter,
this framework enables a direct comparison between implicit and explicit regularization.
We demonstrated the broad utility of this approach by
characterizing training dynamics and deriving prediction risk bounds.
While our results reveal an inherent trade-off between
the generality of basic inequality and the sharpness of the derived bounds,
in specific cases, we still achieve bounds comparable to or tighter than existing specialized analyses.

This trade-off motivates several directions for future research.
One avenue is to refine basic inequalities under stronger assumptions,
such as strong convexity or restricted eigenvalue conditions,
to improve tightness.
Conversely, extending the framework to accommodate relaxed assumptions,
including non-convex or non-smooth loss functions, remains a critical objective.
Broadening the scope of algorithms presents another significant opportunity.
In the deterministic setting, the equivalence between
forward stagewise regression and the lasso path suggests that
our framework may be able to offer new insights for steepest descent methods.
In the stochastic setting, applying this analysis to stochastic gradient descent
would allow for a rigorous characterization of its implicit regularization.
These extensions would further establish basic inequalities as
a versatile tool for analyzing modern optimization.

Definitions and notation

Sets.

For
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
denote its interior and boundary by

{\mathrm{int}}(S)
and

\partial S
.
For a finite set
S
, its cardinality is
|S|
.
The closed Euclidean ball of radius
r>0

centered at
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

{\mathsf{B}}_{d}(r;p):=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\theta-p\|_{2}\leq r\}
, and
{\mathsf{B}}_{d}(r):={\mathsf{B}}_{d}(r;0)
.
Write
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
,
and
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The probability simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\Delta_{d}:=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}=1\}
.
For a matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, its null space is

\mathrm{null}(X)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:Xv=0\}
.

Arithmetic.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the inner product is

\langle u,v\rangle:=u^{\top}v=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}v_{i}
.
The coordinate-wise multiplication is

u\odot v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with

(u\odot v)_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}
for
i\in[d]
.

Functions.

Let
\Omega
be convex.
A function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is convex if

f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\leq\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)

for any
x,y\in\Omega
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
and strictly convex if the inequality is strict for

x\neq y
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
The subdifferential of a convex function
f
at
x
is

\partial f(x)
.
A function
f
is essentially strictly convex
if it is strictly convex on every convex subset of

\{x:\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\}
.
A function
f:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is essentially smooth if it satisfies three conditions:
(i)
{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)\neq\emptyset
;
(ii)
f
is differentiable on
{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
; and
(iii)
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{2}=\infty

for any sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\Omega

converging to a point in
\partial\Omega
.
A function
f
is of Legendre type if it is
both essentially smooth and essentially strictly convex

(
49
)
.
A differentiable function
f
is
\alpha
-strongly convex
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
with some
\alpha>0
, if

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
.
A differentiable function
f
is
L
-smooth
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
if the gradient

\nabla f
is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to
\|\cdot\|
and
its dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
with some
L>0
; that is,

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{*}\leq L\|x-y\|

for all
x,y
.

Random variables.

For a data matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
the empirical covariance matrix is

{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{X}:=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
abbreviated
{\widehat{\Sigma}}
when the context is clear.
A random variable
Z
is sub-Gaussian with parameter

\sigma^{2}
, written
Z\sim{\mathrm{sG}}(\sigma^{2})
, if

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\alpha(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]))]\leq\exp(\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

B.1

Lemma B1

(Descent lemma; gradient descent)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be convex, differentiable,
and
L
-smooth for some
L>0
.
Consider gradient descent iterates in (
1
)
with step sizes
\eta_{t}\in(0,2/L]
.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
,

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta_{t}\big(1-\frac{L}{2}\eta_{t}\big)\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq f(\theta_{t}).

Proof of Lemma
B1
.

By
L
-smoothness,

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})+\nabla f(\theta_{t})^{\top}(\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t})+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Substituting
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t})

as (
1

B.2

In this subsection,

into two:

•

and

be closed convex sets in

A function

\Omega

\alpha

The function

is
L

Lemma B2

(Well-definedness of
{D_{\phi}}
)
.

Under Assumption 1,
the function
{D_{\phi}}(\cdot,v):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

is well-defined if and only if
v\in{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is of Legendre type, it is essentially smooth.
Also, since
\phi
is a proper continuous convex function
on the closed domain
\Omega
,
by Theorem 7.1 in
49
,

\phi
is a closed function.
Therefore, by Theorem 26.1 in
49
,
we know
\partial\phi(v)=\emptyset
if

\partial\phi(v)=\{\nabla\phi(v)\}
if

.
∎

Lemma B3

(Lower bound of
{D_{\phi}}
)
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2,
for any
u\in{\mathcal{C}}
and

Proof of Lemma
B3
.

By Lemma

,

{D_{\phi}}(\cdot,v)

The desired bound directly follows from

\alpha
-strong convexity of

D_{\phi}(u,v)=\phi(u)-\phi(v)-\langle\nabla\phi(v),u-v\rangle\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u-v\|^{2}

∎

Lemma B4

(Interior preservation for mirror descent iterates)
.

Under Assumption 1,
consider mirror descent iterates in (
4
)
with
\theta_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
.
Then,
\theta_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)

for any
t\geq 0
.
In other words,

{D_{\phi}}(\cdot,\theta_{t}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{is well-defined for any}\,\,t\geq 0.

In particular, if
{\mathcal{C}}=\Omega

and
\theta_{0}\in{\mathrm{int}}({\mathcal{C}})
,
then
\theta_{t}\in{\mathrm{int}}({\mathcal{C}})
for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Assume
\theta_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
, then

{D_{\phi}}(\cdot,\theta_{t})
is well-defined by Lemma
B2
.
Recall
\theta_{t+1}
in (
4

\theta_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}}\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle+{D_{\phi}}(\theta,\theta_{t}).

For brevity, define a function

F:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{R}
as

F(\theta)=\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle+{D_{\phi}}(\theta,\theta_{t})
.
By the first order optimality condition,
there exists
g\in\partial F(\theta_{t+1})
such that

\langle g,\theta-\theta_{t+1}\rangle\geq 0

for all
\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
Meanwhile, since
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\Omega

by Theorem 23.8 in
49
,
for any
\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}

\partial F(\theta)=\partial\Big(\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle\Big)+\partial\Big(D_{\phi}(\theta,\theta_{t})\Big)=\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t})-\partial\phi(\theta)-\nabla\phi(\theta_{t}).

We argue by contradiction:
suppose
\theta_{t+1}\notin{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
.
Then
\partial\phi(\theta_{t+1})=\emptyset

since
\phi
is of Legendre type,
as observed in the proof of Lemma
B2
.
Therefore,
\partial F(\theta_{t+1})
is also empty,
which contradicts the existence of the subgradient

Therefore,
\theta_{t+1}\in{\mathrm{int}}(\Omega)
, and thus
{D_{\phi}}(\cdot,\theta_{t+1}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is well-defined.
The proof is completed by mathematical induction.
∎

Lemma B5

(Three-point inequality for mirror descent)
.

Under Assumption 1,
consider mirror descent iterates in (
4
).
Then, for any
z\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
t\geq 0
,

\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-z\rangle\leq{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t})-{D_{\phi}}(z,\theta_{t+1})-D\phi(\theta_{t+1},\theta_{t}).

Proof of Lemma
B5
.

For brevity, define

F(\theta)=\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta\rangle+{D_{\phi}}(\theta,\theta_{t})
.
By Lemma
B2

and the first order optimality condition for
\theta_{t+1}
,

\nabla F(\theta_{t+1})=\eta_{t}\nabla f(\theta_{t})+\nabla\phi(\theta_{t+1})-\nabla\phi(\theta_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\langle\nabla F(\theta_{t+1}),z-\theta_{t+1}\rangle\;\text{for any}\;z\in{\mathcal{C}}.

Rearranging the terms, this is equivalent to

\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-z\rangle\leq\langle\nabla\phi(\theta_{t+1})-\nabla\phi(\theta_{t}),z-\theta_{t+1}\rangle\;\text{for any}\;z\in{\mathcal{C}}.

∎

Lemma B6

(Descent lemma; mirror descent)
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2,

Appendix D
Generalized linear models

Generalized linear models (GLMs) refer to a broad model class
related to distributions in the exponential family,
including linear regression.
An univariate exponential family distribution in canonical form
has the probability density or mass function

p(z|\xi)
proportional to
\exp(\xi S(z)-A(\xi))
.
Here,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
is the natural parameter,

S:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the sufficient statistic
(often, and in our focus,
S(z)=z
),
and
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the cumulant function.
Key identities are
\mathbb{E}[S(Z)]={\dot{A}}(\xi)
and

\mathrm{Var}(S(Z))={\ddot{A}}(\xi)
, where dots denote differentiation.
Examples include:
Gaussian distribution
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with fixed

\sigma^{2}
has
\xi=\mu
,
S(z)=z
, and

A(\xi)=\xi^{2}/2
;
Bernoulli
(p)
has
\xi=\log(p/(1-p))

which is called the logit link,
S(z)=z
, and

A(\xi)=\log(1+e^{\xi})
; and
Poisson
(\mu)
has
\xi=\log(\mu)
which is called the log link,

S(z)=z
, and
A(\xi)=e^{\xi}
.

In a GLM, the natural parameter
\xi
is assumed to be linked linearly to

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\xi=x^{\top}\theta
,
where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Under this assumption, estimation of
\theta
is performed by
maximum likelihood for the chosen exponential family.

Formally, given data
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

and an exponential family characterized by
(S,A)
,
assume that
\xi_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\theta
for
i\in[n]
.
The maximum likelihood estimator is

\hat{\theta}_{0}:=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\log p_{S,A}(y_{i}|x_{i}^{\top}\theta)=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(-S(y_{i})x_{i}^{\top}\theta+A(x_{i}^{\top}\theta)\Big).

(19)

The main article assumes that the sufficient statistic is the identity map;
that is,
S(y)=y
.
However, our framework extends directly to general
S

by replacing
y_{i}
with
S(y_{i})
where appropriate.
For example, the vector
Y
can be simply replaced with

(S(y_{1}),\ldots,S(y_{n}))^{\top}
in (
7
).

The following lemma is used in the discussion of Corollary
2
.

Lemma D1

(GLM solution set is affine; with
\ell(\theta)
in (
7
))
.

Consider the GLM loss
\ell(\theta)
and estimator in (
7
). Suppose that
{\mathcal{C}}
is affine
(e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or unbounded simplex),
and the cumulant function
A
is strictly convex.
Then, the solution set of the GLM is either empty or an affine set.

Proof.

Let
S
be the solution set. Suppose
S\neq\emptyset
.
Pick
s\in S
and
define
U:={\mathcal{C}}\cap(\{s\}+\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:Xv=0\})
,
which is an affine set.
We will show
S=U
.
Clearly
S\supseteq U
since
\ell(\theta)

depends on
\theta
only through
X\theta
.

To show
S\subseteq U
,
pick
s^{\prime}\in S
and let
v:=s^{\prime}-s
.
Since
\ell(s)=\ell(s^{\prime})
and

\ell(\cdot)
is a convex function
by Lemma
E1
,
we know that
\ell(s+cv)=\ell(s)
for any
c\in(0,1)
. Differentiating twice with respect to
c
,

\displaystyle 0=\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}c^{2}}\ell(s+cv)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\langle v,\nabla\ell(s+cv)\rangle=\,\langle v,\nabla^{2}\ell(s+cv)v\rangle=\,

\displaystyle(Xv)^{\top}{\mathrm{diag}}\Big({\ddot{A}}\big(X(s+cv)_{i\in[n]}\big)\Big)Xv

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Xv)_{i}^{2}{\ddot{A}}\big((Xs+cXv)_{i}\big).

Since
{\ddot{A}}(\cdot)>0
due to strict convexity,
we have
Xv=0
.
Therefore
s^{\prime}\in(\{s\}+\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:Xv=0\})
, which implies
S\subseteq U
.
∎

G.1
Bregman-divergence regularized GLM

Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be closed and convex,

\phi:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{R}

The Bregman divergence induced by

The Bregman-divergence-regularized GLM estimator is defined as

{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}:=\,\argmin_{\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}}\big\{\ell(\theta)+\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)\big\}.

(22)

We establish a prediction risk bound for the
Bregman-divergence-regularized GLM estimator,
which recovers Propositions
1
and

5
as special cases.

Proposition 13

(Risk bound; Bregman-divergence-regularized GLM)
.

Suppose
\phi
is
\alpha
-strongly convex on
{\mathcal{C}}

with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
for
\alpha>0
,
and let
\|\cdot\|_{*}
be the dual norm.
Then, for any
\lambda>0
,
an anchor point
z\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
and a reference point
\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
the prediction risk of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
22
)
satisfies

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})\leq{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)+\frac{1}{\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+2\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z).

Proof of Proposition
13
.

By definition of
{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}
in (
22
), we have

\ell({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})+\lambda{D_{\phi}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}},z)\leq\ell(\theta)+\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z).

Following similar steps to those in the proof of
Proposition
1
, this implies that

\frac{1}{n}\big(A(X{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-A(X\theta)\big)\leq Y^{\top}\frac{X}{n}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta)+\lambda\Big({D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)-{D_{\phi}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}},z)\Big),

and thus,

{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)\leq\epsilon^{\top}\frac{X}{n}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta)+\lambda\Big({D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)-{D_{\phi}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}},z)\Big).

By the definition of dual norm and Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\top}\frac{X}{n}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta)\leq\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\|{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-\theta\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\|{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-z\|+\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\|\theta-z\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+\frac{\alpha\lambda}{2}\|{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-z\|^{2}+\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\|\theta-z\|.

Finally, using
{D_{\phi}}(u,v)\geq(\alpha/2)\|u-v\|^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Risk}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}})-{\mathrm{Risk}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\|\theta-z\|+\frac{1}{2\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)+\lambda\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\|{\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}}-z\|^{2}-{D_{\phi}}({\hat{\theta}_{\lambda}},z)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)}+\frac{1}{2\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{2\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha\lambda}\Big\|\frac{X^{\top}\epsilon}{n}\Big\|_{*}^{2}+2\lambda{D_{\phi}}(\theta,z),

where the last inequality holds by AM-GM.
∎

[0,\infty)

g(\lambda_{s})=f({\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{s}})+\lambda_{s}\|{\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{s}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq f({\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}})+\lambda_{s}\|{\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore,

g(\lambda_{b})-g(\lambda_{s})=f({\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}})+\lambda_{b}\|{\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}}\|_{2}^{2}-g(\lambda_{s})\geq(\lambda_{b}-\lambda_{s})\|{\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0,

where equality holds if and only if

{\hat{\theta}}_{\lambda_{b}}=0
.
∎

L.1
Proof of Theorem
7

We begin with a generalized descent lemma for proximal gradient descent.

Lemma L1

(Generalized descent lemma; proximal gradient)
.

Let
f=g+h
with
g
convex and differentiable,
and
h
convex but potentially non-differentiable.
Suppose either

(i)

g
is
L
-smooth on a convex set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\eta\in(0,1/L]
; or

(ii)

g\equiv 0
, so
f=h
,
with no constraint on
\eta>0
.

Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

(i)

for any
\theta\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that

\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\in{\mathcal{C}}
; or

(ii)

for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

we have

(i)

\displaystyle:f(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\leq f(z)+\langle G_{\eta}(\theta),\theta-z\rangle-\frac{\eta}{2}\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2},

(ii)

\displaystyle:f(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\leq f(z)+\langle G_{\eta}(\theta),\theta-z\rangle-\eta\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
L1
.

(i) By the
L
-smoothness of
g
on
{\mathcal{C}}

g(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\leq g(\theta)+\langle\nabla g(\theta),-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}.

By convexity of
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

g(\theta)\leq g(z)+\langle\nabla g(\theta),\theta-z\rangle

for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Combining these two inequalities,

\displaystyle g(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))

\displaystyle\leq g(z)+\langle\nabla g(\theta),\theta-z\rangle+\langle\nabla g(\theta),-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq g(z)+\langle\nabla g(\theta),\theta-z\rangle+\langle\nabla g(\theta),-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\rangle+\frac{\eta}{2}\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2},

since
\eta\leq 1/L
.
Meanwhile, we know

G_{\eta}(\theta)-\nabla g(\theta)\in\partial h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))

due to the following observations.
Recall the definition of the proximal operator and

G_{\eta}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\eta}(\theta-{\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta h}(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta))
.
By the first-order optimality condition for the proximal operator,

0\in\Big({\mathrm{Prox}}_{\eta h}(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta))-(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta))\Big)+\eta\partial h(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta)),

which implies

0\in\Big(\nabla g(\theta)-G_{\eta}(\theta)\Big)+\partial h(\theta-\eta\nabla g(\theta)).

Therefore, by the definition of the subgradient,

\displaystyle h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))

\displaystyle\leq h(z)-\langle G_{\eta}(\theta)-\nabla g(\theta),z-(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\rangle

\displaystyle=h(z)+\langle\nabla g(\theta),z-\theta+\eta G_{\eta}(\theta)\rangle+\langle G_{\eta}(\theta),\theta-z\rangle-\eta\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}.

Finally, since

f(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))=g(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))+h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))
,
combining the derived upper bounds on
g
and
h
gives the following result, which concludes the proof of (i):

f(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\leq g(z)+h(z)+\langle G_{\eta}(\theta),\theta-z\rangle-\frac{\eta}{2}\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}.

(ii) Note that
g\equiv 0
implies that
g
is
L
-smooth on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with any
L>0
.
Recall the upper bound on
h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))

in the proof of (i).
Since
\nabla g(\theta)=0
for any
\theta
,
the intermediate terms for the upper bound of

h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))
become zero:

h(\theta-\eta G_{\eta}(\theta))\leq h(z)+\langle G_{\eta}(\theta),\theta-z\rangle-\eta\|G_{\eta}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
f=h
due to
g\equiv 0
, this concludes the proof.
∎
