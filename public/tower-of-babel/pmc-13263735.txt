ABSTRACT
Highlights•Quantum Federated Learning — integrating CRYSTALS-Dilithium gradient authentication, QKD-secured aggregation, and quantum Byzantine filtering — is proposed as a reference architecture with a structured 2025–2030 roadmap addressing seven evidence-grounded research gaps toward standardised automotive deployment.•A multi-dimensional risk matrix across 46 studies reveals that ML adversarial attacks pose the highest FL-specific vulnerability exposure (0.85) and detection difficulty (0.90), indicating a misalignment between current FL-IDS research focus and the most critical threat class.•Existing Federated Learning-based intrusion detection approaches for autonomous vehicles are critically evaluated, exposing persistent gaps between reported benchmark performance and the requirements of practical, large-scale vehicular deployment.

Background

The global autonomous vehicle market is forecasted to be over USD 600 billion by 2030 [1], and commercial deployments from leading companies like Waymo, Cruise, Tesla, Baidu Apollo, and Mobileye are already traversing major metropolitan regions worldwide. However, this industrialization of self-driving systems has shifted autonomous driving from a domain of computer science research to a safety-critical public infrastructure challenge. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) [2,3] jointly characterize six levels of driving automation in SAE J3016 — ranging from Level 0 (no automation) to Level 5 (full self-driving) — however, each ascendent level increases substantially upon the preceding one vehicle’s computational complexity, sensor integration depth, as well as external network connectivity, resulting in a commensurately expanded attack-scape.

The safety-security nexus in AV systems was one of the very first issues that were systematically documented in the literature. Cui et al. [4] surveyed how cyber intrusions can translate directly into physical harm through manipulation of steering, braking, and acceleration control loops. Several high-profile incidents later confirmed the practical severity of this nexus. A pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona, was struck and killed by an Uber Volvo XC90 moving in autonomous mode in 2018 due to the status of that pedestrian, as classified from the onboard ML detection system, being conflicting across consecutive frames. A 2023 incident in which a Cruise robotaxi ran over and dragged a pedestrian in San Francisco highlighted the intractable hazard when AV decision-making must resolve co-occurring failures of perception, sensor fusion, and V2X safety messaging. These incidents drive not just evolutionary changes to current security mechanisms, but a true re-architecting of AV system-level security.

Multiple complementary surveys have characterised the cybersecurity threat landscape for AVs. Kim et al. Various attack methodologies and corresponding defence mechanisms over sensor layer, communication layer and ML software layer has been analysed by [5] on >100 publications which ultimately led to the conclusion that no single defensive mechanism is sufficient. Pham and Xiong [6] applied this analysis to connected vehicles more generally, showing that the standardized integration of Cellular V2X (C-V2X) over 5 G NR enables improved communication utility and an expanded surface area at the protocol level for attack. Sheehan et al. [7] developed an initial cyber-risk taxonomy for connected and autonomous vehicles, noting that risk profiles differ significantly between SAE automation levels — a theme explored quantitatively in Section 2 below. Chowdhury et al. [8] showed that AV ML perception pipelines are remarkably susceptible to adversarial perturbation attacks, where imperceptible changes to visual inputs lead to systematic misclassification in safety-critical object detection tasks.

On this front, Federated Learning (FL) stands as a principled framework for implementing distributed privacy-preserving intrusion detection within the AV ecosystem. Initially formalised by McMahan et al. FedAvg algorithm [6] and theoretically underpinned by Konečný et al. [10], within the context of communication efficiency, FL permits a fleet of vehicles to train a global detection model collaboratively without any vehicle transmitting its raw sensor or communication data to a central server. Each vehicle, instead, trains locally on its own data and sends only the resulting model parameter updates — gradients or weights — to an aggregation server that calculates a weighted average. This framework respects data sovereignty and saves bandwidth compared to the raw-data-streamed model, but also avoids a central point of compromise (which exists in all centralised learning institutions). Li et al. [11] cover the methodological challenges in FL in detail, finding strongly non-convergent, non-independent and non-identically distributed (non-IID) data as most prohibitive — a concern intimately relevant to AV scenarios in which vehicles traverse disparate geographic and traffic conditions, generating inherently heterogeneous local distributions of data.

The benefits of the FL-based AV security architectures have not been sufficiently addressed in the existing literature, leaving three main limitations without satisfactory resolution. Initially, it is based on the secure aggregation protocol by Bonawitz et al. [12], which ensures individual gradient privacy against the aggregating server but only gives computational security — that is, security based on intractability of certain mathematical problems for polynomial-time adversaries. Such security guarantees collapse in the presence of quantum-capable adversaries who utilize Shor's algorithm [13] to factor the underlying Diffie-Hellman key agreement. Second, Byzantine fault tolerance in FL — the ability to identify and remove malicious clients that submit poisoned gradient updates — has been investigated in the abstract case of generic FL [14,15], but remains unvalidated in the real-time, resource-constrained automotive deployment setting where fleet sizes consistently reach tens of thousands or more vehicles. Third, the non-IID data issue formally defined by Zhao et al. [16] has been overlooked in FL-based AV IDS proposals, most of which utilize extremely rudimentary experimental settings that only use around 3–20 vehicles instead of the hundreds or thousands as shown from commercial releases.

Concurrently, the quantum computing threat to vehicular cryptography has transitioned from a theoretical concern to an engineering urgency. The integer factorisation and discrete logarithm problems underlying RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) — the cryptographic foundations of current V2X security standards, including IEEE 1609.2 and ETSI ITS-G5 — are both efficiently solvable by Shor's algorithm running in polynomial time O((log N)³) on a fault-tolerant quantum computer [13]. IBM's 2023 Condor 1121-qubit processor and subsequent Heron r2 architecture demonstrate the trajectory of quantum hardware development. The NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography standardisation process (2016–2024) produced three primary standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) [[17], [18], [19]] — in direct response to the cryptographic threat posed by anticipated cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs).

In the vehicular context, Zeydan et al. [20] analysed PQC deployment considerations for V2X security management systems; Castiglione and Elia [21] demonstrated PQC integration with CAN bus network segmentation, achieving authenticated channel establishment with 3.2 ms latency on ARM Cortex-M4 hardware; and Verma et al. [22] proposed quantum-secure certificate-less authentication for vehicular ad hoc networks (VANETs). Yet, such studies treat PQC solely as an endpoint-level challenge separate from the distributed learning infrastructure that thus far represents the brain of AV systems. To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing study addressing the integration of PQC with FL aggregation protocols — ensuring that the model parameter exchanges are made computationally private to entanglements and quantum-capable devices.

Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), which achieves information-theoretic rather than merely computational security through quantum mechanical principles, represents a complementary approach. Fowler et al. [23] demonstrated practical key generation via QKD for vehicular-to-infrastructure (V2I) communications at distances up to 10 km. Conrad et al. [24] proposed a topology of moving platforms mounted by QKD through drone-relay quantum transceivers. However, deployment barriers — namely QKD's point-to-point topology constraint, sensitivity to atmospheric attenuation, and key generation rates unable to yield per-message V2V authentication at DSRC Basic Safety Message frequencies — constrain its immediate remit to infrastructure-level, not per-message authentication.

The intersection of FL and quantum computing is relatively uncharted territory in the form of Quantum Federated Learning (QFL) and may be the most consequential avenue for further research in AV security. Biamonte et al. [25], showed that quantum algorithms achieve superpolynomial speedups for certain ML optimisation problems. Majumder et al. For example, the authors of [26] presented a hybrid classical-quantum deep learning approach that demonstrated improved adversarial robustness for AV traffic image classification under adversarial attacks. A conceptual QFL architecture for autonomous driving security is proposed by Wang in [27]. Still, there currently exists no publicly available study of an implemented and validated QFL system on actual automotive hardware under realistic fleet conditions, attack scenarios, and quantum hardware noise models. This implementation gap — between theoretical promise and practical validation — stands as the foundational motivation for this analytical-review.

Autonomous vehicle architecture

Autonomous vehicles are stratified by SAE J3016 into six levels of driving automation (L0–L5). At the perception layer, contemporary AVs integrate LiDAR sensors generating 3D point clouds at up to 20 Hz, monocular and stereo cameras for visual object recognition, millimetre-wave radar for velocity estimation in adverse weather conditions, GPS and inertial measurement units (IMU) for localisation, and ultrasonic sensors for close-range obstacle detection. These sensor modalities are fused through deep neural networks — typically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for camera inputs and PointNet or VoxelNet variants for LiDAR point clouds — to produce a real-time world model that informs planning and control decisions [3]. Each vehicle has an internal communications architecture that is under the control of a bus protocol (ISO 11,898), namely CAN, and connects ECUs responsible for engine management boards, electronic braking systems (ABS, ESC), steering commands, airbag activation commands, as well as retrieval of sensor data.

Originally designed in 1986 for reliability in electrically noisy automotive environments, the CAN bus protocol provides no source authentication, no message encryption, and no access control: all nodes on a bus can transmit and receive all messages simultaneously. Modern vehicles incorporate 70–100+ ECUs interconnected across multiple CAN segments, constituting an extensive unauthenticated internal communication network [6]. More recent in-vehicle network standards — CAN FD, FlexRay, Automotive Ethernet, and MOST — offer higher bandwidth but have not fundamentally resolved the authentication vulnerability for safety-critical message exchanges.

The Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) external communications layer encompasses V2V, V2I, V2P, V2N, and V2C interaction modalities. Two competing standards govern the physical and MAC layers for V2V/V2I: DSRC (IEEE 802.11p/WAVE/1609.x) and C-V2X (3GPP LTE-V2X and 5 G NR-V2X). Both transmit Basic Safety Messages (BSMs) at 10 Hz, each containing vehicle position, speed, heading, and acceleration state — creating a real-time behavioural broadcast that, if compromised, enables both passive traffic surveillance and active attack injection [2]. Fig. 2 shows an Illustration of the physical sensor layer, in-vehicle CAN bus network layer, V2X wireless communications layer, and cloud backend connectivity layer. Each layer presents distinct attack vectors: sensor spoofing at the physical layer, message injection at the CAN bus layer, replay and denial-of-service attacks at the V2X layer, and API compromise or firmware tampering at the cloud layer. SAE automation levels and attack surface expansion

The relationship between SAE automation level and attack surface is non-linear. At L0–L1, the primary attack surface is confined to the OBD-II diagnostic port, basic telematics units, and the Tyre Pressure Monitoring System (TPMS) — all low-bandwidth, low-frequency attack vectors with limited safety consequences. Moving to L2–L3 is the most significant security threshold in the SAE taxonomy: continuous sensor fusion, real-time ML inference, camera-based perception systems and initial V2X connectivity. Sheehan et al. [7] found that the trust dynamics of human and machine in L3 driving systems operating in a conditionally automated mode are most complex, especially since there is a control transition period where neither driver nor automation may be holistically aware of their respective situational states. L4–L5 vehicles do away with the human entirely from the control loop, and this is where symmetric ML model integrity and communications security represent the only guarantees for safe system operation.

In Fig. 3, we quantify this expansion across four categories in the attack surface derived from our analysis of the literature [2–5]. The dotted line in the figure highlights an important non-linear transition at L3, where V2X connectivity is first required, providing the means of network-layer attacks at scale. From L2 onwards, the sensor, network, and ML attack surfaces grow superlinearly while the physical one scales only gradually — something with direct ramifications for which defensive technologies should be prioritised at each level of automation.Fig. 3Attack surface expansion across SAE automation Levels L0–L5.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Cybersecurity threat taxonomy

The existing literature has catalogued AV threats comprehensively [[4], [5], [6]]. The contribution of the present analysis is to stratify these threats analytically according to their interaction with FL-based defences and quantum-era cryptographic vulnerabilities — an analytical lens absent from prior surveys.

Sensor-layer attacks

Sensor spoofing attacks exploit the physical transduction principles of AV sensing modalities. Eykholt et al. [32] showed that adversarially constructed physical perturbations — small stickers placed on a stop sign — caused recurrent misclassification by a ResNet-style classifier and effectively introduced the class of physical-world, adversarial attacks on AV perception. Cao et al. demonstrated LiDAR spoofing through synthetic laser pulse injection to create phantom objects and remove real obstacles from the vehicle point cloud was presented in [33]. Zeng et al. [34] demonstrated that AV navigation could be redirected to arbitrary locations using GPS spoofing with low-cost commodity hardware (less than USD 500 in total). Sensor spoofing attacks have particular relevance for FL-based AV systems, as they represent a mechanism of indirect poisoning of the FL model: if a vehicle further along in the transport system has compromised sensors, its local training data will be corrupted and results in adversarial gradient updates that poison the combined, aggregated global model despite no modification to that vehicle's client software responsible for execution of FL.

Adversarial machine learning attacks

The most rapidly evolving and technically sophisticated threat class reviewed was adversarial attacks on AV ML systems. Goodfellow et al. [35] proved the existence of imperceptible L∞-norm perturbations that, if applied to inputs of neural networks, would result in catastrophic misclassification (introducing what they called the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)). Chowdhury et al. [8] developed a neural network model for the AV context and listed several adversarial attacks on object detection (targeting label switching), semantic segmentation (road boundary removal), and trajectory prediction (false velocity estimation). One threat to FL-based AV security includes data poisoning attacks, in which malicious FL clients introduce charts with their own intentionally adversarial gradient updates that aim to undermine the global model. Blanchard et al. [14] showed that a single Byzantine client among 100 could make the FedAvg global model arbitrarily malicious and established the general severity.

Communication-layer attacks

V2X communication attacks exploit the broadcast architecture of vehicular networks and the authentication limitations of current standards. Sanchez et al. [36] illustrated replay attacks to AV control systems and suggested QPV observer-based detection mechanisms. Thus, Pham and Xiong [6] describe LTE-based C-V2X DoS attacks, in which camera and radar modules fail due to message flooding of the camera/radar software interrupt queue (albeit through software interrupt queue saturation). Although the migration to 5 G NR-V2X provides enhanced core network authentication, it introduces new attack surfaces at the NSMF and V2X Application Server layers, as noted by Korba et al. [37] in their work on zero-day attack detection in V2X networks with 5 G.

In-vehicle network attacks

CAN bus attacks exploit the protocol's inherent absence of source authentication. Access to the OBD-II port with Uconnect infotainment system compromises, demonstrated in a landmark Jeep Cherokee study by Miller and Valasek [38], resulted in full CAN bus control of steering, braking, and acceleration. All these attacks can range from pseudo-security techniques, such as inserting high-frequency random CAN messages to detect ECU memory corruption vulnerabilities (fuzzing), to injecting targeted malicious messages with specific arbitration IDs in the hopes that it will bypass safety-critical control functions (injection). Petit and Shladover [39] reported firmware-level exploits on AV ECUs that survived across power-cycles of the vehicle, which are not visible to normal diagnostic scan tools—this was an example of a particularly stealthy class of attacks with high impact and low probability of detection.

Federated learning for autonomous vehicle security

Mathematical formulation

The FL framework, formalised by McMahan et al. [9] and theoretically extended by Konečný et al. [10], frames distributed model training as the minimisation of a global objective function decomposed across K participating vehicle clients:(1)F(w) = Σ(k = 1 to K) λ_k · L_k(w), λ_k = n_k / Σ_j n_jwhere w denotes global model parameters, λ_k is the relative weight of client k proportional to its local dataset size n_k, and L_k(w) is the local loss function evaluated on client k's data. Each participating vehicle performs T rounds of local Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) before transmitting parameter updates to the central aggregation server:(2)w_k^(t + 1) = w_k^(t) − η · ∇L_k(w_k^(t))where η is the learning rate. The FedAvg aggregation rule constructs the global model update as a weighted sum of client contributions:(3)w^(t + 1) = Σ(k = 1 to K) λ_k · w_k^(t + 1)

For supervised intrusion detection where attack class labels are available, the local objective employs binary cross-entropy loss. For zero-day anomaly detection where attack labels are unavailable, autoencoder-based reconstruction loss is applied [29,30]:(4)L_k^recon(w) = (1/n_k) Σ_i ||x_i^(k) − x̂_i^(k)||²

A critical theoretical limitation of FedAvg that is systematically understated in AV IDS literature is its implicit assumption of bounded gradient dissimilarity across clients. Li et al. [11] formally quantify this constraint through the gradient divergence metric E[||∇L_k − ∇F||²], establishing that when client data distributions are highly heterogeneous — as is structurally the case in geographically diverse AV fleets — FedAvg converges to a solution potentially far from the true global optimum.

Privacy and security mechanisms in FL

The secure aggregation protocol of Bonawitz et al. [12] extends basic FL with a pairwise secret-sharing masking scheme that prevents the central server from recovering individual client gradients while preserving correct global aggregation. Pairs of clients generate shared random masks that cancel when summed across all participants, rendering individual contributions computationally indistinguishable from the aggregate. This protocol, however, provides only computational security — it is secure against adversaries bounded by classical polynomial-time computation but is vulnerable to quantum adversaries capable of solving the underlying Diffie-Hellman problem via Shor's algorithm [13].

Differential Privacy (DP), formalised by Dwork et al. [40] and applied to deep learning by Abadi et al. [41], provides a complementary mathematical privacy guarantee: the probability that any function of the aggregated model output reveals information about individual training samples is bounded by the privacy budget ε. Tian et al. [42] extend standard DP to a correlated noise model accounting for temporal and spatial correlations in AV sensor data, noting that consecutive CAN bus frames from the same vehicle are statistically correlated and that independent Gaussian noise injection as in standard DP overestimates the required noise magnitude. However, the analysis of Tian et al. reveals that correlated DP at privacy budgets ε < 1.0 introduces accuracy degradation of approximately 2–4 percentage points on zero-day detection tasks, creating a privacy-utility tradeoff that constrains high-privacy deployment scenarios.

FL architectures for AV IDS

The FL-IDS literature for AVs has explored three primary architectural variants. Centralised FL (standard FedAvg with a trusted aggregation server) offers the simplest implementation but concentrates trust and vulnerability at the server. Hierarchical FL — in which an intermediate cluster aggregation step precedes the global aggregation — was proposed by Althunayyan et al. [29] as a mechanism for improving Byzantine resilience and reducing per-round communication load for large fleets. Decentralised FL, employing gossip-protocol-based peer-to-peer aggregation without a central server, was examined by Pandi et al. [28] in the context of vehicular network connectivity constraints. Each architectural variant presents distinct tradeoffs between communication efficiency, Byzantine resilience, convergence speed, and single-point-of-failure exposure.

Chellapandi et al. [28] provide the most comprehensive survey of FL for connected and automated vehicles (FL4CAV), classifying approaches across centralised vs. decentralised architectures, data and model heterogeneity types, and security mechanism categories. Their analysis identifies data heterogeneity, unreliable V2X connectivity, and malicious client detection as the three dominant open challenges — conclusions corroborated by the cross-study synthesis presented in Table I. Belenguer et al. [30] specifically survey FL in the IDS context for IoT and vehicular networks, finding that hierarchical and decentralised architectures offer superior Byzantine resilience compared to flat FedAvg, at the cost of increased communication rounds to convergence.

The non-IID data problem

The non-IID data distribution challenge in AV federated learning is analytically more severe than the prevailing literature acknowledges. Zhao et al. [16] formally demonstrated through gradient divergence analysis that FedAvg accuracy degrades by up to 55% in extreme non-IID settings relative to IID baselines — a degradation magnitude that is catastrophic for safety-critical intrusion detection systems with sub-1% false negative rate requirements. In the AV context, the sources of non-IID are structural and invariant to experimental design: vehicles in dense urban environments encounter high pedestrian density, complex multi-lane intersections, and frequent stop-and-go events; highway vehicles predominantly classify high-speed, large-spacing scenarios with infrequent lane changes; rural vehicles encounter non-standard road infrastructure and wildlife-related hazard events. These operational distributions are not merely statistically different — they are operationally orthogonal in the sense that a global model optimised for one distribution may actively degrade performance in another.

Cui et al. [47] address the non-IID problem through personalised FL that maintains a global shared model alongside per-vehicle local adaptation layers, allowing individual vehicles to fine-tune the global model to their local operational distribution. Tian et al. [42] demonstrate that correlated DP can be calibrated to the non-IID degree of each vehicle's data stream, providing simultaneous privacy and convergence benefits. The hierarchical FL approach of Althunayyan et al. [29] partially mitigates non-IID effects by grouping geographically proximate vehicles at the cluster level, implicitly exploiting the assumption that geographic proximity correlates with operational distribution similarity — an assumption that fails in heterogeneous urban cores where vehicles of fundamentally different operational types (taxis, logistics vehicles, private passenger cars) operate in the same geographic region.

Quantum computing for autonomous vehicle security

The quantum threat to vehicular cryptography

The security infrastructure of current vehicular communication systems rests on two computational hardness assumptions: the integer factorisation problem underlying RSA, and the discrete logarithm problem underlying ECC, Diffie-Hellman, and DSA. Shor's algorithm [13], executing in polynomial time O((log N)³) on a fault-tolerant quantum computer, renders both problems efficiently solvable, thereby breaking RSA-2048 and ECC-256 — the cryptographic backbone of IEEE 1609.2 V2V certificate management and ETSI ITS-G5 V2X security. Grover's algorithm [13] provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured search, halving the effective bit-strength of symmetric encryption schemes; AES-128 reduces to approximately 64-bit equivalent security under Grover attack, necessitating migration to AES-256.

The "harvest now, decrypt later" threat model is immediately relevant to AV fleet operators: adversaries collecting encrypted V2X traffic, OTA firmware update channels, and telemetry data streams today can decrypt this data retroactively when cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs) become available. For AV systems generating persistent location histories and behavioural profiles over a 15–20-year hardware lifecycle, this threat is contemporaneous regardless of the precise timeline for CRQC availability. The NIST decision to finalise PQC standards in 2024 [[17], [18], [19]] was explicitly motivated by this harvest-and-decrypt threat model.

Quantum key distribution

Quantum Key Distribution provides information-theoretic security — security that holds against adversaries of arbitrary computational power, including quantum computers — by exploiting the quantum mechanical principle that measuring an unknown quantum state irreversibly disturbs it. The BB84 protocol [49] enables two communicating parties to establish a shared cryptographic key by encoding bit values in photon polarisation states. Eavesdropping attempts disturb the quantum states and elevate the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER):(5)QBER (ε) = d / c, where d = detected bit mismatches, c = comparison bits sampled

If ε exceeds the security threshold (typically 11% for BB84 [49]), the key exchange is aborted and restarted. The no-cloning theorem of quantum mechanics guarantees that an adversary cannot intercept and copy transmitted qubits without introducing detectable disturbance:(6)∄ U s.t. U(|ψ⟩ ⊗ |0⟩) = |ψ⟩ ⊗ |ψ⟩ for all |ψ⟩

This impossibility directly defeats replay and man-in-the-middle attacks on QKD sessions. Quantum entanglement provides an additional security primitive: the E91 protocol [49] exploits Bell inequality violations to verify channel integrity; any adversarial tampering disrupts entanglement correlations and produces a measurable reduction in the CHSH parameter S below the quantum bound of 2√2.

Fig. 13 shows the Schematic of the BB84 protocol applied to vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) key establishment. Alice (AV or RSU sender) encodes bit values in photon polarisation states using randomly selected bases — standard {|0⟩, |1⟩} or diagonal {|+⟩, |−⟩} — and transmits the quantum channel. Bob (receiver) measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis. After transmission, Alice and Bob compare basis choices over a classical channel (basis sifting), retaining only bits where bases matched to form the raw key. QBER estimation on a sample of raw key bits determines whether the channel is secure. The no-cloning theorem guarantees that eavesdropping by Eve introduces detectable errors, elevating QBER above the security threshold. Source: Pan et al. [50], IEEE Access.

Fowler et al. [23] demonstrated practical QKD-derived key generation for V2I communications, achieving approximately 2 kbps key rate at 5 km range using InGaAs single-photon avalanche diodes. Conrad et al. [24] extended QKD to vehicle-mounted mobile platforms through drone-relay quantum channels, demonstrating mobile key distribution feasibility in urban environments. Three deployment barriers systematically understated in these studies merit emphasis: (i) QKD's point-to-point topology is incompatible with the one-to-many BSM broadcast architecture of V2V communication without a trusted relay infrastructure; (ii) free-space optical QKD performance degrades substantially in fog, rain, and particulate conditions — operational contexts that AVs must routinely handle; and (iii) key generation rates of 1–100 kbps are insufficient for per-message V2V authentication at DSRC BSM frequencies of 10 Hz with 350-byte messages, which requires approximately 28 kbps minimum authentication throughput. QKD is therefore most appropriately positioned as a key establishment mechanism for long-lived infrastructure-to-fleet sessions rather than as a per-message V2V security primitive.

Post-quantum cryptography: standards and automotive deployment

The NIST PQC standardisation process (2016–2024) produced four primary standards directly applicable to AV security. FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, based on CRYSTALS-Kyber [17]) provides lattice-based key encapsulation competitive with ECC on ARM Cortex-M4 hardware at NIST Security Level 3 (128-bit post-quantum security). FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium [18]) provides lattice-based digital signatures with verification latency of approximately 4 ms on Cortex-M4. FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, based on SPHINCS+ [19]) provides hash-based stateless signatures with stronger security assumptions but larger signature sizes. FIPS 206 (FN-DSA, based on FALCON) provides compact NTRU lattice signatures but requires floating-point hardware not universally available in automotive ECU designs.

Castiglione and Elia [21] demonstrated CRYSTALS-Kyber integration with CAN bus network segmentation, achieving authenticated channel establishment in 3.2 ms on ARM Cortex-M4 — a result that directly demonstrates PQC deployability on automotive-grade embedded hardware within current latency budgets. Zeydan et al. [20] analysed PQC deployment in V2X Security Credential Management Systems (SCMS), identifying that migration from ECC-based to PQC-based certificate infrastructure requires coordinated updates to Enrolment Authorities, Pseudonym Certificate Authorities, and Certificate Revocation List management systems — an organisational migration challenge that may exceed the technical algorithm transition in complexity and duration.

The hybrid PQC+ECC approach examined by Verma et al. [22] and in related hybrid scheme analyses [[51], [52], [53]] provides a pragmatic transition strategy: combining ECC with lattice-based PQC signatures in a dual-certificate scheme ensures security against both classical and quantum adversaries during the migration period, at the cost of approximately double the signature verification overhead. This approach aligns with NIST migration guidance and is increasingly incorporated into draft V2X security standards under consideration by ETSI TC ITS.

Quantum federated learning for av security: architecture and analysis

Theoretical motivation for QFL

Quantum Federated Learning (QFL) represents the principled integration of quantum computing's computational and cryptographic capabilities with the federated learning training paradigm. Three independent theoretical arguments motivate QFL specifically in the AV security context.Quantum acceleration of local model training: Biamonte et al. [25] demonstrated in a Nature review that quantum algorithms — including the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) — can provide superpolynomial speedups for specific ML optimisation problems relevant to FL training. For AV ECUs where local gradient computation constitutes the primary computational bottleneck per FL round, even a polynomial quantum speedup would enable more frequent global model updates, improving the system's responsiveness to emerging attack patterns.Quantum-secured gradient transmission: The secure aggregation protocol of Bonawitz et al. [12] provides computational security for gradient exchanges but is vulnerable to quantum adversaries. QKD-secured gradient transmission [23] provides information-theoretic security: no adversary of arbitrary computational power can intercept gradient information from a QKD-secured channel without introducing detectable disturbance. For AV FL systems in which model parameters encode safety-critical detection logic, this transition from computational to information-theoretic security assurance constitutes a qualitative improvement in system-level security posture.Quantum-enhanced Byzantine detection: Classical Byzantine-robust aggregation rules — Krum [14], Trimmed Mean [15], Bulyan [56] — require O(n²) pairwise gradient distance computations for n clients, making them computationally prohibitive at fleet scales exceeding several hundred vehicles. Quantum distance estimation algorithms can evaluate pairwise distances in O(√n) time, enabling practical per-round Byzantine filtering at fleet scales of 1000–10,000 vehicles within automotive latency budgets.

[10] — reducing per-round communication overhead by approximately 4 × without statistically significant accuracy degradation. Each quantised gradient package is signed using CRYSTALS-Dilithium [18] before transmission, providing post-quantum authentication that prevents gradient injection attacks by adversaries who have compromised the V2X communication channel but not the vehicle's signing keys.

The Byzantine filtering module applies Quantum Krum — a quantum-accelerated variant of the Krum aggregation rule [14] that leverages quantum distance estimation algorithms to evaluate gradient pairwise similarity in O(√n) time, compared to O(n²) for classical Krum. For a fleet of 1000 vehicles, this represents a 30 × reduction in Byzantine filtering computational time, enabling per-round Byzantine detection within automotive scheduling constraints. The QKD module establishes session keys for the gradient transmission channel; in deployment environments lacking quantum network infrastructure, this module falls back gracefully to hybrid PQC+ECC key encapsulation using CRYSTALS-Kyber + ECC-384 [17].

Research gaps, future directions, and research roadmap

The systematic cross-study analysis presented in earlier sections identifies seven specific, evidence-grounded research gaps at the FL-quantum intersection for AV security. These are not broad thematic observations but precisely formulated deficiencies derived from the convergent limitations identified in Table I, the TRL assessment of Table II, and the security property analysis of Fig. 16. Each research gap (RG) is stated with reference to the specific studies that bound its current state of knowledge.

RG1: absence of validated QFL on real automotive hardware

Despite multiple theoretical QFL proposals — Wang [27] and Majumder et al. [26] — no published study has implemented and validated a QFL system on actual automotive hardware, including ECU platforms, V2X radio modules, or quantum-secured roadside units. Wang [27] and Majumder et al. [26] employ IBM Qiskit quantum simulation environments that do not capture the thermal decoherence, gate error rates, qubit connectivity limitations, and compilation overhead of NISQ-era (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) hardware. The minimum required experimental platform for credible QFL-AV research is a hardware-in-the-loop testbed combining: (a) a real CAN bus network with automotive-grade ECU hardware such as the NXP S32G or NVIDIA DRIVE Orin; (b) NISQ quantum hardware access via cloud API (IBM Quantum, IonQ, or QuEra); and (c) a realistic non-IID AV fleet traffic generator producing representative attack traces from the VeReMi or Car-Hacking datasets. Future research should determine the minimum quantum circuit depth and qubit count required for QFL to demonstrate measurable advantage over classical FL in the AV IDS accuracy-convergence trade-off depicted in Fig. 4.

RG2: quantum byzantine fault tolerance at fleet scale

Byzantine-robust aggregation rules — Krum [14], Trimmed Mean [15], Bulyan [56] — have been extensively studied in classical FL but none has been designed to operate over quantum-secured aggregation channels or to incorporate quantum verification primitives. Classical Krum scales at O(n²) in computational complexity with n client participants, rendering it computationally prohibitive for AV fleets of 1000+ vehicles. Quantum distance estimation algorithms theoretically reduce this to O(√n), enabling per-round Byzantine filtering at automotive fleet scales. This gap requires: (i) algorithmic development of a quantum Byzantine detection rule compatible with the QFL aggregation protocol; (ii) empirical validation on coordinated Byzantine attack scenarios specific to AV FL, including the sensor-spoofing-induced gradient corruption pathway identified; and (iii) characterisation of the minimum Byzantine fraction detectable at each fleet scale under realistic NISQ hardware noise levels.

RG3: standardised benchmark for FL-based AV security

As documented in Table 1, the FL-AV security literature employs at least six distinct datasets (VeReMi [31], CICIDS-2018, Car-Hacking, N-BaIoT, and multiple custom simulation datasets) without standardised FL training/test partitioning strategies, Byzantine client injection protocols, or hardware reference platforms for latency reporting. The apparent performance differences across studies in Table I may therefore partially reflect dataset and experimental configuration differences rather than genuine architectural advantages. The field requires a standardised AV-SecBench suite providing: (a) combined CAN bus, V2X, and sensor attack trace datasets; (b) configurable non-IID FL partitioning protocols; (c) Byzantine client injection at configurable attack fractions; and (d) defined automotive hardware reference platforms with standardised latency and energy reporting methodologies. Without such a benchmark, cross-study comparison — including comparison against QFL proposals — lacks methodological validity.

RG4: energy and latency profiling on automotive ECUs

No FL-IDS study reviewed herein reports energy consumption or thermal budget compliance on real automotive hardware. AV ECUs operate under strict thermal and power envelopes: current ADAS-grade hardware such as the NVIDIA DRIVE Orin operates at up to 60 W, while traditional safety ECUs are typically constrained to 5 W or below. Quantum-aided local training — including hybrid classical-quantum approaches accessing remote quantum backends via API — introduces latency overhead from quantum circuit compilation, classical-quantum data transfer, and shot-based result sampling that must be quantified relative to FL round scheduling constraints before deployment viability can be assessed. Future work should establish energy-accuracy and latency-accuracy Pareto frontiers for FL-IDS architectures on representative automotive platforms and define the power budget constraints that QFL hardware integration must satisfy as quantum coprocessors transition from cloud-accessible to vehicle-embedded deployment.

RG5: cross-layer security composition

The existing literature treats PQC deployment, FL model training, and in-vehicle network security as independent concerns governed by separate threat models. In practice, these layers are compositionally coupled: a V2X eavesdropping attack that compromises the PQC-secured gradient transmission channel simultaneously undermines FL-IDS model update integrity and modifies the anomaly threshold calibration used for in-vehicle CAN bus detection. Cross-layer attack propagation — the evaluation of how an exploit at one security layer cascades through adjacent layers — is entirely absent from the AV FL security literature. Future work should develop formal security composition theorems for multi-layer AV security architectures combining PQC, FL, and in-vehicle network anomaly detection, using compositional security frameworks such as Universal Composability [57] adapted to the automotive deployment context.

RG6: regulatory and standards alignment

ISO/SAE 21434:2021, UNECE WP.29 Regulation No 155, and ETSI EN 303 097 do not address federated learning or quantum security mechanisms. The certification pathway for FL-based IDS is undefined: no existing regulatory framework specifies how a type approval authority should evaluate the security assurance level of a model trained collaboratively across thousands of vehicles without accessing individual vehicle data, or how Byzantine attack resilience should be quantified in a Threat Analysis and Risk Assessment (TARA) according to ISO/SAE 21434. These regulatory gaps constitute a deployment barrier of equivalent consequence to the technical gaps identified above. Future research must engage with UNECE WP.29/GRVA, ISO TC22/SC32, and ETSI TC ITS standardisation working groups to develop FL-specific TARA methodologies and QKD/PQC-specific V2X credential management standards compatible with existing SCMS architectures [20].

RG7: longitudinal adversarial robustness under adaptive attackers

All FL-IDS studies reviewed in Table I evaluate detection performance under a static threat model: a fixed set of attack types is injected once, and detection rates are measured at a single evaluation time point. Real adversarial environments are temporally dynamic — sophisticated threat actors observe deployed detection systems and iteratively adapt their attack strategies to evade detection. Longitudinal evaluation tracking FL-IDS detection performance across multiple cycles of adaptive adversarial co-evolution has not been conducted in the AV context. Future work should adopt a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) adversary model — drawing on the adversarial perturbation framework of Goodfellow et al. [35] and the Byzantine attack taxonomy of Blanchard et al. [14] — to simulate adaptive attackers who refine their gradient poisoning strategies in response to observed changes in the evolving FL-IDS global model. Such evaluation is particularly critical for assessing QFL's claimed advantage in detection accuracy under conditions that are more representative of operational adversarial environments than those used in current benchmarks.

Discussion

Inference 1 — The Simulation-to-Reality Gap Constitutes the Field's Primary Methodological Liability: The FL-AV IDS literature reports impressive benchmark performance — F1 scores exceeding 0.99 for known CAN bus attack detection [29], 99.2% accuracy for IoV anomaly detection [44] — obtained under experimental conditions that systematically differ from operational AV deployment. The experimental fleet sizes of 3–20 vehicles, offline balanced datasets, and static threat models used across the reviewed studies are not representative of the heterogeneous, adversarial, and latency-constrained conditions of commercial AV deployment. The performance delta between benchmark and deployment conditions has not been characterized in any reviewed study, constituting a fundamental reproducibility and validity concern. High-impact future research must prioritize hardware-validated, longitudinally evaluated systems operating at realistic fleet scales over incremental benchmark performance improvements on existing datasets.

Inference 2 — Quantum Security for AVs Requires a Portfolio Approach: The analysis made and Table 2 establishes that no single quantum security technology addresses the full AV threat landscape across all deployment constraints. QKD provides information-theoretic security for key establishment but cannot scale to per-message V2V authentication. PQC provides deployable quantum resistance for authentication and signature verification within automotive latency budgets but offers only computational rather than information-theoretic security. QML offers potential detection accuracy improvements but requires fault-tolerant quantum processors not available within a 5-year deployment horizon. The QFL architecture proposed in Section 5 implements a portfolio approach — PQC for credential management (TRL 6–7, near-term), QKD for aggregation channel session keys (TRL 3–4, medium-term), and quantum optimisation for FL convergence acceleration (TRL 2–3, long-term) — matching each quantum capability to the AV security layer where it provides the greatest practical benefit within realistic deployment timelines.

Inference 3 — The Byzantine Threat in AV FL is Qualitatively More Severe Than in Generic FL: Byzantine attack analysis in generic FL [14,15,56] assumes malicious clients who deliberately submit adversarial gradient updates. In AV FL, the threat model is structurally richer in three respects documented in Section 2: sensor spoofing attacks can induce Byzantine-equivalent gradient corruption in otherwise honest clients without any modification to client FL software; coordinated physical attacks on geographically clustered vehicles can simultaneously compromise multiple clients in the same hierarchical FL cluster; and the safety-critical consequences of AV applications imply that even a 1% detection accuracy degradation from Byzantine influence could result in failure to detect real-time attack events with lethal consequences. These AV-specific threat characteristics motivate the development of dedicated quantum-accelerated Byzantine detection algorithms rather than direct application of classical FL defenses designed for less safety-critical contexts.

Inference 4 — Regulatory Lag Constitutes a Deployment Barrier Equivalent to Technical Immaturity: Technical research maturity in FL and quantum security for AVs is estimated to outpace regulatory framework development by 5–7 years. ISO/SAE 21,434 and UNECE WP.29 Regulation No 155 were developed without provisions for FL-trained models, quantum-secured communications, or the associated certification challenges. Until regulatory certification pathways for FL-based IDS are established — specifying how security assurance levels are assessed without access to individual vehicle training data — automotive OEMs face structural barriers to commercial deployment regardless of the technical maturity of the underlying systems. Addressing this gap requires proactive engagement by researchers with standardization bodies, as called for in RG6.

Conclusion

This paper has presented a rigorously analytical review of Quantum Federated Learning for autonomous vehicle cybersecurity. The contribution of the present work is not a compilation of individual study results but an analytical synthesis that identifies convergent limitations, cross-domain interdependencies, and actionable research gaps that descriptive prior surveys have not exposed.

The review establishes that the AV attack surface expands non-linearly with SAE automation level, with the L3 transition introducing network-layer attack viability at fleet scale that conventional cryptographic mechanisms cannot adequately address. Federated Learning provides a principled privacy-preserving architecture for AV intrusion detection but is constrained by the endemic non-IID data challenge, Byzantine vulnerability at commercial fleet scale, and the structural vulnerability of its aggregation layer cryptography to quantum-capable adversaries. Quantum computing offers information-theoretically secure key distribution through QKD and computationally efficient post-quantum authentication through NIST-standardised PQC algorithms — but deployment at automotive scale faces range, rate, hardware cost, and regulatory maturity constraints that the literature has systematically underweighted.

The convergence of FL and quantum computing in Quantum Federated Learning represents the most theoretically well-grounded and practically urgent research frontier in AV cybersecurity. The QFL reference architecture proposed in this review — integrating CRYSTALS-Dilithium gradient authentication, QKD-secured aggregation channels, and quantum-accelerated Byzantine detection — provides a concrete implementation target for the research community. The seven evidence-grounded research gaps and the structured 2025–2030 roadmap provide the analytical and strategic foundation required to advance QFL from theoretical proposal to standardised automotive security deployment.

As autonomous vehicles progressively assume responsibility for public transportation infrastructure, the integrity of their distributed intelligence systems transitions from an engineering concern to a matter of public safety at national scale. The integration of federated learning and quantum security is not a speculative research direction but a necessary architectural response to the documented vulnerabilities of current AV security infrastructure in a post-quantum adversarial environment.
