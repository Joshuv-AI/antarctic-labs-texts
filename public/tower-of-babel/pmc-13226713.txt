Provenance and economic potential of Egyptian black sands: integrated mineralogical and geochemical insights
Authors: Mohamed Zaki Khedr, Hamada Zaghloul, Eiichi Takazawa, Hesham El-Nahas, Mokhles K. Azer, Sherif Mansour, Tehseen Zafar, Shaimaa Ali El-Shafei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a mineralogical and geochemical investigation of Egyptian black sand deposits to constrain their provenance, depositional controls, and economic potential. Representative samples were collected from the Mediterranean North Coast (Dieba, Damietta, Rosetta, and Balteem) and from the Southern Sinai (Abu Zenima and Nebq). A multidisciplinary approach was applied, integrating mineral separation, grain counting, mineralogical examination using SEM-EDS, mineral chemistry, and bulk geochemistry, along with remote sensing data for Southern Sinai. The results indicate that North Coast black sands are multi-cycle sediments derived mainly from the Nile system and upgraded by coastal reworking. In contrast, Southern Sinai deposits represent first-cycle sediments controlled by local drainage and aeolian processes. Total heavy minerals (THMs) show variable contents averaging ~ 8% in Southern Sinai and ~ 15% along the North Coast, with economic heavy minerals (EHMs) forming ~ 60% and ~ 25% of THMs, respectively. EHMs are dominated by ilmenite (38–56%), magnetite (15–32%), zircon (5–19%), rutile (4–15%), and minor monazite (0‒6%). Mineral chemistry of EHMs, together with geochemical signatures of their host sands indicates polygenetic provenance, involving contributions from mafic–ultramafic, granitoid, metamorphic and sedimentary sources. EHM fractions contain significant contents of rare earth elements (∑REEs: 0.8–3.7 kg/ton), primarily hosted in monazite. Preliminary reserve estimates indicate higher economic potential along the North Coast (0.04‒0.1 million tons/km2 per 1 m depth), while Southern Sinai occurrences are smaller but locally enriched (0.03‒0.14 million tons/km2 per 1 m depth). These findings highlight the significance of Egyptian black sands as promising resources for Fe–Ti oxides, zircon, and REEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53761-4.

Introduction

Black sand deposits represent one of the most important natural reservoirs of strategic heavy minerals such as ilmenite, magnetite, zircon, monazite, rutile, and garnet1–4. These minerals are critical raw materials for numerous industries, including aerospace, electronics, and nuclear energy production, as well as other metallurgical and engineering industries. In addition to these dominant economic minerals, accessory phases such as gold, xenotime, parisite, cassiterite, thorite, and uranothorite may occur in placer systems3,5. These minerals further enhance the strategic value of such deposits.

The heavy mineral assemblages in black sands largely reflect the lithological diversity of source terranes and the efficiency of sedimentary sorting processes6. Metamorphic, mafic–ultramafic, and felsic rocks supply diagnostic minerals such as garnet, amphibole, clinopyroxene, Fe–Ti oxides, zircon, and monazite. Ultra-stable minerals, including zircon, rutile, and tourmaline, may also be recycled from older clastic sedimentary sequences7. Such multi-source inputs, combined with fluvial and coastal processes, produce complex polygenetic placer systems. Their economic potential is controlled by mineral abundance, grain maturity, and compositional characteristics.

In North Africa and the Middle East, significant black sand accumulations occur along the coasts of Egypt, particularly along the North Mediterranean coast (about 11 sites, Fig. 1a). Additional deposits occur in the Sinai Peninsula4,8, and more recent outcrops have been documented in the Southern Eastern Desert (SED) along the Red Sea3. These Egyptian deposits have long been recognized as economically promising and have been investigated in terms of mineralogy, preliminary economic assessments, and provenance interpretations2,5,7–9. However, most of these investigations have relied primarily on mineralogical descriptions or limited geochemical datasets. Integrated approaches combining mineral chemistry, geochemical signatures, and remote sensing remain comparatively limited, particularly in Southern Sinai. This region lies at the intersection of diverse igneous and metamorphic terranes of the Arabian–Nubian Shield (ANS), reflecting considerable geological complexity. Despite this, its black sand deposits remain insufficiently characterized from a provenance and resource-evaluation perspective. Evaluation and study of the economic heavy minerals in Southern Sinai are recently required to supply and support the production line, particularly after the establishment of the Egyptian Black Sand Company in cooperation with the Nuclear Materials Authority.

The present study aims to (1) constrain the provenance of economic heavy minerals in North Coast and Southern Sinai, (2) evaluate the processes controlling their deposition, and (3) assess their economic potential. A multi-proxy approach was applied, combining heavy mineral separation, grain counting, mineralogical description, mineral chemistry, and bulk geochemistry, along with remote sensing data for Southern Sinai. Through this integrated methodology, the study provides new insights into the polygenetic origin of Egyptian black sands and establishes a framework for linking provenance analysis with resource evaluation. The results also contribute to the broader assessment of coastal placer systems as strategic sources of Fe–Ti oxides, zircon, and REE-bearing minerals.

Methodology

Sampling and sample preparation

Thirty representative samples of black sands were collected from six localities (Fig. 1a, b), including four sites along the Mediterranean North Coast (Dieba, Damietta, Rosetta, and Balteem) and two sites in Southern Sinai (Abu Zenima and Nebq). These locations were chosen to capture spatial variability in heavy mineral concentration and sediment characteristics. Sampling was conducted by excavating pits to a shallow depth of 0.5 to 1.0 m. The selected samples were dried, sieved to remove coarse clasts, and then segregated into two representative portions using John’s splitter. The larger portion was weighed and set aside for heavy mineral separation, while the smaller one was retained for bulk geochemical analyses. The heavy minerals were concentrated using a Wilfley shaking table, which is based on wet-gravity separation. Operating conditions were kept constant for all samples to minimize operator bias, following the procedure of Moustafa10. Further purification was achieved through electrostatic and magnetic separation using a Carpco high-tension roll-type electrostatic separator (models HP-167 and HP-114) and a Carpco magnetic separator (model MLH (13) 111-5). The separation process relies on the contrast in specific gravity between gangue (non-economic) minerals, such as quartz (2.65) and green silicates (~ 3.5), and the heavier economic minerals, including monazite (5.2), ilmenite (4.7), zircon (4.6), rutile (4.2), garnet (4.0), and magnetite (4.9). The relative proportions of separated economic and non-economic fractions were determined gravimetrically (Table 2).

Microscopic investigation

After separation, a small representative portion (0.5 gm) of each economic fraction was spread as a single-grain layer on a glass plate and examined with a binocular stereo microscope. The mineral proportions of ilmenite, magnetite, garnet, zircon, rutile, and monazite in each fraction were determined quantitatively based on grain counting and picking (Suppl. Table 2c). Sixteen representative samples of both heavy mineral fractions (6) and economic fractions (10) were selected for detailed mineral identification. A portion (2.5 gm) of each sample was embedded in epoxy resin under vacuum, mounted on glass slides, and prepared as standard 30-µm thin section for examination under reflected-light microscope. Further mineral identification was performed using Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) at Niigata University, Japan. Operating conditions of SEM-EDS included an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a working distance (WD) of 10 mm. The relative proportions of individual minerals in the economic fractions were confirmed via point counting under SEM (Suppl. Table 2c).

Electron microprobe analyses (EMPA)

The major element compositions of heavy minerals (economic and non-economic) were detected using a JEOL JXA-8600SX electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA) at Niigata University, Japan (Suppl. Table 1). Analytical conditions included 15 kv accelerating voltage, 13 nA current, and 1 μm beam diameter. Data correction was achieved through the application of the oxide ZAF matrix correction method. Calibrations were performed using natural and synthetic silicate standards. Minor deviations from ideal oxide totals (< 100%) in nominally anhydrous minerals are attributed to analytical uncertainties, beam interaction effects, and possible submicroscopic inclusions or alteration.

In situ trace-element analyses (EMPA)

The concentrations of trace elements, including rare earth elements (REEs) in economic heavy minerals were estimated in situ via laser ablation‒inductively coupled plasma‒mass spectrometry (LA‒ICP‒MS). Analyses were carried out using a quadruple ICP–MS (Agilent 7500a) coupled to a 213 nm Nd: YAG laser ablation system (New Wave Research UP213) at Niigata University, Japan. The mean values of the in situ data of ilmenite, titanomagnetite, rutile, zircon, and monazite are given in Table 1. Analyses involved laser ablation of spots with diameters ranging from 50 to 80 μm at a frequency of 4 Hz, applying an energy density of 10 J/cm2 per pulse. Each analytical spot required a total acquisition time of approximately 105 s, comprising 45 s of laser ablation and 40 s of background measurement. Carrier gases were maintained at 450 mL min⁻¹ helium and 55 mL min⁻¹ nitrogen. Signal drift during analytical sessions was corrected through the use of five internal standards. For silicate phases, CaO and SiO₂ contents obtained by electron microprobe served as internal references, whereas Al₂O₃, Fe₂O₃, and TiO₂ determined by microprobe were used for Fe–Ti oxides. SRM 612 and SRM 610 reference glasses were used for external calibration of trace-element concentrations in accordance with Pearce11. Data reduction and visualization were carried out using Microsoft Excel and KaleidaGraph (Synergy Software). Analytical accuracy and precision, evaluated against the certified compositions of the reference materials and repeated measurements (n = 7) of the R-185 clinopyroxene standard, were consistently acceptable. Precision was generally better than 12% for most trace elements and better than 15% for Zn, Rb, Cs, Ba, Ta, Pb, U, and Th.

Bulk geochemical analyses

Major- and trace-element compositions of raw sand (9 samples) and economic heavy mineral fractions (17 samples) were analyzed (Suppl. Table 2a) using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The analyses were conducted at the GeoAnalytical Lab, Washington State University (WSU), USA. Samples were ground in agate mills to obtain homogeneous powders. Major elements and selected trace elements (Sr, Nb, Y) were analyzed via XRF using a Thermo ARL XRF Spectrometer. For analysis, the powdered samples were accurately weighed and mixed with di-lithium tetraborate flux in a 1:2 ratio, followed by fusion at 1000 °C in a muffle furnace. The fused beads were cooled, reground, and re-fused to ensure homogeneity and then polished using a diamond lap to produce a flat and smooth analytical surface. Calibration was performed using the USGS reference material (GSP-2, standard 650 CC). Loss on ignition (LOI) was calculated from the weight difference after heating the samples to 1000 °C.

REEs and additional trace elements were determined using ICP-MS (Agilent 7700). Approximately 50 mg of each powdered sample was digested in acid-cleaned Teflon vessels using a mixture of nitric and hydrofluoric acids (3:1 ratio) at 250 °C for a minimum of 8 h. Instrument calibration and sensitivity were established using a blank fused bead prepared from the same flux batch, along with USGS reference standards AGV-2 and RGM-2. Further quality control was ensured through the analysis of additional USGS standards, including DTS-2, BCR-1, and G-2. Detection limits for major, trace, and REEs are provided in Supplementary Table 2a. Samples from SED black sands were analyzed for comparison. Major oxides (wt%) and trace elements (ppm) were subsequently converted to kg/ton to facilitate resource evaluation (Suppl. Table 2b).

Remote sensing of Southern Sinai black sand

Drainage networks in Southern Sinai were mapped using a 30-m resolution digital elevation model (DEM) extracted from the shuttle radar topography mission (SRTM), which employs a spaceborne imaging radar (SIR)-C band instrument. The DEM data were downloaded from the Earth Explorer site (http://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/) of the United States Geological Survey (USGS). The drainage networks were extracted via the hydrology tool provided in the ArcGIS software package.

Lithological units in the study area were delineated using multispectral Landsat-8 (OLI) images of high radiometric resolution (16 bits)12,13. Two cloud-free Landsat-8 (OLI) scenes were downloaded from USGS and captured on October 16, 2021, at 08:01:57.3 AM. The Optimum Index Factor (OIF) technique in ArcGIS software was applied to enhance lithological contrasts. Finally, drainage patterns and lithological mapping were integrated to investigate the sediment sources, transport pathways, and depositional settings of Southern Sinai black sands.

Results

Microscopic description and mineral chemistry of heavy minerals

The examined heavy minerals are classified into opaque and non-opaque categories based on reflected-light microscope as well as SEM-EDS. Opaque minerals are dominated by ilmenite and magnetite, with minor occurrences of Cr-spinel, uranothorite, thorite, gold, sperrylite, and osbornite in the North Coast black sands. The non-opaque mineral suite comprises rutile, pseudorutile, leucoxene, zircon, monazite, cassiterite, and amphibole. Garnet, clinopyroxene, titanite and apatite, along with trace parisite, scheelite, xenotime, cheralite, and fluorcalciopyrochlore, are recorded in the North Coast, whereas barite pertains only to Southern Sinai.

Ilmenite is the dominant opaque mineral, often occurs as subhedral grains (0.1 to 0.7 mm; Fig. 2a), and is locally altered to titanite (Fig. 2b, c). It is characterized by Ti/(Ti + Fe), Fe+ 3/Fe+ 2, TiO2/FeO and Mn/Mg ratios (Suppl. Table 1a), indicative of fresh magmatic ilmenite14. Ilmenite from the Dieba black sand (Table 1) is notably enriched in Zr (3843 ppm), V (~ 1802 ppm), total REEs (∑REEs ≈ 251 ppm), Nb (~ 247 ppm), Ni (~ 242 ppm), Cr (~ 230 ppm), Zn (~ 165 ppm), Co (~ 126 ppm), and Hf (~ 94 ppm) with subordinate concentrations of Th (~ 13 ppm) and U (~ 6 ppm). Its chondrite (CI)-normalized REE pattern is concave (Fig. 3a) and shows a positive slope towards LREE [(La/Yb)N ≈ 3.9 and (Gd/Yb)N = 0.54]. Compared with the primitive mantle (PM) composition15, Dieba ilmenite is strongly enriched in P, U, Th, high field strength elements (HFSEs: Ti, Zr, Nb, and Ta), and HREEs, while exhibiting a marked depletion in Sr (Fig. 3b).

Magnetite occurs as stoichiometric magnetite and titanomagnetite (Suppl. Table 1b). Stoichiometric magnetite (0.1–0.5 mm) is often fractured and mainly occurs as subrounded homogeneous crystals. Titanomagnetite contains variable TiO2 (2.5‒20.7 wt%) content and commonly has exsolved lamellae of ilmenite, showing trellis, banded, and composite intergrowth textures (Fig. 2d). Homogeneous titanomagnetite in Damietta hosts inclusions of apatite and zirconolite (Fig. 4a). Titanomagnetite from Dieba (Table 1) is enriched in V (~ 983 ppm), Zr (~ 164 ppm), Cr (~ 162 ppm), Nb (~ 114 ppm), and Zn (~ 51 ppm). Its REE pattern shows LREE enrichment [(La/Yb)N ≈ 9.5] relative to HREE [(Gd/Yb)N ≈ 3.7, Fig. 3a] with a distinct positive Eu anomaly (Eu/Eu* ≈ 0.5), while PM-normalized patterns resemble those of ilmenite but with HREE abundances that remain close to the PM levels (Fig. 3b).

Rutile mainly occurs as subhedral crystals, whereas leucoxene and pseudorutile (Suppl. Table 1c) represent transitional phases between ilmenite and rutile. The pseudorutile contains lower TiO₂ (56.29–80.23 wt%) and higher FeO (13.65–34.49 wt%) than leucoxene (TiO₂: 83.05–88.53 wt% and FeO: 1.7–9.6 wt%). Rutile from Damietta (Table 1) is enriched in Zr (3839 ppm), V (3028 ppm), Nb (~ 1809 ppm), Hf (~ 183 ppm), and Ta (~ 110 ppm) compared with Dieba rutile (Zr ≈ 61 ppm; V ≈ 102 ppm; Nb ≈ 101 ppm; Hf ≈ 2 ppm; Ta ≈ 1.4 ppm). Conversely, Dieba rutile contains higher REE content (∑REEs ≈ 10 ppm) than Damieta rutile (∑REEs = 0.37 ppm) and exhibits LREE-enriched patterns and relatively flat HREEs (Fig. 3c). Both rutile types are enriched in Ti and HFSEs relative to PM (Fig. 3d).

Zircon (Suppl. Table 1 d) occurs as prismatic to bipyramidal grains (0.1–0.6 mm; Suppl. Figure 1a), locally showing oscillatory zoning (Fig. 2e) or partial replacement by xenotime (Fig. 4b). Some zircon grains in the North Coast black sand are enriched in B (12%; Suppl. Figure 1a) and Hf (2.7%). Zircon from Dieba (Table 1) is enriched in U (~ 202 ppm), Th (~ 254 ppm), ∑REE (~ 566 ppm), Hf (15034 ppm), and Y (837 ppm) compared with Damietta zircon (U ≈ 20 ppm; Th ≈ 11 ppm; ∑REE ≈ 54 ppm; Hf ≈ 444 ppm; Y ≈ 57 ppm). Both types show enriched HREE patterns [(Gd/Yb)N = 0.16 in Diaba and 0.06 in Damietta] relative to LREEs [(La/Yb)N = 0.004 in Dieba and 0.062 in Damietta] and pronounced negative Eu anomalies (Eu/Eu* = 0.13 in Dieba and 0.16 in Damietta, Fig. 3e). They exhibit pronounced positive Th and U anomalies on the PM-normalized multi-element diagram (Fig. 3f).

Monazite (Suppl. Table 1e) mainly occurs as subrounded to oval grains (0.1 to 0.2 mm), typically homogeneous, but locally fractured due to metamictization (Fig. 2f). It is predominantly classified as monazite-Ce (Suppl. Figure 3a). Monazite in the North Coast commonly contains significant values of Th, U, Ag, and Y (Suppl. Figure 1b, c), with a few monazite grains exhibiting notable Os enrichment (Suppl. Figure 1 d). Monazite from Damietta hosts exceptionally high REE concentrations (∑REE ≈ 666017 ppm), alongside substantial concentrations of Y (29645 ppm), Th (50867 ppm), U (7957 ppm), Pb (2758 ppm), and Ga (~ 1621 ppm) (Table 1). Its REE pattern is strongly fractionated, marked by pronounced LREE-enrichment [(La/Yb)N ≈ 207], HREE-depletion [Gd/Yb)N ≈ 25], and negative Eu anomaly (Eu/Eu* = 0.15, Fig. 3g). When normalized to PM composition, monazite is enriched in HFSE, large ion lithophile elements (LILE: Cs, Rb, and Ba), and REE, with notable depletion in Ti (Fig. 3h).

Cr-spinel occurs as fine (< 0.1 mm) to medium (0.2 mm) euhedral to subrounded grains, mostly homogeneous, but locally exhibits compositional zonation (Suppl. Figure 1e). Two compositional types of Cr-spinel are recognized: Cr-rich and Cr-poor spinels (Suppl. Figure 1f, g), showing variable Cr, Mg, and Al contents (Suppl. Table 1f). The Cr-rich type shows elevated Cr2O3 (54.51–55.0 wt%) and Cr# (0.71–0.72), with lower TiO2 (0.23–0.4 wt%) and Al2O3 (14.3–14.7 wt%). By contrast, the Cr-poor type exhibits lower Cr2O3 (18.94–33.85 wt%) and Cr# (0.22–0.43) but higher TiO2 (0.47–0.7 wt%) and Al2O3 (30.44–43.56 wt%). On the Cr2O3 vs. Al2O3 binary diagram (Suppl. Figure 4a), both Cr-spinel types follow the trend of primary spinels in the spinel mantle array. In the Cr# vs. Mg# discrimination diagram (Suppl. Figure 4b), the Cr-rich spinel plots within the fore-arc peridotite field, whereas Cr-poor spinel is scattered across abyssal and back-arc peridotites. This is further seen on the TiO2 vs. Al2O3 binary diagram (Suppl. Figure 4c), where Cr-rich spinel occupies the field of arc peridotite and Cr-poor spinel corresponds to MOR peridotites.

Clinopyroxene (Cpx) forms irregular crystals (0.05–0.13 mm), locally altered to chlorite and occasionally containing Cr-spinel inclusions (Suppl. Figure 1 h). In the North Coast black sand (Suppl. Table 1 g), Cpx ranges in composition from augite to diopside (Suppl. Figure 5a). It exhibits Mg# values of 0.65‒0.94 and contains Al2O3 (0.94–4.73 wt%), Na2O (0.16‒0.53 wt%), and TiO2 (0.48–2.11 wt%), which exceed those recorded in the metamorphic Cpx16, suggesting their magmatic origin. When plotted on the Ti vs. Alt binary diagram, the analyzed Cpx displays a calc-alkaline affinity (Suppl. Figure 5b). On the F1-F2 discrimination diagram (Suppl. Figure 5c), most data occupy the field of ocean floor basalt (OFB) and volcanic arc basalt (VAB).

Amphibole exists as prismatic to rounded grains and belongs mainly to calcic amphiboles (Suppl. Table 1 h) with (B(Na) < 0.5 and B(Na + Ca) > 1.517. Dieba amphibole ranges in composition from pargasite and tschermakite to actinolitic hornblende, whereas the Damietta type varies between magnesio-hornblende and pargasite (Suppl. Figure 6a). In contrast, Abu Zenima amphibole is predominantly actinolite in composition. On the Na/(Ca + Na) vs. (Al/Si + Al), magnesio-hornblende, tschermakite, and pargasite fall within the field of primary amphiboles, whereas actinolite and actinolitic hornblende plot in the field of secondary amphiboles (Suppl. Figure 6b).

Garnet (Suppl. Table 1i) occurs as subhedral grains, sometimes enclosing ilmenite (Suppl. Figure 2a), and is dominated by almandine (~ 30–80%), with variable contents of spessartite (~ 0.6–58%), andradite (0–2.26%), grossular (~ 1.4–29%), and pyrope (~ 1.2–37%), indicative of a magmatic origin18. The Mn, Mg and Fe+ 2 contents of the studied garnet grains closely resemble those of garnet in I-type granites (Suppl. Figure 6c).

Trace opaque minerals include thorite, uranothorite (Fig. 4c), native gold (Fig. 4d), sperrylite (Suppl. Figure 2b), osbornite (Suppl. Figure 2c), and polycrase (Suppl. Figure 2 d).

Trace non-opaque minerals comprise titanite (Suppl. Table 1j), cassiterite, apatite, xenotime, scheelite (Suppl. Figure 2e), fluorcalciopyrochlore (Suppl. Figure 2f), and cheralite. Titanite appears as subrounded to wedge-shaped grains or as an alteration product after ilmenite (Fig. 2b, c). It commonly contains inclusions of titanomagnetite or pyrrhotite (Suppl. Figure 2 g, h). Cassiterite occurs as irregular to subrounded grains, occasionally enclosing columbite-tantalite (Fig. 4e). Apatite is found either as individual subrounded crystals or as inclusions in titanomagnetite (Fig. 4a). Xenotime (YPO4) occurs either as discrete grains (Fig. 4f) or as partial replacements of zircon (Fig. 4b). Barite (0.07–0.1 mm) forms irregular to subrounded grains (Fig. 4g) and hosts strontium (up to 4 wt%). Parisite [Ca(Ce, La)2(CO3)3F2] exhibits well-developed zoning and incorporates Nd, Pr, and Th in its structure (Fig. 4h).

Bulk geochemistry

Economic heavy mineral fraction. Economic heavy fractions of the studied black sand (Suppl. Table 2a) are dominated by Fe2O3 (21.0–56.26 wt%), TiO2 (19.0–39.9 wt%), and ZrO2 (2.0–12.20 wt%), with subordinate amounts (< 4.0 wt%) of Al2O3, CaO, Na2O and MgO. Cr2O3 (up to 0.69 wt%), V2O5 (up to 0.67 wt%), MnO (up to 0.63 wt%) and P2O5 (up to 0.62 wt%) constitute minor amounts.

Abu Zenima economic fractions are notably enriched in Ba and Sr but depleted in Th, Nb, and Y relative to other localities (Suppl. Table 2a). They record higher total REEs (∑REEs ≈ 1481 ppm) than North Coast fractions (∑REEs = 780‒908 ppm). All economic fractions are enriched in total LREEs (∑LREEs ≈ 830‒1306 ppm) relative to HREE (∑HREEs ≈ 144‒174 ppm). Their CI-normalized REE patterns are U-shaped (Fig. 5a), with pronounced negative Eu anomaly (Eu* = 0.05‒0.3) and a positive slope towards LREEs. PM-normalized multi-element diagram shows enrichment in U, Th, HFSEs, and LILEs, with Ni depletion (Fig. 5b). The trace-element and REE distribution patterns (Fig. 5a, b) closely resemble those of heavy mineral fractions from the SED black sand3.

Discussion

Drainage network, lithology, and provenance implications in Southern Sinai

Digital Elevation Model (DEM) analyses reveal that black sand accumulation in Southern Sinai are controlled by two local drainage basins: the Nebq basin on the Gulf of Aqaba (Fig. 6a, b) and the Abu Zenima basin on the Gulf of Suez (Fig. 6c, d). These basins differ markedly in their drainage architecture, lithological composition, and sediment supply, which collectively influence the provenance and distribution of heavy minerals.

The Nebq basin (~ 45.8 km2) consists of a well-developed dendritic drainage network with up to 6 stream orders flowing from elevated terrains in the northwest toward a delta plain at the southeast along the Gulf of Aqaba. The basin surface is dominated by sand and minor gravels (Fig. 6a). False-color composite (FCC) images (bands 9, 5, and 2 in RGB) highlight a diverse assemblage of exposed Neoproterozoic basement rocks belonging to the ANS (~ 870 to 580 Ma). These include calc-alkaline volcanic rocks, metagabbro-metadiorite, alkali-feldspar granite, quartz diorite to granodiorite, weathered grey granites, and Hammamat clastic sediments (Fig. 6b). This lithological diversity, combined with a relatively large drainage area and high stream order, promotes the erosion and mixing of sediments derived from multiple source rocks. Consequently, the Nebq basin is expected to supply a polygenetic heavy mineral assemblage, including contributions from mafic–ultramafic, felsic, and metamorphic lithologies.

In contrast, the Abu Zenima basin is characterized by a smaller drainage system with up to 5 stream orders draining eastward from elevated terrains toward the Gulf of Suez coast (Fig. 6c). FCC imagery indicates that this basin is predominantly underlain by Cretaceous sedimentary rocks (limestone, dolostone, and marl), locally intruded by Miocene dolerite dikes (Fig. 6d). This limited lithological variability and smaller drainage extent suggest a more restricted and localized sediment supply. Accordingly, sediments derived from the Abu Zenima basin are expected to reflect a stronger influence of nearby source rocks, including sedimentary lithologies and minor mafic intrusions. The enrichment in Ba and Sr (Suppl. Table 2a), along with the presence of barite (Fig. 4g), further supports a dominant sedimentary provenance with localized input from nearby lithologies.

Provenance constraints from bulk raw sand geochemistry

Geochemical signatures suggest mixed provenance for the studied raw sands with evidence of sediment recycling. Low Al₂O₃/TiO₂ (~ 0.3‒4.5) and K₂O/Na₂O (0.5–0.8) ratios, together with strong positive correlation between Fe and Mg (r = 0.98), indicate a dominant mafic source contribution19–21. Elevated Cr (323–443 ppm) and Ni (57–120 ppm) further suggest input from ultramafic rocks. However, the presence of quartzose sedimentary signatures (Fig. 7a) in the discrimination diagram of Roser and Korsch22 reflects felsic-dominated sources or significant sedimentary recycling, particularly in North Coast deposits23,24.

Elemental ratios such as La/Sc, Th/Sc, Zr/Sc, and Co/Th (Fig. 7b-d) suggest a mixed provenance involving both felsic and mafic sources, with strong evidence for zircon enrichment and sediment sorting processes25–27. The La–Th–Sc relationships (Fig. 7e) further indicate contribution from metamafic rocks.

The relative enrichment of Southern Sinai deposits (Suppl. Table 1a, Figs. 5c and 9a) is more strongly influenced by local sedimentary and basement inputs (Fig. 6d). In contrast, North Coast and SED black sands show a more homogeneous signature consistent with Nile-derived sediment supply and long-distance transport. They exhibit REE patterns similar to other well-documented coastal placer systems (Fig. 5c), derived from mixed acidic to mafic igneous sources[28, 29, 30]. Variations in Ti/Zr and Zr/Y ratios between Southern Sinai (2.3 and 154, respectively) and North Coast deposits (~ 13 and ~ 36, respectively) further support differences in source composition and/or sedimentary processes30.

Provenance constraints from heavy mineral chemistry

The mineralogical data indicate a polygenetic provenance, involving contributions from mafic–ultramafic, felsic, metamorphic, and sedimentary sources.

Fe-Ti oxides. Textural and compositional features of Fe-Ti oxides are consistent with a predominantly magmatic origin. Ilmenite and titanomagnetite display isovalent trace-element ratios (Zr/Hf = 41 and 36; Nb/Ta = 18.6 and 23.5; Y/Ho = 19 and 23, respectively), consistent with chondritic limits (Zr/Hf = 37, Nb/Ta = 18, and Y/Ho = 29)15. Their well-developed intergrowth textures, including banded, composite, and network exsolution lamellae (Fig. 2d) are typical of mafic igneous rocks31–33. They are likely formed during high-temperature magmatic crystallization followed by cooling processes (Suppl. Figure 7a). The enrichment in V, Ni, Zn, Cr, and Co (Table 1) further supports their derivation from mafic protoliths3,9. In addition, their REE patterns are comparable to those of Fe–Ti oxides originating from mafic sources (Fig. 3a). However, elevated concentrations of Zr (3843 ppm), Hf (94 ppm), Th (13 ppm), U (6 ppm), and ∑REEs (251 ppm) in ilmenite likely reflect the presence of zircon inclusions9. Secondary textures, such as partial replacement by titanite (Fig. 2b) and titanite–ilmenite intergrowths (Fig. 2c), suggest limited inputs from granitoid sources34,35 and/or recycled sedimentary rocks36. Overall, ilmenite is primarily derived from mafic igneous rocks, with subordinate contributions from granitic sources and sedimentary rocks, plausibly from Nubian sanstone.

Rutile. The compositions of rutile grains are consistent with a metamorphic provenance. Low FeO* (0.05–1.24 wt%) and Cr₂O₃ (≤ 0.87 wt%) contents (Suppl. Table 1c; Fig. 8a) and Mg vs. Al discrimination diagram (Fig. 8b) suggest deviation from crustal metamorphic rocks. Zr-in-rutile thermometry37 yields temperatures (~ 530 °C in Dieba and 911 °C in Damietta, Table 1), consistent with metamorphic conditions (430–1100 °C)38,39. However, variation in trace-element composition between two areas (Table 1) indicates contrasting source lithologies. Lower Nb (111 ppm) and Cr (297 ppm) contents in Dieba rutile may reflect metamafic (eclogitic) sources, whereas higher Nb (1809 ppm) and Cr (553 ppm) in Damietta rutile are more consistent with metapelitic or felsic granulitic sources39. Additionally, the Nb/Ta ratio (16.4) of Damietta rutile is close to the chondritic value (Nb/Ta ≈ 18)15, supporting a crustal metamorphic origin. In contrast, the Nb/Ta ratio (~ 75) in Dieba rutile is four times the chondritic value, suggesting partial melting of rutile-bearing eclogite formed by deep subduction of basaltic–gabbroic protoliths40. Variation in PM- and CI-normalized patterns (Fig. 3c, d) further suggests differences in source characteristics. Dieba rutile show similarity with Rosetta rutile, derived from metamorphosed basaltic precursors9, while Damietta rutile exhibits distinct REE distributions. Overall, rutile compositions point to mixed metamorphic sources, although more definitive discrimination would require isotopic constraints.

Zircon. Morphology, zonation patterns, and chemical compositions of zircon collectively suggest a dominantly igneous origin. Most grains exhibit prismatic habits and well-developed oscillatory zoning (Fig. 2e) typical of magmatic zircon41. Trace-element ratios (e.g., Th/U > 0.5 and U/Yb within the continental range) and REE patterns characterized by HREE enrichment, negative Eu anomalies, and positive Ce anomalies (Fig. 3e) are consistent with derivation mainly from granitoid rocks42–44. Furthermore, elemental ratios such as Nb/Hf (0.0002–0.0007), Th/Nb (35.6–75.6), and Hf/Th (39–59) ratios broadly overlap with those reported for post-collisional A-type granites45. This is supported by zircon thermometry (Suppl. Figure 7b), which yields a wide range of temperatures (757–1109 °C) with an average of 982 °C (Suppl. Table 1 d), typical of post-collisional A-type granites. The relative enrichment of Dieba zircon in Hf, Nb, ∑REEs, Th, U, and Y (Table 1) may reflect post-magmatic modification or fluid-assisted alteration46. The replacement of zircon by xenotime (Fig. 4b) further supports such processes47. Although zircon chemistry indicates predominantly granitic sources, sedimentary recycling must be considered. Detrital zircon may be sourced from older sedimentary units such as the Nubian Sandstone48. These quartz-rich sandstones are highly enriched in zircon, representing a significant potential contributor to Nile-derived sediments. Accordingly, the present interpretations pertain only to the examined grains and do not necessarily represent the entire detrital zircon population. More robust provenance constraints would require integration with U–Pb geochronology and Hf isotopic analyses.

Monazite. The absence of curvilinear zonation, together with high LREE contents (Suppl. Figure 3b) and characteristic REE patterns (Fig. 3g) is consistent with derivation from granitic and pegmatitic sources9,49,50. Moreover, elevated Th content (5 wt%) and Th/Ce ratio (0.2) (Table 1) support a granitic source and argue against a hydrothermal origin51. However, like other minerals, contributions from multiple sources cannot be entirely excluded.

Cr-spinel. Both Cr-rich and Cr-poor spinels exhibit compositions typical of mantle-derived Cr-spinel (Suppl. Figure 4a), with Cr# values (0.22–0.72) comparable to those reported from ophiolitic peridotites52,53. Their primary compositions provide critical insights into the petrogenesis and tectonic evolution of their source peridotites3,54–57. Tectonic discrimination diagrams (Suppl. Figure 4b, c) suggest contributions from both supra-subduction zone (SSZ) and mid-ocean ridge (MOR) settings. This compositional variability may reflect the polygenetic nature of Neoproterozoic ophiolites in the ANS, suggesting a transition from early MOR-like (tholeiitic) magmatism to more evolved SSZ-related (boninitic) magmatism during arc development53,58. Estimated parental melt compositions and degrees of partial melting (Suppl. Table 1f) further support this interpretation. The inferred melts display a wide range of Al₂O₃ (12.43–19.13 wt%) and TiO₂ (0.34–1.02 wt%), overlapping with both forearc boninitic melts and MORB and/or back-arc basin basalt (BABB) (Suppl. Table 1k). The calculated degrees of partial melting (F ≈ 14‒23%, Suppl. Table 1f) fall within the range reported for Neoproterozoic SSZ peridotites (14–40)59. Higher melting degrees may be consistent with forearc boninitic magmatism during arc maturation, whereas lower values may reflect earlier MOR-like melt extraction during subduction initiation. The pristine compositions of detrital Cr-spinel in the studied black sands suggest limited alteration during transport and may retain information on mantle processes across the Nubian Shield from Ethiopia to Egypt.

Clinopyroxene (Cpx). Cpx compositions indicate a dominant contribution from mafic-ultramafic igneous sources. Mg# values (0.65–0.88; Suppl. Table 1 g) are consistent with derivation from gabbroic and basaltic rocks, whereas higher Mg# values (> 0.88, up to 0.94) may reflect additional input from ultramafic rocks35. Their high Ti-rich calc-alkaline affinity (Suppl. Figure 5b) suggests an arc-related mafic source. Cpx thermometry60 yields crystallization temperatures of 1077–1166 °C (Suppl. Table 1 g), which fall within the expected range of mafic-ultramafic igneous rocks61,62.The wide compositional variability, including Mg# (0.65–0.94), Al2O3 (0.94–4.73 wt%), Na2O (0.16–0.53 wt%), TiO2 (0.48–2.11 wt%), and Cr2O3 (0.0–0.76 wt%) (Suppl. Table 1 g), likely reflects variable degrees of fractional crystallization and source heterogeneity35. This is further supported by their distribution in the overlap between volcanic arc basalt (VAB) and ocean floor basalt (OFB) (Suppl. Figure 5c), indicating mixed geochemical signatures derived from both arc-related and oceanic mantle sources. These features collectively indicate that Cpx grains are derived from a mixture of mafic and ultramafic lithologies, including basalts, gabbros, and mantle-derived rocks, reflecting a complex and polygenetic provenance.

Amphibole The compositions of amphibole grains suggest mixed sources, including both mafic and felsic lithologies. Pressure-temperature (P–T) estimates indicate variable crystallization conditions (Suppl. Figure 7c; Suppl. Table 1 h), reflecting a heterogeneous provenance. Amphibole from Dieba records higher temperatures (~ 970‒1070 °C) and pressures (~ 8–10 kbar), consistent with values (820–1050 °C) reported for younger gabbros35, suggesting a mafic intrusive source. Conversely, Damietta amphibole yields lower P‒T conditions (~ 710–870 °C at ~ 3.2‒6.9 kbar) comparable to those (786–1005 °C) of I-type granitoids63, supporting a felsic continental origin. The presence of actinolitic hornblende in Dieba further suggests possible hydrothermal overprinting of gabbroic sources.

Garnet Garnet compositions are dominated by the almandine end member (Suppl. Table 1i), consistent with derivation from metamorphic rocks such as gneiss and mica schist or granitic sources11,64. Its wide compositional variability (Suppl. Table 1i; Suppl. Figure 6c, d) reflects multiple source lithologies65. Garnet grains with high pyrope (up to 37.34 mol%) and spessartine contents (up to 58.23 mol%) could be derived from granites64,66 or pegmatites67. The presence of ilmenite inclusions in some garnet grains (Suppl. Figure 2a) may indicate a contribution from high-grade metamorphic rocks68,69 such as granulites (Suppl. Figure 6 d).

Cassiterite Cassiterite is most plausibly derived from granitic pegmatites and stanniferous quartz veins5,70. The presence of columbite–tantalite inclusions in cassiterite (Fig. 4e) supports input from granitic pegmatites70.

Depositional mechanisms and maturity of heavy minerals

The accumulation of heavy mineral deposits in Egyptian black sand is controlled by a complex interplay of sediment supply, transport mechanisms, and weathering intensity6,36. North Coast black sands are mainly supplied by long-distance Nile-derived sediments6,36,48. These deposits are transported through fluvial and aeolian processes and subsequently reworked and deposited as coastal beach placers4. Red Sea Mountains, Ethiopian Plateau and central Africa Plateau are considered the principal sources of heavy minerals in these coastal deposits36. These terrains provide a mixture of igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary materials, which collectively control the composition of heavy minerals in the North Coast black sands6. In contrast, Southern Sinai deposits are controlled by local drainage basins (Fig. 6). These basins exhibit dendritic networks and variable stream orders (Fig. 6), which discharge sediments into the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba.

The spatial distribution of heavy minerals (Table 2) is controlled by both proximity to sediment sources and drainage basin size. Heavy mineral enrichment at Dieba and Rosetta, relative to other North Coast areas, reflects their proximity to former Nile distributaries. These settings favored repeated fluvial–marine reworking, which enhanced heavy mineral concentration and promoted the formation of extensive black sand lenses (Table 2). However, higher concentrations at Abu Zenima (Southern Sinai) compared to Damietta (North Coast) can be attributed to smaller drainage basins. Such basins deliver less diluted sediments, thereby promoting local enrichment despite shorter transport distances.

Textural maturity The grain size (0.1–0.7 mm) and well-rounded to subrounded grain morphology (Figs. 2 and 4; Suppl. Figures 1, 2) of North Coast heavy minerals reflects prolonged fluvial transport followed by wave reworking11. Fractured monazite grains (Fig. 2f) may also result from hydrodynamic effects associated with intense wave action71. In contrast, southern Sinai deposits, although derived from shorter transport pathways (Fig. 6), also display subrounded to well-rounded grains. This apparent textural maturity indicates mechanical sorting and aeolian reworking of older deposits, rather than long-distance transport alone9,72.

Compositional maturity Bulk geochemistry provide constraints on sediment maturity and weathering intensity. The chemical index of alteration (CIA), chemical index of weathering (CIW), and the index of compositional variation (ICV) (Suppl. Table 2a) reveal clear contrasts between Southern Sinai and the North Coast. Lower CIA (~ 5.5) and CIW (~ 5.5) values at southern Sina indicate minimal chemical weathering and rapid erosion of proximal source rocks under arid conditions. In contrast, moderately higher CIA (~ 29) and CIW (~ 30) values along the North Coast suggest partial chemical weathering during prolonged transport and fluvial recycling within the Nile system73,74. The Al₂O₃–(CaO*+Na₂O)–K₂O diagram (Fig. 7f), where all samples plot below the plagioclase–K-feldspar line (CIA < 50), further supports limited chemical weathering overall. High ICV values in Southern Sinai (~ 32) indicate compositional immaturity and are consistent with first-cycle sediments deposited in tectonically active settings75,76. In contrast, lower ICV values along the North Coast (~ 5) reflect mixing with more compositionally evolved detritus and sediment recycling77. Despite these differences, both regions exhibit low K₂O/Al₂O₃ ratios (< 0.2) and high SiO2/Al2O3 (19‒63), indicative of compositionally mature sediments30,78. However, these signatures should be interpreted cautiously, as they may also result from hydraulic sorting and quartz enrichment rather than prolonged chemical weathering.

From the aforementioned observations, North Coast deposits are characterized by both moderate compositional maturity and high textural maturity, reflecting multiple weathering cycles during long-distance transport and coastal reworking. In contrast, Abu Zenima deposits are compositionally immature but texturally mature, indicating first-cycle, locally derived detritus79.

Resource potential and techno-economic significance

Heavy mineral distribution. The studied black sand deposits exhibit significant variation in total heavy mineral (THM) and economic heavy mineral (EHM) concentrations across the Egyptian North Coast and Southern Sinai (Table 2; Suppl. Figure 8). Along the North Coast, THM contents range from ~ 5 to 28%, with an average of ~ 15%. In Southern Sinai, THMs contents vary from ~ 1 to 21%, averaging ~ 8 (Table 2; Suppl. Figure 8a). Locally, black sand lenses exhibit substantial enrichment, reaching ~ 53% in Rosetta and ~ 67% in Dieba (Suppl. Figure 8b). Correspondingly, EHM proportions range from ~ 1 to 10% along the North Coast and from 0.3 to 17% in Southern Sinai, with an average of ~ 4 in both regions. These economic fractions constitute ~ 15–37% of THMs along the North Coast and ~ 14‒89% in Southern Sinai, with averages of ~ 25% and ~ 60%, respectively. These variations reflect differences in sediment supply, depositional processes, and the degree of mineral concentration across the studied areas.

For comparison, typical commercial placer deposits worldwide generally contain ~ 2–20% THM80,81. In this context, the studied black sand deposits fall within or exceed these economic ranges. Compared to SED black sand, where THM and EHM contents rarely exceed 5% and 1%, respectively3, the North Coast and Sinai deposits show substantially higher values with THMs reaching up to ~ 28 and ~ 21 and EHMs up to ~ 10 and 17, respectively (Table 2). These elevated concentrations indicate a significant resource quality and highlight the strong economic potential of the studied deposits, placing them within the range of economically viable heavy mineral placer systems.

The dominant EHMs (Suppl. Table 2c) include ilmenite-leucoxene (38–56%), magnetite (15–32%), zircon (5–19%), rutile (4–15%), monazite (0–6%), and locally cassiterite (0‒17%). Accessory heavy minerals such as barite, parisite, sperrylite, scheelite, and polycrase are also present in the North Coast deposits but are absent or rare in the SED black sand. Barite is particularly common in Southern Sinai, reflecting input from nearby sedimentary provenances (Fig. 6d).

Geochemical variability among North Coast raw sands further reflects differences in mineral supply36. Damietta sands are notably depleted in Fe₂O₃, TiO₂, and ZrO₂ relative to Balteem and Dieba (Suppl. Table 2b; Fig. 9a), consistent with low magnetite and ilmenite abundances (Fig. 10). This depletion is mirrored in the bulk compositions of its EHM fractions, which exhibit lower TiO2 and Fe2O3 (Fig. 9b). Conversely, the relative enrichment of ZrO₂ (106 kg/ton), Hf (2–2.3 kg/ton), and Th (0.5–0.8 kg/ton) in Damietta (Suppl. Table 2b) reflects its higher zircon content (Fig. 10).

The ∑REE concentrations in the EHM fractions range from 0.6 to 3.7 kg/ton, with LREEs forming 72–93% of total REE. Enhanced REE contents (ΣREEs up to 1 kg/ton), together with elevated U (up to 0.12 kg/ton) and Th (up to 0.8 kg/ton) in Damietta fractions (Suppl. These minerals act as the principal carriers of REE and actinides in these deposits.

It should be noted that this estimation is based on several simplifying assumptions, including uniform sediment thickness, lateral homogeneity of the deposits, and a consistent distribution of heavy minerals. In addition, variations in subsurface geometry, sediment compaction, and density were not explicitly considered. Therefore, the calculated tonnages should be regarded as indicative rather than definitive estimates of resources or reserves.

The calculated raw sand tonnage is approximately 2 million tons per km² for all the studied areas (Table 3). The estimated EHM reserves range from 0.04 to 0.1 Mt along the North Coast and from 0.03 to 0.14 Mt Southern Sinai (Table 3). These values are comparable to those reported for the SED (~ 2 mt/km2 of raw sand at 1 m depth)3. However, the EHM reserves in the North Coast and Southern Sinai are about 3‒10 times higher than those in the SED (~ 0.01 mt).

Remote sensing data indicate that black sand deposits cover ~ 1.6 km² in Abu Zenima and ~ 45 km² in Nebq (Fig. 6). Assuming a depth of 1 m, these areas may contain ~ 3.2 mt and 101.3 mt of raw sand, with corresponding EHM reserves of ~ 0.2 mt and 1.5 mt, respectively (Table 3). These results highlight the significant economic potential of Southern Sinai. However, detailed exploration and quantitative resource evaluation, including deeper drilling, grade-thickness mapping, and 3D resource modeling, are required to assess their mining feasibility and economic viability.

Techno-economic implications. From a techno-economic perspective, integrating depositional controls, mineral assemblages, and processing characteristics provides a robust framework for prioritizing exploration and sustainable development of black sand resources in Egypt. The studied black sand deposits along the North coast and Southern Sinai exhibit contrasting techno-economic characteristics controlled by their heavy mineral contents, depositional regime, and geomorphological setting. The North Coast deposits, mainly supplied by the Nile system, form laterally continuous and relatively thick accumulations that are suitable for large-scale mining and centralized processing plants. Such extensive coastal deposits typically provide sustainable feed grades of EHMs, which enhance project feasibility and reduce operational costs82. These characteristics are consistent with economically viable coastal deposits previously documented along the North Coast and currently exploited by the Egyptian Black Sand Company. In contrast, Southern Sinai occurrences (Abu Zenima and Nebq) are smaller, discontinuous, and strongly controlled by local drainage and aeolian reworking (Fig. 6c, d). Although these deposits may locally yield high heavy mineral concentrations (Table 2) due to reduced sediment dilution, their limited lateral continuity generally favors small-scale extraction strategies83. Previous technical evaluations by the Nuclear Materials Authority similarly indicate that deposit value depends primarily on deposit continuity rather than peak heavy mineral concentration.

The dominance of ilmenite, magnetite, zircon, monazite, and rutile (Fig. 10) suggests strong potential for multi-product recovery, which improves economic stability and reduces economic risk. Ilmenite and rutile represent major sources of Ti used in pigments and advanced alloys, zircon is a key source of Zr widely applied in ceramics and nuclear industries, and monazite constitutes an important source of REEs critical for high-technology and renewable energy applications. The coexistence of Ti‒bearing minerals with zircon and REE-bearing monazite (~ 666,017 ppm, Table 1) further increases the strategic value of the deposits. The low CIA values, together with the corresponding high ICV values (Suppl. Table 2a), indicate that heavy minerals are largely preserved in primary crystalline form, which typically improves beneficiation efficiency through gravity and magnetic separation and minimizes processing losses84. Overall, the North Coast deposits represent the most favorable targets for long-term industrial development owing to their scale and continuity, whereas Southern Sinai placers constitute smaller but locally high-grade resources suitable for targeted exploitation.

From a sustainability perspective, the exploitation of black sand deposits should consider possible environmental and social impacts. Large-scale mining along the North Coast may affect coastal landforms, disturb sediments, and impact local ecosystems if not properly controlled. In Southern Sinai, where deposits are smaller and more localized, careful planning is also required to reduce site-specific environmental impacts. Therefore, the use of environmentally responsible practices, such as controlled extraction, land rehabilitation, and proper waste management, is essential to ensure sustainable development of these resources. In addition, black sand mining can contribute to local economic development by creating job opportunities, provided that appropriate environmental regulations are followed.

Conclusions

The Egyptian black sand deposits represent polygenetic placer systems formed through the combined effects of source-rock diversity, sediment routing, and hydrodynamic sorting. Heavy mineral chemistry and geochemical signatures of host raw sands reveal contributions from mafic-ultramafic, granitic, and metamorphic rocks of the ANS, as well as Nubian Sandstone sequences.

Depositional mechanisms and geochemical signatures show that the Mediterranean North Coast deposits reflect multi-cycle recycling through the Nile dispersal system, whereas Southern Sinai placers preserve largely first-cycle, locally derived detritus shaped by short drainage pathways and aeolian reworking. These contrasting depositional pathways strongly control resource distribution. North Coast deposits form laterally continuous, thick accumulations favorable for large-scale exploitation, whereas Southern Sinai deposits are more discontinuous but locally enriched due to limited sediment dilution. Despite these differences, both regions host economically significant concentrations of Fe–Ti oxides, zircon, and REE-bearing minerals, indicating substantial strategic value for future resource development. The integrated mineralogical, geochemical, and techno-economic assessment establishes a genetic and exploration framework linking provenance, sedimentary processes, and economic potential. Such an approach not only clarifies the evolution of Egyptian black sands but also provides a transferable model for evaluating placer systems in arid continental margins worldwide.
