Asia-Pacific analysis of ESMO study on availability, out-of-pocket costs (OPC) and accessibility of antineoplastic medicines
Authors: A.N.M. Wong, P. Franco, H.S. Sheikh, O. Pitiyarachchi, N. Prasongsook, M.V. Ordinario, R. Velasco, V. Agarwala, A. Dinh TL, A. Kapoor, T. Kogawa, H.H. Loong, R. Lui, H.G. Luna, M. Matias, K.Y.Y. Ng, V. Selvaraj, A. Sunggoro, L. Thamlikitkul, N. Cherny

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) conducted a global survey on the availability, out-of-pocket costs (OPCs), and accessibility of cancer medicines in 2015, with an updated survey in 2023. The Asia-Pacific (APAC) region accounts for approximately half of global cancer-related deaths, yet substantial disparities persist in access to essential cancer medicines. A focused analysis of the 2023 ESMO global survey was undertaken to evaluate access trends specific to the APAC region.Patients and methodsData from the 2023 ESMO global survey were analysed to assess the availability, access, and OPC of cancer medicines in APAC countries, using the World Health Organization’s Essential Medicines List as a benchmark. Countries included in the analysis were Australia, Taiwan (China), Japan, New Zealand, Republic of Korea, Singapore, China, Malaysia, Thailand, Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nepal, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Vanuatu, and Vietnam. Selected case studies, including immunotherapy and oral targeted therapies, were used to explore access and regulatory approval timelines. A descriptive comparison of health technology assessment processes across APAC countries was also conducted.ResultsAvailability and access to antineoplastic medicines in APAC remain limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Although novel therapies are generally available, they often incur substantial OPC burdens on patients. The case studies reveal considerable delays in regulatory approval for both immunotherapy and targeted therapies across the region.ConclusionsDespite the introduction of innovative cancer therapies, substantial inequities in access and availability persist across APAC. Coordinated regional policies and streamlined regulatory processes may improve equitable access in the region.

Introduction

Cancer remains the leading cause of death across the Asia-Pacific (APAC) region and represents a substantial proportion of the global cancer burden. Recent estimates indicate that APAC accounts for ∼49.2% of worldwide cancer incidence and 56.1% of global cancer mortality, underscoring the significant public health impact on the region’s diverse populations and health care systems.1 Access to effective and innovative oncology medicine remains uneven and often limited despite this disproportionate burden.

To better understand global disparities in cancer medicine availability, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) conducted a comprehensive international survey in 2015, assessing availability, accessibility, and out-of-pocket costs (OPCs) of cancer therapeutics across countries of varying income levels.2,3 In 2023, ESMO updated this survey to capture evolving access patterns, regulatory landscapes, and economic pressures influencing oncology care worldwide.4 In this most recent analysis, for low-, middle-, and high-income countries, 40% of traditional chemotherapy agents in the World Health Organization (WHO) Essential Medicines List (EML) are only available at full cost to patients.

Within this global context, the APAC region continues to face particularly pronounced challenges. Although it bears roughly half of all cancer-related deaths, access to essential cancer medicines varies widely both between and within countries, shaped by differences in health system infrastructure, reimbursement policies, drug pricing mechanisms, and the availability of universal health coverage. For many populations in APAC, high OPC and inconsistent national formularies further exacerbate inequities in treatment access.

Given the region’s significant disease burden and persistent disparities, a focused analysis of the 2023 ESMO global survey was undertaken to examine access trends specific to APAC. This detailed evaluation aims to provide a clearer understanding of current gaps and the systemic factors influencing the availability and affordability of cancer medicines in the region. Such insights are critical for guiding policy interventions, resource prioritisation, and regional strategies to improve equitable cancer care.

Patients and methods

This study represents a predefined regional subanalysis of the 2023 ESMO global survey evaluating the availability, accessibility, and affordability of anticancer medicines. In the 2023 global survey, 317 reporters and 231 peer reviewers from 126 countries contributed to the data.

For the present analysis, countries within the APAC region were included if complete data were available for the variables of interest. A total of 20 countries were analysed, representing a broad spectrum of economic development and health system structures. Countries were stratified according to World Bank income classifications for the 2023 fiscal year and grouped as high-income, upper-middle-income, and low- and lower-middle-income settings.

Three distinct dimensions of access were examined. Availability was defined as the presence of a medicine within a country, indicating that it is registered and obtainable within the health care system, irrespective of reimbursement status. Accessibility referred to the consistency with which patients are able to obtain a medicine when clinically indicated and was operationalised using ordinal survey categories ranging from ‘Always’ to ‘Never’. For the purposes of the primary analysis, medicines reported as ‘Always’ accessible were considered to reflect reliable access. Affordability as the proportion of total treatment cost borne directly by patients, and categorised as free, <25%, 25%-50%, >50% but not full cost, or full cost.

The primary analysis focused on antineoplastic agents included in the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines, which served as a reference standard for essential cancer care. In addition, an extended analysis was conducted for a subset of systemic therapies with high clinical benefit, defined according to the ESMO Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS). Medicines meeting thresholds corresponding to substantial clinical benefit (grades A-B in the curative setting or 4-5 in the noncurative setting) were included. Selection was based on clinical relevance across common solid tumours and representation of key therapeutic classes, including targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and endocrine agents.

Analyses were conducted at the level of the medicine–country pair, with each observation representing the reported status of a given medicine within a specific country. For each income group and regional aggregation, the denominator was defined as the total number of medicine–country observations for the medicines included in the relevant cohort. Outcomes of interest were the proportion of medicines reported as ‘Always’ accessible and the proportion available only at full OPC. Results are presented descriptively, stratified by income group and, where appropriate, by individual country. Given the descriptive nature of the dataset, no formal hypothesis testing was undertaken.

To contextualise these findings, selected case studies were used to examine timelines from regulatory approval to public reimbursement for representative therapies. In parallel, a qualitative assessment of health technology assessment (HTA) processes across APAC countries was undertaken, focusing on structural features relevant to access, including the presence of formal HTA bodies, decision-making frameworks, and mechanisms for special or compassionate access.

The targeted analysis of the ESMO survey included 20 APAC countries: Australia, Taiwan (China), Japan, New Zealand, Republic of Korea, Singapore, China, Malaysia, Thailand, Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nepal, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Vanuatu, and Vietnam.

OPCs showed a pronounced income gradient: the proportion of WHO EML antineoplastics available only at full cost was lowest in high-income countries (0%), modest in upper-middle-income countries (1.5%), and markedly higher in low-income countries (50.4%).

High-clinical-benefit medicines not included in WHO EML

An extended analysis was conducted focusing on high-clinical-benefit medicines, as defined by the ESMO-MCBS. Included therapies were stratified by tumour type and comprised breast cancer (abemaciclib, ribociclib, pertuzumab, and ado-trastuzumab emtansine), lung cancer (osimertinib and pembrolizumab), colorectal cancer (cetuximab, panitumumab, and pembrolizumab), prostate cancer (abiraterone and enzalutamide), renal cell carcinoma (pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, ipilimumab, nivolumab, axitinib, cabozantinib, and lenvatinib), and melanoma (ipilimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, encorafenib, binimetinib, dabrafenib, trametinib, vemurafenib, and cobimetinib).

High-MCBS medicines are readily accessible in high-income countries (96.8% always accessible), less accessible in upper-middle-income (31.1%) and low-income countries (26.7%) (Figure 2). HTA mapping

HTA mapping included nine countries in the Indo-Pacific region: Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, India, Indonesia, Brunei Darussalam, Philippines, Vietnam, and Singapore. The majority reported the presence of a formal HTA committee, except for Brunei Darussalam. HTA-type activities here are limited and ad hoc, under the oversight of the country’s Ministry of Health.

Quality-Adjusted Life Year-based cost-effectiveness analysis predominated, whereas Thailand and Australia primarily used Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio-based approaches. Explicit willingness-to-pay thresholds were inconsistently defined, with several countries applying gross domestic product per capita-based thresholds (Philippines and India), whereas others reported no formal threshold.

HTA timelines varied substantially across the region. Time from submission to final recommendation ranged from 12 to 20 weeks in Brunei Darussalam to 97-128 weeks in the Philippines, whereas intermediate durations were observed in India (30-56 weeks), Thailand (2452 weeks), Indonesia (31 weeks), Singapore (25 weeks), and New Zealand (86 weeks). Several countries (New Zealand, Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brunei Darussalam) reported the existence of accelerated or expedited HTA pathways, with review durations ranging from 4 to 24 weeks.

Public availability of HTA criteria was reported in most countries, although not uniformly. Requirements for local clinical practice guidelines were uncommon and were observed only in Indonesia. Compassionate use or special access programmes for unapproved drugs were available in most jurisdictions, apart from Indonesia.

Overall, substantial heterogeneity exists in HTA structure, methodological approaches, and decision timelines across the APAC region, reflecting variability in system maturity and policy implementation.

The observed heterogeneity in HTA processes across APAC has implications beyond national boundaries, creating variability not only in access but also in evidentiary standards for reimbursement. This raises the potential value of regional cooperation models, including shared assessment frameworks, joint evidence reviews, or mutual recognition mechanisms, which could reduce duplication, accelerate decision-making, and promote greater equity in access across the region.

As highlighted in our illustrative examples of pembrolizumab for non-small-cell lung cancer, regulatory approval in APAC lags behind international agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration in the United States as well as the European Medicines Agency in Europe. However, the significant lag that stops these cancer treatments from reaching patients is the lag for reimbursement. Discussion

This in-depth APAC analysis should be understood as part of the broader intellectual trajectory of the ESMO Global Consortium programme, which has progressively mapped disparities in access to cancer medicines across Europe, other Low- and Middle-Income Country regions, and now the APAC region. Consistent with prior work by Cherny et al.4, our findings reinforce a central observation: regulatory approval alone does not equate to equitable access. The APAC data extend this paradigm by demonstrating how financing structures, reimbursement systems, and health system capacity fundamentally shape whether therapeutic innovation translates into patient benefit.

Accessibility and availability of cancer medicines varied substantially across the APAC region. Comparable to the ESMO global survey, inequities remain with persistent gaps across the upper-middle- and low-income groups. Although essential cancer medicines were accessible in most countries in APAC, there were notable exceptions such as Myanmar and Papua New Guinea. Furthermore, these accessibility challenges are compounded by substantial OPCs to patients and their families.

The access and availability of medicines of high clinical benefit as ranked by the ESMO-MCBS are more limited than the WHO EML medicines across the APAC region. Only a minority of countries in the upper-middle- and lower-income countries have full access to these high-clinical-benefit medicines, and the OPCs are almost fully borne by patients, even in high-income countries such as Singapore and New Zealand.

The evolving role of the WHO EML warrants consideration in this context. The inclusion of additional high-benefit therapies in recent updates represents an important normative signal regarding standards of care. However, the present findings indicate that EML inclusion alone is insufficient to ensure equitable access. Its impact is contingent upon alignment with national reimbursement frameworks, financing capacity, and procurement mechanisms. Without such alignment, the translation of global recommendations into local access remains incomplete.

These findings highlight a fundamental structural dilemma in modern oncology systems: the competing imperatives of universal provision of essential therapies and selective funding of high-cost, high-benefit innovations. Although WHO EML inclusion reflects a consensus minimum standard of care, the expansion of high-clinical-benefit therapies increasingly challenges fiscal sustainability, particularly in middle-income settings. The APAC data demonstrate that even when such therapies are available, reimbursement gaps and system constraints render them functionally inaccessible, underscoring that access limitations are not merely implementation failures but manifestations of underlying financing constraints.

Although the ESMO-MCBS provides a robust framework for assessing clinical benefit, high clinical benefit does not automatically translate into affordability-adjusted societal value across all health systems. The tension between individual-level benefit and population-level allocative efficiency remains an important, albeit implicit, consideration in resource-constrained settings.

Although regulatory approval delays remain relevant, our findings suggest that reimbursement lag represents the principal barrier to patient access across many APAC systems. These delays are not solely administrative inefficiencies but reflect deeper structural constraints, including limited fiscal capacity, fragmented insurance coverage, and constrained negotiating leverage in the face of global pharmaceutical pricing. The pembrolizumab case study illustrates this clearly: even in high-income settings such as New Zealand, substantial delays in reimbursement relative to Australia highlight that affordability and prioritisation, not regulatory readiness, are often the dominant determinants of access.

A defining feature of the APAC region is its marked heterogeneity, encompassing a wide range of health system models, levels of public financing, and institutional capacity for HTA and price negotiation. Substantial variation in HTA timelines has direct implications for patient access. Prolonged review durations in some settings may delay access to novel therapies, whereas shorter timelines may reflect less formalised processes. The presence of accelerated pathways in multiple countries is encouraging but lacks standardisation and clear criteria for use. This diversity complicates the development of uniform policy solutions and instead points to the need for context-specific strategies. Strengthening regional collaboration may further support more efficient and equitable evidence-based decision-making. Although regional initiatives such as the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation Regulatory Harmonization Steering Committee have been active since 2008, their effect on patient access is likely to be limited in the absence of parallel reforms addressing affordability and reimbursement. In an attempt to address some of these challenges, initiatives such as ATLAS: Asian Clinical Trials Network for Cancers aim to bridge academic-regulatory-industry networks across 10 APAC countries (Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, China, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Philippines, and India).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The underlying data are based on expert reports and may be subject to reporting bias and variability in interpretation of survey categories. The analytical approach, based on medicine–country observations, does not capture within-country heterogeneity or subnational disparities, which may be substantial. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding causal relationships between system-level factors and access outcomes. Furthermore, reliance on publicly available data for the HTA mapping may not fully capture evolving HTA practices.

Importantly, the national-level framing of access likely obscures substantial within-country disparities, which may exceed differences observed between countries. Urban–rural divides, public–private fragmentation, and subnational variability in reimbursement policies are likely major determinants of real-world access. Medicines categorised as ‘available’ at the national level may remain inaccessible to large segments of the population, particularly outside metropolitan centres.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, the findings have clear implications. Inequities in access to anticancer medicines across the APAC region remain substantial and structurally embedded. Bridging these gaps will require sustained policy attention and system-level reform to ensure that the availability of effective therapies translates into timely, affordable, and equitable access for patients.

Improving access to anticancer medicines in the APAC region will require coordinated action that extends beyond regulatory approval to address affordability, financing, and the efficiency of reimbursement processes. In particular, alignment between clinical benefit and funding prioritisation, coupled with strengthened mechanisms for price negotiation and financial protection, will be essential. Without such efforts, existing disparities in access to both essential and high-benefit therapies are likely to persist.
