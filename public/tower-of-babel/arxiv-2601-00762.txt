The Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule as a consequence of a non-singular optical susceptibility in semiconductors
Angiolo Huamán

Abstract
The Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn optical (TRK) sum rules for bulk materials have customarily been obtained by combining the Kramers-Kronig relations with the high frequency limit of the optical susceptibility tensor χ i ​ j \chi_{ij} . Also, a non-singular expression for χ i ​ j \chi_{ij} involve the reduction of some its parts to an effective mass tensor. In this paper we show that the latter procedure is intimately connected to the TRK sum rules, and in fact these sum rules can be obtained from it. In reaching this result, we present before a thorough description of the momentum matrix elements of Bloch eigenfunctions bypassing the so-called 𝒌 − \bm{k}- representation.

II
Introduction

The study of the optical properties of system is of fundamental importance because their connection to the energy bands and wavefunctions serves as a probe of the electronic properties of quantum systems. In fact, before the advent of more modern techniques like ARPES, much information about the electronic states of a system were inferred from its optical response to an applied optical external field.

On the theoretical realm, the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn (TRK) sum rules
3
for the optical susceptibility of a solid remain a crucial tool for validating calculation on optical susceptibilities. Similar to the sum rules in atoms
6
, the optical response of a periodic solid, this is its frequency-dependent optical susceptibility
\chi_{ij}(\omega)
, is constrained by the TRK sum rule, which relates the imaginary part of
\chi_{ij}
to the electron density
n_{e}
of the material. In Gaussian units, this relation reads:

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}]=\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{m}n_{e},

(1)

where
\omega
is the light frequency,
m
and
-e
are the electron mass and charge, respectively. Equation (
1
) is customarily proved by a combination of the Kramers-Kronig relations and the high frequency limit of
\chi_{ij}
, which can be obtained by using beautiful physical arguments
12
;
8
, valid both for insulators, semiconductors and metals, emphasizing that at sufficiently high frequencies, all these materials behave the same.

On another note, in the independent particle approximation (IPA), we can obtain expressions for the optical susceptibility, both linear and nonlinear, by solving the equation of motion of the density matrix in a perturbative way
7
. These equations have the disadvantage of being singular at frequencies close to zero, although it can be shown that this is a mathematical rather than physical feature: by using the properties of the Bloch wavefunction of the underlying material, we can show that the factors giving a singular behavior are in fact zero.

It is the purpose of this article to show that, at least in the case of semiconductors, Eq. (
1
) can be obtained purely from the properties of the Bloch eigenfunctions and their momentum matrix elements (MME), and thus is a consequence of the susceptibility being non-singular at
\omega=0
. A key step in doing this is to reduce the singular factor to an integral of the effective mass tensor over the Brillouin zone (BZ). In doing this, we prefer to avoid the so-called
\bm{k}-
representation, and calculate the MMEs purely from the translation properties of the Bloch eigenstates.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Sec.
III
we present the way Bloch wavefunctions are normalized. Section
IV
deals with the calculation of the MMEs and the effective mass tensor, in a form suitable for the study of optical properties. Our main result, the TRK sum rule from the properties of the Bloch waves is presented in Sec.
V
. The conclusions we arrived at are summarized in Sec.
VI
.

III
Normalization of a Bloch function.

In a perfect crystal of volume
V
and lattice vectors
\bm{a}_{j}
(
j=1,2,3
), the electrons move in a periodic potential
V(\bm{r})
satisfying
V(\bm{r})=V(\bm{r}+\bm{R})
, where
\bm{R}
is point in the lattice,
\bm{R}=\sum_{j}n_{j}\bm{a}_{j}
, with
n_{j}
integers. The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
H=\bm{p}^{2}/2m+V(\bm{r})
, are Bloch waves
\psi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=e^{i\bm{k}\bm{\cdot}\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
satisfying
H\psi_{n\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\psi_{n\bm{k}}
, where
u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
has the periodicity of the crystal:
u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r}+\bm{R})
. In the crystal has
N_{j}
unit cells along the
\bm{a}_{j}
lattice vectors, periodic or Born-von Karman boundary conditions
2
state that the
\bm{k}
vector can only have the values:

\bm{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{n_{j}}{N_{j}}\bm{b}_{j},

(2)

where
n_{j}
are integers and
\bm{b}_{j}
are the reciprocal vectors. The Bloch eigenstates are normalized according to:

\int_{V}d\bm{r}\,\psi_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\psi_{n\bm{k}}=\delta(\bm{k}^{\prime}-\bm{k})\delta_{n^{\prime}n},

(3)

which can be established by diving the volume
V
into unit cells of volume
\Omega
and noting that
\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\bm{\cdot}\bm{R}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{\Omega}\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})
in the limit of a very large solid [under the assumption in Eq. (
2
)]. This imposes the following normalization on
u_{n\bm{k}}
:

\int_{\text{uc}}d\bm{r}\,u_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}^{*}u_{n\bm{k}}=\frac{\Omega}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta_{n^{\prime}n},

(4)

according to which
u_{n\bm{k}}
is dimensionless. It is useful to point out that this normalization is not dimensionless, but the square norm of
\psi_{n\bm{k}}
has dimensions of inverse of a length to the cube. This extra dimension replicates in the matrix elements between Bloch eigenstates. In the following sections, we will see that dimensionally correct matrix elements can be defined at the
same

\bm{k}
point.

IV
The matrix elements of the momentum operator

Because the Bloch eigenfunctions are extended, the calculation of matrix elements between them poses particular difficulties, as it entails the appearance of Dirac deltas in the quasimomentum
\bm{k}
. Before going into a detailed presentation, let us present the most general form of these matrix elements. By definition, they are equal to:

\langle n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}|\bm{p}|n\bm{k}\rangle=\int_{V}d\bm{r}\,\psi_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}\psi_{n\bm{k}},

(5)

where the integrals is taken over the volume
V
of the solid. By replacing
\psi_{n\bm{k}}=e^{i\bm{k}\bm{\cdot}\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}
into the equation above and after reducing it into the unit cell, we get:

\displaystyle\langle n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}|\bm{p}|n\bm{k}\rangle=\hbar

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\Big[\bm{k}\,\delta_{nn^{\prime}}-

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{\Omega}\int_{\text{uc}}d\bm{r}\,u^{*}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}i\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}\Big].

(6)

This result is a statement of the important fact that the momentum operator is diagonal in the quasimomentum
\bm{k}
, and it allows for a natural definition of the MMEs at the
same

\bm{k}
point,
\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}(\bm{k})\equiv\langle n^{\prime}\bm{k}|\bm{p}|n\bm{k}\rangle
:

\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}=\hbar\Big[\bm{k}\,\delta_{nn^{\prime}}-\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{\Omega}\int_{\text{uc}}d\bm{r}\,u^{*}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}i\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}\Big],

(7)

that does have units of momentum. Also, because of time-reversal symmetry we can choose
u_{n\bm{k}}^{*}=u_{n,-\bm{k}}
, thus from Eq. (
7
) we have
\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}(-\bm{k})=\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}^{*}(\bm{k})
.

Before proceeding further, a few comments are necessary. In the coupling with an external oscillating electric field, energy and quasimomentum must be conserved. In the latter case, the quasimomentum of the electron after absorbing a photon should equal to its original quasimomentum plus the quasimomentum of the photon. However, in the optical range
\hbar\omega\simeq 1\,
eV, the photon quasimomentum is much smaller than the electron quasimomentum (within the BZ), so that we can safely assume that the electron does not change its quasimomentum upon absorbing a photon. Mathematically, this amount to including only MMEs with the same
\bm{k}
, as those defined in Eq. (
7
). Having this is mind, from now on we will focus on this kind of MMEs only.

IV.1
Momentum matrix elements based on the Fourier expansion of the Bloch eigenfunctions.

The matrix elements of the momentum operator were given in Eqs. (
IV
) and (
7
). In most calculations involving optical properties of solids, however, the velocity operator is more relevant:

\bm{v}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\bm{r},H].

(8)

Of course, since
H=\bm{p}^{2}/2m+V(\bm{r})
, the usual result
\bm{v}=\bm{p}/m
is recovered, where
m
is the electron mass. Using Eq. (
8

\bm{r}

\bm{k}-

\bm{k}-
dependent coefficients of a wavefunction constructed as a combination of Bloch eigenstates
4
;
13

Although this approach should be considered standard by now, the same expressions for the MMEs can be obtained by insisting with the usual
\bm{r}-
representation, using the periodicity of the functions
u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})

11
. We take this path to a full extent and use it to obtain expressions for the MMEs and the effective mass tensor, as they are often used in articles on first principles optics in semiconductors.

The functions

u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{G}}e^{i\bm{G}\bm{\cdot}\bm{r}}f_{n\bm{G}}(\bm{k}),

(9)

where the sum goes over the lattice vector
\bm{G}
of reciprocal space,
\bm{G}=\sum_{j}n_{j}\bm{b}_{j}

j=1,2,3
and
n_{j}
integers. The normalization in Eq. (
4

9
) and results in:

\sum_{\bm{G}}f_{n^{\prime}\bm{G}}^{*}f_{n\bm{G}}=\delta_{n^{\prime}n}/(2\pi)^{3},

(10)

where we have used
\int_{uc}d\bm{r}\,e^{i\bm{r}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{G}-\bm{G}^{\prime})}=\Omega\,\delta_{\bm{G},\bm{G}^{\prime}}

9

\psi_{n\bm{k}}=e^{i\bm{k}\bm{\cdot}\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}

\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\bm{k}+\bm{G}|^{2}f_{n\bm{G}}+\sum_{\bm{g}}V(\bm{G}-\bm{g})f_{n\bm{g}}=\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}.

(11)

In this equation,
V(\bm{q})

\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{uc}d\bm{r}\,e^{-i\bm{q}\bm{\cdot}\bm{r}}V(\bm{r})=V(\bm{q})

9

7

\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}=(2\pi)^{3}\sum_{\bm{G}}\hbar(\bm{k}+\bm{G})f_{n^{\prime}\bm{G}}^{*}f_{n\bm{G}}.

(12)

It is customary, and also useful, to write the equation above by separating the cases
n^{\prime}=n
and
n^{\prime}\neq n

\bm{p}_{nn}=(2\pi)^{3}\sum_{\bm{G}}\hbar(\bm{k}+\bm{G})|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}(\bm{k}+\bm{G})f_{n\bm{G}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\bm{k}+\bm{G}|^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{g}}V(\bm{G}-\bm{g})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{g}}=f_{n\bm{G}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}+\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}.

By multiplying the equation above by

\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{\bm{G}}(\bm{k}+\bm{G})|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\sum_{\bm{G}}|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2}=\sum_{\bm{G}}\big[\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\bm{k}+\bm{G}|^{2}\big]f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}-\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{g}}V(\bm{G}-\bm{g})f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{g}}.

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\bm{k}+\bm{G}|^{2}f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{G},\bm{g}}f_{n\bm{g}}^{*}V^{*}(\bm{G}-\bm{g})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}\sum_{\bm{G}}f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{k}}f_{n\bm{G}}.

After swapping dummy indices

Moreover, by taking the

IV.2
The effective mass tensor and its relations with the momentum matrix elements.

An essential ingredient to obtain the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule is the effective mass tensor. It is well known that it can be written as second derivative of the energy bands
\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}

2
;
13
, but it is its relation with the momentum matrix elements what matters most for our goals, so we set up to obtain this expression. From Eq. (
17
) we have
11
:

\partial_{i}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}k_{i}+(2\pi)^{3}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2},

(26)

where
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial k_{i}
, and the cartesian components of
\bm{k}
and
\bm{G}
are
k_{i}
and
G_{i}
, respectively (
i=1,2,3

\sum_{\bm{G}}|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2}=1/(2\pi)^{3}

\partial^{2}_{ij}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\delta_{ij}+2(2\pi)^{3}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\text{Re}[\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}\partial_{j}f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}f_{n\bm{G}}],

(27)

Re being the real part. Using the

th component of Eq. (
20
) we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}\partial_{j}f_{n\bm{G}}^{*}f_{n\bm{G}}=i\sum_{n^{\prime}}\mathcal{M}^{*}_{j;n^{\prime}n}\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}f^{*}_{n^{\prime}\bm{G}}f_{n\bm{G}}=

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}^{*}_{j;nn}\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}|f_{n\bm{G}}|^{2}+i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}\\
n^{\prime}\neq n\end{subarray}}\mathcal{M}^{*}_{j;n^{\prime}n}\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}f^{*}_{n^{\prime}\bm{G}}f_{n\bm{G}},

(28)

where we have isolated the term

in the sum over

\bm{G}
is real and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{j;nn}=\mathcal{M}_{j;nn}

(29)

With this, Eq. (

\partial^{2}_{ij}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\delta_{ij}+(2\pi)^{3}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}[\Pi_{ij;n}+\Pi_{ij;n}^{*}].

(30)

The sum
\sum_{\bm{G}}G_{i}f^{*}_{n^{\prime}\bm{G}}f_{n\bm{G}}

can be identified to be proportional to

th component of
\bm{p}_{n^{\prime}n}
. In Eq. (
18

\Pi_{ij;n}=\frac{i}{\hbar(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}\\

(31)

By replacing
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{j;n^{\prime}n}
using Eq. (
25

\Pi_{ij;n}=\frac{1}{m(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}\\
n^{\prime}\neq n\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}}.

(32)

In getting this we have used

30

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\partial^{2}_{ij}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{m}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}

(33)

The fact that it is the second derivative of the energy bands will be crucial for proving the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rules, as we will do in the next sections. This finalizes our study of the MMEs between Bloch eigenstates. In the next section we will pass to the actual problem of proving the TRK sum rule in semiconductors.

V
The TRK sum rule for the linear optical susceptibility.

In semiconductor materials, it can be shown that after a direct calculation of the linear optical susceptibility by using time-dependent perturbation theory,
\chi_{ij}
exhibits a divergence at very low frequencies, which is in contradiction with the fact that insulating materials have a finite static (
i.e.
,
\omega\rightarrow 0
) susceptibility. Fortunately, this divergence is artificial: it can be shown that by a suitable manipulation all the divergences are removed, leaving an expression that is finite a
\omega=0
. It is less widely known, however, that this same procedure lead naturally to the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule, as we shown in the following.

V.1
Non-singular linear optical susceptibility.

An expression for the linear optical susceptibility can be obtained by using time-dependent perturbation theory in the matrix density to obtain the induced current, and from it obtain the induced polarization
10
. There exist many levels of approximation in getting the optical susceptibility: we will assume that the field acting on the material is monochromatic and spatially homogeneous (spatial variations are usually of no effect, although they can be sometimes important
12
). Also, we will work within the IPA: the mutual Coulomb interaction between electrons will be taken as
averaged

out
and included in the crystal potential
V(\bm{r})
. With these assumptions, we have the following expression
7
in Gaussian units:

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{S}=-\frac{e^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{2}}\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\\
(n\neq n^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\Big(mf_{n}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}+

\displaystyle(f_{n}-f_{n^{\prime}})\frac{p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega}\Big),

(34)

where
\omega
is the frequency of the external field,
-e
is the electron charge, and
f_{n}
is the occupancy of the
\psi_{n\bm{k}}
Bloch eigenstate:
f_{n}=1
(
f_{n}=0
) for valence (conductance) states. The dependence on
\bm{k}
has been omitted in the writing.

This equation has the disadvantage of being singular at
\omega\rightarrow 0
, which is unphysical in semiconductors and insulators (the situation is entirely different in metals
16
, and that is another reason this approach does not apply to that kind of materials). The removal of this singularity has been showed elsewhere
15
, although we will repeat it here because of its connection to the TRK sum rule. If we write
\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}=\hbar\omega_{nn^{\prime}}
, we use the partial fraction decomposition:

\frac{1}{\omega^{2}(\omega_{nm}-\omega)}=\frac{1}{\omega\omega_{nm}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\omega_{nm}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{nm}^{2}(\omega_{nm}-\omega)},

(35)

which allows to write Eq. (
V.1
) in the following fashion:

\chi_{ij}^{S}=\frac{\chi_{ij}^{(a)}}{\omega}+\frac{\chi_{ij}^{(b)}}{\omega^{2}}+\chi_{ij},

(36)

with:

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\hskip-2.84526pt\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\\
(n\neq n^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{f_{nn^{\prime}}}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}}p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n},

(37)

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\hskip-2.84526pt\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\\
(n\neq n^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054pt\Big(mf_{n}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}+

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\frac{f_{n}-f_{n^{\prime}}}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}}p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}\Big),

(38)

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\hskip-2.84526pt\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\\
(n\neq n^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{f_{nn^{\prime}}p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}}{\epsilon^{2}_{nn^{\prime}}(\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega)}.

(39)

The term
\chi_{ij}^{(a)}
, accompanying the factor
1/\omega
, vanishes because of time-reversal symmetry:
\bm{p}_{nn^{\prime}}(\bm{k})=-\bm{p}^{*}_{nn^{\prime}}(-\bm{k})
. This can be seen by separating the
\bm{k}
integral into two equal parts and doing the substitution
\bm{k}\rightarrow-\bm{k}
in the second one. The term
\chi_{ij}^{(b)}
, that would give the divergence
1/\omega^{2}
, can be shown to be zero by manipulations that result in its having the form of an effective mass tensor. By separating the term
f_{n}-f_{n^{\prime}}
in the second term and swapping the dummy indices
n
and
n^{\prime}
in the resulting equation we end up with something proportional to:

\displaystyle\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}f_{n}\Big(m\,\delta_{ij}+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n^{\prime}\\
(n^{\prime}\neq n)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}}(p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}+p_{i;nn^{\prime}}p_{j;n^{\prime}n})\Big),

(40)

which has the form of the effective mass tensor, Eq. (
IV.2
):

\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{n}\frac{f_{n}}{\hbar^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}

(41)

the integral above is zero because
\epsilon_{n\bm{k}}
is periodic over the BZ. Thus, in Eq. (
36
) only the term
\chi_{ij}
remains, given by Eq. (
39
). After using time-reversal symmetry and taking the imaginary part, this expression can be written as:

\displaystyle\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}]=\frac{e^{2}\hbar^{2}\pi}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\times

\displaystyle\sum_{v,c}\frac{\text{Re}[p_{i;vc}p_{j;cv}]}{\epsilon_{cv}^{2}}\delta(\epsilon_{cv}-\epsilon),

(42)

where the sum is now restricted to valence (
v
) and conduction (
c
) states. In getting Eq. (
V.1
) we have used:

\frac{1}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega-i\eta}=\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega}+i\pi\delta(\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega),

(43)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the Cauchy principal part and
\hbar\omega
in the rhs is taking as real and positive.
Since these states are separated by an energy gap
\delta\epsilon
(in most semiconductor with practical applications, this gap is the order of
\delta\epsilon=1\,
eV), the difference
\epsilon_{cv}
is always larger than
\delta\epsilon
and frequencies with
\hbar\omega<\delta\epsilon
gives
\chi_{ij}=0
without the need of evaluating the integral.

Before continuing, it must be noted the following. Equation (
V.1
) is an approximation where we neglect the resonances with energy differences
\epsilon_{vc}
, which are of course negative and would give a zero contribution if the photon energy
\hbar\omega
were positive. It would be sone because of the appereance of the Dirac delta
\delta(\epsilon_{cv}+\hbar\omega)
, instead of the one in Eq. (
V.1
). Mathematically speaking, this Dirac delta is zero, but the actual calculation of
\chi_{ij}
often uses the so-called Gaussian broadening, where
\delta(\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}-\hbar\omega)
is replaced by a very sharp Gaussian function. Even in this approach, the contributions from
\delta(\epsilon_{cv}+\hbar\omega)
would be very small, so that the approximation in Eq. (
V.1
) seems appropriate.

V.2
The sum rule.

To proof the TRK sum rule we start from Eq. (
V.1
) and perform the integration
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}]
. The only
\omega
dependence is the Dirac delta, so that
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\delta(\epsilon_{cv}-\hbar\omega)=\epsilon_{cv}/\hbar^{2}
, obtaining:

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}]=\frac{e^{2}\pi}{m^{2}}\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{v,c}\frac{\text{Re}[p_{i;vc}p_{j;cv}]}{\epsilon_{cv}},

(44)

but we already know that Eq. (
V.1
) is zero:

\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,n^{\prime}\\
(n\neq n^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{f_{n}}{m}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}+\frac{f_{nn^{\prime}}}{m^{2}\epsilon_{nn^{\prime}}}p_{j;nn^{\prime}}p_{i;n^{\prime}n}\Big)=0,

(45)

with
f_{nn^{\prime}}=f_{n}-f_{n^{\prime}}
. The double sum in the second term can be decomposed into two sums over valence and conduction states only, because the
f_{nn^{\prime}}
factors are zero for state pairs
(n,n^{\prime})
of the same kind (valence or conduction):

\displaystyle\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{n}\frac{f_{n}}{m}\delta_{ij}+\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,

\displaystyle\sum_{v,c}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{vc}}(p_{j;vc}p_{i;cv}+

\displaystyle p_{j;vc}p_{j;cv})=0.

(46)

Using again time-reversal symmetry, the two terms within the second sum can be shown to be one the complex conjugate of the other, thus:

\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{n}mf_{n}\delta_{ij}+2\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{v,c}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{vc}}\text{Re}[p_{j;vc}p_{i;cv}]=0,

(47)

but
\sum_{n}f_{n}=N_{e}
is the number of valence electrons and the integral
\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,
is over reciprocal space, thus equal to
(2\pi)^{3}/\Omega
. We then get:

\int\,\hskip-5.69054pt\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\sum_{v,c}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{vc}}\text{Re}[p_{j;vc}p_{i;cv}]=\frac{n_{e}}{2m}\delta_{ij},

(48)

where
n_{e}=N_{e}/\Omega
. Thus replacing in Eq. (
44
) we finally get:

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}^{(1)}]=n_{e}e^{2}4\pi\delta_{ij}/m,

(49)

which is the well known TRK sum rule for semiconductors. As a particular case, when we work with cubic materials, it holds that
\chi_{ii}=\chi
and
\chi_{ij}=0
when
i\neq j
, and the expression above simply becomes
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi]=n_{e}e^{2}4\pi/m
.

From Eq. (
49
) it can be noted that
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega\,\text{Im}[\chi_{ij}^{(1)}]=0
for
i\neq j
. However, this follows more straightforwardly from the symmetry of the material rather than the TRK sum rule. For instance, in many crystal classes
1
we have
\chi_{ij}=0
when
i\neq j

5
;
14
, making this case in Eq. (
49
) sort of trivial. Also, the number of
n_{e}
in Eq. (
49
) is the number of
valence
electrons per unit cell volume, which is the kind of weakly bound electron that enters in the high-frequency limit of
\chi_{ij}
, as done in the literature
13
;
8
;
9
.

VI
Conclusions

Taking advantage of the periodicity of the
u_{n\bm{k}}
part of the Bloch eigenstates, we have obtained momentum matrix elements and the effective mass tensor that fully coincide with those obtained using the
\bm{k}-
representation. These expressions then were used to eliminate the singularity at
\omega=0
of the linear susceptibility, as commonly done in the literature. Remarkably, the same condition that makes the optical susceptibility non-singular at
\omega=0
leads to the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn sum rule.
