FGFR4-high expression is associated with an immune-responsive phenotype in HCC and predicts inferior efficacy of lenvatinib plus PD-1 blockade
Authors: Fuqun Wei, Weifu Liu, Zhisheng Chen, Zhongwu Chen, Jingfeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe LEAP-002 study showed that pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib did not provide statistically significant prognostic improvement over lenvatinib monotherapy in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study investigated the potential protective role of high fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4) expression, a therapeutic target of lenvatinib, in immunotherapy for HCC.MethodsThe Kaplan Meier plotter database was used to assess the impact of FGFR4 expression on immunotherapy outcomes; the TCGA-LIHC dataset was analyzed for correlations between FGFR4 expression, immune checkpoint gene profiles, and immune cell infiltration; and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from GSE149614 were used to examine interactions between high- and low-FGFR4 cancer cell subpopulations and immune cells. Validation was performed in tumor specimens from HCC patients treated with lenvatinib in combination with PD-1 inhibitors.ResultsHigh FGFR4 expression predicted favorable immunotherapy outcomes and correlated with immune checkpoint genes such as PCDH1 and CD274, along with reduced M2 macrophage infiltration in TCGA-LIHC. scRNA-seq analysis showed FGFR4 enrichment in cancer cells, with high-FGFR4 cancer cells displaying a unique CXCL1–CXCR2 signaling axis with macrophages. High FGFR4 expression activated the MAPK pathway and increased JUN(+) transcriptional activity, showing a significant positive correlation with CXCL1 levels. In HCC patients treated with lenvatinib plus PD-1 inhibitors, high FGFR4 expression was linked to enhanced M1 macrophage polarization and elevated PD-L1 expression, whereas low FGFR4 expression correlated with better clinical outcomes.ConclusionFGFR4-high expression is associated with an immune-responsive phenotype in HCC and predicts inferior efficacy of lenvatinib plus PD-1 blockade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16098-9.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown significant therapeutic efficacy in advanced HCC, particularly when combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), which can markedly enhance treatment outcomes [1]. However, the effectiveness of different TKIs in combination with ICIs varies considerably. The negative results of the randomized, double-blind phase III LEAP-002 trial indicate that not all combinations of TKIs and ICIs enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs [2], whereas positive results have been observed with the combination of other TKIs, such as rivoceranib and bevacizumab [3, 4]. Lenvatinib exhibits unique multi-target inhibition of FGFR, this distinct characteristic of lenvatinib, compared to other TKIs, raises the question of whether FGFR inhibition may potentially undermine the efficacy of immunotherapy, which warrants further investigation.

The expression level of PD-L1 is closely associated with the sensitivity of tumors to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [5]. Several studies have shown that high FGFR4 expression is strongly correlated with elevated PD-L1 levels in HCC [6, 7]. However, paradoxically, other studies suggest that although FGFR4 inhibitors may increase PD-L1 expression in HCC, they can still enhance the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, potentially by reducing Treg infiltration and other mechanisms [8, 9]. The exact mechanism by which FGFR4 functions in HCC immunotherapy remains to be fully elucidated.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in tumor immune evasion and treatment response. In immune therapy, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition, the polarization status of macrophages is considered a key factor determining treatment efficacy. Research has shown that a higher proportion of M1 macrophages in the TME can enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, while the accumulation of M2 macrophages may hinder immunotherapy effectiveness [10, 11]. Therefore, regulating the polarization state of macrophages—particularly promoting the accumulation of M1 macrophages or inhibiting the formation of M2 macrophages—may be a potential strategy to improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. Recent studies indicate that FGF/FGFR signaling pathways have bidirectional regulatory effects on macrophages [12]. However, the relationship between FGFR4 expression and macrophage polarization in HCC has not been fully explored, and whether FGFR4 may influence immunotherapy efficacy by modulating macrophage polarization remains to be further investigated.

Although previous studies have shown that high FGFR4 expression is associated with poor prognosis in HCC [13], its role in HCC immunotherapy remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the potential impact of FGFR4 expression level on PD-L1 expression and macrophage polarization in HCC, and to further elucidate the relationship between FGFR4 expression level and the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in HCC treatment. This finding may offer new insights into TKI-based combination immunotherapy for HCC and provide both theoretical and clinical value for the development of HCC immunotherapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data download

The expression and clinical data of 33 tumor types were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), and the TCGA_GTEx dataset, which integrates TCGA tumor samples with normal tissues, was downloaded from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The HCC immunotherapy dataset GSE181946 was retrieved using the R package GEOquery [14] and includes 5 responders and 12 non-responders to anti-PD-1 treatment.

Gene expression analysis and prognosis analysis

FGFR4 mRNA expression was analyzed between normal and tumor tissues using TCGA_GTEx samples, TCGA samples, and paired TCGA samples. The association between FGFR4 expression and pathological stage in TCGA-LIHC was assessed using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2.0) [15]. Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis with the log-rank test evaluated correlations between FGFR4 expression and clinical outcomes, including OS and PFS, in TCGA-LIHC. To evaluate the predictive value of FGFR4 for immunotherapy response, FGFR4 expression distributions between responders and non-responders in the GSE181946 dataset were visualized using scatter and histogram proportion plots. In all of the above databases analyses, high and low FGFR4 expression groups were defined using the median expression value as the cutoff. The Kaplan Meier plotter [16] was used to assess OS and PFS in immunotherapy patients, with patients stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on optimal cut-off values. Hazard ratios were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model, and significance was determined by the log-rank test.

Immune infiltration analysis

TCGA-LIHC patients were stratified into high- and low-FGFR4 expression groups using the median expression as a cutoff. The CIBERSORT algorithm quantified immune cell infiltration, and differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Correlations between FGFR4 and 79 immune checkpoint genes [17], including PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, and LAG3, were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation. No multiple testing correction (FDR) was applied.

scRNA-seq and data processing

FGFR4 expression in single-cell RNA-seq datasets (GSE66635, GSE149614) was analyzed using scCancerExplorer (https://bianlab.cn/scCancerExplorer) [18]. Cell-cell communication and transcription factor activity were assessed with CellChat (v1.5.0) [19] and SCENIC (v1.1.2) [20], respectively. High- and low-FGFR4 groups were defined by the median expression. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon test, log2 fold change threshold), and functional enrichment (GO, KEGG, GSEA) was performed with clusterProfiler (hypergeometric test for GO/KEGG; Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for GSEA). FDR correction was not applied.

Enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high- and low-FGFR4 TCGA-LIHC tumor samples were identified using limma R package (v3.56.0) [21] with P < 0.05 and log₂FC > 1. The GSE149614 dataset was processed with Seurat (v5.3.0) [22] and Harmony R package [23] (v1.2.0); HCC cell subpopulations were isolated, dichotomized by median FGFR4 expression, and DEGs were identified via FindAllMarkers. Functional enrichment, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [24], and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), was performed using clusterProfiler R package [25] (v4.8.1).

Tissue sample collection

A total of 23 newly diagnosed patients with advanced HCC were enrolled, and pre-treatment tumor biopsy specimens were collected at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. All patients received lenvatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors, including 15 treated with tislelizumab and 8 with camrelizumab. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (MTCA, ECFAH of FMU [2015]084 − 3), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Scientific, #15596026) and treated with DNase I (NEB, #M0303L). RNA quality and concentration were assessed using NanoDrop OneC, LabChip GX Touch, and Qubit 3.0 with the RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, #Q10210). Libraries were prepared with the KCTM mRNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth, Wuhan, China) for 200–500 bp fragments and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI) in PE150 mode.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded sections from a total of 12 HCC patients (6 responders and 6 non-responders to immunotherapy) were used. Sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) using microwave heating. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂, followed by 3% BSA blocking. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FGFR4 (1:500, PTGLAB, 11098-1-AP), washed, and incubated with a secondary antibody. DAB was used for color development, and hematoxylin counterstaining was performed. Sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Positive staining appeared brownish-yellow under bright-field microscopy, while nuclei were blue. Scoring was performed independently and in a blinded manner by two pathologists with over ten years of experience. Differences between responder and non-responder groups were assessed using a two-tailed t-test.

Immunofluorescence(IF) staining and analysis

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked, and sections were treated with 5% BSA, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by 30 min secondary antibody incubation at 37 °C. TSA-488 and TSA-Cy3 were used for signal amplification, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope, and analysis was done with ImageJ. Antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.3) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1). Group comparisons used Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal data. Spearman correlation assessed molecular associations. Statistical significance was determined with a threshold of P < 0.05. The following indicators were employed to denote levels of significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results

Pan-cancer FGFR4 expression and its prognostic and immunotherapeutic significance in HCC

To characterize FGFR4 expression across cancers, we analyzed the integrated TCGA_GTEx dataset from the UCSC database. This analysis revealed differential FGFR4 expression in various tumor types, with upregulation in some cancers and downregulation in others (Fig. 1A). We further examined FGFR4 expression in 23 types of paired tumor and normal tissues from the TCGA cohort (Fig. 1B). These analyses consistently demonstrated significant FGFR4 upregulation in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC). Investigation of the relationship between FGFR4 expression and HCC clinicopathological characteristics revealed markedly higher FGFR4 expression in patients with elevated serum AFP levels and higher histologic grades (Fig. 1C–D). KM survival analysis showed that high FGFR4 expression significantly correlated with shorter OS and PFS in LIHC (Fig. 1E–F).

To assess the correlation between FGFR4 expression and immunotherapy response, we employed the KM plotter database to analyze the prognostic impact of FGFR4 expression in patients receiving PD-1, PD-L1, or CTLA-4 inhibitors. High FGFR4 expression was associated with prolonged OS in PD-1 inhibitor–treated patients and extended PFS in those receiving PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, whereas no significant association emerged for CTLA-4 inhibitor–treated patients (Fig. 1G–L). A similar pattern was observed in the HCC immunotherapy cohort, where responders exhibited significantly higher FGFR4 expression levels (Fig. 1M), and patients with high FGFR4 expression showed a substantially increased immunotherapy response rate (Fig. 1N).

FGFR4-associated immune landscape in HCC

The expression of immune checkpoint genes serves as a key biomarker for predicting immunotherapy outcomes. To explore potential links between FGFR4 and 79 established immune checkpoint genes, we compared their expression profiles in LIHC samples stratified by high and low FGFR4 expression. A heatmap revealed broad differences in immune checkpoint gene expression between these groups (Fig. 2A), while a correlation heatmap illustrated overall association patterns between FGFR4 and these genes (Fig. 2B). Scatterplots further delineated correlations between FGFR4 and major immunotherapy targets, including PCDH1, CD274, CTLA4, and LAG3 (Figs. 2C–F). FGFR4 showed a significant positive correlation with PCDH1 (R = 0.196, P < 0.001) and a significant negative correlation with CD274 (PD-L1) (R = − 0.199, P < 0.001). We then used the CIBERSORT algorithm to assess immune cell infiltration differences between the FGFR4 expression groups (Fig. 2G). The high-FGFR4 group displayed a significant increase in M0 macrophages and a pronounced decrease in M2 macrophages, implying that FGFR4 may influence immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment by altering macrophage polarization.

Single-cell localization of FGFR4 in HCC

The cellular localization of FGFR4 was determined using the scCancerExplorer database across two HCC scRNA-seq datasets, GSE66635 and GSE149614. UMAP plots revealed the distribution of distinct cell subpopulations (Fig. 3A and D), with FGFR4 predominantly expressed in the cancer cell clusters (Fig. 3B and E). Violin plots further confirmed that FGFR4 expression levels were significantly higher in cancer cells than in other cell types (Fig. 3C and F). These results collectively demonstrate that FGFR4 is primarily localized and highly expressed in cancer cells in HCC patients, implying a potential role in tumor cell–specific signaling regulation.

FGFR4high/low HCC subpopulations communicate with macrophages

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE149614 identified 16 cell subpopulations (Fig. 4A), with cancer cells divided into FGFR4high_Hepatocyte and FGFR4low_Hepatocyte. Global intercellular communication analysis revealed extensive interactions among all 16 subpopulations with varying communication intensities (Fig. 4B). Although both FGFR4high_Hepatocyte and FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulations displayed active interactions with other cell types, there was no significant difference in the number or overall strength of interactions with macrophages (Fig. 4C). Given CXCL signaling’s role in macrophage polarization, we examined CXCL-mediated interactions and found a unique CXCL1–CXCR2 axis between FGFR4high_Hepatocyte subpopulation and macrophages, absent in FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulation (Figs. 4D–E). CXCL1 expression was also higher in FGFR4-high subpopulation (Fig. 4F), suggesting FGFR4-high HCC subpopulation may modulate macrophage phenotypes via CXCL1–CXCR2, reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

Functional enrichment analyses of FGFR4high/low HCC groups

At the single-cell level, GO/KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between FGFR4high_Hepatocyte and FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulations showed that FGFR4high_Hepatocyte cells were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including leukocyte- and lymphocyte-mediated immunity, immune response activation, positive regulation of cell activation, and humoral immune response (Fig. 5A). In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, DEGs from the FGFR4high group were predominantly enriched in steroid and bile acid metabolism pathways, such as steroid metabolic processes, bile acid biosynthesis, cholesterol homeostasis, and lipid homeostasis (Fig. 5C). GSEA further demonstrated consistent enrichment of MAPK-related signaling pathways across both datasets, including P38 MAPK events (Fig. 5B) and MAPK family signaling cascades (Fig. 5D). Overall, these results suggest that FGFR4high HCC cells exhibit both elevated metabolic activity and activated immune-related signaling, particularly via the MAPK pathway.

Transcriptional regulatory landscape of FGFR4high/low HCC cells

Transcription factor activity in FGFR4high_Hepatocyte and FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulations was analyzed using the SCENIC R package (Figs. 6A–B). The FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulation exhibited higher activity of transcription factors such as NR1H4(+), PDX1(+), KLF10(+), and NFE2L1(+), whereas XBP1(+), CEBPG(+), CEBPB(+), and ZNF189(+) were more active in FGFR4high_Hepatocyte subpopulation. NFIC(+) showed the highest activity in the FGFR4low_Hepatocyte subpopulation, while JUN(+) was the most active in FGFR4high_Hepatocyte subpopulation (Fig. 6C). In TCGA-LIHC samples, JUN(+) activity correlated positively with CXCL1 expression (Fig. 6D), suggesting that elevated JUN(+) activity in FGFR4high_Hepatocyte subpopulation may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment via the CXCL1 signaling pathway.

FGFR4 expression predicts immunotherapy response in HCC patients

Lenvatinib is known to inhibit the kinase activity of FGFR1–4. Based on our previous findings, we hypothesized that FGFR4 inhibition might reduce the efficacy of immunotherapy. To test this, pre-treatment tumor biopsy specimens were collected from HCC patients receiving lenvatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors. IHC analysis showed that FGFR4 expression was significantly decreased in responders, as indicated by reduced positive staining areas and lower H-scores (Fig. 7A–B and D–E). Consistently, RNA sequencing revealed markedly lower FGFR4 transcript levels in responders (Fig. 7C). These results suggest FGFR4-high expression status predicts a favorable immune-responsive phenotype on HCC tumors, thereby enhancing their sensitivity to immunotherapy.

Considering that PD-L1 expression and macrophage M1 polarization are key determinants of immunotherapy response, further IF analyses were performed. FGFR4 expression strongly correlated with PD-L1 levels (r = 0.769, P = 0.0001; Figs. 7F–G). High FGFR4 expression promoted M1 macrophage polarization (Fig. 7H) and was positively associated with the M1 marker iNOS (r = 0.832, P < 0.0001; Fig. 7I) and negatively associated with the M2 marker CD206 (r = − 0.708, P = 0.001; Fig. 7J). These findings indicate that FGFR4 expression may influence the immune landscape of HCC. Consistent with our bioinformatic analyses, higher FGFR4 expression in tumor cells was associated with elevated tumor proportion score (TPS) and may promote M1 macrophage polarization, enhancing antitumor immune activity.

Discussion

FGFR4 is highly expressed in various solid tumors, including HCC, and its overexpression is closely associated with tumor proliferation, migration, and poor prognosis [26]. Pan-cancer analysis indicates differential expression of FGFR4 across various tumor types, with a significant upregulation in HCC. Notably, high FGFR4 expression in HCC is strongly correlated with elevated serum AFP levels and higher histological grades. KM survival analysis further supports the association between high FGFR4 expression and shorter OS and PFS. These results suggest that FGFR4 could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for HCC. Previous studies have shown that the FGFR4 signaling pathway promotes HCC progression through the upregulation of amphiregulin, TGF-β, and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), as well as by stimulating epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [26, 27]. However, the relationship between FGFR4 overexpression and the effectiveness of immune therapies remains to be explored further. Consistent with the immunofluorescence analysis of clinical samples from patients with advanced HCC in the present study, previous studies have demonstrated that FGFR4 can regulate PD-L1 expression by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby mediating IGF2BP1-induced PD-L1 overexpression [7]. High PD-L1 expression is a well-established predictor of response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [27]. Several large clinical trials have demonstrated that elevated PD-L1 expression is a reliable predictor of response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in various cancer types, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and urothelial carcinoma [28–30]. However, other studies have failed to validate PD-L1 expression as a predictor of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [31–33], indicating that PD-L1 remains an imperfect biomarker and cannot predict clinical benefit in all patients. Although prior studies have shown that the FGF19/FGFR4 signaling pathway promotes HCC survival and immune evasion by regulating IGF2BP1 [7], and that inhibition of FGFR4 may enhance antitumor immune responses, evidence linking FGFR4 expression levels to immunotherapy outcomes remains limited. In this study, we therefore analyzed the association between FGFR4 expression and response to immunotherapy using the KM Plotter database. We found that high FGFR4 expression was associated with a favorable prognosis in patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Further validation in HCC patient data showed that immune-responsive patients had significantly higher levels of FGFR4 expression compared to non-responders. Immuno-checkpoint gene analysis revealed a significant correlation between FGFR4 expression and key immune molecules, such as PCDH1 and CD274, suggesting that FGFR4 might influence immune therapy effectiveness by modulating immune checkpoint expression. To address this, we performed IHC analysis of tumor specimens from HCC patients treated with lenvatinib in combination with a PD-1 inhibitors. The results showed that patients with high FGFR4 expression exhibited poorer responses to combination therapy, whereas those with low FGFR4 expression demonstrated more favorable therapeutic outcomes. These findings suggest that in FGFR4-high tumors, lenvatinib-mediated inhibition of FGFR4 may diminish sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, thereby reducing overall immunotherapeutic benefit. Conversely, in FGFR4-low tumors, weaker inhibition of FGFR4-related signaling may preserve antitumor immune activity, resulting in improved treatment responses. The above findings suggest that high FGFR4 expression may be associated with a favorable response to immunotherapy in HCC.

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying the association between high FGFR4 expression and favorable immunotherapy response in HCC, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to analyze immune cell infiltration in HCC tissues and observed that high FGFR4 expression was associated with a reduction in M2 macrophage infiltration. M2 macrophages are characterized by anti-inflammatory properties and the ability to suppress immune responses, promoting tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion [28]. The activation of macrophages is a “signal-dependent” process, where various cytokines or colony-stimulating factors can drive macrophages to polarize into different functional phenotypes [29, 30]. Single-cell analysis revealed that FGFR4-high HCC cells engage macrophages through the CXCL1–CXCR2 signaling axis, a pathway known to activate the NF-κB cascade in macrophages, thereby promoting M1 polarization and enhancing antitumor immune responses [31]. Further investigation indicated that activation of this CXCL1–CXCR2 axis may be driven by the MAPK–JUN(+) signaling pathway within FGFR4-high HCC cells. IF analysis of tumor specimens from HCC patients corroborated these findings, demonstrating a positive correlation between high FGFR4 expression and the M1 macrophage marker iNOS, along with a negative correlation with the M2 marker CD206. Although the inclusion of single-cell RNA sequencing enhances analytical resolution, FGFR4 expression is primarily observed in tumor cell subpopulations, and its immune-related effects are still inferred indirectly.

While this study offers initial evidence for the involvement of FGFR4 in shaping the immune response to therapy in HCC, it has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size—particularly in the immunotherapy cohort—may limit statistical power and generalizability. Second, the analyses are based on single-center data without validation in independent, multi-center cohorts. Third, although single-cell RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry analyses were performed, the precise mechanisms by which FGFR4 influences the immune microenvironment—particularly immune evasion and tolerance—are currently inferred from computational analyses rather than direct experimental validation and thus require further investigation. Finally, the observed association between FGFR4 expression and PD-1 inhibitor efficacy requires functional validation through loss- and gain-of-function experiments. Additionally, we were unable to adjust for potential confounding factors that may influence HCC prognosis, and thus could not determine whether the predictive value of FGFR4 expression is independent of established prognostic factors. Future studies using larger cohorts, animal models, and clinical trials are warranted to confirm these findings and to further evaluate FGFR4 as a therapeutic target.
