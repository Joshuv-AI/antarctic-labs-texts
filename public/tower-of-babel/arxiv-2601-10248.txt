Restoring similarity in randomized Krylov methods with applications to eigenvalue problems and matrix functions
Laura Grigori, Daniel Kressner, Nian Shao, Igor Simunec

Abstract
The randomized Arnoldi process has been used in large-scale scientific computing because it produces a well-conditioned basis for the Krylov subspace more quickly than the standard Arnoldi process. However, the resulting Hessenberg matrix is generally not similar to the one produced by the standard Arnoldi process, which can lead to delays or spike-like irregularities in convergence. In this paper, we introduce a modification of the randomized Arnoldi process that restores similarity with the Hessenberg matrix generated by the standard Arnoldi process. This is accomplished by enforcing orthogonality between the last Arnoldi vector and the previously generated subspace, which requires solving only one additional least-squares problem. When applied to eigenvalue problems and matrix function evaluations, the modified randomized Arnoldi process produces approximations that are identical to those obtained with the standard Arnoldi process. Numerical experiments demonstrate that our approach is as fast as the randomized Arnoldi process and as robust as the standard Arnoldi process.

Introduction

Krylov subspace methods are among the most effective approaches for solving large-scale problems, ranging from the solution of linear systems, to the computation of eigenvalues and evaluation of matrix functions.
Given a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and a nonzero initial vector
b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the
m
th Krylov subspace associated with
A
and
b
is generated by repeatedly multiplying
A
with
b
:

\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b):=\mathsf{span}\{b,Ab,\dotsc,A^{m-1}b\}.

The Arnoldi process
[
12
, Sec. 10.5]
is used to construct an orthonormal basis
Q_{m}
for
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b)
, whose main costs are
m
matrix-vector products with
A
, and the orthogonalization of
Q_{m}
via a Gram–Schmidt process with complexity
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2})
.
In particular, orthogonalization may dominate the computational cost when
m
becomes (moderately) large. Several strategies have been considered in the literature to reduce this cost, including incomplete orthogonalization
[
26
]
and restarting
[
27
]
.

Recently, randomized orthogonalization has been proposed to reduce the cost of orthogonalization in the Arnoldi process, for instance with a randomized Gram–Schmidt method
[
1
,
2
]
or with a randomized Householder QR decomposition
[
14
]
.
Given a (low-dimensional) subspace
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the randomized Gram–Schmidt method employs a subspace embedding
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
to sketch vectors in
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
d\ll n
, while approximately preserving their norms.
Compared to standard Gram–Schmidt, the computation and communication costs are reduced because inner products are formed with sketched vectors of length
d
rather than with original length-
n
vectors.
Instead of an orthonormal basis
Q_{m}
, randomized Gram–Schmidt generates an
\Omega
-orthonormal basis
U_{m}
, that is, the columns of
\Omega U_{m}
form an orthonormal basis.
When using high-quality random subspace embeddings, such as the ones surveyed in
[
21
, Sec. 2]
, this ensures — with high probability — that
U_{m}
is a well-conditioned basis.
During the last few years, the resulting randomized Arnoldi process has been employed for the solution of various large-scale
problems
[
30
,
23
,
15
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
6
]
. For a recent overview, we refer to
[
7
]
.

While its performance gains make randomized Arnoldi an attractive choice, the transition from orthonormality to
\Omega
-orthonormality incurs certain nuisances. An important feature of standard Arnoldi is that it not only returns an orthonormal basis
Q_{m}
but also the Hessenberg matrix
G_{m}=Q_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}AQ_{m}
. The latter plays a crucial role in algorithms; for example, the eigenvalues of
G_{m}
are the so called Ritz values that approximate eigenvalues of
A
. Randomized Arnoldi also returns such an
m\times m
Hessenberg matrix, but this matrix is, in general,
not
similar to
G_{m}
and, in particular, its eigenvalues are different.
This discrepancy can lead to both numerical and theoretical difficulties. For example, one may obtain complex eigenvalue approximations even when
A
is Hermitian
[
9
]
. More subtle issues arise in the context of linear systems and matrix functions, where convergence may be delayed
[
15
]
or exhibit sporadic spikes
[
30
]
.
Moreover, from a theoretical viewpoint, establishing convergence results becomes highly challenging
[
23
]
because the numerical range of the Hessenberg matrix is difficult to control, as it is no longer contained in the numerical range of
A
.
All of these issues are caused by the lack of similarity between the Hessenberg matrices and can be resolved by restoring this property.

In this work, we propose a modification of randomized Arnoldi that constructs a Hessenberg matrix similar to the one generated by standard Arnoldi. The key idea is to make all basis vectors
\Omega
-orthonormal
except
for the last one, which is instead orthogonal to all previous vectors. Such a basis can be obtained by performing randomized Gram–Schmidt, followed by the solution of a least-squares problem that enforces orthogonality of the last vector. This general framework enables us to recover, in exact arithmetic, the same approximate solutions as the ones obtained through standard Arnoldi, while still exploiting much of the efficiency of randomized Gram–Schmidt. We demonstrate this approach by applying it to the randomized Krylov–Schur algorithm for eigenvalue problems and the randomized Arnoldi method for computing the action of matrix functions
f(A)b
.
For eigenvalue problems, in addition to establishing its equivalence with standard Krylov–Schur in exact arithmetic, we also prove a backward stability result analogous to that of the standard Krylov–Schur method in
[
28
]
.
Let us remark that the application to matrix functions is closely related to one of the approaches considered in
[
6
]
; see Remark
5
below for more details.

The remainder of the manuscript is organized as follows. In
section

2
we review the standard and randomized Arnoldi process and introduce our similarity-restoring correction to randomized Arnoldi. In
section

3
we apply the proposed framework to the randomized Krylov–Schur algorithm for eigenvalue problems, and in
section

4
we employ it for the computation of matrix functions. Numerical comparisons with both standard and randomized algorithms are presented in
section

5
.

2
A new variant of the randomized Arnoldi process

2.1
Arnoldi and randomized Arnoldi process

Arnoldi process

We start by briefly recalling the standard Arnoldi process, which first normalizes the initial vector by setting
q_{1}=b/\|b\|
and then computes, for
k=1,2,\ldots
, the basis vector
q_{k+1}
by performing a matrix-vector product with
A
and one step of the (classical) Gram–Schmidt process:

w=Aq_{k},\quad g_{k}=Q_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}w,\quad\widetilde{q}_{k+1}=w-Q_{k}g_{k},\quad g_{k+1,k}=\lVert\widetilde{q}_{k+1}\rVert,\quad q_{k+1}=\widetilde{q}_{k+1}/g_{k+1,k}.

Here and in the following, we will ignore the rare (and usually fortunate) event that the Arnoldi process breaks down because of
\widetilde{q}_{k+1}=0
.
Performing
m
steps, collecting the vectors
q_{k}
in an orthonormal matrix
Q_{m}
and the coefficients
g_{k},g_{k+1,k}
in an upper Hessenberg matrix
G_{m}
, we obtain the Arnoldi decomposition

AQ_{m}=Q_{m}G_{m}+g_{m+1,m}q_{m+1}e_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},

(2.1)

where
e_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
denotes the
m
th unit vector.
When
A
is Hermitian, the Arnoldi process reduces to the Lanczos process, and the Hessenberg matrix
G_{m}
becomes a tridiagonal Hermitian matrix.

Randomized Arnoldi process

As mentioned in the introduction, orthogonalization can dominate the computational cost in the Arnoldi process. To accelerate this part, one can replace the Gram–Schmidt process by the randomized Gram–Schmidt process
[
1
]
.
To describe this process, we need the following definition.

Definition 1
.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, a matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
is called an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for a subspace
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
if

(1-\varepsilon)\lVert w\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\Omega w\rVert^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\lVert w\rVert^{2},\quad\forall w\in\mathcal{W}.

The matrix
\Omega
from
Definition

1
is usually called sketching matrix, and it has the benefit of reducing the length of a vector from
n
to
d
, while approximately preserving its norm. Sketching makes sense when the dimension
m
of the subspace
\mathcal{W}
is much smaller than
n
.
In the context of randomized Arnoldi,
\mathcal{W}
refers to the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{m+1}(A,b)
, which is actually of dimension
m+1
.

Often, the subspace
\mathcal{W}
is unknown, or it may change during the execution of the algorithm.
In these situations, it is convenient to use an
oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding
, that is, a sketching matrix
\Omega
that is (necessarily) random and represents a
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for an
arbitrary
subspace of dimension
m
with probability
1-\delta
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
. Several different ways to construct such oblivious subspace embeddings have been proposed in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
for an overview. A Gaussian random sketching matrix
\Omega
comes with strong theoretical guarantees: A sketching dimension
d=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}(m+\log(1/\delta)))
suffices to obtain an oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding
[
32
, Thm. 2.3]
.
However, in many scenarios, it is preferable to use sketching matrices that admit fast matrix-vector products. In this work, we construct
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
as a sparse sign matrix (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Sec. 9.2]
): Each column of
\Omega
is an i.i.d. random vector, with
\xi
random sign entries located at random positions. It was shown in
[
5
, Cor. 2.2]
that
\Omega
is an oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding provided that
d=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}(m+\log(m/\delta)))
and
\xi=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(m/\delta)^{5/2}+\log(m/\delta)^{4})
.
In practice, simply setting
\xi=8
has been observed to yield good results
[
31
]
, which will be used in our experiments as well.

Given an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times n}
for
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
d\ll n
, we say that two vectors
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
in
\mathcal{W}
are
\Omega
-orthogonal if
\Omega w_{1}
and
\Omega w_{2}
are orthogonal in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Given a basis
W
(not necessarily
\Omega
-orthonormal), a new vector
w
is
\Omega
-orthogonalized with respect to
W
by carrying out one step of randomized Gram–Schmidt:

w-W(\Omega W)^{\dagger}\Omega w,

(2.2)

where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of a matrix.

Replacing the Gram–Schmidt process in Arnoldi with its randomized variant (
2.2
) leads to the randomized Arnoldi process, which first
\Omega
-normalizes the initial vector
by setting
u_{1}=b/\|\Omega b\|
and then computes, for
k=1,2,\ldots
, the basis vector
u_{k+1}
as follows:

w=Au_{k},\quad h_{k}=(\Omega U_{k})^{\dagger}(\Omega w),\quad\widetilde{u}_{k+1}=w-U_{k}h_{k},\quad h_{k+1,k}=\lVert\Omega\widetilde{u}_{k+1}\rVert,\quad u_{k+1}=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{k+1}}{h_{k+1,k}}.

Performing
m
steps of this process generates a so-called
randomized Arnoldi decomposition

[
1
]
:

AU_{m}=U_{m}H_{m}+h_{m+1,m}u_{m+1}e_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},

(2.3)

where
[U_{m},u_{m+1}]
contains
\Omega
-orthonormal columns.
The
m\times m
matrix
H_{m}
is upper Hessenberg and collects the quantities

h_{k}
and
h_{k+1,k}
, just as in the standard Arnoldi process.
It is worth noting that, in contrast to standard Arnoldi, the matrix

H_{m}
is, in general, neither Hermitian nor tridiagonal when
A
is Hermitian.

2.2
Restoring similarity

Suppose that we start the Arnoldi process and the randomized Arnoldi process with the same (nonzero) vector
b
, that is,
q_{1}
in
eq.

2.1
and
u_{1}
in
eq.

2.3
are collinear.
As already discussed in the introduction, the corresponding Hessenberg matrices
G_{m}
and
H_{m}
are, in general, not similar, which incurs theoretical and practical issues. In the following, we propose a strategy to modify the randomized Arnoldi process and restore the similarity of the Hessenberg matrix.

Given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, consider the following
Krylov decomposition
:

AU_{m}=U_{m}\widehat{H}_{m}+\widehat{u}_{m+1}c_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{u}_{m+1}=0,

(2.4)

where
U_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
is assumed to be a full-rank matrix (
not
necessarily orthonormal or
\Omega
-orthonormal) and
c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a general vector (not necessarily a scalar multiple of
e_{m}
).
Obviously, the Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.1
takes the form
eq.

2.4
. While
the randomized Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.3
takes the form of the more general Krylov decomposition introduced by Stewart
[
28
]
, it does not take the form (
2.4
) because the orthogonality condition
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{u}_{m+1}=0
is not satisfied.

A Krylov decomposition of the form (
2.4
) implies a similarity relation between
\widehat{H}_{m}
and the Hessenberg matrix generated by the standard Arnoldi process.

Theorem 2
.

Given the Krylov decomposition
eq.

2.4
, let
\widehat{Q}_{m}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{range}(U_{m})
. Then
\widehat{H}_{m}
and
\widehat{G}_{m}:=\widehat{Q}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}A\widehat{Q}_{m}
are similar. Moreover, the following Krylov decomposition holds:

A\widehat{Q}_{m}=\widehat{Q}_{m}\widehat{G}_{m}+\widehat{u}_{m+1}\widehat{c}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{c}_{m}=(\widehat{Q}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m})^{-\mathsf{H}}c_{m},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{Q}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{u}_{m+1}=0.

(2.5)

The relation
eq.

2.5
plays an important role in our further analysis. In the following, we refer to
eq.

2.5
as
orthonormal Krylov decomposition associated with
eq.

2.4
.

Proof of
theorem

2
.

Because
\widehat{Q}_{m}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{range}(U_{m})
, the matrix
R:=\widehat{Q}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}
is nonsingular and
U_{m}=\widehat{Q}_{m}\widehat{Q}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}=\widehat{Q}_{m}R
holds. Multiplying both sides of (
2.4
) with
R^{-1}
gives

A\widehat{Q}_{m}=U_{m}\widehat{H}_{m}R^{-1}+\widehat{u}_{m+1}{c}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}R^{-1}=\widehat{Q}_{m}R\widehat{H}_{m}R^{-1}+\widehat{u}_{m+1}\widehat{c}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}.

Setting
\widehat{G}_{m}:=R\widehat{H}_{m}R^{-1}
completes the proof.

Let us point out the following two important properties of the Krylov decomposition
eq.

2.4
:

1.

The residual is orthogonal to the subspace:
AU_{m}-U_{m}\widehat{H}_{m}\perp\mathsf{range}(U_{m})
;

2.

\widehat{H}_{m}
is similar to a matrix
\widehat{G}_{m}
whose numerical range satisfies
W(\widehat{G}_{m})\subset W(A)
, where we recall that

W(A):=\bigl\{w^{\mathsf{H}}Aw\mid\|w\|=1\bigr\}.

In particular, the eigenvalues of
\widehat{H}_{m}
are always contained in
W(A)
, although, in general,
W(\widehat{H}_{m})\not\subset W(A)
. Among others, this implies that
\widehat{H}_{m}
has real eigenvalues when
A
is a Hermitian.

theorem

2
also works the other way. Suppose one has an Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.1
with orthonormal basis
Q_{m}
and Hessenberg matrix
G_{m}
. This clearly takes the form (
2.5
). Now, if one has another decomposition
eq.

2.4
such that

\mathsf{range}(Q_{m})=\mathsf{range}(U_{m})
then

theorem

2
implies that ensuring the orthogonality condition
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{u}_{m+1}=0
ensures the similarity of

\widehat{H}_{m}
and
G_{m}
.

Implications for linear systems

Suppose that we want to solve a linear system
Ax=b
with the full orthogonalization method (FOM), which was proposed for the general non-Hermitian case in
[
24
]
and further analyzed in
[
11
]
. The defining property of the approximate solution
x_{m}\in\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b)
computed by FOM is that its residual satisfies the Galerkin condition:
Ax_{m}-b\perp\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b)
. When
A
is Hermitian positive definite, FOM reduces to the classical conjugate gradient (CG) method
[
16
]
, and
x_{m}
minimizes the
A
-norm of the error over the Krylov subspace.
Given the Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.1
with
q_{1}=b/\lVert b\rVert
, the approximate solution computed by FOM is given by
x_{m}=Q_{m}G_{m}^{-1}\lVert b\rVert e_{1}
, where
e_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
denotes the first unit vector.

Randomized FOM
[
30
]
employs a randomized Arnoldi process and imposes the sketched Galerkin condition
\Omega(Ax_{m}-b)\perp\Omega\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b)
on the residual. The resulting approximation differs from FOM and, indeed, the convergence of randomized FOM may exhibit irregularities and spikes: see, for instance, the numerical experiments in
[
30
, Sec. 5]
.

On the other hand,
if
we had a Krylov decomposition
eq.

2.4
with
u_{1}=b/\beta
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we could cheaply recover the FOM approximation even if
U_{m}
is not orthonormal. Indeed, letting
x_{m}=U_{m}y_{m}
and imposing
Ax_{m}-b\perp\mathsf{range}(U_{m})
gives us

0=U_{m}^{\dagger}AU_{m}y_{m}-\beta U_{m}^{\dagger}U_{m}e_{1}=\widehat{H}_{m}y_{m}-\beta e_{1}\quad\implies\quad x_{m}=\beta U_{m}\widehat{H}_{m}^{-1}e_{1},

where we used
U_{m}^{\dagger}AU_{m}=\widehat{H}_{m}
, which follows from
eq.

2.4
.

We will show in
section

3
and
section

4
that analogous conclusions apply to eigenvalue computations and matrix function evaluations, respectively.

Similarity-restoring randomized Arnoldi

A common way to solve the least-squares problem
eq.

2.6
is to
form the Gram matrix
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}
, compute its Cholesky decomposition
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}=LL^{\mathsf{H}}
, and then solve

\widehat{h}_{m}=(U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m})^{-1}U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}u_{m+1}=L^{-\mathsf{H}}L^{-1}U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}u_{m+1}.

In the following cost analysis, flop counts are reported assuming real-valued arithmetic for simplicity.
The most costly part of this approach is the computation of the Gram matrix
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}
, which requires
m^{2}n
flops, provided that one exploits symmetry and only computes the upper triangular part of
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}
.
1
1

1

This is done, for instance, by the BLAS routine
syrk
, and appears to be automatically carried out when performing matrix-matrix multiplication in MATLAB.

The
\Omega
-orthogonalization in the randomized Arnoldi process costs another
m^{2}n
flops for updating the columns of
U_{m}
, plus lower-order terms for computing the sketched vectors and the inner products between sketched vectors. Thus, in addition to the matrix-vector products with
A
, the leading term in the computational cost of

1
is
2m^{2}n
flops.

The orthogonalization of
Q_{m}
using classical/modified Gram–Schmidt (CGS/MGS) within the standard Arnoldi process also costs
2m^{2}n
flops. Thus,
algorithm

1
requires the same amount of flops as the standard Arnoldi process and twice as much compared to randomized Arnoldi without the similarity-restoring correction. However,
algorithm

1
significantly benefits from the fact that half of its flops related to orthogonalization are in the computation of
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}U_{m}
, which is a BLAS-3 operation and can take full advantage of modern computational hardware. Indeed, in
section

5
we will see that, in practice, correction only yields a minor increase to the computational time of randomized Arnoldi.
Note that we expect the basis
U_{m}
to be well-conditioned and, hence, the solution of the least-squares problem via Cholesky is numerically stable.

An alternative approach to solving the least-squares problem
eq.

2.6
is to use an iterative method such as LSQR
[
22
]
. Because
U_{m}
is well-conditioned, there is no need to use a preconditioner.
Each iteration of LSQR involves one matrix-vector product with
U_{m}
and one with
U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}
, at a cost of approximately
4mn
flops per iteration. The total cost for
\ell
iterations of LSQR is thus about
4\ell mn
flops, which implies that we can only expect LSQR to be more efficient than the Cholesky approach if LSQR converges in
\ell<m/4
iterations. In practice, since LSQR is inherently sequential and the computation of the Gram matrix exploits BLAS-3 operations, we anticipate that
\ell
actually needs to be significantly smaller than
m/4
in order to observe a practical speedup by using LSQR instead of the Cholesky approach. The numerical comparisons in
section

5
support this conclusion and demonstrate that Cholesky is usually the more efficient approach, except when the subspace dimension
m
is rather large and/or low accuracy for the solution of
eq.

2.6
is sufficient.

In principle, the similarity-restoring correction
eq.

2.7
to the randomized Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.3
could also be employed when the latter is obtained implicitly through

a basis
W_{m}
computed via a cheap partial orthogonalization method, such as the
k
-truncated Arnoldi algorithm;
see, e.g.,
[
23
, Sec. 3]
for more details.
In this work, we do not consider this variant: although for simple problems it may work effectively, for more difficult ones it would be nontrivial to implement the solution of the least-squares problem
eq.

2.6
robustly and efficiently. Indeed, solving
eq.

2.6
accurately is challenging because of the ill-conditioning of
W_{m}
, and in turn computing such a solution would increase the computational complexity of whitening. Addressing these aspects would require further investigation and is beyond the scope of this work.

3.1
Arnoldi, Krylov–Schur and randomized Krylov–Schur method

Basic Arnoldi method

Given an Arnoldi decomposition
eq.

2.1
, the basic Arnoldi method extracts approximate eigenpairs by the Rayleigh–Ritz process. More specifically, it first computes an ordered Schur decomposition

G_{m}\begin{bmatrix}V_{\ell}&V_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{\ell}&V_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}S_{\ell}&S_{\ell\perp}\\
0&S_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

The eigenvalues of
G_{m}
are called Ritz values and, by eigenvalue reordering
[
19
]
, it is ensured that the
\ell<m

wanted
Ritz values, which approximate eigenvalues of interest, appear on the diagonal of the

\ell\times\ell
upper triangular matrix
S_{\ell}
. The matrix
Q_{m}V_{\ell}
is an orthonormal basis for a subspace that approximates the corresponding invariant subspace. Approximate eigenvectors, referred to as Ritz vectors, are obtained by multiplying
Q_{m}V_{\ell}
with eigenvectors of
S_{\ell}
.

Restarting with the Krylov–Schur method

A significant challenge of the basic Arnoldi method arises from the need for storing
Q_{m}
. Slow convergence leads to large
m
and the available memory may be exhausted long before satisfactory approximation quality is achieved. In addition, the cost of Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization in the Arnoldi process increases quadratically with
m
.
Popular algorithms for solving large-scale eigenvalue problems, such as Sorensen’s Implicitly Restarted Arnoldi (IRA) method
[
27
]
and Stewart’s Krylov–Schur method
[
28
]
, address this challenge by combining the Arnoldi process with restarting. Consider an orthonormal Krylov decomposition

AQ_{m}=Q_{m}G_{m}+q_{m+1}{c}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},

where
[Q_{m},q_{m+1}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times(m+1)}
is orthonormal,
G_{m}
is not necessarily upper Hessenberg and
{c}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a general vector (not necessarily a scalar multiple of
e_{m}
). Given an ordered Schur decomposition
eq.

3.1
of
G_{m}
,
the Krylov–Schur method performs restarting by compressing the orthonormal Krylov decomposition from order
m
to order
\ell<m
:

A(Q_{m}V_{\ell})=Q_{m}G_{m}V_{\ell}+q_{m+1}c_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}V_{\ell}=(Q_{m}V_{\ell})S_{\ell}+q_{m+1}(V_{\ell}^{\mathsf{H}}c_{m})^{\mathsf{H}}.

After restarting, one can expand an order-
\ell
Krylov decomposition to order
m
by means of the Arnoldi process and keep iterating this procedure until convergence.

Randomized Krylov–Schur method

To reduce the cost of orthogonalization,
de Damas and Grigori combined the randomized Gram–Schmidt process with IRA
[
8
]
and Krylov–Schur
[
9
]
. Specifically, replacing the Arnoldi process in the Krylov–Schur method with the randomized Arnoldi process yields randomized Krylov decompositions of the form

AU_{m}=U_{m}H_{m}+u_{m+1}{c}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},

where
[U_{m},u_{m+1}]
is
\Omega
-orthonormal. Performing an ordered Schur decomposition of
H_{m}
, analogous to
eq.

3.1
, we can restart the randomized Krylov decomposition by compressing it from order
m
to order
\ell<m
. Similarly, after restarting, the decomposition is expanded again to order
m
using the randomized Arnoldi process, which preserves the
\Omega
-orthonormality of the basis.
Note that the eigenvalues of
H_{m}
are, in general,
not
Ritz values of
A
.

3.2
A new randomized Krylov–Schur method that restores similarity

Inheriting this drawback from the randomized Arnoldi process discussed in
section

2.2
, the randomized Krylov–Schur method does not yield a matrix
H_{m}
that is similar to the matrix
G_{m}
from standard Krylov–Schur. In the following, we propose a new randomized Krylov–Schur method that restores similarity to

by leveraging the similarity-restoring randomized Arnoldi process described in

Initialization

Restarting

Suppose that we start from an order-

Krylov decomposition

Because of

are Ritz values of

Note that

Assuming that

By default, we do

Backward stability

In this section, we will assess the impact of roundoff error on the Krylov decompositions produced by
algorithm

2

after the correction in line
2

(\Omega\underline{U}_{m})^{\mathsf{H}}(\Omega\underline{U}_{m})-I=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}),

(3.12)

where

denotes unit roundoff (
\approx 10^{-16}

absorbs mild constants depending only on

We also assume that

\Omega

(3.14)

Applying the correction and performing matrix-vector products with

will incur further error, which we collect in a residual term

of a perturbed Krylov decomposition

.

Suppose that the assumptions

3.12

3.14

f_{m}^{\Omega}:=U_{m}f(H_{m})\lVert\Omega b\rVert e_{1},\quad\text{where}\quad(\Omega U_{m})^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega U_{m}=I\quad\text{and}\quad H_{m}=(\Omega U_{m})^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega AU_{m}.

(4.3)

This approximation is called
sketched FOM approximation
in
[
23
]
.
In this case, given any basis
W_{m}
of
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,b)
, not necessarily
\Omega
-orthonormal, we can rewrite
eq.

4.3
as

f_{m}^{\Omega}:=W_{m}f((\Omega W_{m})^{\dagger}\Omega AW_{m})(\Omega W_{m})^{\dagger}\Omega b,

see, for instance,
[
23
, Eq. (9)]
.
An explicit expression for the difference between
f_{m}
and
f_{m}^{\Omega}
has been given in
[
23
, Cor. 5.1]
, and it has been used to derive error bounds such as, for example,
[
23
, Cor. 5.4]
. Other theoretical results on the convergence of the approximation
f_{m}^{\Omega}
can be found in
[
6
, Sec. 4]
and
[
15
, Sec. 2.3]
.
Despite these results in the recent literature, the current theoretical understanding of the convergence of the approximation
eq.

4.3
is not completely satisfactory. One serious obstacle is that the numerical range of the projected matrix
H_{m}
in
eq.

4.3
may be significantly larger than the numerical range of
G_{m}
in
eq.

4.1
and may, in fact, not be contained in
W(A)
. In an extreme scenario, this might mean that
f(H_{m})
is not well-defined for a function
f
with singularities outside
W(A)
. More importantly, it leads to spikes in the convergence of the randomized Arnoldi approximation
eq.

4.3
. This kind of behavior has been observed, for example, in
[
15
]
; see also
section

5.2.1
.

4.2
Restoring similarity in randomized Arnoldi for matrix functions

Let us now consider a Krylov decomposition of the form
eq.

2.4
, additionally assuming that
U_{m}
is
\Omega
-orthonormal and
c_{m}=h_{m+1,m}e_{m}
:

AU_{m}=U_{m}\widehat{H}_{m}+h_{m+1,m}\widehat{u}_{m+1}e_{m}^{\mathsf{H}},\quad\text{and}\quad(\Omega U_{m})^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega U_{m}=I,\quad U_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\widehat{u}_{m+1}=0.

Then

U_{m}^{\dagger}AU_{m}=\widehat{H}_{m}+h_{m+1,m}U_{m}^{\dagger}\widehat{u}_{m+1}e_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}=\widehat{H}_{m}.

Using
u_{1}=b/\lVert\Omega b\rVert
and
eq.

4.2
with
W_{m}=U_{m}
gives us the following equivalent expression:

f_{m}=U_{m}f(\widehat{H}_{m})\lVert\Omega b\rVert e_{1}.

(4.4)

Let us stress that this approximation is identical to the standard Arnoldi approximation (
4.1
) and, thus, classical convergence results from the literature, such as
[
3
,
18
]
, apply.

The expression
eq.

4.4
allows us to efficiently compute the Arnoldi approximation
eq.

4.1
even if the basis
U_{m}
is not orthonormal, we thus benefit from the more robust convergence behavior of
eq.

4.1
at a minor increase in computational cost relative to
eq.

4.3
.
If the accuracy of
f_{m}
is not satisfactory and we need to perform additional Arnoldi iterations, we simply discard the corrected quantities
\widehat{H}_{m}
and
\widehat{u}_{m+1}
and construct an expanded randomized Krylov decomposition
eq.

2.3
, ensuring that the basis is still
\Omega
-orthonormal until the next time that we apply the similarity-restoring correction in
eq.

2.4
to compute an approximation to
f(A)b
.
Since the cost of solving the linear least-squares problem
eq.

2.6
can quickly add up, we recommend to compute the approximation to
f(A)b
using
eq.

4.4
only once every few iterations. The resulting algorithm is summarized in
algorithm

3
, where the approximation
eq.

4.4
to
f(A)b
is computed once every
\ell
iterations.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we compare our similarity-restoring algorithms numerically against standard and randomized algorithms from the literature for the computation of eigenvalues and matrix functions.
All numerical experiments in this section have been implemented in Matlab 2022b and were carried out on an AMD Ryzen 9 6900HX Processor (8 cores, 3.3–4.9 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. Scripts to reproduce numerical results are publicly available at
https://github.com/nShao678/Similarity-restoring-randomized-Arnoldi-code
.

The randomized Gram–Schmidt process can be implemented in several different ways; see, for example,
[
1
,
2
]
. In its
k
th iteration, let us denote by
U_{k}
an
\Omega
-orthonormal basis and by
w
the new vector that needs to be
\Omega
-orthonormalized.
In our experiments, we use the following two variants for this calculation.

RGS:

Use a QR decomposition of
\Omega U_{k}
to compute
\widetilde{u}_{k+1}=w-U_{k}(\Omega U_{k})^{\dagger}\Omega w
. This approach corresponds, for instance, to the
backslash
operator in MATLAB.

RCGS2:

Apply a randomized classical Gram–Schmidt process twice: first compute
\widetilde{w}=w-U_{k}(\Omega U_{k})^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega w
and then compute
\widetilde{u}_{k+1}
by performing an
\Omega
-reorthogonalization, as
\widetilde{u}_{k+1}=\widetilde{w}-U_{k}(\Omega U_{k})^{\mathsf{H}}\Omega\widetilde{w}
.

In both implementations, the new basis vector
u_{k+1}
is obtained with the
\Omega
-normalization
u_{k+1}=\widetilde{u}_{k+1}/\lVert\Omega\widetilde{u}_{k+1}\rVert
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a modification of the randomized Arnoldi process that restores similarity with the Hessenberg matrix from the standard Arnoldi method by enforcing orthogonality between the last Arnoldi vector and the previous subspace.
Our analysis and experiments show that the modified process recovers the accuracy and robustness of the standard Arnoldi process while preserving the efficiency of the randomized variant.
