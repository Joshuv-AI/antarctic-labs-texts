Ferroptosis-Related Diagnostic Biomarkers in Diabetic Kidney Disease: A Multi-Omics and Multi-Cohort Study
Authors: Xu Li, Huan Hong, Hong Li, Zhenhua Fang, Jie Wang, Xiuli Wang, Xinting Sui, Halinuer Shadekejiang, Mingzhu Liang, Xinyu Gan, Jiaming Liu, Chen Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the primary factors leading to end-stage renal disease. Ferroptosis, as a mechanism-related form of programmed cell death, has garnered increasing attention in DKD research. This study aimed to identify and validate ferroptosis-related diagnostic biomarkers for DKD.MethodsPublic DKD transcriptomic datasets were analyzed using differential expression analysis, WGCNA, and multiple machine-learning algorithms to identify key ferroptosis-related genes and construct a logistic regression diagnostic model. Candidate biomarkers were validated in Nephroseq V5 platform, a real-world clinical cohort by ELISA, a human kidney single-cell RNA-seq dataset, and an STZ-induced DKD rat model.ResultsThirty-two ferroptosis-related module-specific differentially expressed genes were identified, from which COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36 were selected as key biomarkers. The four-gene diagnostic model showed strong discriminatory performance in the training dataset and maintained diagnostic value in three independent validation datasets. In the real-world cohort, creatinine-corrected urinary levels of the four corresponding proteins were positively correlated with urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio and negatively correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate, and the combined urinary predictor showed high diagnostic performance for DKD. Single-cell analysis revealed cell-type-specific expression patterns, including COL14A1 enrichment in juxtaglomerular/interstitial cells and TYRO3 enrichment in podocytes, with remodeling of TYRO3-associated intercellular communication in DKD. In DKD rat kidneys, Col14a1 and Acsl4 were upregulated, whereas Tyro3 was downregulated, supporting the disease relevance of these markers.ConclusionThis multi-omics and multi-cohort study identifies COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36 as ferroptosis-related biomarkers associated with DKD renal injury. Their creatinine-corrected urinary protein levels may serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker panel for DKD diagnosis and risk stratification.

Introduction

The global burden of diabetes continues to rise rapidly. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, and this number is projected to increase to 853 million by 2050.1 Approximately 40% of individuals with type 2 diabetes progress to diabetic kidney disease (DKD).2 Clinically, DKD is often identified and monitored using urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and current guidelines recommend regular assessment of both indicators in diabetic populations.3 However, UACR and eGFR mainly reflect established renal injury or functional decline, and real-world screening remains insufficiently implemented.4 Therefore, identifying sensitive and scalable molecular biomarkers for earlier DKD detection and risk stratification remains an urgent unmet need.

Ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation.5 In the field of DKD, mounting evidence indicates that ferroptosis is not a bystander effect but a pathologically coupled component closely linked to renal cell injury, amplified inflammation, and structural remodeling under diabetic conditions.6 Ferroptosis-related molecular changes, including downregulated solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), elevated lipid peroxidation and upregulated acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), are detected in renal tissues of DKD patients and animal models.5,7 At the mechanistic level, renal tubular epithelial cells under diabetic conditions exhibit weakened Xc−/GPX4 defenses and accumulated lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis inhibitors (such as ferrostatin-1) partially reverse these alterations and mitigate tubular injury in diabetic mice.7,8 Furthermore, podocyte injury is critical to the progression of DKD. Ferroptosis also mediates this injury, and ferroptosis inhibitors or interventions can ameliorate podocyte damage induced by high glucose or diabetes.9,10 These findings suggest that the “ferroptosis-renal injury” pathway is amenable to intervention. Ferroptosis-associated molecules also demonstrate potential as diagnostic biomarkers. Clinical studies have reported correlations between serum GPX4, ACSL4, and iron metabolism markers with DKD severity, eGFR, or urinary albumin excretion.11 Combined GPX4, ACSL4, malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) effectively predicted DKD (AUC = 0.804, P < 0.001).12 These findings indicate that ferroptosis-related molecules may not only participate in DKD pathogenesis but also provide potential diagnostic or risk-stratification biomarkers.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify and validate ferroptosis-related diagnostic biomarkers for DKD using an integrated multi-omics and multi-cohort strategy. We first combined differential expression analysis, weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA), and multiple machine-learning algorithms to screen candidate ferroptosis-related genes from public DKD transcriptomic datasets. We then evaluated their diagnostic performance in independent validation datasets and assessed their clinical relevance at the protein level in a real-world cohort using serum and urine ELISA assays. Furthermore, we used single-cell RNA sequencing to determine their cell-type-specific expression patterns and infer related intercellular communication changes, followed by validation in an Streptozotocin (STZ) induced DKD rat model. Most previous DKD biomarker studies have mainly relied on transcriptomic screening or single-level validation. The novelty of this study lies in the integration of transcriptomic discovery, clinical protein-level validation, single-cell localization, communication-network analysis, and animal-model confirmation, providing a multi-layered evidence chain for ferroptosis-related biomarkers in DKD.

Screening of Ferroptosis Genes Associated with Diabetic Kidney Disease

First, DESeq2 (v1.42.1) was used to perform differential gene expression analysis on the DKD group and control group in the GSE142025 dataset. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 (|log2FC| > 1) and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P value < 0.0001. Further WGCNA analysis was performed using the WGCNA14 package (v1.73). We calculated the variance for all genes in GSE142025 and selected the top 50% genes with the highest variance for analysis. Soft threshold power was set to 17 to achieve a scaling topology fit index of 0.80. Additional key parameters included: deepSplit = 2, minimum module size = 50, and module merging cutoff height = 0.15. We assessed correlations between module signature genes and the phenome (DKD and control groups), recording all genes within each module. Genes within each module were considered module signature genes. We selected the module with the highest absolute correlation coefficient (|r|) and performed an intersection analysis between its genes and those of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) and DEGs, generating a Venn diagram. Genes identified at these intersections were defined as ferroptosis-related module-specific differentially expressed genes (FM-DEGs). FRGs were collected from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). Using “ferroptosis” as the search keyword, we retained only “protein-coding” genes with a “relevance score > 1” yielding a total of 1093 FRGs.

Machine-Learning Screening of Key Genes

Using the GSE142025 dataset, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed on FM‑DEGs to construct a diagnostic model for DKD, and genes were selected at a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Random forest (RF)15 analysis was conducted using the randomForest package (v4.7.1.2) in R (set.seed = 1234, ntree = 1000). Rank feature importance using MeanDecreaseGini and evaluate model performance via 10-fold cross-validation with five replicates. Define the inflection point with the minimum cross-validation error as the optimal FM-DEG subset. Support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) was then applied iteratively to remove low-contribution features, thereby identifying the most informative subset. Perform least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression using the glmnet package (v4.1.8) in R (set.seed = 1234, alpha = 1, family = “binomial”) with five-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal λ (lambda); select FM-DEGs with non-zero coefficients at λ.min as candidate features. These four algorithms were selected because they represent complementary feature-selection strategies, including conventional association-based screening, ensemble tree-based importance ranking, margin-based recursive feature elimination, and regularization-based sparse modeling. Key genes were identified through four methods-univariate logistic regression analysis (P < 0.05), RF (based on highest MeanDecreaseGini score), SVM-RFE, and LASSO regression (selected by λ.min).

Analysis of Clinical Correlation Between Key Gene Expression and Renal Function and Their Diagnostic Efficacy

To assess the clinical relevance of key genes in publicly available renal tissue data, we utilized Nephroseq V5 (an online data mining platform integrating and standardizing transcriptomic and clinical phenotypes across multiple kidney diseases) (http://v5.nephroseq.org/). Two anatomical regions were selected from DKD-related datasets: glomerular tissue (13 controls, 9 DKD cases) and tubulointerstitial tissue (12 controls, 10 DKD cases). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess associations between gene expression and eGFR. Scatter plots overlaid with linear fits and 95% confidence intervals were used to visualize trends.

In the real-world cohort, key gene‑encoded protein and ferroptosis-related protein levels were quantified in serum and urine using ELISA. Pearson correlation analysis was then used to assess linear associations between key gene‑encoded proteins (including urine ratios after correction) and renal function/injury markers (eg, eGFR, UACR). ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of each indicator for DKD, and the AUC values were calculated and compared.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA was performed to quantify COL14A1 (Cat# YX-031526H, Sinobestbio, China), ACADSB (Cat# YX-010305H, Sinobestbio, China), TYRO3 (Cat# YX-202533H, Sinobestbio, China), ZFP36 (Cat# YX-261442H, Sinobestbio, China), GPX4 (Cat# YX-22231H, Sinobestbio, China) and ACSL4 (Cat# YX-010331H, Sinobestbio, China) levels in the serum and urine collected from subjects, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated from known concentrations of recombinant.

Single-Cell Atlas of Key Gene Expression and Cell-Cell Communication Analysis

To systematically characterize key gene expression across renal cell types, we analyzed a human kidney scRNA-seq dataset (GSE183276) comprising 20 control and 14 DKD samples, totaling 64,726 high-quality cells (21,650 control; 43,076 DKD).16 Data preprocessing and quality control were performed using Seurat R package (v5.1.0), excluding genes expressed in <3 cells and cells expressing <200 genes. Filtering criteria included nCount between 1,000–13,517 (the upper limit determined by the interquartile range method: Q3 + 1.5 × IQR), nFeature >600, cellular complexity >0.8, and mitochondrial gene content <30%. Genes expressed in ≥100 cells were retained. Analyses included normalization, scaling, cell cycle correction, clustering, and annotation, with a fixed random seed (1234) to ensure reproducibility.

Cell clusters were annotated using ScType,17 leveraging kidney-specific marker genes. Each cluster was assigned the highest-scoring cell type exceeding the 25th percentile of cluster cell numbers; clusters not meeting this threshold were labeled as “Unknown”. Differential gene expression between DKD and control groups was assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and results were visualized via FeaturePlot, DotPlot, VlnPlot, and ggplot2.

Cell-cell communication was inferred using CellChat R package (v2.2.0), quantifying the number and strength of intercellular interactions. Signaling pathways involving key genes were specifically examined, and differences between DKD and control groups were visualized with heatmaps, and network diagrams.

Animal Model of STZ-Induced DKD

Fourteen 5‑week‑old male specific‑pathogen‑free Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats with body weight of (189.7 ± 16.0)g were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xinjiang Medical University. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC-20230627-19) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and relevant national guidelines of China.

Animals were housed under controlled conditions (temperature 25 ± 2 °C, humidity 50 ± 5%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and allowed ad libitum access to food and water. General health, activity, and food and water intake were monitored daily. Humane endpoints (body weight loss >20%, severe lethargy, or inability to access food or water) were predefined. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering during the experiment.

After one week of acclimatization in cages, rats were randomly assigned to the control (n=6) or DKD group (n=8) using a computer-generated random number list. Each animal served as the independent experimental unit. Sample size was determined according to preliminary experiments and literature.18 All procedures and measurements were performed in a random order, and cage positions were randomly arranged to minimize confounding factors.

The control group received standard chow, whereas the DKD group was fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet (35% fat) for four weeks. Diabetes was induced in the DKD group by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 35 mg/kg), while the control group received citrate buffer, according to literature.18 Successful diabetes induction was defined as random blood glucose > 16.7 mmol/L. Blood glucose, body weight, and UACR were monitored every four weeks. UACR was selected as the primary outcome and used for sample size calculation. The total duration of the experiment was 17 weeks (1 week acclimatization, 4 weeks dietary intervention, and 12 weeks after STZ injection).

Only DKD rats with sustained random blood glucose >16.7 mmol/L were included. Two DKD rats died after STZ administration due to severe hyperglycemia and were excluded. Twelve rats (6 per group) were finally analyzed.

At the endpoint, rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water, then deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg). Euthanasia was performed by exsanguination via the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia. Both kidneys were immediately harvested and weighed. A portion of the left kidney was collected for transmission electron microscopy, while the remaining tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The right kidney was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

Blinding was not feasible during treatment and assessment due to distinct phenotypic differences between groups. All measurements were performed in a standardized order to reduce observer bias.

Assessment of Animal Renal Function

Urinary creatinine levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer. Urinary albumin was quantified using an ELISA kit (Cat# YX-130121R, Sinobestbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.0.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Renal tissue was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature in the dark. Samples were dehydrated through graded ethanol and 100% acetone, infiltrated, and embedded in 812 epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged using a Hitachi transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

RNA Extraction and Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from rat kidney tissue using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were determined, and 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription premix. For quantitative PCR, 2 μL of cDNA was used as a template with SYBR Green qPCR premix to amplify target genes. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Histopathological and Immunofluorescence Analysis

Kidney tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at 3 μm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, periodic acid-Schiff, and Masson’s trichrome according to standard protocols.

For immunofluorescence, tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval and non-specific binding blocking. Sections were incubated with anti-CD68 primary antibody (Cat# YM8367, Immunoway, USA), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and fluorescent labeling using a dual-triple multiplex immunofluorescence kit. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching medium. Images were captured using a LEICA microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all data processing and analyses in this study were performed using R software (Version 4.3.0). Normality was examined by Shapiro‑Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups of continuous variables, statistical significance for normally distributed variables was assessed using the independent Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed via the Mann–Whitney U-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were conducted via one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normal data. For data with a normal distribution, Pearson correlation analysis was used. For non-normally distributed data, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied. Logistic regression was applied to calculate the combined predictive values of four key gene-encoded proteins in serum and urine for the diagnosis of DKD, generating serum combined predictor and urine combined predictor. ROC curves were plotted using MedCalc software (Version 23.3.3) to evaluate diagnostic efficacy and calculate the AUC. The DeLong test was adopted to compare the differences in AUC values among various indicators.

Unless explicitly stated, all reported P values are two-tailed, with a threshold of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of Ferroptosis-Related Genes in Diabetic Kidney Disease

Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of this study. To systematically identify key molecules associated with ferroptosis in DKD, we established an integrated analysis strategy comprising “differential expression screening, co-expression network localization, intersection of ferroptosis gene sets”. Differential analysis in the training dataset GSE142025 (Figure 2A) identified 1,072 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 857 upregulated and 215 downregulated genes (Table S2). A heatmap highlights the top 20 DEGs (Figure 2B). Subsequently, WGCNA was performed on the training set expression matrix to identify co-expression modules strongly associated with the DKD phenotype. Soft thresholds were selected based on scale-free topology criteria (Figure 2C). Genes ranked in the top 50% by variability were clustered into 12 modules (Figure 2D), and module correlations with the phenotype were calculated. Results revealed significant positive correlation between the MEbrown module and DKD (r = 0.79, P = 1.62×10−7), and significant negative correlation between the MEblue module and DKD (r = −0.79, P = 1.62×10−7) (Figure 2E), suggesting these modules may harbor core molecular networks for DKD. We intersected DEGs, key genes from the two major modules, and pre-curated FRGs (Figure 2F), ultimately identifying 32 FM-DEGs (Table S3; MEbrown contributed 8, MEblue contributed 24). Further functional enrichment analysis of these FM-DEGs revealed involvement in multiple biological processes and signaling pathways related to cell death and inflammatory immunity (Figure 2G and H). These included ferroptosis, apoptosis, and inflammatory pathways such as TNF/NF-κB, IL-17, and Toll-like receptor signaling. This suggests that ferroptosis-related molecular networks may be tightly coupled with the processes of inflammatory amplification and tissue damage in DKD (Figure 2H). These 32 FM-DEGs were therefore used as candidate features for subsequent machine-learning-based biomarker screening and diagnostic model construction. Development of a Ferroptosis-Related Key Genes Diagnostic Model for DKD

Based on the aforementioned 32 FM-DEGs, univariate logistic regression screening was first performed, yielding 23 candidate genes significantly associated with DKD (P < 0.05; Table S4). Subsequently, multiple machine learning algorithms were employed to screen key genes. The residuals of the random forest model converged rapidly and stabilized with increasing tree numbers (Figure 3A). Genes were ranked based on their contribution using the MeanDecreaseGini metric (Figure 3B). The top 10 FM-DEGs with the maximum MeanDecreaseGini values at the minimal error inflection point were identified. Then, SVM-RFE performed feature recursive elimination. Five-fold cross-validation indicated the lowest model error occurred with 7 features (Figure 3C), yielding the average ranking of these 7 key genes (Figure 3D). Finally, sparse regression via LASSO retained 4 genes (COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36) with non-zero coefficients at the optimal penalty parameter (Figure 3E and F). Based on the expression profiles of the four key genes, a logistic regression diagnostic model was constructed to calculate a risk score. A nomogram was generated in the training dataset GSE142025 (Figure 4A) to visualize the combined contribution of the four genes to the probability of DKD occurrence. The calibration curve indicated good agreement between the model’s predicted probabilities and actual outcomes (Figure 4B). Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated stable net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities (Figure 4C), suggesting potential clinical utility. Regarding discriminative performance, ROC analysis showed an AUC of 1.000 in the training set (Figure 4D). Applying the same coefficients to three independent external validation sets, the model maintained good generalization: GSE96804 AUC=0.993 (Figure 4E), GSE30528 AUC=0.752 (Figure 4F), GSE30529 AUC=0.808 (Figure 4G). Further stratification of the training set into high- and low-risk groups based on median riskscore revealed that single-gene ROC analysis indicated ACADSB (AUC=0.982) and TYRO3 (AUC=0.900) demonstrated stronger discriminatory capabilities for stratification, whereas ZFP36 (AUC=0.655) exhibited relatively limited discriminatory power (Figure 4H and I). Validation of Key Gene Expression and Diagnostic Models in a Real-World Cohort

We further evaluated the corresponding protein products in serum and urine samples from an independent real-world cohort. The real-world cohort included 51 DKD cases, 51 T2DM cases, and 50 healthy controls. Baseline characteristics of the three groups are shown in Table 2. Compared with the T2DM group, the DKD group exhibited significantly lower ALB and eGFR, while BUN, Scr, and UACR were significantly elevated (all P<0.05), indicating a typical “decreased renal function + increased proteinuria” phenotypic gradient in this cohort. No significant differences were observed between DKD and T2DM groups in gender, age, TP, TG, TC, FBG, or HbA1c (Table 2). In this cohort, we employed ELISA to measure serum and urinary levels of four key gene‑encoded protein: COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36 (Table 3, Figure 5A–D). To distinguish sample types, serum was denoted as s-, and urine as u-. Urinary markers were uniformly converted to u-marker/uCR (urine creatinine-corrected) prior to statistical analysis to mitigate the impact of varying urine dilution. Overall trends showed that in serum, sCOL14A1 and sZFP36 were generally higher in T2DM and DKD compared to controls, exhibiting a “T2DM > DKD > Control” distribution gradient; sACADSB followed a Control > DKD > T2DM pattern; sTYRO3 was elevated in both T2DM and DKD relative to controls (Figure 5A–D; Table 3). In urine, after uCR correction, uCOL14A1/uCR, uACADSB/uCR, uTYRO3/uCR, and uZFP36/uCR were overall upregulated in the DKD group. Notably, uACADSB/uCR and uTYRO3/uCR exhibited more pronounced elevation trends in DKD compared to both T2DM and controls (Figure 5A–D; Table 3). These findings validate the disease relevance of these key gene‑encoded proteins and support their feasibility as detectable biomarkers for clinical stratification. To further validate the biological consistency of the “ferroptosis axis,” we simultaneously measured serum and urine levels of the classic ferroptosis markers GPX4 and ACSL4 (Figure S1A and B; Table 3). Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals Cell Type-Specific Expression of Key Genes in Human Kidneys, with TYRO3-Associated Communication Axes Undergoing Remodeling in DKD

We utilized the publicly available human kidney scRNA-seq dataset GSE183276 to analyze the localization and potential functions of key ferroptosis-associated molecules in DKD at the cellular resolution. After quality control, 32,429 high-quality cells were obtained. Unsupervised clustering combined with annotation of classical markers yielded 14 renal cell populations (Figure 8A), covering podocytes, endothelial cells, juxtaglomerular cells, proximal tubule cells, loop of Henle cells, distal tubule cells, connecting tubule cells, principal cells (collecting duct system), α-intercalated cells (collecting duct system), β-intercalated cells (collecting duct system), renal interstitium (mesenchymal cells), renal vesicle cells, hematopoietic cells, immune cells. Building on this, we further demonstrated the proportion of cell composition across different individual samples (Figure 8B) and the cell number distribution within each cell population (Figure 8C). Marker gene expression across cell types is shown in Figure 8D. At the level of cell-type-specific expression, four key genes exhibited distinct compartmental preferences (Figure 8E): COL14A1 was primarily enriched in juxtaglomerular cells and renal interstitium (mesenchymal) cells; ACADSB was mainly localized to proximal tubule cells; TYRO3 showed highest enrichment in podocytes; and ZFP36 demonstrated broader expression across multiple cell populations. Further comparison of expression differences within cell populations between DKD and controls (Figure 8F–I) revealed: COL14A1 showed the most significant upregulation in juxtaglomerular cells under DKD conditions (Figure 8F); conversely, ACADSB was significantly downregulated in proximal tubule cells and exhibited a consistent downward trend across multiple distal/collecting duct-associated subpopulations (Figure 8G); TYRO3 was markedly reduced in podocytes and also decreased in some non-podocyte populations (Figure 8H); ZFP36 showed significant downregulation in most cell types except hematopoietic cells (Figure 8I). Altered Expression of Key Genes and Ferroptosis Marker Acsl4 in Kidney Tissue of STZ-Induced DKD Rats

To further examine whether these molecular alterations could be recapitulated in vivo, we established an DKD rat model and assessed renal phenotypes together with key gene expression changes. We established a rat model of type 2 diabetes-associated DKD using a “high-fat, high-sugar diet combined with STZ injection” regimen (Figure 10A). The DKD group showed a greater increase in random blood glucose levels than the control group following STZ injection (Figure 10B), accompanied by restricted body weight gain (Figure 10C). UACR significantly increased in the DKD group starting from week 14 and further worsened at the endpoint (Figure 10D). At the endpoint, the kidney index (kidney weight/body weight) in the DKD group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Figure 10E). Transmission electron microscopy revealed extensive fusion and effacement of podocyte foot processes, thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, as well as mitochondrial swelling, loss of cristae, and outer membrane rupture in podocytes of the DKD group (Figure 10F), indicating impairment of the glomerular filtration barrier and the occurrence of podocyte ferroptosis. HE, PAS, and Masson staining further confirmed glomerular hypertrophy, mesangial cell proliferation, and mesangial matrix expansion in the DKD group. PAS staining indicated glycogen-like deposits and thickening of the basement membrane and mesangial areas, while Masson staining revealed increased collagen deposition and fibrotic tendencies (Figure 10G). Immunofluorescence revealed markedly increased CD68-positive cell infiltration in the DKD group (Figure 10H), indicating activation of the renal inflammatory microenvironment. Discussion

DKD remains one of the primary causes of end-stage renal disease (ESRD)19,20 and is associated with a substantial healthcare burden.21 Therefore, developing more sensitive and scalable molecular biomarkers, as well as risk assessment models, is of practical significance for early screening and stratified management of DKD. This study established an integrated evidence chain for ferroptosis-related genes (COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36) in DKD, covering discovery in public databases, validation in real-world cohorts, single-cell localization and cell communication analysis, and validation in animal models. Their creatinine-corrected urinary protein levels may serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker panel for diagnosis and risk stratification of DKD.

Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and iron homeostasis imbalance, is interconnected with oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation.22,23 Its mechanisms and therapeutic potential in kidney diseases, including DKD, have garnered extensive attention. Ferroptosis-related alterations, including impairment of the SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant axis, increased lipid peroxidation, and upregulated ACSL4 expression, have been reported in preclinical and clinical DKD studies.5,7 In a clinical cross-sectional study, DKD severity was negatively associated with serum GPX4 and positively associated with serum ACSL4 levels.11 These results align with our observation of reduced serum GPX4 and increased ACSL4 in DKD patients relative to healthy controls, further supporting the rationale for screening ferroptosis-related diagnostic biomarkers.

By integrating multiple machine learning algorithms, we identified four ferroptosis-related key genes with potential diagnostic value. Among these, COL14A1 and TYRO3 were the two genes that demonstrated consistent results across bulk transcriptomic data analysis, scRNA-seq analysis, and was further validated in DKD rat model. In this study, we observed a significant upregulation of COL14A1 in renal tissues from both patients and rat models with DKD. Notably, its mRNA expression was inversely correlated with eGFR in DKD patients and positively associated with UACR in DKD rats, indicating that COL14A1 may be a critical contributor to the initiation and progression of DKD. COL14A1 encodes type XIV collagen involved in collagen fibrillogenesis24 and serves as a collagen-binding adapter within the extracellular matrix (ECM).25 During DKD progression, excessive ECM protein accumulation and dysregulated degradation cause glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, which are key structural alterations that drive the progressive decline in renal function and ultimately lead to ESRD.26 COL14A1 can activate the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway, thereby promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).27 EMT plays a pivotal role in renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis.28 In this study, Masson staining demonstrated increased collagen deposition within and surrounding the glomeruli of DKD rats, suggesting the presence of fibrosis. Whether COL14A1 exerts both pro-ferroptotic and pro-fibrotic effects in DKD remains to be confirmed by further studies.

scRNA-seq revealed that COL14A1 is predominantly expressed in juxtaglomerular cells and renal interstitium (mesenchymal) cells. And COL14A1 expression in juxtaglomerular cells was significantly higher in the DKD group than in controls. Mesenchymal cells can differentiate into juxtaglomerular cells and fibroblasts, contributing collectively to renal interstitial fibrosis.29,30 Juxtaglomerular cells are key regulators of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), which governs glomerular perfusion pressure and systemic blood pressure. Abnormal activation of the RAS, leading to elevated intraglomerular pressure, is a well-recognized hallmark and pathogenic mechanism of DKD.31

Given the increased expression of COL14A1 in juxtaglomerular cells in DKD, we hypothesize that COL14A1 may be involved in local ECM remodeling of the afferent arterioles and aberrant RAS activation, thereby contributing to disease pathogenesis. Notably, to date, there have been no reports describing the specific function of COL14A1 in juxtaglomerular cells. Our findings therefore offer a novel perspective for investigating the cell-type-specific roles of COL14A1 in the kidney.

TYRO3 is a member of the TAM (TYRO3, AXL, and MER) family of receptor tyrosine kinases. TYRO3 has been reported to exert pleiotropic immunosuppressive effects on immune responses driven by Toll-like receptors and cytokines,32 and confers anti-apoptotic effects in podocytes by activating the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)/c-jun-P53 signaling pathway.33 Pharmacological activation of TYRO3 has been shown to attenuate proteinuria, glomerular injury, and podocyte loss in db/db mouse models of DKD,34 supporting a renoprotective role for TYRO3.

In our analysis from public databases, TYRO3 expression was markedly downregulated in podocytes from patients with DKD and positively correlated with eGFR. Consistently, Tyro3 mRNA expression was significantly reduced in the DKD rat model compared with controls and showed a negative correlation with UACR. Collectively, these findings highlight TYRO3 as a potential protective factor in the development and progression of DKD. At the humoral level, serum soluble TYRO3 (sTYRO3) levels were higher in the DKD group than in the healthy control group. This phenomenon of “low tissue expression but high humoral concentration” suggests that a specific pathophysiological mechanism may be responsible for the release of sTYRO3.

TAM receptors can be cleaved by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 10 and γ-secretase to generate soluble fragments.35 The elevated sTYRO3 acts as a “decoy receptor” that competitively binds ligands, thereby antagonizing signal transduction mediated by the intact membrane-bound receptor.36 Consistent with our findings, OCHODNICKY P et al37 reported elevated plasma sTYRO3 levels in DKD patients with UAER >300 mg/24 h, compared with both healthy controls and diabetic patients without albuminuria (UAER <30 mg/24 h). In contrast, XIONG Y et al38 found that serum levels of soluble TAM receptors (TYRO3, AXL, and MER) were significantly lower in DKD patients than in patients with type 2 diabetes and healthy controls. These inconsistent clinical results indicate complex regulation of TAM receptors in DKD. While membrane-bound TYRO3 confers renal protection, circulating soluble TYRO3 varies with disease severity, albuminuria degree and pathological stages.

Interestingly, the cell-cell communication analysis revealed that GAS6-TYRO3 signaling is attenuated in DKD podocytes, which may contribute to impaired podocyte crosstalk and disease progression. This finding is further supported by clinical evidence indicating that higher circulating GAS6 levels are associated with a reduced risk of DKD.39 In conclusion, our findings, together with previous evidence, suggest that TYRO3 may serve as a promising diagnostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for DKD. Although evidence from prior studies indicates that TYRO3 inhibits ferroptosis in non-renal contexts,40,41 it remains to be elucidated whether its protective role in DKD (particularly in podocytes) is mediated through suppression of ferroptosis.

Although UACR and eGFR remain indispensable for the diagnosis and monitoring of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), they primarily reflect established albuminuria or renal function decline and fail to fully capture early molecular injury or disease heterogeneity. Consistent with recent evidence that combined urinary biomarker panels are superior to single markers,42 our real-world cohort data confirmed that creatinine-corrected urinary combination of COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3 and ZFP36 achieved better diagnostic efficacy than individual indicators and was comparable to the classic marker UACR. Notably, these four proteins were moderately positively correlated with UACR and negatively correlated with eGFR. We built a four-gene logistic regression model with strong diagnostic efficacy across multiple transcriptomic datasets. These findings support the potential of these key genes and their encoded proteins as promising biomarkers for DKD diagnosis and risk stratification, with substantial clinical translational value.

Notably, the directionality of gene expression levels (GEO/Nephroseq) does not fully align with that of serum protein levels (real-world cohort). Such tissue-serum expression discordance warrants in-depth mechanistic investigation. Further mechanistic investigations into receptor cleavage, protein secretion patterns, cellular origins, and regional expression characteristics in renal tissues are warranted to bridge tissue-level transcriptional evidence and clinical serological applications. Optimization of cutoff thresholds based on real-world clinical data and expansion of cohorts covering diverse disease stages and treatment histories can substantially improve the generalizability and clinical applicability of the predictive model.

Our study integrates bioinformatic screening, clinical correlation analysis, and animal validation to identify DKD ferroptosis-related biomarkers, yet the findings remain exploratory. Unlike conventional descriptive studies, this work uniquely integrates renal transcriptomics with real-world serum and urinary protein quantification, bridging the translational gap between basic molecular features and clinical practice. Nevertheless, several limitations and clinical barriers exist. The retrospective bias, cohort heterogeneity and limited real-world samples, along with shared cohort use for feature selection and modeling, may induce overfitting and limited generalizability. The causality between key genes and DKD ferroptosis remains unvalidated. Furthermore, several obstacles still hinder clinical translation, including detection costs, variable batch consistency of detection reagents, and a lack of unified regulatory standards. We finally propose a clear future research roadmap: prospective multicenter cohorts will validate biomarker diagnostic efficacy, functional experiments will clarify underlying mechanisms, and optimized detection protocols will resolve practical translational bottlenecks.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified COL14A1, ACADSB, TYRO3, and ZFP36 as ferroptosis-related candidate biomarkers associated with DKD renal injury. Multi transcriptomic analysis, real-world protein-level validation, single-cell analysis, and animal-model evidence support their potential relevance to DKD diagnosis and risk stratification, particularly as a creatinine-corrected urinary biomarker panel. However, these findings remain exploratory. Further prospective clinical studies and functional mechanistic experiments are required to confirm their diagnostic performance, prognostic value, and potential therapeutic relevance in DKD.
