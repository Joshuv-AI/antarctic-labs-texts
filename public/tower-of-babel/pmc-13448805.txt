Screening of a potential coxsackievirus B4 vaccine strain through analysis of biological features
Authors: Wei Guo, Xiang Li, Junwei Chen, Zhaoyang Chu, Jiansheng Liu, Shengbo Song, Changzeng Feng, Shaohui Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoxsackievirus B4 (CVB4) is a major pathogen involved in hand, foot, and mouth disease as well as aseptic meningitis, and is associated with type 1 diabetes mellitus. Currently, no effective antiviral drugs or vaccines are available for CVB4 infections. Drawing on successful experience with inactivated vaccines for enterovirus 71 and poliovirus, we developed an inactivated CVB4 vaccine based on Vero cells. The highly pathogenic CVB4 virus strain 140/CHN/2019, isolated from Vero cells and classified as genotype D, reflecting the circulating strains in China. Through three cycles of plaque assays and successive passages, the clone strain KM140-G01 could adapt to Vero cells and grew to a titer exceeding 10⁷ CCID50/mL. The experimentally inactivated CVB4 vaccine, developed from KM140-G01, effectively elicited high levels of neutralizing antibodies in BALB/c mice, with titers ranging from Log2 7.2 to 11.0 and effectively neutralized various clinical isolates (> Log210). Maternal antibodies provided 100% protection against a lethal challenge with CVB4 isolates in lactating BALB/c mice. Therefore, the KM140-G01 strain in this study is a viable new candidate for the development of a coxsackievirus B4 vaccine. Introduction

Coxsackievirus B4 (CVB4) is classified within the coxsackievirus B subgroup of the enterovirus B cluster, which is part of the enterovirus genus in the family Picornaviridae, genus Enterovirus1. CVB4 is transmitted primarily through the fecal–oral route but can also be transmitted through the respiratory route. Although CVB4 infection is usually mild or asymptomatic, it can cause potentially deadly conditions such as meningitis, encephalitis, pneumonia, and myocarditis2–9. Additionally, CVB4 is closely associated with type 1 diabetes10,11. From 2004 to 2016, a hospital in Italy assessed seropositivity rates and neutralizing antibody titer tests in 2,459 participants and observed a CVB4 seropositivity rate of 45.2%12. In China, CVB4 has been frequently detected in hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) cases since the 2008 HFMD outbreaks. Moreover, CVB4 was the primary causative agent of HFMD in Shandong in 2011, and in Yunnan in 2013, 2015, and 201613–16. Because no effective antiviral drugs or vaccines are currently available for CVB4 infection, the development of a CVB4 vaccine is urgently needed17.

Currently, vaccines developed with Vero cells have been authorized for preventing poliovirus and enterovirus 71 (EV-A71)18,19. Several studies have explored CVB4 vaccine development. Davydova et al. have reported that a formalin-inactivated CVB4-E2 vaccine induces both humoral and cellular immune responses, thereby delaying diabetes in non-obese diabetic mice20. Stone et al. have demonstrated that formalin-inactivated CVB4, either alone or in a hexavalent formulation with inactivated CVB1–6, elicits neutralizing antibodies and protects NOD mice against acute CVB4 infection21. Wang et al. have found that β-propiolactone-inactivated CVB4 vaccines elicit high titers of neutralizing antibodies and protect mice against lethal CVB4 infection22. Vaccines vary in their ability to cross-neutralize different genotypes of the same serotype enterovirus. The cross-neutralization among different CVB4 genotypes remains unknown and poses a challenge in CVB4 vaccine development.

To address this issue, we sought to develop a novel inactivated CVB4 vaccine by screening prevalent CVB4 strains in China. Since the isolation of the CVB4 prototype strain JVB in 1951, CVB4 isolates have diverged into five VP1 genotypes. Chinese CVB4 isolates belong primarily to genotypes D and E. In this study, the KM140 strain, belonging to the D genotype, was preliminarily selected from 29 CVB4 strains, on the basis of its biological characteristics. After three rounds of plaque purification, the KM140-G01 clone was successfully isolated. Subsequently, 15 serial passages of the KM140-G01 clone were conducted to evaluate its genetic stability. The inactivated CVB4 was then prepared as a vaccine antigen, and its immunogenicity and protective efficacy were evaluated in BALB/c mice. The vaccine elicited potent neutralizing antibodies and protected against lethal CVB4 infection in mice.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study complies with the relevant standards for animal experimentation facilities outlined in the People’s Republic of China’s “Environmental and Facility Requirements for Laboratory Animals” (GB14925-2023). Animal husbandry management and experimental procedures adhere to regulations such as the “Yunnan Provincial Regulations on Laboratory Animal Management,” with every effort made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used. A one-week acclimatization period was implemented prior to initiating any experiments. All mice were provided by the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences [SCXK(Dian)K2022-0002] and housed within the experimental animal center facilities of the same institute [SYXK(Dian)K2022-0006]. They were maintained in individually ventilated cages with free access to water and food. All experimental protocols complied with the Chinese Guidelines for the Welfare and Ethics Review of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (DWSP202108001). When performing experiments on newborn mice, procedures were conducted as gently as possible using the least traumatic instruments, and the mice were returned to their mothers promptly after the procedures were completed. At the end of the experiments, all mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation.

Cells and viruses

CVB4 isolates (3E3/CHN/2019, 4E2/CHN/2019, 55E2/CHN/2019, 56E2/CHN/2019, 77E2/CHN/2019, 88E2/CHN/2019, 94E2/CHN/2019, 95E2/CHN/2019, 98E2/CHN/2019, 100E2/CHN/2019, 105E2/CHN/2019, 107E2/CHN/2019, 120E2/CHN/2019, 122E2/CHN/2019, 129E2/CHN/2019, 135E2/CHN/2019, 137E2/CHN/2019, 157E2/CHN/2015, 158E2/CHN/2019, 170E2/CHN/2019, 171E2/CHN/2019, 175E2/CHN/2019, 176E2/CHN/2019, 177E2/CHN/2019, 140/CHN/2019, 141V4/YN/CHN/2018,138V4/YN/CHN/2019, 136V4/YN/CHN/2019 and A155/YN/CHN/2009) were stored at the IMB, CAMS, and PUMC (Kunming, China) (Table S1).The propagation of viruses in Vero cells was performed in minimal essential medium (MEM) containing 2 mML-glutamine, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Vero cells (Vero 76, ATCC CRL-1587) were provided by the IMB, CAMS, and PUMC (Kunming, China). The infectious titers of the virus were determined with the Reed–Muench method23.

RT-PCR, sequencing, and typing

The VP1 sequence and entire genome of CVB4 were amplified as previously described24. Briefly, a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) was used to isolate viral RNA from the supernatants of infected cells. Amplification of partial VP1 sequences and the complete genome was performed with a PrimeScript One Step RT-PCR Kit Ver. 2 (Takara, Dalian, China). Subsequently, sequencing was conducted with an ABI 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). The primers used for amplification and sequencing are detailed in Table S2.

Phylogenetic evaluation and sequence alignment

Phylogenetic analysis of 69 CVB4 strains, including 40 obtained from GenBank, 29 isolated in this study was performed through the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 7.0, on the basis of the full VP1 sequence (852 nt). Sequence alignment was performed in Geneious 9.0.2.

Plaque purification

The CVB4 strains were purified in Vero cells via three rounds of plaque assays. Briefly, a series of tenfold dilutions of the aforementioned viruses (from 10–2 to 10–6) was added to Vero cells in six-well plates and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 1 h. Subsequently, each well was supplemented with 2 mL MEM containing 1.5% methylcellulose, then incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After plaques were observed, they were carefully picked and introduced into 96-well plates with Vero cells. After samples exhibiting a cytopathic effect (CPE) were further identified as CVB4 by molecular typing, the next round of plaque purification was performed.

Genetic stability

The CVB4 clonal strains were cultured 15 times in Vero cells, and its genetic stability was evaluated according to nucleotide and amino acid sequence changes, as well as infectious titer. Specifically, viral RNA from P1, P5, P10, and P15 was extracted, and the entire genome was amplified and sequenced with the aforementioned method. The infectious titer was subsequently determined.

Animal infection

Three-day-old BALB/c mice were intracerebrally injected with the CVB4 virus strain at doses of 103.5, 104.5, or 105.5 CCID50 per mouse. Control mice were inoculated with 30 μL of supernatant from uninfected cells per mouse and housed separately from mice in the infected group. All mice were monitored for 7 days. Clinical symptoms were scored as follows: 0, healthy; 1, lethargic with decreased activity; 2, emaciated; 3, showing limb weakness, sparse fur, kyphosis, and/or movement difficulties; and 4, critically ill or deceased25.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry analyses

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed according to previously reported methods25. Briefly, 3-day-old BALB/c mice were inoculated with KM140-G01 P5 at 103.5, 104.5, or 105.5 CCID50 per mouse. The mice were euthanized after their clinical scores reached 4. Upon dissection of suckling mice that have reached a clinical score of 4, the tissues were fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution, followed by dehydration, embedding, and sectioning. The tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), then examined under a microscope to identify any histopathological changes.

IHC tissue sections were dewaxed, dehydrated, and boiled, then incubated with rabbit anti-CVB4 polyclonal antibodies provided by the IMB, CAMS, and PUMC (Kunming, China) at a 1:1,000 dilution overnight at 4 °C. After washing, goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 50 min at room temperature. Signal detection was performed with a histochemical DAB coloring kit (Servicebio, China). After hematoxylin staining and drying, the sealed sections were analyzed under a microscope.

Viral loads in tissues of KM140-G01-infected mice

The viral loads in various tissues of mice infected with KM140-G01 at 105.5 CCID50 were measured as previously described26. In brief, after the clinical score reached 3, tissues (pancreas, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, small intestine, forelimb muscle, hindlimb muscle, and brain tissue) from infected mice were separated, weighed, and ground. TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) was used to extract total RNA from each tissue. Viral RNA levels were quantified with a One Step PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit for Perfect Real Time PCR (Takara, Dalian, China). Primers for CVB4vp1-F3 (5′-TGTCCTGTTTCCACTGCTGTT-3′), CVB4vp1-R3 (5′-GAAGAATCAGTGGAGCGTGC-3′), and CVB4vp1-P3 (CTCTGAACAAATCC5´FAM 3´MGB) were used (Table S3). Additionally, a recombinant plasmid containing the CVB4 VP1 gene was constructed and linearized. CVB4 VP1 RNA obtained through in vitro transcription was used to assess CVB4 copy number. The viral load in each tissue was measured in three animals for each replicate.

Preparation of an inactivated CVB4 whole-virus vaccine

Vero cells were inoculated with the CVB4 KM140-G01 P5 strain at a MOI of 0.01. When the CPE reached 90%–95%, the cell culture was harvested, mixed with formalin solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) at a 4,000:1 volume ratio, and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. After inactivation, the virus underwent three blind passages in Vero cells, with no CPE observed, suggesting successful inactivation. After removal of cellular debris by centrifugation (4 °C, 8,000 rpm, 30 min), the inactivated supernatant was concentrated 100-fold with ultrafiltration membranes (100 kDa PES membrane, Millipore, USA) and purified with a Capto Core 400 gel column (Cytiva, Massachusetts, USA). The antigen concentration of the vaccine was determined with a Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Rockford, Illinois, USA). The vaccine was prepared by dilution of the antigen with phosphate buffer and addition of aluminum adjuvant.

Neutralizing antibody assays

Neutralizing antibody titer determination was performed as previously described25. Briefly, 4-week-old female BALB/c mice were divided into four groups, with ten mice per group, and administered 3 μg, 5 μg, 10 μg, and 20 μg CVB4 inactivated vaccine through subcutaneous injection on days 0, 14, and 28, respectively. In accordance with prior practice, serum samples were collected on days 0, 14, 28 and 49. Each negative control was administered the same volume of PBS or 0.35 mg/mL aluminum adjuvant. All mice underwent daily monitoring for any clinical signs.

After inactivation at 56 °C for 30 min, the serum was serially diluted two-fold in MEM. Subsequently, a 96-well plate was used to mix 50 μL diluted serum and 50 μL CVB4 at 100 CCID50. After incubation for 2 h, 104 Vero cells were added to each well. Vero cells and antiserum were used as controls. Concurrently, for viral back-titration, a tenfold dilution of 50 µl CVB4 virus (100 CCID50) was prepared in eight replicates per dilution and mixed with 50 µL MEM. Next, 104 Vero cells were added to each well. After 7 days, the experiment was considered valid if the virus infectious titers were between 32 and 320 CCID50/50 µL. The neutralizing antibody titer was determined according to the maximum dilution that prevented 50% of CPE.

Evaluation of the protective efficacy of maternal antibodies

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with 20 μg inactivated CVB4 vaccine, or an equal volume of PBS or aluminum adjuvant (five mice per group). The inactivated vaccine was prepared by diluting the antigen with phosphate buffer and adding aluminum hydroxide adjuvant (2024-01-S, Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, China) to a final aluminum concentration of 0.35 mg/mL. When the female mice became pregnant, the mice in the vaccine group were injected with a booster vaccine. After birth, 3-day-old BALB/c mice were inoculated via intracerebral injection with 105.5 CCID50 of the homologous CVB4 KM140-G01, 141V4/YN/CHN/2018, 138V4/YN/CHN/2019, or 136V4/YN/CHN/2019 strains, and clinical scores and body weight were monitored over the next 7 days.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare the difference of CVB4 viral load between each tissue with the liver or brain. Neutralizing antibody titers (Log₂-transformed) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Data are presented as mean ± SD. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Accession numbers

In this study, the VP1 sequences of the 25 CVB4 strains and the full genome sequences for KM140-G01 generations 1, 5, 10, and 15 have been submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PQ553590–PQ553614 and PQ553586–PQ553589, respectively. Additionally, the VP1 sequences for the remaining four strains—141V4/YN/CHN/2018, 138V4/YN/CHN/2019, 136V4/YN/CHN/2019, and A155/YN/CHN/2009—are available under accession numbers PQ473509–PQ473511 and KM890276, respectively.

Results

Primary characterization of viral isolates

Based on full VP1 sequences, phylogenetic analysis showed that the 28 CVB4 isolates from this study, along with one isolate from Tianjin, China, clustered with epidemic strains from outside mainland China within genotype D (Fig. 1). In contrast, all previously characterized Chinese isolates, including A155/YN/CHN/2009, belonged to genotype E. These findings indicate that the genotype D epidemic strain of CVB4 has emerged in China and is tending to outnumber Genotype E. Among these isolates, the complete VP1 sequences showed nucleotide and amino acid sequence homology of 94.0%–100% and 97.8%–100%, respectively. In addition, they showed 78.3%–78.5% nucleotide similarity and 85.7%–86.4% amino acid similarity with the full VP1 sequence of the CVB4 prototype JVB strain (X05690).

The symbol ▲ indicates the CVB4 prototype strain. ● indicates the strain studied in this work.

Following adaptation in Vero cells, four CVB4 virus strains (140/CHN/2019, 141V4/YN/CHN/2018, 138V4/YN/CHN/2019, and 136V4/YN/CHN/2019) exhibited distinct cytopathic effects within 3–5 days and achieved infectious titers greater than 10⁶·⁰ CCID₅₀/mL. After three rounds of plaque purification in Vero cells, each of the four strains was passaged to the fifth generation. Among them, strains 140/CHN/2019 P5 and 141V4/YN/CHN/2018 P5 reached titers of 107.25 CCID₅₀/mL, whereas 136V4/YN/CHN/2019 P5 and 138V4/YN/CHN/2019 P5 reached only 10 6.25 CCID₅₀/mL. The latter two strains were excluded from further study due to their lower titers, which are suboptimal for downstream purification and yield.

Genetic stability of the CVB4 strains

The two CVB4 strains (KM140-G01, originating from 140/CHN/2019, and 141V4/YN/CHN/2018) were subsequently passaged to the fifteenth generation, respectively. The analysis of the whole-genome sequences revealed that the KM140-G01 strain exhibited six mutations located at positions 307, 447, 1166, 4251, 4342, and 4390 of the CDS region; among them, three amino acid mutations (103G → R, 389 K → R and 1448 S → A) were observed in the CDS region in the P5 generation, and were located in the VP2, VP3 and 3A regions, respectively. In the P15 generation, four amino acid mutations (103R → G, 149W → G, 1417S → R, and 1464Y → H) were observed in the CDS region, and were located in the VP2, 2C, and 3A regions, respectively. However, during passaging, KM140-G01 exhibited fewer mutation sites compared to strain 141V4/YN/CHN/2018 (Table 1). Structural analysis revealed that residues VP2 103 and 149 are located inside the capsid structure and are not exposed on the surface of the viral particle. Thus, these mutations are unlikely to participate directly in antibody recognition. In contrast, VP3 residue 389 is located in a surface-exposed region (Fig. S1). However, the K389R at this position is conservative, because K → R substitution generally preserve the overall protein fold while mainly affecting local electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding27. Since the neutralizing epitope of CVB4 has not been identified, it cannot be ruled out this VP3 K → R substitution may affect antigenicity. Nevertheless, the overall surface topology and charge character of this region appear to remain largely unchanged. And the infectious titers of the KM140-G01 strain at passages P1, P5, and P10 were 107.25 CCID50/mL, and the infectious titer at P15 was 107.375 CCID50/mL. The high viral titer indicating high virus yield. Thus, KM140-G01 was selected as the candidate strain for CVB4 vaccine development.

Pathogenicity in suckling mice

A pathogenicity test was conducted on 3-day-old suckling mice receiving the three KM140-G01 doses (103.5, 104.5, and 105.5 CCID50/30 μL) and a control group. The mice were monitored daily for weight, clinical score, and survival rate (Fig. 2A). The 105.5 CCID50 dose experimental group showed a decrease in body weight after infection; symptoms appeared on day 1 and were relatively severe. On day 2, the mice were either near death or deceased, and the survival time of this dose group was 2–3 days. The 104.5 CCID50 and 103.5 CCID50 dose groups showed little weight gain and poorer growth than the control group; symptoms in those dose groups appeared 1–2 days post-infection. The survival time of the KM140-G01 dose groups did not exceed 7 days. KM140-G01 exhibited pathogenicity in all three dose groups, with a mortality rate of 100%.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses of the KM140-G01 strain

The tissues from the experimental groups (the KM140-G01 strain at doses of 103.5, 104.5, and 105.5 CCID50) and the control group were subjected to HE staining to observe pathological changes. In the experimental groups receiving strain KM140-G01 at all three doses, the following severe pathological changes were observed in the lungs, brain, and liver (Fig. 2B, Fig. S1). The lungs showed localized alveolar septal thickening (black arrow), mild congestion and bruising of the alveolar septa (red arrow), localized alveolar luminal dilatation (yellow arrow), and scattered inflammatory cell infiltration (blue arrow). The brain exhibited cellular degeneration and necrosis in the temporal lobe region (black arrow), localized scattered hemorrhage (red arrow), and scattered inflammatory cell infiltration (blue arrow). The liver tissue showed turbid swelling of hepatocytes (black arrow), hydropic degeneration of hepatocytes (brown arrow), localized dilatation of the central vein (red arrow), localized hyperplasia of small bile ducts (purple arrow), localized regeneration of hepatocytes (green arrow), and scattered inflammatory cell infiltration (blue arrow).

IHC was performed on tissues from the suckling mice infected with three doses (CCID50: 103.5, 104.5, and 105.5) of strain KM140-G01, respectively. CVB4 antigen distribution was observed in the lungs, brain, and liver of lactating mice in the 105.5 CCID50 dose group (Fig. 2C), whereas no clear CVB4 antigen distribution was observed in other tissues. A similar distribution of CVB4 antigen was observed in the tissues of lactating mice in the 103.5 and 104.5 CCID50 dose groups, except that the amount of antigen was relatively low (Fig. S2). No changes in tissue pathology or viral antigen detection were found in the control groups (Fig. 3).

Tissue viral loads in KM140-G01-infected mice

The viral load in tissues from 105.5 CCID50 KM140-G01 -infected suckling mice was determined with the CVB4 TaqMan one-step RT-qPCR detection method. The brain and liver had significantly higher viral loads than the other tissues (P < 0.01; viral loads exceeding 108.0 log10 copies/mg). The viral load in the heart, lungs, upper limbs, and lower limbs was approximately 107.0 log10 copies/mg, whereas the viral load in the pancreas, spleen, kidney, and small intestine was lower than observed in other tissues and did not exceed 106.0 log10 copies/mg (Fig. 4).

Immunogenicity evaluation of inactivated CVB4 vaccine

Immunogenicity analysis indicated neutralizing antibody levels of Log2 7.2–9.6 at 21 days after three immunizations in the 3 μg, 5 μg, and 10 μg groups; Log2 11.0 at 21 days after three immunizations in the 20 μg group; and Log2 9.2 at 14 days after two immunizations in the 20 μg group (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the experimental groups did not exhibit any noticeable clinical symptoms during vaccination. Cross-neutralization experiments indicated a neutralizing antibody potency greater than Log2 10.0 against other CVB4 strains (141V4/YN/CHN/2018, 138V4/YN/CHN/2019, and 136V4/YN/CHN/2019, belonging to the D genotype, and A155/YN/CHN/2009, belonging to the E genotype and isolated from a patient with encephalitis.

Protective effect of maternal antibodies in neonatal mice against lethal CVB4 challenge (KM140-G01). Body weight, mean clinical score, and survival rate were monitored in three-day-old BALB/c mice following intracerebral infection with a lethal dose (105.5 CCID50/30μL) of KM140-G01.

Maternal antibody protection

The suckling mice in the experimental group were born to 8-week-old female BALB/c mice vaccinated with the CVB4 vaccine, whereas the control group suckling mice were born to maternal groups administered PBS or aluminum adjuvant. All mice in the control group died within 3 days after administration of a lethal dose (105.5 CCID50) of strains KM140-G01, 141V4/YN/CHN/2018, 138V4/YN/CHN/2019, and 136V4/YN/CHN/2019. In contrast, the experimental group exhibited no noticeable clinical symptoms post-challenge, continued to grow normally, and achieved a 100% protection rate (Fig. 5, only KM140-G01 displayed). Because the challenge doses used were fully lethal in all control animals, complete survival of pups born to immunized dams represents a stringent demonstration of protective efficacy.

Discussion

Although CVB4 has become an important pathogen, no effective drugs or vaccines are available. Several studies on CVB4 vaccine development have suggested that use of inactivated vaccines might be an effective approach19–22. However, the strains used for vaccine production have been either non-Chinese strains or RD cell-adapted strains. Variations in cross-neutralization responses have been identified among EV-A71 genotypes. The vaccine for the EV-A71 C4 genotype has the strongest cross-neutralizing antibody response against homologous C4 genotypes but is less potent against heterologous genotypes28. Chinese CVB4 strains exhibit differences in their nucleotide and amino acid sequences compared to isolates from other countries. Furthermore, RD cell-adapted strains are unsuitable for human vaccine manufacturing. Therefore, creation of a new inactivated CVB4 vaccine that is more efficient at preventing CVB4 infection in China was necessary.

CVB4 has evolved into five genotypes since its prototype strain JVB was first isolated in 1951. Chinese CVB4 isolates are of genotypes D (2015 and 2019) and E (2000–2019). The 28 isolates in this study were of genotype D, thus indicating the evolution of Chinese CVB4 isolates. This research on the characteristics of various CVB4 clinical isolates in China provides essential insights for identifying a CVB4 vaccine candidate. Inactivated vaccine development relies on viral strains that are well-adapted and genetically stable, and exhibit high pathogenicity. Vero cells are a cellular matrix that can be used in the production of human vaccines. The 140/CHN/2019 strain, adapted to Vero cells and exhibiting the highest viral titer, moderate virulence, and the most characteristic CPE, was selected for further plaque purification. After 15 consecutive passages of the KM140-G1 clone, originating from 140/CHN/2019, the infection titers at passages P1, P5, P10, and P15 were ≥ 107.25 CCID50/mL. The VP1 protein, the primary antigenic site, showed no amino acid mutations. Furthermore, the nucleotide and amino acid sequence homology across the entire genome was 99.6%–99.7% and 99.5%–99.7%, respectively, among the above generations of viruses. In addition, the neutralizing antibodies against KM140-G1 P6 could effectively neutralized P5, P10, and P15 viruses, with neutralizing antibody titers ranging from 1:576 to 1:768. Previous studies also suggest that individual amino acid mutations generally do not impair immunogenicity29,30. Therefore, KM140-G01 showed favorable adaptability to Vero cells and genetic stability, and might serve as an ideal vaccine candidate. However, the potential impact of these variations on viral pathogenicity warrants further investigation.

Immunogenicity is crucial in the development of vaccines. Antiserum from mice immunized with the KM140-G01-based inactivated CVB4 vaccine cross-neutralized both homologous (genotype D) and heterologous (genotype E) CVB4 strains with titers > 1:1024, consistent with prior findings22. This lays the foundation for further vaccine development.

Three-day-old BALB/c mice received intracerebral injections of CVB4 strains at a dose of 105.5 CCID50 per mouse. Decreased body weight and activity, or death, were observed in the suckling mice. The KM140-G01 strain demonstrated a preference for the lung, brain, and liver. No changes were observed in the control groups. Previous studies have shown that meningitis and severe respiratory disease can also be caused by CVB4 (2,4), thus further supporting an association between CVB4 infection and meningitis, as well as severe respiratory disease. This experiment provides an effective means of evaluating the in vivo protective effects of CVB4 vaccines.

Adult female mice receiving the inactivated CVB4 vaccine produced protective antibodies, which were passed to their offspring and protected against deadly doses of CVB4. No histopathological alterations were observed in the brain, liver, and lungs, whereas evident pathological changes were detected in these tissues in control mice (data not shown). Furthermore, no adverse reactions associated with the vaccine have been observed thus far. These findings suggest that our experimentally inactivated CVB4 vaccine might protect against meningitis, severe respiratory illnesses, and hepatitis due to CVB4 infection. In this study, BALB/c mice were chosen to replicate the acute infection model of CVB4 strain KM140-G01. However, no pathological damage or viral antigen distribution was observed in the pancreatic tissue. In this study, viral load in tissues was detected by qRT-PCR, a highly sensitive method that reflects the distribution of CVB4 particles via viremia. Histopathology and IHC, however, assess tissue damage and viral localization, reflecting the terminal stage of infection but with relatively lower sensitivity. The results indicate that intracranial inoculation of CVB4 induced significant systemic viremia, with viral nucleic acid detected in multiple tissues including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, small intestine, brain, and limb muscles. Further analysis suggests that although viremia was present in cardiac and limb muscle tissues, it did not cause obvious tissue damage or substantial viral replication within the observation period, leading to the discrepancy between viral load detection and histopathological/IHC findings. In addition, this might be associated with factors such as the tissue tropism preference of the KM140-G01 virus strain, the duration of the infection state, and the mouse strain, which require further investigation in subsequent experimental studies.

The limitations of this study are as follows: While numerous literature reports suggest that intracerebral injection, similar to other administration routes, does not interfere with outcome assessment31,32, employing a universal inoculation route or one that mimics human infection pathways would generate results that more accurately mirror the human condition. Furthermore, the use of neonatal mice, which have immature immune systems, coupled with the difficulties in their husbandry, may also impact vaccine evaluation results. These factors necessitate refinement in future research.

In summary, we screened and obtained an ideal CVB4 inactivated vaccine candidate, KM140-G01, and demonstrated its favorable safety, immunogenicity, and genetic stability, thus laying groundwork for further CVB4 vaccine development.
