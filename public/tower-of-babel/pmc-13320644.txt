Barriers to Nutrition Security in the US
Authors: Caroline E. Owens, Ronit Ridberg, Julia Reedy Sharib, Hope C. Craig, Dariush Mozaffarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study of US adults assesses associations among barriers to healthy eating, sociodemographic characteristics, and health conditions driven by poor diet.

Introduction

Poor nutrition is a leading driver of morbidity and mortality, accounting for an estimated 45% of cardiovascular disease mortality, 70% of new diabetes cases, and $1.1 trillion in annual health care spending and lost productivity in the US.1 Limited access to healthy foods contributes to poor nutrition, and food insecurity—a lack of access to sufficient food for an active and healthy life—has long been used as a proxy of that access.2 Food insecurity conceptually encompasses multiple dimensions, including food availability, access, utilization, stability, and nutritional quality; however, common measures primarily capture experiences related to financial strain and food quantity.3,4,5 Furthermore, the strength of the inverse association between food insecurity and diet quality varies by race and ethnicity in the US.6,7,8 Consequently, interventions to address food insecurity have largely targeted financial barriers, with inconsistent effects on diet quality and health outcomes.9

In contrast, nutrition security emphasizes “consistent access, availability, and affordability of foods and beverages that promote well-being and prevent and, if needed, treat disease.”8,10,11 Barriers to healthy eating extend beyond cost; for instance, family and taste preferences may limit acceptance of healthy foods; limited nutrition knowledge, time constraints, and cooking equipment may affect people’s abilities to select and prepare healthy foods; and structural and environmental factors, such as food retail access and rurality, influence the availability and affordability of healthy foods.12,13 Although federal policy and public health efforts increasingly prioritize nutrition security,14 these barriers and their distribution across populations remain incompletely characterized.

The 2-item Nutrition Security Screener (NSS) was recently validated among 20 523 US adults.15 Prior validation focused on item 1, which assesses presence or absence of nutrition insecurity. In contrast, item 2 provides more granular clinically and practically relevant information on which specific barriers contribute to the risk of difficulties with eating a healthy diet. The current study extends the prior validation efforts through analyses of these barriers and their associations with each other and with food and nutrition security, sociodemographic factors, and health conditions, which to our knowledge have not been systematically examined. Understanding how factors that impede healthy food consumption co-occur and vary across populations may inform clinical, policy, and systems-level strategies to equitably improve diet-related health. Our primary aim in this study was to explore how 13 barriers to healthy eating captured in item 2 of the NSS co-occur and whether they are associated with sociodemographic characteristics and health outcomes in a large survey of US adults who provided data on barriers to nutrition security.

Methods

Setting and Population

This cross-sectional study included US adults from a national survey on perceptions of Food Is Medicine,16 fielded February to April 2023. Participants were recruited and surveyed through the Qualtrics panel service, with oversampling among people with annual household incomes less than $50 000. Sampling strategies and results are described elsewhere and in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1.16 Inclusion criteria were age of 18 years or older, ability to read and understand English, and internet access. Following a written description of the study, participants were asked to indicate their informed consent electronically. The study was approved by the Tufts University institutional review board and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Nutrition Security Screener

The first NSS item asks, “Thinking about the last 6 months, how hard was it for you or your household to regularly get and eat healthy foods that support your well-being?” Likert-scale responses ranged from 1 (“very hard”) to 5 (“not hard at all”). Responses of “very hard” (1), “hard” (2), or “somewhat hard” (3) were defined and validated as a positive screen for nutrition insecurity. Full item wording is provided in eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1.

The second NSS item assesses barriers to healthy eating: “There are many reasons why people can’t eat healthy foods as much as they think they should. In the last 12 months, how often have each of the following limited you or your household’s ability to eat healthier meals?” Participants indicated whether each of 13 predefined barriers occurred “never,” “sometimes,” or “often” or was “not applicable”; 1 open-ended response (“other: please specify”) allowed participants to list additional reasons. The barriers reflected a range of potential challenges. Given that at least 1 response to a barrier item was provided by everyone in the analytic sample, nonresponses to other barriers were treated as negative (never true). In this article, barriers are presented in abbreviated form, with full text for each item shown in eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

Covariates and Outcomes

Covariates included self-reported age, gender, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, and annual household income (eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1), selected a priori based on their established associations with food security and potential to confound associations between nutrition security barriers and health outcomes.17 Gender categories were female, male, nonbinary or third gender, and prefer not to say. Race and ethnicity categories were American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Other Pacific Islander; Asian or other; Hispanic/Latinx; non-Hispanic/Latinx Black (hereafter, Black); and non-Hispanic/Latinx White (hereafter, White). “Other” race and ethnicity was not broken down further in accordance with 2020 US Census racial groups and was grouped with “Asian” due to small sample sizes of each in the purposive sampling target. Food security was assessed using the 2-item Hunger Vital Sign (HVS)18; participants were classified as food insecure if they indicated that either or both HVS items were sometimes or often true. This classification was chosen because of its wide use in clinical practice and its high sensitivity.18 Primary health outcomes were health conditions—self-reported physician diagnoses of cancer (excluding skin cancers), type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, obesity, and stroke.

Statistical Analysis

The survey was intended to be broadly generalizable rather than to generate nationally representative estimates; as such, no survey weights were applied. Cronbach α was calculated to assess interrelatedness of the barriers included in item 2 of the NSS.19 To examine barrier co-occurrence, covariance matrices and pairwise tetrachoric correlations were evaluated. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to empirically characterize patterns of co-occurrence among the 13 prespecified barriers, with factors retained based on eigenvalues greater than 1; further details on EFA are provided in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1.

Multivariable logistic regression explored associations between demographic characteristics and each barrier, adjusting for food security status. Age was treated as a continuous variable using the median value within each category. Annual household income was recategorized into the following: lower income (<$25 000 and $25 000-$49 999), middle income ($50 000-$99 999), and higher income ($100 000-$150 000 and >$150 000). Given the lack of systematic research on barriers to nutrition security across the US population, these analyses were considered hypothesis generating. The false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.20

To assess evidence of criterion validity, we analyzed barrier factor scores as independent variables in multivariable-adjusted regressions of diet-related diseases. Regression analyses were then performed using binary, ordinal, and severe barrier scores constructed to capture overall exposure, frequency, and persistence of barriers (details are in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1). All analyses were conducted from March 18 to November 9, 2025, using R, version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing); extension packages are in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 1. Two-tailed P < .05 was considered significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of 3009 survey respondents, 9 (0.3%) did not answer any items about barriers to healthy eating and were excluded, resulting in 3000 adults who were evaluated. Of those, 2785 (92.8%) responded to at least 8 barrier items; more than half (1588 [52.9%]) responded to all 13 items, 1207 [40.2%]) responded to 8 to 12, 177 (5.9%) responded to 3 to 7, and 28 (0.9%) responded to 1 or 2. A total of 1518 participants (50.6%) identified as female, 1466 (48.9%) as male, 15 (0.5%) as nonbinary or third gender, and 1 (0.0%) preferred not to say. Fifty participants (1.7%) identified as American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Other Pacific Islander; 148 (4.9%) as Asian or other race and ethnicity; 433 (14.4%) as Black or African American; 466 (15.5%) as Hispanic/Latinx; and 1903 (63.4%) as White (Table 1). Most (1983 [66.1%]) were between ages 18 and 49 years. For about one-third (1051 [35.0%]), the highest level of educational attainment was high school graduate, and most (1564 [52.1%]) reported annual household income below $50 000. Overall, 1253 (41.8%) screened as food insecure and 1317 (43.9%) screened as nutrition insecure, with only moderate correlation between these measures (r = 0.63).

Reported Barriers

Adults with nutrition insecurity (NSS item 1) experienced a mean (SD) of 7.8 (3.0) barriers to healthy eating, whereas those with nutrition security reported a mean (SD) of 4.4 (3.2) barriers. Across participants, 564 (18.8%) reported 10 or more barriers, 1242 (41.4%) reported 5 to 9, 1049 (35.0%) reported 1 to 4, and 145 (4.8%) reported none. The barrier subitems demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.84). Data on response completion across barriers are shown in eTable 2 in Supplement 1. Overall, younger participants, males, and individuals experiencing food or nutrition insecurity completed a greater number of barrier survey items than older participants, females, and individuals with food or nutrition security. Frequencies of reported barriers to healthy eating, by nutrition security status, are shown in eTable 3 in Supplement 1. Among 1317 participants with nutrition insecurity (43.9%), the most frequently reported barriers were cost (1273 [96.7%]), family traditions (1080 [82.0%]), limited choices in stores (948 [72.0%]), and store location (916 [69.6%]). Among participants with nutrition security (1639 [54.6%]), the most frequent barriers were family traditions (1134 [69.2%]), cost (1121 [68.4%]), and taste (738 [45.0%]). Most open-ended responses (n = 218) were aligned with 1 of the predefined items, though some highlighted additional challenges, such as lack of social support (“I do not have a support system to encourage me to eat healthy”) and health conditions affecting diet (“Gastrointestinal medical condition makes eating healthy foods difficult”).

Clustering of Barriers

Most barriers were only modestly intercorrelated (mean [SD] r = 0.45 [0.13]). Correlations were strongest between time to cook and time to shop (r = 0.86), cooking knowledge and nutrition knowledge (r = 0.78), store location and choices in stores (r = 0.70), and store location and transportation (r = 0.68) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). EFA suggested a 2-factor solution explaining 61.4% of the total variance (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Items with strong loading on factor 1 included store location (factor loading coefficient, 0.69), transportation (0.64), choices in stores (0.55), and physical function (0.47). Cooking equipment exhibited cross-loading on both factors (0.52 on factor 1 and 0.35 on factor 2), as did time to shop (0.35 on factor 1 and 0.53 on factor 2). Items with strong loadings on factor 2 included cooking knowledge (0.67), time to cook (0.65), nutrition knowledge (0.57), and taste (0.52). Cost and family tradition did not load strongly on either factor (coefficients <0.40).

Both nutrition insecurity and food insecurity were independently associated with higher factor 1 scores (nutrition insecurity: β, 0.40 [95% CI, 0.33-0.46]; food insecurity: β, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.51-0.64]) and factor 2 scores (nutrition insecurity: β, 0.22 [95% CI, 0.15-0.30]; food insecurity: β, 0.13 [95% CI, 0.05-0.21]) (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). Identifying as male (β, 0.10 [95% CI, 0.04-0.14]) and as Black (β, 0.09 [95% CI, 0.02-0.16]) were each associated with higher factor 1 scores. Higher income was associated with lower factor 1 scores (β, 0.16 [95% CI, −0.23 to −0.9]), while other gender and racial and ethnic identities, age, educational attainment, and middle income were not. In contrast, sociodemographic factors were generally not associated with factor 2 scores, except for slightly lower scores observed among Black adults (β, −0.11 [95% CI, −0.20 to −0.02]) and higher scores among those with higher income (β, 0.21 [95% CI, 0.12-0.30]) and middle income (β, 0.14 [95% CI, 0.05-0.23]) compared to those with lower income.

Nutrition Security, Food Security, and Reported Barriers

Associations among sociodemographic characteristics, food security, nutrition security, and barriers to healthy eating are summarized in Table 2 and eTable 4 in Supplement 1. Both nutrition insecurity and food insecurity were independently associated with higher odds of each barrier except taste (Table 2). Effect sizes (adjusted odds ratios [AORs]) were generally comparable between the constructs; however, presence of food insecurity was associated with higher odds of barriers related to physical function (AOR, 3.41; 95% CI, 2.70-4.32), cooking equipment (AOR, 2.98; 95% CI, 2.37-3.74), and transportation (AOR, 4.65; 95% CI, 3.70-5.87) compared with corresponding estimates for nutrition insecurity (physical function: AOR, 1.88 [95% CI, 1.49-2.37]; cooking equipment: AOR, 2.04 [95% CI, 1.63-2.57]; transportation: AOR, 2.09 [95% CI, 1.66-2.64]).

Barriers and Health Outcomes

Each 1 SD of higher scores for both factor 1 and factor 2 was associated with higher prevalence of heart disease, even after adjusting for food and nutrition security status (Table 3). Factor 1 was also associated with higher odds of type 2 diabetes (AOR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.14-1.66), stroke (AOR, 3.09; 95% CI, 2.10-4.60), and cancer (AOR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.05-1.80), whereas factor 2 was associated with higher odds of high cholesterol (AOR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.00-1.27) and obesity (AOR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.07-1.35).

Summing barrier scores (range, 0-13) resulted in a mean (SD) of 5.91 (3.57) for the binary score, 4.03 (2.71) for the ordinal score, and 2.16 (2.31) for the severe score (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). In multivariable-adjusted regression, associations between barrier scores and health conditions varied by nutrition security status (Table 4). Among adults with nutrition security, higher barrier scores were consistently associated with higher odds of type 2 diabetes (binary score AOR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04-1.16), heart disease (binary score AOR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.07-1.24), and obesity (binary score AOR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.04-1.14) but were not consistently associated with cancer, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or stroke. Among adults with nutrition insecurity, only heart disease (AOR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.03-1.22) and stroke (AOR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.02-1.25) were associated with higher binary scores. Ordinal and severe scoring approaches generally showed the highest AORs. For example, among individuals with nutrition security, each 1-unit increase in the ordinal score was associated with higher odds of type 2 diabetes (AOR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.09-1.28), heart disease (AOR, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.13-1.38), obesity (AOR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.06-1.21), and stroke (AOR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.03-1.48). Complete case analysis among the 1578 people (52.6%) who responded to all 13 barriers demonstrated consistent results across these scoring approaches (eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

Findings from this large national survey of US adults suggest that nutrition security, assessed by the NSS, can be characterized by number and co-occurrence of specific barriers. Barriers were more common among individuals experiencing nutrition insecurity and food insecurity, independently, compared with those not experiencing these states. In data-driven analyses, barriers clustered into 2 factors. Using summed scoring, barrier scores were consistently associated with increased prevalence of diet-related conditions, including type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and obesity, among individuals with nutrition security and with heart disease among individuals with nutrition insecurity. Odds of these conditions were generally highest for scoring of barriers that leveraged the full range of ordinal responses or the most severe responses.

Among individuals defined as having nutrition security by item 1 on the survey, a higher number of barriers was associated with diet-related diseases, suggesting that nutrition insecurity may still be present. Similarly, among individuals defined as having nutrition insecurity by item 1, a higher number of barriers was associated with higher odds of diet-related diseases, suggesting that the number of barriers may help differentiate the severity of nutrition insecurity. These results provide evidence of the construct and criterion validity of item 2 of the NSS and suggest that frequent barriers provide a complementary, additional indicator of the presence and severity of nutrition insecurity beyond the first item. Further prospective research is needed to refine understanding of these associations.

Our results highlight how barriers to nutrition security are differentially experienced across demographic strata. While considerable research has documented variation in food security by age, gender, race, ethnicity, income, and educational level,17,21,22 this study comprehensively assessed disparities in specific barriers to healthy eating, with actionable insights. For example, females were more likely to report cost as a barrier, suggesting that financial support programs may be particularly relevant, whereas males showed a higher need for interventions addressing nutrition assistance and nutrition knowledge. Similarly, compared with White adults, Black adults more frequently had transportation barriers but less frequently had barriers related to cost, while Hispanic/Latinx adults more frequently had barriers related to uncertainty about nutrition assistance eligibility and to family traditions. These findings align with prior evidence on population differences in perceptions of what constitutes healthy eating23,24; racial disparities in transportation access25; and challenges related to immigration, ethnicity, and language for Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program participation.26

Barrier scores were not associated with cancer regardless of nutrition security status. Higher barrier scores were associated with higher odds of other chronic conditions, and these associations were more consistent, among adults with nutrition security, particularly type 2 diabetes, obesity, and heart disease, whereas among adults with nutrition insecurity, higher scores were associated with heart disease. The independent association of summed barrier burden and barrier domain scores with several chronic conditions, even among participants screening as nutrition secure on NSS item 1, suggests that these barriers capture additional dimensions of access to a healthy diet associated with disease risk. These results call for more prospective research on whether and how to sum the data provided by both items of the NSS. For now, our results suggest that implementation and interpretation of both items separately is reasonable.

Efforts to refine the conceptualization and operationalization of nutrition security have emphasized constructs such as healthy diet and nutritional status,11 environmental circumstances, and psychosocial functioning,12 reflected in brief screeners and scales.27,28 To our knowledge, the NSS is the only brief tool that evaluates specific barriers, consistent with the wide array of constructs that may limit healthy eating.12

Our results suggest the NSS may help fill the call for practical tools to identify and assess dimensions of nutrition security associated with health outcomes. Incorporating the NSS into health screening within clinical settings could enable a more robust analysis of the relationship between nutrition security and health outcomes by linking responses to electronic medical record data. Identification of barriers may also be used to inform more targeted interventions, programs, and policies, thereby enhancing the practical utility of the NSS in clinical and public health contexts.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of potential limitations. The barriers were developed for the US context and may not be generalizable to other settings. Although derived from expert knowledge and published literature, these barriers may not capture all challenges to accessing and eating healthy foods; however, based on open-ended responses in this survey, these predefined items appear likely to capture many of the most common barriers. Our analyses should not be interpreted as strictly nationally representative but as reflecting experiences within a large, diverse, generalizable sample with intentional representation of US residents with lower incomes. While the NSS is intended to use a common recall period for both items, this national survey used a 6-month recall for item 1 and a 12-month recall for item 2. The different periods created temporal misalignment that may result in some misclassification when comparing responses, as some of the reported barriers may have preceded the 6-month status classification from item 1. Further testing of recall time frames is warranted. For people who endorsed at least some barriers, other missing responses were assumed to reflect the absence of barriers, but 92.8% of participants responded to at least 8 barriers, and sensitivity analyses were conducted.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that barriers to nutrition security are frequently co-occurring, cluster in specific ways, and are unevenly distributed across sociodemographic subgroups in the US, with higher burdens associated with adverse cardiometabolic conditions that are known to be driven by poor diet. The results suggest that the NSS may be a practical tool for identifying specific barriers to healthy eating, which may help guide more tailored, multicomponent clinical, community, and policy interventions to promote nutrition and health.
