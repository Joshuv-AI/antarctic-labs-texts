Morphological Characteristics and Significance of Bulliform Phytoliths in Haploid Rice
Authors: Xiangan Tang, Jue Yu, Fangming Mao, Song Yan, Houyuan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe morphology of bulliform phytoliths serves as a key proxy for tracing the origin and evolution of ancient rice cultivation in archaeobotanical research; however, the correlation between these morphological traits and the genetic background of rice is unclear. To the best of our knowledge, for the first time, this study demonstrates that chromosome ploidy level is a key genetic factor influencing rice phytolith morphology, based on a comparative analysis of fan-shaped bulliform phytoliths from haploid and diploid rice. Compared with diploid rice, we found that the bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice exhibited two significant differences: (1) an overall reduction in morphological dimensions (fan width HL = 34.8 μm, fan length VL = 37.6 μm), with dimensions reduced by ~ 18%, a decrease in the number of fish-scale facets (~ 6.7), and an extremely low proportion of bulliform phytoliths with ≥ 9 fish-scale facets (~ 7.0%); and (2) a 46.8% decrease in the ratio of fan face length to stalk length (B/A) of phytoliths (~ 0.58), with very few short-stalk types (accounting for 0.0%–1.5%) and a predominance of long-stalk types (accounting for ~ 86.6%). These results revealed the regulatory mechanism governing chromosome ploidy on plant cell silicification patterns, manifesting as both quantitative (reduced size and fewer facets) and qualitative (possible absence of certain morphotypes) changes. This study provides important empirical evidence and a theoretical foundation to bridge modern rice genetics and archaeobotany.Keywords: Haploid, Diploid, Rice, Bulliform phytoliths, Chromosome

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the most important food crops in the world. Investigating the trajectory of its origin, domestication, and subsequent dispersal is not only central to historical agricultural research, but also fundamental for understanding the development of farming civilizations in East and Southeast Asia. Current scholarly debates on the rice domestication process and the attributes of archaeological remains focus on three main issues: distinguishing wild from domesticated rice, differentiating japonica from indica, and understanding how human activities drive morphological changes in rice plants (Zhang and He 2015). The most commonly employed fossil remains as indicators in studies of rice origins and domestication traits include carbonized grains (Zhao 2014; Zhang 1999), spikelet bases (Deng et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2007, 2016a, b), and phytoliths (Lu et al. 1996, 2002; Huan et al. 2015; Gu et al. 2021; Zhao 1998). These three categories of morphological identification markers have contributed substantially to advancing research regarding the origins of rice agriculture (Lu 2017; Zhao 2018).

Rice is a typical silicon-accumulating plant that produces abundant and morphologically distinct phytoliths during its growth and development. In particular, bulliform, scooped bilobate, and double-peaked husk phytoliths, which are characteristic of the genus Oryza, possess significant taxonomic value (Wang and Lu 1993; Fuller et al. 2010; Piperno 2006, 2014; Zhao et al. 1998). Through quantitative analyses of its morphology and facet patterns, researchers have established a relatively mature and reliable set of criteria for identifying rice in an archaeological context. This methodological framework has successfully revealed the origins and early dispersal trajectories of rice agriculture across various regions, particularly in the middle and lower Yangtze River valley of China (Lu et al. 2002; Zhao 1998; Zhang et al. 2024; Zuo et al. 2017).

However, the current criteria for identifying rice phytoliths are derived from morphological studies of diploid (2n) wild and cultivated rice. Previous studies have demonstrated that phytolith morphology is influenced not only by species factors but also by plant developmental stage, environmental conditions (e.g., soil fertility and moisture), and the process of human domestication (Lu 2017; Tang et al. 2022b, 2026; Wang et al. 2019b). With respect to the relationship between phytoliths and genetic background, Zhang et al. (2019) reported that phytolith content was significantly higher in Lsi1 and Lsi2 wild-type rice than in their corresponding mutants. Tang et al. (2022a) examined the effects of gene introgression from domesticated rice on bulliform phytoliths in wild rice. One underexplored factor is plant ploidy level—specifically, whether changes in the number of chromosome sets affect the development and morphology of rice phytoliths, a question that has not yet received systematic investigation. Notably, plant communities in historical periods were not genetically uniform populations but rather complex ecosystems potentially comprising multiple cytogenetic types. Therefore, whether polyploid rice domestication occurred during ancient times remains an open question requiring further investigation.

A haploid is defined as an individual whose cells contained only one set of chromosomes (n), the same chromosome number as the gametophyte, and half that of normal diploid somatic cells. In rice, haploids can be produced using various approaches, including distant hybridization, anther culture, inducer line methods, and their formation is essentially independent of whether the plant is wild or domesticated. These haploids are of great value in modern genetic research and dihaploid breeding programs (Chaikam et al. 2019; Jin et al. 2025; Germana 2011).

Significant progress has been made in research on haploid rice in terms of agronomic traits and physiological characteristics (Cao et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2019a). However, the morphological features and formation mechanisms of phytoliths in haploid rice have not been reported. This gap not only limits our understanding of the developmental biology of silicified cells in rice, but also hinders the broader application of phytolith analysis in archaeological research. Therefore, investigating the characteristics of phytoliths in haploid rice will help fill this research gap, offering novel insights into the developmental biology of plant silicified cells and providing a more refined and comprehensive morphological foundation for archaeological criteria used in rice phytolith identification. Such advancements will significantly enhance the potential of phytolith analysis to detect genetic variations in ancient rice.

Rice bulliform phytoliths are characterized by their high yield and abundance within archaeological strata (Wang and Lu 2012). Haploid rice is a unique genetic material distinguished by its dwarf stature and high sterility, with a somatic chromosome set only half that of diploid rice. This makes it a natural control for investigating the regulatory mechanisms of genetic background on phytolith development. Consequently, haploid rice leaves were chosen as the experimental material to investigate the morphological characteristics of these phytoliths. By comparing the morphological ornamentation of fan-shaped bulliform phytoliths (ICPT 2019) between haploid and diploid rice under identical environmental conditions, this study aimed to (1) clarify the specific morphological features of bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice and (2) evaluate the impact of chromosomal ploidy variation on phytolith morphology. These findings are expected to provide empirical data that support the interdisciplinary integration of fields such as archaeobotany and plant genetics.

Experimental rice varieties

Diploid rice: A total of 21 diploid rice samples were included, consisting of 10 japonica (Oryza sativa subsp. japonica Kato) and 11 indica rice (Oryza sativa subsp. indica Kato) varieties (Table 1). The japonica rice samples, designated 2n-J01 to 2n-J10, were Yunlu 106, Hugeng 137, Fengxin Wanjing, Wuyujing 5, W3195, Egeng 403, 20S692 Geng, Wankennuo 1, Sasanishiki, and Ganningjing 2. The indica rice samples, designated as 2n-I01 to 2n-I11, were Baihua Xiang 8, Yuehe Simiao R, Habataki, 9311, Ehu Wannian 05, Huanghuazhan, Huazhan, e9311, Zhongzao 35, Qin 1 S, and Teng 383 S. All diploid rice samples were collected from Sanya (Hainan Province) and Nanchang (Jiangxi Province), China, between 2021 and 2022.

Phytolith Analysis

Leaf samples were collected from mature rice plants, cleaned, oven-dried, and cut into small segments. Phytoliths were extracted using the wet ashing method (Wang and Lu 1993). Briefly, approximately 1.0 g of leaf sample was soaked overnight in 30 mL of concentrated nitric acid in a 50 mL PTFE beaker and then digested on a hot plate (< 170 °C) with repeated acid additions until the solution became clear. The residue was washed twice with distilled water and twice with anhydrous ethanol by centrifugation (7500 rpm, 3 min per wash). The permanent slides were mounted with neutral balsam and examined at 400× magnification using a light microscope (Nikon Ci-S) (Tang et al. 2022a).

The measurement parameters for bulliform phytoliths included fan length (VL), fan width (HL), stalk length (A), fan face length (B), ratio of fan face length to stalk length (B/A), and the number of fish-scale facets (Fig. 1, i–iv). Note that the fan width (HL) measured in this study covers a portion of the symmetrical “braids” flanking the fan-shaped bulliform phytoliths. Morphometric measurements were performed on relatively symmetrical bulliform phytoliths, whereas fish-scale facets were counted on all bulliform phytoliths with clearly visible patterns under the microscope (including both symmetrical and asymmetrical types). Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft 365 and the Data Processing System (DPS) software. For each sample, 100–400 bulliform phytoliths were measured for morphological parameters (Fig. 1, iii, iv). Fish-scale facet counting was conducted based on the method published by Huan et al. Results and Analysis

Induction of Rice Haploids

Two-line sterile rice varieties were crossed and triple-crossed with elite indica or japonica materials, followed by selfing to generate the following five populations: (01) Shen 08 S/Nanjing 46//19 Xiang F₂, (02) Shen 08 S/Nanjing 46//Weiliangyou 8612 F₃, (03) 1892 S/Jing 22//Yongyou 1538 F₃, (04) Yuan 88 S/(Ge 5121 S/R610) F₂, (05) Shen 08 S/Ganningjing 4 F₃. Sterile plants selected from these five populations were crossed with the haploid inducer line HI285 (Liu et al. 2024), which was introduced from the China National Rice Research Institute (CNRRI). The resulting F₁ generations were planted, and haploid plants were selected based on distinct, easily identifiable phenotypic traits: dwarfism, slender stems, short panicles, and particularly small spikelets that were sterile and unable to set seed upon pollination (Fig. 2).

Morphological Characteristics of Bulliform Phytoliths in Haploid Rice

The morphological parameters of the bulliform phytoliths from the five haploid rice varieties are summarized in Fig. 3. Fan width (HL) ranged from 31.7 ± 4.9 μm to 39.8 ± 8.1 μm, and fan length (VL) ranged from 33.7 ± 4.9 μm to 42.6 ± 6.9 μm, with overall mean values of 34.8 μm and 37.6 μm, respectively.

Morphotype distribution (HL, VL) of bulliform phytoliths in haploid and diploid rice. “**” in the figure indicate highly significant differences between different rice varieties (P < 0.01)

In comparison, diploid rice had a mean HL of 42.3 μm and mean VL of 46.1 μm. Subspecies level analysis revealed that the ten japonica varieties had HL ranging from 37.5 ± 5.3 to 44.8 ± 6.8 μm and VL from 41.2 ± 5.9 to 50.9 ± 8.7 μm (means: HL 42.2 μm, VL 47.4 μm). The eleven indica varieties showed HL ranging from 39.0 ± 4.4 to 48.5 ± 8.1 μm and VL from 38.1 ± 4.7 to 53.0 ± 7.5 μm (means: HL 42.4 μm, VL 44.9 μm) (Fig. 3).

The bulliform phytoliths of haploid rice exhibited significantly reduced dimensions compared with their diploid counterparts, with HL decreasing by 17.7% and VL by 18.4%. These results demonstrated a marked reduction in the morphological size of bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice.

The ratio of the base portion length (B) to the non-base portion length (A) of the rice bulliform phytoliths is shown in Fig. 4. The B/A ratio of the five haploid rice lines ranged from 0.54 ± 0.18 to 0.61 ± 0.24, with a mean of 0.58. In contrast, the mean B/A ratio of the 21 diploid rice lines was 1.09, with japonica rice ranging from 0.79 ± 0.29 to 1.14 ± 0.36 (mean 0.97) and indica rice ranging from 1.02 ± 0.49 to 1.53 ± 0.69 (mean 1.20).

B/A ratios of bulliform phytoliths in haploid and diploid rice. “**” in the figure indicate highly significant differences between different rice varieties (P < 0.01)

Compared to diploid rice, the B/A ratio of haploid rice phytoliths decreased significantly by 46.8% (Fig. 4).

Classification Characteristics of Bulliform Phytoliths in Haploid Rice

The B/A ratio of the bulliform phytoliths varied significantly between haploid and diploid rice samples. Based on their B/A ratios, the samples were classified into three subtypes (Zhang and Wang 1998): long-stalked (B/A < 0.8), intermediate (0.8 ≤ B/A ≤ 1.2), and short-stalked (B/A > 1.2). The proportion of long-stalk-type bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice ranged from 81.0 to 92.9% with a mean of 86.6%. In diploid rice, the average proportion of the long-stalk type was 30.4%, with japonica rice ranging from 15.7 to 59.0% (mean 35.2%) and indica rice ranging from 14.3 to 38.9% (mean 26.1%) (Fig. 5).

Type distribution of bulliform phytoliths in haploid and diploid rice

Compared to diploid rice, the proportion of long-stalk-type phytoliths in haploid rice showed a 1.85-fold increase, representing a 1.46-fold increase over japonica and a 2.32-fold increase over indica. These findings indicate that bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice are predominantly of the long-stalk type.

The proportion of short-stalk-type bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice ranged from 0.0 to 1.5%, with a mean of 1.0%. In diploid rice, the average proportion of the short-stalk type was 33.0%, with japonica rice ranging from 8.0 to 39.5% (mean 21.7%) and indica rice ranging from 30.0 to 62.9% (mean 43.2%) (Fig. 5).

Compared to diploid rice, the proportion of short-stalk-type phytoliths in haploid rice decreased significantly by 97.0%, representing reductions of 95.4% relative to japonica and 97.7% relative to indica. This indicates that short-stalk-type bulliform phytoliths are extremely rare in haploid rice, suggesting the possible absence of this morphological category.

Fish-Scale Facets on Bulliform Phytoliths in Rice

As shown in Fig. 6, the number of fish-scale facets per bulliform phytolith in the haploid rice ranged from 3 to 12. The mean number of fish-scale facets across the five haploid rice lines was 6.7, with individual line means ranging from 6.4 ± 1.2 to 7.0 ± 1.3. In diploid rice, the number of fish-scale facets per bulliform phytolith ranged from 4 to 14. The mean number of fish-scale facets in diploid rice was 8.9, with values ranging from 8.4 ± 1.2 to 9.2 ± 1.7.

Fish-scale facets on bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice. * and ** in the figure represent significant (P < 0.05) and extremely significant (P < 0.01) differences between rice varieties, respectively

The proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 fish-scale facets in haploid rice ranged from 3.0 to 9.9%, with a mean of 7.0%. In diploid rice, the mean proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 fish-scale facets was 58.4%, with japonica rice ranging from 48.4 to 63.7% (mean 55.4%) and indica rice ranging from 60.2 to 62.3% (mean 61.3%) (Fig. 6).

Compared with diploid rice, the proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 fish-scale facets in haploid rice was significantly reduced by 88.0%. Specifically, compared with japonica rice and indica rice separately, the proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 fish-scale facets was reduced by 87.4% and 88.6%, respectively.

Discussion

This study reveals for the first time that chromosomal ploidy levels significantly influence rice phytolith morphology. Compared to diploid rice, haploid rice exhibited: (1) an ~ 18% reduction in phytolith dimensions; (2) dominance of long-stalk types (~ 86.6%) with scarce short-stalk types (0.0%–1.5%) and a B/A ratio of ~ 0.58; and (3) fewer fish-scale facets, with only 7.0% of phytoliths having ≥ 9 facets. Taken together, these three findings, encompassing both quantitative (smaller size and fewer facets) and qualitative (possible absence of certain morphological types) changes, reveal the substantial impact of chromosomal ploidy on plant cell silicification patterns, with important implications for both plant genetics research and archaeobotanical applications.

Factors Influencing Phytolith Morphology in Haploid Rice

Mechanisms of size reduction: The approximately 18% reduction in bulliform phytolith size observed in haploid rice may be attributed to factors operating at both the cytological and physiological levels (Fig. 7). (1) At the cytological level, haploid plants have a shorter developmental duration and a slower rate of cell division than diploids. As a result, they are typically characterized by reduced cell size and overall organ diminution. Because phytoliths are formed through silicification within the cell lumina, their final dimensions are fundamentally constrained by the physical space available in the host cell. Thus, the overall reduction in cell volume directly accounts for the corresponding decrease in phytolith size (Forster et al. 2007; Jacquier et al. 2020). (2) At the physiological level, reduced chromosomal ploidy may interfere with the processes governing Si uptake (mediated by transporters such as Lsi1 and Lsi2), translocation, and deposition within the cell lumina (Ma et al. 2006, 2007a, b; Yamaji and Ma 2007). Halving the gene dosage likely results in a diminished abundance or activity of relevant transporter proteins, thereby reducing the total Si available to specific silicified cells and ultimately contributing to the formation of bulliform phytoliths with reduced morphological dimensions.

Mechanisms underlying the impact of missing morphotypes: The extremely low abundance of short-stalked bulliform phytoliths in haploid rice (0.0%–1.5%) represents a particularly notable finding. Morphological variations in bulliform phytoliths from rice leaf epidermis, such as stalk length, reflect the developmental and differentiation status of the cell population. In the haploid state, the chromosome set is halved (from 2n to n), altering the developmental program or differentiation pattern of leaf epidermal cells, resulting in the absence of short-stalked phytoliths. This can be attributed to the following two factors (Fig. 7): (1) Developmental inhibition: The short-stalked morphotype may represent a specific developmental stage or subtype of bulliform cells. Weaker physiological conditions or distinct gene expression profiles of haploid plants (Dwivedi et al. 2015; Dunwell 2010) may disrupt the normal progression of this stage or subtype, ultimately preventing the cells from differentiating into short-stalked phytoliths. (2) Selective silicification: Silicification may occur selectively even when the morphological structure of short-stalked cells is present. In haploid plants, limited silica resources may be preferentially allocated to long-stalked bulliforms or other phytolith types that are critical for maintaining leaf architecture. Consequently, short-stalked bulliform phytoliths remain insufficiently silicified and unable to form stable siliceous structures, making them difficult to detect.

While this study explores four key factors affecting phytolith morphology in haploid rice—namely, physical constraint, metabolic constraint, developmental blockage, and selective silicification—the potential involvement of additional factors and the identification of the most influential one warrant further investigation.

3.Causes of reduced phytolith facets: In this study, haploid rice exhibited a relatively low number of fish-scale facets (approximately 6.7), with the proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 facets at only ~ 7%. Comparison with existing data from diploid rice (Huan et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2024)—where the proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 facets is approximately 17% in wild rice and 60% in domesticated rice—reveals that haploid rice has significantly fewer such facets and a markedly reduced proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 facets. Fujiwara (1976) first observed that bulliform phytoliths from domesticated rice exhibited tortoise shell-like leading edges, whereas those from wild rice were large and irregular. Lu et al. (2002) subsequently reported that domesticated rice bulliform phytoliths typically bear 8–14 fish-scale facets, compared to less than nine in most wild rice. Although recent research suggests that water conditions influence the number of fish-scale facets on bulliform phytoliths (Zhang et al. 2024), the present study demonstrates that ploidy level also plays a significant role. The specific mechanisms by which the ploidy level regulates phytolith facet formation via its influence on silicification processes require further experimental investigation.

Implications for Rice Phytolith Identification in Archaeology

Under natural conditions, haploid plants exhibit low survival and complete sterility (Dwivedi et al. 2015; Dunwell 2010; Yang and Wang 2024). In modern populations of both wild and cultivated rice, naturally occurring haploids are extremely rare (< 0.1%) and are nearly negligible. Most haploids are artificially induced, making it highly unlikely that they are associated with wild-to-domesticated transitions central to agricultural archaeology. Consequently, the morphological traits observed in haploid rice phytoliths did not statistically affect the current criteria used to distinguish between wild and domesticated rice. However, in archaeological analysis, a rice phytolith assemblage within a stratum that simultaneously exhibits overall reduced size and morphological simplification of fan-shaped phytoliths—specifically the absence of short-stalked bulliform types—may serve as a “micro-morphological fingerprint” of abnormal ploidy levels, particularly haploidy. Such a pattern would indicate the presence of a distinctive ancient rice population containing haploid or chromosomally variant individuals, thereby providing microscopic evidence for the exploitation of specialized genetic materials in ancient agriculture.

Regarding the differentiation between indica and japonica rice, Fujiwara and Sasaki (1978) observed that bulliform phytoliths in indica rice are characterized by a B/A > 1, appearing as the “thin and round α-type,” whereas those in japonica rice have a B/A < 1.0, described as the “thick and pointed β-type.” Zhang and Wang (1998) further classified bulliform phytoliths into three categories based on stalk length: long-stalked (B/A < 0.8), short-stalked (B/A > 1.2), and intermediate (0.8 ≤ B/A ≤ 1.2). The general trends of indica rice exhibiting higher B/A values and japonica rice exhibiting lower B/A values are well established (Wang et al. 1997). However, in the present study, no consistent trend was observed. Among the 10 japonica rice samples (mean B/A ~ 0.97), long-stalked types accounted for ~ 35.2% and short-stalked types accounted for ~ 21.7%, and among the 11 indica rice samples (mean B/A ~ 1.2), long-stalked types comprised ~ 26.1% and short-stalked types ~ 43.2%. In haploid rice, the B/A ratio of bulliform phytoliths (~ 0.58) was 46.8% lower than that of diploid rice, with long-stalked forms (B/A < 0.8) reaching 86.6%, whereas short-stalked forms (B/A > 1.2) represented only 0.0%–1.5%.

This finding introduces a critical caveat for traditional domestication studies. In particular, caution is warranted when interpreting the predominance of long-stalked bulliform phytoliths accompanied by the absence of short-stalked bulliform phytoliths in archaeological sites as direct evidence of japonica rice, as such a pattern may, in some cases reflect abnormal ploidy levels within the ancient rice population. This study demonstrates that ploidy level is a significant factor influencing the morphological types of bulliform phytoliths, a finding that not only provides important insights for phytolith-based rice species identification and archaeological analysis, but also opens a new perspective for investigating the genetic diversity of ancient rice.

Research Outlook

Traditional archaeobotanical methods have an inherently limited ability to recover chromosome-level information from ancient crops. Ancient plant communities are not genetically uniform populations but rather complex ecosystems encompassing a range of cytogenetic variants. This study demonstrates that the ploidy level significantly influences rice phytolith morphology, suggesting that phytolith analysis can serve as a proxy for reconstructing aspects of the genetic structure of past plant populations. This study shows that halving the rice chromosome set from 2n to n directly alters the morphology and facets of bulliform phytoliths and may even result in the absence of certain morphotypes, revealing genetic variation in rice leaves with identifiable microscopic imprints on phytoliths.

During early domestication and cultivation, humans consciously or unconsciously selected desirable traits, such as larger grains or specific growth types (Fuller 2007), and individuals with chromosomal number abnormalities—whether haploid, diploid, or polyploid—may be inadvertently retained within crop populations. These findings open a new research avenue for exploring the genetic structure of ancient rice populations and shed light on how early human societies utilized and selected for genetic variation.

Rice and wheat are members of the Poaceae family, yet their domestication histories differ markedly. Cultivated wheat includes six natural groups: Triticum monococcum, T. urartu, T. turgidum, T. timopheevii, T. aestivum, and T. zhukovskyi—spanning diploid, tetraploid, and hexaploid species (Feldman and Levy 2023; Peng et al. 2011). In contrast, Asian rice cultivated under natural conditions remains exclusively diploid and consists of only two subspecies: indica and japonica. This raises an intriguing question for future research: Did polyploid rice ever arise during the domestication process?

In modern “one-line” hybrid rice breeding, the rapid and accurate screening and identification of haploid materials from hybrids is a critically important task, and the identification of haploids prior to chromosome doubling constitutes an essential step in this process. Common methods for distinguishing haploids from diploids in rice include morphological observation, flow cytometry, chromosome counting, high-throughput sequencing, morphological markers, and molecular markers (Ghalagi et al. 2023). Although chromosome counting and flow cytometry offer high accuracy in haploid identification (Lin et al. 2025), their application in practical haploid breeding is constrained by complex procedures and demanding instrumental requirements. In this regard, further research on phytoliths from haploid rice may establish phytolith-based screening as a simple and feasible alternative “micro-morphological fingerprint” for the rapid identification of haploids in breeding practice.

Conclusion

This study reveals, for the first time, that ploidy level is a key factor influencing rice phytolith morphology. Compared to diploid rice, haploid rice exhibits an ~ 18% reduction in overall bulliform phytolith size, fewer fish-scale facets, and an extremely low proportion of phytoliths with ≥ 9 facets. The B/A ratio decreased significantly by 46.8%, with short-stalked types being extremely scarce (0.0%–1.5%) and long-stalked types dominating. These changes, both quantitative (reduced size and fewer facets) and qualitative (possible absence of certain morphotypes), demonstrate the profound effect of ploidy on the silicification patterns of plant cells.
