Investigation of gold mineralization along the Al Amar Fault Zone using integrated geophysical and remote sensing methods in the Saudi Arabian Shield
Authors: Mohamed A. Abd El‑Wahed, Mohamed Attia, Osama M. K. Kassem, Walaa Mahmoud Saad, Abdullah Othman, Saada A. Saada, Ahmed M. Eldosouky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study of gold mineralization along the Al Amar Fault Zone, Saudi Arabian Shield, Ar Rayn and Ad Dawadimi terranes is undertaken using an integrated multi-method approach. The research effort aims to improve geological mapping of lithology, structure, and hydrothermal alteration by integrating Landsat-8 multispectral imagery, aeromagnetic geophysical data, and targeted field validation to overcome the limitations posed by remote, inaccessible desert terrain. Advanced image processing techniques, such as principal component analysis and Logsigm Function (LSF), enable precise discrimination of major rock units, thrust contacts, and zoned hydrothermal alteration systems (phyllic-argillic, propylitic, and iron oxide assemblages) associated with mineralization. Automated lineament extraction and aeromagnetic analysis imply multi-phase, depth-dependent structural controls, with NNE-NE and NW-trending faults and shear zones serving as major conduits for hydrothermal fluids and gold deposition. Fieldwork supports a spatial association between high lineament density and intense alteration with known mineral occurrences, especially in the Al Khunayqiyah and Al Amar mine sectors, highlighting the significance of structural permeability in mineralization localization. Gold in the Al Amar area is mainly found in structurally controlled quartz veins, breccia zones, and stockworks, often at volcanic-intrusive contacts. Here we present results that illustrate the complicated tectonic evolution of the Al Amar Fault Zone with multiple episodes of deformation and magmatism and the variety of types of mineral deposits that formed in response, including epithermal, VMS, and IOCG systems. Overall, the study demonstrates that integrating remote sensing, geophysical, and field data provides a robust three-dimensional exploration framework that improves understanding and targeting of gold mineralization in the structurally complex terranes of the Arabian Shield.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63435-w.

Introduction

Natural resources and mineralization are important for sustainable economic growth and diversification. Regional geological patterns in basement terranes, such as the Saudi Arabian Shield (S-AS), influence mineral systems and are important for successful mineral exploration. However, parts of the S-AS, such as the Muhayriqah area, remain poorly investigated, particularly regarding the relationships among gold mineralization, surface geology, and crustal architecture1–4.

Saudi Arabia is home to many ancient gold-mining sites (Fig. 1a). The main mines in the area include Mahd Al-Dhab, Al-Hajar, Al-Amar, Bulghah, Suhkaybarat, and Bi’r Tawilah5–9. The gold occurrences in Saudi Arabia can be classified into three main types based on their tectonic settings and host rocks: (i) in volcanosedimentary sequences, generally in juvenile oceanic settings rich in Cu, Zn and S, such as the Al Amar and Mahd Al-Dhab mines10; (ii) in carbonatized ophiolitic serpentine rocks (listvenites) along suture zones such as the Bi’r Tawlah site11; and (iii) Intrusion-Related Gold Systems (IRGS), where the mineralization is associated with late-stage diorite-to-granite intrusions emplaced into arc-metamorphosed volcaniclastics, such as Bulghah and Zalm, characterized by low sulfide minerals such as pyrite, arsenopyrite and pyrrhotite12.

Accessibility often limits field data collection in remote desert areas with rugged terrain, leaving major gaps in geological investigations. To overcome these difficulties, the use of multi-sensor satellite remote sensing is becoming imperative for better lithological discrimination and structural analysis in such complex terrains18–28. The best spatial and spectral resolution of multispectral imagery is particularly well suited to imaging characteristic absorption features of alteration minerals in the VNIR and SWIR ranges, enabling detection of zones of hydrothermal alteration associated with mineralized systems29–35. In addition, the use of multi-source data, such as Landsat and ASTER, in conjunction with the subsurface imaging capabilities of radar, may significantly improve lithological mapping and the extraction of structural features17,36–43.

Advanced analyses of potential field (PF) data have been increasingly used in recent methodologies to map structural features at different depths and to enhance geological edge detection44,45. These enhanced techniques are particularly useful for exposing geological features that may be ambiguous or indistinct in traditional datasets46–52. Tracing subsoil and superficial lineaments enables the construction of coherent structural models that represent geological systems at different depths53–60. This multiscale approach improves understanding of the spatial relationships between geological controls and mineralization, emphasizing areas where complex structural features may enhance mineral exploration success61–66. Combining remote sensing, geophysical methods, field observations, and geological analysis enables more robust interpretations of deep and shallow structures and enhances our understanding of the geological relationships and their linkages to mineral resources67–73.

The Al Amar Suture Zone of the Arabian Shield hosts significant gold mineralization, which is particularly notable given the region’s rich gold endowment and complex tectonic evolution74,75. The Arabian Shield is a significant portion of the extensive Arabian-Nubian Shield and is predominantly composed of Neoproterozoic crystalline rocks. The Al Amar Suture Zone is a major structural boundary within the shield, resulting from the tectonic amalgamation and collision of multiple crustal terranes76,77. This suture is characterized by ophiolite assemblages and shear zones, both of which are important in the localization of mineralization.

The Al Amar Suture Zone is located between the Ad-Dawadimi and Ar-Rayan terranes (Fig. 1a, b) and has experienced a series of deformation events that have led to the formation of a variety of mineral deposits, including gold deposits. The influence of shear zones, particularly the Najd Fault System, on the distribution of gold across the Arabian Shield and the Al Amar area is well established77.

Apart from the practical difficulties of accessing remote desert sites, geological research in the Al Amar region faces serious methodological problems. Surface observations have shallow depth penetration, limiting subsurface structural controls on mineralization, and spectral ambiguities from similar mineralogical signatures across different lithologies can hinder accurate discrimination of rock units based on remote sensing alone. The limitations of both data can be overcome by fusing Landsat-8 remote sensing with aeromagnetic data. Aeromagnetic data provide critical depth information about buried structures and intrusive bodies that are not visible at Earth’s surface. At the same time, multispectral imagery can detect surface alteration minerals and lithological contacts despite spectral overlap. This integrated approach allows a more complete view of both surface and sub-surface geology within the Al Amar Fault Zone. The present study builds on previous work, using an integrated approach that combines multi-sensor satellite data, including Landsat-8, with targeted fieldwork. The method aims to improve litho-structural mapping, identify potential zones of hydrothermal alteration, and provide new information on the tectonic history of the Amar Fault Zone.

Ad Dawadimi and Ar Rayn terranes

The eastern sector of the Arabian Shield comprises the Neoproterozoic Afif composite terrane together with the Ha’il, Ad Dawadimi, and Ar Rayn terranes (Fig. 1a, b). The Ad Dawadimi terrane is especially notable for its dominance by pelitic and psammopelitic schists and phyllites, with the Abt Schist (also known as the Abt Formation) being particularly prominent75–77. These metasedimentary rocks are intruded by significant dioritic and granitic bodies. Scattered ophiolitic massifs and mélanges displaying boninitic geochemical signatures are interpreted as forearc ophiolites, indicating a tectonic setting adjacent to a subduction zone76,78.

The Abt Schist is primarily composed of chlorite-sericite schist, with smaller volumes of phyllite, slate, arenite, conglomerate, and dolomitic limestone. Geochronological data indicate these units were deposited between 620 and 615 million years ago (Ma)79,80. The Abt Schist is widely interpreted as representing a turbidite-filled forearc trench, about 200 by 50 km in size, situated above an east-dipping subduction zone beneath the Al Amar volcanic arc78,81. However, alternative interpretations suggest the Abt Schist may have formed as back-arc basin sediments above a west-dipping subduction system under the Afif microplate76,82, or as marginal basin or accretionary prism deposits linked to a west-dipping oceanic slab beneath a volcanic arc in the Afif terrane13,83. Nonetheless, the absence of chaotic sedimentary structures and high-pressure, low-temperature metamorphic minerals typical of accretionary prisms challenges these alternative models75. Isotopic and detrital zircon evidence, including U–Pb, 40Ar/39Ar, and Sm/Nd data, indicate sedimentation persisted in the Ad Dawadimi terrane until approximately 620 Ma84.

Structurally, the eastern boundary of the Ad Dawadimi terrane, adjacent to the Ar Rayn terrane, is marked by subhorizontal, asymmetrical folds that verge WSW and plunge SSE85. In contrast, the western sector features subhorizontal, WNW-verging, asymmetrical folds with a NE-SW extensional overprint. Deformation and metamorphism intensify eastward, especially toward the Al Amar-Idsas fault85.

The Ar Rayn terrane is a magmatic arc complex, dated at over 690 Ma, composed of bimodal volcanic and volcano-sedimentary rocks of the Al Amar Group14,77. These rocks show geochemical characteristics intermediate between tholeiitic and calc-alkaline affinities83. The Al Amar Group is subdivided into the lower Sidriyah Formation (mainly mafic-to-intermediate volcanics and volcaniclastic rocks) and the upper Shalahib Formation (mainly felsic and tuffaceous volcanics)15,86. The Sidriyah Formation includes aphyric and porphyritic andesites, conglomerate-breccia, chert, and carbonate beds; these are intruded by syn- and post-tectonic granodiorite-to-tonalite bodies. The Shalahib Formation features volcanic breccias, ignimbrites, lapilli tuffs, and carbonate interbeds, overlain by subaqueous tuffs and chert. Microtonalite and trondhjemite sheets also occur, cutting across bedding and foliation.

The Ar Rayn terrane is further distinguished by widespread syntectonic arc-related intrusive complexes of trondhjemite-tonalite-granodiorite (TTG) affinity (Fig. 1b), with trondhjemite and tonalite being most prevalent. These intrusions show various deformation textures and often form discrete grayish and whitish masses83. Mesocratic tonalite and granodiorite intrusions are in sharp contact with host volcanic units, with granodiorite commonly exposed and characterized by abundant clots and knots83. Post-tectonic intrusions (alkali feldspar granite, leucogranite, and pegmatite dikes) disrupt both volcanic rocks and TTG complexes, often aligning with major shear zones and fault-related foliations77,83. Ophiolitic fragments, volcanically derived and metasedimentary rocks, often interbedded with chert and dolomitic marble, are dispersed throughout the terrane, especially along the Al Amar-Idsas fault83. The ophiolitic assemblage includes partially carbonated ultramafic rocks, layered gabbros, pillow basalts, and plagiogranite bodies. The northern Al Amar-Idsas fault is recognized as a high-angle thrust zone with exposures of serpentinite, metabasalt, tuff, chert, and minor listvenite83. It occasionally contains deep-marine sediments (marble, chert) within the mélange87,88.

Additionally, the region hosts younger volcanic suites, the Hamir Group, which unconformably overlies the Al Amar Group and is mainly exposed in the Gabal Hamir and Wadi Sidarah districts. The Hamir Group, subdivided into the Silib and Sidarah Formations, consists of basaltic to andesitic flows and pyroclastic rocks (Silib), and lithic volcanic rocks, sandstone, and pebbly siltstone (Sidarah). The Ruqa’an complex is an arc-related layered mafic–ultramafic intrusion, characterized by serpentinized ultramafic cumulates, gabbro, troctolite, leucogabbro, and tonalite dikes, with sulfide mineralization in basal portions83,87,89,90.

Tectonic evolution involved substantial crustal remobilization in the Ar Rayn and Afif regions81. The Ar Rayn terrane likely functioned as an oceanic island arc above an eastward-subducting plate, with westward transport, and the Abt Schist forming an accretionary wedge containing ophiolitic fragments81,91–93. Geochemical and age data classify Ar Rayn intrusives into three types: (1) low-Al TTG (arc origin, 632–616 Ma, west); (2) high-Al TTG/adakite (arc affinity, 689–617 Ma, central/east); and (3) syn- to post-orogenic alkali granites (607–583 Ma)83. Subduction-related arc magmatism ceased around 615 Ma, making the Ar Rayn arc among the youngest in the Arabian-Nubian Shield84.

Finally, the Ar Rayn terrane is mineral-rich, with significant Au–Ag–Zn–Cu-barite and other metallic deposits, attracting major mineral exploration efforts since the 1950s83,84.

General geology

The regional geological framework consists of three major lithological units, namely the Abt Formation, the Al Amar Group, and a suite of intrusive rocks (Fig. 1b). The Abt Formation is composed mainly of schist and metagreywacke, metamorphosed volcanosedimentary rocks exhibiting a high degree of deformation and generally classified within the greenschist facies (Fig. 2a, b). A major metamorphic event in this unit occurred during the late Cryogenian to Ediacaran, ca. 650 to 542 million years ago84. It occurs in a large area of the Ad Dawadimi terrane and is spatially associated with several granitic intrusions (syenogranite and alkali-feldspar granite; Fig. 2c) and with layered gabbro and ophiolite mélange zones with forearc features. The Abt Formation was deposited in a marine basin along the eastern margin of the proto-Arabian-Nubian Shield, where volcanic eruptions from the Al Amar arc strongly influenced the sedimentary environment. The resulting sediments were then folded and metamorphosed by tectonic processes83.

Andesitic tuffs, lava flows and breccias and stratified andesitic and rhyolitic tuffs (Fig. 2d, e), welded tuffs, subordinate dolomite, calcareous siltstone and pyritic chert are typical of the Al Amar Group (Fig. 1b). Most of these rocks record greenschist facies metamorphism, but higher grade rocks occur in the eastern part of the group. These include amphibolite-facies phyllites, quartz-feldspar schists, biotite-hornblende schists, garnet-bearing paragneisses and quartz-feldspar gneisses. The U–Pb zircon ages indicate that the oldest volcanic and sedimentary rocks of this group are ~ 690 Ma old83.

The Al Amar Group (Fig. 1b) is divided into the Sidriyah and Shalahib formations15,86. The Sidriyah Formation is composed primarily of basaltic and andesitic volcanic and volcanogenic rocks (Fig. 2d–g) with minor marble and siltstone. Conversely, the Shalahib Formation comprises more felsic volcanic and volcanogenic rocks, such as rhyolite and dacite flows, tuffs, ignimbrites, and interbedded carbonate and cherty sedimentary rocks (Fig. 2h, i). The Sidriyah Formation generally lies below the Shalahib, but rapid lateral variation around volcanic centres can obscure simple stratigraphic relationships83. The presence of ignimbrites in the Shalahib Formation suggests episodes of subaerial exposure, indicating that the volcanic arc was constructed in environments with alternating shallow-marine and subaerial conditions83.

Vaslet et al.86 and Manivit et al.15 distinguished two main sequences of the Al Amar Group: a more metamorphosed eastern sequence and a less metamorphosed, monoclinal western sequence. Differences in structural and metamorphic characteristics further support this distinction, and the authors suggest that the traditional formation names be replaced by “eastern” and “western” sequence terminology to improve mapping clarity. The eastern sequence contains numerous septa and pendants in the central and eastern parts of the terrane. Conversely, the western sequence forms a narrow, semi-continuous belt along the western margin of the terrane, bounded by the Al Amar fault83. Tectonic and magmatic forces have formed about seven elongate, north-trending belts within the Amar Group of the Ar Rayn terrane87. The group is intruded by syn- and post-tectonic igneous bodies (667–580 Ma), such as gabbros and strongly sheared granitoids (granite, granodiorite, tonalite, and trondhjemite) (Fig. 2j). Other lithologies include andesitic and rhyolitic flows, paragneisses, and limestone.

Geochemically, both the eastern and western sequences include a spectrum of compositions from basalt to rhyolite. For lithogeochemical comparison, rocks were classified into felsic extrusive, mafic-intermediate extrusive, and porphyritic felsic intrusive categories based on silica content83. Notably, felsic volcanic rocks from the eastern sequence are distinguished from those of the western sequence by higher rubidium (Rb) and strontium (Sr) contents but lower sodium (Na) contents, possibly reflecting hydrothermal or deuteric alteration processes in the western belt83. Additionally, the eastern sequence felsic volcanics are enriched in immobile trace elements such as Y, Zr, Yb, and La relative to those in the west, though rare earth element (REE) geochemistry does not provide a clear distinction83.

Porphyritic felsic intrusions, mainly trondhjemitic in composition, are most common in the northern part of the western sequence belt83. Based on lithogeochemical and spatial relationships, these are interpreted as hypabyssal, subvolcanic equivalents of the western sequence extrusive felsic rocks83,87,90. Their importance is highlighted by their genetic link to epithermal mineralization in the western sequence, which hosts gold, silver, zinc, copper, and barite83,87,90.

Outlined below is a summary of the local geology for the Al Khunayqiyah and Al Amar areas along the Al Amar suture zone:

Al Khunayqiyah area

This region encompasses a range of rock types, including Abt schist, granitic gneiss, volcano-sedimentary rocks, and post-orogenic granites (Fig. 2a). The Abt schist Formation is composed of fragmented and crenulated chlorite-mica schist and extends broadly between the Al Amar suture and the Halaban ultramafic belt93. Granitic bodies intrude both the Abt Formation and volcano-sedimentary sequences in the Ad Dawadimi and Ar Rayn terranes (Fig. 2a). These include largely undeformed to slightly deformed leucocratic monzogranite, as well as smaller volumes of syenogranite and biotite-muscovite granite, which typically form topographic highs. Post-tectonic granite yields U–Pb zircon ages ranging from 605 ± 5 to 598 ± 35 Ma, with additional SHRIMP U–Pb ages of 579 ± 3 Ma and 565 ± 2 Ma84. The volcano-sedimentary units, which comprise andesitic and rhyolitic flows, limestones, and marbles, have undergone mild folding and metamorphism, typically to greenschist facies in the west and amphibolite facies in the east, forming north-south-trending belts or heavily eroded outcrops within older rocks.

Al Amar area

Divided by the Al Amar-Idsas Fault (Fig. 2b), the eastern part of this area contains the Al Amar Group, which is further subdivided into the Sidriyah Formation at the base and the overlying Shalahib Formation. The Sidriyah Formation consists primarily of mafic to intermediate volcanic and pyroclastic rocks, including both aphyric and porphyritic andesites, as well as andesitic tuff, breccia, cherty tuffite, and carbonate layers. The Shalahib Formation comprises a lower volcanic unit characterized by lava flows, breccias, ignimbrites, volcanic domes, and dikes. Additional lithologies here include reworked rhyolitic breccia, ignimbrite, crystal tuff, dolomitic limestone, and chert. These volcanic rocks are locally overlain by subaqueous sedimentary rocks, such as tuff and cherty tuffite, which bear evidence of contemporaneous tectonic activity.

Methodology

Landsat data and processing

To establish a robust basis for lithological, structural, and alteration mapping, this investigation utilized cloud-free, terrain-corrected Landsat-8 Level 1 T imagery sourced from the USGS Earth Explorer platform. The dataset encompasses eleven spectral bands, offering a range of spatial resolutions: 30 m for the visible, near-infrared, and shortwave infrared bands (Bands 1–7 and 9), 15 m for the panchromatic band (Band 8), and 90 m for the thermal infrared bands (Bands 10 and 11). A systematic pre-processing workflow was executed using Envi 5.3, ArcMap 10.8, and PCI Geomatica 2016, including layer stacking, subsetting to the study area, and geometric registration to the WGS-84 UTM Zone 38 N coordinate system. To correct for atmospheric interference and enable accurate spectral analysis, the Internal Average Relative Reflection (IARR) correction method was applied.

IARR was selected because it is well validated for geological mapping in arid environments such as the Arabian Shield. Unlike FLAASH or ATCOR, which require unavailable atmospheric profiles, IARR effectively enhances spectral contrasts for lithological discrimination without compromising relative reflectance accuracy.

Subsequently, a variety of image enhancement and transformation techniques were employed to maximize the extraction of geological information. False Color Composites (FCC) were generated to provide initial visual contrasts for differentiating lithological units and highlighting structural features. Advanced processing methods, including Decorrelation Stretching (DS), Band Ratios (BR), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA), were used to refine rock-unit characterization, delineate structural lineaments, and specifically target zones of hydrothermal alteration. While band ratios amplify subtle spectral contrasts by dividing pixel values between two bands, PCA reduces data redundancy by transforming correlated bands into uncorrelated principal components. For structural analysis, the first principal component (PC1), which captures the most significant information on linear features, was computed for the entire study area and for each sector. This processing employed the LINE algorithm in PCI Geomatica to perform automatic lineament extraction, using edge-gradient thresholds of 50 and 30.

In this study, a refined selection of spectral indices derived from Landsat-8 OLI data was employed to map hydrothermal alteration zones with greater specificity. The chosen band ratios were guided by eigenvector loadings (Tables 1 and 2) derived from the PCA of the Al Khunayqiyah and Amar areas, where the majority of known ore sites are concentrated. The statistical rationale for this selection is based on the principle that PC loadings represent the contribution of each spectral band to the principal component. High loadings with opposite signs for specific bands indicate that the PC maximizes the spectral contrast between those bands. Therefore, band ratios utilizing these specific combinations effectively enhance the spectral features associated with particular minerals, while suppressing background lithological variations.

These loadings informed the selection of optimal band combinations for detecting key mineral groups as follows: The 7/6 ratio leverages the strong negative and positive loadings observed for bands 6 and 7 in PC2 and PC3 (Tables 1 and 2). This contrast enhances the spectral response of carbonate and Al-OH-bearing minerals, which have diagnostic absorption features in the SWIR region (e.g., ~ 2.2 μm for Al-OH, ~ 2.3 μm for carbonates). The 6/4 ratio is sensitive to ferrous silicate minerals due to their absorption features in the visible and near-infrared (e.g., ~ 1.0–1.2 μm for Fe2+), which is effectively captured by the contrast between the visible (Band 4) and SWIR (Band 6) loadings. The 6/5 ratio effectively delineates ferric iron oxides such as hematite, goethite, and jarosite, as the presence of Fe 3+ causes strong absorption in the visible spectrum (Band 4–5) while being more reflective in the SWIR (Band 6). The eigenvector loadings for bands 5 and 6 in PC3 and PC5 (Tables 1 and 2) demonstrate the statistical basis for this contrast, making it characteristic of gossanous materials and iron-rich cap rocks. The spatial correlation observed among these derived products reveals a coherent and zoned hydrothermal system, providing a detailed architecture of alteration within the study area.

Magnetic

Aeromagnetic data

Nowadays, aero-geophysical data are used to map lineaments and structural features at both shallow and deep depths. The reduced-to-the-north-pole (RTP) data for the Al Amar and Al Khunayqiyah areas were obtained from the survey conducted by Aero-Service94. These RTP aero data were improved by Zahran et al.95. Aero-geophysical data were processed utilizing the Geosoft V.8.3 software (2015)96.

Regional/residual separation

The PF data reflect the combined effect of underground sources at various depths rather than a single structural element. To differentiate between deeper/regional and shallower/residual components, the RTP data will be separated into regional (REG) and residual (RES) datasets97. The regional component captures long-wavelength anomalies associated with deep-seated frameworks, whereas the residual component represents shorter-wavelength characteristics associated with shallow sources. This REG-RES separation improves the characterization of near-surface features that are essential for geological/structural interpretation and mineral exploration98–102. To effectively isolate REG-RES’s components, we conducted frequency-domain analysis.

Euler deconvolution (EuD)

This method has become more common since it has been automated and is quick to understand, working with both grid and profile data109–111. Thompson112 presented EuD as an automated method to identify the location and depth of magnetic sources along profiles. It was adapted for grid magnetic data by Reid et al.109. EuD is designed to provide solutions for strike, dip, principal physical property (in our example, magnetism), and the entire or structural index (SI). The important advantage of this method is that the results are independent of the type of geologic sources or the direction of magnetic induction113. Because it is automated and easy to understand, the EuD procedure is well known. According to Ravat114, the EuD technique is unaffected by remanence and does not require prior knowledge of the magnetization direction. Where: ƒ is the magnetic feld at location (x, y, z) and B is the base level of the field (regional value at the point (x, y, z)) and SI is the structural index or degree of homogeneity109.

The least-squares inversion of a moving window of data points is used to solve the equation for the source position. The field data must sufficiently sample the anomalies in the data in order to produce an accurate estimate of the source locations. The structural index that is employed determines the depths of the Euler solutions from the potential data. As a result, four sets of grids are needed as input data for Euler deconvolution: the total magnetic field, the first horizontal derivative in x and y, and the vertical derivative in z. Magnetic inclination and declination have no effect on it. Therefore, reduction to the pole data can be used to ameliorate the situation, as the north-south-extended geologic characteristics show a low signal-to-noise ratio in the data115.

It is important to select a window size (cell size) that is both large enough to account for significant variation in the field gradients and small enough to exclude major impacts from many sources. In other words, small windows have a poor representation of broad anomalies originating from deep sources, and vice versa. Evidence suggests that the maximum depth returned is approximately twice the size of the selected structural index, and that a sufficient data sample determines the quality of the depth estimate. Therefore, choosing the SI and the best criteria for selecting solutions are essential to applying this method successfully.

Field study

In the course of geological and structural investigations, field observations are essential for comprehending the processes and history of the Earth. Such observations generally encompass comprehensive notes regarding rock types, stratigraphic relationships, mineral composition, and structural characteristics, including faults, folds, and joints. The orientations of bedding, fault planes, and fold axes are measured using instruments such as a compass and clinometer. Additionally, sketches, photographs, and GPS coordinates are frequently used to accurately document locations and outcrops. This field data serves as the foundation for interpreting geological history, reconstructing tectonic events, and creating geological maps.

Automatic lineament extraction

Lineament extraction was carried out on the first principal components (PC1) of the Landsat-8 images for the whole study area and separately for the Al Khunayqiyah and Al Amar mine sectors, where most of the ancient ore occurrences are concentrated (Fig. 6a). The extraction was performed using the LINE algorithm available in PCI Geomatica. Edge-gradient thresholds of 50 and 30 were adopted to optimize detection of linear features. The edge-gradient thresholds of 50 and 30 were chosen through a systematic, iterative sensitivity analysis in which values from 100 to 10 were tested. Thresholds between 100 and 60 failed to capture subtle lineaments, while values below 20 led to excessive noise and spurious detections. The thresholds of 50 and 30 were found to be the best trade-off between sensitivity and clarity to allow repeated detection of major and subtle structural features. The obtained lineaments were further processed in ArcMap to generate frequency-azimuth diagrams showing the trend of the lineaments and density maps per km2 for the different lithological units (Fig. 6a).

Structural setting

The Ar Rayn terrane, part of the Neoproterozoic Arabian Shield, lies west of the Ad Dawadimi terrane and is separated from it by the Al Amar Fault Zone (AAF) (Fig. 12a, b). The foliation in the Abt Formation (Dawadimi terrane) strikes north-south, dips westward (Fig. 9a). It faces westward, indicating the direction of younging (Fig. 12a). Tight, intrafolial folds (Fig. 12b) with north-south axial planes suggest at least two deformation phases: D1, associated with foliation, and D2, which formed the folds87. Petrographically, this formation includes muscovite-biotite-quartz-feldspar schist, as well as feldspathic and volcanic metagreywackes87,90.

The Ar Rayn terrane features a magmatic arc complex and syn- to post-orogenic intrusions75,83. The Al Amar Group (> 689 to ~ 625 Ma) comprises tholeiitic to calc-alkaline basaltic and rhyolitic volcanics, as well as volcaniclastic rocks, divided into eastern and western sequences83,116–120. High-Al TTG/adakite is present in the east, while low-Al TTG is found in the west, indicating west-dipping subduction and progressive arc development83,120. The Al Amar Fault serves as a major intra-arc structure that localizes magmatism and mineralization and may have enabled significant transcurrent displacement during the amalgamation of the Ar Rayn and Ad Dawadimi terranes83,116–120.

The Ar Rayn terrane is characterized by north-trending folds, faults, foliation (Fig. 9a), and localized schistosity (Fig. 12c). These structural features are crosscut by northwest-trending sinistral transcurrent faults exhibiting normal displacement118–121. The terrane’s structural fabric reflects three distinct deformation events. First (D1), the eastern sequence of Al Amar Group rocks developed foliation and localized schistosity. Subsequently (D2), oblique subduction during terrane amalgamation caused monoclinal folding in the western sequence of Al Amar Group rocks83. The initial deformation phase (D1) produced north-striking foliation and localized schistosity (Fig. 12c) within the eastern sequence of the Al Amar group. In contrast, the western sequence lacks these features and is instead distinguished by monoclinal folding and tilting associated with the D2 deformation event. The older age of the eastern sequence is indicated by the intrusion of the earliest-dated rocks, supporting a geological history influenced by D1 deformation. The D2 deformation phase was triggered by east–west contraction resulting from the convergence of the Ar Rayn and Ad Dawadimi terranes, which generated prominent north- and northwest-trending shear zones (Fig. 12d, e) that subdivide the region into distinct fault blocks83. The D3 deformation event led to the tilting and folding of Hamir group rocks into synclinoria in the southern portion of the study area, likely during late orogenesis following terrane amalgamation and possible continental collision83. Additionally, D3 deformation produced northwest-trending Najd faults and shearing along the Al Amar fault. Group-3 rock intrusions constrain the timing of the amalgamation event to approximately 616–607 Ma83.

A comprehensive petrological and kinematic analysis of the Al Amar region in the northern Ar Rayn terrane shows that metavolcanic and granitoid formations underwent deformation with kinematic vorticity numbers (Wm) ranging from 0.4 to 0.687,90,113–118,122,123. This reflects a mixed strain regime involving both simple and pure shear mechanisms122. Nappe stacking from thrusting along the Al Amar–Idsas fault produced subhorizontal foliations under high pressure, indicating vertical shortening during ductile strain accumulation122. The Halaban–Al Amar region experienced three tectonic phases: (1) convergence of the Afif and Ar Rayn plates, with oceanic crust (ophiolitic Urd group) between them81; (2) collision during the Al Amar orogeny, estimated at ~ 670 Ma; and (3) post-orogenic intracratonic events, including dioritic and leucogranitoid pluton emplacement from ~ 670 to 630 Ma81.

The Al Amar Fault Zone is the most recent suture in the Saudi Arabian Shield. Its activity dates to the middle to late Ediacaran period (~ 689 to 583 Ma), a time also marked by several other regional sutures16,117–129. The Al Amar-Idsas fault zone is defined by north-trending structures and prominent shear foliation that dips steeply to the west or is nearly vertical. Structural fabrics are of diverse orientations within the fault zone, such as north-south, northwest-southeast and northeast-southwest trends, and are often associated with steep plunges and well-developed mineral lineations. The tectonic history of the area is characterized by shear-zone deformation often associated with granitic intrusions. The Abt Formation consists of metamorphosed clastic sediments and chlorite-sericite schists119. The schists are moderately to strongly foliated and have thin bedding parallel to the shears119. This formation consists mainly of thin beds with widespread shearing and schistosity that are generally parallel to bedding, except at fold closures where they are crenulated by later folding events. The foliation in the Abt Formation trends north-south, dips to the west and faces west, indicating the direction of younging. The existence of tight, north-south trending axial planes of intrafolial folds also indicates the operation of at least two different deformation phases. The foliation is attributed to the earlier D1 phase, while the folds developed during the subsequent D2 phase87,120.

West of the Al Amar fault, volcanosedimentary rocks exhibit weak to moderate deformation, whereas granite gneiss is more intensely deformed. Weakly deformed volcanic rocks are observed near structural contacts. In this region, metamorphic rocks such as gneissic granite are intersected by syn- and post-tectonic intrusions (667 − 580 Ma) that exhibit local shearing and brecciation87,120. Numerous listric normal faults with NE-SW trends are observed throughout the study area, extending northward and southward. Although these faults are too minor to be individually mapped, they segment the region into a series of tilted blocks.

During this study, field observations supporting the proposed dextral horizontal slip along the Al Amar Fault included the presence of sigmoidal quartz porphyroclasts within mylonite (Fig. 12f). Microscale analyses of oriented thin sections revealed shear fabrics indicative of both dextral (Fig. 12g, h) and sinistral (Fig. 12i) movements. This combination of observations complicates the precise interpretation of the dominant sense of displacement along this major structural zone on the easternmost margin of the ANS.

Mineral deposits in the Ar Rayn terrane

The Ar Rayn terrane is known to contain various mineral resources including epithermal Au–Ag–Zn–Cu–barite deposits, Khnaiguiyah-type volcanic-hosted Zn–Cu–Fe–Mn mineralization, orogenic gold veins and potential Iron Oxide-Copper-Gold (IOCG) and porphyry copper systems83,130,131. The felsic volcanic rocks of the Shalahib Formation (Al Amar Group) are commonly associated with epithermal Zn–Pb ± Ag deposits120. Porphyry Cu ± Mo ± Au and IOCG mineralization is mainly related to synorogenic intrusive bodies close to the suture zone83,130. Orogenic gold veins are usually oriented along north-trending faults, particularly near to the AAF, suggesting their formation is related to the amalgamation of terranes132. The gold bearing veins occur most often in tightly folded, thrust bounded ophiolitic sequences16.

The Ar Rayn terrane is characterized by the presence of Khnaiguiyah-type Zn–Cu–Fe–Mn deposits, a major source of zinc, which are not known from other parts of the Arabian Shield. The Khnaiguiyah deposit comprises four major ore bodies with reserves varying from 10.56 Mt at 7.41% Zn and 0.82% Cu to 2.66 Mt at 15.10% Zn and 0.80% Cu126. This kind of deposit is identified by the clear signs of deformation and metamorphism that impact the volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) mineralization, and the stratiform, Mn-rich layers point to a hydrothermal origin related to ancient seafloor processes83,130. Besides, epithermal Au–Ag–Zn–Cu–barite veins are important producers of precious and base metal resources in the Ar Rayn terrane133–137.

An example of this is the Al Amar deposit, which hosts 1.55 Mt at 12.31 g/t Au in reserves and 3.0 Mt at 5.1 g/t Au in inferred reserves. Epithermal Au–Ag–Zn–Cu-barite vein deposits in the Ar Rayn terrane are concentrated near volcanic centers along the Al Amar fault zone within a tilted caldera setting and display characteristics of low-sulfidation epithermal veins83,130. Local talc alteration and carbonatization suggest a possible submarine origin. Copper–gold prospects in the northwest indicate the potential for porphyry Cu systems, with copper bearing veins associated with porphyritic intrusions83,130. The Ar Rayn terrane contains many, generally small, fault-controlled IOCG deposits of magnetite or hematite, with the largest resource being in the magnetite deposits of the Jabal Idsas district; hematite-rich deposits with copper and gold; all following a paragenetic sequence from amphibole to gold83,130. Orogenic gold vein deposits are characteristically low in sulfides and have average gold grades of 6–9 g/t. However drilling results suggest limited tonnage, and although some deposits yield local placer gold, these deposits remain under-explored83,130. The Al Amar Fault Zone is a persistent steeply-dipping structural boundary between the Ar Rayn and Ad Dawadimi terranes and a major conduit for mineralizing hydrothermal fluids. Its complex polyphase tectonic history (D1: foliation and schistosity development, D2: folding and monoclinal tilting, and D3: Najd transcurrent faulting and shearing) has produced a complex network of permeability pathways that focus and guide fluid flow. The north-trending foliation and local schistosity, particularly of the eastern sequence of the Al Amar Group, provided early migration pathways for fluids during the D1 stage and possibly also include the earliest alteration zones. Tight intrafolial folds and monoclinal tilting of the D2 phase created structural traps at fold hinges and along shear zones, which are spatially related to hydrothermal alteration and gold deposition. The D3 event produced NW-trending shear zones and subsidiary faults that enhanced secondary permeability and reactivated earlier structures, probably leading to remobilization or overprinting of mineralization.

Lineament characteristics of mineralized zones

Analysis of the Al Khunayqiyah area (Fig. 13a) shows that the major lineament trends are NNE and WNW, with minor trends in NE, NW, and ENE directions (Fig. 13b). This multi-directional pattern is evident in the complex structural history which may have facilitated fluid circulation and mineralization. The lineament density in this sector is moderately high with a marked concentration in the eastern part indicating spatial association between structural intensity and known mineralization. Conversely, the Al Amar mine area (Fig. 13c) shows moderate to low overall lineament density. The prevailing trends in this area are NNE-NE and then NW and WNW directions (Fig. 13d). The relatively lower density may either be a reflection of a genuinely less fractured structural domain, or the influence of surficial cover masking the expression of deeper lineaments. However, the fact that NNE-trending orientations are maintained throughout both mineralized sectors suggests that this structural orientation is regionally important in controlling mineralization.

Allocation of hydrothermal alterations

Spectral indices derived from Landsat-8 OLI were used to map hydrothermal alteration zones in Al Khunayqiyah and Al Amar areas. The band ratios were selected based on the eigenvector loadings from a principal component analysis of the mineralized areas where most known ore deposits are concentrated (Tables 1 and 2). The bands were chosen based on pairs of bands with high contrasting eigenvector loadings, which statistically increases the spectral contrast of specific mineral groups (as explained in the methodology). The ENVI identify tool was used to determine the alteration classification thresholds, identifying bright pixels corresponding to known mineralized sites. The DN values recorded range from 0.09 to 0.16 for all band ratios which are used as cut off values to define alteration zones.

The 7/6 ratio application successfully discriminated carbonate and Al-OH bearing minerals such as kaolinite, illite and carbonates, typical of phyllic and argillic alteration assemblages in both sectors of interest (Figs. 14a and 15a). The 6/4 ratio was useful for identifying ferrous silicate minerals such as chlorite and epidote, indicative of propylitic alteration zones (Figs. 15b and 16b). Meanwhile, the 6/5 ratio highlighted the distribution of ferric iron oxides such as hematite, goethite, and jarosite, representative of gossanous material and iron-rich cap rocks, indicative of oxidized sulfide systems (Figs. 14c and 15c).

The study of the Al Khunayqiyah area shows that the easternmost sector is the most altered and contains the greatest concentration of the mapped mineral assemblages, which coincide precisely with the location of ancient mine workings (Figs. 13a and 14). This apparent discrepancy could be explained by differences in the level of exposure, history of erosion or presence of buried mineralization under a cover surface.

Al Amar gold mine

The Al Amar deposit (Fig. 20a) is primarily an epithermal vein-style deposit. However, recent research by Surour and Bakhsh138 identified conformable VMS-style mineralization on the 640 level, suggesting that vein-type mineralization may represent remobilized VMS sulphide mineralization at depth. The Al Amar mine region includes the Abt Formation to the west of the Al Amar Fault zone (AAF) and the Al Amar Group to the east. The Abt Formation features a thick, folded sequence of conglomerate, greywacke, siltstone, shale, volcanic flows, and garnet-biotite hornfelsic schist (c. 800 Ma), intruded by a 724 Ma trondhjemite146. The Abt Formation features a series of N-trending quartz veins that run parallel to and intersect the foliation planes (Figs. 21a, b, c, and d). Elongated marble outcrops with distinctive mineralized quartz veins containing pyrite, calcite, fluorite, and copper minerals (Fig. 21e) are visible along the suture zone79,87. The Al Amar Group (667 Ma) is intruded by various syn- and post-orogenic rocks146. It is divided into lower meta-andesite and upper silicic volcanic flows, further classified into four volcanic units based on lithology and geochemistry147,148. The volcanic sequence comprises four units: Unit 1 contains intermediate- and mafic-pyroclastics, ash falls, and an upper lava flow. Unit 2 primarily consists of felsic pyroclastics with jasper and chert bands typical of the Al Amar group. Unit 3 includes intermediate to felsic pyroclastics, volcanic massive sulfides, barite, and Fe–Mn carbonates. Unit 4 features volcanic siltstone, sandstone, crystal tuff, and volcanogenic carbonate138,139. The Al Amar deposits are categorized into volcanic massive-to-submassive sulfides confined to Unit 3138, stratiform deposits, and vein-type deposits found in Units 1, 2, and 3. The upper 250 m succession displays mineralized quartz veins containing native gold, characterized by milky quartz149–151.

The Al Amar deposit (Fig. 20a), discovered in metavolcanic rocks, was initially identified as gold-copper-lead-zinc vein mineralization, then reclassified as a massive sulphide, and later as an epithermal gold deposit. It is associated with five volcanic cycles, with early mineralization occurring in quartz-rich veins and sulphide lenses within felsic volcanic units138. Most gold is concentrated in two main zones: the North vein running WNW-ESE and dipping toward SW, and the sub-parallel Breccia vein. Early mineralization appeared as a stockwork of quartz (Fig. 21f), pyrite, chlorite, sericite, barite, and sphalerite in ‘Unit 2.’ Sulphide lenses with barite and talc occur at the top of felsic ‘Unit 3’138.

The Al-Amar gold mine generates 150 kg of waste annually during extraction, with zones of silicification, chloritization, and pyritization151. It features well-crystallized minerals like pyrite, chalcopyrite, and sphalerite, along with smaller amounts of galena, magnetite, tetrahedrite, and hematite138. Gold resides within a sulfide and quartz mineral matrix152. Despite significant deposits of gold, silver, zinc, and copper, the mine extracts gold at low concentrations152,153. The annual raw gold ore production can reach 200,000 tons, but 40% is discarded in tailings dams, which contain an average of 1–12 µg g − 1 of gold and silver, respectively151,152,154.

The Umm Ash Shalahib mine area

The Umm Ash Shalahib region (Fig. 20b) is the largest mining hub in the belt and lies within the volcanic-volcaniclastic Al Amar Group143,144,155. Subvolcanic rhyolite intrudes both sedimentary and volcanic rocks, and is closely linked to mineralization, either within the rhyolite or at its contact with surrounding rocks. Updated maps show three distinct rhyolite formations: the northernmost consists of northeast-trending rhyolite dikes145,156; the central is mainly rhyolitic intrusive breccia with milky quartz veinlets; and the southern is elongated and contains intrusive breccia cemented by quartz155. The primary host rocks for mineralization are rhyolitic tuffs and flows, which exhibit shearing and alteration, including carbonatization, resulting in calcareous rhyolitic tuffs and epidote-quartz rocks145. Other important host rocks include marbles and skarns, which are rich in calcite, garnet, and diopside145. Stratigraphic analysis indicates that the rocks are calc-alkaline, with more pyroclastic deposits than lava flows and a greater abundance of interbedded carbonate sedimentary rocks156.

The main mineralization at Umm Ash Shalahib occurs as small, northeast-trending, massive lenses containing disseminated sulfides and magnetite (Fig. 20b)145. They are classified as massive sulfide-magnetite, disseminated sulfide-magnetite, massive magnetite, and disseminated magnetite. In the upper parts of the rhyolitic and andesitic deposits, a particularly good development of martitization of magnetite, characterized by hematite lamellae along the octahedral planes of magnetite, is observed145. Historically, several gossan outcrops were mined in the southern part of the district, including open pits and trenches along oxidation zones. These gossans are characterized by strong brown to reddish-brown ferruginous features within a siliceous, clay-like groundmass, with widths of 50 cm to 10 m and oxidation depths of up to 40 m in old trenches145. The alteration signature of the host rocks includes pervasively silicified rhyolite breccia and tuffs. The ore minerals observed in these gossans are oxidized iron varieties, malachite and chrysocolla145.

The ancient workings at Umm Ash Shalahib are the most thoroughly examined prospect in the gold belt. The area includes a rhyodacite flow dome, an older pyroclastic unit, and a younger epiclastic sedimentary formation. Trenches reveal significant shearing and quartz stockwork oriented N60°E and dipping 70° NW, although the main structural trend is NW-SE. Mineralization occurs as: (1) disseminated gold (< 6 μm) in exhalative barite lenses, with gold values up to 45 g/t, associated with pyrite, sphalerite, galena, chalcopyrite, and minor silver sulphosalts; (2) gold disseminated in siliceous alteration and quartz stockwork zones along NE-striking faults, with gold values from 0.5 to 10 g/t, associated with pyrite, chalcopyrite, covellite, chalcocite, and rutile; and (3) gold mineralization along structural contacts or at dike margins139,157–159. While gold mineralization is predominant, copper-zinc mineralization is also present in volcanogenic massive sulphide (VMS)-type rhyolite breccia. Magnetite-bearing skarns are found as well. The base metal content at Umm Ash Shalahib indicates that an epithermal gold metallogenic model is more appropriate than a traditional orogenic gold model. Despite significant intersections from recent exploration, such as a 1.65 m interval yielding 40.7 g Au/t, 51 g Ag/t, 0.16% Cu, 0.45% Zn, and 0.05% Pb, recent efforts have focused on stratabound base metals rather than cross-cutting structures that could host gold mineralization139.

Recent studies have identified three mineralized bodies through drilling activities. The A1 body holds 110,000 tonnes with average grades of 10.6 g Au/t, 55.5 g Ag/t, and 5.2% Zn. The D1 body consists of 60,000 tonnes at 4 g Au/t, 4 g Ag/t, and 4% Zn. The E1 and E2 bodies together amount to 900,000 tonnes, with grades of 7.2% Zn and 3 g Ag/t139,160.

Jabal At Taybi gold site

Ancient mining at the Jabal At Taybi gold site was concentrated on base metals. In the 1970 s, BRGM explored copper and zinc mineralization, which showed significant mineralization, but the gold potential was initially overlooked139. In the 1990 s, attention turned to gold, with detailed geological, geochemical, and drilling studies based on models from the Al Amar deposit and on new gold discoveries139. Bedrock sampling in 1994 revealed gold and silver mineralization associated with barite and siliceous breccia at the margin of a carbonate body hosted in rhyolitic tuff139. This mineralization exhibited an epithermal geochemical signature and high precious-metal grades, and was then recommended for further mapping, sampling, geochemical and geophysical surveys, and drilling139. Not much has been done since Ma’aden took over in the late 1990 s despite the promising results and positive comparisons with other gold deposits139. Samples for gold and silver were ultimately taken at the Taybi, where a major barite-carbonate formation within rhyolite was found to contain high silver concentrations, with up to 199.5 g Ag/t. The carbonate is associated with a jasperoid siliceous facies and siliceous breccias139.

The Khnaiguiyah Zn-Cu deposits

The Khnaiguiyah deposit, located at the northern end of the Ar Rayn belt, is a significant Zn-Cu mineralization site. It lies within a volcanosedimentary sequence of ignimbritic lavas and tuffs interbedded with carbonate and chert, and exhibits an alkaline chemical affinity139,159. The stratigraphy comprises four volcanic cycles139,159: (i) intermediate volcanic rocks and banded ignimbrites forming the footwall of type 1 mineralization; (ii) NE-trending fissural silicic lavas and carbonate lenses forming ridges that host type 1 mineralization; (iii) thick ignimbritic flows and tuffaceous equivalents serving as the hanging wall for type 1 and hosting type 2 mineralization; and (iv) post-erosion ignimbrite and lahars from volcanism V4 overlying earlier sequences. The initial volcanic activity was associated with northeast-southwest normal faulting, followed by regional uplift and caldera formation139,159.

Within the Khnaiguiyah area, nine distinct mineralized prospects have been identified, comprising two principal mineralization types. The first type is characterized by stratiform lenses that contain magnetite, hematite, pyrite, sphalerite, chalcopyrite, and galena, which display both vertical and lateral zoning and are frequently capped by a carbonate assemblage139,159. The second type primarily consists of pyrite, chalcopyrite, hematite, and sphalerite, with variable amounts of lead and bismuth tellurides, and is associated with felsic volcanic domes139,159. Both mineralization types exhibit hydrothermal alteration: Type 1 is characterized by alteration of minerals such as epidote, garnet, amphibole, pyroxene, and chlorite, whereas Type 2 predominantly alters quartz, sericite, and pyrite139,159.

Umm Ash Shash prospect

The Umm Ash Shash (Wadi Al Furu) prospect is a Cu-Ag occurrence hosted within a belt of andesitic volcanic and volcanosedimentary rocks, located some 15 km south of Al Amar. Take samples of up to 1.7% Cu and 150 g Ag/t152. They classified the mineralization in the area as an Intrusion-Related Gold System (IOGC)130. Geological sketch maps were made at a scale of 1:1,000, and 20 surface samples were collected, giving values up to 4.5% Cu, 0.4 g Au/t, and 64.36% Fe130,139.

The Umm Ash Shash area consists of a complex lithologic sequence of the Al Amar Group, including mafic to felsic tuffs, carbonates, conglomerate breccias, chert, and felsic porphyry. The structural fabric in the Umm Ash Shash area is oriented N-S and dips steeply to the east139. Mineral alterations (phyllic, propylitic, silicification, and carbonatization) are present and are usually associated with pyrite, forming the mineralized rocks. Three main prospects (A, B and C) have been identified139. Prospect A contains malachite, azurite, and hematite in a cherty tuffite unit associated with ancient mining, with grab samples assaying up to 19,470 ppm Cu, 133 ppm Zn, 21 ppm Pb, and 37.4 ppm Ag. Prospect B is a carbonate unit with fine-grained disseminated pyrite and sphalerite that hosts calcite-vein zinc mineralization and returned up to 2,800 ppm Zn, 1,470 ppm Pb, 153 ppm Ag, and 99 ppm Cu, plus a single high but likely unrepresentative 20,120 ppm Zn sample from the vein152. Prospect C is characterized by disseminated pyrite in chert, with generally low metal values but locally up to 7,700 ppm Zn, 1,754 ppm Pb, and 303 ppm Ag139. Discussion

Lithological and structural controls on gold mineralization along the Al Amar Fault Zone

The Al Amar Fault Zone (AAF) is a major tectonic suture within the Neoproterozoic Arabian Shield, separating the Ar Rayn terrane from the Ad Dawadimi terrane75,83. This fault zone plays a critical role in localizing gold mineralization due to its complex lithological framework and structural history. The integration of remote sensing, aeromagnetic data, and field validation allows these lithological and structural controls to be mapped at regional scale and refined at depth, which is not possible from surface geology alone.

Gold mineralization in the Amar Fault Zone is closely associated with a diverse suite of host rocks. On the western side of the AAF, the Abt Formation consists of muscovite-biotite-quartz-feldspar schists, feldspathic and volcanic metagreywackes, and interbedded conglomerates, greywackes, and hornfelsic schists87. To the east, the Al Amar Group comprises tholeiitic to calc-alkaline volcanics and volcaniclastic rocks, reflecting a magmatic arc setting and divided into distinct eastern and western sequences83,116–118,130. These rocks have undergone deformation, metamorphism, and hydrothermal alteration, enhancing their potential to host mineralized quartz veins and sulfide lenses87,90,123. Remote sensing image processing (Fig. 5) successfully discriminated these lithological units across the study area, and field validation confirmed the interpreted contacts, demonstrating that spectral mapping can effectively delineate prospective host rocks where surface exposure is adequate.

Gold is primarily concentrated within: (i) Felsic volcanic and volcaniclastic units (notably the Al Amar Group), which are favorable for the development of stockwork quartz veins and massive sulfide-hosted mineralization83,138; (ii) Metasedimentary schists and greywackes of the Abt Formation, which provide chemically and structurally receptive sites for hydrothermal fluid flow and gold deposition87; (iii) Syn- and post-orogenic intrusions that locally enhance permeability and serve as both sources and traps for mineralizing fluids83. The aeromagnetic data (Fig. 7) provide a regional framework for these host rocks by revealing magnetic contrasts that correspond to the distribution of volcanic versus sedimentary units, thereby refining the geological map in areas of poor exposure.

The AAF itself is a long-lived, N-S to NW-SE trending, steeply dipping shear zone. Its multi-phase tectonic history has produced a complex structural architecture characterized by: (i) North-trending foliation and localized schistosity (D1 phase, especially in the eastern sequence of the Al Amar Group), providing planar conduits for fluid migration87,123; (ii) Tight intrafolial folding (D2 phase), generating structural traps where gold-bearing fluids can concentrate87; (iii) Prominent north- and northwest-trending shear zones and subsidiary faults created during terrane amalgamation (D2 and D3 phases), further subdividing the host rocks into fault blocks and enhancing secondary permeability83; (iv) Multiple sets of faults and lineaments (NNE, NE, NW, ENE), which control the orientation and continuity of mineralized zones76,116–118,130; and (v) Listric normal faults and tilted blocks that fragment the area and may focus hydrothermal fluid flow into structural lows and intersections83. Shear sense indicators (dextral and sinistral movements) and the presence of mylonitic and brecciated rocks along the fault zone record intense deformation, which is critical for repeated fracturing and fluid pumping during mineralization87,123. The aeromagnetic LSF maps (Figs. 9b and 10a and b) directly support this multi-phase history by separating shallow (RES) from deep (REG) structural trends. The persistence of NNE-NE trends at both levels indicates basement control, while the greater dominance of NW trends at depth confirms the overprint of Najd-related (D3) structures, as observed in the field (Figs. 12d-i). This depth-dependent information is a key contribution of the geophysical data that cannot be obtained from surface mapping alone.

The temporal evolution of these deformation phases is directly linked to distinct alteration assemblages (Supplementary Table 2). D1 was accompanied by propylitic alteration, D2 represents the main phyllic-argillic episode, and D3 is associated with gossan development. The D2-D3 transition was the most favorable for gold deposition, as ductile fabrics and brittle fracturing created optimal permeability for hydrothermal fluids.

Gold is commonly found in low-sulfidation epithermal quartz veins (e.g., Al Amar deposit), typically parallel or oblique to the main fault zone, and within stockworks in felsic volcanics83,138. At depth, gold can occur within remobilized VMS-style sulfide bodies, particularly in altered felsic units138. Gold grades and tonnages are often highest where major faults, foliation planes, and fold hinges intersect, thereby increasing porosity and promoting precipitation from mineralizing fluids83,138. Zones of intense hydrothermal alteration (silicification, chloritization, pyritization, carbonatization, phyllic and argillic assemblages) are spatially associated with both high-density lineament zones and mineralized quartz veins16,139. The correlation between alteration, lineament density, and known gold occurrences underscores the critical role of structural permeability in localizing mineralization.

The remote sensing lineament extraction (Fig. 6) quantifies this correlation by showing that the NNE-NE trend is the most prominent regional orientation, directly matching the field-measured shear zone orientations (Figs. 8e and 12d). The lineament density map (Fig. 6b) shows that moderate-to-high densities coincide with field-mapped zones of enhanced fracturing (e.g., the mylonite location in Fig. 12f), thereby providing a predictive tool for targeting permeable structural sites.

The application of targeted band ratios guided by eigenvector loadings from principal component analysis (Tables 1 and 2) successfully delineated a coherent and zoned hydrothermal alteration system. The 7/6 ratio mapped phyllic-argillic alteration (carbonate and Al-OH-bearing minerals) (Figs. 13a and 14a), the 6/4 ratio delineated propylitic alteration (ferrous silicate minerals) (Figs. 13b and 14b), and the 6/5 ratio highlighted ferric iron oxides diagnostic of gossans and iron-rich cap rocks (Figs. 13c and 14c). The spatial correlation among these products reveals systematic zonation, with propylitic alteration occupying broader areas, phyllic-argillic alteration more restricted and spatially associated with known mines, and iron oxide caps marking the most prospective zones. Field validation confirmed that these spectral anomalies correspond to actual alteration minerals in outcrop and ancient workings (Figs. 13a and 14). The integration of alteration mapping with lineament density (Figs. 13d and 14d) shows that the most intense alteration occurs at the intersection of multi-directional fracture networks, demonstrating how remote sensing can guide exploration to structurally controlled hydrothermal systems.

Lineament patterns provide crucial insights into the structural controls on this alteration system. In the Al Khunayqiyah area, the dominant trends are NNE and WNW, with subordinate orientations of NE, NW, and ENE (Fig. 13a and b). This multi-directional pattern created enhanced permeability through fracture intersection, explaining the moderately high lineament density concentrated in the eastern part where ancient mines occur (Fig. 14d). The marked coincidence between zones of intense alteration and high lineament density in this area (Figs. 14d and 15d) provides compelling evidence that fracture networks created permeability pathways focusing hydrothermal fluids and enhancing water-rock interaction. The aeromagnetic data for Al Khunayqiyah (Figs. 16 and 17) independently support this interpretation: the LSF-RES map (Fig. 16g) identifies NNE, NW, N-S, and NE as the main shallow structural directions, and the intersection of NNE and WNW trends corresponds to the highest alteration intensity, validating the remote sensing lineament analysis.

The Al Amar mine area exhibits moderate-to-low lineament density, with dominant NNE-NE trends (Figs. 13c and d and 15d). The consistent presence of NNE-oriented trends across both mineralized sectors underscores the regional significance of this structural orientation in controlling fluid flow and mineralization, even where overall fracture density is lower. The aeromagnetic data for the Al Amar mine area (Figs. 18 and 19) resolve an apparent discrepancy: the eastern sector hosts most of the old mines but shows lower alteration signatures. The RTP map (Fig. 18a) reveals a strong positive magnetic anomaly there, and the LSF-REG (Fig. 18f) and LSF-RES (Fig. 18g) maps show N-S- and NE-trending structures at depth. This integration suggests that mineralization in the eastern sector may be controlled at depth or concealed beneath surface cover, a hypothesis supported by field observations of listric normal faults and tilted blocks. The combination of remote sensing, aeromagnetics, and field data thus provides a three-dimensional view of the mineralizing system, identifying both shallow surface targets and deeper hidden potential.

Integrated remote sensing and aeromagnetic analysis for gold exploration

The integration of remote sensing, aeromagnetic data, and field validation enables the mapping of these lithological and structural controls at a regional scale and their refinement at depth, which is not possible using surface geology alone.

The integration of Landsat-8 image processing (Fig. 5), automatic lineament extraction (Figs. 6, 7 and 8a), and aeromagnetic LSF maps (Figs. 9b and 10a and b) provides a regional-scale framework that links surface lithology to subsurface structure. Remote sensing successfully discriminated among the Abt formation, the Al Amar group, and syn- to post-orogenic intrusives. At the same time, the aeromagnetic data revealed depth-dependent structural trends (NNE-NE persisting at both regional and shallow levels, and NW trends dominant at depth). This integration confirms that NNE-NE orientations represent basement anisotropies controlling fluid flow, and NW trends reflect Najd-related (D3) overprinting, as independently observed in field shear fabrics (Figs. 12d–i).

Gold concentration in the AAF is known to favor felsic volcanic and volcaniclastic units of the Al Amar Group, metasedimentary schists of the Abt Formation, and syn- to post-orogenic intrusions83,87,121. The remote sensing alteration indices (7/6, 6/4, 6/5 ratios; Figs. 14 and 15) successfully mapped phyllic-argillic, propylitic, and gossanous zones, respectively. Field validation confirmed that these spectral anomalies correspond to actual alteration minerals in outcrop and ancient mine workings (Figs. 13a and 14). The spatial correlation among the three alteration products reveals systematic zonation, with iron oxide caps marking the most prospective areas.

Lineament patterns derived from remote sensing (Fig. 13) directly correlate with field-measured structures. In the Al Khunayqiyah area, the dominant NNE and WNW trends (with subordinate NE, NW, ENE) create multi-directional fracture intersections that enhance permeability. The moderate-to-high lineament density in the eastern part coincides with ancient mines (Fig. 14b and d), and the marked coincidence with intense alteration (Figs. 14b and d and 15b and d) demonstrates that fracture networks focused hydrothermal fluids. The aeromagnetic LSF-RES map (Fig. 16g) independently confirms NNE, NW, N-S, and NE as the main shallow structural directions, validating the remote sensing interpretation.

In the Al Amar mine area, the lineament density is moderate to low, with dominant NNE-NE trends (Figs. 13c and d and 15d). This consistent NNE orientation in both mineralized sectors attests to its regional significance. The aeromagnetic data (Figs. 18 and 19) resolve an important discrepancy: although the eastern sector hosts most of the old mines, it shows less surface alteration. The strong positive magnetic anomaly in the RTP map (Fig. 18a) and the N-S/NE-trending structures at depth in the LSF-REG and LSF-RES maps (Fig. 18f and g) suggest that mineralization may be controlled at depth or concealed beneath cover. Field observations of listric normal faults and tilted blocks support this.

The lineaments extracted from the LSF investigation show a clear spatial correlation with the main shear zones and faults identified in the aeromagnetic analysis (Figs. 9, 10, 16 and 18). Most of the major NE- and NW-trending features coincide with known structural elements, and thus both datasets are considered good indicators of the main tectonic fabric of the study area. This insight emphasizes the importance of the LSF method for identifying surface and sub-surface signatures of regional structural elements. Furthermore, some of the lineaments derived from LSF do not have obvious counterparts on existing geological maps, which indicates the presence of previously hidden or unrecognised faults and structural elements.

The combination of remote sensing (surface lithology, lineaments, alteration), aeromagnetics (subsurface structural architecture) and field validation (shear fabrics, fault contacts, alteration verification) provides a three-dimensional image of the mineralizing system as a whole. This approach detects shallow surface targets (e.g., eastern Al Khunayqiyah) and deeper hidden potential (e.g., eastern Al Amar mine area), emphasizing the significance of multi-data integration for exploration in the Arabian Shield.

The different characteristics of the Al Khunayqiyah and Al Amar areas may reflect differences in their exhumation and erosion histories. The Al Khunayqiyah zone exhibits widespread surface hydrothermal alteration, indicating a moderate to higher erosion rate that has exposed mineralized rocks. Conversely, the eastern Al Amar area is characterized by a higher density of structural lineaments, but only minor surface alteration. This contrast suggests that the hydrothermal system may be only partially exposed and may still be partially concealed by surface cover or at greater depths. Finally, the presence of significant magnetic structural features and persistent anomalies along these structural corridors also supports the interpretation that the area contains favorable deep conditions for mineralization. The eastern Al Amar zone is therefore a promising exploration target, requiring further ground geophysical investigations and drilling to confirm the existence of concealed mineralised zones.

Recommendations for future work

To validate the geophysical anomalies identified in the eastern Al Amar sector and advance our interpretation from speculative to substantive, we recommend a phased exploration program comprising three integrated workstreams: first, a targeted drilling campaign designed to intersect the interpreted structural and alteration zones at depth, thereby providing direct geological evidence of concealed mineralization; second, deep soil and rock geochemical surveys combined with downhole geochemical analysis to characterize the metal tenor and alteration mineralogy of the concealed targets, which is essential for assessing the economic potential of the system; and third, the integration of drill-core hyperspectral data and geophysical logging with our existing 3D model, which would significantly improve the resolution and accuracy of the subsurface interpretation by enabling real-time mineral mapping and quantitative validation of the remote sensing and aeromagnetic signatures.
