Mesial temporal involvement in epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy revealed by stereoelectroencephalography: illustrative case
Authors: Yasushi Iimura, Osamu Akiyama, Kazuki Nomura, Hiroharu Suzuki, Takumi Mitsuhashi, Tetsuya Ueda, Kazuki Nishioka, Hiroyuki Onuki, Hidenori Sugano, Akihide Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntracranial epidermoid tumors are rare benign lesions that are typically nonepileptogenic; however, temporal lobe involvement may result in drug-resistant epilepsy. The relationship between the lesion and the epileptogenic network remains poorly understood, particularly due to limited electrophysiological evaluation of mesial temporal structures.OBSERVATIONSA 52-year-old left-handed woman with drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy associated with an epidermoid tumor underwent staged surgical management. Intracranial epidermoid tumors are rare benign lesions that arise from ectodermal inclusions during embryogenesis and are most commonly located in extra-axial compartments such as the cerebellopontine angle.1,2 Although generally considered nonepileptogenic, epidermoid tumors involving or extending into the temporal lobe may present with drug-resistant epilepsy.3–5

The mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis in tumor-related epilepsy are complex and extend beyond the structural lesion itself, often involving the surrounding cortex and distributed epileptogenic networks. However, in epidermoid tumor–related epilepsy, the relationship between the tumor and the epileptogenic zone remains poorly understood,5 and surgical strategies have largely been based on anatomical considerations rather than electrophysiological evidence. In particular, the involvement of mesial temporal structures, including the hippocampus, has not been systematically evaluated, partly due to the limited use of invasive intracranial electrocorticography (ECoG) in epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy.4 Previous reports have relied on subdural grid electrodes, which sample the cortical surface and are therefore limited to recordings from structures such as the parahippocampal gyrus rather than the hippocampus itself.4 In contrast, stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) enables direct recording from deep mesial temporal structures, including the hippocampus, allowing a more comprehensive evaluation of the epileptogenic network.

Here, we report a case of epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy investigated using SEEG, demonstrating hippocampal involvement beyond the tumor margin. This case highlights the value of a staged SEEG-guided approach in elucidating epileptogenic networks and optimizing surgical strategy.

Illustrative Case

Patient History

A 52-year-old left-handed woman first presented at age 25 years with focal impaired consciousness (FIC) seizures preceded by an aura, later evolving into focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. Thereafter, FIC seizures occurred on a daily to weekly basis. The semiology was characterized by impaired awareness, oral automatisms, motionless staring, and behavioral arrest. Despite treatment with multiple antiseizure medications, including carbamazepine, lamotrigine, perampanel, levetiracetam, brivaracetam, and zonisamide, seizures persisted on a daily to weekly basis. She was considered to have drug-resistant epilepsy and was referred to our department for further evaluation and management.

Presurgical Evaluation

On the prolonged scalp video electroencephalography, interictal epileptic discharges originated from the left temporal region. MRI demonstrated an approximately 4-cm cystic mass that showed high intensity on diffusion-weighted imaging, low intensity on T1-weighted imaging, high intensity on T2-weighted imaging, and heterogeneous intensity on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging, without gadolinium enhancement (Fig. 1A–F). The lesion extended from the uncus to the entorhinal cortex and further into the posterior fossa, with mild compression of the brainstem. The left hippocampal head (HH) demonstrated high intensity on FLAIR imaging, without obvious atrophy (Fig. 2A–F). Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) showed hypometabolism in the mass and the surrounding regions extending from the entorhinal cortex to the uncus (Fig. 1G and H), as well as in the HH to the hippocampal body (HB) (Fig. 2G and H). Functional MRI demonstrated left-hemisphere dominance for language. On the Wechsler Memory Scale–Revised (WMS-R), the verbal memory score was 89, the visual memory score was 71, the general memory score was 81, the attention/concentration score was 84, and the delayed recall score was 79.

Initial Surgery

A staged strategy was adopted because the lesion extended across the middle and posterior fossae, the epileptogenic mechanism remained uncertain, and aggressive one-stage epidermoid removal in the dominant hemisphere was considered potentially high risk due to adhesions to surrounding neurovascular structures. Following histopathological confirmation and posterior fossa tumor decompression, SEEG was performed to define the epileptogenic network before tailored temporal lobe surgery. A mass extending into the posterior fossa was resected via a lateral suboccipital approach. Histopathological examination confirmed that the mass components consisted predominantly of keratin debris, and the cyst wall was lined by keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with a granular layer, consistent with an epidermoid tumor. Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful course without any new neurological deficits. No aseptic meningitis, a complication characteristic of epidermoid tumor, was observed, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 8.

Subsequently, SEEG was performed to determine whether the hippocampus was involved in the seizure onset zone.

SEEG Implantation and Findings

Seven SEEG electrodes were implanted in the left temporal lobe under guidance of the ROSA ONE Brain system (Zimmer Biomet) to evaluate mesial temporal involvement and its relationship to the mass, targeting the entorhinal cortex, amygdala (Amy), HH, HB, posterior temporal cortex, superior temporal gyrus, and supramarginal gyrus (Fig. 3). Interictal spikes were generated from anterior and posterior lateral temporal cortex and mesial HH and HB. Habitual FIC seizures were captured, with EEG onset characterized by a baseline shift followed by low-voltage fast activity in the mesial HB and HH and posterior temporal cortex, with subsequent propagation throughout the left temporal lobe (Fig. 3). These findings indicated that the seizure onset zone involved the HH and HB, as well as the mesial posterior temporal region.

Functional mapping demonstrated a significant increase in high–gamma band power (60–140 Hz) in electrodes located in the posterior left temporal lobe during the auditory naming task, suggesting the involvement of language function in the posterior left temporal lobe.6 Based on these findings, a limited anterior temporal lobectomy was planned to preserve language function.

Second Surgery and Postoperative Course

Based on the SEEG findings, the patient underwent left anterior temporal lobectomy, consisting of the anterior lateral temporal lobe and HH, including the epidermoid tumors. Intraoperative ECoG was performed via a transsylvian approach to the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle. Recordings were obtained from electrodes placed in the Amy, HH, HB, and anterior lateral temporal cortex (LT1–3, representing 3 electrodes placed along the lateral temporal cortex from anterior to posterior) (Fig. 4A). Interictal spikes were predominantly generated from the HB, while less prominent activity was observed in the HH and LT1 (Fig. 4A). Following anterior temporal lobectomy including resection of the epidermoid tumor (Fig 4B–D), intraoperative ECoG was repeated, with recordings obtained from the Amy, HH, and HB. Interictal spikes were identified in the HH (Fig. 4E), and therefore partial resection of the HH and HB was performed. The pia mater overlying the medial temporal lobe was fragile, and to avoid injury to the brainstem, the hippocampus was removed in a piecemeal fashion using suction rather than en bloc; therefore, no tissue specimen of hippocampus was available for pathological examination.

Postoperative MRI confirmed complete resection of the epidermoid tumor, with partial resection of the hippocampus (Fig. 5A–C). Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful course without any new neurological deficits. No aseptic meningitis was observed, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 11. At the 1-year follow-up, the seizure outcome was classified as International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) class III, with no new neurological deficits. Residual seizures were limited to auras, with only a single FIC seizure observed.

Neuropsychological assessment using the WMS-R revealed improvement in memory function over time. At 6 months postoperatively, the verbal memory, visual memory, general memory, attention/concentration, and delayed recall scores were 72, 69, 66, 79, and 57, respectively. At 1 year postoperatively, the corresponding scores had improved to 86, 75, 80, 89, and 86, respectively.

Discussion

Observations

This case provides insights into the epileptogenic mechanisms and surgical strategy for epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy guided by SEEG. Epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy is rare,1,2 and detailed electrophysiological characterization of the epileptogenic networks in epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy remains limited.4 The present case adds to the literature by demonstrating mesial temporal involvement and highlighting the role of SEEG in guiding surgical decision-making.

This case differs from previous reports of epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy in several important aspects. Prior studies evaluated epileptogenicity mainly through anatomical and pathological correlations, while invasive monitoring with chronic subdural electrodes provided only indirect and spatially limited assessment of seizure onset.4 Recordings using subdural electrodes are limited in their ability to directly sample deep mesial temporal structures, and activity from the hippocampus is often inferred indirectly from the parahippocampal gyrus.7

In contrast, SEEG in the present case enabled direct recording of ictal activity from the hippocampus, allowing precise delineation of mesial temporal involvement. This direct sampling of deep structures provides a more accurate characterization of the epileptogenic network and reduces the risk of mislocalization inherent to surface-based recordings. SEEG has been shown to be a safe and effective method for investigating deep epileptogenic structures in medically intractable epilepsy.8 These differences underscore the advantage of SEEG over subdural electrodes in evaluating mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, particularly when the relationship between the lesion and deep epileptogenic structures is unclear.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the epileptogenic involvement of the hippocampus in the present case may be explained by epidermoid tumor–hippocampus interaction. A predisposition to adhesion between the epidermoid tumor and the hippocampus may have rendered hippocampal pyramidal neurons abnormally susceptible to chemical mediators contained within the epidermoid tumor.4 Repeated epileptic discharges arising from this altered microenvironment may, in turn, have led to progressive neuronal loss within the hippocampus. This mechanism may partly explain the mesial temporal epileptogenicity identified by SEEG.

Although dual pathology cannot be completely excluded, several findings suggest a potential relationship between the epidermoid tumor and mesial temporal epileptogenesis in the present case. The hippocampal MRI abnormality was characterized by FLAIR hyperintensity without significant atrophy, which may be more consistent with reactive change than with typical hippocampal sclerosis. Furthermore, although the entorhinal cortex was in direct contact with the epidermoid tumor, SEEG demonstrated seizure onset in the HH and HB rather than the entorhinal cortex itself. This finding argues against a simple direct-contact mechanism and may instead support the development of a secondary mesial temporal epileptogenic network through chronic inflammation, gliosis, or long-standing tumor-brain interaction.9–12

This interpretation is consistent with contemporary concepts of epileptogenic networks, in which anatomically connected temporal lobe structures may undergo progressive recruitment into an epileptogenic network over time.10,11

The present findings may also be interpreted in the context of low-grade epilepsy-associated tumors (LEATs), in which the epileptogenic zone is often centered on the lesion and surrounding neocortex, but may extend beyond the tumor itself. Previous studies on tumor-related epilepsy have consistently shown that the epileptogenic zone often extends beyond the structural lesion, reflecting involvement of the surrounding cortex and distributed epileptogenic networks.12,13 In temporal lobe LEATs, seizure control is influenced not only by complete tumor removal but also by the extent of resection beyond the lesion. Tailored resection incorporating hippocampectomy and/or corticectomy has been associated with improved seizure outcomes compared with lesionectomy alone, and extended resection independently predicts seizure freedom.14 Consistent with this concept, electrophysiological evidence from intracranial EEG studies in tumor-related epilepsy has demonstrated that the seizure onset and early spread zone (SOSz) frequently extends beyond the tumor margin, often involving regions located more than 1.5 cm from the lesion, and that complete resection of the SOSz is associated with favorable seizure outcomes.15 This suggests that epileptogenic activity may involve mesial temporal structures beyond the tumor, supporting a network-level mechanism rather than a purely lesion-centered process.

The ILAE class III outcome in the present case may reflect incomplete resection of the mesial temporal epileptogenic network, particularly residual HB or tail structures. Alternatively, persistent seizures may indicate a more extensive temporal epileptogenic network involving both mesial and lateral temporal regions. The limited extent of resection, including partial hippocampal resection and restricted anterior lateral temporal resection, was intentionally chosen to preserve memory function in the dominant hemisphere. Preoperative neuropsychological assessment demonstrated relatively preserved verbal memory function (WMS-R verbal memory score 89), and functional MRI suggested left-hemisphere language dominance despite the patient’s left-handedness. Therefore, extensive resection of the dominant mesial temporal structures, including the HB and hippocampal tail, was considered to carry a potential risk of postoperative memory decline. However, this tailored strategy may have reduced the likelihood of complete seizure freedom. Previous studies have demonstrated that more extensive resection of the dominant hippocampus and lateral temporal neocortex is associated with greater postoperative memory decline,16 underscoring the balance between seizure control and cognitive preservation in dominant temporal lobe epilepsy surgery. Importantly, SEEG findings influenced surgical planning by identifying hippocampal involvement while simultaneously highlighting the need to balance seizure control against the risk of postoperative cognitive decline.

Lessons

This case highlights the importance of evaluating the epileptogenic network beyond the structural lesion in epidermoid tumor–related temporal lobe epilepsy. Even in the presence of a clearly identifiable lesion, mesial temporal structures such as the hippocampus may be involved.

SEEG enables direct assessment of deep epileptogenic structures and may provide critical information for surgical planning that cannot be obtained with surface-based recordings.

Surgical strategies should therefore be individualized based on electrophysiological findings rather than anatomical considerations alone. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited by the single-case design, and further studies are required to clarify the role of SEEG in epidermoid tumor–related epilepsy.
