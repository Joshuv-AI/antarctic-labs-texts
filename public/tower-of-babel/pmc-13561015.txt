Baseline and longitudinal measures of continuity of primary care and COPD-related service utilisation: a cohort study using Korean National Health Insurance Service claims data
Authors: Ae Jeong Jo, Sejung Kim, Eunjung Choo, Nam Kyung Je, Eun Jin Jang, Iyn-Hyang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo compare baseline, cluster-based and trajectory-based classifications of continuity of primary care (COC) and to examine their associations with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)-related hospitalisation and emergency department (ED) visits.DesignPopulation-based retrospective cohort study.SettingNational Health Insurance claims data from South Korea (Korean National Health Insurance Service), 2014–2021.Participants92 977 patients aged ≥40 years newly diagnosed with COPD between 2015 and 2016, with at least four ambulatory primary care visits during the exposure period.Primary and secondary outcome measures: The primary outcome was the incidence of COPD-related hospitalisation; the secondary outcome was the incidence of COPD-related ED visits. Introduction

Although numerous studies have investigated the association between continuity of care (COC) and patient outcomes, the overall evidence base remains limited by substantial methodological heterogeneity. A key methodological concern is the inappropriate temporal alignment between continuity measures and outcome assessment. In a systematic review published in 2010, van Walraven and colleagues excluded 82 of 139 eligible studies because continuity and outcomes were measured concurrently without an appropriate analytical framework.1

COC is inherently dynamic and varies over time. When exposure and outcome windows are improperly aligned, effect estimates may be biased or misleading. Inadequate handling of time-varying exposures is well recognised as a major source of bias in analyses of healthcare administrative data.2 Despite increasing awareness of this issue, similar methodological shortcomings have persisted over the past decade, underscoring ongoing challenges in achieving conceptual and operational consistency in COC research.3

Choo et al provided the first empirical assessment of the impact of temporal alignment strategies on estimated continuity effects.4 They evaluated 12 analytical scenarios that combined three patients inclusion criteria with four temporal alignment approaches. Particularly of four temporal alignment, only two were methodologically appropriate: modelling continuity as a time-varying exposure in concurrent analyses, or applying sequential models with clearly separated exposure and outcome periods. Alternative approaches were shown to systematically inflate effect estimates.4

Nevertheless, important limitations remain unresolved. Concurrent time-varying models are often hindered by substantial patient attrition over extended follow-up periods, limiting their practical applicability.5 Conversely, sequential models typically rely on baseline measures of continuity, raising concerns about adequately capturing a dynamic exposure using a single time point. To address these limitations, the present study examines longitudinal patterns of COC and evaluates new classification approaches alongside conventional methods to determine whether they provide improved analytical performance and a more coherent framework for assessing continuity.

Methods

Study design

This population-based cohort study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.6

Data source

We analysed anonymised national health insurance claims data from 2014 to 2021 provided by the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS). The KNHIS database covers all Korean residents enrolled in the National Health Insurance (NHI) programme and the Medical Aid (MedAid) scheme. It contains de-identified information on patient socio-demographic characteristics, diagnostic codes, healthcare utilisation—including inpatient and outpatient services as well as prescription records—and mortality data.7

Study population

Patients newly diagnosed with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) between 2015 and 2016 were identified using clinical-based claims data. Eligible individuals were aged ≥40 years at the index date and had at least four ambulatory visits to a primary care provider during the exposure period. COPD was defined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes J42–J44, excluding McLeod syndrome (J43.0). To ensure the inclusion of incident cases, patients with any COPD-related claim in the year preceding the index date were excluded. We further excluded patients who visited tertiary care facilities, died or experienced COPD-related hospitalisation or emergency department (ED) visits during the exposure period. Consistent with prior methodological recommendations, patients with very low healthcare utilisation were excluded to enhance real-world applicability and reduce the risk of type II error.5 Although continuity indices can be calculated using as few as two visits,8 very low visit counts may overestimate continuity and fail to reflect a meaningful patient–physician relationship. Accordingly, a minimum of four ambulatory visits was required for study inclusion. After applying all eligibility and exclusion criteria, the final analytical cohort consisted of 92 977 unique patients.

Measurement of continuity of primary care

Continuity of primary care was defined as the degree to which a patient’s outpatient visits were concentrated among primary care physicians. Because the organisation and function of primary care differ across healthcare systems,9 we operationalised primary care as community-based outpatient care provided by facilities other than tertiary hospitals. In South Korea, patients are covered by a universal health insurance system and require a referral only when accessing tertiary hospitals. Consequently, primary care may be delivered either in clinics or hospital outpatient departments, depending primarily on geographical accessibility and patient preference.

Continuity of care was measured using the Bice and Boxerman Continuity of Care Index (COCI).10 The COCI is a dispersion-based metric that captures the concentration of visits across providers and is particularly suitable for claims-based analyses in settings where patients may consult multiple physicians.8 Given the relatively weak gatekeeping role of primary care in South Korea and the high degree of patient choice in provider selection,11 the COCI has been recommended as an appropriate measure of continuity in this context.

Definition of continuity cohort and study time frame

In the prior study, continuity groups were classified using a predefined cut-off based on baseline COCI values, and analyses were conducted in the full study population (hereafter referred to as the baseline COCI cohort).5 In the present study, we applied two additional methods to define continuity cohorts: a K-means clustering approach (cluster-based cohort) and a trajectory-based approach (trajectory-based cohort). For the cluster-based method, a sliding-window approach was used to calculate COCI, in which a fixed 2-year window, beginning at each patient’s index date, was advanced by 1 year at each step and patients were grouped using K-means clustering based on these repeated measures. For the trajectory-based method, longitudinal changes in COCI were modelled using polynomial functions. Patients with similar COCI trajectories across five consecutive measurement points (Time 1 to Time 5) were grouped using group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM), allowing classification based on both the level and temporal pattern of continuity. High-continuity and low-continuity groups were defined according to the relative distribution of continuity of care within each method, with higher COCI values indicating higher continuity and lower values indicating lower continuity.

The overall study period spanned 2014–2021, with cohort-specific timelines summarised in online supplemental table S1. The index date was defined as the first recorded COPD diagnosis between 2015 and 2016 and the 12 months preceding the index date were designated as the pre-index period. The exposure period differed by cohort: for the baseline COCI cohort, it comprised the 2 years following the index date (January 2015 to December 2016), whereas for the cluster-based and trajectory-based cohorts, it extended from January 2015 to December 2019.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of COPD-related hospitalisation during the outcome period, as hospitalisations for ambulatory care sensitive conditions are commonly used as indicators of primary care quality. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of COPD-related ED visits. COPD-related hospitalisations and ED visits were identified using the ICD-10 diagnosis codes described above. For all continuity classification methods, the outcome period commenced after completion of the COCI assessment period used to define continuity and continued until December 2021. Patients were followed from the end of the exposure period until the occurrence of an outcome event, death or the end of data availability, whichever came first.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori as potential confounders of the association between continuity of care and COPD-related outcomes and were ascertained using claims records. Individual demographic characteristics included age and sex. Socioeconomic status was proxied by NHI contribution level, categorised as high, moderate or low, reflecting household income. Insurance type was also included, distinguishing between NHI and MedAid, the latter covering approximately 3% of the South Korean population.12 Residential location at the index date was classified as large urban (metropolitan cities), small urban (other cities) or rural areas based on population density. Baseline health status was assessed using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Indices (ECIs), derived from outpatient and inpatient diagnosis codes recorded during the 12-month pre-index period.13 ECIs were incorporated to account for multimorbidity burden. We additionally adjusted for selected chronic and allergic conditions—including asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, cancer, osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis—identified during the pre-index year. COPD-related treatment intensity was captured using medication indicators, including prescriptions for inhaled COPD therapies at the index date and systemic corticosteroid use during the exposure period. Healthcare utilisation patterns were further characterised by defining frequent outpatient visitors as patients with more than 14 clinical visits during the initial 2 years, corresponding to the 90th percentile or higher. Disability status at the index date was categorised as none, moderate or severe according to administrative registration. Smoking status was classified as person who has never smoked, person who smoked previously, person who smokes currently, or no record based on available claims-linked health screening data.

Statistical analysis

Longitudinal changes in COC according to baseline COCI were examined using Sankey diagrams to visualise year-to-year transitions among COCI categories. Insights from this exploratory analysis guided the development of three continuity classification methods: baseline COCI-based, cluster-based and trajectory-based. For the K-means clustering analysis, the optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method, based on the within-cluster sum of squares derived from Euclidean distances between patients’ five repeated COCI measurements. To ensure the robustness of these results, sensitivity analyses were performed by varying the number of clusters. In contrast, the optimal number of groups for the trajectory-based approach was primarily identified using the Bayesian information criterion. Additionally, model adequacy was evaluated based on the average posterior probability of group membership and the odds of correct classification. Sensitivity analyses for this approach involved fitting trajectory models with various polynomial orders (linear, quadratic and cubic) to assess the stability of the identified groups. To evaluate the suitability of a two-group classification scheme used in prior studies, Euclidean distances between patients were calculated from COCI values at five consecutive time points. The elbow method was applied to determine the optimal number of clusters by assessing within-cluster sum of squares.14 The resulting number of clusters was used as initial centres for K-means clustering to derive cluster-based continuity groups. For patients who were censored (eg, due to death, insufficient follow-up or fewer than four ambulatory visits) before completing the full sequence of repeated COCI measurements, missing values were addressed using the last observation carried forward method. By imputing the final available COCI value, the entire study population was retained without attrition, thereby facilitating robust comparisons across different models using an identical sample size. Also, the stability of the identified groups was further validated via bootstrapping. By analysing fluctuations in the number of clusters relative to sample variations, the final cluster architecture was established. Agreement between continuity classifications obtained from different methods was assessed through cross-tabulation. Cohen’s kappa and Fleiss’ kappa statistics were calculated and visualised to quantify classification consistency.15–17 Baseline characteristics were summarised by continuity group and classification method using means and SD for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

For outcome analyses, the incidence of COPD-related hospitalisations and ED visits was compared across continuity groups defined by each classification method. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models accounted for death as a competing risk. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, insurance programme, insurance contribution level, residential area, disability status, ECI, comorbid asthma, COPD inhaler use at the index date and systemic corticosteroid use during the exposure period, smoking status and frequent outpatient visits. Individuals whose information about insurance contribution, living area and smoking status were missing were not excluded and missing values were considered as an independent category in the statistical models. The proportional subdistribution hazards assumption was assessed by including interaction terms between the covariates and log-transformed follow-up time. No significant violations were observed. As a supplementary assessment of the proportional hazards assumption, we performed a time-stratified analysis to compare HRs across defined intervals (0–1, 1–3 and >3 years), ensuring the stability of the estimates over the follow-up duration. Adjusted HRs with 95% CIs were reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and R V.4.3.1.

Results

Study population selection

The study population selection process is summarised in figure 1. A total of 92 977 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis. The eligible cohort had a mean age of approximately 64 years; approximately half (50.3%) were aged ≥65 years, 57.9% were women, 93.6% were covered under the NHI programme (6.4% under MedAid) and 58.8% resided in large urban areas at the index date (online supplemental table S2).

Temporal trends in continuity of care index

Longitudinal changes in COCI were visualised using Sankey diagrams for each measurement year (figure 2; online supplemental table S3). Nearly half of the patients were censored after the first 2-year assessment due to fewer than four visits or occurrence of the study outcome. By the fifth measurement period (Time 5), 13 564 patients (14.6%) remained under observation. At Time 1, patients with perfect continuity (COCI=1) represented the largest group (66 688; 71.7%), followed by those with intermediate continuity (COCI 0.4 to <0.6; 11 990; 12.9%). Among patients retained in the cohort, the proportion with perfect continuity remained above 50% throughout follow-up, reaching 76.7% (10 397 of 13 564) at Time 5.

Sankey diagram of transitions in continuity of care index. COCI, Continuity of Care Index.

Transitions between continuity groups revealed that from Time 1 to Time 2, 2.9% of patients with high continuity (COCI=1) shifted to the low-continuity group (COCI <1). Conversely, among patients with COCI <1 at baseline, 22–39% of those in the two lowest strata (COCI <0.4) transitioned to the high-continuity group. More than half of patients with intermediate continuity (COCI 0.4 to <0.8) also transferred into the high-continuity group. Beyond this initial interval, downward transitions from high to low continuity remained rare (1–2%), whereas upward transitions from low-continuity groups declined to below 40% but remained substantial.

Agreement matrix between cohort classification algorithms

The two-group classification scheme used in a previous study was reassessed using two new algorithms. The elbow method (figure 3a) indicated that the optimal number of clusters was two, identified at the point where the rate of decline in within-cluster variance sharply levelled off. Patients were subsequently grouped using K-means clustering based on Euclidean distances. Results from the GBTM were consistent with this finding (figure 3b), with the two-group model providing the best fit. This model differentiated a high-COCI trajectory group (68%), which maintained consistently high continuity, from a low-COCI trajectory group (32%), which exhibited persistently low continuity throughout follow-up.

Table 1 shows the agreement matrix for the three classification algorithms (see online supplemental table S4 for details). When groups with higher mean COCI values in the two novel algorithms were categorised as high-continuity groups, the overall agreement among the three methods was 87.2%. Agreement was high between the trajectory-based cohort and the baseline COCI cohort (89.5%), as well as between the trajectory-based and cluster-based cohorts (89.8%). Cohen’s kappa values ranged from 0.73 to 0.80, indicating substantial to near-perfect agreement and a Fleiss’s kappa value of 0.78 further supported substantial concordance across methods. Risk comparison for hospital admission and ED visits by continuity classification method

As shown in table 2, patients in the low-continuity group consistently exhibited higher adjusted HRs for hospital admission across all classification methods: 2.73 (95% CI 2.49 to 2.99) in the trajectory-based cohort, 2.43 (95% CI 2.22 to 2.66) in the baseline COCI cohort and 2.26 (95% CI 2.06 to 2.47) in the cluster-based cohort. A similar trend was observed for ED visits, with adjusted HRs of 3.48 (95% CI 3.05 to 3.96) in the trajectory-based cohort, 2.55 (95% CI 2.25 to 2.88) in the cluster-based cohort and 2.32 (95% CI 2.04 to 2.63) in the baseline COCI cohort. Across all methods, the trajectory-based approach yielded the highest effect estimates, whereas the baseline COCI cohort yielded the lowest. Model diagnostics indicated no meaningful violation of the proportional subdistribution hazards assumption in any of the three classification models (time-interaction terms, all p>0.05). To further assess the temporal stability of our estimates, we performed a time-stratified analysis by comparing HRs across three follow-up intervals (0–1, 1–3 and >3 years). The HRs exhibited consistent directionality across all time strata, and no significant deviations from the proportional subdistribution hazards assumption were detected. These results collectively indicate that the observed effects remained robust throughout the study duration; however, model fit statistics were marginally more favourable for the trajectory-based model, consistent with its stronger discrimination between high-continuity and low-continuity groups.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study investigated longitudinal changes in the COCI among Korean patients using three approaches: baseline COCI, cluster-based and trajectory-based methods. Despite differences in methodology, the findings were largely consistent with previous studies relying solely on baseline COCI, reflecting the relative stability of continuity patterns in this population.

Transitions between continuity groups were illustrated in the Sankey diagram. Among patients with perfect continuity (COCI=1) during the first 2 years, nearly half remained under follow-up, more than half were excluded due to eligibility criteria and a small proportion transitioned to lower-continuity groups. Conversely, a substantial proportion of patients with baseline COCI <1 shifted to the perfect continuity group. These observations suggest that classifications based solely on baseline COCI may underestimate the impact of continuity, as subsequent improvements are not captured, potentially attenuating observed differences between groups. This dynamic helps explain why, in risk comparisons for hospital admissions and ED visits, adjusted HRs were lower when groups were defined using baseline COCI than when trajectory-based methods were applied. In settings where continuity is more variable, trajectory-based methods may therefore offer greater precision in capturing its effects.

Although dichotomising COCI at a fixed threshold can lead to misclassification, our analyses confirmed that the prior decision to classify patients into two groups was appropriate.5 This conclusion was supported by the elbow method, which identified two distinct clusters and the GBTM analysis, which similarly revealed two clearly differentiated continuity trajectories over time. Baseline COCI group definitions are efficient, require minimal data and are straightforward to interpret. However, they assume stability of group characteristics and may introduce bias in longitudinal analyses. Trajectory-based methods overcome this limitation by incorporating temporal variation, but they require repeated measurements, larger datasets and more complex modelling. In populations with relatively stable continuity patterns or substantial missing data, the additional value of trajectory-based approaches may be limited.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first empirical analysis to classify continuity groups using newly introduced methods and to directly compare their performance. To maintain comparable sample sizes across approaches, patients lost to follow-up due to COCI un-calculation were imputed. While this approach preserved the analytical structure, it may have introduced some noise and results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the application of these methods to a Korean population may limit their generalisability to other settings. Although our covariate set was comprehensive—including socio-demographic factors, insurance-based socioeconomic proxies, area of residence, disability status, Elixhauser comorbidity burden, specific chronic and allergic comorbidities, COPD treatment intensity and healthcare utilisation frequency—claims data inherently lack information on some potentially important confounders, such as spirometric disease severity (eg, forced expiratory volume in 1 s), symptom burden, health literacy, social support and patient preference for a regular provider. Residual confounding from these unmeasured factors cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations.

Implications for policy and research

Choosing an approach to define continuity groups requires balancing efficiency with temporal resolution. In populations with relatively stable continuity patterns, baseline COCI provides a practical and valid measure. In more dynamic settings, trajectory-based approaches may more accurately capture changes over time, although they entail greater analytical complexity and require more extensive data. Future research should conduct comparative analyses across diverse populations and further refine methodologies to provide evidence-based guidance on the optimal measurement of continuity in longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

Using COCI to quantify COC, this study compared baseline, cluster-based and trajectory-based patient classification strategies and found broadly consistent associations with service utilisation. However, observed transitions between continuity groups over time indicate that baseline classification, while efficient and pragmatic, may underestimate continuity effects when care patterns change. Trajectory-based methods capture temporal dynamics more effectively but entail substantially greater data and modelling requirements. These findings highlight important methodological trade-offs and suggest that the choice between baseline and longitudinal continuity measures should be guided by the expected stability of care patterns and the aims of longitudinal analyses.
