An auditable pipeline for fuzzy full-text screening in systematic reviews: integrating contrastive semantic highlighting and LLM judgment
Authors: Arya Rahgozar, Pouria Mortezaagha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFull-text screening is the major bottleneck of systematic reviews (SRs), particularly in domains such as population health modelling of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), where decisive eligibility information is scattered across long and heterogeneous full texts. In this methodological study, we introduce a scalable and auditable pipeline that reframes inclusion and exclusion as a fuzzy decision process. We evaluate the approach within the Population Health Modelling Consensus Reporting Network (POPCORN) and benchmark it against statistical and crisp baselines. Articles are parsed into overlapping chunks and embedded with a domain-adapted model; for each criterion (Population, Intervention, Outcome, Study Approach), we compute contrastive similarity (inclusion–exclusion cosine) and a vagueness margin, which a Mamdani fuzzy controller maps into graded inclusion degrees with dynamic thresholds in a multi-label classification setting. A large language model (LLM) judge adjudicates highlighted spans with tertiary labels, confidence scores, and criterion-referenced rationales; when evidence is insufficient, fuzzy membership is attenuated rather than hard-excluded. In a pilot on an all-positive gold set ( full texts; chunks), the fuzzy system achieved document-level recall of 81.25% (Population), 87.50% (Intervention), 87.50% (Outcome), and 75.00% (Study Approach) with 95% Wilson confidence intervals, exceeding statistical baselines (recall 56.25–75.00%) and crisp baselines (recall 43.75–81.25%). Strict “All Criteria” inclusion was reached for 50.00% of articles, compared to 25.00% and 12.50% under the baselines. Cross-model agreement on justifications was 98.27%, and human–machine agreement was 96.07%. A pilot review showed 91% inter-rater agreement () with screening time reduced from ∼20 minutes to under 1 minute per article at a significantly lower cost. These results suggest that fuzzy logic, combined with contrastive highlighting and explainable LLM adjudication, delivers high recall, stable rationales, and end-to-end traceability. We emphasize that the reported recall figures were computed on an all-positive gold set (no excluded documents), so specificity, ROC/PR curves, and AUC are not defined here; the complete operating-characteristic analysis on a mixed-label corpus will be reported in a forthcoming study.Keywords: Systematic review automation, Full-text screening, Fuzzy logic (Mamdani), Multi-label text classification, Explainable AI, Large Language Models (LLMs), Contrastive semantic similarity, Biomedical NLP, Evidence synthesis

Introduction

Systematic reviews (SRs) are the cornerstone of evidence-based medicine and biomedical research, providing the most reliable synthesis of scientific knowledge for clinical, policy, and research decisions (Tawfik et al. 2019). Their rigor rests on pre-specified questions, comprehensive searches, explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, and transparent appraisal. However, the scale of contemporary publishing has overwhelmed traditional workflows: recent estimates indicate that more than 75 clinical trials and 11 SRs appear each day (Bastian et al. 2010), and the pool of potentially eligible studies has expanded far beyond the available reviewer capacity (Qureshi 2023; Ofori-Boateng et al. 2024). In practice, teams now screen thousands of citations and frequently advance hundreds to full text (O’Mara-Eves et al. 2015; Marshall et al. 2018).

Full-text screening as the bottleneck: Unlike title/abstract triage, full-text assessment requires careful reading of long, heterogeneous documents. Decisive information may be scattered across sections, buried in tables/figures, or described in discipline-specific or evolving terminology (Hasny et al. 2023; Gates et al. 2019). The task is laborious and error-prone. Reviewer fatigue and cognitive overload are common, and even with shared protocols, experts often disagree on eligibility at this stage (Gates et al. 2019). This disagreement reflects not only human inconsistency, but also a property of the data: full-text evidence is inherently ambiguous, context-dependent, and, in a precise sense, fuzzy.

The inherently fuzzy nature of full-text decision-making

By “fuzzy”, we mean that the signals that support inclusion/exclusion are graded, partial, and distributed. Populations are described broadly in one section and narrowly in another; interventions may be referenced indirectly; outcomes are reported with non-standard or shifting terminology. Criteria are seldom satisfied in an all-or-nothing manner; rather, eligibility emerges from partial matches and multi-dimensional semantic relationships. Consequently, two diligent reviewers can reasonably reach different conclusions on the same article, with consensus achieved only after a discussion that weighs context and degree—a phenomenon well documented for full-text screening (Gates et al. 2019).

Limits of traditional and probabilistic approaches

Crisp rule-based systems are explicit and transparent but poorly matched to graded contextual evidence; they cannot represent partial relevance or uncertainty (Esri 2025; Gates et al. 2019). Probabilistic machine learning offers improvements, but still reduces decisions to thresholds based on opaque scores and often lacks persuasive case-specific explanations (O’Mara-Eves et al. 2015; Marshall et al. 2018; Hasny et al. 2023). Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) improve coverage and flexibility. Yet LLMs alone do not guarantee consistent recall at SR standards, nor do they natively provide calibrated, graded decisions for the “gray-zone” cases that dominate full-text assessment (Guo et al. 2023; Kohl and Schön 2023).

Why fuzzy logic is needed

Fuzzy logic (Zadeh 1965) offers a human-aligned, principled calculus to represent degrees of membership and partial truth (Simkute et al. 2024; Wibowo and Deng 2012; Esri 2025; Bahadir Gökmen et al. 2025). In the context of full-text screening, much of the challenge arises not from simple ambiguity, which implies multiple possible interpretations, but from vagueness, where key eligibility signals are imprecise, graded, or previously non-measurable (Liu et al. 2024). Fuzzy logic rectifies this vagueness by transforming qualitative, diffuse evidence into quantifiable degrees of support, enabling systematic comparison across heterogeneous text spans (Liu et al. 2024). It encodes the interaction of signal strength and contextual nuance in a transparent rule base (Drnovšek et al. 2025), mirroring how expert panels reason: weighing multiple, partially supportive statements until a consensus degree of inclusion is reached. Crucially, fuzzy systems are both interpretable and auditable, allowing users to inspect membership functions, rule activations, and the exact path from text to decision (Mitrakas et al. 2025; Nzoundja Fapi et al. 2025).

This architecture targets high recall with bounded reviewer effort and an auditable chain from document text to decision. In this sense, “LLM-as-judge” is best understood as a structured validator and rationale generator that sits on top of the fuzzy controller, not as a free-standing decision-maker. The fuzzy layer determines which spans are evidentiary and how strongly they support each criterion; the LLM is then asked to confirm or contest those spans and to articulate why, under a fixed schema and prompt. This design mitigates hallucination risk, supports reproducibility, and keeps final inclusion aligned with pre-specified SR standards.

Related work and comparative approaches

Automated study selection has evolved along several methodological lines, including manual screening platforms, probabilistic machine learning, transformer-based classifiers, fuzzy logic systems, and, more recently, LLM-based prompting pipelines. Classic screening tools such as Covidence and Rayyan (Gates et al. 2019) provide structured workflows, conflict resolution, and tagging interfaces, but rely entirely on human judgment. They offer transparency but no automation beyond keyword filters. Active learning frameworks such as Abstrackr and ASReview (O’Mara-Eves et al. 2015; Marshall et al. 2018) were among the first to introduce machine learning into SR workflows, typically using SVMs or logistic models to reduce title/abstract workload. While effective at early-phase triage, these systems do not operate at full text and provide limited interpretability. Comparison of approaches for systematic review screening

Transformer-based models, including BERT and domain-adapted variants (BioBERT, SciBERT), now represent state-of-the-art performance for automated SR screening (Hasny et al. 2023). These models often outperform classical probabilistic approaches and can be integrated into active learning loops. However, they express decisions through opaque logits and require substantial labeled training data. Their binary thresholding also struggles with borderline cases, and they provide no structured justification tied to protocol criteria. Recent efforts add attention visualization or saliency maps, but these explanations remain coarse and difficult to relate directly to eligibility criteria.

Parallel to probabilistic and transformer approaches, fuzzy logic has been explored for text classification tasks that exhibit graded or ambiguous signals. Recent work demonstrates the effectiveness of fuzzy neural networks and hierarchical fuzzy classifiers in multi-label and noisy-text settings (Ahmad et al. 2025; Drnovšek et al. 2025). Fuzzy additive models for explainable AI (Bahadir Gökmen et al. 2025) and other rule-based fuzzy systems offer transparent reasoning pathways, enabling partial membership and interpretable aggregation of uncertain evidence. A recent comprehensive survey of interpretable fuzzy methods (Zhang et al. 2026) further highlights the central role of fuzzy rule systems, fuzzy sets, and neuro-fuzzy architectures in transparent decision-making, and documents key trends such as accuracy–interpretability trade-offs and human-in-the-loop reasoning. This literature reinforces the suitability of fuzzy inference as a principled foundation for explainable screening in settings characterized by ambiguity and graded evidence. However, existing applications focus primarily on short text or structured attributes rather than long-form biomedical literature, and they rarely integrate with semantic embedding models or LLMs.

LLM-based screening pipelines have gained interest due to models’ ability to parse complex scientific language. Early prompting frameworks show promising recall for title/abstract screening (Guo et al. 2023; Kohl and Schön 2023). Some recent studies apply LLMs for broader document triage, classification, or scenario interpretation in adjacent fields (Teichert and Shah 2026). However, LLMs often exhibit inconsistent judgments, lack grounding in protocol definitions, and may hallucinate rationale text. Moreover, LLM outputs are typically non-deterministic and difficult to audit, limiting their direct use as stand-alone decision-makers for SR workflows.

Hybrid systems combining rule-based logic with ML or LLM components have emerged as another promising direction. These systems attempt to balance transparency with flexibility, using deterministic rules for constraint enforcement and ML models for semantic inference. Such designs mirror expert committee reasoning in SRs, but practical deployments remain limited. A notable gap in prior work is the integration of semantic highlighting, fuzzy aggregation, and LLM adjudication within a single, auditable pipeline.

Our contribution builds on and extends these strands. We combine domain-adapted embeddings for contrastive highlighting, a Mamdani fuzzy controller for aggregating graded signals, and a constrained LLM-as-judge for validating and explaining only the fuzzy-selected spans. Unlike transformer classifiers, our method does not require large labeled datasets, and unlike pure LLM pipelines, it constrains the model within a structured, auditable decision process. The design targets high recall, transparent rationale generation, and the ability to trace each inclusion decision to specific text spans. These capabilities are not jointly offered in prior work across SR automation platforms, transformer-based approaches, fuzzy logic methods, or LLM-based systems.

Evaluation overview

We evaluate the proposed system using a multi-metric battery appropriate for screening tasks: recall (on an all-positive gold set), reviewer agreement, explanation quality, and efficiency; and, for future mixed-label benchmarks, specificity and threshold-free discrimination (ROC/PR curves and AUC), alongside calibration and ablations. This combination aligns with best practices in explainable AI and targets real-world deployability.

In summary, the exponential growth of the literature and the fundamentally graded nature of full-text evidence argue for a shift from crisp or purely probabilistic screening to a fuzzy, explainable paradigm. By integrating contrastive highlighting, Mamdani inference, and LLM adjudication, our approach seeks to deliver high recall, transparent rationales, and scalable operations suitable for living SRs.

This study is a methodological proof-of-concept that introduces and evaluates an automated full-text screening pipeline for systematic reviews; it is not itself a systematic review or tutorial.

Methodology

Full-text semantic highlighting, fuzzy logic screening, and explainable LLM judgment

We build a pipeline that operationalizes the intrinsically fuzzy, context-dependent nature of full-text eligibility. The system combines: (i) semantic highlighting to surface evidence spans tied to explicit criteria, (ii) a Mamdani fuzzy inference layer to map similarity and ambiguity into graded inclusion scores, and (iii) LLM-based adjudication to provide auditable, criterion-referenced rationales. The design targets high recall with bounded reviewer effort and end-to-end traceability: every automated decision is linked to concrete text and a short explanation.

The system supports multi-label assignment at the chunk level. When is small (high overlap), the chunk may satisfy several criteria simultaneously; we therefore evaluate μ against per-criterion dynamic thresholds and retain all labels whose scores exceed their cutoffs. Results

Overall full-text system performance

We evaluated the pipeline on a test set of N=16 full-text articles drawn from the Population Health Modelling Consensus Reporting Network (POPCORN) in noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), spanning heterogeneous study approaches and reporting completeness. The corpus yielded text chunks for semantic analysis. Each chunk was scored against four inclusion criteria—Population, Intervention, Outcome, and Study Approach—and then aggregated to document-level decisions as described in Algorithm 1.

Discussion

The accelerating pace of biomedical publishing makes fully manual systematic reviews increasingly untenable. With thousands of new articles weekly, review teams must triage large evidence bases and, at full text, determine where decisive information resides and how to interpret heterogeneous, context-dependent language. Our results show that a pipeline expressly designed for this setting, namely contrastive highlighting, fuzzy aggregation, and explainable LLM adjudication, recovers high recall while maintaining traceability and minimizing reviewer effort.

Operational impact and cost efficiency

Automating both initial triage and deep full-text screening compresses timelines from months to days, enabling “living” updates at a cadence compatible with policy and guideline work (Dennstädt et al. 2025).

In practical use, the pipeline operates as a reliable first-pass reviewer: it rapidly highlights criterion-relevant spans, assigns interpretable fuzzy scores, provides concise LLM explanations, and escalates only ambiguous borderline cases to humans, concentrating expert time where it matters most.

Reviewers can inspect only the top-evidence chunks rather than reading entire documents, substantially reducing workload while maintaining recall. Because every decision is logged with the text span, fuzzy score, and explanation, the system supports audit trails, protocol refinement, and training of new reviewers. Its low computational cost enables high-throughput screening and frequent re-analysis, making it suitable for living systematic reviews. The modular design also allows teams to update criteria texts or swap LLM back-ends without retraining any components.

Cost tracking indicates approximately 200 documents can be processed for under $5 USD in LLM API fees (Figure 8). An in-memory cache avoids redundant calls on repeat analyses, yielding negligible marginal cost and near-instant reruns—important for iterative protocol refinement and audit cycles.

Protocol fidelity and operating characteristics

A core requirement for SR automation is strict adherence to protocol definitions. By explicitly contrasting inclusion and exclusion texts, the pipeline anchors decisions in expert-curated criteria. The fuzzy layer converts continuous semantic evidence and explicit vagueness (margin) into graded membership, avoiding the brittleness of crisp rules and the opacity of threshold-only probabilistic scoring. On our all-positive gold set, document-level recalls favored the fuzzy approach over statistical and crisp baselines across criteria, and strict “All Criteria” inclusion rates reflected the same advantage. The design also models lack of decisive evidence: if a highlighted span is later judged insufficient by the LLM, its contribution is attenuated rather than forcing hard exclusion, preserving recall on borderline cases while still de-prioritizing weak evidence.

Stability and face validity of explanations are supported by cross-checks: a stronger model (GPT-5) agreed with GPT-4.1-mini on 625/636 commented chunks (98.27%), and human review agreed on 611/636 (96.07%). We interpret these as indicators of robustness in the rationale generation and filtering scheme, not as substitutes for gold-standard evaluation.

Transparency, explainability, and auditability

Adoption hinges on auditability. Every stage—chunking, embeddings, contrastive scores, fuzzy rule activations, defuzzified μ, LLM labels and rationales—is logged with text offsets, enabling reconstruction of the reasoning chain from document span to decision. The explanations double as training material for new reviewers and support quality assurance and dispute resolution. This traceability directly addresses common barriers to deploying AI in SR workflows and is difficult to realize with conventional black-box classifiers.

Model reliability, hallucination, and reproducibility

Although the pipeline constrains the LLM to operate only over fuzzy-selected spans, eligibility rationales may still be susceptible to hallucination or over-interpretation of weak evidence. Restricting the LLM to fixed prompts, predefined schemas, and explicit evidence snippets mitigates these risks but cannot eliminate them. For SR settings, human verification of borderline cases remains essential.

Reproducibility poses an additional challenge. Commercial LLMs evolve rapidly, and version drift (for example, parameter updates or model replacements) may alter judgments even with identical inputs. To address this, the pipeline logs prompts, spans, scores, and model metadata, enabling re-analysis with pinned versions or portable local models in production environments.

Dependence on third-party APIs also introduces stability and vendor lock-in concerns. Pricing changes, rate limits, or model deprecation may affect long-term usability. While the architecture allows the LLM backend to be swapped (for example, between OpenAI, Anthropic, or locally hosted Llama models), each substitution requires renewed validation to ensure consistent behavior.

Finally, LLMs may amplify biases present in their training data. Although the fuzzy controller provides structure and reduces free-form generation, it cannot fully prevent biased rationales or asymmetric interpretation of evidence across populations or intervention types. Ongoing audits, topic-specific calibration, and human-in-the-loop oversight are therefore necessary to maintain fairness, reliability, and SR protocol fidelity.

Scalability and generalization

The pipeline is modular and model-agnostic. Criteria texts can be revised without code changes; the fuzzy controller’s universes and rules are interpretable; the LLM layer is batched and swappable. These traits facilitate rapid retargeting to new review topics and maintenance of “living” reviews. Beyond biomedical SRs, the same pattern—contrastive highlighting → fuzzy aggregation → explainable adjudication—applies wherever diffuse, context-dependent evidence is scattered across long documents (e.g., environmental policy assessments, pharmaco-economic evaluations, social science syntheses). Finally, performance and reliability are likely domain-dependent. Terminology, document structure, and reporting conventions vary substantially across biomedical subfields, and model behavior may shift under these conditions. Accordingly, claims of generality should be interpreted cautiously until validated on topic-specific corpora.

Threats to validity

Label composition: The present test set contains only gold positives; as noted in Results, specificity and threshold-free discrimination metrics (ROC/PR curves and AUCs) are undefined here. Mixed-label benchmarks will enable full operating-characteristic reporting, calibration analysis, and paired comparisons.

Agreement vs. ground truth: High cross-model and human agreement (98.27% and 96.07%) indicate stability and face validity but do not guarantee correctness; shared biases or correlated errors are possible.

Domain shift: Generalization to highly atypical article structures or subdomains with idiosyncratic terminology warrants further evaluation; ablations on highlighting, margin ranges, and rule shapes will help quantify sensitivity.

Human factors: Reviewer behavior may change when assisted by explanations (anchoring, confirmation); prospective human-in-the-loop studies are needed to measure net effects on accuracy and efficiency. LLM rationales may also subtly steer reviewer interpretation, amplifying pre-existing biases; interface design should minimize anchoring and require explicit evidence checks.

Model-version drift: LLM behavior may change with backend updates or parameter shifts, affecting reproducibility unless explicit version pinning and logging are used.

Limitations and future work

This proof-of-concept has several limitations. First, the evaluation set contained only gold-positive articles, which restricted our analysis to recall and prevented assessment of specificity, ROC/PR curves, AUC, and full calibration. Future work will therefore evaluate the pipeline on larger, mixed-label corpora with pre-registered thresholds and comprehensive operating-characteristic reporting.

Second, while the manuscript compares the full pipeline against statistical and crisp baselines—an initial ablation of the fuzzy component—more granular component-level ablations (for example, removing the contrastive margin, semantic highlighting, or the LLM adjudication layer) require a larger and more diverse dataset to yield stable conclusions. These ablations will be performed once such a dataset is available.

Third, a deeper examination of edge cases and disagreement patterns will help refine prompts, membership function universes, margin ranges, and defuzzification cutoffs. Decision-curve and workload analyses will also be conducted to quantify reviewer effort saved at fixed recall.

Finally, we will assess portability across domains, reporting standards, and LLM back-ends, and explore lightweight confidence calibration for the LLM judgments. Specificity and other performance metrics will be incorporated in future studies as negative labels become available in fully annotated datasets.

To support full operating-characteristic evaluation, we are in the process of assembling a larger mixed-label dataset that includes both included and excluded full texts across multiple NCD modelling subdomains. This expanded corpus will allow us to assess specificity, calibration, and robustness, and to conduct more granular ablation studies once sufficient data diversity is available.

Ethical and privacy considerations

In this proof-of-concept, all experimentation used open-access full-text research papers as source material. Patient information is not a concern in this setting, as no identifiable health records are processed. We recognize that potential infringement risks concern both the underlying content and editorial access rights, particularly when such materials are used with GPT-based systems. To mitigate these risks, we limit inputs to licensed or open-access papers when interacting with non-local GPT models.

The system operates as a secure integrated workflow, combining semantic search over a structured database with LLM-based question-resolution and analytical modules, without ever fine-tuning or training models on copyright-protected full texts. Instead, it employs a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) approach in which relevant excerpts are retrieved only as ephemeral context for a given query. These excerpts are (i) used in real time to answer a specific question, (ii) never stored or reused outside that session, and (iii) never redistributed or published verbatim. Meaningful human creative input is incorporated into any AI-generated outputs.

LLM-generated rationales, while constrained to fuzzy-selected spans, may still be susceptible to hallucination or biased interpretations. To reduce this risk, the workflow logs all prompts, spans, labels, and decisions; emphasizes evidence verification; and allows reviewers to hide LLM explanations during initial assessment to minimize anchoring. Overreliance on AI outputs is an ethical concern in SR workflows, and final inclusion decisions must remain human-controlled. Training materials and user interfaces should highlight that explanations reflect model reasoning rather than authoritative ground truth.

Reproducibility also carries ethical and regulatory implications. Commercial LLMs evolve rapidly, and version drift may alter outputs even under identical inputs. To support transparent audit trails and compliance with institutional or regulatory expectations, all analyses record model identifiers, temperatures, and prompt versions. The modular design allows migration to pinned local models when required by governance policies or to avoid vendor lock-in, pricing changes, or API instability.

All content is processed under controlled access, and decisions and rationales are logged for audit. No copyright material is repurposed as training data, and all processing respects licensing terms and editorial rights. Responsible use requires clear provenance tracking, model versioning, reproducible reporting, and routine monitoring for bias amplification or degradation in model behavior.

Conclusion

Full-text screening in systematic reviews is intrinsically fuzzy: decisive evidence is scattered across sections, expressed heterogeneously, and rarely admits crisp, binary rules. We reframed the task accordingly and introduced a pipeline that (i) highlights criterion-relevant spans via contrastive semantic similarity, (ii) aggregates evidence with a transparent Mamdani fuzzy controller, and (iii) provides auditable, criterion-referenced rationales through an LLM judge while conservatively handling the lack of evidence case. This design couples high recall with end-to-end traceability and practical efficiency.

On an all-positive gold set ( documents; chunks), the fuzzy system achieved higher document-level recall than both statistical and crisp baselines across Population, Intervention, Outcome, and Study Approach. It also doubled the strict “All Criteria” inclusion rate compared with the crisp baseline. Explanations remained stable under a stronger model (GPT-5 agreement: 98.27%) and closely matched human judgments (96.07%), while a small reviewer study showed high agreement (91%, ) and reduced per-article screening time from ∼20 minutes to under 1 minute. The system is also inexpensive to run at scale (about $5 USD per ∼200 documents), and every decision is logged from span to summary, supporting audit, training, and dispute resolution. Because the evaluation set contained only gold-positive articles, the recall figures reported here do not reflect specificity, ROC/PR behaviour, or AUC; the complete operating-characteristic analysis on a mixed-label corpus will be reported in a forthcoming study.

Fuzzy logic proved to be a natural formalism for full-text inclusion/exclusion: it preserves graded signals, exposes its rule activations, and integrates cleanly with modern NLP and LLMs. Beyond biomedical SRs, the same blueprint—contrastive highlighting → fuzzy aggregation → explainable adjudication—applies wherever long, heterogeneous documents embed diffuse, context-dependent evidence.

A detailed discussion of methodological limitations, domain dependencies, and directions for future work is provided in the dedicated “Limitations and Future Work” subsection 4.7. That section outlines the evaluation constraints of this proof-of-concept and planned expansions to mixed-label corpora, ablation analyses, and robustness assessments.

Taken together, our results suggest that fuzzy, explainable screening can transform full-text review from a brittle, labor-intensive bottleneck into a transparent, scalable process suitable for living reviews and institutional deployment. Our open-source implementation provides a practical starting point for reproducible adoption and community refinement.
