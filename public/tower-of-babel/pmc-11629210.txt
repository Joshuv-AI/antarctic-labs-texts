Methodological inconsistencies and variations in environmental pollution reports: A case study of the mining-affected Geita region, Tanzania
Authors: Elys Mwigamba, Clavery Tungaraza, Mariam Nguvava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarious studies report pollutant levels in the environment to raise awareness and call for government intervention to protect the environment and public health. However, inconsistent findings across studies complicate environmental actions and policy-making processes in many countries. Geita region in Tanzania is one of the areas with a high focus on metal pollution problems emanating from artisanal, small-scale, and industrial mining operations. This article presents a review of environmental studies conducted in the region as a case study to show the impacts of inconsistent methodological approaches resulting to varying pollutant levels of heavy metals (Hg, As, Pb, Zn and Ni) in soil, sediment, rice and water. The diversity of metal pollutant concentrations is caused by varying sample treatment methods, which result from a lack of standardised analytical methods for various pollutants within the country. For example, two different studies reported different total mercury (THg) concentrations in soil samples near the Geita gold mine: 1.89 mg/kg and 0.0625 mg/kg. Similarly, arsenic (As) levels were reported as 126.1 mg/kg and 5.5 mg/kg, while lead (Pb) concentrations varied between 23.46 mg/kg, 2.58 mg/kg, and 17.99 mg/kg in different studies of the same area. Classical analytical instruments were used in the analyses, including Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS), Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES), Energy Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF), and Cold Vapour Atomic Fluorescence Spectrometry (CV-AFS). However, there have been diverse sample pre-treatment methods, including sample drying techniques, sample digestion, and chemical mixture ratios, that contribute to the differences in results. Although sampling seasons and weather conditions were not reported by the different authors, they likely contributed to the observed variations. To address these discrepancies, this review suggests establishing standardised analytical methods for different pollutants to ensure uniform procedures and comparable results in the country. This standardisation would aid in effective decision-making and policy formulation to manage environmental and health risks in mining-affected and other polluted areas.Keywords: Analytical methods, Standard methods, Metal concentrations, Environmental policy

Introduction

Inconsistent reporting of environmental pollutant levels in a particular area can lead to misinformed decisions and ineffective environmental management practices. Understanding the factors contributing to these inconsistencies is crucial for improving consistency and quality of environmental pollutant data. It is known that in addition to human error [1,2], the primary factors contributing to analytical inconsistencies is the diversity of methodologies caused by the absence of Standardised methods for data sampling, sample preparation procedures, and analytical methods [3]. This is because different sampling techniques and analytical procedures can introduce variability in results, even when analysing the same pollutant. The choice of sampling locations, sample preparation procedures, analytical instruments, and data analysis techniques employed by different laboratories can all contribute to inconsistencies in reported pollutant level concentrations [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. These consequently affect the interpretation of the environmental status of a particular area and, in turn, similarly impact the ability to make informed decisions for effective environmental management.

A primary concern of inconsistent reporting of pollutant levels is that it fails to present a true extent of environmental pollution, thereby hindering the development and implementation of effective mitigation strategies [8]. It poses significant challenges for policymakers and environmental agencies in making informed environmental quality protection decisions [9]. Also, this may impede the accurate assessment of pollution severity and the identification of priority areas for intervention [10]. Furthermore, it undermines public confidence in governmental institutions and environmental agencies. Meanwhile, environmental agencies and policymakers depend on accurate data in order to make informed decisions regarding environmental remediation strategies, pollution control measures, and public health interventions [9]. Exposure to inconsistent or contradictory information regarding the trend of environmental quality can contribute to public hesitation, and minimises sense of trust in entities entrusted with safeguarding public health. It has been observed that a lack of trust can impede efforts to encourage community engagement in environmental management and promote environmental awareness [11]. When data are unreliable or contradictory, it becomes difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of pollution control measures, track trends in pollution levels, and identify areas of concern that require immediate attention. This can lead to delays in implementing necessary interventions and exacerbate problems in human health and the surrounding environment.

Achieving uniformity in pollutant analysis is the goal of using standardised procedures, methodologies, criteria, and reporting formats for measuring and reporting environmental pollutant concentrations [12,13]. This allows the international community and individual countries to set permissible limits for different substances in different environments. For instance, the International Organization for Standardisation (ISO) and the World Health Organization (WHO) have played a pivotal role in developing and disseminating standards [14,15]. These guidelines cover a wide range of pollutants, including air contaminants, water pollutants, and soil pollutants. They offer a comprehensive framework for sample collection, analytical methods, and data quality assurance through measures like quality control.

Individual countries have standardised methods for different pollutant analyses. For example, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) has numerous methods for analysing pollutants in water, and soil [[16], [17], [18]]. In the United Republic of Tanzania, standards are regulated by the Tanzania Bureau of Standards [19]. This includes standards and limits for pollutant levels in water, food crops, and soil. However, no standardized guidelines or procedures have been provided for analysing these levels. As a result, researchers in Tanzania have employed different methodologies for sampling, treatment, and analyses of different pollutant levels in various environments. This raises concerns about the accuracy and reliability of the reported pollutant levels and the temporal trends in different environments. The lack of standard methodologies has led to inconsistencies in reporting pollutant levels across different regions and contradictions regarding the actual pollution levels in the environment, which hinders effective decision-making.

In mining-impacted sites like Geita region in Tanzania, inconsistent pollutant reporting can have particularly severe consequences because mining operations often accelerate the release of pollutant metals, including heavy metals, into the surrounding environment [20,21]. As have been reported [22,23,24,25], the release of these pollutants can have detrimental impacts on human health, altering the quality of water sources, soil, sediment, food crops, and animals. When there is inconsistent reporting, the population becomes vulnerable due to the government's limited understanding of the extent of the problem, which depend on precise environmental status. Therefore, standardised analytical methods for different environmental pollutants are essential to ensure the comparability of results and the accurate interpretation of environmental status not only in terms of nominal pollutant values, but also with regard to their spatial and temporal distributions. This review aims to highlight the need for standardisation by showing how different analytical methodologies bring about different assessments of the environmental status in the same areas. It is a challenge arising from the lack of standard methods for analytical approaches in investigating pollution in the environment. This paper uses the metal pollution issues in the Geita region as a case study.

Review approach

This review has been done by accessing different published reports from Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, and Research Gate sources. Whereby, publications with reported pollutant levels in water, soil, sediments and food crops within Geita region in Tanzania were reviewed. Pollutants that were mostly reported by more than one author were summarised in five tables while indicating sampling method, sampling treatment, analytical method used and their concentrations reported by authors. From each of the accessed publications, summaries of reported metal concentrations, analytical methods, pre-treatment methods and author(s) were presented. More clarity was made by separating different types of metal in different tables.

The results in Table 1a, b, and c indicate varying Total mercury (THg) analytical methods for sediment or soil, water, and rice, respectively. In Table 1a samples were taken from Shenda artisanal gold mine, where samples were Air-dried for 2 weeks followed by drying at 40 °C in oven. The digestion process involved a 4:1 mixture of HCl and HNO3 followed by oxidation by using BrCl. Before analyses, samples were pre-treated by hydroxylamine HCl (NH2OH.HCl) and SnCl2. The analyses of treated samples were done by using Cold vapour atomic fluorescence (CV-AFS) method. The results obtained were in the rage of 0.0008–0.0608 mg/kg. Meanwhile, a different investigation at various sites in Geita District treated samples by first sieving, then air-drying at 25 °C, followed by oven drying at the same temperature. The samples were digested using aqua regia (3:1 HCl:HNO₃) on a 95 °C hotplate. The final analyses were performed using an AAS in flame mode. The results ranged from 0.03 to 2.3 mg/kg.

A study in the vicinity of Geita Gold Mine (GGM) [25] indicated that samples were air-dried, followed by digestion using HNO₃ in a microwave at 250 °C. The final processed samples were measured using ICP-OES, resulting in total mercury (THg) levels between 0.0075 and 0.0375 mg/kg. Other sample treatments for samples collected from Nyarugusu Gold Mine included oven drying at 105 °C, which was then treated with a mixture of acids, resulting in 25.4 mg/kg THg after analysis with AAS [26]. A short distance from Nyarugusu Mine, samples from the Rwamgasa mining area were treated differently. Samples were dried at 30 °C, then digested with aqua regia (1:1:1 HNO₃: HCl: demineralised H₂O). The analysis using CV-AAS and ICP-AES indicated THg residue levels ranging from 0.005 to 9.2 mg/kg. Finally, it was observed that samples from areas around GGM indicated THg residues in soil and sediment ranging between 1.12 and 2.86 mg/kg; these samples were oven-dried at 60 °C, followed by mixing with a cellulose binder and pulverisation. The analyses were performed using the Energy Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF) method [28].

Investigation of total mercury (THg) in water samples (Table 1b) is another example of a varying analytical methods approach. Samples from areas around the GGM [25] had THg residue concentrations ranging from 0.00 to 0.30 μg/L after being filtered and preserved at a temperature between 4 and 5 °C, then analysed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Meanwhile, samples from Rwamgasa Gold Mine were reported to have an average concentration of 0.04 μg/L; the filtered samples were preserved with a mixture of HNO₃ and K₂Cr₂O₇ and then analysed using CV-AFS [27].

More different analytical approaches were observed among different investigators on THg levels in rice cultivated in different parts of the Geita region (Table 1c). Each investigator prepared rice grains by grinding, but the observed differences were in drying methods and the chemicals used for digesting samples. Samples from Shenda Gold Mine were air-dried, then digested using a mixture of HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ heated at 70 °C in a water bath. THg in the rice samples was obtained by oxidation with BrCl, followed by neutralization using NH₂OH.HCl and treatment with SnCl₂. The analyses were performed using the CV-AFS method, and the results ranged from 0.075 to 0.159 mg/kg [23]. Rice samples from areas around GGM were treated differently. Firstly, drying was done in an oven at 60 °C, then the pulverised samples were pressed into pellets for analysis using EDXRF [28]. The reported concentrations of THg residues ranged from 0.27 to 1.13 mg/kg. Rice samples from Mugusu artisanal gold mining village were investigated [29] and reported to have average THg residues of 0.026 mg/kg. However, the sample treatment was conducted using a mixture of acids (1:2; HNO₃:H₂SO₄) under reflux, where the liquid was analysed under reflux.

A review of various analytical methods for As is shown in Table 2a (for soil/sediment) and 2b (for water). The soil and sediment samples were all collected from the surface to a maximum depth of 30 cm; however, the sample treatments varied. Table 2a presents samples from an area in the vicinity of GGM. The investigators [25] air-dried and digested the samples using a microwave method in a mixture of HNO3 at an elevated temperature of 250 °C. The digested samples were then analysed using ICP-OES, with As concentrations ranging from 0 to 126.1 mg/kg. Another investigation examined the environment of the Rwamgasa gold mine, where soil and sediment samples were dried at 30 °C, then digested using an aqua regia and water mixture (1:1:1; HNO3 + HCl + demineralised H2O). The final analysis was performed using either CV-AAS or ICP-ES, and reported As values ranged from 1 to 67 mg/kg [27]. The area around GGM was investigated again by other researchers [28], who treated the sediment samples by drying them in an oven at 60 °C, followed by sieving and mixing with cellulose binders. Additional physical manipulations of samples were done to form pellets, which were then analysed using an EDXRF machine. The reported As concentrations were between 1 and 67 mg/kg.

The analysis of As residues in water (Table 2b) was conducted using various sample treatments and analytical methods. It was observed that the water sample collected from an area in the vicinity of GGM was simply filtered and stored at a temperature between 4 and 5 °C before being analysed using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS). The recorded concentrations ranged from 0.40 to 1.70 μg/l [25]. In contrast, water samples collected from various points within the Geita district were filtered and then acidified with HNO3 to lower their pH to 4 before analysis using ICP-MS. The average recorded concentration for these samples was 46.70 μg/l [30]. A final observation relates to an investigation conducted at the Rwamgasa gold mine, where filtered water samples were treated with a mixture of HNO3 and K2Cr2O7, brominated, and finally analysed using a CV-AFS method. The average As concentration from this area was found to be 0.87 μg/l [27].

The review results of Lead (Pb) in soil samples (Table 3) showed a variety of methods. At the Nyarugusu gold mines, the samples were dried in an oven at 105 °C, then digested using a mixture of three different acids: HNO₃, H₂SO₄, and HClO₄, before being analysed with AAS. The results indicated a concentration range of 0.01–18.5 mg/kg across different samples [26]. However, at the Rwamgasa gold mine, the soil samples were dried at 30 °C, then digested with an aqua regia mixture of 1:1:1; HNO₃: HCl: demineralised H₂O. Both CV-AAS and ICP-ES were used for analyses, where metal concentrations ranging was from 2 to 16 mg/kg [27]. Samples from one location near the Geita gold mine indicated that three investigators [28,31,32] used different methods of sample handling. In one case, the samples were dried at 60 °C, mixed with a cellulose binder, pulverised, homogenized, and pressed into pellets before being analysed with EDXRF, resulting in a concentration range of 13.83–29.73 mg/kg. Meanwhile, soil samples that were directly digested with a 1:1 mixture of H₂SO₄ and HNO₃ showed metal residues of 2.58 mg/kg. Lastly, air-dried samples digested using aqua regia and then mixed with EDTA solution prior to analysis by ICP-OES indicated a metal concentration of 17.99 mg/kg.

Table 4 presents a summary of zinc (Zn) residues in soil from the vicinity of the Geita Gold Mine (GGM) [25,28,32] and the Nyarugusu ASM [26], analysed after different sample treatments. In the vicinity of GGM, one analysis involved air-dried samples, which were microwave-digested with HNO₃ and then analysed using an AAS machine. The determined concentration range was 6.3–121.6 mg/kg. Another investigation [28] treated the samples by drying them at 60 °C, then mixing them with a cellulose binder, pulverizing, and homogenizing them. After analysis with EDXRF, the concentration range was 6.60–46.25 mg/kg. Lastly, results indicated different handling where the samples were air-dried, followed by a digestion process involving a 3:1 mixture of HCl and HNO₃ (aqua regia). Prior to analysis, EDTA solution was added, and after further physical manipulation, the samples were analysed using ICP-OES, resulting in an average Zn concentration of 20.13 mg/kg [32]. At the Nyarugusu ASM, the samples for Zn analysis were oven-dried at 105 °C. The post-treatment process involved cooling and mixing the samples with distilled water, followed by analysis using an AAS machine. The observed concentration range was between 0.001 and 23.5 mg/kg.

Nickel (Ni), as presented in Table 5, was reported in soil samples all collected in the vicinity of GGM. However, the analytical approaches used by various investigators differed, as did the values of Ni concentrations in the samples. It was first observed that the samples were air-dried and then digested using a microwave with concentrated HNO3 at 250 °C. Analyses using ICP-OES indicated a Ni concentration range of 4.5–65.7 mg/kg [25]. Another observation [28] indicates a different approach, where the samples were oven-dried at 60 °C, sieved, and then mixed with a cellulose binder and subjected to other physical manipulations to form pellets for analysis using EDXRF. The reported concentration range for this method is 0.81–61.83 mg/kg. Further review shows that soil samples were treated with a mixture of acids: conc. H2SO4, conc. HNO3, and HClO4. The extract was analysed with AAS, from which the reported average concentration was 5.33 mg/kg [31]. The final observation from the same area indicates that samples were air-dried and sieved. A USEPA method 3051 is noted to have been used, which included digesting the samples using aqua regia (3:1; HCl: HNO3), treating the extract with an EDTA solution, and then analysing it using ICP-OES. The reported average concentration result is 7.43 mg/kg [32]. All these samples were from the same area but were treated differently, resulting in different Ni concentration values.

Discussion

In this review, analytical instruments have not been considered important factors in establishing accurate pollutant levels in the environment, nor have the sampling methods, as all are accepted and approved approaches for ensuring reliable research outcomes. However, discrepancies in reported heavy metal concentrations within the region may be associated with the different sample treatment methods used by various investigators (Table 1a, Table 1b, Table 1c, Table 2a, Table 2b, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5). Each author employed different methods with no reference to locally established (Tanzania) methods for metal analytical procedures. This diversity in methodologies may have significantly affected the reported concentrations, even in studies conducted within the same area. Although the investigations were carried out in different years, the magnitude of concentration differences is alarming for samples taken from within the same investigation area under similar influencing factors, such as mining activities. In this study, it was difficult to establish the sampling time for each individual investigation, but it is known that differences in sampling time/season are important for determining other parameters that influence the quantification of contaminants in the environment, such as seasonal variations and associated weather conditions. Apart from the challenges in harmonizing the sampling seasons across all investigations, a clearer area for harmonization is the analytical methods used for different metals.

This review highlights clear differences in the analytical procedures used by different investigators on the same parameters. An example is a study conducted around the Geita Gold Mine (GGM) [28], which is summarised in Table 1a. The reported mean concentration of Hg in soil was about 1.89 mg/kg, which is higher than the 0.0625 mg/kg reported by another investigator [25] in the same location. Although all reported values were within the maximum allowable limit set by the Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS) [33] and WHO/FAO [34] (2 mg/kg in soil), it is necessary to have comparable values for decision-making or action. The same authors [25] reported concentrations of As in soil, with the maximum concentration around GGM found to be approximately 126.1 mg/kg, exceeding both the WHO/FAO and TBS limits of about 20 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg, respectively. This value is also significantly higher than the 5.5 mg/kg reported in a different study [28] (Table 2a). The reported concentration of about 5.5 mg/kg was within the WHO/FAO [34] limit but greater than TBS recommended soil quality value [33].

The concentrations of Pb exhibited similar inconsistencies. The reported mean concentration of Pb in soil samples around the Geita Gold Mine was about 23.46 mg/kg [28], which is higher than the concentration observed by other authors. For example, a concentration of about 2.58 mg/kg was reported around the Geita Gold Mine [31], while another report [32] for the same location indicated 17.99 mg/kg (Table 3). All these values were within the acceptable limits set by the TBS and WHO/FAO (200 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg, respectively) as presented in Refs. [33,34]. However, the significant difference in concentrations raises the question: why is there such a discrepancy? A more methodologically differentiated approach was observed in the analysis of Pb [28], where the sample treatment involved mixing with a cellulose binder, resulting in a concentration of about 23.46 mg/kg. In contrast, another study [31] used the addition of H2SO4 and HClO4 to enhance the digestion of soil, resulting in a reported concentration of 2.58 mg/kg; both levels remained within the TBS and WHO/FAO limits [33,34]. The addition of aqua regia digestion [32], based on the US EPA method, was also employed before analysing the concentration of Pb in soil (Table 3), which yielded a different approach with a reported concentration of about 17.99 mg/kg.

A study conducted in the vicinity of the GGM [25] reported a maximum concentration of Zn in soil of about 121.6 mg/kg, which was higher than the maximum concentration of 20.13 mg/kg reported around GGM [32] and the 121.6 mg/kg reported near GGM [28] (Table 4). Although the reported levels of Zn were within the TBS limit (150 mg/kg) [33] and the WHO/FAO limit (300 mg/kg) [34], the significant variation in results due to the different methodologies used, as presented in Table 4, raises concerns about the accuracy of reported metal levels in the environment. These differences are more closely linked to the analytical methods for metals. For example, prior to the analysis of Zn in soil, a microwave digestion method was used [25], resulting in a range of about 6.3–121.6 mg/kg. In contrast, a concentration of about 20.13 mg/kg was reported by another method that involved aqua regia digestion [32]. Additionally, samples were mixed with a cellulose binder [28] before analysis, which yielded a reported concentration of 25.99 mg/kg. Although these concentrations were within the acceptable limits set by the TBS [33] and WHO/FAO [34], the addition of H2SO4 and HClO4 was also reported [31], resulting in a concentration of about 5.33 mg/kg.

The significant impact of different analytical methods is evident in Table 5, where varying concentrations of Ni in soil are shown, despite all samples being collected from the same location, GGM. It is observed that each report indicates different maximum concentrations of Ni in the soil: 65.7 mg/kg [25], 61.83 mg/kg [28], 7.43 mg/kg [32], and 5.33 mg/kg [31]. All reported concentrations were within the TBS recommended limit of 100 mg/kg in soil. However, some ([25,28]) exceeded the WHO/FAO [34] recommended concentration limit of 50 mg/kg. The major observation in establishing the certainty of the reported concentrations lies in the differences in sample treatment methodologies. All studies that reported concentrations either below or above the WHO/FAO [34] recommended limit employed different analytical approaches, making it difficult to harmonize the reported concentration values.

The variations in analytical techniques, sample treatment methods, and the resulting inconsistencies do not support the use of reliable data for policy-making or setting actions. As observed, the results are not comparable. In general, these discrepancies may have contributed to inconsistencies in reported pollutant levels in the environment, thereby reducing trust in the pollution data regularly reported by different environmental agencies in Tanzania.

Conclusion and recommendations

It can be concluded that the use of different methodologies in sample treatment during pollutant analysis can result in variations in the reported pollutant levels, both in different areas and even within the same area. This is due to the fact that each methodology has its own strengths and weaknesses depending on the laboratory working conditions, which include sampling, storage, treatment, and other factors such as temperature variation, time, and the volume of reagents used. All these factors can reduce the accuracy and reliability of reported results.

The variation of pollutant level results reported in the Geita region, even within the same location, implies a need for standard analytical methods when investigating pollutant levels in the environment. The National environmental agencies and government institutions must establish clear, standardised methods recommended for assessing the environmental status in the country. Recommended methods should target specific types of samples, such as soil, sediment, water, vegetables, and other food crops. Moreover, existing standardised methods as document by EPA and ISO by adhering to recommended limits as presented in WHO and national standards (TBS) should be considered. In quality control, chemical reagents used, control charts, field blanks, and calibration standards should be standardised to ensure the quality of results reported; but also, validation of the analytical instruments by considering the detection limits, matrix spikes and certified reference materials. This will facilitate sound decision-making, especially when decisions are based on the extent of environmental contamination. This review indicates that almost all studies are sporadically undertaken, resulting in varying values that can hardly be used as testimony for decision-making.
