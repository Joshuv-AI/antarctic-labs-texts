Molecular diagnosis of Trichinella spp.: current status and future prospects
Authors: Su-Ming Yu, Ting-Ting Li, Bao-Quan Fu, Nian-Zhang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrichinellosis, a significant parasitic zoonotic disease, poses a risk to public health as well as economic implications for the safety of animal feed. Consumption of raw or undercooked meat containing Trichinella larvae can lead to trichinellosis infection. Many molecular diagnostic methods have been developed to identify Trichinella spp. at muscle or intestine stages. However, no molecular diagnostic technique is currently advised for routine testing on Trichinella infection in food animals, particularly at the early stage of infection. Here, the authors review the development of molecular diagnostic techniques of Trichinella spp., such as PCR, RT-PCR, LAMP, RPA and other methods to detect Trichinella DNA. Recently, the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats and CRISPR-associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas) technology holds great promise for diagnostic testing by providing rapid, sensitive and specific methods for detection. Diagnosis of Trichinella spp. based on CRISPR-Cas system may be a promising method meeting the needs of individual testing.Keywords: Trichinella, Trichinellosis, Molecular diagnosis, Zoonosis, CRISPR-Cas

Introduction

Trichinellosis is a serious parasitic disease distributed worldwide. It is a global public health issue (Dacal et al. 2020). Trichinella has a wide range of hosts and can infect over 100 species of mammals, birds, and reptiles (Zarlenga et al. 2020). The parasite’s life cycle is to be completed within the same host and mainly consists of three developmental stages: adult (AD), newborn larvae (NBL) and muscle larvae (ML) (Ding et al. 2020). Human trichinellosis is acquired by consuming raw or undercooked meat containing the infective larvae. Once the larvae penetrate into intestinal epithelial cells, the worm undergoes four molts and develops into adults (Bilska-Zając et al. 2021). The delivered NBL migrate to the skeletal muscles through the circulatory system, where they transform the host’s muscle cells into nurse cells (NCs) and parasitize them (Gottstein et al. 2009).

Reports of Trichinella infections exist worldwide, except in Antarctica (Feidas et al. 2014). Occurrence of trichinellosis has been reported in 55 countries, with 65,818 cases and 42 deaths reported from 1986 to 2009 (Murrell & Pozio, 2011). In recent years, the overall incidence and prevalence intensity of human trichinellosis have shown a declining trend, with a marked and consistent decrease in the number of newly reported cases (Dubey et al. 2024). However, localized outbreaks continue to occur. For instance, in 2010, 64 cases were reported in Argentina (Calcagno et al. 2014); in 2019, 11 cases were confirmed in Spain (Moral et al. 2022); and in 2022, 6 infections were documented in North America (Cash-Goldwasser et al. 2024). These incidents underscore the necessity of maintaining epidemiological surveillance and testing for Trichinella to ensure food safety.

The primary source of human acquired Trichinella is ingestion of undercooked pork or derived products (Bilska-Zając et al. 2021; Eslahi et al. 2022). For pigs, the global prevalence of Trichinella reached 2.02% by the end of 2021 (Borhani et al. 2023). In recent years, trichinellosis has recognized as a re-emerging zoonotic disease in certain regions of the world (Crisóstomo-Jorquera And Landaeta-Aqueveque 2022), including in the Philippines (Lagrimas et al. 2021), Chilean (Hidalgo et al. 2019), Iran and Turkey (Borhani et al. 2023; Moazeni et al. 2019). In addition, reports of human trichinellosis due to the consumption of wild animals, such as black bears, are also increasing in certain regions (Crisóstomo-Jorquera et al. 2022).

To prevent the transmission of these zoonotic parasites to humans, it is crucial to test meat samples from susceptible animals in slaughterhouses or wild game processing plants (Franssen et al. 2014). The International Commission on Trichinellosis (ICT) recommends testing all meat from susceptible animals intended for human consumption for Trichinella (Noeckler et al. 2019). Meat inspection for Trichinella is of great importance in preventing the infection and spread of this disease for humans. Meanwhile, monitoring wild animals helps researchers to assess the transmission pathways of Trichinella in nature (Mukaratirwa et al. 2013).

Testing meat for Trichinella will significantly reduce the incidence of human trichinellosis (Zhang et al. 2022a, b). An ideal diagnostic method should ensure the sensitivity and specificity required for large-scale monitoring of animal meat while offering simple operation steps and high detection efficiency. Until now, various diagnostic methods developed for the detection of Trichinella infection can be used to monitor animal populations and ensure food safety (Diaz et al. 2020). In the past few decades, diagnostic techniques have undergone continuous development, resulting in the establishment of various methods for detecting Trichinella infection (Shimoni & Froom, 2015 Gamble et al. 2019). These methods include pathogenetic diagnosis, serological diagnosis, and molecular biology-related diagnosis (Shimoni & Froom, 2015).

The magnetic stirrer artificial digestion method for pathogenetic diagnosis remains the gold standard method for diagnosing trichinellosis to date (Bai et al. 2017). However, it may be missed in routine meat inspection due to low worm burdens or sampling deviations (Diaz et al. 2020; Gajadhar et al. 2019). Detection of anti-Trichinella IgG by ELISA is a commonly used method for monitoring trichinellosis and conducting epidemiological surveys (Bruschi et al. 2019). But the method has a window period between the parasite infection and antibody positivity when using excretory-secretory (ES) antigens of ML, making it less effective for detecting early infections (Yang et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2017; Han et al. 2024).

Several studies have demonstrated that molecular diagnostics can be used to rapidly and accurately detect Trichinella and its specific species by identifying certain regions of the parasite’s genome (Barlow et al. 2021). Currently, however, molecular diagnostics are not recommended for routine testing in food animals. The application of these methods does not align with the needs of large-scale monitoring; instead, they are better suited for detecting Trichinella in wild animal meat intended for human consumption, as well as for point-of-care diagnosis of suspected infection cases. Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of such techniques remains limited due to high costs and other constraints.

In this review, we will summarize the development of molecular methods for diagnosing trichinellosis. Our aim is to analyze and evaluate the best methods for consideration in various scenarios, providing insights into the study of rapid, sensitive, accurate, and easily-handled molecular diagnostic methods for Trichinella early detection.

The literature review on the molecular diagnosis of the presence of Trichinella was conducted by searching the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI, https://www.cnki.net/), Scirus (http://www.scirus.com/), Google Scholar (http://scholar.google.com/), and PubMed (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/) databases for relevant publications up to July, 2025. The search terms included ‘trichinellosis’, ‘Trichinella’, ‘Molecular Diagnosis’, ‘Detection’, ‘PCR’, and other technique-specific terms such as ‘qPCR’, ‘LAMP’, and ‘RPA’, along with their Chinese equivalents. Publications were selected for inclusion based on their relevance to the topic of molecular diagnostic methods for Trichinella spp. Original research articles and significant reviews published in English or Chinese were prioritized.

Conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

PCR technology has been demonstrated to be a rapid, sensitive, and specific method for the genetic diagnosis and species identification of Trichinella, with a detection sensitivity of up to 0.001 pg (equivalent to 0.01 larvae) (De Souza Rosés et al. 2020). The simplicity and specificity of PCR make it well-suited for the early detection of Trichinella infection. Parasite DNA can be detected in the blood as early as 5 days post-infection (dpi), with a detection sensitivity ranging from 1 to 200 larvae (Caballero-Garcia And Jimenez-Cardoso 2001). PCR can even detect Trichinella DNA in mouse feces from 3 h to 21 days post-infection, with a detection sensitivity of up to 0.016 larvae (Liu et al. 2017). A key requirement for a PCR marker is that it targets a parasite-specific gene (Barlow et al. 2021). The β-carbonic anhydrase (β-CA) gene, one of parasite-specific genes, is developed a PCR method to detect Trichinella larvae (Zolfaghari et al. 2016). This method has applications in meat inspection at slaughterhouses, quality control in the food industry, and medical laboratories (Dimzas et al. 2019). In meat inspection, PCR has demonstrated higher sensitivity compared to the artificial digestion method, detecting lower parasite loads.

Currently, there are only a limited number of studies that use PCR methods alone to detect Trichinella. In clinical diagnostics, PCR is more commonly combined with serological methods or Trichinella microscopy, or employed in multiplex PCR for genotyping studies of Trichinella (Zarlenga et al. 1999). In most cases, multiplex PCR can be used to identify species infection of Trichinella, which is recommended by ICT (Pozio & Zarlenga, 2019). The method for Trichinella genotyping is widely used in epidemiological studies, especially in regions where multiple genotypes of Trichinella co-circulate (Boros et al. 2021; Uakhit et al. 2023).

The PCR method has shown good performance in detecting and genotyping Trichinella in frozen muscles, with successful detection even in samples stored at −20 °C and carrying extremely low parasite loads (0.05–0.6 larvae per gram of muscle tissue) (Pozio et al. 2001). The PCR-sequencing integrated approach was also used for Trichinella detection and genotyping. In mainland China, various isolates were identified across diverse animal species, with the majority of investigations based on frozen muscle tissue (Fu et al. 2009; Jiang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. b, 2024). However, if the sample suffers severe freeze damage, the effectiveness of PCR detection may be compromised (Franssen et al. 2019).

The primary limitation of using PCR methods alone for Trinchinella detection is the risk of sampling error due to low parasite loads, particularly because DNA extraction is usually performed on very small tissue samples. To overcome this limitation, it is necessary to perform multiple PCR assays on tissues collected from different muscle regions. When food processing plants use PCR for Trichinella detection in meat products, they must also consider the relatively long reaction time and negatively impact detection efficiency, especially when processing a large number of samples (Scandrett et al. 2018). Thus, the method is recommended to combine with other methods to achieve both rapid detection and high accuracy, minimizing the chances of missed or incorrect diagnoses, such as ELISA (Dacal et al. 2020). On the other hand, the wide application of PCR is limited due to the high cost of detection equipment, the need for a controlled operating environment, and the requirement for specialized personnel. So they are primarily used for laboratory testing rather than point-of-care testing (POCT) (Pavic et al. 2019).

Real-time PCR assay (RT-PCR)

RT-PCR relies on the specific hybridization between probes designed for a particular species and the DNA in the sample. Only DNA that is complementary to the specific probe will hybridize with it. RT-PCR has gained increasing attention for its simplicity, speed, and high sensitivity, and it is widely used in microbiology for the detection of bacteria, viruses, and parasites (Gadkar & Filion, 2014).

RT-PCR can be used for both qualitative detection and quantitative measurement of parasite DNA. One study showed that the results of RT-PCR were consistent with those of traditional diagnostic methods (Trichinoscopy and artificial digestion) (Dimzas et al. 2021). While RT-PCR has higher detection sensitivity, varied from 0.01 to 7 larvae/g of tissue, it avoids the cumbersome procedures associated with traditional diagnostic methods. Wang et al. (2023a) conducted RT-PCR detection for Trichinella in sheep muscle, and sensitivity analysis demonstrated a 100% positive detection rate when adding 1 larva of Trichinella to every 5 mg of diaphragm muscle sample. This method was able to detect Trichinella infection even when microscopy results were negative, with a minimum concentration of Trichinella DNA detected as low as 0.0026 ng/µL (equivalent to 0.0064 larvae). Another study also indicated that RT-PCR could detect 1 ng of Trichinella DNA in 250 mg of mouse muscle, and Trichinella DNA could be detected in the diaphragm of mice on the 7th day post-infection (Tantrawatpan et al. 2013). In the detection of Trichinella in fox tissues, RT-PCR was designed to target the internal transcription spacer 2 (ITS2) region of the Trichinella genome. The results showed that RT-PCR had a very high sensitivity for detecting larvae, with a detection limit of approximately 0.01 larvae per gram of tissue (Atterby et al. 2009). Variations in the reported detection limit are primarily attributed to the choice of infection host model, the DNA extraction method employed, and the sensitivity of the primer and probe sets.

RT-PCR using the Rep gene was employed to detect early-stage Trichinella infection in mice, achieving 100% sensitivity when testing the diaphragm 8 days after artificial infection. This method can quickly and sensitively determine whether mice are infected with Trichinella. Similar to PCR, RT-PCR also demonstrates excellent specificity in Trichinella detection, with no cross-reactivity with more than a dozen parasites, including S. stercoralis (Tantrawatpan et al. 2013). In a report on the detection of Trichinella in mouse muscle, RT-PCR achieved a 100% detection rate at 4 dpi, while a concurrent Nano-based ELISA only reached 77.8%, and traditional ELISA had a detection rate of just 44.4%. This supports the utility of RT-PCR as an early diagnostic tool for Trichinella infection (Mohammad et al. 2023).

Based on the excellent detection efficiency of RT-PCR, it performs well in various field testing scenarios. In the field of epidemiological surveys, RT-PCR can not only be used to detect Trichinella infections but also differentiate isolates at the species or genotype level (Cuttell et al. 2012). Researchers utilized RT-PCR to detect Trichinella in the muscle tissues of carnivores in Bosnia and Herzegovina, successfully identifying infections in over ten animal species, including Ursus arctos, and performed genetic typing using RT-PCR (Omeragić et al. 2024). In the food testing sector, RT-PCR can be applied to detect Trichinella in various meat products during quarantine. If positive samples are found during quarantine, it aids relevant authorities in taking timely actions regarding the implicated animals (Wang et al. 2020).

To enhance the detection efficiency of real-time PCR methods, high-resolution melting analysis (HRMA) was used to analyze changes in fluorescent signals during the melting of different sequences through computer algorithms. This generates distinct melting curves, enabling the differentiation of various Trichinella species (Reslová et al. 2017).

However, the application of real-time PCR in clinical settings faces limitations due to difficulties in primer-probe design, the need for technical expertise, the dependence on professional equipements and the high cost of specialized reagents. Similar to PCR, RT-PCR has significant constraints in POCT. Given that RT-PCR can achieve good detection results in the early stages of Trichinella infection, combining with ELISA is one of the viable strategies to improve the accuracy of Trichinella diagnosis.

Digital droplet PCR (ddPCR)

Digital droplet PCR combines microfluidic partitioning with endpoint PCR detection to achieve absolute quantification without requiring standard curves. This technology demonstrates superior sensitivity in detecting low-abundance targets (< 1%) and enhanced tolerance to PCR inhibitors, particularly in complex biological matrices (Kuypers & Jerome, 2017; Tschritter et al. 2024). A recent study utilizes the ddPCR tool to assess the presence of zoonotic pathogens in Canadian polar bears, revealing insights into host-pathogen dynamics in the rapidly changing Arctic. For Trichinella spp., the prevalence rate was 75%. (Tschritter et al. 2024). These findings underscore ddPCR’s potential for monitoring zoonotic pathogens in marine ecosystems while highlighting the need for increased surveillance of emerging parasitic infections in arctic carnivores. Meanwhile, limitations to the clinical adoption of ddPCR include its technical complexity, requirement for specialized expertise, low throughput, and significant laboratory infrastructure, coupled with a relatively high cost per test.

Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP)

LAMP efficiently amplifies target genes in large quantities under isothermal conditions within a short period (Soroka et al. 2021). This technique has found application in detecting pathogens from human, animal and plants (Das et al. 2019). LAMP technology can amplify the target sequence over 100 times in less than an hour, with a sensitivity level 10–100 times higher than traditional PCR, and slightly lower than that of RT-PCR (Lin et al. 2013).

LAMP offers significant advantages for Trichinella detection as a rapid (< 70 min), isothermal (63 °C), and instrument-minimal technique. Several previous studies demonstrated that the technique could effectively detect Trichinella in muscle samples, with a sensitivity of 0.01 larvae/g of muscle tissue, which is over 10 times higher than that of PCR. Meanwhile, it shows no cross-reactivity with other parasites like A. lumbricoides, demonstrating good specificity (Li et al. 2012; Kong et al. 2021). Similar to PCR, LAMP can detect Trichinella DNA in feces during the early stages of infection (Lin et al. 2013; Garg et al. 2022). The ability to obtain results via fluorescence staining and minimal equipment requirements (e.g., water bath) positions LAMP as a promising tool for field diagnostics and direct meat inspection.

Despite these strengths, several studies have highlighted challenges with LAMP, including primer design complexity, risk of carryover contamination, and false positives, and some other key limitations hinder LAMP broader adoption. LAMP is generally acknowledged to be slightly lower than RT-PCR (Lin et al. 2013; Garg et al. 2022). This performance gap influences its suitability for large-scale surveillance where RT-PCR may be preferred. Crucially, LAMP currently struggles with multiplexing, hindering the simultaneous detection and differentiation of Trinchinella species and geographical strains in a single reaction (Crego-Vicente et al. 2024). Cost is another factor, with analysis indicating LAMP (~$5.90/sample) is more expensive per test than qPCR (~$3.78/sample) and alternative isothermal methods like recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA, ~$5.36/sample) (Barlow et al. 2021). These factors currently constrain LAMP’s widespread implementation for Trichinella despite its field potential.

Given its suitability for resource-limited settings and user-friendliness for non-experts, LAMP is promising for clinical and field applications. Future applications should focus on optimizing primer design using bioinformatic algorithms to reduce the risk of nonspecific amplification. Additionally, developing lyophilized reagents and microfluidic chips with pre-loaded reagents could enable one-step operation by simply adding samples, thereby minimizing contamination risks.

Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA)

RPA is an isothermal nucleic acid amplification technique enabling rapid pathogen detection (typically < 1 h) without thermal cycling, functioning effectively between 25 and 45 °C (Daher et al. 2016; James & Macdonald, 2015). Its application in diagnosing Trichinella spp. infections is particularly significant. Li et al. (2019) developed an RPA assay targeting the mitochondrial small subunit ribosomal RNA (rrnS) gene, combined with lateral flow detection (LF-RPA). This assay could detect 12 known Trichinella species within 25 min, achieving a sensitivity of 100 fg DNA, approximately 10-fold higher than conventional PCR. Crucially, potential inhibitors present in muscle tissue did not significantly impair Trichinella detection using this LF-RPA method. These attributes establish LF-RPA as a simple, rapid, highly sensitive, and specific tool for diagnosing Trichinella in livestock and clinical samples. However, external factors such as temperature, stirring, and SDS concentration can influence RPA results; therefore, the impact of inhibitors should be eliminated during experimental procedures. (Tan et al. 2022; Li et al. 2018; An et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2015; Rattaprasert et al. 2023).

Specifically optimizing and validating RPA for Trichinella across diverse settings requires further study. The limitation of LF-RPA for detecting Trichinella would potentially miss samples with very low parasite loads. Primer design is prone to yielding non-specific bands, complicating interpretation. Additionally, it still requires basic lab equipment for nucleic acid extraction, hindering true field deployment. Nevertheless, the proven performance of LF-RPA for Trichinella, coupled with the potential for integration with other technologies, positions it as a highly promising field-deployable diagnostic tool warranting expanded investigation (Danthanarayana et al. 2023).

CRISPR-Cas technology

In recent years, the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats and CRISPR-associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas) system has evolved beyond gene editing into a powerful diagnostic platform, leveraging its high-precision nucleic acid targeting for pathogen detection (Gleerup & Mogensen, 2022; Hillary et al. 2021). Key effector proteins (e.g., Cas12a/b, Cas13a, Cas14) enable sequence-specific recognition via guide RNAs (gRNAs), triggering collateral cleavage of reporter molecules for signal generation (Bonini et al. 2021; Kaminski et al. 2021). The development of Cas12a-based DETECTR led the system to achieve attomolar sensitivity through fluorescence readouts (Chen et al. 2018). CRISPR-Cas diagnostics offer an enhanced specificity and sensitivity of molecular detection (Kellner et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023b), and increase significant advantages in POCT due to operational simplicity (Gootenberg et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2022; Mao et al. 2023). Nowadays, the CRISPR-Cas system is widely applied in detecting diverse pathogens including Plasmodium, Leishmania, Babesia, and antimicrobial-resistant bacteria (Sima et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2022).

CRISPR-Cas system promises significant advances for Trichinella detection, offering low parasite loads (sub-microscopic infections) sensitivity without specialized equipment (Mao et al. 2023), which enhances user-friendliness for applications like meat safety screening (Liu et al. 2022). This technology could help achieve trichinellosis elimination goals through improved clinical management, enhanced surveillance, and more effective control programs, particularly in the resource-limited settings where the disease often causes the greatest burden. However, key limitations of CRISPR-Cas system to detect Trichinella include the time-consuming nucleic acid extraction, which yields low concentration and purity and consequently hinders amplification and POC use (Cao et al. 2025). Further research on the CRISPR-Cas system should focus on optimizing sample preparation, boost nucleic acid quality, cost, and validation to translate this powerful molecular tool into practical, life-saving diagnostic applications.

Conclusion and perspective

Trichinellosis poses a significant threat to both human health and the livestock industry (Malone et al. 2024). Numerous reports indicate that in recent years, both the incidence and disease burden of human trichinellosis have significantly decreased. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that outbreaks continue to occur. Hence, rapid and accurate diagnosis of the disease is crucial for its treatment and prevention. Until now, significant progress has been made in serological diagnostic techniques for Trichinella in recent years. Currently, ELISA is the predominant serological test for Trichinella diagnosis, which widely applied across diverse contexts, including epidemiological surveillance, wildlife monitoring, and clinical investigations (Scandrett et al. 2018). However, limitations in the detection window and inadequate specificity present significant challenges that must be addressed.

The molecular diagnostic methods are increasingly gaining attention due to their high specificity and sensitivity (Table 1). Thermocycling methods (e.g., PCR/qPCR) provide exceptional accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility, making them valuable for detecting early or low-burden infections (Gamble et al. 2019). Isothermal amplification techniques (e.g., LAMP, RPA) further enhance field applicability by eliminating dependence on thermal cyclers. However, when referring to their practicality for routine surveillance and POCT scenarios, molecular techniques are hindered by complex workflow (DNA extraction), specialized infrastructure (for thermocycling) and some other unresolved field-deployment barriers (Ribicich et al. 2021). Particularly, molecular diagnostic methods may not be universally applicable for Trichinella detection in meat, particularly when dealing with samples exhibiting low parasitic burdens (Gabriël et al. 2022).

In conclusion, molecular-based methods offer high sensitivity and specificity for detecting Trichinella spp., enabling species identification. Their high throughput potential is a significant advantage over conventional artificial digestion method. However, their widespread application, especially in resource-limited settings, is constrained by the requirement for skilled personnel, and relatively high operational costs. Future efforts should focus on developing cost-effective, automated, and field-deployable kits to facilitate their integration into routine diagnostics and surveillance programs for trichinellosis.
