Intradural extramedullary cystic lesions: an interesting cauda equina cysticercosis case report and a literature review

Background

Cysticercosis, a common parasitic infection, is caused by cysticercus [1]. Nevertheless, spinal involvement only has a rate ranging from 0.25 to 5.8% of patients [2]. Pure spinal cysticercosis is quite exceptional and often difficult to be diagnosed. It is therefore necessary to be familiar with the imaging findings of spinal cysticercosis. Fortunately, the characteristic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings of a living cysticercus and a dying cysticercus were presented simultaneously in this unusual case.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman presented with a 2-month history of increasing pain in her right buttock, rectal tenesmus and uncontrolled micturition. Unsuccessful prior treatments had included indomethacin and mecobalamin for more than 6 weeks. Physical examination revealed saddle hypoesthesia as well as rectal and bladder sphincter dysfunction. T2-weighted MRI of the lumbar spine revealed multilocular and serpentine intradural extramedullary cystic lesions at the level of L5–S2 compressing the cauda equina (Fig. 1A). The serpentine lesion had a ‘dot sign’ appearance (Fig. 1A, white arrowhead), and the multilocular cyst showed a ring-enhancing wall of about 2 cm diameter after gadolinium injection (Fig. 1B, red arrowhead). All blood, urine and stool test results were within normal ranges. We couldn’t find evidence for a tumor, infection or arachnoid cyst. After communicating with the patient, and before the diagnosis was confirmed, surgery was performed to avoid irreversible neurological dysfunction. We removed the L5 and S1 spinous processes and cut open the dura mater. Cystic tissues were seen squeezing the cauda equina backward. We then carefully excised the cysts and lifted them upwards to remove the cystic lesion. Pathological examination of the surgical samples revealed cysticercus cellulosae (Fig. 1C), leading to the final diagnosis of intraspinal cysticercosis. On further questioning we discovered that the patient was a pig farmer, but she had never eaten raw pork nor found any wormlike object in her faeces. Anthelmintic treatment was initiated with albendazole 0.9 g daily for 2 months and prednisone 60 mg daily for 2 weeks. At the 6-month follow-up visit, the patient was asymptomatic.

Discussion

Cases of cysticercus located in the cauda equina are very few in the literature. Signs and symptoms may vary from vague backache, radiculopathy, and myelopathy to cauda equina syndrome, depending on the location of the cyst [3, 4]. Notably, spinal neurocysticercosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients with a cystic lesion in the spinal subarachnoid space [5]. Additionally, recurrence of primary spinal neurocysticercosis should be considered in the diagnosis of a spinal arachnoid cyst if there is a past history [6]. Urgent surgical decompression of the cord and peripheral nerves usually should be conducted for spinal neurocysticercosis to prevent further worsening of the neurologic symptoms [3, 5, 7].

Intraspinal cysticercosis, usually with serious neurological deterioration, is rarely diagnosed because its clinical manifestations are nonspecific [8, 9], and most physicians might not be familiar with its imaging features. Neurocysticercosis presents four different stages in imaging. Vesicular cyst stage: hypointense T1-WI and hyperintense on T2-WI cystic lesions, with mass effect and minimal contrast enhancement. Colloidal cyst stage: cystic lesions with perilesional edema and ring enhancement. Nodular-granular stage: no cystic component and presents nodules with diffuse enhancement. Granular-calcified stage: multiple punctate calcifications [10–12]. The stages can be distinguished in spinal cord lesions but are more easily recognized in the brain parenchyma [13]. Spinal lesions in neurocysticercosis are rare, and the most involved thoracic segment complicated with syringomyelia can also occur due to the obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid flow [12]. Fortunately, the characteristic MRI findings of a living cysticercus and a dying cysticercus were presented simultaneously in this unusual case. Despite being caused by parasitic infection, the disease sometimes results in negative serology, especially if antibiotic treatment had been given previously. Detailed personal history and enzyme-linked immunoelectrotransfer blot (EITB) assay of the cerebrospinal fluid are helpful in confirming the diagnosis [14]. Unfortunately, these tests were not performed in our patient. At present, the primary therapy for intraspinal cysticercosis is surgery [15].

Conclusions

In summary, normal negative serology result do not rule out cysticercosis. The characteristic imaging findings of different cysticercus cysts as well as detailed personal history and EITB usually provide useful diagnostic clues.
