Combined purslane ethanolic extract and metformin attenuate cognitive dysfunction in HFD/STZ-induced diabetic rats via modulation of oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and neurotransmitters
Authors: Naglaa Bahr, Abd El-Kader M. Abd El-Kader, Alaa Eldin Salah Eldin, Eatemad A. Awadalla, Maha A. El Demellawy, Doaa A. Ghareeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes is associated with a number of significant long-term effects. In this study we consider that purslane which possesses numerous of pharmacological properties, and metformin, an antidiabetic drug, may have a therapeutic effects on diabetes-induced memory impairments in rats.MethodsForty male albino rats were randomly divided into five groups. Group I served as control group. The other four groups were first fed on HFD followed by a single interpretonial (i.p.) dose of STZ at a dose of (35) mg/kg then the groups were divided as following Group II diabetic group Group III, PEE group administered with oral dose of purslane ethanolic extract (100 mg/kg) for another four weeks. Group IV, MET group administered with oral dose of metformin (100 mg/kg) for another four weeks. Group V (PEE + MET) administered with oral dose of combination of both purslane ethanolic extract (50 mg/kg) and MET (50 mg/kg) for another four weeks. During the treatment rats were tested for memory and learning abilities (Morri’s water maze test). Hippocampal samples were collected for biochemical, and histological measurements. Biochemical evaluation included (NO and TBARS) as an oxidative stress marker, (GSH, GPX, SOD, Catalase) as antioxidant, and inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1β, interleukin-IL-6). Also, P-tau protein, (dopamine and GABA) as neurotransmitters, and for cholinergic system (acetylcholinesterase) were assessed, in addition to histological examinations of hippocampus.ResultsDiabetic rats showed a marked cognitive impairment in the Morris water maze test and alteration in the other biochemical and histological features. Intrestingly, PEE and MET treatments partially dramatically enhanced antioxidant levels. Also, reduced oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory mediators, and, phosphorylated tau levels. In addition, PEE and MET treatments partially modulated neurochemical profiles associated with memory function. The combined PEE + MET treatment showed the most pronounced improvement, reflecting synergistic effects. Individual data points highlighted consistent trends across animals. Also, it exhibited a significant restoration of normal hippocampal architecture, as confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin staining.ConclusionsThe data obtained indicated that PEE, either alone or in combination with MET, has strong neuroprotective potential against STZ/HFD-induced diabetes. These safeguarding effects are probably because of its strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease characterized by chronically elevated blood sugar levels. This disease is brought on by insufficient insulin secretion and/or insufficient insulin action in the target tissues [1]. Diabetes mellitus is a prevalent metabolic disease that is linked to long-term consequences such as peripheral neuropathy, nephropathy, angiopathy, and retinopathy. Furthermore, diabetes was also known to cause cognitive impairment as early as 1922 [2]. Since then, research have shown changes in neurotransmission, structural and electrophysiological abnormalities, and neurobehavioral changes, including higher risk of depression and specific cognitive impairment [3]. Moreover, diabetes-related acute metabolic and vascular disturbances (such as severe hypo- and hyperglycemic episodes and stroke) do not appear to be a major factor in the development of the reported cerebral symptoms of diabetes. Even though these cognitive impairments seem to be low to moderate in severity, they can seriously impair day-to-day functioning and negatively impact quality of life [4].

Diabetes has long-term effects on the brain at the structural, neurophysiological, and neuropsychological levels. A number of pathogenic factors, including hypoglycemic episodes, cerebrovascular changes, insulin’s role in the brain, and the mechanisms of hyperglycemia-induced damage, appear to be involved in the pathogenesis of cerebral dysfunction in diabetes [5]. One of the main characteristics of type 2 diabetes is oxidative stress, which is typified by an excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) [6], these molecules accumulate in mitochondria, impairing mitochondrial activity, and creating inflammation and organs dysfunction [7].

Because of its high rate of oxygen consumption, high lipid content, and relative lack of antioxidant enzymes compared to other tissues, the brain is particularly susceptible to oxidative damage. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are involved in numerous neurodegenerative diseases, because neuronal cells are particularly vulnerable to oxidative assaults [8]. Also, it has long been known that immune cell stimulation frequently results in the release of reactive oxygen species [9], and both acute and chronic inflammatory states are linked to notable changes in redox equilibrium because of the increased production of oxidants [10]. Therefore, as reviewed by Spychalowicz et al. [11], reducing oxidative stress through the administration of antioxidants has been assessed as a potentially helpful anti-inflammatory therapy. Overall, numerous research’ findings have unequivocally demonstrated the close connection between oxidative stress and inflammation. Previous research has shown that, chronic inflammation in type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and its leakage to the brain may promote microglial activation and the generation of inflammatory mediators like interleukins (IL) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [12].

Involved in synaptic transmission, neurotransmitters are a vast family of chemical messengers that control physiological processes in the central and peripheral nervous systems [13]. There are over 100 identified neurotransmitters. Their molecular structure, chemical group, method of action, and physiological roles (inhibitory or excitatory) can all be used to categorize them [14]. Changes in neurotransmitter balance and synaptic activity are linked to cognitive impairment and dementia, which are consequences of obesity and type 2 diabetes [15]. Peripherally, glucotoxicity has been shown to raise glutamate levels, which activate pancreatic N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors and cause β-cell malfunction and neuronal damage; inhibiting these β-cell function was enhanced both in vivo and in vitro by receptors [16]. A deeper comprehension of the metabolic connections between AD and DM opens the way to the discovery of new treatment options that can stop the advancement of AD and treat T2DM, in addition to offering fresh insights into the genesis and interaction between these two conditions.

By raising insulin resistance and stopping the liver from making gluconeogenesis, metformin mostly reduces plasma glucose levels. Further research indicates that it has anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-oxidative properties [17]. Nowadays, metformin is thought to be a miracle medication that could help with a number of diseases, including AD [18]. Even while T2D patients depend on anti-diabetic medications such glucophage and sulfonylurea to manage hyperglycemia, other individuals seem to find the side effects and consequences of these medications unacceptable [19]. Diabetic ketoacidosis, gastrointestinal problems, and severe hypoglycemia are some of these adverse consequences [20].

Naturally occurring phytochemicals are inexpensive, easily absorbed, and mostly safe. As a result, they are widely used in nations to reduce morbidity and death from chronic illnesses [21]. Diabetes sufferers frequently use purslane, which has recently piqued the interest of scientists. Antioxidant-acting substances found in purslane include vitamins A, C, and E, it also includes phenolic alkaloids, beta carotene, oleracin A, B, and E, and other nutrients that have been demonstrated to increase the body’s total antioxidant capacity (TAC) [22]. Moreover, numerous researches have demonstrated purslane’s neuroprotection and significant improvement of insulin resistance [23]. Purslane’s neuroprotective and cognitive-enhancing properties are mostly attributed to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [24]. Thus, by monitoring its impact on the hyperglycemic, oxidative stress-inflammatory axis, and T2D model, the current study aimed to shed insight on the neuroprotective role of purslane (PEE), metformin (MET), and their combination on AD-like disorders.

Materials and methods

Animals

Forty Male Albino Sprague-Dawley rats (Rattus norvegicus) weighing between 110 and 150 g were obtained from the Faculty of Medicine’s animal house at Alexandria University. Rats were kept in cages made of polycarbonate in groups of eight. They were maintained under conventional humidity and temperature settings with a 12-hour photo period. The animals had unlimited access to water and were fed a constant diet. All animal experiments were carried out in compliance with established protocols and were supervised by the Pharmaceutical and Fermentation Industry Development Center (PFIDC), City of Scientific Research & Technological Applications’ Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (IACUC #0.80–2 A-124). A freshly prepared high-fat diet (HFD) consists of 17% carbohydrates, 25% protein, and 58% fat [25]. The rats were divided into five groups after they had acclimated: Group I (Control group) was fed on normal diet, Group II (Diabetic group) was fed on HFD for four weeks, following a single intraperitoneal (ip) dose of streptozotocin (STZ) (35 mg/kg dissolved in 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer, pH 4.5/kg), the rats were allowed unrestricted access to food and water with 5% glucose for the first 24 h to make up for any potential hypoglycemia. Until the animals were confirmed to have diabetes at fasting blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dl, the non-diabetic rats were removed from the experiment, Group III (PEE group) diabetic rats were treated with an oral dose of purslane ethanolic extract (100 mg/kg) [26] for four weeks, Group IIII (MET group) diabetic rats were treated with an oral dose of metformin (100 mg/kg) [27] for 4 weeks. Group V (PEE + MET group) diabetic rats were orally treated with MET (50 mg/kg) combined with PEE (50 mg/kg) for 4 weeks (Scheme 1).

Experimental design

Materials

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) (Cat. No. ER1393), interleukin-6 (IL6) (Cat. No. ER0042), interleukin-1β (IL1β) (Cat. No. ER1094) and the phosphorylated tau (Cat. No. ER1304) ELISA kits were purchased from Fine Test Co., China, streptozotocin (STZ) (Cat. No. 188835), dopamine (Cat. No. D2960000), and GABA (Cat. No. 03835) were purchased from sigma aldrich company, USA. MET was purchased from Merck Serono company, Germany, and all other chemicals were in stander grade.

Purslane ethanolic extract (PEE) preparation

At the Herbarium of Aswan University in Egypt, fresh purslane was recognized and labeled as voucher specimens after being bought at the neighborhood market. Following the instructions given by Ezeabara et al. [28], some modifications were made to the purslane extract production. The plant components (stems and leaves) were air dried for four weeks at room temperature using a sterilized mortar and pestle. The dried purslane leaves and stems were ground into a powder and then submerged in 70% ethanol (1:4). A rotary evaporator (Buchi, US) was used to evaporate the ethanol overnight. The residual ethanol was then allowed to evaporate at ambient temperature. The semi-solid extracts were then lyophilized using a freezing dryer (SP Scientific, US), yielding 58 g. After being lyophilized, the plant extracted powder was kept at -20 °C for later examination.

Morris learning and memory functions test (Water Maze apparatus)

The Morris Water Maze (MWM) test was carried out using a 120 cm diameter, 50 cm height black circular pool filled with 30 cm of water at 26 ± 2 °C, according to Saxena et al. [29]. The pool was split into four quadrants: N, S, E, and W. In the northeast quadrant, a concealed black platform with an 8 cm diameter was positioned 1 cm below the water’s surface. To obscure the platform, 1 kg of starch powder was added to the water. Rats were trained to locate the submerged platform, with a maximum trial time of 180 s. Each rat underwent three trials per day for five consecutive days, followed by a 60-second probe trial on the sixth day. Every trial’s latency time the time needed to locate the platform was noted, and a gradual decrease in latency signified effective learning. Rats were placed on the platform for 20 s prior to the next trial if they were unable to locate it within 180 s. In order to eliminate fragrance cues, the water was agitated in between experiments. Rats were free to swim for 60 s after the platform was removed during the probe trial. The number of crossings over the previous platform site was counted using video replay to evaluate memory recall.

Biochemical assays

Preparation of tissue samples

By the end of the experiment, rats of the different groups were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation 24 h after the last dose, and then the animals were sacrificed by decapitation. Brains were immediately dissected on an ice-cold plate, and the hippocampus was isolated and divided into two parts, one part was stored at -80 °C for biochemical assays, and the other part was used for histological investigation.

Oxidative stress markers

The amount of nitric oxide (NO) was determined by measuring the bright-reddish-purple color that was absorbed at 550 nm based on the generated Azo dye [30]. As previously described by Chen and Tappel [31], the amount of thiobarbituric acid reactive compounds (TBARS) in hippocampus homogenate was determined. The method by Zhu et al. [32], which entailed letting GSH react with 5,5’-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) to form a yellow-colored 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoic acid product, was used to test glutathione (GSH) activity. Also, glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was meaured involved subtracting the excess GSH after an enzymatic reaction from the total GSH in the absence of the enzyme [32]. The method that was used to quantify the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) is according to Marklund [33]. The same technique was used to quantify catalase (CAT) [33].

Detection of inflammatory cytokines, phosphorylated tau and neurotransmitter

Using a quantitative sandwich enzyme immunoassay approach, we assessed hippocampal TNF-𝛼, IL6, IL1β and phosphorylated tau in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. While the method was used to quantify the cholinergic marker acetylcholine esterase (AChE) in the brain homogenate according to Ellman et al. [34], in which a yellow color was observed at 405 nm when AChE reacted with 5, 5-Dithiobisnitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) as a second substrate and acetylcholine thioiodide (ACTI) as a first substrate.

Dopamine and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels were measured with acetonitrile-water (35:65, v/v) as the mobile phase and HPLC detection. Using a modified version of Kang’s approach to derivatize GABA and dopamine [35].

Histological examination

Each group’s brain tissue was extracted, cleansed with ordinary saline, and stored right away in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution. It was then gradually dehydrated in ethyl alcohol (50–99%), clarified in benzene, and embedded in melted paraffin wax at 58–62˚C. Hematoxylin and eosin were used to stain tissue slices that were 5 μm thick for microscopic examination. Light microscopy (Olympus BX43F Tokyo163-0914 Japan), camera software (Olympus DP74 Tokyo 163–0914 Japan), and personal computer were used to examine changes in the hippocampus’s histological structure. The images were analyzed to count the cell number/µm2 using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

The means ± standard error (SEM) of the gathered data were displayed. Using Minitab 12 software, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc analysis (Tukey test) were utilized to compare and statistically evaluate the gathered data. Statistical significance was considered when (p < 0.05) was obtained.

Results

Water Maze apparatus

The Morris water maze’s hidden platform version was used to assess spatial memory over the course of five days (Fig. 1A). On the sixth day, a probe experiment was conducted (Fig. 1B). A significant difference among groups was observed by one-way ANOVA, first day (F(4,35) = 3572.04, P < 0.001), second day (F(4,35) = 608.60, P < 0.001), third day (F(4,35) = 14872.14, P < 0.001), fourth day (F(4,35) = 5343.28, P < 0.001), and fifth day (F(4,35) = 10319.59, P < 0.001). The escape latency of diabetic rats (high-fat diet + STZ) was significantly higher than that of controls (P < 0.001), suggesting poorer spatial learning. Treatment with purslane extract (PEE), metformin (MET), or their combination significantly reduced escape latency relative to diabetic rats (P < 0.001). Control rats demonstrated improvement in performance over successive days, while diabetic rats showed little change between the first and further days. In contrast, PEE, MET, and PEE + MET treated groups exhibited enhancement after four weeks of treatment as shown in (Fig. 1A).

During the probe test, A significant difference among groups was observed by one-way ANOVA (F(4,35) = 10.78, P < 0.001).As diabetic rats crossed the former platform location significantly fewer times than controls (P < 0.001), whereas treatment with PEE, MET, or their combination restored crossing frequency to near-normal levels (P < 0.001) as displayed in (Fig. 1B). All animals displayed normal swimming ability, with no significant differences in locomotor activity among groups.

Effects of PEE and/or MET on hippocampal oxidative status

NO and TBARS levels in the hippocampus, a significant difference among groups was observed by one-way ANOVA in NO and TBARS levels among groups (F(4,35) = 600.79, P < 0.001) and (F(4,35) = 381.98, P < 0.001), respectively. Diabetic rats were significantly higher (p < 0.001) compared with the control group. When PEE, MET, or both were administered to diabetic rats, the hippocampus homogenate’s oxidative stress indicators significantly decreased (p < 0.001) compared to the diabetic group (Fig. 3A and B). Conversely, One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in hippocampal antioxidant parameters (GSH, GPx, SOD, and catalase) among groups (F(4,35) = 88.97, P < 0.001), (F(4,35) = 1322.73, P < 0.001), (F(4,35) = 496.03, P < 0.001), and (F(4,35) = 36.02, P < 0.001), respectively. Diabetic rats displayed a substantial decrease in hippocampus nonenzymatic antioxidant levels (GSH) and enzymatic antioxidant (GPx, SOD, and catalase) activities (p < 0.001) (Table 1). When diabetic rats were administered with PEE, anti-diabetic reference medicines MET, or both, antioxidant indicators were significantly elevated in comparison to the diabetic group (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The levels of inflammation in the hippocampus were evaluated by measuring the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in hippocampus homogenate (Fig. 4A and B, and 4C). Diabetic group showed a marked increase of these cytokines’ levels (p < 0.001) compared with the control group. While diabetic rats treated with PEE, MET, or their combination showed a significant decrease in the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (p < 0.001) compared with the diabetic group.

Histological studies

The microscopic examinations (Figs. 6 and 7) of the hippocampal sections (CA1) revealed that the diabetic group displayed various abnormalities in the hippocampal tissue. These changes included vacuolated neurocytes with edema and degenerative and necrotic modifications of pyramidal cells. Also, in the stratum radiatum (SR) and stratum oriens (SO) layers, blood capillary congestion was noted. Also, decreased thickness of the pyramidal cell layers and areas with neuronal loss was viewed (Fig. 6C) with significant cell count reduction (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7) compared to control group (Fig. 6A and B). Also, A significant difference among groups was observed by one-way ANOVA, (F (4,35) = 83897.95, P < 0.001).

Interestingly, the hippocampus of groups treated with PEE, MET or their combination appeared with a normal structure (Fig. 6D and E, and 6F) in addition to restoration of normal number of cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7) similar to that of the control group to a large extent.

Discussion

This study sheds a light on the neuroprotective effect of PEE alone or in combination with MET. This could provide a fresh start for the creation of purslane extract-based therapies. The relationship between AD and T2DM is complicated; both conditions are associated with oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and inflammatory response. According to the results listed above, the diabetic group showed an increase in escape latency and significant decrease in the number of crossings over as compared with the control group; Our results are in line with a previous study by Hassan et al. [36], who demonstrated that various deficits in cognition associated with diabetes. Interestingly, the diabetic groups treated with PEE, MET, or PEE + MET showed a significant increase in crossings over and a significant decrease in escape latency when compared to the induced group. These results are consistent with earlier research by Parsa et al. [37], who found that purslane extracts improved a number of cognitive functions, such as stress response, anxious behavior, memory consolidation, and spatial learning.

In terms of the oxidative profile, the diabetic group’s hippocampal NO and TBARS levels were significantly higher and the antioxidants significantly lower than those of the control group; these findings are consistent with the findings of Ali et al. [38], who claimed that insulin resistance and ROS production were both triggered by hyperglycemia. Contrary, PEE, MET, and its combination showed a marked inhibition in oxidative stress and increases in the levels of antioxidant status in the hippocampus; These data run with Karimi et al., [39] and Al-Dallee et al. [40], Who attributed these effects to the antioxidant compounds found in purslane such as α-tocopherols, omega-3 fatty acids, ascorbic acid, terpenoids, alkaloids and flavonoids like kaempferol and apigenin derivatives.

Furthermore, our current findings demonstrated that pro-inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) were elevated in HFD + STZ rats. Our findings are consistent with earlier research by Balbaa et al. [41], who indicated that the HFD/STZ rats demonstrated an increased in serum levels of pro-inflammatory markers TNF-α and IL-6 as well as increased concentrations of brain pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α protein levels. It’s interesting to see that pro-inflammatory marker levels were considerably lower with PEE and MET combined therapy than with either medication alone. These results are consistent with earlier research [36], who attributed this decrease to kaempferol that inhibits the anti-inflammatory properties.

In addition, the present results indicated that rats administrated with HFD/STZ showed an increase in phosphorylated tau level in the hippocampus that are considered the most common features of AD disease; These results run with Previous study reported by Feng et al. [42], who showed that oxidative stress is likely to play a critical role in tau hyperphosphorylation, which is regulated by tau protein kinase activation and the suppression of phosphatase. The same author showed that tau hyperphosphorylation may be induced by oxidative stress through the direct interaction with tau protein kinase and phosphatase, particularly GSK3𝛽 and PP2A, respectively, because they are predominant and play an important role. However, PEE therapy both by itself and in combination with MET demonstrated inhibition of the level of phosphorylated tau. This inhibition might be brought about by an effective component in PEE such Omega-3 fatty acids, β-carotene, and α-linolenic acid [43].

Dysregulation of brain processes is reflected in abnormal neurotransmitter levels [44]. Also, Teleanu et al. [45], and Valladolid-Acebes et al. [46], found that alterations in neurotransmitter balance and synaptic activity are associated with dementia and cognitive decline, which are developing outcomes of type 2 diabetes and obesity. According to our data, the diabetic group’s AchE activity increased. Similar results were reported by Pignalosa et al. [15], who suggested that diabetes-induced increases in AchE activity lead to a drop in acetylcholine levels in the synaptic cleft, which reduces the effectiveness of cholinergic neurotransmission and causes progressive cognitive impairment and other neurological dysfunctions. These results may support the current study’s findings. Concerning with the effect of PEE and MET, the present data found modulated levels of dopamine, GABA and AChE. In this line [24, 47], hypothesized that melatonin an effective component in purslane has antioxidant effect which may linked to its capacity to enhance cognitive functioning. Furthermore, the histological results, demonstrated neuronal shrinkage and degradation in the CA1 region, which indicated a severe structural disruption of the hippocampus of the diabetic group, confirmed the previous biochemical parameters and provided strong evidence of neurodegeneration associated with type 2 diabetes. Contrary, our findings demonstrated that the neuroprotective activity and antioxidant qualities of PEE preserved the hippocampus’s structure in a manner that was almost normal. This was demonstrated by the histological findings of the hippocampus of the PEE-treated group either alone or in combination with MET. This work was performed on a rat model, which may limit the direct translation of the findings to humans. Additionally, although key markers of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation were evaluated, other molecular pathways involved in diabetes-induced cognitive dysfunction were not investigated. Therefore, further studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore additional mechanisms.

Conclusion

The findings collectively indicated that PEE, especially when combined with MET, confers neuroprotective effects against HFD/STZ-induced type-2 diabetes and cognitive impairment. The mechanism likely involves the suppression of oxidative stress, modulation of inflammatory cytokines, and inhibition of tau hyperphosphorylation. These outcomes underscore the therapeutic potential of herbal–drug combinations targeting metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders such as T2DM and AD.
