Role transition in comorbidity management: a qualitative study of dementia home care trajectories
Authors: Dongfei Ma, Ting Liang, Xiaoying Zang, Shengze Zhi, Yanjie Zhao, Changxu Jin, Jiao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coexistence of comorbidities is quite common for people living with dementia. Dementia-related symptoms pose challenges for comorbidity management. Nevertheless, our understanding of the comorbidity management process for people living with dementia is rather limited. The aim of this study was to understand the experience of comorbidity management in people living with dementia and to explore the home care trajectories of comorbidity management guided by transition theory.MethodsQualitative methods based on reflexive thematic analysis were employed. A combination of purposive sampling and maximum variation sampling methods was used, and 18 family caregivers and 8 individuals with dementia were recruited. Semistructured interviews were conducted at a memory clinic in Northeast China between November 2022 and March 2023. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsOur findings described the comorbidity management of people living with dementia as a role transition process, which was mapped as a dynamic trajectory comprising four stages with a critical point: ‘I can do it myself’, self-management under family supervision, partial substitution by family members, and complete substitution. To slow the progression of the loss of self-identity, three factors were proposed: consideration of cultural values and beliefs, tailored approaches, and the principle that self-management cannot exist without family support. Among them, Confucian culture and filial piety served as the starting point and context for family caregivers’ involvement in comorbidity management. The last theme explored the specific pattern of ill-timed transition as a type of care response.ConclusionsThe findings of this study provide a new perspective on supportive interventions for comorbidity management in people living with dementia. Future longitudinal studies should be conducted to validate our results. Interventions developed on the basis of our findings will help manage comorbidities, striving to preserve self-identification as much as possible while ensuring safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04209-z.Keywords: Dementia, Comorbidities, Disease management, Caregivers, Qualitative research

Background

Dementia frequently afflicts older adults, and people living with dementia in China account for one quarter of the total dementia population worldwide [1]. Older people living with dementia often have other chronic conditions, with 70% to 90% of this population having at least one comorbidity [2, 3]. In this study, the term “comorbidity” refers to the presence of additional diseases or conditions coexisting with dementia. Chronic diseases such as hyperlipidemia (68.1%), hypertension (39.9%), and diabetes mellitus (19.5%) often coexist in people living with dementia, resulting in a greater burden of comorbidities [4]. People living with dementia and comorbidities have a more complex medical condition and an increased risk of clinical challenges and poor prognosis, and comorbidities are associated with high mortality and more emergency department admissions [5–7]. Moreover, comorbidities can seriously affect the quality of life of people living with dementia and their caregivers, placing a heavy burden on families and health care systems [5].

Simultaneous management of comorbidities for patients with dementia requires sufficient self-management skills and experience [8]. Patients with comorbidities are usually required to take multiple medications and complex medication regimens [9]. Lifestyle management, self-monitoring of symptoms, and frequent access to health care services allow patients to take control of their disease and directly affect its outcomes [10–12]. There are high requirements for patients’ cognitive function, executive function, and judgment and decision-making ability [13]. However, as dementia progresses with progressive functional decline, people living with dementia and comorbidities face additional management problems related to their comorbidities, such as a missed medication dose, an inability to identify symptoms, and an inability to participate in decision-making regarding treatment [14, 15]. This process, referred to as home care trajectories, involves the gradual shift of care responsibilities from the patient to the caregiver. Currently, the dependency progression model is widely applied in dementia care. This model classifies the patients’ degree of dependency based on their performance in activities of daily living, which falls into three categories: independent, independent but with instrumental functional impairment, and dependent. The purpose of this model is to help assess the progression of dementia and determine the patients’ level of dependency as well as the care burden on caregivers. While our study focused on the care trajectory in the comorbidity management of dementia patients and shared certain similarities with the dependency progression model, our trajectory adopted the perspective of familial dyadic interaction, with a focus on comorbidity and dynamic development. The findings are expected to provide unique value for guiding the dynamic adjustment of caregiving roles.

Our research team conducted a scoping review of the literature on managing comorbidities in people living with dementia [16]. The results of the review suggested that current health care systems tend to focus on a single disease and are not friendly to the management of comorbidities, especially dementia and comorbidities [17, 18]. In existing research, an in-depth description of the management of dementia and comorbidities is lacking, as well as studies that explore the dynamic and related conditions of the management of dementia and comorbidities. Therefore, providing feasible guidance for interventions and the management of dementia and comorbidities is difficult.

This study employs the theoretical framework developed by the nursing scholar Afaf Ibrahim Meleis [19]. Widely applied globally in health policy formulation, nursing research, and evidence-based practice, this theory assists healthcare professionals in effectively managing and facilitating health transitions among patients, families, and communities [20]. In the context of dementia, patients transition from independent individuals to care-dependent individuals, while their family members assume the role of caregivers. Concurrently, the expectations of families and society toward patients and caregivers, as well as their ability to manage comorbidities, undergo significant changes [21]. Consequently, individuals with chronic illnesses experience role transitions during the progression of dementia. Guided by transition theory, this study adopts a qualitative research approach to explore the characteristics of transitions related to comorbidity management in dementia patients, including changes, temporal dimensions, and transition triggers, from the perspective of self-management transitions. Transition theory is applicable to the analysis of situations with clear transition trajectories, as it can accurately capture the stage-specific characteristics and key nodes in the transition process. In contrast, theories such as social cognitive theory and the theory of planned behavior are more suitable for explaining the formation and changes of individual behaviors in stable states (e.g., the development of health behaviors, skill acquisition, etc.). The cognitive decline in people living with dementia is a continuous process with dynamic transition characteristics. Therefore, transition theory is applicable to describing and addressing issues related to the dynamic trajectory of comorbidity management in people living with dementia.

In China, the majority of people living with dementia are cared for by family caregivers in their homes [22]. When people living with dementia are unable to manage comorbidities independently, family caregivers become a proxy for comorbidity management [23]. Therefore, from the perspective of people living with dementia and family caregivers, understanding the experience of comorbidity management in people living with dementia, exploring the home care trajectories of comorbidity management, and identifying the mechanism of the dynamic are important. The results of this qualitative study on the basis of participant experience will help to identify potential solutions for comorbidity management in people living with dementia and improve relevant interventions and guidelines.

Methods

Aims

Qualitative methods were used to explore the experience of complex, dynamically changing management of comorbidities in people living with dementia from the perspectives of both the people themselves and family caregivers, guided by transition theory [24]. By incorporating the perspectives of people living with dementia and family caregivers, we aimed to explore the dynamic trajectory and mechanism of comorbidity management.

Design

This study utilized a qualitative approach based on reflexive thematic analysis. Semistructured, in-depth interviews with persons with dementia and family caregivers were conducted in Northeast China. The interviews took place between November 2022 and March 2023.

Participants

During recruitment, purposive sampling was employed to recruit people living with dementia and family caregivers [25]. People who met the following criteria were included: (a) diagnosed with mild or moderate dementia; (b) maintained the ability to communicate at a basic level; (c) diagnosed with dementia and another chronic illness for at least 6 months; and (d) providing informed consent (Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE ≥ 21, provided jointly by the patient and the caregiver; MMSE < 21, provided by the caregiver). Family caregivers who met the following criteria were recruited: (a) were family members who provided health care and daily life care support to people with mild, moderate or severe dementia; (b) had taken care of people living with dementia for at least 6 months; and (c) provided unpaid care. The maximum variation sampling technique [26] was also applied to recruit a heterogeneous sample with the aim of capturing the different experiences of comorbidity management and home health care.

Persons with dementia and their family caregivers visiting memory clinics serving people living with dementia in Northeast China were evaluated by two researchers, and suitable people living with dementia and family caregivers were invited to participate. The memory clinic was designated a Core Advanced Cognitive Center by China’s National Health Commission. It provides cognitive assessment and care services for more than 20 million residents in Northeast China. Patients and family caregivers had the right to choose whether to participate in this study alone or to discuss with their family members together in accordance with their own wishes. The interviews were arranged with the participants who responded to the invitation, and they were assured that participation was voluntary. The participants were then interviewed at the memory clinic on the day of their visit.

In the qualitative approach, the sample size was monitored continuously to identify when saturation occurred [27]. When three consecutive interviews did not produce new codes, we considered the sample size to be sufficient. Code emergence stabilized by interview n = 21. See the appendix for specific criteria to determine data saturation. Furthermore, a table illustrating the process of saturation was added to provide transparent and visual evidence (see the Appendix).

Data collection

All the interviews were conducted by the same researcher (Dongfei Ma) to ensure consistency. The interviewer had received in-depth theoretical training in qualitative interviews. Before the interviews, an experienced qualitative researcher instructed the interviewer and conducted a simulated interview. On the basis of a previous scoping review and transition theory [16], an interview guide for patients and caregivers was developed (see the Appendix). The expressions of the questions slightly differed across the different interviewees. During the interviews, the participants were encouraged to share their experiences and perspectives openly, and they were allowed to answer questions that were not in the order of the interview guide. The interviews were conducted around the research question, giving the participants enough time to think and provide supplementary answers. Before the participants shared their experiences with comorbidity management, the interviewer asked them about their conditions related to daily life and home care with the aim of establishing a trusting relationship with the participants. The interview began by asking the participants about their dementia and comorbidity conditions (e.g., “when were your hypertension and memory problems identified and diagnosed, respectively?”). All the interviews were conducted in a quiet room at the memory clinic and were audio-recorded. The average interview duration was 36 min. Facial expressions, body language, and reflections were recorded in field notes. Within 24 h after the interview, the recordings were transcribed verbatim. Two researchers (Shengze Zhi and Yanjie Zhao) checked the transcripts to ensure accuracy. Related field notes were also attached to the transcripts. The first two interviews were conducted under the supervision of a nursing professor.

The interviewer is a 30-year-old female with over five years of experience in Geriatric nursing and dementia care research. She has extensive expertise in qualitative research and has published two studies related to dementia care. She has received specialized training in qualitative research and interviewing and was raised in a Confucian cultural environment in China. Prior to the interviews, the interviewer had no pre-existing relationship with the participants, thereby avoiding any potential influence arising from prior connections. Trust was established with participants by discussing life after dementia before the formal interviews. During the one-to-two interviews, any sensitive content identified was further explored in a one-on-one conversation with the participant after the session. Throughout the research process the interviewer maintained a reflective journal and engaged in regular discussions with the research team to examine her own influence on the research process. Additionally, the interviewer strived to maintain neutrality during the interviews and encouraged participants to share their authentic experiences through active listening and open-ended questioning.

Data analysis

The study conducted seven dyadic interviews and eleven one-on-one interviews to gather comprehensive data. Data analysis was guided by Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach [28]. Because thematic analysis is very flexible and facilitates distinguishing, identifying and interpreting themes (i.e., significant patterns in the data), we considered it appropriate for use with an interpretive descriptive methodology. The data analysis was guided by two questions: (a) how does comorbidity management change dynamically during the progression of dementia, and (b) what affects comorbidity management during change? This was the lead proposed to guide data analysis on the basis of transition theory. The two researchers (Dongfei Ma and Jiao Sun) responsible for data analysis had been engaged in dementia care research for many years and had received systematic training in qualitative research. Years of research experience in dementia care had enabled the researchers to be well acquainted with core issues such as caregivers’ role conflicts, patients’ functional decline trajectories and comorbidity management. When analyzing data, they could quickly capture key information related to comorbidity management in dementia, making thematic analysis more in line with the actual conditions of dementia. Qualitative research experience and systematic training had helped the researchers adhere to unified coding rules, handle research controversies scientifically, avoid subjective assumptions, and ensure the scientificity of the data analysis process as well as the reliability of the results. However, the researchers’ background might lead them to form fixed cognitive frameworks, which might cause them to overlook the unique details of this study. Therefore, during the analysis, the researchers adopted the strategy of defamiliarized examination, set aside conclusions from previous studies, and re-examined the research theme of this study. Dyadic interviews (involving both patients and caregivers) and individual interviews were coded separately to ensure a detailed and unbiased capture of each participant’s perspective. During the thematic analysis phase, the coded data from both formats were integrated and analyzed comprehensively.

During the data familiarization phase, the researchers read the transcript repeatedly to immerse themselves in the data. The researchers generated an initial list of ideas about what was in the data and what was interesting about the data. When they understood the depth and breadth of the data, succinct, shorthand descriptive, or interpretive labels for pieces of information that might be relevant to the research questions, so-called codes, were produced. The researchers worked systematically through the entire dataset, attending to each data item with equal consideration and identifying aspects of data items that were interesting and might be informative for developing themes. The coders read the text line by line, marking keywords, phrases, or paragraphs relevant to the research question. Based on the marked content, initial codes were formed. During the coding process, the coders recorded preliminary understandings and reflections on the data. After the initial coding was completed, the initial codes were categorized, merging similar or repetitive codes, and each code was provided with a clear definition and examples to ensure consistent application by coders. Once the codebook was established, the coders re-coded the data according to the codebook to ensure consistency and accuracy. When two researchers encountered disagreements during coding, they discussed with the team to reach a consensus. If consensus could not be achieved, the research supervisor arbitrated the decision.

Phase three began when all relevant data items had been coded. The coded data were reviewed and analyzed with respect to how different codes might be combined according to shared meanings so that they might form themes or subthemes. The pattern of codes and data items communicated something meaningful that helped answer the research questions. The researchers assembled the codes into initial candidate themes and debated their meanings.

For the next step, the researchers had to conduct a recursive review of the candidate themes in relation to the coded data and the entire dataset. A series of key questions helped to review potential themes (e.g., was this a theme or just a code). Patton’s dual criterion was applied to observe internal homogeneity within themes and external heterogeneity among themes [29]. The final thematic framework that resulted from the review of the candidate theme was identified. The researchers were then tasked with presenting a detailed analysis of the thematic framework. Each theme provided a coherent and internally consistent account of the data that could not be explained by the other themes. In the final phase, the researchers completed the final inspection of the report. Themes were connected in a logical and meaningful manner, building a cogent narrative of the data. NVivo 12 (NVivo qualitative data analysis software; QSR International Pty Ltd., Version 12, 2018) was used for the data analysis. The interviews were conducted in Mandarin, with transcriptions and analyses completed in Chinese. The final manuscript was translated into English and reviewed by bilingual experts proficient in both languages.

Rigor

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines were followed [30]. The interviewer and researchers who conducted the data analysis were trained and experienced in qualitative research. The interviewer had not established relationships with the participants (both persons with dementia and family caregivers) before the start of the research. During the data collection and analysis, reflexive discussions and meetings were conducted with the aim of detecting and avoiding any potential bias. The team members met frequently for discussion, and disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Jilin University School of Nursing (Approval No. 2022110304) on November 2, 2022. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were approached in the memory clinic and informed of the aim of the research. Consent to participate was obtained, and anonymity and privacy were guaranteed.

Summary of comorbidities

In this paper, the dynamic trajectory of the comorbidity management of people living with dementia, named the ‘Properties: A Gradual Transition in Engagement’, which shows how comorbidity management is transferred from people living with dementia to their family caregivers, was identified through thematic analysis (Fig. 1). Figure 1 depicts the progressive decline in cognitive function among people living with dementia and the corresponding shift in comorbidity management from patients to caregivers. The area under the curve represents tasks performed by patients, showing a gradual decrease over time, while the area above the curve reflects tasks assumed by caregivers, demonstrating an increase. The curve features critical points, marking the transition where caregivers fully take over the management of all comorbidities. The dynamic trajectory of the role transition process consists of four stages: ‘I can do it myself’, self-management under family supervision, partial substitution by family members, and complete substitution. These four stages are progressive, incompletely segmented, and difficult to reverse. Thus, support for the comorbidity management of people living with dementia should aim to maintain the current stage as much as possible rather than reversing the trajectory. This dynamic variability is associated with declining cognitive function and shifts in family support attitudes. Second theme of the findings, titled ‘Conditions: how to slow down on the path of losing self-management’, illustrates how individuals with dementia and their family caregivers can slow the process in which patients lose their self-involvement in comorbidity management. The last theme, titled ‘Patterns of response: ill-timed transition’, described the emergence of inappropriate transition patterns arising from the difficulties in addressing challenges during the transition in comorbidity management. The above results were analyzed and derived under the guidance of transition theory. The Table 3 demonstrates how transition theory aligns with and is supported by research findings.

The connection between transition theory and research findings

Properties: a gradual transition in engagement

In transition theory, Change Triggers are events or circumstances that initiate the process of transition, prompting individuals or systems to move from one state or phase to another. In the comorbidity management of people living with dementia, the transition from normal cognition to cognitive impairment represents a Change Trigger in the health-disease continuum. In transition theory, Engagement refers to the active involvement and participation of individuals or systems in the process of adapting to and managing the changes brought about by a transition. This theme describes the roles within the transition process, where people living with dementia gradually relinquish control and involvement in the management of comorbidity as their cognitive function declines, while family caregivers take over and increase their participation in comorbidity management.

Stage 1: ‘I can do it myself’

The quote ‘I can do it myself’ was used to name stage 1, which describes how people living with dementia have the same ability to self-manage their comorbidities as other aging people with chronic diseases do and can independently perform tasks related to comorbidity management. Some people diagnosed with dementia are considered to still be in stage 1 of comorbidity management. Some people living with dementia manage and take medications well. Several family caregivers thought that the person they cared for could not even undergo a physical examination and visit a doctor on his or her own and had the ability to judge the results of the examination and understand, perceive, and manage his or her lifestyle. Cognitive impairment did not appear to have a substantial effect on these dementia patients’ self-management of comorbidities.

No problem with taking meds—even someone like me can remember and manage my own medications. I’m still handling it myself now. (Even with) memory decline, it hasn’t affected me—no mix-ups with taking the wrong pills or wrong timing, such as taking them twice or three times a day or when to take them during flare-ups. Never made a mistake. Therefore, I’m still managing it on my own. (P09)

Some people living with dementia and their family caregivers actively developed self-management strategies to overcome the decline in memory, language, and other functions, which helped them maintain the stage of ‘I can do it myself’. However, many participants said that people living with dementia were unable to self-manage comorbidities when dementia developed because these patients did not self-manage their disease even if they did not have cognitive problems.

He does not manage (comorbidity) now. My husband’s self-management ability is too poor, unlike others’. His self-management ability is particularly lacking, and he has worked all these years. However, since we’ve been married, he just hasn’t interfered in housework in our home, and now it is… (F16)

Stage 2: Self-management under family supervision

With the decline in cognitive function, some family caregivers gradually participated in the management of comorbidities, the first pattern of which was supervision. The underlying reason was that the family caregiver assessed the patient’s cognitive function and self-management ability and considered that there was a possibility of potential adverse events.

I feel that she is getting worse and worse. I did not replace her, and I just have to keep an eye on her here to judge what she can do. I cannot just do nothing. Forgetting to take the medicine or taking the wrong medicine is not okay. Anyway, I try to let her do things by herself, I watch from the side and let her manage her comorbidity herself

Sometimes I still need to remind her, and occasionally she forgets. When she is busy with other things, if something comes up, or if she misses it, she might forget. However, there’s always someone supervising her. Basically, when she’s at my place, I supervise her. When she goes back home, my mom takes over the supervision. (F12)

Although supervision was regarded as an effective way to support self-management and avoid adverse events, some family caregivers stated that the excessive supervision of people living with dementia could cause a psychological burden and violate patients’ dignity and autonomy. Thus, they emphasized that the level of supervision should be moderate.

Stage 3: Partial substitution by family members

When some tasks could not be performed independently or under supervision, this part of the comorbidity management task was replaced by a family caregiver. The tasks that were first replaced were often more difficult or required higher-level cognitive and executive functions, such as developing comorbidity management schedules, comprehensively understanding the condition of the comorbidity, creating medication lists, purchasing and dispensing medications, accessing health care services, and making important comorbidity management decisions. However, the excessive involvement of some family caregivers and excessive concern about adverse events led to premature deprivation of patients’ participation in comorbidity management.

I came with him, and he didn’t know (treatment of comorbidities and other relevant information). At the beginning, he did not know when we performed the regular review. This matter has never passed through his hands. This matter has always been in my charge. The doctor told me everything; I just told him how to take the medicine. The doctor did not let him smoke, so he did not smoke or drink. [The doctor] let him eat lean meat but not eat fatty meat; he also obeyed. When he was discharged from the hospital, everything was relayed to me. Then, I passed on to him what he should do and what he should not do. (F10)

Stage 4: Complete substitution

Eventually, the comorbidity management of people living with dementia entered stage 4. At this stage, all comorbidity management tasks were carried out completely by family caregivers. Some people living with dementia, due to severe cognitive impairments, were no longer able to plan and execute various comorbidity management tasks, nor could they take care of themselves in daily life. The symptoms related to dementia placed enormous pressures related to care on patients’ families, with the focus of home care shifting from comorbidities to dementia. Some family caregivers said that they were afraid of safety accidents, such as taking medications incorrectly or refusing to take medications, when people living with dementia were performing tasks alone. Therefore, they chose to take over completely.

Critical points

“Critical Points” in transition theory refer to key moments or thresholds where a system undergoes significant and often irreversible changes, leading to a new state or phase. In Stage 4, the existence of such “Critical Points” propels comorbidity management into an entirely new phase. The occurrence of medication errors, refusal to take medications, and a lack of medication awareness, coupled with an impaired ability to remember or correctly perform tasks, served as critical tipping points that propelled the transition of comorbidity management to stage 4, characterized by complete substitution by family caregivers.

If he were normal and could manage his comorbidity by himself, that is, if there were no problems with his cognition, for example, if he wasn’t addled, he could actually manage it on his own. However, as long as his cognitive functions are problematic, for example, if the dosage of a medicine is supposed to be half a tablet per dose, he will take a whole tablet instead. Or, he forgets that he has already taken this medicine, so he takes it again, which would be very dangerous. (F05)

Conditions: how to slow down on the path of losing self-management

In transition theory, “Conditions” refer to the specific circumstances, factors, or environments that influence the process and outcomes of a transition, shaping how individuals or systems adapt and evolve. This theme elucidates the pivotal roles that factors such as cultural sensitivity, tailored approaches, and family support play in influencing the transition process of comorbidity management.

Consideration of cultural values and beliefs

Owing to Confucian culture and filial piety, maintaining the integrity and unity of the family is highly important for people living with dementia and their family caregivers.

Some family caregivers believed that people living with dementia were not independent individuals but part of the family. People living with dementia must try their best to manage their comorbidities by themselves for the sake of their family, including family members and children.

Other family caregivers upheld the values of an independent personality. They considered that they should respect an individual’s independence as well as the patient’s right to know and right to manage his or her own comorbidity independently. They believed that excessive supervision and involvement in comorbidity management would cause people living with dementia to lose their sense of self.

Because you should know that whether it’s a child or an older person, first of all, their personalities are independent. They want to be independent and live their own lives. I’ll step in when I should, and I do not interfere when I should not. I’m just playing an assisting role. In accordance with their wishes, their rights should never be deprived at any time. Therefore, when you ask me about when exactly to take his place, I do not know. I’ll just do my best. (F09)

Tailored approaches

The self-management of comorbidities showed heterogeneity among different people living with dementia and at different times. The cognitive status, comorbidity characteristics, management plans, and personal experiences of people living with dementia shaped the current situation of individuals’ self-management of comorbidities.

He knows about his hypotensive drugs. He remembers taking it every morning when he gets up. However, occasionally, he forgets too. He knows to take it in the morning, and when the time comes and we ask him, ‘Haven’t you taken your medicine yet?’ He knows that. He has been taking it for many years, so he remembers these two kinds of medicine and knows when it is time to take them. It seems that he has already gotten used to it and does not feel too much pressure about it. Well, after all, it has been many years. (F16)

Following the onset of cognitive impairments, some patients developed adaptive strategies to address challenges related to memory decline and impaired executive functioning, with this ability associated with individual characteristics, occupational background, and educational level. Notably, individuals with higher educational attainment demonstrated a greater capacity to adapt proactively to cognitive changes through structured approaches to self-management.

Being a professor, my father is not an illiterate individual or a blue-collar worker. He can independently read and organize all household instruction manuals, meticulously labeling each page to reference its content. If he could not recall specific details, he would further mark the manuals separately for clarity. (F09)

I made a small notebook for her, which was used to record what time she took injections (of insulin) every day and what time she took other medicine. I designed the notebook format and printed it. For example, when she takes her insulin in the morning, she just needs to tick the right box for convenience. I made such a health diary notebook. (F12)

Principle that self-management cannot exist without family support

Whether people living with dementia self-manage comorbidities at the practical level actually depends on their family. After weighing the patient’s cognitive level, the severity of comorbidities, the patient’s self-management ability and the possible adverse consequences that could be caused by the patient’s self-management, family caregivers showed either a positive or a negative attitude toward self-management.

Family caregivers expressed their expectations for people living with dementia to manage their comorbidities and their own lives. They hoped that even if patients were living with dementia, they could still manage themselves independently for a certain period. Family caregivers with a positive attitude supported patients in self-managing their comorbidities by creating a supportive and dementia-friendly environment to exercise, develop and maintain their existing self-management capabilities. Other family caregivers held a negative attitude toward patients’ self-management of comorbidities. Owing to the fear of adverse events such as medication errors, they chose to manage the comorbidities for the patients.

I think that as long as she is still able to take care of herself, she should try her best to complete all the things she can do. I will remind her repeatedly and keep telling her. Most likely, I won’t say things like, ‘You can’t handle this, I’ll do it for you’. So far, I still prefer that she should do the things she can on her own. (F13)

Now I’m facing it alone—my own business, I handle it myself. Who else can you count on? You tough it out. My principle is this: if you can step over the threshold yourself, you do it first. Only when you truly can’t manage anymore should you ask for help. Hmph, you can’t just go around begging for favors left and right—that’s losing face. (P01)

Patterns of response: ill-timed transition

Some family caregivers reported facing challenges during the transition process of comorbidity management, making it difficult to achieve the goals of managing comorbidities with the patients. One caregiver described how the patient’s psychiatric symptoms hindered communication, making it difficult to promote the patient’s self-management of comorbidities.

The key issue lies here—I can’t encourage him. Whenever I try to encourage him, he get angry. As soon as I said something, he would immediately accuse me of being wrong. He flare up at the slightest provocation. Now, just having normal communication is already quite good. There’s not much to say about disease management either. It’s hard to manage because he doesn’t listen to me. (F07)

The negative attitude of people living with dementia toward disease management further hindered the promotion of proactive comorbidity management transitions.

I’ve become indifferent to death. If someone has an incurable disease, then death is inevitable, and it’s fine—it’s a of liberation. I’m being pessimistic, but what difference does it make if I’m positive or not? What can being positive or not achieve? (P16)

Additionally, the healthcare system is not designed for patients living with dementia and comorbidities, making it often difficult for patients with dementia and comorbidities to access healthcare services.

She can’t come for follow-up visits on her own because she has a fear of hospitals. The processes like registration and payment are too complicated. They make her anxious. (F01)

Discussion

This study explored the experiences and processes of people living with dementia and their family caregivers during the management of comorbidities guided by transition theory. The participants described the role transition process in which people living with dementia gradually lost their ability to self-manage their comorbidities. This process included the following four stages: ‘I can do it myself’, self-management under family supervision, partial substitution by family members, and complete substitution. Through thematic analysis, the conditions influencing patients’ ability to maintain their self-management of comorbidities as much as possible were identified. These conditions included cultural values and beliefs, tailored approaches, and the principle that self-management cannot exist without family support. These results reflect how the handover of tasks is accomplished between people living with dementia and family caregivers during the process of comorbidity management. Culture, individual differences, and the family play important roles in maintaining patients’ independence and in assisting in completing comorbidity management.

This study further validates and expands the transition theory and its application contexts. Meleis et al. proposed that transition is a movement from one relatively stable state to another, characterized by change, dynamics, and instability. The four-stage trajectory of comorbidity management identified in this study vividly delineates how role transfer is not an instantaneous event but rather a prolonged, phased, and dynamically evolving process, aligning with the emphasis on its “processual nature.” Transition theory posits that the experience and outcomes of transition are influenced by multiple conditions and factors. Our results confirm and elucidate the unique manifestations of these factors in the specific context of comorbidity management among dementia patients, as well as the resulting response patterns. The findings reflect two types of response patterns: one, a healthy adaptation model where families develop personalized coping strategies supported by cultural beliefs; the other, a difficult adaptation model marked by familial conflict due to stress, or patient ineffective coping and role confusion. Thus, this study strongly validates the core concepts of transition theory and demonstrates its operationalization in the context of dementia comorbidity management. Simultaneously, it extends the application of transition theory to East Asian or similar cultural contexts, enriching the theoretical boundaries of its applicability. Another theoretical contribution of this study is the exploration of role distribution, interaction patterns, collaborative mechanisms, and potential conflicts in specific dyads during transitions. In the cognitive decline transition involving patients with dementia and their family caregivers, the roles of both gradually shift from family members to caregivers and their cared-for. The positive impact of East Asian culture on comorbidity management and the potential conflicts within ‘familial transition dyads’ are key findings of this research.

The results showed that family caregivers gradually replaced the comorbidity management of people living with dementia to mitigate problems related to untimely intervention and oversupport from family support due to uncertainty surrounding the ability assessment. Supporting people living with dementia to live independently in the community and at home is a global public health goal [31]. However, owing to impaired function, it is not possible to live completely independently [32]. Determining how to assess the ability to live and manage comorbidities independently to help family caregivers provide comorbidity management support for people living with dementia in a timely and appropriate manner is an urgent problem that needs to be solved. This study proposes a four-stage trajectory of comorbidity management in people living with dementia, which in part reveals the extent to which family caregivers provide support to people living with dementia and, in particular, clarifies the distinction between supervision and substitution. Existing interventions for people living with dementia do not consider the different stages of functional change in dementia, and it is necessary to construct realistic comorbidity management interventions for the families of people living with dementia according to different stages to promote the balance between independence and safety. In the ‘I can do it myself’ stage, training focuses on educating caregivers about early comorbidity symptoms, promoting patient self-monitoring, and encouraging adherence to treatment plans. During self-management under family supervision stage, modules emphasize teaching caregivers how to assist patients in tracking symptoms, managing medications, and coordinating care while maintaining patient autonomy. For the partial substitution by family members stage, training provides caregivers with skills to actively manage comorbidities, such as administering medications, monitoring vital signs, and recognizing warning signs of complications. In the complete substitution stage, modules focus on advanced caregiving techniques, including managing complex medical regimens, navigating healthcare systems, and ensuring patient comfort and safety while addressing caregiver stress and burnout. These interventions aim to support caregivers in effectively managing comorbidities across the dementia care continuum. Role transition is pivotal for adapting to different stages of role expectations. This study highlights the potential for oversubstitution during caregiver role transitions. A qualitative study involving interviews with 15 spousal caregivers revealed the challenges that caregivers face in adapting to role transitions [33]. Research across diverse cultural contexts has consistently identified the difficulties associated with role transitions in dementia care. Consequently, appropriate interventions to improve role transitions are deemed necessary.

Current findings indicate that the role of people living with dementia in decision-making regarding comorbidity management often exhibits an “all-or-nothing” pattern. Specifically, people with dementia tend to fully dominate disease-related decisions when their cognitive functions remain relatively intact. However, once family caregivers perceive risks in independent decision-making, patients’ participation in decision-making immediately terminates. Current research identifies three primary forms of decision-making participation for people with dementia: advanced care planning, shared decision-making, and surrogate decision-making [34]. Among these, shared decision-making has unique advantages in promoting the involvement of patients, family caregivers, and clinicians in the decision-making process, thereby increasing patient autonomy and decision satisfaction [35]. Consequently, this approach has become the most widely adopted form of decision-making. The research results revealed certain deficiencies in the participation of people living with dementia in decision-making in China. To address these insufficiencies active promotion of shared decision-making and other participatory forms through educational and public awareness activities is recommended, thereby safeguarding the autonomy rights of people living with dementia.

Family caregivers’ perceptions of autonomy and independence in people living with dementia are influenced by culture and values. Unlike the independence of people with normal cognition, the independence of people living with dementia is considered to involve active participation in and sharing of their own comorbidity management, rather than the assumption of all management tasks. Under Confucian culture, the independence of people living with dementia is associated with family and kinship, that is, actively participating in the management of comorbidities for the sake of their children and loved ones. This finding is similar to the results of multiple studies conducted by our team [16, 36, 37]. Confucian culture shapes how family caregivers decide to use physical restraints for people living with dementia, care for their loved ones, and adapt to their roles. In addition, some family caregivers believe that the dignity and autonomy of patients should be respected; that is, whether to provide support for comorbidity management should be determined according to patients’ wishes. This finding reflects the different impacts of diverse values on dementia care. Western culture, which is centered on individualism, emphasizes the independence and self-determination of individuals. For people living with dementia, Western care philosophies place greater emphasis on preserving patients’ autonomy, encouraging them to complete daily activities as independently as possible and reducing their reliance on others. Care plans are typically designed to focus on the needs and wishes of the patient rather than being driven by the expectations of family or relatives [38, 39]. In other countries and cultures, the views of people living with dementia and their caregivers regarding independence and comorbidity management remain unclear [40]. The impact of the diversity and collision of world cultures on the attitudes toward and views of the participation of people living with dementia in comorbidity management has also not been discussed.

The cultural norms shaped by filial piety, which emphasize family responsibility, provide a robust support system for patients living with dementia managing comorbidities [41]. The active involvement of family members can enhance patients’ adherence to treatment plans and offer both emotional and practical support, which are crucial for effective comorbidity management [42]. However, while filial piety positively contributes to comorbidity management, it may also lead to over-protection or paternalistic attitudes within families. Family members might prioritize their own perceptions of the patient’s needs over the patient’s autonomy, thereby limiting the patient’s active participation in their own care. Additionally, the emphasis on family harmony and the avoidance of conflict may hinder open discussions about challenges of managing comorbidities, potentially obstructing the development of tailored and effective care strategies. Therefore, collaboration between healthcare providers and patients’ families to encourage shared decision-making, while respecting the patient’s autonomy, represents an effective approach that acknowledges the strengths of Confucian values while addressing their potential limitations.

The findings of this study reveal that in Confucian cultures, dementia care is deeply rooted in family-centric values, where caregiving roles often shift dynamically between patients and family members, reflecting a collective responsibility and filial piety [43]. In contrast, Western dementia care models prioritize individualized, patient-centered approaches, emphasizing professional support and multidisciplinary collaboration. While Confucian caregiving highlights harmony and moral obligations, Western practices focus on patient autonomy and the integration of community resources [44]. For example, Chinese families often find it difficult to accept the idea of sending their loved ones to live in nursing homes, even though such facilities may provide more professional medical care. Instead, caring for a family member with dementia at home, despite the significant financial and emotional burdens it entails, is seen as a way to uphold the family’s reputation and harmony [45]. In contrast, sending individuals with dementia to nursing homes or professional care facilities is quite common in Western countries, as family members generally believe that such institutions can provide higher-quality care. They do not view placing a loved one in a nursing home as a moral failure [46]. This cultural divergence underscores the need for culturally sensitive care strategies that bridge family-centered traditions with professionalized support systems.

For people living with dementia and their caregivers, fixed and unchanging interventions are not satisfactory [47]. The results of our study indicated that personal identities, educational experiences, personality traits, and comorbidity conditions and experiences of patients with dementia and comorbidities shaped their patterns and habits of managing their comorbidities, whereas cognitive function determined whether they could maintain consistent management capabilities. Consistent with the findings of other studies, the self-management ability of people living with dementia as found to be affected by many factors. Even among people with the same degree of dementia severity, the ability to self-manage comorbidities also varies [48–50]. Therefore, it is necessary to implement personalized interventions to support the comorbidity management of people living with dementia. In particular, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive assessment of patients’ previous management situations, identify the stage-specific characteristics of current comorbidity management, and incorporate patients’ cognitive functions to design implementable support plans for these patients.

Implications for future research and practice

First, the customization of targeted plans for the families of people living with dementia at different stages of comorbidity management is worth considering. Most current dementia intervention programs lack provisions for comorbidity management for people living with dementia. Future research is needed to develop fully feasible and personalized family-centered interventions for people living with dementia at different stages of comorbidity management on the basis of the results of this study. In Stage 1, the promotion of knowledge and behavioral changes related to dementia and comorbidity management facilitates patients’ engagement in self-management, requiring the development of health education materials. In Stage 2, nurse-led coaching modules can enhance adherence while preserving autonomy. In Stage 3, professional dementia care guidance can provide comorbidity management support. In Stage 4, the involvement of dementia care specialists offers respite for family caregivers. Stages 1 and 2 can rely primarily on nurses’ guidance and education, while Stages 3 and 4 necessitate the intervention of professional caregivers to assist with care delivery. Second, home care practices should consider balancing the autonomy and safety of patients. The comorbidity management tasks performed independently by people living with dementia and those taken over by family members should cover all aspects without any overlap. Family caregivers can supervise some of the tasks that people living with dementia perform appropriately. In addition, it is necessary to conduct in-depth discussions on the attitudes and perceptions of people living with dementia and their families regarding the self, independence, and dignity under multiculturalism and diverse values. The development of comorbidity management interventions for people living with dementia in different cultures may be a potential approach for improving the outcomes of comorbidity management and enhancing their participation. Finally, health care support for patients with comorbidities should be enhanced. When the symptoms of dementia in patients gradually worsen, their comorbidities are often overlooked. Therefore, education on dementia comorbidity management should be provided to health care providers and families to increase their attention to comorbidities. In light of the developmental trajectory of comorbidity management, healthcare professionals assess patients’ self-management capabilities by evaluating their cognitive function, memory, and other relevant factors. This assessment aims to determine the tasks that patients can currently perform independently, those that may be prone to errors even when attempted independently, and those that cannot be executed even with family supervision. On the basis of these evaluations, targeted training is provided to family caregivers, who offer practical caregiving skills and support. Considering the educational level of caregivers, it is essential to provide visually and symbolically oriented tools, verbal and audio aids, community and caregiver engagement, as well as simplified and interactive formats to promote equity in dementia care for those with lower educational attainment.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study. The sample size of the study is limited and may not be able to reflect all comorbidity situations. The data saturation determined by the research team ensured the study was adequate in terms of depth rather than breadth. This study was conducted in one memory clinic only. In addition, the study did not longitudinally track changes in the comorbidity management of people living with dementia. Instead, data were collected on the basis of the recollections of patients and family caregivers; thus, recall bias was likely to exist. Future studies could adopt a longitudinal research approach, collecting data at multiple time points to reduce reliance on participants’ memory of past events. However, given the prolonged progression of dementia, it is necessary to consider extending the study period. Integrating multiple data sources, such as medical records and caregiver diaries, can help validate participants’ accounts and reduce recall bias. In this study, the inclusion of both patients and caregivers has, to some extent, created a mutual validation effect. Additionally, encouraging participants to document their experiences in real-time can also minimize reliance on retrospective recall. Finally, the fact that the interviewer had no prior relationship with the participants, coupled with the dual involvement of patients and caregivers in some interviews, may have led interviewees to conceal certain negative aspects of comorbidity management. Establishing a rapport with patients and their caregivers by discussing the current state of dementia care prior to the interviews could help mitigate the potential adverse effects of these factors. Some interviews were conducted one-on-one, and in cases where joint interviews were deemed to potentially influence responses, the interviewer would follow up with one participant individually after the session. These measures helped reduce the impact of social desirability and encouraged more candid expressions. Our interviews were framed in a neutral and non-judgmental manner, which also facilitated participants’ comfort in discussing their personal experiences. Future research could supplement interviews with anonymous surveys to encourage participants to share more honest and less socially desirable responses.

Conclusion

The comorbidity management of people living with dementia involves dynamic transfers along with alterations in cognitive function. From self-managing comorbidities to being supervised and substituted by their families, people living with dementia eventually lose all of their ability to participate in their comorbidity management. Maintaining self-identity and promoting patients’ active participation in comorbidity management depend on the development of interventions that are suitable for the older participation of family caregivers for different individuals on the basis of culture and values. Future research should verify and further explore the dynamic changes in the comorbidity management of people living with dementia through longitudinal studies. Multicultural, multistage support programs must be developed for comorbidity management of dementia and to provide health care support in terms of comorbidities to the families of people living with dementia. These programs are designed to be inclusive, adaptable, and responsive to the evolving needs of individuals managing comorbidities. Furthermore, striking a delicate balance between respecting patient autonomy and ensuring safety remains a pivotal consideration in refining practice, emphasizing the need avoid prioritizing one at the expense of the other.
