Clinical characteristics of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and its association with significant liver fibrosis in hospitalized patients with chronic hepatitis B: a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Chun-Xia Wang, Jun Kang, Jia-Wei Zhang, Xiao-Yan Yuan, Dong Wang, Mao-Quan Li, Da-Feng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoexistence of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is becoming more common in clinical settings, but the relationship between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis in CHB remains uncertain.AimTo delineate the clinical profile of hospitalized CHB patients with concomitant MASLD and investigate the relationship of MASLD with significant liver fibrosis.MethodsA total of 776 hospitalized patients with CHB were retrospectively evaluated in this single-center cross-sectional analysis at the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu between December 2020 and December 2024. Using the current diagnostic criteria for MASLD, participants were assigned to either the CHB-only group or the CHB+MASLD group. Demographic data, laboratory findings, virological profiles, and FibroScan-derived liver stiffness measurements were collected. Between-group differences in clinical profiles were examined, followed by an evaluation of whether MASLD was associated with significant liver fibrosis.ResultsThe analysis comprised 776 patients with CHB, including 203 (26.2%) with concomitant MASLD. Patients in the CHB+MASLD group tended to be younger and had a shorter duration of hospitalization than those in the CHB-only group. They also had higher albumin and platelet levels but lower total bilirubin, AST, and prothrombin time values. Patients with CHB+MASLD were less likely to receive antiviral treatment. Multivariable modeling provided no evidence that MASLD was independently related to significant liver fibrosis (OR = 1.473, 95% CI: 0.970–2.236; P = 0.069). Restricting the analysis to patients whose ALT and AST values did not exceed 5 × the upper limit of normal produced an effect estimate in the same direction, but statistical significance was still not achieved (OR = 1.374, 95% CI: 0.860–2.195; P = 0.183). Older age, male sex, and elevated AST levels were consistently related to significant liver fibrosis in both the primary and sensitivity analyses. HBV DNA showed a significant association only in the primary model. The interaction between MASLD and antiviral treatment status was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.232).ConclusionConcomitant MASLD was common among hospitalized patients with CHB. In the adjusted model, MASLD did not show a clear independent relationship with significant liver fibrosis. In contrast, older age, male sex, and higher AST levels were linked to greater odds of significant liver fibrosis. Because all participants were inpatients, these findings should not be directly extrapolated to the broader CHB population. Further multicenter studies enrolling patients across inpatient, outpatient, and community settings are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis B, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Liver fibrosis, Transient elastography, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a major public health challenge worldwide. An estimated 300 million individuals globally are living with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [1]. Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate that chronic HBV infection contributed to nearly 1 million deaths in 2022, with most of these deaths attributable to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. China bears a particularly large burden of CHB, with approximately 80 million individuals estimated to be infected with HBV [3]. During the course of CHB, liver fibrosis is a major pathological consequence that accompanies disease advancement. More advanced fibrosis is linked to an increased probability of future cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Evidence from previous studies indicates that, without effective intervention, individuals with significant liver fibrosis (≥ F2) have a 10%–30% probability of developing cirrhosis within five years [4]. Accordingly, identifying predictors of clinically significant liver fibrosis at an early stage in CHB patients is important for risk evaluation and prompt clinical intervention.

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents a major form of chronic liver disease worldwide and is closely related to obesity, insulin resistance, and other metabolic disorders [5]. Globally, MASLD is estimated to occur in approximately 25%–30% of adults, whereas population-based studies in China have reported prevalence estimates ranging from 29% to 46% [6]. With the rising burden of MASLD, concurrent CHB and MASLD have become increasingly common, especially in individuals with concomitant metabolic syndrome [7]. Current evidence indicates that concurrent CHB and MASLD may reflect intertwined pathophysiological mechanisms rather than merely the coexistence of two separate liver diseases. In MASLD, insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, and persistent low-grade inflammatory activity can exacerbate hepatic injury; conversely, CHB-related immune dysregulation and hepatocellular damage may further disturb lipid metabolism. Through these reciprocal effects, the coexistence of the two conditions may contribute to progression toward hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis and may subsequently increase the likelihood of hepatocellular carcinoma [8].

Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether MASLD is associated with significant liver fibrosis in patients with CHB. Previous evidence suggests that MASLD may facilitate fibrotic progression in CHB through mechanisms involving inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and fibrosis-related signaling pathways [9–12]. In the study by Diao et al. [13] concomitant hepatic steatosis was identified as an independent factor associated with significant liver fibrosis among previously untreated patients with CHB and normal alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (OR = 2.559, 95% CI: 1.212–5.403). However, findings from previous studies have remained inconsistent. In certain CHB subgroups, metabolic abnormalities were not clearly related to the progression of hepatic fibrosis, whereas other investigations indicated that MASLD might result in underestimated liver stiffness measurement values [9]. This heterogeneity may partly reflect differences in assessment methods and study designs. Hepatic steatosis may alter the biomechanical properties of liver tissue and interfere with shear wave transmission, which can in turn affect elastography-derived liver stiffness measurements and lead to estimates that do not fully reflect the underlying degree of hepatic fibrosis [14].

According to the 2023 international multi-society consensus, the nomenclature of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was revised to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). This updated terminology places greater emphasis on metabolic abnormality-based diagnosis and provides a more appropriate classification framework for CHB patients with concomitant hepatic steatosis [15]. This nomenclatural update may reduce heterogeneity arising from inconsistent definitions across previous studies and facilitate more detailed analyses based on metabolic abnormalities. Transient elastography (TE; FibroScan) is a non-invasive, reproducible technique that is routinely used to assess liver fibrosis. Its performance for fibrosis assessment has been investigated extensively in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB). A meta-analysis pooling data from 28 studies and 4,540 untreated CHB patients yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.84 for significant liver fibrosis [16], suggesting potential value of TE in fibrosis stratification among CHB patients. However, studies on CHB complicated by MASLD based on the new diagnostic criteria remain limited, especially among hospitalized patients in western China, and relevant data are still insufficient. Existing studies have mostly adopted the previous definition of NAFLD and have mainly focused on outpatient or community-based populations, with relatively limited attention paid to hospitalized patients with CHB, who may represent a more severe clinical subgroup. Accordingly, whether MASLD is associated with significant liver fibrosis warrants specific evaluation in hospitalized patients with CHB under the current MASLD diagnostic criteria.

Hospitalized patients with CHB receiving care at the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu constituted the study cohort. Liver fibrosis was evaluated with FibroScan. Classification into the CHB-only and CHB+MASLD groups followed the current MASLD diagnostic criteria. Differences in clinical characteristics across the groups were examined, followed by an evaluation of MASLD in relation to significant liver fibrosis. These findings were intended to support risk assessment and clinical decision-making among patients from western China with CHB and concomitant MASLD.

Patients and methods

Study population

Medical records from 8,675 hospitalized patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) who received care at the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu between December 2020 and December 2024 were screened in this single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis. Application of the predefined eligibility criteria yielded 776 patients for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Ethical approval for this retrospective study was provided by the Ethics Committee of the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu (approval No. YJ-K2025-87-01), and all study procedures were consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. The institutional review board waived the requirement for written informed consent because of the retrospective study design.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for CHB specified in the Guidelines for the prevention and treatment of chronic hepatitis B (version 2022); and complete liver transient elastography (FibroScan) measurements together with the required laboratory data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: incomplete demographic or clinical information; presence of malignant tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma; chronic liver diseases other than CHB, such as alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, hepatitis C, hepatitis D, or suspected drug-induced liver disease; human immunodeficiency virus co-infection; and excessive alcohol consumption, defined as an alcohol intake of > 30 g/day for men or > 20 g/day for women.

Diagnostic criteria

CHB was diagnosed in accordance with the Guidelines for the prevention and treatment of chronic hepatitis B (version 2022) [17]. Patients were considered to have CHB when hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) remained positive or hepatitis B virus deoxyribonucleic acid (HBV DNA) remained detectable for more than 6 months.

MASLD was identified according to the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated (Non-Alcoholic) Fatty Liver Disease (version 2024) [18]. After excluding excessive alcohol intake and other recognized etiologies of hepatic steatosis, MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis detected by liver ultrasonography during the current hospitalization, or reconfirmation of a previous imaging-based diagnosis of fatty liver during the same hospitalization, along with at least one metabolic abnormality criterion. Metabolic abnormalities were identified according to the following thresholds: (1) overweight or obesity, indicated by BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m² or waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 85 cm in women; (2) hypertension, indicated by blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or ongoing antihypertensive treatment; (3) abnormal glucose metabolism, identified by fasting plasma glucose ≥ 6.1 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7%, a previous diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus, or homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) ≥ 2.5; and (4) dyslipidemia, identified by fasting triglyceride ≥ 1.70 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤ 1.0 mmol/L in men or ≤ 1.3 mmol/L in women, or current lipid-lowering treatment.

Group criteria

Patients with CHB were categorized into the CHB-only group and the CHB+MASLD group in accordance with the MASLD diagnostic criteria.

Data collection

Data for the 776 hospitalized patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) who received care at the Public Health Clinical Center of Chengdu from December 2020 to December 2024 were retrieved from the inpatient electronic medical record system. The dataset covered clinical characteristics, laboratory tests, virological measurements, and imaging data. Demographic and clinical information comprised age, sex, length of hospital stay, time since CHB diagnosis, antiviral treatment status, and comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Laboratory measurements comprised hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (Tbil), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), urea, creatinine (Cr), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and prothrombin time (PT). HBV DNA was recorded as the virological variable, whereas liver fibrosis stage was derived from FibroScan results. For laboratory, virological, and imaging measurements, the earliest available value within 7 days of admission was selected. Before analysis, records retrieved from the inpatient electronic medical record system underwent repeated checks for completeness, accuracy, and internal consistency, and the analytic dataset was finalized after these checks were completed.

Liver fibrosis assessment and staging criteria

Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) was performed using the FibroScan® 502 system (Echosens, Paris, France). Examinations were performed either in the morning in a fasting state or after fasting for at least 2 h. Participants were placed in the supine position with the right arm fully abducted, and measurements were obtained from the right hepatic lobe through a right intercostal space. All examinations were independently performed by qualified healthcare professionals who had completed formal Echosens training, obtained the relevant certifications, and had several years of practical experience. Each examination was based on no fewer than 10 valid measurements, with their median serving as the liver stiffness measurement (LSM), expressed in kilopascals (kPa). Technical reliability was defined by a success rate of ≥ 60% together with an interquartile range-to-median ratio (IQR/median) of ≤ 0.30. Fibrosis stages were categorized according to the instrument manual and thresholds reported in previous Chinese studies [19, 20]: F0–F1, < 7.3 kPa; F2, 7.3–9.7 kPa; F2–F3, 9.8–12.4 kPa; F3–F4, 12.5–17.5 kPa; and F4, > 17.5 kPa. For the binary outcome used in this study, F2–F4 was classified as significant liver fibrosis. A liver biopsy-based study reported that LSM showed good diagnostic performance for distinguishing Scheuer stage S ≥ 2 and S ≥ 3, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.818 and 0.887, respectively [21]. In addition, a recent study of patients with concomitant CHB and NAFLD also adopted an LSM threshold of ≥ 7.3 kPa to define significant liver fibrosis [22]. Because all enrolled patients in the present study had CHB and MASLD was analyzed as a concomitant metabolic condition within the CHB population, the same FibroScan-based liver stiffness criteria were applied to both the CHB-only and CHB+MASLD groups to ensure consistency of the outcome definition and comparability between groups.

Statistical analysis

R software version 4.5.1 was used for all statistical analyses. Continuous variables were examined for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since none satisfied the normality assumption, these variables were reported as median (P25, P75). Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test, as applicable. Categorical variables were reported as n (%) and evaluated with Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the characteristics of the data. For regression analyses, liver fibrosis was dichotomized as F0–F1 (without significant liver fibrosis) and F2–F4 (significant liver fibrosis), with significant liver fibrosis serving as the outcome. Univariable logistic regression models were fitted separately for individual clinical variables, and the corresponding effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Subsequently, based on previous literature and clinical relevance [23, 24], MASLD, age, sex, HBV DNA, AST, time since CHB diagnosis, and antiviral treatment status were prespecified for entry into the primary multivariable logistic regression model to determine whether MASLD was independently associated with significant liver fibrosis. Potential multicollinearity among the selected variables was assessed before model estimation using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Because marked hepatic inflammatory activity may influence FibroScan-based liver stiffness measurements, a sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding patients whose ALT or AST levels were greater than five times the upper limit of normal. The multivariable logistic regression model was then refitted with the same covariates used in the primary model. Possible differences in the association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis according to antiviral treatment status were also evaluated. For this purpose, separate analyses were conducted among patients who had and had not received antiviral treatment. The model included MASLD status, antiviral treatment status, age, sex, HBV DNA level, AST level, time since CHB diagnosis, and the interaction term between MASLD status and antiviral treatment status. ORs for the association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis were estimated separately in treated and untreated patients, and a likelihood-ratio test was used to assess whether the association differed between the two groups. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and group comparisons of the study population

After the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, 776 hospitalized patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these patients, 203 (26.2%) had concomitant metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), whereas 573 (73.8%) were categorized as CHB-only. Compared with the CHB-only group, patients with CHB+MASLD were younger and had shorter hospital stays. Their liver-related laboratory profile also differed, with higher platelet and albumin levels (P < 0.001) and lower total bilirubin, AST, and prothrombin time values (P < 0.001). The CHB+MASLD group had higher triglyceride concentrations than the CHB-only group [1.55 (1.16–2.26) mmol/L vs. 1.10 (0.80–1.50) mmol/L; P < 0.001]. In contrast, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations were lower in the CHB+MASLD group [0.95 (0.79–1.16) mmol/L vs. 1.07 (0.78–1.36) mmol/L; P < 0.001]. Antiviral treatment was less frequent among patients with CHB+MASLD than among those in the CHB-only group. No between-group differences were found for sex distribution, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ALT, GGT, or HBV DNA load. The pattern of liver fibrosis stages differed between the groups (P = 0.003); specifically, F4 fibrosis was present in 17.2% of the CHB+MASLD group and 29.5% of the CHB-only group (Table 1).

Exploratory stratified analysis according to the number of metabolic abnormalities in patients with CHB+MASLD

To further explore whether metabolic burden was associated with differences in clinical characteristics and fibrosis severity among patients with CHB+MASLD, an exploratory stratified analysis was performed according to the number of metabolic abnormalities. Patients were divided into three groups: one metabolic abnormality group (n = 162), group with two metabolic abnormalities (n = 32), and group with three or more metabolic abnormalities (n = 9). Significant differences across the three groups were found for age, male proportion, ALB, creatinine, and TG levels. In the groups with one, two, and three or more metabolic abnormalities, the median ages were 36.00 (31.00–48.00), 53.50 (46.75–58.75), and 48.00 (45.00–53.00) years, respectively (P < 0.001), and the proportions of men were 77.2%, 59.4%, and 55.6%, respectively (P = 0.045). ALB levels were 41.20 (38.05–44.38), 39.05 (36.40–42.92), and 43.90 (43.60–47.10) g/L, respectively (P = 0.049); creatinine levels were 67.00 (57.00–74.75), 57.50 (48.75–70.50), and 56.00 (53.00–59.00) µmol/L, respectively (P = 0.029); and TG levels were 1.52 (1.14–2.06), 1.66 (1.25–2.28), and 2.68 (2.44–3.27) mmol/L, respectively (P = 0.015). Significant liver fibrosis was present in 76.5%, 90.6%, and 88.9% of patients with one, two, and three or more metabolic abnormalities, respectively. Patients with at least two metabolic abnormalities had numerically greater proportions of significant liver fibrosis than those with only one abnormality, although the three-group comparison yielded no statistical significance (P = 0.153). Comparable Hb, PLT, AST, and HDL-C values were observed among the subgroups. Neither ALT nor HBV DNA differed significantly across groups (P = 0.057 and 0.139, respectively) (Table 2).

Comparison of clinical characteristics between patients with and without significant liver fibrosis

Fibrosis staging identified 169 patients as F0–F1 (without significant liver fibrosis) and 607 as F2–F4 (significant liver fibrosis). The F2–F4 subgroup showed a higher median age, a greater male proportion, and a longer duration of hospitalization than the F0–F1 subgroup, with all comparisons reaching statistical significance. Laboratory findings showed lower levels of Hb, PLT, ALB, and HDL-C in the significant liver fibrosis subgroup, whereas Tbil, ALT, AST, GGT, PT, HBV DNA, and TG levels were higher; all comparisons were statistically significant. Time since CHB diagnosis was comparable between the two subgroups. The proportion of patients receiving antiviral treatment also did not differ significantly between the two subgroups. The prevalence of MASLD did not differ significantly between the two subgroups; however, the distribution of the number of metabolic abnormalities differed significantly (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Analysis of factors correlated with significant liver fibrosis in chb patients

Significant liver fibrosis, defined as F2–F4, was used as the outcome variable in the logistic regression analyses. Univariable analysis showed that age, length of hospital stay, male sex, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and several hematological, hepatic function, coagulation, and virological indicators were associated with significant liver fibrosis (Table 4). MASLD was not significantly associated with significant liver fibrosis in the univariable model (OR = 1.092, 95% CI: 0.737–1.617, P = 0.662). Covariates for the primary multivariable model were selected a priori according to clinical relevance. After adjustment for age, sex, HBV DNA level, AST level, time since CHB diagnosis, and antiviral treatment status, the estimated OR for MASLD was 1.473 (95% CI: 0.970–2.236; P = 0.069), indicating that the independent relationship remained statistically non-significant. In the multivariable model, higher odds of significant liver fibrosis were observed with older age (OR = 1.033, 95% CI: 1.017–1.049; P < 0.001), male sex (OR = 1.987, 95% CI: 1.359–2.907; P < 0.001), higher HBV DNA level (OR = 1.116, 95% CI: 1.013–1.229; P = 0.026), and higher AST level (OR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.002–1.005; P < 0.001). In contrast, neither time since CHB diagnosis nor antiviral treatment status was significantly related to significant liver fibrosis in the primary model (Table 5). To limit the possible effect of marked hepatic inflammatory activity on FibroScan measurements, patients whose ALT or AST levels were greater than five times the upper limit of normal were excluded in the sensitivity analysis. The sensitivity analysis showed a similar pattern, with an OR of 1.374 for MASLD (95% CI: 0.860–2.195; P = 0.183). Older age (OR = 1.040, 95% CI: 1.020–1.060, P < 0.001), male sex (OR = 1.776, 95% CI: 1.130–2.793, P = 0.013), and higher AST level (OR = 1.011, 95% CI: 1.002–1.021, P = 0.014) remained independently associated with significant liver fibrosis, whereas HBV DNA level, time since CHB diagnosis, and antiviral treatment status were not statistically significant (Table 6; Fig. 2). In the analysis stratified by antiviral treatment status, the OR for MASLD was 3.192 among patients not receiving antiviral treatment (95% CI: 0.822–12.384, P = 0.093) and 1.360 among those receiving antiviral treatment (95% CI: 0.881–2.098, P = 0.165); neither association reached statistical significance. The interaction between MASLD and antiviral treatment status was also not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.232) (Table 7).

Discussion

A total of 776 inpatients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) were retrospectively reviewed in a cross-sectional investigation conducted at a single center. The analysis focused on characterizing the clinical profile of patients with concomitant metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and examining the association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis. MASLD was present in 26.2% of the hospitalized CHB cohort. Relative to the CHB-only group, the CHB+MASLD group was younger, had a shorter length of hospitalization, and showed better-preserved hepatic functional reserve and a more favorable disease-severity profile, as indicated by higher ALB and PLT levels and lower Tbil, AST, and PT values. In addition, the CHB+MASLD group had a lower proportion of patients receiving antiviral treatment than the CHB-only group. The overall distribution of liver fibrosis stages differed significantly between the two groups. However, with F2–F4 categorized as significant liver fibrosis, univariable logistic regression provided no evidence of an association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis. In the multivariable model incorporating age, sex, HBV DNA, AST, time since CHB diagnosis, and antiviral treatment status, the OR for MASLD rose to 1.473; After excluding patients with ALT or AST values > 5 × the upper limit of normal, the estimates were generally consistent with those obtained from the main model. Neither stratified analyses nor interaction testing according to antiviral treatment status suggested that antiviral treatment status modified the association of MASLD with significant liver fibrosis. Overall, MASLD did not emerge as an independent correlate of significant liver fibrosis in hospitalized CHB patients. Conversely, greater age, male sex, and higher AST were consistently linked to significant liver fibrosis across both the main and sensitivity models, while the association involving HBV DNA was observed only in the main model.

The coexistence of CHB and MASLD has become increasingly common. Worldwide, approximately 300 million individuals are affected by CHB, while the prevalence of MASLD among adults is estimated at 25%–30% and is projected to rise to 55% by 2040 [1, 6, 25]. The prevalence of MASLD in this cohort was 26.2%, comparable to the 25%–30% reported in previous studies. However, all participants in the present study were hospitalized patients with CHB, whose clinical characteristics may differ from those of outpatient or community-based CHB populations. Hospitalized patients with CHB may have more severe liver disease, higher inflammatory activity, poorer liver functional reserve, more comorbidities, and more complex treatment histories. Therefore, the prevalence of MASLD, the spectrum of liver fibrosis severity, and the association of MASLD with significant liver fibrosis observed here should be considered specific to an inpatient CHB cohort rather than generalized directly to the broader CHB population. In the present study, hospitalized patients with concomitant MASLD were relatively younger. This may be related to the earlier occurrence of obesity, dyslipidemia, and other metabolic disorders in younger populations in recent years. A nationwide survey showed that the associations of obesity with hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were more pronounced among younger adults [26]. A population-based survey from the United States also documented a recent increase in the prevalence of MASLD among adults aged 20–45 years [27]. Patients with concomitant CHB and MASLD also had shorter hospital stays than those with CHB alone. They had higher platelet and albumin levels and lower total bilirubin, AST, and prothrombin time values, reflecting a more favorable liver-related laboratory profile. These differences may be related to variations in age distribution, disease stage, and timing of clinical presentation between the two groups. Obesity, dyslipidemia, or imaging evidence of hepatic steatosis may prompt some patients to undergo clinical evaluation earlier, allowing CHB to be identified before liver function becomes substantially impaired.

Available evidence on the link between MASLD and liver fibrosis in CHB remains inconsistent. Diao et al. [13] identified severe hepatic steatosis as an independent correlate of significant liver fibrosis among treatment-naïve CHB patients with normal ALT values. Consistent with this observation, a large biopsy-based study found that significant liver fibrosis occurred more frequently in CHB patients with concomitant MASLD [24]. By contrast, findings from a multicenter retrospective study by Yao et al. [28] indicated that CHB patients with concomitant NAFLD tended to have milder liver fibrosis. Con et al. [29] performed serial non-invasive fibrosis assessments in patients with CHB who had no advanced liver disease at baseline and similarly found no clear association between concomitant MASLD and fibrosis progression. These discrepancies may be related to differences in the study populations, definitions of fatty liver disease, and methods used to assess liver fibrosis. Previous studies differed in the antiviral treatment status, ALT levels, and baseline liver disease severity of the included patients. Some studies specifically examined severe hepatic steatosis, whereas others grouped patients according to whether they fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for NAFLD or MASLD. Fibrosis outcomes were evaluated using different methods, including liver biopsy, transient elastography, and serial assessment with non-invasive fibrosis markers. This heterogeneity in study populations, disease definitions, and assessment methods may influence both the magnitude and direction of the association between MASLD and liver fibrosis.

Differences in the approach used for fibrosis assessment may partly contribute to variations in the observed results. In the presence of hepatic steatosis, liver stiffness values obtained by transient elastography may be affected. It has been suggested that vibration-controlled transient elastography (FibroScan) could yield lower estimates of fibrosis severity in patients with fatty liver disease, as hepatic fat may change the mechanical characteristics of liver tissue and consequently influence stiffness readings [30]. In addition to hepatic steatosis, hepatic inflammatory activity may also affect liver stiffness measurements. Fung et al. [31] found that even mild-to-moderate elevations in ALT may be accompanied by increased liver stiffness in patients with CHB, and that this increase may not be entirely attributable to liver fibrosis itself, potentially resulting in overestimation or misclassification of fibrosis severity. Because hospitalized patients with CHB often have greater inflammatory activity, both hepatic steatosis and inflammatory status should be considered when evaluating FibroScan results.

The overall distribution of liver fibrosis stages differed between the CHB+MASLD and CHB-only groups. However, this difference may have reflected differences in the composition of individual fibrosis stages rather than a difference in the overall prevalence of significant liver fibrosis. More specifically, F4 fibrosis was less frequent among patients with CHB+MASLD, whereas intermediate fibrosis stages were comparatively more common. However, when F2–F4 was defined as significant liver fibrosis, univariable logistic regression analysis showed no association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis. After adjustment for age, sex, HBV DNA, AST, time since CHB diagnosis, and antiviral treatment status, the OR for MASLD increased from 1.092 to 1.473 but still did not reach statistical significance. Because substantial hepatic inflammatory activity can influence liver stiffness values obtained using FibroScan, patients with ALT or AST values greater than five times the upper limit of normal were additionally excluded. In the sensitivity analysis, the direction of the association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis remained broadly consistent with that observed in the main model, but the association was still not statistically significant. This suggests that the main model findings were unlikely to have been driven entirely by marked elevations in aminotransferase levels. Nevertheless, available evidence does not yet support an independent association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis in hospitalized patients with CHB. Of note, the OR estimates for MASLD were above 1 in both multivariable models, although the 95% CIs crossed 1. Thus, although this study did not identify a clear independent association, a modest effect of MASLD cannot be completely excluded. Statistical uncertainty does not mean that MASLD has no role in CHB-related liver fibrosis. From a pathophysiological perspective, MASLD may induce mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory responses through insulin resistance and lipotoxicity, thereby promoting hepatic stellate cell activation and liver fibrosis progression [32]. Although an independent association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis was not demonstrated in this study, patients with coexisting CHB and MASLD still require comprehensive management. In addition to appropriate antiviral treatment, attention should be directed toward metabolic risk control, including weight management, glycemic control, lipid regulation, and blood pressure control, along with periodic evaluation of liver fibrosis.

Beyond MASLD, the main model identified older age, male sex, higher HBV DNA levels, and higher AST levels as factors independently related to significant liver fibrosis. Recent studies have described comparable patterns. A prospective investigation identified age > 50 years and HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL as independent predictors of significant liver fibrosis in CHB [33]. Findings from another study indicated that advanced liver fibrosis occurred more commonly in older patients and was accompanied by a higher proportion of men and increased AST levels [34]. Increasing age may reflect the cumulative effects of long-term liver injury, higher HBV DNA levels indicate more active viral replication, and elevated AST is associated with hepatocellular injury and inflammatory activity. Following exclusion of individuals with ALT or AST values > 5 × the upper limit of normal, older age, male sex, and higher AST levels remained associated with significant liver fibrosis, whereas HBV DNA was no longer statistically significant. This change may have resulted from alterations in the sample composition and reduced statistical power after the exclusion of some patients with greater inflammatory activity, and does not indicate an absence of a relationship between HBV replication and liver fibrosis. Long-term effective suppression of HBV replication can promote regression of liver fibrosis, and histological regression may even occur in some patients with cirrhosis [35]. Given that antiviral treatment may affect the course of liver fibrosis, this study further examined whether antiviral treatment status modified the relationship between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis. In the analysis stratified by antiviral treatment status, the adjusted OR for MASLD was 3.192 among patients not receiving antiviral treatment and 1.360 among those receiving antiviral treatment; however, neither association reached statistical significance. The interaction between MASLD and antiviral treatment status was also not statistically significant. The wide confidence interval in the untreated group may have been related to the small number of patients who were not receiving antiviral treatment. The current findings provide no clear evidence that antiviral treatment status modifies the relationship between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis.

In patients with CHB and concomitant MASLD, an exploratory stratified analysis based on the number of metabolic abnormalities showed that several clinical characteristics differed across groups, including age, sex distribution, albumin, creatinine, and triglyceride levels. Regarding significant liver fibrosis, the proportions of patients with significant liver fibrosis among those with one, two, and three or more metabolic abnormalities were 76.5%, 90.6%, and 88.9%, respectively. Patients with at least two metabolic abnormalities showed a numerically higher prevalence of significant liver fibrosis than those with only one metabolic abnormality; nevertheless, no statistically significant overall difference was observed among the groups. These results suggest that the number of metabolic abnormalities may help differentiate the clinical profiles of CHB patients with concomitant MASLD. Nevertheless, its relationship to the severity of significant liver fibrosis remains uncertain. Given the limited number of patients with ≥ 3 metabolic abnormalities (n = 9), this finding should be regarded as exploratory. Larger-scale studies are warranted to determine whether a dose–response relationship exists between metabolic comorbidity burden and the risk of significant liver fibrosis in CHB patients with concomitant MASLD.

This study has several strengths. All patients underwent liver fibrosis assessment using FibroScan, thereby reducing measurement differences arising from the use of different assessment methods. The study collected demographic data, laboratory indicators, virological markers, and liver function-related indicators, and variables were prespecified based on clinical relevance for inclusion in the multivariable model. In addition, the robustness of the findings was assessed by repeating the analysis after removing patients with markedly elevated ALT or AST levels. Stratified analyses by antiviral treatment status, together with an interaction test, were used to determine whether the association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis varied between treatment strata. Among patients with concomitant MASLD, participants were further grouped by the number of metabolic abnormalities to provide an initial description of clinical heterogeneity across different levels of metabolic burden. The study also has several limitations. To begin with, it was conducted as a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study; therefore, only statistical associations between MASLD, significant liver fibrosis, and related clinical parameters could be evaluated, and causal relationships could not be established. Because follow-up data were unavailable, the relationships of MASLD with liver fibrosis progression, response to antiviral therapy, and long-term liver-related outcomes in patients with CHB could not be directly evaluated. Second, this study used a complete-case analysis. Of the 8,675 hospitalized patients with CHB assessed at the outset, most could not be retained for analysis because the required clinical information, FibroScan measurements, or laboratory results were unavailable. The likelihood of undergoing FibroScan and having complete clinical data may have been related to patients’ liver disease severity, metabolic status, and clinical decisions regarding testing, thereby affecting both the observed distribution of liver fibrosis and the estimated association between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis. Moreover, identification of MASLD in this cohort relied primarily on imaging evidence of hepatic steatosis. Despite its routine use in clinical practice, ultrasonography has relatively low sensitivity for mild hepatic steatosis. Liver fibrosis was assessed using FibroScan rather than liver biopsy. Hepatic steatosis and inflammatory activity may affect liver stiffness measurements, resulting in differences between FibroScan-based fibrosis staging and histological fibrosis staging. Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) data from FibroScan were also not collected in this study; therefore, the degree of hepatic steatosis could not be graded, and the relationship between different degrees of hepatic steatosis and significant liver fibrosis could not be further analyzed. Future studies may combine CAP, MRI-PDFF, MRE, or liver biopsy to more accurately assess hepatic fat content and the severity of liver fibrosis. Because the subgroup with three or more metabolic abnormalities included only nine patients, the statistical power of this subgroup analysis was limited, and the corresponding findings require validation in studies with larger sample sizes. Finally, all participants were inpatients recruited from a single center. Compared with outpatient or community-based CHB populations, these patients may have more severe liver disease, more comorbidities, and more complex treatment histories. Hospitalization may be related to both metabolic abnormalities and liver disease severity, and the observed association between MASLD and liver fibrosis may therefore have been affected by selection bias, resulting in either overestimation or underestimation of the effect. Accordingly, the findings of this study are primarily applicable to hospitalized patients with CHB. Future multicenter prospective cohort studies that include inpatient, outpatient, and community populations are needed to validate these findings and to evaluate the relationships of MASLD with liver fibrosis progression, response to antiviral therapy, and long-term liver-related outcomes.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cross-sectional analysis, the clinical profile of CHB patients with concomitant metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was characterized, and differences in significant liver fibrosis according to MASLD status were explored. Approximately 26.2% of the CHB cohort had MASLD. Compared with patients with CHB alone, those with concomitant MASLD were younger and showed relatively better hepatic functional reserve. Multivariable adjustment did not provide evidence that MASLD was independently related to significant liver fibrosis. Excluding patients with ALT or AST values > 5 × the upper limit of normal yielded results that were broadly consistent with those of the main model. Older age, male sex, and higher AST consistently emerged as factors related to significant liver fibrosis across the main and sensitivity models, whereas HBV DNA achieved statistical significance only in the main model. Stratified analyses and interaction testing according to antiviral treatment status did not suggest that antiviral treatment status altered the association of MASLD with significant liver fibrosis. Although an independent relationship between MASLD and significant liver fibrosis was not demonstrated, prompt recognition and integrated management of metabolic abnormalities remain clinically important for CHB patients. Because the study cohort was restricted to hospitalized patients, these findings may not be generalizable to the wider CHB population. Multicenter prospective studies that also enroll outpatient and community-based patients are needed to determine whether the same associations are present in the wider CHB population.
