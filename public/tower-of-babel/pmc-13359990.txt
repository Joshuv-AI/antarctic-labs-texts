Early laboratory-based differentiation of severe influenza pneumonia and influenza-associated encephalopathy in children using a multi-model, stability-aware machine-learning workflow: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuqing Yan, Xinyi Li, Xiaoshan Li, Lu Kuang, Min Guo, Tiantian Xu, Liang Hua, Changbing Wang, Bing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza-associated encephalopathy and acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE) are rare but devastating complications of pediatric influenza, and early differentiation from severe pneumonia without neurological involvement is challenging at admission. We aimed to develop and internally validate an early prediction model to distinguish severe influenza A pneumonia from influenza-associated central nervous system (CNS) involvement using routine admission laboratory tests. The intended clinical use is early triage: to prioritize neurological evaluation (e.g., EEG/neuroimaging when indicated), closer monitoring, and escalation of care for children predicted to be at higher risk of CNS involvement.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort at a tertiary pediatric center, consecutive children hospitalized with RT-PCR–confirmed influenza A from 1 January 2023 to 1 June 2025 were classified as mild pneumonia (Group 1), severe pneumonia without neurological involvement (Group 2), or CNS involvement (Group 3) using prespecified clinical, neurophysiological, and neuroimaging criteria. The primary prediction contrasted Group 2 versus Group 3 using routine laboratory tests obtained within 6 h of admission. Several machine-learning algorithms were trained and tuned using internal resampling and a held-out validation set. Bootstrap stability selection identified robust markers, and a parsimonious elastic-net logistic regression model was derived. We assessed discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit at prespecified risk thresholds (0.15, 0.25 and 0.35).ResultsAmong 227 eligible children (median age 4.2 years), 95 (41.9%) had mild pneumonia, 74 (32.6%) severe pneumonia without neurological involvement, and 58 (25.6%) influenza-associated CNS involvement; mortality was 0%, 4.1%, and 22.4%, respectively. In the primary task of distinguishing Group 2 from Group 3 using admission laboratory tests, the ten prespecified algorithms achieved out-of-fold AUCs of 0.77–0.90, and simple probability averaging achieved an AUC of 0.91. Stability selection identified 11 robust biochemical markers; a parsimonious elastic-net logistic regression model restricted to eight markers achieved a validation AUC of 0.89 with good calibration (Brier score 0.12) and consistently positive net benefit on decision-curve analysis across prespecified risk thresholds (0.15, 0.25, and 0.35).ConclusionsIn internal validation, an eight-marker model differentiated severe pneumonia without neurological involvement from influenza-associated CNS involvement with a validation AUC of 0.89 and good calibration. This stability-aware framework provides an implementable baseline for multicenter external validation and refinement, rather than a ready-to-use clinical tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13371-x.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains a major cause of pediatric morbidity and mortality worldwide. The World Health Organization estimates that annual influenza epidemics result in roughly 1 billion infections, including 3–5 million cases of severe illness and 290 000–650 000 respiratory deaths, with a disproportionate burden in young children and in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Recent surveillance from North America and other regions has underscored that children continue to experience substantial influenza-associated hospitalizations and deaths, despite the availability of vaccination and antiviral therapy [2]. Among the complications of pediatric influenza, neurological involvement—classically termed influenza-associated encephalopathy or encephalitis (IAE) and its fulminant subtype, acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE)—is of particular concern because of its abrupt onset, rapid progression and high risk of death or long-term neurodisability [3–5]. Recent reports from the United States during recent high-severity seasons have described clusters of pediatric IAE and ANE with case-fatality proportions exceeding 20–40% and frequent persistent deficits among survivors, highlighting the vulnerability of children to severe influenza-related CNS injury [3, 6].

Clinically, IAE and ANE are now recognized as part of a spectrum of influenza-associated neurological disease rather than as isolated entities. Seizures or convulsions, “mild” encephalopathy with full recovery, and ANE with bilateral thalamic lesions and multi-organ failure lie on a continuum in which early symptoms often overlap and diagnostic boundaries are blurred [7–9]. Early neuroimaging or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies may be normal or non-specific in the first days of illness, and the characteristic deep-brain lesions of ANE often appear only after clinical deterioration [8, 10]. Consequently, children who are evolving toward CNS involvement may initially present with fever, respiratory symptoms and non-specific systemic signs that are indistinguishable from those of severe pneumonia without neurological disease. In parallel, febrile seizures are common in the pediatric age range and may coexist with respiratory distress, further complicating bedside differentiation between self-limited seizures, early IAE and impending ANE [5, 11, 12]. Frontline clinicians therefore must frequently make high-stakes decisions—escalation of respiratory support, urgent neuroimaging, transfer to tertiary care—under substantial diagnostic uncertainty.

Existing tools provide only partial support for this decision-making. Many pediatric prediction models focus on respiratory severity (e.g., severe or refractory pneumonia) or on risk stratification within the encephalopathy spectrum itself (for example, distinguishing ANE from milder IAE or convulsions among children who already present with neurological manifestations) [8, 13]. These approaches do not directly address the more upstream and practically important question encountered at admission: among all hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed influenza A infection, which patients are likely to follow a primarily respiratory course (including severe pneumonia without CNS involvement) and which are at high risk for early CNS involvement (encompassing IAE and ANE)? At the same time, methodological advances in machine learning (ML) have shown promise for pediatric infectious diseases and acute respiratory illnesses, enabling early risk stratification based on routinely collected demographic, clinical and laboratory variables [14–17]. These developments suggest an opportunity to adapt robust, transparent ML workflows to the specific differential diagnosis between severe influenza pneumonia and influenza-related CNS involvement, provided that models are well-calibrated and their clinical utility is explicitly evaluated.

In this context, we aimed to develop and internally validate an early differential diagnosis model to distinguish severe influenza A pneumonia without neurological manifestations from influenza-associated CNS involvement (IAE/encephalitis and ANE) in children, using only information available shortly after hospital presentation. Previous work from our center identified biochemical markers of systemic injury and blood–brain barrier dysfunction among children with influenza-related neurological complications [8], we deliberately adopted a pragmatic, binary CNS outcome (presence vs. absence of CNS involvement), rather than attempting to subdivide IAE and ANE in a small retrospective cohort where early diagnostic misclassification is likely. To emphasize clinical relevance, we evaluated the model on a held-out validation set in terms of (i) how well it separates children with CNS involvement from those without (discrimination; AUC), (ii) how closely predicted risks match observed event rates (calibration), and (iii) whether using the model would improve triage decisions compared with default strategies (clinical utility; decision-curve net benefit). This was undertaken with the dual aim of exploring an early triage-oriented differential-diagnosis tool and providing a transparent, reproducible framework for future multicenter external validation.

Methods

Study population and case ascertainment

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, a tertiary pediatric center, enrolling consecutive hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed influenza A virus (IAV) infection between January 1, 2023 and June 1, 2025. Confirmation required a positive nucleic acid amplification test (reverse-transcription PCR) on a respiratory specimen, treated as the diagnostic reference for influenza in hospitalized patients. Influenza A infection was confirmed by real-time RT-PCR performed on oropharyngeal (throat) swab specimens using a PCR–fluorescence probe assay (DAAN Gene, Guangzhou, China) targeting a conserved region of the influenza A matrix (M) gene, and run on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cycle threshold (Ct) values were extracted from laboratory reports and are presented descriptively (median [IQR]) to document molecular positivity; in line with the World Health Organization (WHO) 2024 molecular detection protocols [18], Ct ≤ 35 was considered positive. Because Ct values are assay- and platform-dependent and are not standardized across different workflows, Ct values were not used for between-assay quantitative comparisons or for downstream modelling [19, 20].

For each eligible hospitalized case, we extracted demographic information, perinatal and medical history, clinical assessments, and early laboratory parameters obtained within 6 h of admission (the earliest available results from the routine clinical workflow). If multiple measurements were available, the earliest value was retained to represent the patient’s initial condition.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded a priori if any of the following applied: (1) age > 16 years or < 1 month at admission; (2) culture-confirmed bacterial co-infection at baseline (positive blood culture and/or respiratory culture [sputum or endotracheal aspirate] obtained within the admission sampling window); (3) a more appropriate alternative diagnosis explaining the acute presentation (e.g., bacterial meningitis/encephalitis, severe trauma, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, or decompensated inborn errors of metabolism); (4) severe pre-existing neurologic disease precluding attribution (e.g., epileptic encephalopathy, severe cerebral palsy); (5) admission ≥ 7 days after symptom onset, to preserve an early-presentation prediction setting based on admission (≤ 6 h) laboratory tests and to minimize heterogeneity and time-related confounding from later-phase complications and treatment effects; or (6) > 30% missing data across candidate predictors for that encounter.

Clinical grouping schema

Eligible encounters were classified into three mutually exclusive clinical categories using prospectively specified operational criteria that reflect information available to frontline clinicians at the time of admission. Classification did not use any blood or biochemical laboratory variables; it relied solely on respiratory severity criteria, clinical neurologic manifestations, EEG findings, and neuroimaging.

Group 1 (mild pneumonia) comprised children managed with routine inpatient care who had no evidence of severe lower respiratory tract involvement and no clinical signs or symptoms of central nervous system (CNS) involvement.

Group 2 (severe pneumonia without neurologic involvement) comprised children who met at least one respiratory severity criterion but had no neurological signs or symptoms, explicitly excluding any patient with seizures or other CNS findings. Respiratory severity criteria included peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) < 90% on room air, the need for supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support (e.g. nasal oxygen, non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation), marked respiratory distress such as intercostal or suprasternal retractions, or extensive pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging. The SpO₂ threshold of 90% is consistent with World Health Organization–informed pediatric practice and contemporary respiratory care recommendations that consider SpO₂ < 90% as an indication for oxygen therapy in children with respiratory distress [21, 22]. Because simple febrile seizures are common between 6 and 60 months of age and are typically generalized, brief (< 15 min), non-recurrent within 24 h, and unaccompanied by focal neurological deficits, children who strictly met al.l criteria for a simple febrile seizure were retained in Group 2 if they otherwise fulfilled respiratory severity criteria and had no additional neurological signs [23, 24].

Group 3 (CNS involvement: encephalopathy/encephalitis) comprised children with evidence of central nervous system involvement, defined by at least one of the following: (i) acute brain dysfunction lasting ≥ 24 h (altered level of consciousness, abnormal behavior, or cognitive change) or clinical seizures or status epilepticus; (ii) electroencephalography demonstrating diffuse slowing or epileptiform discharges; (iii) neuroimaging (MRI/CT) showing brain parenchymal lesions supportive of influenza-associated encephalopathy (IAE), including patterns consistent with acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE) such as symmetric bilateral thalamic lesions with or without additional deep gray or brainstem involvement [25]. Neuroimaging was not uniformly available in Group 3. For descriptive purposes, among children with available MRI/CT, we classified ANE-pattern by characteristic symmetric bilateral thalamic lesions [26] (with or without additional symmetric deep gray matter or brainstem involvement); children with neuroimaging that was normal or abnormal but not meeting the ANE-pattern definition were categorized as non-ANE IAE. This subtyping was used only to describe heterogeneity and was not used to define the modelling outcome. Group assignment was hierarchical: any child meeting the CNS involvement criteria was classified as Group 3 irrespective of respiratory severity or the need for respiratory support (including invasive mechanical ventilation). Respiratory support variables were recorded and reported descriptively (e.g., oxygen therapy and mechanical ventilation) but were not part of the Group 3 definition.

Although ANE is widely regarded as a fulminant subtype within the broader IAE spectrum, characterized by rapid neurological decline and characteristic bilateral lesions, early clinical differentiation between milder IAE and ANE can be challenging at the point of initial decision-making, especially before definitive neuroimaging or expert adjudication is available [27, 28]. We characterize heterogeneity within Group 3 by reporting the proportion of an ANE-like subgroup, defined by a documented ANE diagnosis or ANE-typical neuroimaging patterns. However, to minimize misclassification and to maintain mutually exclusive groups for model development, we did not model IAE versus ANE as separate prediction targets. In parallel, we categorically excluded any patient with seizures or other neurological findings from Group 2. This pragmatic grouping strategy is consistent with contemporary public-health surveillance frameworks and neuroradiologic literature, which emphasize that early sub-typing of IAE versus ANE is often uncertain and may be unreliable in routine clinical practice [3, 27, 29].

Data handling and quality control

After applying exclusion criteria, records were harmonized to a unified data dictionary. Factor-type variables were encoded as binary indicators (0/1). Numeric variables were retained on their original clinical scale for descriptive summaries; model-building steps using standardized inputs are detailed in the Machine-Learning Methods. Missing values occurred only in continuous predictors and were handled by median imputation. To prevent information leakage, imputation parameters were derived from the training data only: within cross-validation, the median for each predictor was re-estimated within each training fold and then applied to the corresponding validation fold; for the final held-out validation set, medians were estimated from the full training split and applied once to the validation split. Reporting aligns with STROBE recommendations for observational studies [30, 31].

Ethics

This study was approved by the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center Ethics Committee (Approval Number: [2023]271B00). All analyses used de-identified data extracted from the electronic medical record; informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating patients via the electronic medical record system, in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Statistical analysis and machine-learning workflow

Baseline characteristics were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences among the three clinical groups (Groups 1, 2, and 3) were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables.

We implemented a multi-model, stability-aware machine-learning workflow to develop and internally evaluate an early laboratory-based differential-diagnosis model.

Study design and prediction tasks

We prespecified two clinically motivated binary prediction tasks with CNS involvement (Group 3) as the target outcome: (i) the primary task contrasted severe pneumonia without neurological involvement (Group 2) versus Group 3, and (ii) a secondary exploratory task contrasted mild pneumonia (Group 1) versus Group 3. We did not fit a single three-class model because the intended use case was early identification of CNS involvement for triage, which is a binary clinical decision. For the primary task, we additionally examined model behavior at three prespecified probability thresholds (pt = 0.15, 0.25, and 0.35) in the training and held-out validation sets. The secondary task was included as an exploratory contrast to contextualize the primary triage problem and was not intended as a clinically deployable decision model.

Outcomes and predictors

For each two-group comparison, the outcome (“Severity”) was encoded as a binary indicator (0/1). Candidate predictors included demographics, perinatal and clinical history, and early admission laboratory indices. Categorical predictors were coded as binary dummy variables, and continuous predictors were standardized using training-set parameters only. A complete list of candidate predictors considered for model development is provided in Supplementary material: Table S1.

Model development and validation

The full dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and a held-out validation set (20%), stratified by outcome. Model development was restricted to the training set. We used nested cross-validation, with an outer 10-fold cross-validation loop for performance estimation and an inner 5-fold loop for hyperparameter tuning. To identify robust predictors, we applied bootstrap-based stability selection across ten heterogeneous base learners and defined a final stability pool for downstream modeling. We then fitted two elastic-net logistic regression models (α = 0.5): a parsimonious model restricted to the stability pool and a full model including the complete candidate set. Predicted probabilities were calibrated on the training data and then applied to the held-out validation set.

Performance evaluation

On the validation set, model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals calculated by DeLong’s method. Calibration was evaluated using the Brier score, Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and calibration intercept and slope. Clinical utility was assessed by decision-curve analysis across threshold probabilities from 0.15 to 0.50 [32–35]. Threshold-specific confusion matrices and derived classification metrics were summarized at pt = 0.15, 0.25, and 0.35 for both the training and validation sets. Additional implementation details, prespecified tuning grids, missing-data handling, and reproducibility specifications are provided in Supplementary material.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 1 January 2023 and 1 June 2025, 227 hospitalized children with laboratory-confirmed influenza A infection met the inclusion criteria after application of predefined exclusions (age > 16 years or < 1 month, alternative primary diagnoses, major pre-existing neurological disorders, admission ≥ 7 days after symptom onset, or > 30% missing data). The enrolment, exclusion and allocation process is summarized in Fig. 1. Of these children, 95 (41.9%) were classified as mild pneumonia (Group 1), 74 (32.6%) as severe pneumonia without neurological involvement (Group 2), and 58 (25.6%) as having influenza-associated CNS involvement (Group 3). The overall median age was 4.17 years (interquartile range [IQR] 1.70–6.10), and 138 (60.8%) patients were male. Mortality showed a marked gradient across groups, from 0/95 (0%) in Group 1, to 3/74 (4.1%) in Group 2 and 13/58 (22.4%) in Group 3. Baseline demographic, clinical and laboratory characteristics for the three groups are summarized in Table 1. Within Group 3, brain CT/MRI was available for 52/58 (89.7%) children (Table 2). Among those imaged, 44/52 (84.6%) had abnormal findings, including thalamic involvement in 20/52 (38.5%), bilateral thalamic involvement in 16/52 (30.8%), cerebral edema/swelling in 16/52 (30.8%), and multifocal lesions in 22/52 (42.3%). Using documented diagnosis and ANE-typical imaging patterns, 30/58 (51.7%) were classified as having an ANE-like phenotype, illustrating substantial within-group heterogeneity (Table 2). Extra-pulmonary organ involvement or multiorgan injury was documented in 25/58 (43.1%), most commonly hepatic dysfunction (8/58, 13.8%), gastrointestinal bleeding (8/58, 13.8%), and cardiac dysfunction/myocardial injury/heart failure (6/58, 10.3%). Acute management was intensive: 44/58 (75.9%) required ICU/PICU admission, 35/58 (60.3%) invasive mechanical ventilation, 26/58 (44.8%) vasopressors/inotropes, and 38/58 (65.5%) ICP-lowering therapy; systemic corticosteroids and IVIG were administered to 41/58 (70.7%) and 44/58 (75.9%), respectively (Table 2). A representative brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan from a child with typical acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE) is shown in Fig. 2.

Missing data patterns and imputation summary

Missing-data patterns for candidate predictors are summarized in Table S5. After patient-level exclusions, the amount of missing data was limited and confined to continuous laboratory variables. Apart from procalcitonin (PCT), which had 18.1% missing data, all other predictors had < 4% missing data. As prespecified, missing continuous values were imputed using training-data medians (with fold-specific estimation within cross-validation) to generate fully observed model matrices for analysis.

Performance of base learners and stability-based feature selection

In the primary task (Group 2 vs. Group 3), all ten base learners achieved good out-of-fold (OOF) discrimination (Table 3). AUC values ranged from 0.77 (SVM-Rbf) to 0.90 (Random Forest), with BART and XGBoost also performing strongly (AUC 0.89 for both). These higher-performing models combined reasonably low Brier scores (~ 0.13–0.14), accuracies around 0.80–0.83 and a balanced sensitivity–specificity profile. LASSO and the remaining learners (GAM, CatBoost, MARS, LightGBM, SVM-Lin) showed AUCs around 0.78–0.87 with modest variation in threshold-dependent metrics.

Simple probability averaging across all ten learners (VOTE10) provided a small but consistent gain over single models, with the highest mean AUC (0.91 ± 0.03), the lowest Brier score (0.13 ± 0.01) and balanced accuracy (0.83 ± 0.04; sensitivity 0.76 ± 0.06; specificity 0.88 ± 0.04). Ridge-based stacking (STACK) achieved a similar OOF AUC (0.91 ± 0.03) but, at the default cut-off, produced near-zero sensitivity and perfect specificity, indicating extreme conservatism and the need for explicit re-thresholding before any clinical use.

The stability heatmap (Fig. 3) and voting summary (Table 4) show that a small subset of biochemical markers was reproducibly selected across models and bootstrap resamples. Procalcitonin and CK-MB received the maximum of 10 votes, being ranked among the top 20 predictors in ≥ 60% of bootstrap replicates by all ten learners. hs-CRP received 9 votes, and CK received 7 votes. ALT, AST, total protein, albumin, globulin, the albumin/globulin ratio and total bilirubin each obtained 6 votes, driven by consistent selection in LASSO, BART, GAM, MARS, SVM-Lin and SVM-Rbf. Applying the prespecified threshold of ≥ 6 votes, these 11 variables formed the final stability pool for parsimonious model construction.

The ROC curves were nearly superimposed (Fig. 4), indicating no appreciable difference in overall classification ability.

The final parsimonious elastic-net model retained eight of the 11 stability-pool predictors (Table 7). Procalcitonin and CK were the strongest positive predictors, with interquartile-range shifts associated with approximately 20-fold and 2–3-fold increases in the odds of CNS involvement, respectively, and bootstrap-corrected confidence intervals excluding the null. CK-MB also contributed positively but with wider intervals. In contrast, hs-CRP showed a consistent negative association, indicating that, conditional on the other markers, higher hs-CRP values were more typical of severe pneumonia without CNS involvement. Total protein, globulin, the albumin/globulin ratio and total bilirubin added smaller effects reflecting subtler patterns of protein metabolism and hepatic dysfunction. ALT, AST and albumin were included in the stability pool but were not retained in the final elastic-net model (coefficients shrunk to zero), indicating limited incremental predictive value after accounting for the retained liver/protein-related markers, likely due to correlation among these variables. Overall, a compact panel of routinely measured biochemical markers reproduced the performance and clinical utility of a much larger predictor set while improving parsimony and calibration.

Threshold selection and clinical implications

To illustrate potential clinical use, we evaluated the elastic-net models at three prespecified thresholds (pt = 0.15, 0.25 and 0.35), corresponding to increasingly stringent definitions of “high risk” for influenza-associated CNS involvement (Table 8). In the training set, a low threshold of 0.15 yielded very high sensitivity but modest specificity. For example, ENet_pool achieved sensitivity 0.96 with only two false negatives but specificity 0.50, whereas ENet_full reached sensitivity 1.00 with no false negatives and specificity 0.57. These operating points represent a “rule-out” strategy prioritising avoidance of missed CNS involvement at the cost of more false positives and additional investigations.

ENet_pool achieved sensitivity 0.91 and specificity 0.77 (accuracy 0.83), while ENet_full achieved sensitivity 0.98 and specificity 0.83 (accuracy 0.90). At pt = 0.15 and 0.25, mean ΔNB values were essentially zero with confidence intervals spanning both positive and negative values; at pt = 0.35, the parsimonious model showed a modestly positive mean ΔNB, but the interval again included zero. Taken together, these results suggest that the choice between thresholds should be driven by local tolerance for false negatives versus false positives rather than by material differences between the two elastic-net specifications.

Secondary task (Group 1 vs. Group 3) and reproducibility

In the exploratory secondary task comparing mild pneumonia (Group 1) with CNS involvement (Group 3), model performance was uniformly high across base learners and ensembles (Table S2; AUC 0.92–0.98). These near-ceiling metrics are expected given the marked clinical and biochemical separation between mild and CNS-complicated presentations and should be interpreted primarily as a case-mix (spectrum) effect rather than evidence of a useful real-world triage model. We therefore report this analysis for context and workflow transparency, but did not pursue further optimization or implementation for this contrast.

To support reproducibility, prespecified tuning grids for each algorithm and global resampling/evaluation settings (including seeds, folds, stability-selection thresholds, and bootstrap settings) are provided in Tables S3–S4 and the central configuration file (config.yml). This implementation-level documentation is intended to enable independent reproduction and future external evaluation.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of 227 children hospitalized with influenza A infection, we used a multi-model, stability-aware machine-learning workflow to address a clinically difficult problem: early differentiation between severe influenza pneumonia without neurological involvement and influenza-associated encephalopathy/encephalitis using only routine laboratory tests obtained within approximately 6 h of admission. Eleven biochemical markers of inflammation and multiorgan injury were selected by a 10-model bootstrap stability procedure, and a parsimonious elastic-net model based on eight of these markers achieved good discrimination (AUC 0.89), acceptable calibration and clinical net benefit comparable to a full-predictor model.

Interpretation of key laboratory predictors

Procalcitonin emerged as the most stable and influential predictor of CNS involvement in our multivariable framework. Although PCT is widely discussed in the context of severe pneumonia and suspected bacterial co-infection [36–39], experimental and clinical data indicate that PCT can also rise during viral respiratory infection in proportion to disease severity, reflecting systemic inflammatory and organ-dysfunction burden rather than bacterial infection alone [40]. In pediatric influenza-associated neurological complications, higher serum PCT has also been reported in more severe phenotypes such as acute necrotizing encephalopathy [25]. Importantly, our primary comparison already contrasted two groups enriched for severe illness (Group 2 vs. Group 3). In this setting, higher PCT likely captured incremental systemic injury and inflammatory burden that accompanied CNS involvement beyond isolated respiratory failure, after accounting for other laboratory markers in the model [25, 41].

In contrast, high-sensitivity CRP showed a negative association with CNS involvement after adjustment for other markers. Traditionally, higher CRP correlates with extensive pulmonary involvement and bacterial superinfection in p.

ediatric pneumonia [42]. In our cohort, however, peak hs-CRP values clustered in the severe pneumonia group rather than the CNS group. This likely reflects case-mix rather than any protective role of CRP. First, many Group 3 patients had catastrophic neurological deterioration with relatively modest radiographic lung involvement; for these children, cytokine-mediated blood–brain barrier disruption and microvascular injury may dominate the clinical picture more than exuberant lung consolidation [4, 5]. Second, CRP synthesis depends on preserved hepatic function; in the setting of multiorgan failure or hepatic dysfunction, which are common in influenza-associated encephalopathy and acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), CRP responses can be blunted despite intense inflammation [43]. Thus, in our cohort and within the multivariable model, lower hs-CRP should be interpreted cautiously and not used in isolation to down-triage suspected CNS involvement when PCT and organ-injury markers are markedly abnormal.

Creatine kinase-MB and total CK were also positive predictors of CNS involvement. Influenza-associated myositis and rhabdomyolysis with elevated CK, as well as influenza-related myocarditis in which CK-MB may be elevated, have been described in pediatric series and can accompany severe systemic illness [44–46]. In our cohort, the co-occurrence of elevated CK/CK-MB together with higher PCT and abnormal liver/protein indices is more consistent with a broader systemic injury phenotype (multi-organ involvement) than with lung-limited disease, rather than implying a specific diagnosis such as myositis or myocarditis. Similar patterns of extra-pulmonary organ injury have been reported in severe influenza-associated encephalopathy/ANE cohorts [47, 48]. The remaining variables retained in the parsimonious model (total protein, globulin, albumin/globulin ratio and total bilirubin) collectively point to hepatic involvement/capillary leak and altered protein metabolism [49–52]. Finally, although ALT, AST and albumin entered the stability pool, they were not retained in the final elastic-net model (coefficients shrunk to zero), indicating limited incremental predictive value once correlated liver/protein markers were included. This behavior is expected in elastic-net regularization under correlated predictors and does not imply that these biomarkers are “invalid” or biologically irrelevant.

Overall, the selected predictors are biologically plausible for a syndrome characterized by hypercytokinaemia, blood–brain barrier disruption, cerebral oedema and multiorgan dysfunction [25, 49, 51]. Our contribution is to quantify the multivariable, joint predictive value of the stability-selected admission markers as a compact panel, designed for early differential diagnosis between severe pneumonia and CNS involvement, rather than prognostication within established encephalopathy.

Clinical implications

Despite using only eight routine laboratory variables, the parsimonious elastic-net model showed AUCs near 0.90 and reasonable calibration in the internal validation set, with positive decision-curve net benefit. Although there is no universally binding AUC threshold for clinical usefulness, values in the range of 0.80–0.90 are commonly interpreted as indicating good discrimination; however, discrimination alone is insufficient, and calibration and decision-curve results must also be considered when judging potential clinical utility. These internally validated findings suggest that simple, interpretable models may support early risk stratification in similar settings, but their clinical usefulness and transportability require confirmation in larger, external cohorts [53]. Threshold-specific analyses indicate that probability cut-offs around 0.25–0.35 offer a pragmatic trade-off between early detection of CNS involvement and avoidance of excessive false positives, suitable for guiding decisions on neuroimaging, EEG and early neurology consultation. In settings that prioritize minimizing missed CNS-involvement cases, a lower cut-off (e.g., 0.15) can be considered to favor sensitivity, acknowledging a higher false-positive rate and downstream resource use. After external validation and, if necessary, local recalibration, this eight-marker model could be implemented as a web-based or electronic medical record–embedded probability calculator to support early triage, rather than as a fixed points-based rule at the current stage.

The secondary task contrasting mild pneumonia (Group 1) with CNS involvement (Group 3) produced near-perfect discrimination across almost all models. We interpret this as an “easy” separation driven by profound clinical and biochemical differences rather than a realistic triage problem. Similar optimism has been reported in other ML studies where the classes differ by late-stage organ failure rather than by subtle early features [53]. We therefore did not attempt further model simplification or implementation for this contrast, and focused instead on the more clinically challenging decision between severe pneumonia and evolving CNS injury.

Treatment patterns in Group 3 are reported descriptively; given confounding by indication in retrospective cohorts, we did not attempt causal inference regarding treatment effects, and the identified biomarkers should not be interpreted as predictors of immunomodulatory treatment benefit [54].

Methodological considerations and reproducibility

Methodologically, this study illustrates a transparent workflow aligned with recent TRIPOD/TRIPOD-AI recommendations for prediction model development and reporting [55, 56]. We combined nested cross-validation, a 10-model bootstrap stability-voting scheme, ensemble benchmarking and explicit calibration and decision-curve analyses within a scripted pipeline controlled by a configuration file. This limits researcher degrees of freedom, reduces overfitting and facilitates reproducibility. The finding that a parsimonious stability-selected model matches the performance of a full-predictor model suggests that most predictive information resides in a compact subset of markers, and that additional complexity mainly introduces noise. The code structure can be readily adapted to larger multicenter datasets, allowing external validation and context-specific recalibration with minimal modification.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective single-center study with a modest number of CNS-involvement cases. Although we used nested cross-validation and bootstrap procedures, the model has undergone internal validation only; external validation across institutions, seasons, and viral subtypes is required before any clinical deployment [55]. We also deliberately restricted predictors to laboratory tests obtained within approximately 6 h of admission and excluded neurological symptoms, imaging, and cerebrospinal fluid findings, because these variables contributed to case adjudication and would therefore behave as outcome-defining features rather than independent predictors. Accordingly, the model should be viewed as an adjunct for early laboratory-based risk stratification rather than a substitute for definitive neurodiagnostic assessment.

Within Group 3, we described heterogeneity by reporting an ANE-like subgroup based on documented diagnosis and/or ANE-typical neuroimaging patterns, but we did not model IAE and ANE as separate prediction targets. In routine practice, this distinction often requires timely high-quality neuroimaging and expert review, and imposing strict subtype labels in a relatively small retrospective dataset could increase misclassification and model instability [49, 51]. We did not perform host genetic testing, such as RANBP2 analysis, and therefore cannot assess the contribution of genetic susceptibility in this cohort [57, 58]. Occult bacterial co-infection may not have been fully excluded because culture-based screening can miss concurrent infection in retrospective datasets, which may partly influence inflammatory markers such as PCT. Similarly, elevations of CK and CK-MB were interpreted as markers of systemic tissue injury, but specific attribution to myocarditis, myositis, or rhabdomyolysis could not be systematically confirmed because standardized adjudication was not uniformly available [59].

Finally, several host factors known to influence influenza severity—such as prematurity, chronic respiratory disease, neuromuscular impairment and incomplete vaccination [60–62]—were collected but did not survive stringent stability selection and penalization, probably due to limited power and the dominant signal from acute biochemical markers in this cohort. Their absence from the final model should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect, but rather as a limitation of sample size and design. Treatment patterns in Group 3 were reported descriptively only, and no causal inference regarding treatment effects or biomarker-predicted treatment benefit was attempted because of confounding by indication [63].

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cohort, we developed and internally validated an early differential-diagnosis model to distinguish severe influenza pneumonia without neurological involvement from influenza-associated CNS involvement in children using routine admission laboratory tests. A parsimonious elastic-net model based on eight stability-selected markers showed good discrimination and calibration, with clinical net benefit comparable to a full-predictor model. These findings are consistent with systemic inflammation and multiorgan injury being important correlates of influenza-associated encephalopathy and provide a transparent, reproducible framework that now requires external multicenter validation before any consideration of integration into clinical care pathways.
