Activity‐dependent transit dose assessment in 192Ir high‐dose‐rate brachytherapy surface applicator using GafChromicTM EBT4 film
Authors: Daniel Liu, Emily Draeger, Christopher J Tien, Fada Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo characterize the newly released GafChromicTM EBT4 film for transit dose measurement in high‐dose rate brachytherapy (HDR‐BT) surface applicators and to systematically investigate the relationship between transit dose and 192Ir source activity.MethodsEBT4 film was used to measure dose at 3 mm depth from a horizontally oriented Leipzig‐style surface applicator connected to a 192Ir HDR‐BT remote afterloading unit. Transit dose was systematically characterized across eight different source activities ranging from 3.372 to 9.716 Ci (13,724 U to 39,544 U) using measurements with seven nominal dwell times (5‐100 seconds, scaled to 10 Ci (40,700 U)). Transit dose was derived from linear extrapolation to zero dwell time and compared with dose calculated (10‐Ci source with 100‐s nominal dwell time, excluding transit contributions) using the treatment planning system (TPS) Eclipse (Version 16.1).ResultsEBT4 film demonstrated excellent dose‐response linearity with nominal dwell time (adjusted R2 > 0.99) across all source activities, confirming its suitability for transit dose measurement. Transit dose contributions ranged from 0.3% to 4.3% of TPS dose (100‐s nominal dwell time at 10 Ci), with maximum contribution from the 9.543 Ci source (0.225±0.040 Gy) and minimum from the 5.502 Ci source (0.017±0.025 Gy). Transit dose showed poor linear correlation with source activity (adjusted R2 = 0.497), indicating that factors beyond source activity influence transit dose magnitude.ConclusionsThis study validates EBT4 film as a reliable tool for transit dose measurement in surface brachytherapy. The observed non‐linear relationship between transit dose and source activity reveals the critical influence of experimental setup variables, particularly source guide tube geometry, which affects the source‐to‐target distance during transit. These findings demonstrate that transit dose is impacted by multiple interdependent factors including source activity, guide tube configuration, and setup geometry. For clinical applications, this highlights the importance of standardized setup protocols and empirical measurement for accurate transit dose assessment.Keywords: High‐dose‐rate brachytherapy, Leipzig surface applicator, radiochromic film, transit dose

INTRODUCTION

The calculated dose in standard high‐dose rate brachytherapy (HDR‐BT) treatment planning systems (TPSs) does not account for the “transit dose” that is accumulated during source transit between the HDR‐BT remote afterloading unit (RAU) and dwell positions (approaching and retracting) and, to a lesser degree, between dwell positions. The magnitude of transit dose depends on multiple factors including source motion dynamics (acceleration, uniform speed motion, and deceleration phases), distance between RAU and target, dwell positions, dwell times, applicator shielding, 1 , 2 source activity, and critically, the geometry of the source guide tube connecting the RAU to the applicator. The omission of transit dose introduces uncertainty in dosimetry that may impact treatment delivery accuracy and clinical outcome assessment in HDR‐BT.

Transit dose measurement has been investigated across various HDR‐BT applications, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 but surface applicator treatments remain understudied. 9 Surface brachytherapy presents unique challenges for transit dose assessment due to the shallow treatment depths and proximity to critical structures, where even small dosimetric uncertainties can have clinical implications. Furthermore, the relationship between transit dose and source activity, while theoretically expected to be linear under identical conditions, has not been systematically characterized across multiple source activities in surface applicator configurations.

Radiochromic film dosimetry has proven valuable for HDR‐BT dose verification, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 with most studies utilizing GafChromic™ EBT2 or EBT3 models (Ashland Specialty Ingredients, G.P., Wilmington, DE, USA). The recently introduced GafChromic™ EBT4 model offers improved signal‐to‐noise characteristics 16 compared to EBT3 while maintaining high spatial resolution and low energy dependence, 17 yet its application to HDR‐BT transit dose measurement has not been evaluated.

This study addresses these gaps by: (1) characterizing EBT4 film for transit dose measurement in a Leipzig‐style surface applicator, 18 (2) systematically investigating the relationship between transit dose and source activity across eight different 192Ir sources, and (3) evaluating the clinical implications of transit dose contributions in surface brachytherapy treatments. These objectives aim to improve the understanding of transit dose behavior in surface applications and provide guidance for clinical implementation of transit dose corrections.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Rationale of transit dose measurement

Transit dose is derived from a series of measurements with varying dwell times using linear extrapolation methodology. The total delivered dose consists of two components: dwell dose (delivered while the source is stationary at the dwell position) and transit dose (delivered during source movement between the RAU and dwell position). Dwell dose scales linearly with dwell time, while transit dose remains constant regardless of dwell time since it depends only on the source motion profile.

For the same source motion pattern, the transit dose is proportional to the source activity. When the source approaches the target location (r → 0), the transit dose rate increases up to the dwell dose rate.

The y‐intercept of this linear relationship of equation (1) at zero dwell time represents the transit dose, as this is the dose delivered when no stationary dwell occurs. This extrapolation method allows separation of the transit dose component from the total measured dose.

Measurement setup

GafChromic™ EBT4 film was used to measure dose under a Leipzig‐style surface applicator (diameter = 30 mm) connected to a GammaMedplus iX (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) 192Ir HDR‐BT RAU. The experimental configuration is shown in Figure 1. A single‐dwell position design was employed to isolate transit dose contributions from RAU‐to‐applicator source movement, eliminating additional transit dose components that would arise from inter‐dwell position travel.

Setup of measurement. (A) A Leipzig‐style surface applicator (diameter = 30 mm) was connected to a Varian GammaMedplus iX 192Ir HDR‐BT RAU. The source was positioned at the center of the applicator. (B) A piece of EBT4 film (100 mm × 100 mm) was placed at a depth of 3 mm under the surface with a 15 cm thick solid‐water phantom for backscattering.

The 192Ir source was positioned at the geometric center of the surface applicator. EBT4 film pieces (100 mm × 100 mm) were positioned at 3 mm depth under the applicator surface, with a 15 cm thick solid‐water phantom providing adequate backscatter conditions. 1 Critical to the experimental design, the source guide tube was maintained in a straight configuration to minimize transit dose variations that could result from guide tube curvature effects.

Transit dose characterization was performed across eight different source activities ranging from 3.372 to 9.716 Ci (13,724 U to 39,544 U). For each source activity, seven different nominal dwell times were investigated: 5, 10, 15, 30, 50, 80, and 100 seconds (normalized to a standard 10 Ci source activity). Quality control measures included use of an unirradiated control film and consistent film orientation marking. Each film was labeled with its corresponding nominal dwell time and marked with an arrow indicating the source head‐to‐tail direction to ensure consistent positioning. Film batch consistency was maintained by using EBT4 films exclusively from lot number 07052201 throughout the study.

Film scanning and analysis

All irradiated and control films were processed following vendor‐recommended protocols and established clinical procedures. 16 , 19 , 20 Films were scanned approximately 48 hours post‐irradiation using an EPSON Expression 11000XL flat‐bed scanner (Epson America, Inc., Los Alamitos, CA, USA). To ensure scanning consistency and minimize artifacts, films were positioned at the scanner bed center with the directional arrow markers aligned from top to bottom of the scanner bed. A clear plexiglass plate secured film positioning during scanning. Images were acquired at 254 dots per inch resolution (0.1 mm × 0.1 mm per pixel) using 48‐bit color mode to capture red, green, and blue channels, then saved in tagged image file format (.tiff).

ImageJ software (version 1.53c) was used for quantitative film analysis. 21 Representative film analysis is shown in Figure 2 for the 9.543 Ci source with 100‐second nominal dwell time. Red‐channel grayscale values were converted to optical density, then to absorbed dose using a calibration curve derived from 6 MV linac irradiation of films from the same batch. Energy dependence validation from our previous EBT4 characterization showed low dose differences (<2–5%) between 6 MV photons and 250 kVp x‐rays. 16 Given the ∼380 keV average photon energy of 192Ir, the 6 MV calibration curve provides acceptable accuracy with uncertainty comparable to or lower than 250 kVp x‐ray applications.

The source orientation is indicated by an arrow. (B) The central circle (r = 2 mm) was defined as the ROI for film analysis on the red channel image. A standardized region‐of‐interest (ROI) localization protocol was implemented to ensure consistent analysis across all films. First, a 42.4 mm × 42.4 mm outer square was centered on the maximum dose region, followed by placement of perpendicular reference lines through the square's center. A circular ROI (radius = 2 mm) was then positioned at the intersection of these reference lines. This systematic approach ensured accurate and reproducible ROI positioning while maintaining consistency across all film analyses. Mean dose and standard deviation within each ROI were recorded.

The experimental setup was modeled in Eclipse TPS (Version 16.1, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) using previously validated solid applicator models. 11 , 12 TPS‐calculated doses (excluding transit dose contributions) were compared with corresponding film‐measured doses to establish baseline agreement and validate the measurement methodology.

Transit dose determination

Transit dose values were determined using linear extrapolation analysis performed with Origin software (version 2021b). For each source activity, film‐measured doses (averaged within the central circular ROI of radius = 2 mm) were plotted as a function of nominal dwell time across seven data points (5, 10, 15, 30, 50, 80, and 100 seconds).

Linear regression analysis was performed using Origin's built‐in fitting algorithms, with the regression line extrapolated to zero dwell time. The y‐intercept of this extrapolation represents the transit dose, as this corresponds to the dose delivered when no stationary dwell occurs. The standard error of the transit dose (y‐intercept) was automatically calculated by Origin's linear fitting routine, providing uncertainty estimates for each transit dose determination.

This linear extrapolation procedure was systematically applied to all eight source activities spanning 3.372 to 9.716 Ci (13,724 to 39,544 U), yielding individual transit dose values for each activity. The complete dataset enabled investigation of transit dose behavior as a function of source activity. All transit dose results are reported as mean ± standard error, with the standard error reflecting the uncertainty in the linear extrapolation process.

Statistical analysis

Linear correlation between transit dose and source activity was evaluated using adjusted R2 values to assess the strength of the relationship. The adjusted R2 accounts for the number of parameters in the model, providing a more conservative measure of correlation quality than standard R2 values.

RESULTS

Film‐measured dose distribution

Representative dose distribution from EBT4 film analysis is presented in Figure 3 for the 9.543 Ci (38,840 U) source with 100‐second nominal dwell time. The film demonstrated uniform dose distribution with well‐defined spatial characteristics, including clear dose gradients from the center outward. Both vertical and horizontal dose profiles across the central axis showed symmetric distribution patterns, confirming proper source positioning and film alignment. Dose measurements were successfully obtained from all 56 irradiated films (8 source activities × 7 dwell times) from the same batch (lot number 07052201), ensuring consistent film response characteristics throughout the study.

Dose distribution on an example film irradiated by the source of 9.543 Ci (38,840 U) with 100‐s nominal dwell time. The film‐measured dose map and the vertical and horizontal dose profiles across the center are illustrated.

Linear relationship between delivered dose and nominal dwell time

Film‐measured dose demonstrated excellent linear correlation with nominal dwell time across all source activities. Figure 4 presents representative data from the 9.543 Ci source, showing the linear relationship between total delivered dose and nominal dwell time. Linear regression analysis yielded adjusted R2 values exceeding 0.99 for all eight source activities, confirming the validity of the linear extrapolation approach for transit dose determination. The high correlation coefficients validate the underlying assumption that total dose consists of linearly additive dwell and transit components.

Film‐measured dose versus nominal dwell time. Data from A = 9.543 Ci was used as an example. Linear fit was performed for the data. The intercept at 0 s was determined to be the transit dose at this source activity. Each measurement data point was expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The transit dose (intercept a, (Gy)) was expressed as mean ± standard error.

Transit dose characterization

Transit dose values derived from linear extrapolation are summarized in Table 1. Transit dose magnitudes ranged from 0.017 ± 0.025 Gy to 0.225 ± 0.040 Gy across the investigated source activities. When expressed as a percentage of TPS‐calculated dose (100‐second nominal dwell time at 10 Ci reference activity, without considering transit dose), transit dose contributions varied from 0.3% to 4.3%. The maximum transit dose contribution of 4.3% occurred with the 9.543 Ci source, while the minimum contribution of 0.3% was observed with the 5.502 Ci source. Notably, neither the maximum nor minimum transit dose contributions corresponded to the highest (9.716 Ci) or lowest (3.372 Ci) source activities, respectively.

Transit dose versus source activity relationship

The relationship between film‐derived transit dose and source activity is illustrated in Figure 5. Linear regression analysis revealed poor correlation (adjusted R2 = 0.497), indicating that transit dose cannot be reliably predicted from source activity alone. The linear fit extrapolated to zero source activity yielded a y‐intercept of ‐0.068 ± 0.060 Gy rather than the expected zero value, further confirming the inadequacy of a simple linear model for this relationship.

Film‐derived transit dose as a function of source activity from 3.372 to 9.716 Ci (13,724 to 39,544 U). The curve fit with a negative intercept does not suggest a well correlated linear relationship (adjusted R2 = 0.497). Each transit dose data point was expressed as mean ± standard error.

DISCUSSION

Validation of EBT4 film for transit dose measurement

This study successfully demonstrates the utility of GafChromic™ EBT4 film for transit dose measurement in surface brachytherapy applications. The excellent linear correlation (adjusted R2 > 0.99) between film‐measured dose and nominal dwell time across all source activities validates the fundamental assumption underlying transit dose determination methodology. The consistent film response and spatial resolution capabilities of EBT4 film make it well‐suited for the precise dose measurements required in transit dose studies.

Our transit dose findings are consistent with published literature for similar HDR‐BT surface applicator configurations. Jeong et al. reported 2.8% transit dose contribution for a 30‐second dwell time using the same RAU‐applicator combination. 9 Our observed range of 0.3% to 4.3% for 100‐second treatments encompasses this published value when scaled appropriately, providing confidence in our measurement methodology and results.

Complex relationship between transit dose and source activity

The poor linear correlation (adjusted R2 = 0.497) between transit dose and source activity represents a significant finding that challenges the commonly assumed linear relationship. Several factors are likely to contribute to this complexity.

First, the 192Ir source motion profile significantly impacts transit dose delivery. Figure 6 illustrates two different source speed patterns: (A) the realistic acceleration‐uniform speed‐deceleration profile, and (B) an idealized uniform speed pattern. According to Varian specifications, the source travels at 50 cm/s during uniform motion phases. For our 130 cm source guide tube length, this corresponds to approximately 2.6 seconds of transit time for one trip (RAU to target or target to RAU) and 5.2 seconds for the round trip. The complex motion profile with varying speeds during acceleration and deceleration phases creates a non‐uniform dose delivery pattern that differs from simple theoretical models assuming constant source velocity.

Source motion speed profiles during HDR brachytherapy source transit. (A) Realistic acceleration‐uniform speed‐deceleration profile and (B) Idealized uniform speed profile.

Second, source guide tube configuration critically affects transit dose magnitude and distribution. Figure 7 demonstrates the impact of guide tube geometry: (A) straight source guide tube configuration providing maximum source‐to‐target distance with minimum transit dose, (B) curved guide tube configuration with shortened effective distance and increased transit dose, and (C) an experimental setup with curved source guide tube between the RAU and the target location. Even minor variations in source guide tube configuration between measurements with different source activities can substantially impact transit dose. Source guide tube curvature directly affects the source‐to‐target distance during transit, with straighter configurations potentially resulting in longer transit paths and lower transit doses, while curved configurations may increase transit dose contributions. These geometric variations could introduce systematic differences that mask the expected linear relationship with source activity. A dynamic Monte Carlo simulation with the source motion in predefined curved path might be helpful to estimate the transit dose under complicated setup conditions.

Source guide tube geometry configurations: (A) Straight guide tube configuration, (B) Curved guide tube configuration and (C) Experimental setup showing curved source guide tube positioning.

Third, the relatively large error bars in Figure 5 associated with transit dose determinations (standard errors ranging from ±0.015 to ±0.091 Gy) suggest that measurement precision may limit the ability to detect subtle linear relationships. Statistical uncertainties in the linear extrapolation process could obscure underlying correlations between transit dose and source activity.

Clinical implications

The observed transit dose contributions of 0.3% to 4.3% have meaningful clinical implications for surface brachytherapy treatments. While these percentages may appear small, they represent absolute dose uncertainties that could impact treatment outcomes, particularly for shallow treatments where dose gradients are steep and normal tissue sparing is critical.

The non‐linear relationship between transit dose and source activity has important implications for clinical practice. Current approaches that assume proportional scaling of transit dose with source activity may introduce systematic errors in dose calculations. Our findings suggest that empirical measurement of transit dose for each source activity may be necessary for accurate dosimetric assessment, particularly when sources are replaced or have undergone substantial decay.

For quality assurance protocols, these results emphasize the importance of standardized setup procedures, particularly regarding source guide tube configuration. A straight source guide tube geometry is recommended to reduce the transit dose. Consistent positioning and geometry control can minimize setup‐dependent variations in transit dose, improving the reliability of treatment delivery.

Study limitations and future work

This study was limited to a single RAU‐applicator combination, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other HDR‐BT systems. Future investigations should include multiple RAU vendors such as the Elekta Flexitron and other applicator designs to establish broader applicability of these findings.

The observed non‐linear relationship between transit dose and source activity warrants further investigation with improved experimental controls. Future studies should focus on rigorous control of source guide tube geometry and enhanced measurement precision to better characterize this relationship. The complex interplay between source motion dynamics, source guide tube geometry, and dose delivery suggests that accurate Monte Carlo simulation studies would be valuable for quantifying transit dose contributions under various geometric and motion conditions. Such computational studies could provide detailed insights into the dose delivery patterns during acceleration, uniform motion, and deceleration phases, helping to separate the contributions of different factors to the overall transit dose.

Additionally, systematic investigation of source motion dynamics across different RAU systems and their impact on transit dose would provide valuable insights into the underlying physics governing transit dose behavior in HDR brachytherapy.

CONCLUSIONS

This study validates GafChromic™ EBT4 film as a reliable tool for transit dose measurement in HDR surface brachytherapy, with transit dose contributions ranging from 0.3% to 4.3% of treatment planning system calculated dose. The observed poor linear correlation between transit dose and source activity (adjusted R2 = 0.497) reveals that transit dose behavior is more complex than previously assumed, influenced by source guide tube geometry and motion dynamics.

For clinical practice, these findings recommend empirical transit dose measurement rather than theoretical scaling for source activity. Standardized and consistent setup protocols and quality assurance integration are essential for accurate dosimetric assessment in surface brachytherapy treatments.
