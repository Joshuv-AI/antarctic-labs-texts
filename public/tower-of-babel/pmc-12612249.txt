A comprehensive cross-sectional study identifying awareness of cardiovascular risk factors among staff and spouses participating in the Shiraz cohort heart study
Authors: Nader Parsa, Armin Attar, Mohammad Javad Zibaeenezhad, Ali Karimi-Akhormeh, Anahita Ahmadi, Maurizio Trevisan, Lisa Wallin, Pari Mahlagha Zaheri, Fatemeh Jafari, Mehrab Sayadi, Alireza Moaref, Iman Razeghian-Jahromi, Mohammad-Reza Paknezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the high prevalence of hypertension (HTN), hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus (DM) as risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and other chronic conditions in middle and older age, this study was designed to assess the prevalence of these risk factors among a subset of participants in the Shiraz cohort heart study (SCHS). The focus is on staff and spouses of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS) involved in the study in Shiraz, Iran. The aim is to determine the prevalence of these risk factors and evaluate their awareness of clinical symptoms that may not be apparent in the early stages of cardiovascular disease. It is hypothesized that some participants might be unaware of their cardiovascular risk factors and the potential impact on their health. This cross-sectional study analyzed data collected starting in 2018 from staff members and their spouses participating in the Shiraz cohort heart study which will go over a 10-year period. A systematic random sampling method, based on the university’s list, was employed. Data collection involves questionnaires and participant records, with statistical analysis conducted using SPSS software version 22. Of the 1191 study subjects who participated in the study, 53.6% (638) were males and 46.4% (553) were females and 89.9% (1071) were married. The age range was 25 to 72 years, and their mean and standard deviation of age was 44.29 ± 8.94 years. Among participants, lipid profile abnormalities related to total cholesterol were 37.5% (447), with low density lipids (LDL) 7.6%, (91), high density lipids (HDL) 46.9% (559), and triglycerides (TG) 17.6% (210) respectively. Furthermore, among study participants, hypertension abnormalities were 41.2% (491) with abnormal SBP, 41.8% (498), abnormal DBP, 52.6% (627) abnormal SBP or DBP and 22.3% (265) with both abnormal SBP and DBP. Regarding FBS and DM as risk factors for CVD, 5.4% (64) had abnormal FBS. The study identifies valuable results on the prevalence of elevated blood pressure, abnormally high lipid levels (cholesterol and triglycerides) and high blood sugar that can lead to HTN and DM. Furthermore, a significant proportion of participants were unaware they had abnormal blood pressure. The study also highlights a less-than-optimal adherence to the prescribed medication treatment regimens among people who were aware they had HTN and diabetes, which pinpoints the need for a robust public health campaign to enlighten the populous and assist health authorities in making appropriate screening decisions and interventions to reduce the burden of these types of diseases in the community.Keywords: Hypertension, Hyperlipidemia, Diabetes, Awareness, CVDSubject terms: Obesity, Dyslipidaemias, Hypertension, Diabetes, Dyslipidaemias, Obesity, Pre-diabetes

Introduction

Considering the high prevalence of lipid disorders in Iran and the confirmed relationship of this risk factor with cardiovascular diseases, the classification of people based on this risk factor has found a special place in the discussion of preventing cardiovascular events. Therefore, this study was designed and implemented to investigate the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as hyperlipidemia, diabetes and hypertension in the target population, specifically.

The 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study found that ischemic heart disease, stroke, and hypertensive heart disease are the first, second, and twenty-second leading causes of death in people aged 50 to 74, respectively1. The average age of death from coronary artery disease in developed countries is about 80 years, while in developing countries it is about 68 years2.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension (HTN) are important risk factors for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD)3. With a global prevalence of 8.5% in 2014, various estimates suggest that the number of people with diabetes will increase from 422 million to 642 million in the world by 20404. The exponential growth of diabetes can be attributed to the increasing proportion of the elderly population, socioeconomic development, urbanization, highly processed diets, and decreased physical activity5. In Iran, the prevalence of diabetes in adults aged 25–70 has been reported as 11.9% (2011), which shows a 35% increase compared to 2005. It is estimated that in 2030, nearly 9.2 million Iranians are likely to have diabetes6. Additionally, the prevalence of HTN has been reported in different regions, from 23.7% in Sri Lanka7 to 43.5% in Malaysia,8 and 26.2% in Iran9. A meta-analysis study found that reducing hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) by 0.9% may reduce cardiovascular events by 9%10 and lowering systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 10 mm Hg could reduce myocardial infarction by 12% and stroke events by 23%.11

If HTN is not diagnosed or treated, it can lead to uncontrolled BP and may lead to poor health outcomes12. In addition, many people with diabetes are unaware of complications from uncontrolled blood glucose levels4. A significant percentage of patients are unaware of their disease from 30% in Iran to 86% in Tunisia in the Middle East and from 24.1 to 75, 1% in other parts of the world13,14. Delayed DM diagnosis increases management cost and reduces disease prognosis15. Early detection of undiagnosed HTN can help prevent and slow the progression of the disease, which can lead to serious complications including stroke and heart disease16.

Therefore, in this study, it was decided to investigate awareness or lack of awareness of HTN, hyperlipidemia and diabetes as important risk factors for CVD in a subset of participants in the Shiraz cohort heart study (SCHS). The focus was on staff and spouses of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS) involved in the study in Shiraz, Iran.

Method

This cross-sectional study utilizes data from the Shiraz University Cohort Study (SCHS), which commenced in 2018. The study includes staff members and their spouses, if married, resulting in a sample size of 1191 participants who entered the study by systematic sampling according to staff list with a 100% response rate. This ongoing study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences and methods carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all study subjects and/or their legal guardians.

The study inclusion criteria were employees and spouses of the SUMS, resident of Shiraz and the surrounding urban areas for at least one recent year aged 40–70 years old.

The exclusion criteria included individuals who were unable to perform testing, answer questions, or attend the center, and these individuals were excluded from the study.

As per the criteria of the Shiraz cohort heart study, baseline measurements were carried out during enrollment and will be repeated five more times over 2-year intervals as follow-up. At baseline, the participants passed the steps in two phases. Every phase will be carried out in 1 day.

Phase 1

Diverse data was collected from the participants (Table 1). All the questionnaires were provided electronically with dedicated web-based software. An identification number was assigned to each participant. This number, as a digital barcode, was inserted in sampling accessories and was also shown on questionnaires. All questionnaires were filled in through trained interviewers.

Data collection using different methods at baseline.

The data collection and sampling methodology employed a self-administered questionnaire tailored for this research, comprising sections on demographic variables such as gender, age, marital status, educational attainment, and employment at the university. Additionally, it included items assessing participants’ awareness of diagnosed conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Medical records were reviewed to extract relevant clinical data. Blood pressure measurements were obtained using a standardized sphygmomanometer. Further biochemical assessments, such as fasting blood glucose and lipid profiles, were conducted to characterize participants’ metabolic status.

Blood samples were taken by a physician or nurse and were sent on the same day to the laboratory of Shiraz Healthy Heart House center for intended clinical tests. The identity of laboratory technicians was recorded as well for resolving any possible ambiguity.

To obtain blood pressure the participants were asked to relax for 10 min and the measurements were obtained from both arms and measured consecutively (OMRON). After 10 min, the measuring was repeated. This procedure was done for a third time, and the mean of the three measurements was recorded as the participant’s blood pressure.

All clinics were equipped with similar 12-lead, computer-based digital ECG machines. Automatic measurement of relevant parameters, monitoring the vectors in two-dimensional/three-dimensional format, the possibility of ECG comparison between subjects and automatic diagnosis by its software (Cardiax) were among the capabilities. The physician evaluated the ECG and it was further reviewed by a cardiologist to confirm the diagnosis.

The anthropometric indices measured included height, weight, wrist circumference, waist circumference, and hip circumference, which were the parameters recorded at this stage. Parameters were defined as follows:

On completion a membership card including information such as name, national identification, contact number, date of the next visit, phone numbers and address of the Cardiovascular Research Center which is the central office for management and coordination of the cohort program, and the probable existence of a specific disease were given to the participants. If a participant was not willing to continue the study, he/she was excluded and another matched from the same subgroup was randomly assigned.

Phase 2

Step 1: informing the participants about the results of the first phase.

Step 1: informing the participants about the results of the first phase.

In this session, the participants were informed about the Framingham risk assessment, laboratory tests results, blood pressure and ECG. Any probable warning or need for higher medical consultation or other prescriptions was given to them. This free health status screening provided a kind of encouragement for participants to commence and stay in the study.

2.Step 2: family and medical questionnaire.

Step 2: family and medical questionnaire.

The interview was conducted in a private setting, during which data collection forms were completed. These forms captured detailed medical histories, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) history, blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hospitalizations and their documented causes, smoking status, substance abuse, and sleep patterns. Additionally, information on the presence of thirteen other conditions—such as asthma, allergies, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic renal and liver disorders, hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, goiter, rheumatologic diseases, peptic ulcer, gastroesophageal reflux disease, gastritis, and cancer—was recorded. Participants were encouraged to bring their current or previous medications to ensure accurate documentation. The medical histories of first-degree relatives, especially concerning CVDs and associated risk factors, were also documented.

3.Step 3: nutritional habits questionnaire.

Step 3: nutritional habits questionnaire.

The original framework of nutritional habits form was based on the Food Frequency Questionnaire with some modifications based on Iranian and Shiraz native food patterns17. The eating habits of the participants were collected over a 1-year period on standard serving sizes. Information on daily energy and nutrient intake was extracted.

4.Step 4: physical activity questionnaire.

Step 4: physical activity questionnaire.

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to measure the physical activity of participants during the past 7 days. Activities in four different areas including work, commuting, housework, leisure time and also sitting time were collected18. The scores were either in categories (low, moderate and high) or as continuous variables (metabolic equivalent task [MET]).

5.Step 5: psychology questionnaire.

Step 5: psychology questionnaire.

The tool used in this step was a 28-item version of the General Health Questionnaire. It was employed to assess mental health status, identifying individuals with or at risk for psychiatric disorders19. The scores were in continuous measures or Likert scale.

Questionnaires were then focused on knowledge of cardiovascular disease and risk factors among the staff and their spouses. Data collection involved questionnaires and participant records, with statistical analysis conducted using SPSS software version 22. A systematic random sampling method, based on the university’s list, was employed.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive statistics section, mean ± SD were used for quantitative variables, and number (%) was used for qualitative variables. In the analytical part, the Chi-square test was used to compare the qualitative variables between different groups and t-test was used to compare the mean of variables between two groups. All tests were performed at a significance level of 5% in SPSS software version 22. Also, One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey post hoc test were also used. The level of significance in all tests was considered 0.05.

Discussion

Uncontrolled high blood pressure, diabetes and hyperlipidemia and are risk factors for cardiovascular diseases. Findings of this study identified the prevalence of participants unaware of the risks of high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia and diabetes among adults aged 25–72 years and its association with the risk factors of cardiovascular diseases in Shiraz University Cohort Heart Study Staff and their Spouses.

This study results are similar to previous studies of prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in study participants, the awareness of risk factors and the control and uncontrolled rates that identify gender disparities20–23.

The study aimed to assess the awareness and unawareness of risk factors for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes among staff and their spouses participating in the Shiraz University Cohort Heart Study in Shiraz, Iran. Additionally, it investigated the relationship between these risk factors and cardiovascular disease.

Based upon the study results, most of the people were male and married, and in terms of education, bachelor’s degree was the most frequent. The average age of people was lower than the subjects in this study in terms of marriage, as in this study, most people were married. Thus, study subjects may have entered at an older age due to their working and educational conditions, in terms of marriage status, were expected to be married after a few years of work experience and having an income.

In this present study, of all those who were aware of their HTN, 86.6% still had abnormal blood pressure and only 13.4% had controlled blood pressure. However, in a study done in Nepal, 44% who were aware they had HTN were controlled21. It is worrying that despite the availability of inexpensive drugs for the management of HTN, the overall treatment and control rates are not satisfactory. Thus, treatment efforts may be hindered by low perceived risks of hypertension, low health literacy, lack of motivation, and medication costs in low-resource settings. Additionally, in this present study, 48.6% of new hypertensive subjects were diagnosed, which means they were previously unaware about their disease.

This study found that diabetes and hypertension were important risk factors for developing cardiovascular diseases especially if the population were unaware, they have this disease or are aware, but not well controlled. Similarly, a study in India also found a significant rise in the level of diabetes and hypertension from two study periods from 2004 to 05 to 2011–12 that placed them at risk of the later development of cardiovascular diseases22. Comparatively, in a study conducted in the United States between 2011 and 2016, 32.8% had diagnosed and 4.5% had undiagnosed hypertension and a total of 10.0% had diabetes diagnosed and 2.0% were undiagnosed23.

However, in this study, 61.5% of people with diabetes were controlled subjects, while it was less in another study24. The very low control rate of type-2 diabetes suggests that intensive interventions and increased clinical attention should be initiated among diabetics to reduce blood glucose levels urgently.

Analysis of glycemic and lipid status indicated that a significant proportion of participants had abnormal levels of CVD risk factors. For example, 37.5% had high cholesterol, 46.9% had low HDL, 7.6% had high LDL, 17.6% had high TG, and 5.4% had abnormal FBS levels. These findings emphasize the need for preventive measures and effective management strategies to control these risk factors among university employees. In China, the prevalence of hyperlipidemia was reported to be 49.8%, as well25.

In this study, we concluded that people with uncontrolled blood pressure have the highest level of FBS, which was contrary to a previous study, which showed that those with diabetes have a higher chance of controlled blood pressure26. The reason can be that diabetics who are most at risk are expected to be more concerned about their health status27.

Prior studies also showed that women have more controlled blood pressure than men, which was in line with our results26. Regarding diabetes, like this study, another study also demonstrated that women have more controlled diabetes than men28. One of the reasons that more men have undiagnosed and uncontrolled HTN and more women have controlled HTN is that women are more apt to seek out and thus receive more health care services29.

Contrary to this study, where the highest level of high cholesterol was in people unaware of their HTN, in the study of Park et al., people who were aware and treated their HTN had a higher cholesterol level30. In addition, unlike our study that the highest levels of LDL and TG were found in people unaware of their HTN and the lowest levels of HDL were in people with uncontrolled HTN, the study conducted by Rajati and colleagues found that those with dyslipidemia were more aware, treated and controlled31. According to the results of the previous studies, those who have hyperlipidemia or HTN have less awareness, treatment and control of diabetes, which is in line with the results of our study that people who are not aware of their disease have higher Cholesterol, TG, LDL, HDL, SBP and DBP32,33. Perhaps the reason blood lipids are higher in people unaware and with uncontrolled hypertension and diabetes is that these people do not undergo periodic health checks due to lack of awareness of the disease and subsequently have poor diet and physical activity, but people who are aware about their disease constantly check their blood factors and blood pressure.

Study strengths and limitations

Study data was collected based upon strict quality assurance (QA) quality control (QC), testing which ensures a good representation of the study population due to the accuracy, validity, and reliability of data. Moreover, strengths of the current study can be attributed to the low bias, valid and reliable data recording with highly accurate data with QA-QC and testing considering that the information was first filled in the standard questionnaire form by the study population. Subsequently, well-trained experts input the electronic files following a standardized guideline designed to minimize bias in the study.

One notable aspect of this research was the utilization of cardiovascular risk factors among staff and their spouses in the Shiraz University Cohort Heart Study. The data demonstrated a geographically normal distribution, which was achieved through the application of specific inclusion criteria, resulting in minimal dropout rates.

However, the study may have limitations in that the inclusion criteria only enrolled persons aged 40–70 years old with cut points ranges, regardless of prevalence of dyslipidemia in younger or older indicated age ranges of the study participants. In addition, the age and gender distributions of participants in this study are comparable with those in the entire population in Shiraz metropolis, possible potential selection bias cannot be ruled out.

This baseline cohort study, from which this cross-sectional analysis was derived, may have inherent limitations due to its design. Caution is advised when interpreting the results, as subjects were selected based on the availability of laboratory measurements. This selection criterion could affect the generalizability of the findings to the broader population.

Thus, this study was conducted based on a population-based randomized controlled method with strict QA-QC and testing, which ensures a good representation of the study population, and the reliability of collected data.

In terms of limitations, the study relied on self-reported data that could be susceptible to recall bias. In addition, the study focused on a specific community that may limit the application of the findings to other populations or regions. Ultimately, the study did not consider some potential distortion factors, such as lifestyle habits or genetic talents that could affect observed outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into the population studied and serves as a basis for further research and interventions in addressing hypertension, hyperlipidemia and diabetes-related issues.
