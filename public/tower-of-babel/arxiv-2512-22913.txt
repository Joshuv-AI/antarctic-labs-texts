Quasi-continuum descriptions of rarefaction and dispersive shock waves in Fermi-Pasta-Ulam lattices with Hertzian potentials
Su Yang

Abstract
In the present work, we review two well-established quasi-continuum models of a Fermi-Pasta-Ulam lattice with Hertzian type potentials, and utilize these two models to approximate the discrete dispersive shock waves (DDSWs) which are numerically observed in the simulation of the lattice. To perform analysis on the various characteristics of the DDSW, we analytically derive the Whitham modulation equations of the two quasi-continuum models, which govern the slowly varying spatial and temporal dynamics of distinct parameters of the periodic solutions. We then perform a very useful reduction of the Whitham modulation system to gain a system of initial-value problems whose solutions can provide important insights on edge features of the DSWs such as their edge speeds. In addition, we also study the numerical rarefaction waves (RWs) of the lattice based on the two quasi-continuum models. In particular, we analytically compute and compare their self-similar solutions with the numerical discrete RW of the lattice. These comparisons made for both DSWs and RWs reveal to be reasonably good, which suggest the impressive performance of both quasi-continuum models.



Keywords:
FPU lattices, Quasi-continuum approximations, Rarefaction waves, Dispersive shock waves, DSW fitting

Introduction

Nonlinear waves are ubiquitous in various physics settings. Among these various nonlinear wave phenomena, the so-called dispersive shock wave
[
16
]
is defined as a non-stationary disperive wave structure which has raised considerably amount of interests recently. In particular, this wave structure is studied and numerically observed in lots of mathematical physics models including the well-known Toda lattice
[
5
,
25
,
11
,
23
]
, the nonlinear Schrödinger models
[
21
,
29
,
9
,
15
]
, and some discrete lattices
[
27
,
30
,
24
,
28
]
. Moreover, besides its numerical emergence, the DSW can also be discovered in physics experimental environments
[
22
,
14
,
19
]
. Importantly, the core of the DSW is the periodic solutions associated with the model of interest, and the analysis of DSW needs to be performed based on the so-called Whitham modulation theory
[
26
,
1
,
4
,
3
,
2
]
, which reveals the evolution dynamics of the slowly-varying parameters of the periodic solutions. The DSW-fitting method
[
13
]
leads to a reduction of the Whitham modulation system, which then yields a system of simple-wave ordinary differential equations which encode the important information on multiple edge characteristics of the DSWs. The rarefaction waves, on the other hand, are classified as a simple wave
[
26
,
8
]
. For this specific wave structure, dispersive models are also prototypical media for its numerical emergence (e.g. the standard Korteweg-De Vries (KdV) equation
[
13
]
). Moreover, RWs can be analytically described by the self-similar solutions of the associated dispersionless version of the dispersive models, and this therefore provides a methodology for the analysis of the numerical RWs.

In the present work, we shall study both the DSW and RW observed in a FPU-type lattice. Previously, Ref.
[
11
]
has proposed two integrable quasi-continuum models which are KdV and Toda equations to approximate only the DSW numerically observed in a granular chain. Instead, other works including
[
27
,
28
]
provide bi-directional and non-integrable uni-directional quasi-continuum models to approximate discrete DSWs in the granular crystal lattice. The present work shall analogously apply quasi-continuum approximations for the discrete nonlinear dispersive waves in the lattice, but however via two ”non-standard’ models: The so-called log-KdV equation and generalized-KdV equation with a Hölder continuous nonlinearity.

This paper is structured as follows. In section

6

7

8

2
Model description and theoretical setup

In this work, we study the so-called Fermi-Pasta-Ulam (FPU) lattice whose equation of motion can be described as the following second-order differential-difference equations (DDEs),

\frac{d^{2}x_{n}}{dt^{2}}=V^{\prime}\left(x_{n+1}-x_{n}\right)-V^{\prime}\left(x_{n}-x_{n-1}\right),

(2.1)

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
x_{n}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
refers to the displacement of the
n
th particle at time
t
, and
V
is a potential function governing the pairwise interaction between the particles.

For technical convenience, it is more preferable to write the DDEs in Eq. (
2.1
) in terms of the relative displacement (aka. strains)
u_{n}=x_{n}-x_{n-1}
so that Eq. (
2.1
) becomes,

\frac{d^{2}u_{n}}{dt^{2}}=V^{\prime}(u_{n+1})-2V^{\prime}(u_{n})+V^{\prime}(u_{n-1}).

(2.2)

In this paper, we are interested in the following homogeneous fully nonlinear potential function,

V(x)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\left|x\right|^{1+\alpha},

(2.3)

where
\alpha>1
.

With such potential function of
V
in Eq. (
2.3
), the equation of motion at the level of the relative displacement can be further written as follows,

\frac{d^{2}u_{n}}{dt^{2}}=\Delta\left(u_{n}|u_{n}|^{\alpha-1}\right),

(2.4)

where
u_{n}(t)\leq 0
,
\alpha>1
denotes a parameter, and
\Delta\left[f\right]\equiv f_{n+1}-2f_{n}+f_{n-1}
is the discrete Laplace operator. We note that in this paper, without loss of generality, we are only interested in the scenario where
u_{n}(t)
is negative for all
n
.

The lattice (
2.4
) shall be main focus of this present work, and we claim it is a dispersive model. To see this, we look for a plane wave solution in the form of
u_{n}(t)=\overline{u}+a\text{e}^{i(kn-\omega t)}
and we substitute it into the lattice (
2.4
) to obtain the following two-branch linear dispersion relation, upon eliminating the small parameter
0<a\ll 1
,

\omega(\overline{u},k)=\pm 2\sqrt{\alpha}|\overline{u}|^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}\sin\left(\frac{k}{2}\right).

(2.5)

Obviously, the linear dispersion relation (
2.5
) does not always have zero derivative with respect to the wavenumber
k
, so the lattice in Eq. (
2.4
) is a discrete dispersive model. Since the main goal of this work is to study the DSWs of the lattice (
2.4
), we equip the DDEs in (
2.4
) with the following so-called Riemann initial data,

u_{n}(0)=\begin{cases}u_{-},\quad n\leq 0,\\
u_{+},\quad n>0,\end{cases}\quad v_{n}(0)=\begin{cases}v_{-},\quad n\leq 0,\\
v_{+},\quad n>0.\end{cases}

(2.6)

where
v_{n}(t):=du_{n}/dt
, and
u_{\pm},v_{\pm}
are four real constants referring to the homogeneous background values. Figures
1
and
2
depict the dynamics of the lattice (
2.4
) subject to the ICs (
2.6
), where an initial upward jump leads to the DSW, while a downward jump yields a RW.

Next, we review two quasi-continuum models proposed in Ref.
[
17
]
. To this end, as
\alpha\to 1^{+}
, we introduce the following change of variables,

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)\sim v(\xi,\tau);

(2.7)

\displaystyle\xi=2\sqrt{3}\epsilon\left(n-t\right),\quad\tau=\sqrt{3}\epsilon^{3}t,

where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a formal smallness parameter which depends on
\alpha
and is to be determined.

Now setting
\epsilon=\sqrt{\alpha-1}\ll 1
, and substituting the ansatz (
2.7
) into the Eq. (
2.4
) yields the following log-KdV equation
[
17
]
, by collecting terms at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})
,

v_{\tau}+\left(v\ln|v|\right)_{\xi}+v_{\xi\xi\xi}=0,

(2.8)

We then compute the linear dispersion relation of Eq. (
2.8
) by assuming the following plane-wave ansatz:

v(\xi,\tau)=\overline{v}+a\exp\left[i(k\xi-\omega\tau)\right],

(2.9)

where
0<a\ll 1
denotes a smallness parameter. We note that the plane-wave in Eq. (
2.9
) has the background
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{R}
.

A direct substitution of Eq. (
2.9
) into the log-KdV equation eq. (
2.8
) yields the following linear dispersion relation, upon eliminating
a
,

\omega(\overline{v},k)=\left(1+\ln\left|\overline{v}\right|\right)k-k^{3}.

(2.10)

Moreover, a generalized KdV equation with Hölder-continuous nonlinearity can also be derived as an approximation for Eq. (
2.4
):

v_{\tau}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(v-v|v|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)_{\xi}+v_{\xi\xi\xi}=0.

(2.11)

For Eq. (
2.11
), we similarly compute its linear dispersion relation by plugging the plane-wave ansatz (
2.9
) into Eq. (
2.11
), and eliminating the smallness parameter
a
to obtain that,

\omega(\overline{v},k)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|\overline{v}\right|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)k-k^{3}.

(2.12)

In work
[
17
]
, the two quasi-continuum models are proposed to approximate the discrete solitary wave of the granular crystal lattice (
2.4
). However, since our purpose is to apply them to approximate the discrete DSW numerically observed in the lattice (
2.4
), it is hence still necessary to conduct such throughout reviews on the derivations of these relevant models. In addition, we observe that the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
), in principle, are valid whenever
\alpha
is close to
1
. Regarding this observation, we mainly concentrate on the scenario of
\alpha=1.1
throughout this work.

3
Traveling solitary-wave solutions

In this section, we review the solitary-wave solutions of the two models of Eq. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
). To this end, we assume the solitary-wave solution in the form,

v(\xi,\tau)=V(z),\quad z=\xi-c\tau,

(3.1)

where
c
is a real constant referring to the speed of propagation of the traveling solitary wave.

Log-KdV solitary wave:

Substituting the anstaz in Eq. (
3.1
) into Eq. (
2.8
) yields,

-cV_{z}+\left(V\ln|V|\right)_{z}+V_{zzz}=0.

(3.2)

Assuming that
\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}V(z)=0
, a direct integrating of Eq. (
3.2
) yields the following traveling solitary-wave solution
[
17
]
,

V(z)=-\text{e}^{1/2+c}\text{e}^{-z^{2}/4}.

(3.3)

Figure
3
shows the spatial profile of this Gaussian-profile solitary wave (
3.3
) and also its associated space-time evolution dynamics.

Figure 3:
The Gaussian ”dark” solitary wave in Eq. (
3.3
). The left panel
(a)
depicts the spatial profile of the dark solitary wave at
\tau=0
, while the right panel
(b)
displays the space-time evolution dynamics of such solitary wave with a speed of propagation
c=0.5
.

Generalized-KdV solitary wave:

On the other hand, for the generalized-KdV equation (
2.11
), substitution of the ansatz (
3.1
) into Eq. (
2.11
) yields,

-cV_{z}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(V-V|V|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)_{z}+V_{zzz}=0.

(3.4)

Again we suppose
\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}V(z)=0
, and then we integrate Eq. (
3.4
) to obtain the following solitary-wave solution
[
17
]
,

V(z)=-\left(1-\mu\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}F\left[\sqrt{1-\mu}z\right];\quad\mu=c\left(1-1/\alpha\right),

(3.5)

where

F(\zeta)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{A}\cos^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}\left(\widetilde{B}\zeta\right);\quad|\zeta|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\widetilde{B}},\\
0,\quad|\zeta|\geq\frac{\pi}{2\widetilde{B}},\end{cases}

(3.6)

and

\widetilde{A}=\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2\alpha}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)},\quad\widetilde{B}=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha-1}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}.

(3.7)

We finally end this section of reviewing the solitary-wave solutions with a note. It is very important to realize that these solitary-wave solutions (Eqs. (
3.3
) and (
3.5
)) play an essential role in the DSW as it is expected that the profile of the solitonic edge of the DSW ought to agree with those of their associated solitary waves.

4
Periodic solutions

In this section, we discuss briefly the existence of the periodic solutions to the two quasi-continuum models. To the best of knowledge, it is very unfortunate that these periodic solutions are not amenable to analytical treatment. However, it shall be useful to visualize the potential curves of the associated co-traveling frame ODE to understand their existence.

On the one hand, for the log-KdV equation, upon integrating the Eq. (
3.2
), we obtain that,

\left(V_{z}\right)^{2}=\left(c-\ln|V|+\frac{1}{2}\right)V^{2}+2AV+B,

(4.1)

where
A,B
are two constants of integration.

Analogously, for the generalized-KdV equation (
2.11
), we integrate Eq. (
3.4
) to arrive at,

\left(V_{z}\right)^{2}=\left(c-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)V^{2}+\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{(\alpha-1)(\alpha+1)}|V|^{1/\alpha+1}+2EV+F,

(4.2)

where
E,F
are two constants of integration.

We shall visualize the potential curves associated with Eqs. (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) to analyze the type of distinct traveling-wave solutions of the log-KdV (
2.8
) and generalized-KdV (

) equations. In particular, the potential curves are:

\displaystyle P_{\text{log-KdV}}(V)=-\left(c-\ln|V|+\frac{1}{2}\right)V^{2}-2AV-B,

(4.3)

\displaystyle P_{\text{g-KdV}}(V)=-\left(c-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)V^{2}-\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{(\alpha-1)(\alpha+1)}\left|V\right|^{1/\alpha+1}-2EV-F.

(4.4)

5
Conservation laws and Lagrangian densities

In this section, we list some important conservation laws of the two quasi-continuum models. The purpose of mentioning these necessary conservation laws is that they shall be the prerequisite of the derivation of Whitham modulation equations. In particular, we will utilize the so-called averaging the conservation laws approach (see section
6
for details) for such derivation. Meanwhile, we mention, in addition, the corresponding Lagrangian densities of the two quasi-continuum models since they can also be very useful in the derivation of the modulation system despite through another method entitled to averaging the Lagrangian.

Log-KdV conservation laws:

For the log-KdV equation, it admits the following conservation of
mass
and
momentum
,

\displaystyle v_{\tau}+\left(v\ln\left|v\right|+v_{\xi\xi}\right)_{\xi}=0,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(v^{2}\right)_{\tau}+\left(vv_{\xi\xi}+\frac{v^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\ln\left|v\right|-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right)_{\xi}=0,

generalized-KdV conservation laws:

For the generalized-KdV equation,

\displaystyle v_{\tau}+\left[\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(v-v\left|v\right|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)+v_{\xi\xi}\right]_{\xi}=0,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(v^{2}\right)_{\tau}+\left[vv_{\xi\xi}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\left|v\right|^{1/\alpha+1}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]_{\xi}=0.

In addition, it is relevant to notice that the two quasi-continuum models of Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) conserve the energy as well:

E(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(v_{\xi}\right)^{2}\text{d}\xi-\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(v)\text{d}\xi,

(5.3)

where
W(v)=\frac{v^{2}}{2}\left(\ln|v|-\frac{1}{2}\right)
for Eq. (
2.8
), and
W(v)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}|v|^{1/\alpha+1}\right)
for Eq. (
2.11
). However, since we will not need the energy conservation law for the derivation of the Whitham modulation equations (See Section
6
), we omit the associated fluxes for brevity.

Furthermore, we notice that the two quasi-continuum models Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) both admit Lagrangian structures with the following closed form Lagrangian densities,

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\psi_{\xi}\right|\left(\psi_{\xi}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(\psi_{\xi}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{\xi\xi}\right)^{2},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{\xi}\psi_{\tau}+\frac{\alpha}{2\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\psi_{\xi}\right)^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(\alpha-1)(\alpha+1)}\left|\psi_{\xi}\right|^{1/\alpha+1}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{\xi\xi}\right)^{2},

respectively.

6
Whitham modulation equations

We now derive the Whitham modulation equations which shall govern the slowly-varying spatial and temporal dynamics of the parameters of the periodic solutions of the quasi-continuum models. Firstly, it is important to observe that both quasi-continuum models of Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) are PDEs of order
3
, so this suggests that there are three parameters in the periodic solutions of both models. Hence, we need three equations to form a closed modulation system which governs the slow spatial and temporal evolution dynamics of those parameters. To compute the modulation equations, we first assume a slowly modulated wave in the following form,

v(\xi,\tau)=\varphi(\theta;X,T)+\delta v_{1}\left(\theta;X,T\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),\quad 0<\delta\ll 1,

(6.1)

where
X=\delta\xi
and
T=\delta\tau
are two slow variables, and
\theta=S(X,T)/\epsilon
is an associated fast phase that satisfies, via the chain rule,

\theta_{\xi}=S_{X}=k(X,T),\quad\theta_{\tau}=S_{T}=-\omega(X,T).

(6.2)

We also note that the functions
\varphi(\theta;X,T)
and
v_{1}(\theta;X,T)
in Eq. (
6.1
) are assumed to be periodic solutions with a fixed spatial period with respect to the fast phase of
\theta
. Based on the section
4
, we know the analytical form of the periodic solutions are not obtainable, but they exist, of course, due to the phase-plane analysis performed (See Figure
4
). Hence, it is reasonable to expect that in principle, one has the freedom to fix the period of the periodic solution of
\varphi(\theta)
to any arbitrary value via a process of reparametrization (See
[
16
]
for an example on the reparametrization of the periodic solution of the standard KdV equation). For this reason, we assume the period of
\varphi(\theta)
to be
2\pi
in
\theta
.

What follows immediately from the definition of the fast phase
\theta
in Eq. (
6.2
) is that the compatibility condition of
S_{XT}=S_{TX}
leads to the following equation,

k_{T}+\omega_{X}=0,

(6.3)

which is the so-called
conservation of waves
, and it is the first modulation equation within the closed modulation system. To find the remaining modulation equations, we shall apply the method of averaging the conservation laws
[
12
,
13
]
. To this end, we first define the following average operation: For a given function
F
,

\overline{F(\phi)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\phi)\text{d}\theta.

(6.4)

Log-KdV modulation equations:

For the log-KdV equation (
2.8
), we apply the average operation defined in Eq. (
6.4
) to the two conservation laws in Eq. (
5.1
) and collect terms on the distinct order of
\delta
to obtain that,

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi\ln\left|\phi\right|}\right)_{X}=\mathcal{O}(\delta),

(6.5)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}\right)_{T}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\overline{\phi^{2}}+\overline{\phi^{2}\ln\left|\phi\right|}-3k^{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}=\mathcal{O}(\delta).

We then drop all the terms at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
and higher to arrive at,

\displaystyle k_{T}+\omega_{X}=0,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi\ln\left|\phi\right|}\right)_{X}=0,

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}\right)_{T}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\overline{\phi^{2}}+\overline{\phi^{2}\ln\left|\phi\right|}-3k^{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}=0.

Generalized-KdV modulation equations:

Similarly, for the generalized-KdV equation (
2.11
), we applied the average operation to its conservation laws in Eq. (
5.2
) to obtain at, at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
,

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{T}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\overline{\phi}-\overline{\phi\left|\phi\right|^{1/\alpha-1}}\right)_{X}=\mathcal{O}(\delta),

(6.7)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}\right)_{T}+\left[\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\overline{\left|\phi\right|^{1/\alpha-1}}\right)-3k^{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right]_{X}=\mathcal{O}(\delta)

Ignoring terms at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
then yields,

\displaystyle k_{T}+\omega_{X}=0,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{T}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\overline{\phi}-\overline{\phi\left|\phi\right|^{1/\alpha-1}}\right)_{X}=0,

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}\right)_{T}+\left[\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\overline{\left|\phi\right|^{1/\alpha-1}}\right)-3k^{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right]_{X}=0.

We note that the system (
6.6
) and (
6.8
) represent the final complete closed system of modulation equations for the log-KdV (
2.8
) and generalized-KdV (
2.11
) model, respectively. Meanwhile, we should end this section with a few comments. Firstly, it is worthwhile to mention that averaging the conservation laws is not the only methodology for the derivation of the Whitham modulation equations, one can also apply the method of multiple scale expansion
[
13
]
or averaging the Lagrangian
[
27
]
. But, these three methods are expected to be equivalent in the sense that the final modulation system derived through either method should coincide. For an illustrative purpose, we also apply the method of averaging the Lagrangian to derive the full modulation system, and we leave the details to appendix
Appendix
for interested readers.

7
Reduction of the modulation equations

In this section, we discuss how the Whitham modulation system derived in section
6
can be reduced to the so-called simple-wave ordinary differential equation which encodes the important information on the edge features of the DSW at both its linear and solitonic edge. We note, however, that such reduction can only be performed at either the harmonic or the solitonic limit of the modulation equations. Since the derivation of the simple-wave ODE is similar for both modulation system of the two quasi-continuum models, we only show the details of the derivation for the log-KdV modulation system.

At the harmonic limit, it is expected that the frequency
\omega
simply asymptotes to the corresponding linear dispersion relation, denoted as
\omega_{0}
, of the model. Moreover, the latter two modulation equations in Eq. (
6.6
) shall reduce into one equation, so that the modulation system now becomes,

\displaystyle k_{T}+(\omega_{0})_{X}=0,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\phi}\right)_{T}+\left(\overline{\phi}\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|\right)_{X}=0.

We note that, to obtain the system (
7.1
), we have also two facts at the harmonic limit:
(a)
The term
\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}
asymptotes to
0
and
(b)
:

\overline{F\left(\phi\right)}=F\left(\overline{\phi}\right).

(7.2)

These two facts can attribute to the small amplitude oscillation of the wave, which occurs at the linear edge of the DSW (See
[
12
]
for more elaborations on the two facts). Then, we cast the system (
7.1
) into the following matrix form,

\begin{bmatrix}k\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}_{T}+\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{k}\omega_{0}&\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\omega_{0}\\
0&1+\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}k\\
\overline{\phi}\end{bmatrix}_{X}=\vec{0},

(7.3)

and notice that coefficient matrix possesses the left eigenpair:
\left(\lambda,\vec{v}\right)
, where

\lambda=\partial_{k}\omega_{0},\quad\vec{v}=\left[1+\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|-\partial_{k}\omega_{0},-\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\omega_{0}\right].

(7.4)

We then multiply this left eigenvector on both sides of Eq. (
7.3
) to obtain the following characteristic form,

\left(1+\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}\right)\frac{dk}{dT}-\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\omega_{0}\frac{d\overline{\phi}}{dT}=0,

(7.5)

which can be further simplified into,

\frac{dk}{d\overline{\phi}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\omega_{0}}{1+\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}}.

(7.6)

Eq. (
7.6
) is the so-called simple-wave ODE mentioned before, and we moreover notice that it needs to be equipped with the condition that
k\left(v_{+}\right)=0
since the linear-edge wavenumber at the solitonic edge of the DSW has to be
0
. Thus, the simple-wave ODE in Eq. (
7.6
) with the constraint that
k(v_{+})=0
forms an initial-value problem.

On the other hand, at the soliton limit of the modulation system, it will be much more computationally friendly to introduce the following conjugate dispersion relation
[
12
]
,

\widetilde{\omega}_{s}(\overline{\phi},\widetilde{k})=-i\omega_{0}\left(\overline{\phi},i\widetilde{k}\right),

(7.7)

where
\widetilde{k}
denotes the conjugate wavenumber. Then, a similar argument leads to the following simple-wave ODE,

\frac{d\widetilde{k}}{d\overline{\phi}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{1+\ln\left|\overline{\phi}\right|-\partial_{\widetilde{k}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}},\quad\widetilde{k}(v_{-})=0.

(7.8)

As a final note before we end this section, we shall solve these two IVPs in Eq. (
7.6
) and (
7.8
) in section
9
in order to gain insights and theoretical predictions on various DSW edge features such as the linear and solitonic egde speeds. The reductions of the Whitham modulation system at the harmonic and soliton limits is entitled to the
DSW fitting
method.

Rarefaction waves

In this section, we analytically compute the self-similar solutions associated with the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) which shall serve as approximations to the rarefaction waves observed numerically in the granular lattice (
2.4
). To this end, we assume the following self-similar ansatz,

v(\xi,\tau)=S(\kappa),\quad\kappa=\frac{\xi}{\tau}.

(8.1)

Log-KdV RW:

For the log-KdV equation (
2.8
), we notice that its dispersionless limit reads,

v_{\tau}+\left(v\ln|v|\right)_{\xi}=0,

(8.2)

which is subject to the downward step initial condition:

v(\xi,0)=\begin{cases}v_{-},\quad\xi\leq 0\\
v_{+},\quad\xi>0,\end{cases}

(8.3)

where
v_{-}>v_{+}
.

A direct substitution of the self-similar ansatz of Eq. (
8.1
) into Eq. (
8.2
) yields,

-\kappa S_{\kappa}+\left(S\ln|S|\right)_{\kappa}=0.

(8.4)

Then, solving Eq. (
8.4
) for
S
yields the following self-similar solution,

v(\xi,\tau)=\begin{cases}v_{-};\quad\xi\leq\left(1+\ln|v_{-}|\right)\tau,\\
-\exp\left(\xi/\tau-1\right);\quad\left(1+\ln|v_{-}|\right)\tau<\xi\leq\left(1+\ln|v_{+}|\right)\tau,\\
v_{+};\quad\xi>\left(1+\ln|v_{+}|\right)\tau\end{cases}

(8.5)

To compare analytical self-similar solution in Eq. (
8.5
) with the discrete RW of the granular lattice Eq. (
2.4
), we transform the spatial and temporal coordinates
(\xi,\tau)
back into
(n,t)
so that,

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=v(n,t)=\begin{cases}v_{-},\quad n\leq b_{-}(t),\\
-\exp\left(\frac{2(n-t)}{\epsilon^{2}t}-1\right),\quad b_{-}(t)<n\leq b_{+}(t),\\
v_{+},\quad n>b_{+}(t),\end{cases}

(8.6)

where

b_{\pm}(t)=t+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}t\left(1+\ln|v_{\pm}|\right).

(8.7)

Generalized-KdV RW:

Similarly, for the generalized-KdV equation, we notice that its corresponding dispersionless system is given as follows,

v_{\tau}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(v-v|v|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)_{\xi}=0.

(8.8)

Then, substitution of the ansatz Eq. (
8.1
) into Eq. (
8.8
) yields,

-\kappa S_{\kappa}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(S-S|S|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)_{\kappa}=0.

(8.9)

Solving for
S
in Eq. (
8.9
) yields,

v(\xi,\tau)=\begin{cases}v_{-},\quad\xi\leq\frac{\alpha-|v_{-}|^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\tau,\\
-\left[\alpha-\left(\alpha-1\right)\frac{\xi}{\tau}\right]^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)},\quad\frac{\alpha-|v_{-}|^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\tau<\xi\leq\frac{\alpha-|v_{+}|^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\tau,\\
v_{+},\quad\xi>\frac{\alpha-|v_{+}|^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\tau.\end{cases}

(8.10)

We then transform the self-similar solution in Eq. (
8.10
) to the spatial and temporal coordinates of
(n,t)
to obtain that,

u_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}v_{-};\quad n\leq c_{-}(t),\\
-\left[\alpha-\frac{2(\alpha-1)(n-t)}{\epsilon^{2}t}\right]^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)};\quad c_{-}(t)<n\leq c_{+}(t),\\
v_{+};\quad n>c_{+}(t),\end{cases}

(8.11)

where

c_{\pm}(t)=t+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}t\left(\frac{\alpha-|v_{\pm}|^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\right).

(8.12)

9
DSW fitting

In this section, we apply the methodology of DSW fitting in order to gain some insights on the edge features of the DSW of the quasi-continuum models. In particular, we shall make analytical and quantitative predictions on the DSW characteristics including the edge speeds and wavenumber.

The DSW-fitting method
[
12
]
essentially boils down to a system of
simple-wave
ordinary differential equations (ODEs) which encode the information of the wavenumber at the two edges of the DSW. On the one hand, we recall from section
7
that near the linear edge of the DSW, the wavenumber
k
shall satisfy the following initial-value problem (IVP) which is reiterated here for convenience,

\frac{dk}{d\overline{v}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{v}}\omega_{0}}{A(\overline{v})-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}},\quad k(v_{+})=0.

(9.1)

where
\omega_{0}
denotes the linear dispersion relation of the model, and we will specify the definition of
A(\overline{v})
later.

On the other hand, we also recall from section
7
that near the solitonic edge of the DSW, the so-called conjugate wavenumber, denoted by
\widetilde{k}
, should fulfill the following simple-wave ODE,

\frac{d\widetilde{k}}{d\overline{v}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{v}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{A(\overline{v})-\partial_{\widetilde{k}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}},\quad\widetilde{k}(v_{-})=0,

(9.2)

where
\widetilde{\omega}_{s}(\overline{v},\widetilde{k})=-i\omega_{0}\left(\overline{v},i\widetilde{k}\right)
refers to the conjugate dispersion relation.

With the explicit linear dispersion relations in Eqs. (
2.10
) and (
2.12
) of the two quasi-continuum models, we solve the two IVPs in Eqs. (
9.1
)-(
9.2
) to first gain analytical predictions on the linear-edge wavenumber which is denoted as
k^{-}
.

Log-KdV DSW fitting:

For the log-KdV equation (
2.8
), we note first that
A(\overline{v})=1+\ln|\overline{v}|
, and we recall that its linear dispersion relation is given in Eq. (
2.10
). Then, solving the IVP in Eq. (
9.1
) yields,

k(\overline{v})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\ln\left(\frac{|\overline{v}|}{|v_{+}|}\right)}.

(9.3)

Hence, the linear-edge wavenumber
k_{-}
should be given as:

k_{-}=k\left(v_{-}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\ln\left(\frac{|v_{-}|}{|v_{+}|}\right)}.

(9.4)

Furthermore, we compute the group velocity at
k_{-}
to obtain the linear-edge velocity of the DSW, denoted by
s_{-}
,

\displaystyle s_{-}=\partial_{k}\omega_{0}\left(v_{-},k_{-}\right)=1+\ln|v_{-}|-3k_{-}^{2}.

(9.5)

On the other hand, near the solitonic-edge of the DSW, we solve the IVP specified in equation (
9.2
) with the conjugate dispersion relation:

\widetilde{\omega}_{s}(\overline{v},\widetilde{k})=\left(1+\ln|\overline{v}|\right)\widetilde{k}+\widetilde{k}^{3}.

(9.6)

A direction integration of the IVP in Eq. (
9.2
) yields,

\widetilde{k}(\overline{v})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\ln\left(\frac{|v_{-}|}{|\overline{v}|}\right)}.

(9.7)

Then, the solitonic-edge speed, denoted by
s_{+}
, is obtained by computing the phase velocity,

\displaystyle s_{+}=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{\widetilde{k}}\left(v_{+},\widetilde{k}_{+}\right)=1+\ln|v_{+}|+\widetilde{k}_{+}^{2}.

(9.8)

where
\widetilde{k}_{+}=\widetilde{k}(v_{+})
.

Generalized-KdV DSW fitting:

For the DSW of the generalized-KdV equation (
2.11
), we analogously follow the procedures which we performed for the log-KdV DSW fitting. Namely, Noticing that
A(\overline{v})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}|\overline{v}|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)
, and the conjugate dispersion relation:

\widetilde{\omega}_{s}(\overline{v},\widetilde{k})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}|\overline{v}|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)\widetilde{k}+\widetilde{k}^{3}.

(9.9)

Solving the two IVPs in Eqs. (
9.1
) and (
9.2
) yields,

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3(\alpha-1)}\left(-|\overline{v}|^{1/\alpha-1}+|v_{+}|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)},

(9.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3(\alpha-1)}\left(\left|\overline{v}\right|^{1/\alpha-1}-\left|v_{-}\right|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)}.

Then, the linear and solitonic-edge speeds of the generalized-KdV DSW is given as follows,

\displaystyle s_{-}=\partial_{k}\omega_{0}\left(v_{-},k_{-}\right)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|v_{-}\right|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)-3k_{-}^{2},

(9.11)

\displaystyle s_{+}=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{\widetilde{k}}\left(v_{+},\widetilde{k}_{+}\right)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|v_{+}\right|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)+\widetilde{k}_{+}^{2}.

where now
\omega_{0}
is given in Eq. (
2.12
), and
k_{-}=k(v_{-}),\widetilde{k}_{+}=\widetilde{k}(v_{+})
.

We notice that these DSW-fitting theoretical predictions on the edge speeds (Eqs. (
9.5
), (
9.8
) and (
9.11
)) of the DSW of the two quasi-continuum models will be compared to the numerically measured DSW edge speeds in the later section to examine the performance of the DSW-fitting analysis on the DSW edge characteristics. To this end, we have to transform these DSW-fitting theoretical predictions on the DSW edge speeds in Eqs. (
9.5
), (
9.8
) and (
9.11
) into the speeds associated in the
(n,t)
spatial and temporal coordinate, and this can be achieved by the following observation,

\frac{dn}{dt}=\frac{\alpha-1}{2}\frac{d\xi}{d\tau}+1.

(9.12)

Numerical validation

We are now ready to compare the theoretical predictions on different edge features (e.g. edge speeds) of the DSWs of the quasi-continuum models with those which will be measured numerically based on the time evolution dynamics of the quasi-continuum models and the discrete lattice. To begin with, we discuss, in detail, some indispensable numerical setup which include the integration schemes and the approach to compute the edge speeds of the DSWs numerically, etc…

10.1
Integration schemes and initial conditions

Firstly, for the discrete lattice in Eq. (
2.4
), we apply a fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method for time integration with Neumann boundary conditions to solve the differential-difference equations (
2.4
). Next, for the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
), we utilize the exponential time differencing RK
4
(ETDRK
4
) time integration scheme
[
18
]
with a pseudo-spectral method of spatial discretization to perform time stepping of the two PDEs. Next, for the initial conditions of the quasi-continuum models, we consider the following ”box-type” initial data,

v_{0}(\xi)=v(\xi,0)=v_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{+}-v_{-}\right)\left[\tanh\left(\eta\left(\xi-a\right)\right)-\tanh\left(\eta\left(\xi-b\right)\right)\right],

(10.1)

where
\eta>0
is a constant characterizing the smoothness of the initial jumps, and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
are two parameters which decide the left and right location of the initial jump, respectively. Specifically, one should expect that as
\eta
decreases, the initial jumps shall become smoother. The main purpose of producing a smooth IC is to avoid the potential modulational instability which can possibly occur when one uses an IC with a sharp jump as it associates with high-frequency components in Fourier space. Although one reason to apply the IC in Eq. (
10.1
) is to have a smoother version of the standard Riemann initial data which has a sharp jump, another key reason is that the pseudo-spectral discretization method assumes the periodic boundary conditions. Figure
6
Shows two ICs with different values of
\eta
, and we can clearly see that when
\eta=10
, the IC has a jump which is much shaper than that corresponding to
\eta=0.1
.

and
b=500
.

One the one hand, to compare the dynamics of the lattice (
2.4
) with that of the log-KdV equation (
2.8

s_{n}\equiv\dot{u}_{n}
,

s_{n}(0)=-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon v_{\xi}\left(\xi,0\right)-\sqrt{3}\epsilon^{3}\left[\left(v(\xi,0)\ln|v(\xi,0)|\right)_{\xi}+v_{\xi\xi\xi}(\xi,0)\right],

(10.2)

where
\xi=2\sqrt{3}\epsilon n
.

On the other hand, to compare the dynamics of the lattice (
2.4

s_{n}(0)=-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon v_{\xi}(\xi,0)-\sqrt{3}\epsilon^{3}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\left(v(\xi,0)-v(\xi,0)|v(\xi,0)|^{1/\alpha-1}\right)_{\xi}+v(\xi,0)_{\xi\xi\xi}\right).

(10.3)

10.2
Numerical estimation of DSW edge speeds

We then discuss briefly the method utilized to numerically measure the solitonic and linear-edge speeds of the DSWs of both quasi-continuum models of Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) and the lattice (
2.4
). On the one hand, for the solitonic-edge speed of the DSWs which is denoted as
s_{+}
, we first compute the global minimizer of the field
v(\xi,t)
at a given time
t
. Namely, we find
\xi_{+}=\text{argmin}_{\xi}v(\xi,t)
, and we simply treat such
\xi_{+}
as the solitonic location of the DSWs. To numerically measure the solitonic-edge speed of
s_{+}
, we fix two time snapshots, say
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Then, we compute the two solitonic locations, denoted as
\xi_{+}^{1}
and
\xi_{+}^{2}
, of the DSW at these two
t
. The numerical solitonic-edge speed is then given as follows,

s_{+}=\left|\frac{\xi_{+}^{2}-\xi_{+}^{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\right|.

(10.4)

Finally, since the formula in Eq. (
10.4
) yields a
s_{+}
in the coordinate of
(\xi,\tau)
, one shall transform it into an associated
s_{+}
in the
(n,t)
coordinate by applying Eq. (
9.12
). On the other hand, for the numerical linear-edge speed which is denoted as
s_{-}
, we still pick two time snapshots of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Then, we try to pinpoint the linear-edge location of the DSWs as follows: we first define the following two quantities,

I^{U}=u_{-}+\frac{\left|u_{-}-u_{+}\right|}{N},\quad I^{L}=u_{-}-\frac{\left|u_{-}-u_{+}\right|}{N},

(10.5)

where
N
is a positive integer. Next, we compute local maximum and minimum of the DSW which belong to the two windows of
\left(I^{U}-\widetilde{\epsilon},I^{U}+\widetilde{\epsilon}\right)
and
\left(I^{L}-\widetilde{\epsilon},I^{L}+\widetilde{\epsilon}\right)
. For these local extremes, we apply a least-square based method to fit two straight lines, and these two lines shall intersect at a point, say
\xi_{-}
. We then simply regard such
\xi_{-}
as the linear-edge location of the DSWs. We shall obtain
\xi_{-}^{1}
and
\xi_{-}^{2}
at two distinct time snapshots of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and we apply the formula in Eq. (
10.4
) with
\xi_{+}
replaced by
\xi_{-}
to numerically evaluate the linear-edge speed of
s_{-}
of the DSWs.

11
Conclusions and future challenges

In this paper, we reviewed two previously established quasi-continuum models which serve as approximations of the granular crystal lattice. We studied their analytical traveling solitary-wave solutions as they are important to demonstrate the solitonic egde profile of the DSW. The periodic solutions of the two quasi-continuum models, although not amenable to analytical treatment, were shown to exist by our detailed phase-plane analysis. Based on these periodic solutions, we derive the complete and closed Whitham modulation system for both quasi-continuum models, which governs the slowly varying spatial and temporal dynamics of all the relevant parameters of the periodic traveling waves. We further performed an indispensable reduction on the Whitham modulation system and obtained a simple-wave ODE that actually encodes some crucial information on some edge features of the DSWs such as the edge wavenumber and speeds. We then compared these theoretical predictions with their associated numerical counterparts and thereafter verify that both quasi-continuum models indeed provide reasonable approximations of the DSWs to that of the lattice. Finally, we also studied and approximated the rarefaction waves of the lattice via computing the analytical self-similar solutions of the dispersionless correspondences of the quasi-continuum models, and compared it with the numerically observed RWs from the lattice, and such comparison also exhibits good agreement.

However, there are still many open questions and we here only list two of them. The first one is regarding the Whitham modulation systems for the quasi-continuum models. In particular, although we have successfully derived the closed modulation system for both models, the question is how explicitly each modulation system is closed? To the best of our knowledge, this problem unfortunately cannot be resolved under any analytical approaches mainly because the periodic solutions do not admit an analytical form. Hence, it is impossible to know what precise parameters of the periodic solutions to be expected, although it may quite likely include the amplitude, wavenumber, and/or speed of propagation of the periodic traveling wave. This issue can perhaps be bypassed via applying a data-driven discovery of the Whitham modulation equations. Namely, one can attempt to utilize some machine-learning based (e.g. SINDy
[
7
]
, neural ODE
[
10
]
, and/or Koopman method
[
6
,
20
]
) method to perform numerical identification of the Whitham modulation equations as a substitute of Eqs. (
6.6
) and (
6.8
) which are all expressed with the variable of wave mean
\overline{\phi}
. Instead, the identified equations shall not necessarily be expressed in terms of the wave mean
\overline{\phi}
, but the modulation equations may can be expressed in terms of other essential parameters of the periodic solutions such as the wave amplitude and traveling speed. Secondly, we notice that the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) are all first-order (in time) uni-directional models. The work
[
17
]
proposed a second-order (in time) bi-directional quasi-continuum model for the lattice (
2.4
), so it shall be very interesting to perform a similar study with such bi-directional model and investigate how it can be used to study the RWs and DSWs in the lattice (
2.4

and can be applied for the bi-directional model, as well. All these open directions are currently under investigations and will be reported in future publications.

Appendix

In this Appendix, we derive, in detail, the Whitham modulation equations for the log-KdV equation by the method of averaging the Lagrangian for an illustrative purpose. To this end, we notice first that for periodic solutions of the log-KdV equation (
2.8
), the behavior of the newly defined field
\psi
shall generally be,

\psi=\beta\xi-\gamma\tau+\varphi(\theta),\quad\theta=k\xi-\omega\tau,

(11.1)

where
\beta,\gamma
are two arbitrary real constants, and the function
\varphi(\theta)
is assumed to be periodic with a fixed spatial period
2\pi
and with a zero average, namely
\overline{\psi}=0
.

For such phase of
\theta
, the traveling-wave ansatz of
V(\theta)
now satisfies,

k^{2}V_{\theta}^{2}=\left(c_{p}+\frac{1}{2}\right)V^{2}-V^{2}\ln\left|V\right|+2AV+B\equiv G(V),

(11.2)

where
c_{p}=\omega/k
is defined as the phase speed, and
A,B
are two constants of integration.

Importantly, it is worthwhile to note that the parameter pair of
(\beta,\gamma)
is a counterpart to
(k,\omega)
and we call
\vartheta=\beta\xi-\gamma\tau
the
pseudo-phase

[
27
]
. We note that
V(\theta)
relates to
\psi\left(\xi,\tau\right)
and its associated derivatives as follows,

\displaystyle\psi_{\xi}=\beta+k\varphi_{\theta}=V,

(11.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{\tau}=-\gamma-c_{p}\left(V-\beta\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{\xi\xi}=kV_{\theta}.

Then, a direct substitution of Eq. (
11.3
) into the Lagrangian density in (
5.4
) yields,

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}

\displaystyle=-k^{2}V_{\theta}^{2}+\left(A-\frac{1}{2}\gamma+\frac{1}{2}c_{p}\beta\right)V+\frac{1}{2}B.

(11.4)

We compute the average Lagrangian,

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathbb{L}\text{d}\theta=kW\left(A,B,c_{p}\right)+\left(A-\frac{1}{2}\gamma+\frac{1}{2}c_{p}\beta\right)\beta+\frac{1}{2}B,

(11.5)

where the so-called ”action integral”
W(A,B,c_{p})
is defined as follows,

W(A,B,c_{p})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\left[\left(c_{p}+\frac{1}{2}\right)V^{2}-V^{2}\ln\left|V\right|+2AV+B\right]^{1/2}\text{d}V

(11.6)

Now, it is important to observe that the averaged Lagrangian in Eq. (
11.5
) depends on six parameters of
\bm{p}=\left[A,B,\omega,k,\beta,\gamma\right]
, but we shall show that these six parameters are not all independent. In fact, it is readily to see that,

\beta=\overline{V},

(11.7)

and such relation reduces the six parameters now only to five. Moreover, due to the fixed
2\pi
period of the periodic function of
\varphi(\theta)
, one can show that,

\oint\frac{1}{\sqrt{G(V)}}\text{d}V=\frac{2\pi}{k}.

(11.8)

The relation in Eq. (
11.8
) is referred to as the non-linear dispersion relation, and it further reduces the five parameters into four independent ones. Hence, this indicates that we need four modulation equations to form a closed and complete modulation system. To this end, we notice that for the modulated periodic wave, we have to assume all the relevant parameters of
\bm{p}
as functions of slowly varying spatial and temporal variables. Namely,
\bm{p}=\bm{p}\left(X,T\right)
, where we recall that
X=\delta\xi
and
T=\delta\tau
. Similar to the method of averaging the conservation laws in Section
6
, we introduce the same fast phase as in Section
6
, and moreover the generalized fast pseudo-phase
\vartheta=\delta^{-1}\widetilde{S}(X,T)
, where
\widetilde{S}(X,T)
is a slow pseudo-phase, satisfying,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\xi}=\widetilde{S}_{X}=\beta(X,T),

(11.9)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\tau}=\widetilde{S}_{T}=-\gamma(X,T),

and the modulation equations simply follow from the following averaging variational principle,

\delta\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(A,B,\omega,k,\beta,\gamma\right)\text{d}X\text{d}T=0,

(11.10)

In particular, the variation with respect to
A
and
B
yields,

kW_{A}+\beta=0,\quad kW_{B}+\frac{1}{2}=0.

(11.11)

which coincide with Eq. (
11.7
) and (
11.8
), respectively.

Moreover, the variation with respect to the two slow phases of
S
and
\widetilde{S}
yields,

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{\omega}\right)_{T}-\left(\mathcal{L}_{k}\right)_{X}=0,\quad k_{T}+\omega_{X}=0.

(11.12)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\right)_{T}-\left(\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\right)_{X}=0,\quad\beta_{T}+\gamma_{X}=0,

where we clearly note that the latter two equations in system (
11.12
) are the two equations of conservation of waves. Finally, the four PDEs in Eq. (
11.12
) form the closed and complete Whitham modulation system for the log-KdV equation.
