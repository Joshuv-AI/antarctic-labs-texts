Cervicovaginal cytokine profiles and their associations with colposcopic findings in adolescent girls and adult women in South Africa: an exploratory cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColposcopy is used to evaluate cervical inflammation and detect precancerous and other cervical lesions; however, access remains limited in many low-resource settings. Identification of cervicovaginal immune biomarkers associated with specific colposcopic findings may provide insight into mucosal immune alterations linked to increased susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), although these associations do not imply diagnostic or predictive utility in a cross-sectional context.MethodsIn this exploratory cross-sectional study, 203 adolescent girls (14–19 years) and adult women (25–35 years) from KwaZulu-Natal (KZN; n = 105) and the Western Cape (WC; n = 98) underwent colposcopic assessment for cervical ectopy, discharge, injury/inflammation, and leukoplakia. Cervicovaginal cytokines were quantified using multiplex Luminex assays. Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Trichomonas vaginalis, and bacterial vaginosis (BV) were assessed. Logistic regression models identified correlates of colposcopic findings. Exploratory model performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), were calculated to describe within-dataset model behaviour.ResultsOverall, 75% of participants had at least one colposcopic finding. Leukoplakia was more prevalent in KZN (36% of adolescents and 22% of adults), while cervical discharge was most common in WC (36% in both age groups). In univariate analyses, injectable progestin use was associated with any colposcopic finding (OR = 2.10, p = 0.04), implant use was associated with ectopy in KZN (OR = 6.94, p = 0.04), and BV was associated with cervical discharge (OR = 3.37, p = 0.04). In the exploratory multivariable models, selected cervicovaginal cytokines showed finding- and site-specific associations with colposcopic outcomes. In KZN, higher granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) concentrations and parity were associated with cervical ectopy, while BV was associated with cervical discharge. In WC, interleukin-6 (IL-6) and macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1β were associated with cervical ectopy, whereas lower MIP-1α concentrations were associated with leukoplakia. Measures of model performance varied across outcomes and sites, and should be interpreted within the context of the exploratory study design.ConclusionCervicovaginal cytokine profiles were differentially associated with specific colposcopic findings in a site-specific manner. These findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, highlighting potential biological correlates of cervical changes. Background

The cervix plays a critical role in fertility, pregnancy, childbirth, and overall female reproductive health [1–4]. Colposcopy is a clinical tool used to visualise cervical changes and identify findings such as cervical ectopy, abnormal discharge, erythema, oedema, trauma-related lesions (including ecchymosis, lacerations, or abrasions), and leukoplakia, which may appear as white plaques on the cervix [5]. While some colposcopic findings, such as trauma or ectopy, have clearer clinical implications, others, including leukoplakia, may be benign or associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection or squamous neoplasia; importantly, leukoplakia warrants biopsy to exclude malignancy [6]. Despite its clinical utility, access to colposcopy remains limited in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to requirements for specialised equipment, trained personnel, and pathology laboratory services [7].

Colposcopy is inherently a subjective, pattern-recognition–based procedure, and interpretation may vary depending on clinician experience, lesion visibility, and examination conditions, despite the use of standardised terminology systems. Importantly, the International Federation for Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy (IFCPC) colposcopic terminology emphasises that visual assessment is dependent on examination adequacy, lesion visibility, and transformation zone characteristics, and that apparently similar findings may represent biologically distinct processes [8]. Furthermore, histologically confirmed high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2+) may be present even in cervices that appear normal at colposcopic examination, highlighting limitations in visual diagnosis and biopsy targeting [9].

Genital tract inflammation, characterised by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, has been strongly associated with increased risk of HIV acquisition. Cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-6, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, MIP-1α, and interferon gamma–induced protein (IP)-10 recruit and/or activate CD4⁺ T cells, which are the primary target cells for HIV infection [10–13]. In 2023, adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) accounted for approximately 44% of the 1.3 million new HIV infections globally, with those in sub-Saharan Africa contributing disproportionately, representing 62% of new infections worldwide [14]. Biological susceptibility of the female genital tract, combined with inflammatory conditions and compromised mucosal immune protection, further heightens HIV vulnerability among AGYW.

Existing literature has demonstrated links between cervical findings, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and HIV susceptibility. Sexually transmitted infection (STI)–associated cervical lesions and inflammation have been linked to elevated concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α [15–18]. Increased expression of IL-8 and monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1 has also been implicated in cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) [19–22].

Importantly, colposcopic findings represent a heterogeneous group of cervical epithelial changes with distinct biological and clinical implications. For example, ectopy is often a physiological feature, particularly in younger women, whereas abnormal discharge may reflect infection or dysbiosis, and leukoplakia may arise from a spectrum of benign, inflammatory, or HPV-associated pathological processes [23–25]. These findings therefore represent biologically and clinically non-equivalent endpoints, underscoring the need for outcome-specific interpretation when examining associated mucosal immune profiles.

Despite existing evidence linking inflammation and cervical pathology, it remains unclear which specific cervicovaginal cytokines are associated with distinct colposcopic findings, and whether these relationships differ across clinical and demographic contexts. A clearer understanding of these associations may provide insight into the biological processes underlying visible cervical epithelial changes, particularly in populations at high risk of HIV acquisition. Given the heterogeneity of colposcopic findings and the limited data linking specific cervical phenotypes to mucosal immune profiles, further investigation is warranted.

Therefore, this study adopted an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach to examine associations between cervicovaginal cytokines and specific colposcopic findings in adolescent girls and women in South Africa. The aim was not to develop diagnostic or predictive biomarkers, but rather to characterise potential biological patterns underlying clinically observed cervical epithelial changes within a high-risk population context.

Methods

Study design, participants, and sample collection

The Mucosal Injury from Sexual Trauma/Contact (MIST/C) study was a longitudinal cohort of HIV-uninfected adolescent girls (14–19 years) and adult women (25–35 years) [24]. Participants were recruited from two sites in South Africa: a rural site at the CAPRISA Vulindlela Research Clinic (VRC) in Mafakathini, KwaZulu-Natal (KZN), and a peri-urban site at the Desmond Tutu Health Foundation (DTHF) Adolescent Clinic in Phillipi East, Western Cape (WC). Only baseline data were used for the analyses reported here.

The study was approved by the University of KwaZulu-Natal Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BF504/17), the University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee (696/2017), and the Seattle Children’s Research Institute (STUDY00000462). For participants younger than 16 years, both written parental or guardian consent and written participant assent were required. A waiver of parental consent was obtained for participants aged 16 years and older, allowing them to provide written informed consent independently.

At baseline, demographic, behavioural, and sexual history information was collected via interviewer-administered questionnaires. Clinical examination included a speculum examination, colposcopy, and cervical imaging, all performed by trained clinicians. STI and BV testing were performed at each site. At KZN, urine samples were screened for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae using the Xpert® CT/NG assay (Cepheid, USA), while lateral vaginal wall swabs were used to detect Trichomonas vaginalis via wet mount microscopy and the OSOM® Trichomonas Rapid Test (Sekisui Diagnostics, USA). At WC, vaginal swabs were tested for C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, and T. vaginalis at the Bio Analytical Research Corporation (BARC) reference laboratory using multiplex PCR (genesig® kits, Primerdesign™, UK). Differences in STI testing methods between sites necessitated separate analyses for KZN and WC.

Cervicovaginal secretions were collected using a clinician-inserted menstrual cup, left in place for 30 min, and then retrieved. Samples were transported on ice and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Measurement of biomarkers from cervicovaginal secretions

Concentrations of 18 cytokines: monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, MIP-1α, MIP-3α, MIP-1β, IL-8, interferon gamma-induced protein (IP)-10, interferon (IFN)-α, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-1α, tumour necrosis factor–related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), IL-1β, IL-6, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), IL-10, IL-17, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) were measured from thawed genital secretion samples using the R&D Systems Luminex Performance Assay Kit (R&D Systems, USA). These cytokines were selected based on prior studies linking them to genital inflammation and HIV susceptibility [11, 13].

Samples were thawed, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and cell-free supernatants processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Assays were run on the Bio-Plex 200 System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, South Africa), with standard curves generated using five-parameter logistic regression in Bio-Plex Manager Software to calculate cytokine concentrations.

Colposcopy examination

Colposcopy was performed by trained clinicians following the Declaration of Helsinki and South African Good Clinical Practice guidelines [26, 27]. The vulva and perineum were first inspected for signs of trauma, including erythema, bruising, abrasions, or lacerations. A sterile speculum was then inserted for visualisation of the cervix using the Eva System colposcope (MobileODT, Israel). Digital images were captured and labelled using participant identification numbers only.

Images were reviewed in a blinded fashion by the study gynaecologist and classified into four key colposcopic categories: (i) Ectopy: presence of columnar epithelium on the ectocervix, (ii) Discharge: visible mucopurulent or abnormal cervical discharge, (iii) Inflammation/Injury: erythema, oedema, or trauma-related lesions (abrasions, lacerations, or bruising) and, (iv) Leukoplakia: white plaques on the cervix, potentially indicative of HPV infection or squamous neoplasia. Colposcopic assessments were performed according to the Manual for the Standardization of Colposcopy for the Evaluation of Vaginal Products – Update 2004 [28]. Participants were excluded if images were missing or of insufficient quality due to blurring. Participants requiring further care were referred to appropriate health services.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted separately for KZN and WC using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0.2.0, GraphPad Prism v10, and RStudio v2023.06.0 + 421.

Associations between demographic, socio-behavioural, and sexual behavioural characteristics and colposcopic findings (any finding, ectopy, discharge, leukoplakia, inflammation/injury) were explored using univariate logistic regression. Log10-transformed cytokine concentrations were compared across colposcopic categories using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Formal correction for multiple testing was not applied, as analyses were designed to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating; therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution and considered at risk of type I error. Individual p-values should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence, given the large number of comparisons performed.

Variables and cytokines identified as statistically significant and/or clinically relevant (based on prior literature or biological plausibility) were included in multivariable models. The multivariable models were constructed as exploratory analyses to identify potential combinations of variables associated with colposcopic findings. Stepwise forward logistic regression was used to build association models, sequentially adding variables based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to select the most parsimonious model. Model significance was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test, and goodness-of-fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Given the relatively small sample size, particularly within subgroups, and the inclusion of multiple predictors, there is a risk of overfitting, model instability, and sparse data bias. Some estimates may therefore be imprecise, as reflected by wide confidence intervals or extreme odds ratios. Model results should be interpreted as exploratory and not as evidence of robust or generalisable associations.

Model performance was evaluated using the optimal probability cutoff determined by the Youden Index [29]. The metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV); however, these were not intended to assess clinical utility and should be interpreted within the exploratory context of the analyses, particularly given variability in model fit and potential overfitting.

Results

Descriptive findings: Study population and prevalence of colposcopic findings

The study included 203 participants: 138 adolescents aged 14–19 years and 65 adults aged 25–35 years. Overall, 153 participants (75%) presented with at least one colposcopic finding, including discharge, ectopy, injury/inflammation, or leukoplakia. Colposcopic findings were more common among adolescents (78%) and among users of progesterone-based injectable contraceptives (79%) (Table 1).

In the WC, adolescents most frequently presented with discharge (36%), followed by leukoplakia (24%), injury/inflammation (16%), and ectopy (10%). Among adults in WC, injury/inflammation was the most prevalent finding (30%), followed by leukoplakia (22.5%), discharge (17.5%), and ectopy (15%). Overall, the distribution of colposcopic findings differed by both site and age group (Fig. 1).

Unadjusted associations: Demographic, behavioural, and clinical factors associated with colposcopic findings

Univariate analyses are presented in Table 1. Residence in KZN was associated with lower odds of having any colposcopic finding compared with WC (odds ratio [OR] = 0.46, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24–0.89, p = 0.02). Use of progesterone-based injectable contraceptives was associated with higher odds of having a colposcopic finding (OR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.02–4.32, p = 0.04). Participants with BV or those testing positive for Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Trichomonas vaginalis had higher proportions of colposcopic findings; however, these associations did not reach statistical significance.

Stratified analyses demonstrated site-specific associations (Supplementary Table 1). In KZN, ectopy was significantly associated with contraceptive implant use (OR = 6.94, 95% CI: 1.12–42.70, p = 0.04) and with ever having given birth (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.03–8.10, p = 0.04). Being an adult was associated with increased odds of injury/inflammation (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 0.98–7.87, p = 0.05). Discharge was significantly associated with BV (OR = 3.37, 95% CI: 1.05–10.78, p = 0.04). Other factors, including sexually transmitted infections, use of sexual enhancers, and contraceptive methods other than implants, were not significantly associated with specific colposcopic findings.

In WC, no significant associations were detected between demographic, behavioural, or clinical variables and colposcopic findings (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, residence, age, contraceptive use, reproductive history, and BV appeared to influence specific colposcopic findings, with patterns differing by province.

Exploratory cytokine associations: Associations between cytokine concentrations and colposcopic findings

Given the number of cytokines assessed, these analyses were exploratory and p-values are presented without correction for multiple testing.

In KZN, participants with at least one colposcopic finding had significantly higher concentrations of several cytokines, including MIP-1β (p = 0.02), IFN-α (p = 0.05), TNF-α (p = 0.03), TRAIL (p = 0.006), IL-1β (p = 0.05), IL-6 (p = 0.01), IL-10 (p = 0.02), and G-CSF (p = 0.05) (Fig. 2A).

In KZN, TRAIL was associated with the presence of any colposcopic finding (OR = 1.45, p = 0.01), with good model fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.88). The ectopy model showed poor fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.02); however, G-CSF (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.16–3.57, p = 0.02) and ever having given birth (OR = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.09–9.56, p = 0.04) remained as significant associations, although these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given poor model fit and potential instability. BV was associated with discharge (OR = 3.37, p = 0.04), although model fit statistics could not be computed due to limited sample size.

In WC, IL-6 was associated with increased odds of ectopy (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.43–6.28, p = 0.01), while MIP-1β was inversely associated (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.15–0.88, p = 0.03); however, this model demonstrated poor fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.02), and the observed associations may be influenced by small sample size and model instability. The leukoplakia model showed good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.81), with MIP-1α significantly associated with decreased odds of leukoplakia (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.25–0.68, p = 0.0008).

Model performance varied by outcome and sites (Table 3). In KZN, the model including TRAIL for any colposcopic finding showed moderate sensitivity and specificity (0.64 and 0.67, respectively), with higher PPV (0.81) than NPV (0.46). The ectopy model demonstrated high sensitivity (0.95) and moderate specificity (0.61), while the discharge model showed relatively high sensitivity (0.83) but lower specificity (0.42).

In WC, the ectopy model incorporating IL-6 and MIP-1β showed comparatively higher accuracy (0.82) and specificity (0.86) within this dataset. The leukoplakia model based on MIP-1α demonstrated moderate sensitivity (0.78) and specificity (0.63). Discussion

This study investigated clinical and immunological factors associated with specific colposcopic findings among adolescent girls (14–19 years) and adult women (25–35 years) in two South African provinces. Colposcopic findings were highly prevalent, particularly among adolescents, supporting previous evidence that younger women are more likely to present with BV, STIs, and cervical ectopy, all factors that may increase susceptibility to HIV [12, 30, 31]. Colposcopic findings represent heterogeneous cervical changes with distinct biological and clinical implications. Ectopy is often physiological, particularly in young women, whereas discharge may reflect infection or dysbiosis, and leukoplakia may arise from a range of benign or pathological processes.

Leukoplakia exhibited an inverse relationship with cervicovaginal cytokine concentrations; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Leukoplakia is a non-specific colposcopic feature with heterogeneous underlying causes, including benign keratinisation and HPV-associated pathology, and the biological mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear [32]. The International Federation for Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy (IFCPC) terminology further emphasises that colposcopic interpretation depends on examination adequacy, lesion visibility, and transformation zone characteristics, and that visually similar findings may represent biologically distinct processes [8]. Leukoplakia is therefore classified as a non-specific abnormal finding because it may reflect either benign epithelial change or underlying high-grade disease.

Evidence from previous studies also demonstrates that visual colposcopy is an imperfect surrogate for histopathology. Histologically confirmed CIN2 + lesions may be present even in cervices that appear normal during colposcopic examination, suggesting that limitations in lesion visibility and recognition contribute to missed disease [9]. These considerations reinforce that the colposcopic findings evaluated in the present study should be interpreted as exploratory visual markers of cervical biology rather than definitive indicators of histological pathology.

The distribution of colposcopic findings differed by site. In KZN, leukoplakia was most common, whereas in WC, discharge and injury/inflammation predominated. These regional differences likely reflect variations in sexual behaviours, contraceptive practices, and prevalence of BV and STIs [33], emphasising the need for context-specific screening and prevention strategies. Contraceptive use, particularly progesterone-based injectables, was significantly associated with colposcopic findings, nearly doubling the odds of presentation. While evidence regarding DMPA and NET-EN and their direct links to genital inflammation is mixed [34–36], these contraceptives may increase mucosal permeability [37] and alter the vaginal microbiome [38, 39], potentially enhancing local immune activation and vulnerability to HIV and other STIs [36, 38, 39]. Consistent with this, BV was associated with discharge on colposcopy, reflecting its known effects on epithelial disruption and mucosal inflammation [40]. Discharge was also linked to elevated inflammatory cytokines, supporting its association with ongoing infection or dysbiosis [41].

Each colposcopic finding carries distinct biological implications. Ectopy, in which columnar epithelium extends onto the ectocervix, is common during adolescence and early reproductive years [42]. This thinner tissue is more susceptible to trauma and infection [42, 43] consistent with its association with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and G-CSF, which may recruit HIV target cells, such as CD4 + T cells and dendritic cells, to the cervicovaginal mucosa [44–46]. In contrast, leukoplakia, which may be idiopathic or HPV-related, was associated with lower cytokine concentrations in WC [37]. One possible explanation is that keratinisation could alter cytokine diffusion into cervicovaginal secretions; however, this remains speculative in the absence of histological or HPV data [47]. Injury or inflammation observed on colposcopy may represent transient trauma or tissue repair, which could explain weaker cytokine associations. The limited associations observed for this outcome may also reflect heterogeneity within this broad category, which combines distinct biological processes including inflammation, mechanical trauma, and tissue repair.

Colposcopy is most commonly used in the evaluation of abnormal cervical cytology and suspected neoplasia; however, it also has broader clinical applications, including the assessment of cervicitis, mucosal trauma, and other non-oncologic cervical abnormalities. In many low-resource settings, access to cytology-based screening programmes remains limited, highlighting the importance of understanding biological correlates of cervical changes in such contexts. Recent studies continue to demonstrate that HPV16/18-associated lesions carry substantially higher risk for CIN2 + compared with other high-risk HPV genotypes, supporting the ongoing importance of accurate cervical assessment and risk stratification in HPV-positive women [48].

However, colposcopy remains constrained by the need for specialised equipment and trained personnel. Furthermore, contemporary evidence indicates that even with improved imaging and standardised terminology systems, colposcopy may fail to identify all clinically significant lesions, particularly when abnormalities are subtle or not visually apparent [9, 48]. These challenges highlight the importance of exploring complementary approaches; however, the current findings remain preliminary and not yet applicable to clinical decision-making.

Exploratory multivariable analyses identified combinations of cytokines and clinical factors associated with specific colposcopic findings. Model performance varied across outcomes and sites, and several models demonstrated suboptimal goodness-of-fit. The use of stepwise selection, together with relatively small subgroup sizes, increases the risk of overfitting, sparse data bias, and unstable estimates. Some associations were accompanied by wide confidence intervals or inconsistent model performance, further indicating limited precision. In KZN, TRAIL was associated with the presence of any colposcopic finding, while G-CSF and parity were associated with ectopy, and MIP-1α was associated with leukoplakia. While some models showed higher negative predictive value within this dataset, these metrics are influenced by outcome prevalence and do not indicate clinical utility. Overall, model performance differed by site, with variation in sensitivity and specificity across outcomes. Importantly, colposcopic accuracy itself varies substantially across clinical settings and operators, with reported sensitivities ranging widely in the literature [48], further supporting cautious interpretation of exploratory predictive modelling based on visual cervical findings. Interpretation of models, particularly in small subgroups, should be cautious [49, 50].

Taken together, this study suggests that abnormal colposcopic findings result from an interplay of clinical exposures, including contraceptive use, BV, and STIs, and local biological responses captured by cytokine profiles. Adolescents may be particularly vulnerable due to the convergence of ectopy, higher infection rates, and contraceptive exposure, all contributing to a pro-inflammatory cervical environment. Importantly, ectopy and discharge were associated with inflammatory cytokines that correlate with HIV susceptibility, whereas leukoplakia exhibited a relatively non-inflammatory profile. These observations highlight that not all abnormal colposcopic findings carry equivalent biological or clinical implications.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, the subjective but blinded nature of colposcopic assessment, and small subgroup sizes that limit the robustness of exploratory models. The modelling approach was exploratory and may be subject to overfitting, particularly in smaller strata. In addition, colposcopy is inherently a subjective visual technique, and previous studies have demonstrated variability in the detection of high-grade lesions, including cases where disease is not visually apparent despite abnormal histology [9, 48]. The absence of histological confirmation and HPV genotyping for specific lesions further limits interpretation of the biological significance of individual colposcopic findings, particularly leukoplakia. Longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the reproducibility and biological relevance of cytokine associations.

Conclusion

Colposcopic findings were common among adolescent girls and adult women and were shaped by biological, behavioural, and demographic factors. Ectopy and discharge were associated with inflammatory cytokine profiles linked to HIV susceptibility, whereas leukoplakia showed a comparatively less inflammatory profile. Exploratory multivariable analyses identified site-specific associations between selected cytokines, clinical factors, and colposcopic findings; however, these models are not clinically validated and should be interpreted with caution. Overall, these findings provide insight into the biological correlates of cervical changes and highlight the importance of the mucosal immune environment in HIV susceptibility. Further validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts is required to determine whether these associations are reproducible and biologically meaningful.
