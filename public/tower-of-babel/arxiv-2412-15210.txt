Tokenisation is NP-Complete
Philip Whittington, Gregor Bachmann, Tiago Pimentel

Abstract
In this work, we prove the NP-completeness of two variants of tokenisation, defined as the problem of compressing a dataset to at most δ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta} symbols by either finding a vocabulary directly ( direct tokenisation), or selecting a sequence of merge operations ( bottom-up tokenisation).



1
Introduction

Tokenisation is at the heart of natural language processing (NLP) being the first step required to use a language model (LM).
Given a string of characters
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
, a tokeniser converts it into a string of
subwords

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
.
Language models are then trained to estimate distributions over subword strings—never seeing the original character strings.
Despite its prominent role, however, much remains unknown about tokenisation.
We still do not know, for instance, what makes a good tokeniser
(
Gowda and May 2020
;
Cognetta et al. 2024
)
:
which characteristics should its produced subwords
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
have to be a good starting point for language modelling?
If we knew this, then we could define an
objective function
which we could evaluate tokenisers with.

Another open question is how to—given such an objective function—efficiently find a tokeniser which maximises it.
Byte pair encoding
(
Gage 1994
;
Sennrich et al. 2016
, BPE;)
, for instance, is a greedy solution to find a tokeniser which maximises a text’s compression.
UnigramLM
(
Kudo 2018
)
is a heuristic method to find a tokeniser that maximises its tokenised text’s unigram log-probability.
Both these methods, however, are approximate: they do not necessarily find an optimal tokeniser according to their objective function.
This raises the question of whether finding such optimal tokenisers efficiently is even possible.

In this paper, we answer this question (at least partially), proving the NP-completeness of several variants of this tokenisation problem.
Specifically, we focus on finding tokenisers that maximise the
compression
of a text.
1
1

1

The compression achieved by a tokeniser correlates with downstream language modelling performance
(
Gallé 2019
;
Zouhar et al. 2023a
)
and
computational efficiency.

Given this objective, we then define the
tokenisation problem
as the task of finding a tokeniser which compresses a dataset to at most
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}
symbols.
Notably, prior work imposes different constraints on how tokenisers are defined; here we consider two variants.
In
direct tokenisation
, the desired compression must be reached by choosing a vocabulary (i.e., a set of subwords) which is directly used to represent the text.
In
bottom-up tokenisation
, the desired compression must be reached by finding a sequence of merge operations instead, which we apply to the input text.

We prove the NP-hardness of both of these tokenisation problems (as well as of some variants thereof) by reducing from the
max 2-satisfiability
(
max-2-SAT
) problem.
2
2

2

Concurrent work by
Kozma and Voderholzer 2024
also proves the NP-completeness of bottom-up tokenisation. In fact, they prove its APX-hardness.

Practically speaking, our results imply that we are unlikely to find an efficient algorithm for the problem of finding optimal tokenisers, and that we should focus on approximate algorithms (such as BPE or UnigramLM) instead.

How to Choose a Tokeniser?

In theory, a researcher’s choice of tokeniser should not influence their language model’s quality.
This is because we can extract word-level
(
Pimentel and Meister 2024
;
Oh and Schuler 2024
)
or character-level
(
Phan et al. 2024
;
Giulianelli et al. 2024
)
distributions from any subword-level language model.
Thus, regardless of which tokeniser is used, a sufficiently expressive language model should be able to represent the exact distributions over characters or words that we are interested in.

In practice, however, a bad choice of tokeniser
can have undesirable effects on downstream applications.
For instance, performing standard arithmetic tasks (e.g.,
317+421
) can be difficult even for large models
(
Nogueira et al. 2021
;
Muffo et al. 2022
)
due to the arbitrary splitting of numbers into subwords.
Indeed, simple changes in how numbers are tokenised can improve performance in such tasks
(
Singh and Strouse 2024
)
.
Similar issues arise when prompting LMs to count letters in words, where even advanced models such as
GPT-4

infamously cannot correctly count the number of occurrences of the letter
r
in the word
strawberry
.

This raises the question of how to select a good tokeniser.
Ideally, we would choose the tokeniser which maximises downstream language modelling performance.
Unfortunately, we do not know how to measure such performance without fully training a model, making its direct maximisation computationally infeasible.
Rather, we thus optimise proxy objectives—assumed to correlate with downstream performance.
Among these are unigram log-probability
(
Kudo 2018
)
, Rényi efficiency
(
Zouhar et al. 2023a
)
, and compression
(
Gallé 2019
)
.

We focus on compression in this paper.
Denoting our tokenisation’s
objective function
as
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}
, we write this objective as:
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})=-|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|
.
Improved compression leads to: (i) more efficient training and inference, due to shortened inputs;
3
3

3

Recent work tries to improve the computational efficiency of byte-level models
(
Yu et al. 2023
;
Pagnoni et al. 2024
)
.

(ii) improved downstream performance, at least to a certain extent
(
Gallé 2019
;
Rust et al. 2021
;
Zouhar et al. 2023a
;
Goldman et al. 2024
)
;
4
4

4

Although, see also
Ali et al. 2024
, who argue that compression might be a necessary but not sufficient condition for good downstream performance, and
Schmidt et al. 2024
, who argue that compression and downstream performance have a more complex relationship than prior work suggests.
and
(iii) fairer multilingual treatment, given models’ limited context lengths and the per-token costs to use proprietary models
(
Petrov et al. 2023
;
Ahia et al. 2023
)
.

3
Defining a Tokeniser

A tokeniser can be defined as a 3-tuple
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{detok}}\rangle
, composed of a vocabulary, a tokenisation and a detokenisation function.
Before defining these terms, however, we require some notation.
Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}={\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}_{2}\cdots{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}_{|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}|}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}
be a
character-string
, i.e., a sequence of characters
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}
from alphabet
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}
.
Further, let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}\!=\!\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
be a dataset of character-strings.
5
5

5

We note that we use set notation here, but our datasets are actually multisets—datasets can include the same string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
multiple times. We show that tokenisation is still NP-complete for datasets with no repetitions in
section

6.3
. Further, we impose no constraint on the kind of string present in these datasets: each
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}_{n}
can be either a raw or pre-tokenised character-string (i.e., either a full document or a whitespace word).

A subword
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}\!\in\!{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
represents a non-empty character-string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
. (Sequence
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
can have length one.)
Finally, let
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2},\cdots,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|}\rangle}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}
be a
subword-string
.
Just like a single subword, a subword-string
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}
represents a character-string via the concatenation of its subwords’ characters:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{concat}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\circ...\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|}

(1)

and we say that a pair of character and subword strings are equivalent if:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\circ}}}{{=}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\iff{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}={\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{concat}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})

(2)

Given the notation above, we can now define the items in tuple
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{detok}}\rangle
.
A tokeniser’s
vocabulary
is a set of subwords
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}\subset{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
; we say its size is
|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}|=|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}|+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
.
Further, a
detokenisation function
is defined as
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{detok}}:{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}

and given a subword-string it outputs the character-string it represents.
This function thus is simply defined as
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{detok}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\texttt{def}}}{{=}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{concat}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})
.

Finally, we are left with defining a
tokenisation function

{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}\to{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}
, which maps from character- to subword-strings.
Notably, these functions always ensure the equivalence
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\circ}}}{{=}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
for
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\mathop{=}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})
.
Several tokenisation functions, however, are compatible with this constraint, as given a vocabulary, many subword-strings may be equivalent to the same character-string.
For instance, given

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}\!=\!\{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}a},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}c},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}t},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}at}\}
, the string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}\!=\!{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\langle c,a,t\rangle}
could be tokenised as
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\langle c,a,t\rangle}
or as
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\langle c,at\rangle}
.
Most researchers define tokenisation functions in one of two ways, which we term direct and bottom-up tokenisation functions here; we define these next.

3.1
Direct Tokenisation Functions

In direct tokenisation, a character-string is directly replaced by an optimal subword-string.
To implement this, one must thus first define what
optimal
means; this is done through objective function
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}
which, given a subword-string, returns a score.
Given a previously chosen vocabulary
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
(we discuss how to find
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
in
section

5
), a direct tokenisation function then encodes string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
as:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}]({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})}=

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\,\,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\circ}}}{{=}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}

In words, given a vocabulary
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
, function
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}
returns the optimal subword-string
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}
which is equivalent to the input character-string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
.
We then set
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\texttt{def}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}]({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})}
.
Different choices of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}
recover methods such as UnigramLM
(
Kudo 2018
)
or PathPiece
(
Schmidt et al. 2024
)
.
Notably, in general, this function is not efficiently computable.
6
6

6

In fact,
Geh et al. 2024
shows that it is NP-complete for
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})\!=\!\sum_{t=1}^{|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{t}\!\mid\!{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}_{<t})
, where
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a language model.

In this paper, we are concerned with tokenisers that use compression as their objective: that is, for which
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathfrak{G}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})=-|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|
.
In this case, we can rewrite the direct tokenisation function as:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}]({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}}|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\,\,{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\circ}}}{{=}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}

Importantly, in the case of compression, this equation can be computed efficiently (as shown in
section

5.1
).

3.2
Bottom-up Tokenisation Functions

In bottom-up tokenisation, one starts with a set of character-strings, and merges their symbols bottom-up, one pair at a time.
7
7

7

Currently, this is likely the most common tokenisation function, being used in popular tokenisers such as, e.g., GPT-4’s
(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
, LLaMA’s
(
Touvron et al. 2023a
;
Touvron et al. 2023b
)
, and Pythia’s
(
Biderman et al. 2023
)
.

Formally, let
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{M}}
be a
merge
, defined as a pair of subwords:
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}=\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\rangle
.
Further, let
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{M}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\texttt{def}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}\mathop{\times}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}
.
Now, let
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}
be a functional; given merge
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}=\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\rangle
, it returns a function
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}]:{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}^{*}\to({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}\cup\{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\})^{*}
which operates on string
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
left-to-right, replacing every occurrence of
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}
followed by
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}
in it with subword
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}
.
E.g., given
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\langle wo,r,ld\rangle}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}=\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}wo},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}r}\rangle
, the output of

{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}]({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}})
is
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\langle wor,ld\rangle}
.

Consider now
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{M}}^{*}
, a sequence of merges.
Given a character-string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}^{*}
, a bottom-up tokenisation function compresses it as:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\uparrow}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}]({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})}=\bigg(\bigodot_{z=1}^{|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}|}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}_{z}]\bigg)({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})

(5)

where
\bigodot
represents function composition, e.g.,

\bigodot_{z=1}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}_{z}]={\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}_{2}]\odot{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{merge}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}m}_{1}]
.
Bottom-up tokenisers then set
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\texttt{def}}}{{=}}\!{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\uparrow}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}]}
.
Further, a merge sequence
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}
is also used to set a bottom-up tokeniser’s vocabulary as:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}={\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}\cup\{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\mid\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}}\rangle\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}\}

(6)

where
|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}|={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
implies this vocabulary has size
|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}|=|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}|+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
, as before.

4
Maximum 2-Satisfiability

Our paper’s goal is to prove the NP-completeness of tokenisation.
To show this, we must reduce an NP-hard problem to tokenisation in polynomial time.
We will rely on the

The NP-hardness of

Definition 1
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{{J}}
be a set of variables;
each of these variables are assigned values
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{j}\in\{\mathtt{F},\mathtt{T}\}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathpzc{x}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{{J}}\in\{\mathtt{F},\mathtt{T}\}^{{J}}

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}=\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}^{1}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}^{2})\}_{i=1}^{{I}}
be a set of clauses,
8
8

8

max-2-SAT
also allows clauses to have a single literal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}
. In this case, we can always rewrite the clause as
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i})
with no change to the solution of this decision problem.

where each literal
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}
represents either a variable
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}_{j}
or its negation
\neg{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}_{j}

The

requires deciding whether there exists an assignment for which at least
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\psi}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\psi}\leq

\displaystyle\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathpzc{x}}\in\{\mathtt{F},\mathtt{T}\}^{J}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathbbm{1}\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}^{1}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L}_{i}^{2}\}

is an indicator function which evaluates the clause and returns one if the clause is satisfied by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathpzc{x}}
and zero otherwise.

For mathematical convenience, we will write
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathrm{M2S}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\psi})
for a function which returns

if its input is satisfiable under a

As a concrete example, consider the set of variables
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}_{2}\}

.
The assignment

For this example, we thus have that

{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathrm{M2S}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}},4)=\mathtt{F}

5.1
Direct Tokenisation is in NP

A decision problem is in the nondeterministic polynomial time class (NP) if, given a
certificate

of polynomial length, one can verify that certificate in polynomial time.
Specifically, a certificate usually encodes a decision problem’s solution, allowing us to verify its satisfiability.
In the case of direct tokenisation, this certificate would be a vocabulary
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
which leads a dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
to be compressed to at most
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}
symbols.
Verifying this certificate simply requires computing the sum in
eq.

9
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}|{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}[{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}]({\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}})}|

(10)

Lemma 1
.

The direct tokenisation decision problem, as in
3
, is in NP.

Proof.

As noted above, whenever
|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}|\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
, each
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}_{n}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
can be included in the vocabulary
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
and fully compressed to a single symbol; we can thus verify the problem’s satisfiability by simply checking that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}\geq|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}|
as this is the best reachable compression.
Assuming
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
to be bounded by
|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}|
—and therefore polynomial in the input—we have that the certificate
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
also has polynomial length.
Given such a certificate
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
, verifying it simply requires computing the sum in
eq.

10
.
In turn, computing this sum requires
|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}|
calls to function
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}
.
It follows that, if function
{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{tok}}_{\text{\pointer}}}
runs in polynomial time, then direct tokenisation is in NP.
Luckily, this function can indeed be computed efficiently using
Schmidt et al. 2024
’s (
Schmidt et al. 2024

O(|{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}|^{2})
time.
∎

5.2

We now use a reduction from

Reduction 1

If a

First, if

6.3
Other Definitions of Tokenisation

We now expand our discussion to consider variations of the above tokenisation problems.

Deduped Datasets.

Our definitions of both direct and bottom-up tokenisation allow datasets
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
to include repeated entries.
It is common, however, to deduplicate datasets in NLP—thus removing repeated entries.
A small change to both our reductions is enough to adapt it to this deduplicated dataset case: simply append each string in the repeated datasets (either
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{2}
in
1
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{1}
to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}_{4}
in
2
) with a unique character
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}a}_{y}\}_{y=1}^{\infty}
and increase the target compression size
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}
accordingly (by
{f}+{f}^{\prime}
or
{f}+{f}^{\prime}+{f}^{\prime\prime}+{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, respectively).
These new characters will never be included in optimal tokenisers’ solutions, and thus the previous proofs hold, with the difference that each dataset will require extra symbols once compressed.

A Single Long String.

In the previous sections, we considered tokenisers trained on a dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
.
Work on compression, however, usually considers a single long string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
as its input.
It is easy to see that direct tokenisation is not an NP-complete problem if its input is a single long string; including this string in vocabulary
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
already achieves optimal compression.
Bottom-up tokenisation, however, is still NP-complete even when given a single string as input.
As before, this can be shown with a similar strategy to
2
, but where we first append each string in dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
with a unique character
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}a}_{y}\}_{y=1}^{\infty}
and then concatenate all these strings.
As in the deduped case above, characters
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}a}_{y}
will never be merged by any optimal tokeniser; they will thus serve as virtual string delimiters and will not affect our proofs beyond an increase to the target compression size
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}
.

A Hybrid Approach.

Finally, the last variant we consider is a hybrid between direct and bottom-up tokenisation, where we find a merge sequence
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}
which—when we extract a vocabulary from it as
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}={\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\Sigma}\cup\{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}\circ{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}\mid\langle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}_{2}}\rangle\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{m}}\}

Tokenisation’s Connection to Compression

The variants of tokenisation that we consider here—with compression as their objective function—are closely related to the field of dictionary compression.
In both fields, we wish to reduce the size of an input (
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
) by exploiting repetitive elements.
In fact, the most popular tokenisation algorithm to date, BPE, was originally proposed as a compression algorithm
(
Gage 1994
)
and has only somewhat recently been ported into NLP to find tokenisers
(
Sennrich et al. 2016
, by)
.

Not surprisingly, prior work has also considered, from a theoretical perspective, the compression tokenisers achieve.

Zouhar et al. 2023b
, for instance, analyse bottom-up tokenisation and prove an approximation bound on the compression achieved by the tokenisers found using BPE.
More recently,
Kozma and Voderholzer 2024
also analyses bottom-up tokenisation, proving a tighter bound on this compression achieved by BPE.

A set of popular dictionary compression methods,
straight-line programs

(
Kieffer and Yang 2000
;
Charikar et al. 2005
, SLP;)
, can be used to illustrate the similarities and differences between tokenisers and compressors.
9
9

9

See
Lohrey 2012
for an overview of straight-line programs, and
Kempa and Prezza 2018
;
Kociumaka et al. 2023
for a more detailed overview of compression in general.

Given a string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
, an SLP describes a grammar from which
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
can be uniquely derived.
Formally, an SLP is a set of rules of form
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}X}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}a}
or
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}X}\to{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}A}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}B}
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}X},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}A},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}B}
are called nonterminals and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}a}
is a terminal.
10
10

10

Although not originally defined that way, SLP’s grammars are typically assumed to be in Chomsky normal form (CNF), for simplicity. This does not make a big difference for compression, but will be important for our purposes.

Starting from a special nonterminal
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}S}
, applying these rules exhaustively—until only terminals are left—produces exactly the desired string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
.
Notably, given a string
{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
, it is NP-complete to find the smallest SLP which generates it
Charikar et al. 2005
.

On the one hand, SLPs in Chomsky normal form are closely linked to bottom-up tokenisation; each of its rules expands to two nonterminals, and thus corresponds to a merge.
However, while SLPs must find the minimum number of merges (or rules) to fully compress a string into a single symbol, bottom-up tokenisers must maximally compress the string given a fixed number of merges.
On the other hand, SLPs which are not in Chomsky normal form are closely linked to direct tokenisation.
In this case, a direct tokeniser could be converted into an SLP with depth two; this grammar has a start rule
S\to{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
, and a rule from each subword to its characters
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
.
Again, while SLPs must find a minimal grammar representing the string, direct tokenisers must only minimise the size of rule
S\to{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}
given a fixed number of rules
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}c}}}
.

The paragraph above highlights two important differences between tokenisers and compressors.
First, tokenisers aim to reduce only the size of the resulting tokenised text (i.e.,
|{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}}}|
), whereas compressors also consider the size of the compression information (e.g., considering the size required to store
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}
, which would be
\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s}\in{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathcal{S}}}|{\color[rgb]{0,0.44,0.57}\mathtt{detok}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}s})|
).
This is because tokenisers must create shorter inputs for NLP algorithms, while compressors must make information compact.
Second, tokenisers and compressors have different optimisation parameters.
Compression algorithms always compress a string to the best extent possible (e.g., for SLPs, until a single nonterminal is reached).
Instead, tokenisation algorithms are given a maximum vocabulary size (i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K}
) and find tokenisers which only compress their input as much as possible until this limit is reached.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we proved the NP-completeness of two variants of tokenisation.
These results underline that finding optimal tokenisers most likely will remain a difficult quest and that research should focus on approximate algorithms instead.
Regarding those, there is potential both in improving the analysis of currently used algorithms, such as BPE, as well as in designing other, more involved algorithms.
Towards the latter, a more detailed look at the connections to compression algorithms might be fruitful.
While we investigated the complexity of two forms of tokenisation, similar results for other variants (e.g., with other objective functions) remain open; this would be exciting future work.
