Machine-Learning-Based Prediction of Long-Term Efficacy of Nemolizumab: Post Hoc Analysis of Pooled Data from Two Phase III Clinical Trials
Authors: Makoto Kawashima, Tomoya Maeda, Kotaro Iwasaki, Masashi Takizawa, Noriaki Kaneda, Kenji Kabashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNemolizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that specifically inhibits the receptor for interleukin-31, the major pruritogen in atopic dermatitis (AD). While the patient profile associated with the early therapeutic response to nemolizumab in AD has been increasingly delineated, the characteristics of patients who benefit from long-term nemolizumab therapy remain unclear. This study aimed to identify early clinical features predicting long-term improvement in pruritus Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score among patients receiving nemolizumab.MethodsThis study included patients participating in two Japanese clinical trials (JP01 and JP02) who did not achieve a 50% improvement in pruritus VAS score (VAS50) at week 16 after initiation of nemolizumab treatment. Patients who achieved VAS50 at week 52 were classified as responders, and those who did not were nonresponders. A machine-learning decision tree model was developed using 213 candidate variables. Model performance was evaluated using the F1 score. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values were used to interpret variable importance.ResultsAmong 118 eligible patients, the final model achieved an F1 score of 0.737. The two strongest predictors of long-term response were the absence of worsening of AD and/or the occurrence of other cutaneous disorders until week 16, and a weekly mean itch scale score < 2.1 at week 16. Patients meeting both criteria were highly likely to achieve VAS50 by week 52.ConclusionsMachine-learning identified straightforward and clinically applicable predictors of long-term nemolizumab efficacy in patients who did not have early VAS improvement. These findings may support decision-making for treatment continuation beyond week 16.Trial Registration NumberJapicCTI-173740 (JP01), and JapicCTI-183894 (JP02).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01768-0.

Introduction

Nemolizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to interleukin-31 (IL-31) receptor A (IL-31RA) to inhibit the IL-31 signaling pathway, which plays a role in pruritus transmission [1, 2]. Phase III clinical trials on nemolizumab in patients with atopic dermatitis (AD) in Japan (JP01 and JP02) showed that administering 60 mg nemolizumab subcutaneously every 4 weeks along with topical corticosteroid (TCS) or topical calcineurin inhibitor (TCI) use resulted in a greater reduction in pruritus and improvement in the signs and symptoms of AD than a placebo plus a topical agent [3, 4]. By improving pruritus and skin lesions, nemolizumab improves patients’ quality of life [5]. In Japan, nemolizumab is approved to mitigate pruritus associated with AD, and to treat AD itself in several other countries.

Recent advances have led to rapid diversification in AD treatment, highlighting the need to select optimal therapeutic strategies tailored to each patient’s condition. Given that long-term treatment continuity is required for AD management, both patients and physicians seek early perceived efficacy and sustained long-term effectiveness. In the JP01 and JP02 trials, 34.3% (excluding those who received rescue therapy) and 45.5% of patients, respectively, achieved a 50% improvement in the pruritus Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score (VAS50) at week 16 [4]. Furthermore, with long-term nemolizumab continuation (68 weeks in JP01 and 52 weeks in JP02), 57.3% and 55.7% of patients achieved VAS50, respectively [4]. These findings demonstrate three distinct patterns of patient responses: those who achieve early therapeutic effects against pruritus (rapid responders), those who benefit from long-term treatment continuation (responders), and those who show no response to therapy (nonresponders). This trend was similarly observed in improvement in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) scores [5], indicating heterogeneity in patient responses to nemolizumab treatment.

Clinically, deciding whether to continue long-term treatment with nemolizumab for AD is based on its antipruritic effects. In Japan, the optimal use guidelines recommend evaluating treatment response within 16 weeks and discontinuing therapy if no sufficient clinical benefit is achieved [6]. However, many patients require continued administration to maintain disease remission, and thus, it may be appropriate to continue treatment if clinically justified [6]. Therefore, the ability to predict long-term effectiveness at an early stage is crucial not only for supporting clinical decision‑making in accordance with these recommendations but also for sustaining patients’ motivation for treatment and minimizing the overall economic burden. Recently, real-world evidence from patients with AD treated with nemolizumab has increased. Masuo et al. investigated the characteristics of patients who responded rapidly to nemolizumab by stratifying them by treatment responsiveness [7]. Similarly, Sugiyama et al. investigated the patient profiles of those in whom nemolizumab treatment is considered appropriate on the basis of clinical observations and cutaneous adverse events [8]. Therefore, aggregating and analyzing real-world clinical data are crucial for the development of effective AD therapies. However, data on the characteristics of responders and nonresponders who eventually exhibit therapeutic effects under long-term nemolizumab administration are scarce, as such insights are likely to emerge only through long-term patient monitoring.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) methodologies for analyzing clinical data have drastically enhanced our ability to forecast patient outcomes, medication efficacy, and safety profiles. Although statistical predictive models have helped identify clinically relevant predictors [9], several studies have used more complex ML models to characterize patients with AD who are likely to benefit from biological agents and systemic treatments [10, 11]. Data analysis using ML enables the prediction of long-term efficacy from existing data without requiring extended patient monitoring. Because identifying characteristics of patients who are more likely to respond to long-term treatment with nemolizumab is critical, we aimed to develop an ML model to predict long-term efficacy (up to week 52) in patients who did not achieve VAS50 at week 16 and to identify early clinical indicators characterizing long-term responders in pooled data from the JP01 and JP02 Phase III trials.

Methods

Study Sample

This study included 211 patients with AD who were ≥ 13 years. These patients were administered 60 mg nemolizumab every 4 weeks for up to 52 weeks in two Japanese Phase III clinical trials: JP01 (JapicCTI-173740) and JP02 (JapicCTI-183894) [3, 4]. The patients had inadequate pruritic responses (score of ≥ 3 on a 5-level itch scale; range, 0–4, with higher scores indicating worse itching [12], and a VAS score for pruritus of 50 or more on the day of randomization) to treatments including medium-potency TCS or TCI for at least 4 weeks and oral antihistamines for at least 2 weeks. In the JP01 trial, concomitant AD medications (medium-potency TCS, TCI, and oral antihistamines) remained unchanged through week 16, with no limitations on their use thereafter until week 52. Patients who received rescue therapy due to AD exacerbations being included in the analysis. In the JP02 trial, no restrictions were placed on concomitant medications, except that patients who had received systemic steroids, immunosuppressants, phototherapy, desensitization therapy, or modulating therapy within 28 days prior to initiating nemolizumab were excluded.

Criteria for Responder Classification

Nemolizumab responders were defined as patients who achieved VAS50. Patients who met the VAS50 at week 16 were defined as “rapid responders” and were excluded from ML model development. Of the patients who did not achieve VAS50 at week 16, those who subsequently achieved VAS50 at week 52 were identified as “responders,” whereas those who did not were “nonresponders.” These latter two groups were included in ML model development. After 16 weeks of nemolizumab treatment, 121 of 211 patients failed to achieve VAS50. Data from 118 patients were included in the analysis after excluding 3 patients with missing values in at least one potential explanatory variable. ML Model Development

ML model development was conducted following the flow chart in Fig. 2. This approach incorporated 213 variables as predictors, including patient demographics, such as age, sex, height, and weight; disease severity measures, including EASI score, pruritus VAS score, and quality of life-associated patient-reported outcome, subscores; and laboratory parameters, at baseline and week 16, as well as the differences from the baseline to week 16 (Table S2). Strongly correlated pairs of explanatory variables (correlation coefficients > 0.8) were delineated to avoid multicollinearity. Subsequently, network analysis was performed to calculate the degree centrality for each factor, and the factors with the highest centrality were eliminated. This process was repeated until the correlation coefficients among all factors were < 0.8. Factors with a variance of 0 were considered to contain no informational value and were excluded.Fig. 2Process diagram for ML model development. ML machine learning

Process diagram for ML model development. ML machine learning

The patient dataset was randomly partitioned into an 80% training subset for ML model development and a 20% testing subset for performance assessment. A series of analyses confirmed the absence of specific bias in the VAS score distributions across the split datasets. A decision tree model was selected for the ML model [13], with the F1 score used to assess model performance. The F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, is especially relevant for evaluating models prioritizing both sensitivity and specificity [14, 15]. The model’s hyperparameters were optimized with GridSearchCV [16] within the range specified in Table S3. After an exhaustive search and stepwise selection [17], two explanatory variables were determined for use (Figure S1). The model was constructed using all combinations.

The entire process from data partitioning to model evaluation was iterated 10,000 times, extracting models with average F1 scores in the top 10% as “top models.” Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values were used to quantitatively evaluate the contribution of explanatory variables in the predictive model to identifying potential responder patient profiles [18]. SHAP values have positive and negative values, with the magnitude of the absolute value reflecting the explanatory variable’s contribution to distinguishing between “responders” and “nonresponders.” The relationships among explanatory variables were investigated by averaging the SHAP values for each patient across specific combinations of top models and examining variation in SHAP values across explanatory variables. This led to redeveloping the decision tree model (the integrated model) based on the progression of SHAP values for each variable. All analyses were performed in Python 3.8.18 with the SHAP (v.0.45.0) and scikit-learn (v.1.3.0) packages [18, 19].

Ethics

The present study was a post hoc analysis of pooled data from two previously conducted Japanese Phase III clinical trials, Study-JP01 (JapicCTI-173740) and Study-JP02 (JapicCTI-183894). Because this study involved only secondary analysis of data from the original trials and did not involve any new recruitment, intervention, or contact with participants, additional ethical approval was not required. The original trials were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and all other applicable regulatory requirements. The trial protocols and related documentation were approved by the institutional review boards at each participating center, including the Sapporo Skin Clinic IRB. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians prior to initiation of any study procedures and treatment. Publicly available ethics committee reference numbers/IRB numbers for the original trials could not be identified from the published reports.

Many patients presented with an itch scale score of approximately 2.1 or 3.1 at week 16; the shift from positive to negative SHAP values occurred at an itch scale score of 2.1, indicating that this threshold is a critical criterion in predictor of responders. Among the 69 patients with a mean itch scale score < 2.1, the SHAP values were > 0. However, among the 49 patients with a mean itch scale score ≥ 2.1, the SHAP values were < 0 (Fig. 3a), indicating a tendency to classify patients with an itch scale score < 2.1 as responders and those with an itch scale score ≥ 2.1 as nonresponders at week 52.

AD/OTCD, a categorical variable denoting exacerbation in AD or other cutaneous disorders, was defined as 0 for no exacerbation and 1 for exacerbation. Among the 24 patients who experienced worsening of skin symptoms (AD/OTCD = 1), the SHAP values were < 0, indicating the tendency to be classified as nonresponders. Conversely, among the 94 patients without deterioration in skin symptoms (AD/OTCD = 0), the SHAP values were > 0, suggesting the tendency to be classified as responders (Fig. 3b).

Longitudinal changes in pruritus VAS scores within each group and the distribution of percent change in the pruritus VAS score from baseline to week 52 were analyzed to investigate the characteristics of patients with discrepancies between predicted and actual responder status (Fig. 5b–e). Patients incorrectly predicted as responders but observed to be nonresponders typically showed intermediate VAS improvements (~40–50%) that did not reach the VAS50 threshold. Conversely, patients predicted to be nonresponders who were actually responders did not show consistent distinguishing features. Overall, these findings indicate that patients most likely to achieve VAS50 at week 52 are those who show no worsening of skin symptoms up to week 16 and maintain a mean itch scale score < 2.1 at that time, reinforcing the utility of early clinical indicators to guide long-term nemolizumab therapy.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop an ML model using two clinical trial datasets for nemolizumab to predict whether patients with AD who did not achieve VAS50 at week 16 will go on to demonstrate improvements at week 52 with nemolizumab treatment. In Japan, the guidelines for the use of nemolizumab recommend considering treatment discontinuation if efficacy is not observed 16 weeks after treatment initiation [6]. The insights obtained from our results may help inform decisions about whether to continue nemolizumab therapy.

We analyzed 118 patients with AD who participated in two Japanese Phase III trials (JP01 and JP02), had continuously received 60 mg of nemolizumab for 52 weeks, and did not achieve VAS50 by week 16. Although the conditions for concomitant medication use differed between JP01 and JP02, the proportions of rapid responders and responders were comparable across trials (Fig. 1), suggesting that these restrictions had a minimal impact on achieving VAS50.

During model construction, explanatory variable pairs with high F1 scores were initially narrowed down (Table 1). Among the top ten explanatory variable pairs, the mean itch scale score at week 16 or the change from weeks 0 to 16 consistently ranked highly, indicating the importance of the itch score at week 16 in predicting the pruritus VAS score at week 52. Notably, the predictive importance of the week 16 itch scale should be interpreted with caution, because this measure could partly reflect the persistence of symptom trajectory rather than an independent prognostic factor. In addition to AD/OTCD, clinical laboratory values, including urinalysis, protein, HDL cholesterol, and ALP, were evaluated as potential explanatory variables for the final model. Although these values are not exclusive to AD, they are associated with pruritus. For instance, patients with chronic kidney disease often experience pruritus due to skin dryness and uremic toxin accumulation, with elevated urinalysis protein levels and low HDL cholesterol [20, 21]. Additionally, elevated ALP levels indicate primary biliary cholangitis, a condition that causes pruritus in most patients [22]. HDL cholesterol levels negatively correlate with pruritus symptom burden in patients with primary biliary cholangitis [23]. Nonetheless, when constructing the predictive model for responders versus nonresponders to nemolizumab, the SHAP value distribution for these variables was limited. Despite high F1 scores, they were not included as explanatory variables.

The integrated model identified responders to nemolizumab as patients with no worsening of skin symptoms by week 16 after treatment and a pruritus score of < 2.1 at the same time point. Of the 51 responders, 38 (74.5%) matched these characteristics. In cases with skin symptom exacerbation (AD/OTCD = 1), patients were predicted to be nonresponders regardless of the itch scale score at week 16, with 20 of 25 patients (80.0%) being classified as nonresponders. According to multicenter retrospective studies, certain patients treated with nemolizumab have been observed to develop distinct clinical skin symptoms that tend to occur early in the treatment and are not significantly associated with baseline disease severity or biomarkers [24]. Although discontinuation of nemolizumab may be necessary in severe cases, mild symptoms can often be managed with strong potency agents. In fact, 25 of 90 rapid responders (patients who achieved VAS50 at week 16) (27.8%) experienced an exacerbation of skin symptoms by week 16, indicating that worsening skin conditions by week 16 with nemolizumab do not predict a lack of early improvement in VAS scores.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based exclusively on Japanese Phase III clinical trial populations, and therefore, its external validity remains uncertain, particularly in non-Japanese patients or in those with previous failure of biologic treatment. Second, as an exploratory post hoc analysis of controlled clinical trials, the findings may not fully generalize to real-world practice, and the strict inclusion criteria, limited sample size, and post hoc design raise the possibility of selection bias and overfitting despite repeated model training and internal validation. Third, the AD/OTCD variable was based on investigator-reported adverse events within a controlled trial environment, which does not reflect the lower visit frequency and less granular documentation in routine clinical care. Moreover, the heterogeneous nature of “other cutaneous disorders” may introduce variability (details in Table S4). Fourth, the model only applies to patients who do not achieve VAS50 at week 16 and therefore does not inform decision-making for rapid responders, who should not discontinue treatment even if they experience transient skin worsening. Finally, to enhance interpretability and clinical usability, the integrated model was simplified to include only two variables. The model predicts the likelihood of improvement in pruritus, rather than comprehensive AD control; future models should incorporate multidimensional outcomes, including EASI, patient-reported outcomes, and biomarker responses.

Conclusions

Applying ML, early clinical factors were identified that predict mid- to long-term improvements in pruritus VAS scores among patients treated with nemolizumab. Our findings show that the absence of worsening cutaneous symptoms through week 16 and an itch scale score < 2.1 at week 16 are robust yet straightforward indicators of subsequent long-term response. These predictors may enable clinicians to make more informed decisions about continuing nemolizumab administration beyond week 16 in patients who do not initially demonstrate VAS50. Although these results may not be universally generalizable, they underscore the importance of ongoing monitoring and individualized treatment decisions for patients receiving nemolizumab. Future studies that incorporate real-world datasets and broader clinical outcomes are essential to refine predictive tools and identify patients who may benefit most from long-term nemolizumab therapy.
