Carbapenem resistance in community-acquired urinary tract infections caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli in Morocco: a multicentre study (January 2022–December 2023)
Authors: Aboubakr Khazaz, Fatima El Otmani, Ihssane Benzaarate, Fatna Bourjilat, Adil El Hamouchi, Lala Fanomezantsoa Rafetrarivony, Bich-Tram Huynh, Tania Crucitti, Sylvain Brisse, Kaotar Nayme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) are leading causes of urinary tract infections (UTIs). In Morocco, carbapenem resistance data are scarce and largely hospital-based. We investigated carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates from community settings in Morocco.Material and MethodsIn this prospective multicentre study, we analysed 1296 non-duplicate E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates recovered from outpatients with community-acquired UTIs (CA-UTIs) across 12 Moroccan cities (January 2022–December 2023). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed following EUCAST guidelines. All phenotypically carbapenem-resistant isolates (n = 54, 13 E. coli, et 41 K. pneumoniae) were subjected to whole-genome sequencing (WGS) using Illumina technology to identify resistance determinants, mobile genetic elements and clonal lineages.Results E. coli predominated [n = 1000, 77.2% (95% CI: 74.8–79.4)] while K. pneumoniae accounted for 22.8% [n = 296 (95% CI: 20.6–25.2)]. Carbapenem resistance rates were significantly higher in K. pneumoniae than in E. coli [ertapenem: 21.1% (95% CI: 16.6–26.4) versus 2.4% (95% CI: 1.6–3.6); imipenem: 15.0% (CI: 11.2–19.6) versus 1.9% (95% CI: 1.2–3.0)].WGS revealed a high clonal diversity [eight sequence types (ST) in E. coli and 12 in K. pneumonnie]. Carbapenemase-mediated resistance was predominant in K. pneumoniae (notably blaNDM-14, blaNDM-7, blaNDM-5 and blaOXA-48). By contrast, E. coli primarily exhibited efflux and permeability defect mechanisms with sporadic carbapenemase-encoding gene carriage (blaNDM or blaOXA-48). The K. pneumoniae international high-risk clone ST147 producing NDM-14 was dominant (n = 23 isolates), followed by ST307 (n = 4), whereas ST410 was the most frequently identified among E. coli (n = 5).ConclusionCarbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae in community UTIs in Morocco, are driven by multiple clones. These findings underscore the urgent need to expand community-based AMR surveillance beyond hospital settings.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among Gram-negative bacteria represents a significant global health threat.1 Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) are leading aetiological agents of urinary tract infections (UTIs).1 Of particular concern is the emergence and proliferation of carbapenem-resistant strains, as carbapenems represent last-resort antibiotics for the management of severe infections.2 Carbapenem resistance is primarily driven by the acquisition of carbapenemase-encoding genes, often in combination with outer membrane porins alterations and overexpression of efflux pumps.

The spread of carbapenemase genes is frequently mediated by mobile genetic elements that enable horizontal gene transfer and rapid interspecies dissemination.3 These mechanisms are frequently associated with specific high-risk clonal lineages, characterized by multidrug resistance and persistence in both hospital and community settings.4 Notably, K. pneumoniae ST147 and ST307, as well as E. coli ST410, have emerged as major contributors to the global spread of carbapenem resistance.4–6 A deeper understanding of the epidemiology and molecular factors underpinning carbapenem resistance is therefore essential to inform effective infection control and therapeutic strategies.

Recent surveillance studies from the Middle East7,8 and North Africa9,10 (MENA) region have reported an increasing community-onset carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) among urinary isolates, but with marked geographic heterogeneity.7–10 These reports highlight the need for multicentre outpatient data to clarify local drivers and guide empirical therapy.

Importantly, structured surveillance frameworks focusing on community-onset UTIs and CRE11,12 have demonstrated that resistance patterns in in CA-UTIS may differ substantially from those observed in hospital-based cohorts, with direct implications for empirical treatment recommendations and antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in under-represented regions such as North Africa.11

In Morocco, data on carbapenem resistance remains limited13,14,15 and are predominantly derived from hospital-based investigations.13,16,17 In addition, although several carbapenemase genes and high-risk ST have already been described in Moroccan hospital-based studies,13,14 no previous investigation in Morocco has specifically addressed carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae in a multicentre, multi-regional cohort restricted to outpatients with CA-UTIs using whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Moreover, data from North Africa remain limited.9,10

The rising incidence of carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae in clinical settings raises concern regarding their potential community-level dissemination. However, to date, no study has specifically explored the prevalence and molecular epidemiology of carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae in CA-UTIs. This knowledge gap significantly hampers the setup of public health policies on AMR in Morocco. Here, we aimed to assess the frequency of carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolated from CA-UTIs in Morocco, and to characterize their genomic resistance and clonal backgrounds.

Methods

Origin of bacterial isolates

This prospective multicentre study included 1296 non-duplicate E. coli (n = 1000) and K. pneumoniae (n = 296) isolates, recovered from outpatients with CA-UTIs, between January 2022 and December 2023.

Isolates were collected through private medical analysis laboratories located in 12 Moroccan cities: Casablanca (n = 963), Beni Mellal (n = 114), Tanger/Tétouan (n = 62), Agadir (n = 44), El Jadida (n = 34), Oujda (n = 22), Marrakech (n = 15), Laayoune (n = 15), Settat (n = 14), Fes (n = 7) and Rabat (n = 6).

Participating laboratories were recruited on a voluntary basis and asked to submit consecutive eligible isolates during the study period. CA-UTI were defined as symptomatic UTIs occurring in outpatients with no hospitalization, no residence in a long-term care facility and no indwelling urinary catheter within the preceding 3 months.12,18 Only urine cultures yielding E. coli or K. pneumoniae were included. A single isolate per patient was retained.

For each isolate, patient age, gender, date of collection (if available) and species identity were recorded.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2008) and the CIOMS guidelines (2002). Ethical approval was obtained from the Comité d’Éthique pour la Recherche Biomédicale de Rabat (CERB; reference 85/21).

As the study involved bacterial isolates obtained through routine diagnostic procedures and no identifiable patient data were collected, individual informed consent was not required under national regulations. The ethical approval permits the sharing of anonymized microbiological and genomic data generated in this study.

Culture and storage of isolates

Isolates were recovered on conservation agar (Bio-rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) and subcultured overnight at 37°C in Brain Heart Infusion broth (BHI) (Bio-rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). They were subsequently isolated on MacConkey agar (BioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) and incubated at 37°C overnight. Species identification was confirmed using the API 20E system (Biomérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) or the Vitek 2 system (BioMérieux, Craponne, France). All isolates were stored at −20°C in BHI supplemented with 15% glycerol.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed by disc diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (Bio-Rad, Marne-la-Coquette, France) according to EUCAST recommendations. The following antibiotics (bioMérieux, Marcy L’Étoile, France) were tested for all isolates: ampicillin (10 µg), ticarcillin (75 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20–10 µg), cefalexin (30 µg), ceftazidime (10 µg), ertapenem (10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25–23.5 μg).

For carbapenem-resistant isolates, additional antibiotics were tested including cefoxitin (30 µg), ticarcillin-clavulanic acid (75–10 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefepime (30 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), ceftazidime-avibactam (10–4 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg) and tobramycin (10 µg). Results were interpreted according to EUCAST clinical breakpoints version 11.0 (2021). This version was applied uniformly to all isolates to ensure comparability of results across the study period (2022–2023). Disc diffusion breakpoints for Enterobacterales including carbapenems, remained unchanged between EUCAST 2021 and subsequent versions, supporting comparability with current standards. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain.

For analysis, isolates categorized as Susceptible (S) at increased exposure (I) and Resistant (R)’ were grouped as non-susceptible (R). MDR was defined as resistance to at least one antibiotic from three or more antibiotic classes (beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides and sulphonamides).19 Carbapenem resistance was defined as non-susceptibility to ertapenem and/or imipenem.

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production

Carbapenemase production was detected using the RAPIDEC® CARBA NP test (BioMérieux, Craponne, France), which detects carbapenem hydrolysis via pH change. In parallel, the immunoassay NG-Test CARBA 5 (NG Biotech, Guipry, France) was used to detect and differentiate the five major carbapenemase families (KPC, NDM, VIM, OXA-48 and IMP). Isolates were also cultured on Chromagar CRE (CHROMagar, Paris, France) to screen for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales. This medium detects carbapenem resistance regardless of mechanism.

Whole-genome sequencing

Bacterial isolates showing carbapenem resistance were subjected to WGS. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh cultures of K. pneumoniae and E. coli grown in Luria–Bertani medium (Oxoid, UK) at 37°C for 24 h using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany).

DNA quantification was performed using the Qubit dsDNA BR Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), which features an extended dynamic range of up to 4000 ng/µL, ensuring accurate quantification of bacterial DNA.

Libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). Isolates collected between January and May 2022 were sequenced at the Institut Pasteur de Paris on a Nextseq 550 apparatus (Illumina Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). The remaining isolates were sequenced at the sequencing platform of the Institut Pasteur du Maroc on a NextSeq 2000 sequencer (Illumina Inc. San Diego, CA, USA).

Bioinformatics analyses of sequence data

For assembly, we used bioinformatics analysis tools and pipelines available on the Galaxy Africa platform (https://africa.usegalaxy.eu/). The bioinformatics workflow began with quality control of raw sequence data using FastQC and Trimmomatic tools to ensure high-quality reads. Next, de novo assembly was performed using SPAdes (v.3.14.1). Per-isolate quality control metrics (N50 value, number of contigs and total assembly size) are provided in Supplementary data Table S3 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Whole-genome assemblies of K. pneumoniae and E. coli isolates were analysed using Kleborate v.3.2.3,20,21 a genotyping tool designed to detect clinically relevant genomic features. Species identification was based on Mash distance to curated references, with strong matches defined by a distance ≤0.02. STs were assigned using the internal MLST module, which references the EnteroBase scheme for E. coli and the BIGSdb-Pasteur scheme for K. pneumoniae.22,23

Acquired antimicrobial resistance genes, chromosomal mutations and gene truncations in both species were identified using Kleborate,21 which integrates a curated CARD database tailored to each species complex.

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using SKA2 v.2.1.0, which generated core SNP alignments based on split k-mer analysis. Multiple sequence alignments were then used to infer maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees with RAxML v.8.2.12, applying the GTR + GAMMA model. Midpoint rooting was performed with the Bio.Phylo module of Biopython v.1.79. Clonal clusters were defined using a threshold of ≤21 pairwise core SNPs, consistent with recent genomic studies on Enterobacterales transmission dynamics.24 Recombination analysis was not performed, as the phylogenetic analysis was merely performed for clustering purposes rather than evolutionary ones. The resulting trees were visualized interactively using the Microreact platform, allowing dynamic exploration of phylogenetic structure and associated metadata.

Interactive phylogenetic trees annotated with metadata (city, year, species, ST and carbapenemase genes) are publicly available at https://microreact.org/project/5EfxCHp4ui2vfq1LoY7mMR-kpp-carb-a-maroc-sara for K. pneumoniae and https://microreact.org/project/sMDKRqRBFrivejqBBA8MC1-ecoli-carba-maroc-sara for E. coli.

Identification of plasmids was achieved using Pathogenwatch with the resulting data available in the project on Pathogenwatch (‘https://pathogen.watch/collection/kqfie1m7×7kg-k-pneumoniae-sara-project-morocco’ and ‘https://pathogen.watch/collection/phz3krys71ry-e-coli-sara-project-morocco’). Sequencing data have been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under BioProject PRJEB104706.

Data availability and submission

WGS data generated in this study are available in the ENA under the SARA project (BioProject PRJEB104706). Accession numbers for all sequenced isolates are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Differences in proportions were assessed using the Chi-square test. A P value inferior to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3. (R Core Team, 2020) with functions from the stats package.

Results

Epidemiological data

Clinical data were available for 1094 isolates of the 1296 total isolates including 849 of 1000 E. coli and 245 of 296 K. pneumoniae, for which demographic characteristics were further analysed.

The majority of isolates were obtained from female patients for both E. coli (67.8%) and K. pneumoniae (56.7%). The age group with the highest number of isolates for both species was ≥61 years, with 420 E. coli and 113 K. pneumoniae isolates, respectively (Table 1). Differences in age group distribution by gender were statistically significant for both species (P value 2.89 × 10−6 for E. coli and P = 0.0161 for K. pneumoniae).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing results

All collected isolates (1000 E. coli and 296 K. pneumoniae) were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility. The rates of non-susceptible isolates for each species are presented in Figure 1. K. pneumoniae exhibited higher resistance rates than E. coli for all tested antibiotics, including carbapenems. Exceptions were cefalexin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, for which resistance rates did not differ significantly between the two species.

As expected, due to its native chromosomal beta-lactamase, K. pneumoniae showed the highest resistance rate to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (95% CI: 92.5%–97.1%, n = 282). In E. coli, resistance rates were also high, particularly to ampicillin (83.6%, 95% CI: 81.2%–85.7%, n = 836) and ticarcillin (75.9%, 95% CI: 73.2%–78.5%, n = 759). Among third generation cephalosporins, K. pneumoniae exhibited notably high resistance to ceftazidime with a rate of 50.5% (95% CI: 44.8%–56.2%, n = 150).

Approximately half of the isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin, with rates of 54.8% (95% CI: 48.9%–60.6%) (n = 162) for K. pneumoniae and 45% (95% CI: 42.0%–48.1%, n = 450) for E. coli. Among aminoglycosides, amikacin was relatively more effective than gentamicin in both species. In E. coli, resistance was moderate, (16.9%, 95% CI: 14.6%–19.5%, n = 169) for amikacin and for gentamicin (22.2%, 95% CI: 19.6%–25.1%, n = 222). By contrast, K. pneumoniae showed higher resistance levels, with rate of 30.5% (95% CI: 25.4%–36.2%, n = 90) for amikacin and 43% (95% CI: 37.1%–49.0%, n = 127) for gentamicin.

Marked differences were observed for carbapenems. K. pneumoniae exhibited resistance rates of 21.1% (95% CI: 16.7%–26.2%, n = 62) to ertapenem and 15.0% (95% CI: 11.2%–19.7%, n = 44) to imipenem, whereas E. coli displayed notably lower resistance rates, with 2.4% (95% CI: 1.5%–3.5%, n = 24) to ertapenem and 1.9% (95% CI: 1.1%–2.9%, n = 19) to imipenem.

Among carbapenem-resistant isolates (62 K. pneumoniae and 24 E. coli), the RAPIDEC® CARBA NP test detected carbapenemase production in 32 K. pneumoniae and two E. coli isolates. NG-Test CARBA-5 further characterized these isolates, revealing NDM type carbapenemases in 29 K. pneumoniae isolates, OXA-48 in one and co-production of NDM and OXA-48 in two isolates. Among the E. coli isolates, one produced NDM and one produced OXA-48.

Isolates negative by both RAPIDEC® CARBA NP and NG-Test CARBA-5 were subsequently assessed using chromogenic medium specific for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae. This medium detected an additional 9 K. pneumoniae and 11 E. coli carbapenem-resistant isolates (Figure 2). Strains resistant to at least one of the tests (RAPIDEC® CARBA NP, NG-Test CARBA-5 or chromogenic medium Chromagar CRE) were selected for WGS.

Further antibiotic susceptibility testing of carbapenem-resistant strains

Carbapenem-resistant isolates (41 K. pneumoniae and 13 E. coli) were further subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing with expanded antimicrobial (Tables S1 and S2). Resistance to ertapenem, imipenem and meropenem was observed in 54/54, 40/54 and 19/54 isolates, respectively. By species, resistance rates in K. pneumoniae were 41/41 (100%) for ertapenem, 34/41 (82.9%) for imipenem and 17/41 (41.5%) for meropenem. In E. coli, resistance rates were 13/13 (100%), 6/13 (46.2%) and 2/13 (15.4%), respectively, for ertapenem, imipenem and meropenem. Overall, 19 isolates were resistant to all three carbapenems. These triple-resistant isolates were predominantly K. pneumoniae (17/19), of which most (14/17) harboured carbapenemase genes, mainly blaNDM-14, but also blaNDM-5, blaNDM-7 and blaOXA-48. These genes were frequently associated with blaCTX-M-15 and, in K. pneumoniae, with alterations in OmpK35 and OmpK36.

Forty K. pneumoniae isolates were classified as MDR, exhibiting diverse antibiotypes, frequently combining resistances to beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and sulphonamides (Table S1). Notably, seven K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to all tested antibiotics, including both the standard and supplementary panels.

Ceftazidime-avibactam resistance was observed in 12 carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates and in none of the E. coli isolates. Comparison of phenotypes and genotypes (Tables S1 and S2), showed that ceftazidime-avibactam non-susceptibility was predominantly associated with NDM-producing isolates (11/12), although 17 NDM-positive isolates remained ceftazidime-avibactam susceptible by disc diffusion. One ceftazidime-avibactam non-susceptible isolate (KPP309) lacked a blaNDM determinant and carried blaDHA-1, suggesting alternative resistance mechanisms.

Nevertheless, the MDR rate among E. coli was also high, with 10 out of 13 isolates being resistant to at least three antibiotic classes.

Sequence types identification and phylogenetic analysis

WGS revealed a diverse range of STs among the isolates. In K. pneumoniae, 13 STs were identified, including two novel previously uncharacterized types: one ST557-SLV (single locus variant) differing from ST577 in gapA and one ST180-SLV differing from ST180 in the rpoB gene. ST147 was predominant (24/41 isolates, 58.5%), largely detected in Casablanca (22/24 isolates), followed by ST307 (seven isolates) and ST16 (three isolates) (Figure 3).

Genotypic analysis of resistance determinants

A wide variety of resistance determinants was identified, corresponding to resistance to multiple antibiotics, including aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolone, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and beta-lactams/carbapenems (Figure S1 and S2).

Among K. pneumoniae (n = 41), 30 isolates (73%) harboured one or more carbapenemase-encoding genes (Figure 3). blaNDM-14 was the most prevalent, detected in 24 isolates (59%), followed by blaNDM-5 (three isolates, 7%) and blaNDM-7 (two isolates, 5%). A single isolate harboured blaOXA-48 which did not carry a NDM encoding gene (Figure 3). Co-occurrence of blaNDM-5 and blaOXA-181 was observed in two isolates.

Alterations in outer membrane porins were frequent, with OmpK35 identified in 31 isolates (76%) and OmpK36 alterations in three isolates (7%). Among genes involved in beta-lactam resistance, blaCTX-M-15 was highly prevalent (36 isolates, 88%), followed by blaOXA-9 (25 isolates, 61%) and blaOXA-1 (11 isolates, 27%).

Fluoroquinolone resistance was observed in all strains (n = 41) and was linked to mutations in both gyrA and parC in 34 isolates (83%). Plasmid-mediated resistance genes were also identified, including qnrS1 (30 isolates, 66%) and qnrB4 and qnrB6 (5 isolates each, 12%). The aac(6’)-Ib-cr gene, conferring resistance to both aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones, was found in 28 isolates (68%). Other aminoglycoside resistance genes included aadA (18 isolates, 44%) and aph(3’)-VI (15 isolates, 37%). Resistance to sulphonamide resistance genes sul1 and sul2 were present in 36 (88%) and 34 (83%) isolates, respectively. In addition, five isolates had an mgrB mutation that confers resistance to colistin.

In E. coli, carbapenem resistance due to enzymatic mechanisms was identified in three isolates, harbouring the blaNDM-5 gene, blaOXA-48 or a non-defined blaNDM gene. Efflux pump components and regulatory genes were detected, including marR (two isolates), cyaA (two isolates) and nfsA (two isolates). Beta-lactam resistance was conferred by genes such as blaTEM variants (10 isolates, 76.9%), blaCTX-M-15 (10 isolates, 69%), blaCTX-M-14b (one isolate) and blaOXA-1 (nine isolates, 69%), AmpC beta-lactamase blaCMY-2 and blaDHA-1 were each detected in one isolate. Aminoglycosides resistance was mostly mediated by aac(6’)-Ib-cr5 (eight isolates, 62%), aph(3'’)-Ib (seven isolates, 54%) and aadA5 (five isolates, 38%). Fluoroquinolone resistance arose from mutations in gyrA (D87N and S83L) (nine isolates each, 69%) and parC (S80I) (10 isolates, 77%), along with plasmid-mediated resistance gene qnrB4 detected in one isolate.

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance was associated with sul1 and sul2 62% (each eight isolates, 61.5%) and dfrA1 gene (five isolates, 38.5%).

Mobile genetic elements

The plasmid analysis identified Col(pHAD28) as the predominant replicon along K. pneumoniae isolates, found in 31 of the 41 isolates. Other plasmid types were less frequent, including Col(MG828) and IncFIA(pBK30683) each detected in three isolates, whereas ColRNAI and IncFIB(K) were identified in two isolates each (Figure 3).

In contrast, E. coli isolates exhibited a wider variety of mobile genetic elements. IncFIA and IncFIB(AP001918) were the most common plasmids, each identified in nine of the 13 strains. IncQ1 and Col(BS512) were also frequently detected, present in six and four isolates, respectively. Other plasmids, such as IncX4 and IncB/O/K/Z, were found in two isolates each, while unique plasmid types, such as IncFIB(pNDM-Mar), ColRNAI, were identified in single isolates (Figure 4).

Discussion

The rise of AMR in community-acquired infections constitutes a growing yet often underestimated public health threat. All isolates analysed in this study were obtained from outpatients without recent hospitalization, supporting their community origin and providing novel data compared with previous Moroccan reports restricted to hospital settings.13 The broad geographic coverage (from Tangier and Oujda in the North to Agadir and Laayoun in the South) suggests that carbapenem resistance is disseminated nationwide. The novelty of this study lies in showing that carbapenemase genes already documented in Moroccan hospital-based settings,13,14 together with high-risk clones previously described worldwide in hospital and community settings using WGS,4–6 are also circulating among outpatients with CA-UTIs in Morocco.

We observed a marked increase in carbapenem resistance rates compared with previous national data. In E. coli, imipenem resistance increased from 0.4% to 1.9%,25 and ertapenem resistance from 1.1% to 2.4%,17 while in K. pneumoniae, imipenem resistance rose from 1.1% to 15%.26 These findings indicate that carbapenem resistance is no longer confined to healthcare settings. This contrasts with previous multicentre studies on CA-UTIS that reported negligible carbapenem resistance among outpatients.27 Across nearly all antibiotic classes, most notably carbapenems, K. pneumoniae exhibited higher resistance rates than E. coli underlying its well-recognized capacity to acquire resistance determinants.

Among E. coli isolates, ST410 was the most frequent lineage and included NDM producers, ST10 producing the OXA-48 carbapenemase was also detected. ST410 is a globally disseminated high-risk clone associated with beta-lactam resistance, and carrying a wide array of fluoroquinolone and aminoglycoside resistance determinants.4 ST410 has been involved in outbreaks in Denmark,28 France,29 China,30 South Korea,31 the USA32 and Egypt.33

The carbapenem resistance profiles observed among the 13 sequenced E. coli isolates reveals the coexistence of enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms. Most isolates resistant only to ertapenem did not have carbapenemase gene, but harboured ESBLs (e.g. blaCTX-M-15, blaOXA-1). (Table 2). Ertapenem resistance may occur in carbapenemase-negative isolates through alternative mechanisms such as porin alterations, efflux upregulation, or Protein Binding Penicillin mutations34,35 highlighting the contribution of chromosomal adaptations acting synergistically with betalactamases.

These findings reinforce current recommendations suggesting that ertapenem, and to some extent imipenem, lack specificity as screening markers for carbapenemase production, whereas meropenem offers greater predictive accuracy, especially when combined with phenotypic or molecular confirmation methods.36

In our dataset, this pattern was reflected by the observation that meropenem resistance was restricted to a smaller subset of isolates than ertapenem or imipenem resistance, supporting its greater discriminatory value for carbapenemase-associated phenotypes. By contrast, isolated ertapenem resistance may also arise from non-carbapenemase mechanisms, such as reduced permeability or porin alterations.

In K. pneumoniae, ST147 producing NDM-14 predominated, confirming and extending our previous report15 and indicating spread of this high-risk clone in the community. ST307 was also detected. Twenty-four ST147 isolates harboured blaNDM-14, a variant recently reported in France and linked to cases expatriated from Morocco.37 In that study, the blaNDM-14-carrying plasmid was fully reconstructed by long-read sequencing and shown to be an IncFIB plasmid of 54 061 bp, consistently present across ST147 strains (GenBank accession: PRJNA925431) suggesting successful plasmid-mediated dissemination. The genetic environment of blaNDM-14 included a truncated ISAba125 interrupted by ISSpu-2 and an upstream aph(3’)-VI gene, a configuration distinct from typical NDM contexts.37 This clone has been implicated in multiple outbreaks worldwide. Its recurring emergence across continents highlights its pandemic potential and ability for local adaptation.36

Seven K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited pan-resistance, representing a major public health concern.19 Of these, four belonged to ST147 producing NDM-14, and all harboured Col(pHAD28) plasmids. while others included Two ST16 co-producing NDM-5 and OXA-181, and also carrying Col(pHAD28) or Col440II, and one ST307 isolate producing NDM-7 and harbouring Col(pHAD28).

Resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam was restricted to a subset of blaNDM-positive K. pneumoniae isolates. Although avibactam does not inhibit metallo-β-lactamases, the persistence of phenotypic susceptibility in some NDM-producing isolates has been reported and may reflect variability in enzyme expression levels, permeability defects or methodological limitations of disc diffusion testing,38–41 including zinc availability.

Metallo-β-lactamases require zinc as a cofactor and low in zinc levels in testing media may reduce activity, leading to false-susceptible result.41 Zinc supplementation can restore enzyme activity and reveal ceftazidime-avibactam resistance in isolates initially testing susceptible.41 Porin mutations affecting drug permeability may also influence susceptibility although their impact on ceftazidime-avibactam phenotypes remains unclear.40 These findings align with recent regional reports of low ceftazidime-avibactam susceptibility among carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales.42 The absence of MIC testing in our study limits definitive classification and should be considered when interpreting ceftazidime-avibactam susceptibility results.38,39

WGS revealed distinct carbapenem resistance strategies between K. pneumoniae and E. coli. K. pneumoniae predominantly relied on horizontally acquired carbapenemase genes, including blaNDM-14, blaNDM-5, blaOXA-48 and blaOXA-181, frequently combined with porin mutations and mgrB mutations.5,9 E. coli more frequently displayed chromosomal mechanisms such as overexpression of efflux pumps marA, mutations in regulators and a combination of OmpC and beta-lactamases, which contribute to reduced carbapenem susceptibility.43,44 Our observations align with previous data,45,46 suggesting enhanced horizontal gene acquisition in K. pneumoniae and greater genomic plasticity in E. coli.

From a One Health perspective, both ST410 and ST147 have been increasingly detected in environmental reservoirs and the food chain, wastewater, poultry and retail meat47,48 suggesting potential routes for community transmission beyond clinical settings.

The carbapenemase landscape in Morocco has evolved. Earlier reports (before 2018) identified blaOXA-48 and blaIMP as predominant genes,49 whereas recent data (since 2022), indicate expansion of blaNDM variants,16 consistent with global trends50 and similar shifts observed in other regions.51

Several factors should be considered when interpreting these findings. The uneven geographic distribution of isolates may affect representativeness. The use of disc diffusion without MIC confirmation may limit precision for borderline. Short-read sequencing restricted full plasmid reconstruction, and long-read sequencing improves resolution of resistance plasmids and transmission pathways. Finally, the absence of clinical outcome and antimicrobial treatment data limited assessment of clinical consequences of the observed resistance.

Conclusions

This study provides a large-scale phenotypic and genomic investigation of carbapenem-resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolated from outpatients with UTIs across Morocco. The detection of high-risk clones and pan resistant lineages and expanding NDM variants signals silent community dissemination into the community. These findings underscore the need to strengthen investment in genomic AMR surveillance, antibiotic stewardship and One Health-oriented interventions to kerb further spread of MDR bacteria across clinical and environmental interfaces.
