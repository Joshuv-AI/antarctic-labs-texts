High daily fructose intake and late-evening fruit consumption are associated with ultrasonographically assessed hepatic steatosis in family medicine outpatients
Authors: Emine Ediz, H Nejat Küçükdağ, Mehmet Ali Özel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough high fructose consumption is known to be associated with metabolic disorders, specific intake thresholds and consumption patterns related to hepatic steatosis have not been fully defined. This study aimed to quantify daily fructose intake and examine its association with ultrasonographically assessed hepatic steatosis, including the potential identification of intake thresholds associated with steatosis. This cross-sectional study included 189 adults (89 men, 100 women). Daily fructose intake was assessed using a seven-day food consumption record. Hepatic steatosis was evaluated and graded by hepatobiliary ultrasonography. Metabolic parameters, including fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and lipid profiles, were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression and ROC analyses were performed to assess associations and identify cutoff values. Hepatic steatosis was detected in 63.0% of participants. Individuals with a daily fructose intake > 50 g had significantly higher triglyceride, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR levels compared with those consuming ≤ 50 g (p < 0.005). Evening or night-time fruit consumption was independently associated with higher odds of hepatic steatosis (OR = 4.12, p = 0.004). ROC analysis identified a threshold value of 51.07 g/day for daily fructose intake. In multivariable analysis, fructose intake above this threshold emerged as a primary factor associated with hepatic steatosis (OR = 14.64, 95% CI: 5.96–35.96; p < 0.001), followed by body mass index and age. The model demonstrated good discriminatory performance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.613; accuracy 84.1%). A daily fructose intake above approximately 51 g/day showed a significant association with hepatic steatosis in this study population. In addition, consumption timing—particularly fruit intake in the late evening—was identified as an important behavioral factor associated with steatosis. These findings highlight the potential relevance of both intake quantity and timing in future dietary recommendations and research on hepatic steatosis.Keywords: Fructose intake, Hepatic steatosis, Ultrasonography, ROC analysis, ChrononutritionSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Fructose is a naturally occurring monosaccharide found in fruits, vegetables, and honey. In addition to its presence in whole foods, fructose is widely incorporated into manufactured products in the form of high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) and sucrose due to its low cost and high sweetness capacity. Sweetened beverages, packaged snacks, desserts, and processed foods collectively contribute to increased daily fructose intake in many populations1.

Following ingestion, fructose is metabolized primarily in the liver through insulin-independent pathways. This process bypasses the rate-limiting steps of glycolysis and can lead to enhanced substrate availability for de novo lipogenesis, increased hepatic triglyceride accumulation, oxidative stress, and elevated uric acid production. As a result, high fructose intake has been linked to metabolic and cardiometabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance, obesity, dyslipidemia, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)2.

NAFLD is defined by the accumulation of fat in hepatocytes (≥ 5%) in the absence of excessive alcohol intake and is now recognized as the most common chronic liver disease worldwide3. Prevalence estimates suggest that NAFLD affects approximately one-quarter of the global population and demonstrates a rising trend in parallel with increasing obesity and metabolic syndrome rates3. NAFLD encompasses a broad spectrum ranging from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis4. Accordingly, accurate assessment of hepatic fat accumulation is essential in both clinical and research settings. Ultrasonography remains the most widely used screening and assessment tool due to its availability, low cost, and non-invasive nature5.

Although experimental and clinical studies have suggested a link between fructose consumption and hepatic fat accumulation6, the daily fructose exposure patterns of community-based adults and their relationship with ultrasonographically assessed steatosis are not well characterized.

This study aimed to assess daily fructose intake and examine its association with ultrasonographic hepatic steatosis among adults attending our family medicine outpatient clinic, including the evaluation of intake thresholds that may be associated with differences in steatosis status.

Methodology

Study design and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Family Medicine Outpatient Clinics of Duzce University Research and Training Hospital, a tertiary care centre whose family medicine clinic predominantly serves individuals presenting for periodic health examinations. Adults who presented to the outpatient clinic between January 1, 2024, and the end of the data collection period were screened for eligibility.

A consecutive sampling method was applied, and all individuals meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. Participants received a Food Consumption Record Form to complete daily over a seven-day period. Those who returned with a properly completed form were included in the study.

Adults aged 18 years or older who attended the outpatient clinic and had sufficient cognitive capacity to complete the questionnaire were screened for eligibility. To avoid the confounding effects of acute metabolic stress, recruitment was limited to patients presenting for routine periodic health examinations or follow-up visits for stable chronic conditions; individuals presenting for acute care (e.g., acute infections) were not included.

Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to participate, cognitive impairment or a psychiatric disorder preventing effective communication, pregnancy, and failure to complete at least 90% of the questionnaire items. To rule out secondary causes of hepatic steatosis, patients with known chronic liver diseases (such as viral or autoimmune hepatitis), prior acute liver injury, active malignancy, severe uncontrolled systemic illnesses, or the use of known steatogenic medications (e.g., systemic corticosteroids, methotrexate, tamoxifen, amiodarone) were excluded. Furthermore, any current or past alcohol consumption was a strict exclusion criterion, meaning all included participants were lifetime abstainers. (This completely alcohol-free profile is highly prevalent in the Turkish population due to cultural reasons, which allowed us to entirely eliminate alcohol as a confounding factor.) Finally, adherence to a specifically fructose-restricted diet, as determined by self-report during the initial clinical interview, was also an exclusion criterion.A total of 189 participants were enrolled.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis determined that a minimum of 189 participants was required to detect a statistically significant difference with a 5% Type I error (α = 0.05), 80% power (1–β = 0.80), and an effect size of 0.226, corresponding to a 95% confidence level. The effect size was estimated based on differences in fructose intake between participants with and without hepatic steatosis reported in previous studies.

Data collection

Two structured instruments were used: a Descriptive Questionnaire Form and a Food Consumption Record Form, both prepared through literature review.

Participants recorded their consumption of fructose-containing foods and beverages daily for seven days, noting the type of food, amount consumed, and time of intake. Upon returning with completed forms, hepatobiliary ultrasonography was performed to assess liver fat content and liver size.

Descriptive questionnaire

The questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics (age, marital status, education level, income), dietary habits (including fruit and sweet preferences), hunger response. Physical activity status was also evaluated by asking participants whether they engaged in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (e.g., brisk walking) per week. Additionally, adherence to specialized dietary patterns that exclude specific food groups (e.g., vegetarian, vegan, or gluten-free diets) was explicitly queried.

Food consumption record form

The form included foods high in fructose and provided space for participants to add other consumed items. We quantified fructose intake (g/day) using nutrient values sourced from the TurKomp National Food Composition Database (version 1.0)7 and estimated portion sizes according to TUBER guidelines from the Turkish Ministry of Health8.

Hepatobiliary ultrasonography

As part of the study protocol, hepatobiliary ultrasound was performed specifically for research purposes for all eligible participants who completed the dietary records. Ultrasound examinations were performed by the same radiologist using a GE Logiq S8 (GE Healthcare, WI, USA) device. To prevent observer bias and ensure the validity of the imaging assessment, the radiologist performing and interpreting the examinations was completely blinded to the participants’ clinical data and daily fructose consumption levels.

Liver echogenicity, posterior acoustic attenuation, vascular border clarity, and liver dimensions were evaluated. Specifically, liver size was assessed by measuring the maximum craniocaudal length in the right midclavicular line. Hepatic steatosis was graded as:

Grade 1 (mild) – mild diffuse echogenicity, normal diaphragm and vessel visualization.

Grade 2 (moderate) – moderate echogenicity with partial loss of diaphragm/vessel clarity.

Grade 3 (severe) – marked echogenicity with poor visualization of posterior liver and vascular structures.

Statistical analysis

Normality of distributions was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variance using the Levene test.

Group comparisons used Independent Samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, according to distribution.

Categorical variables were analyzed with Pearson Chi-square, Fisher’s Exact, or Fisher–Freeman–Halton tests, as appropriate.

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median (interquartile range) or min–max for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are summarized as frequency and percentage.

For comparative analyses, daily fructose intake was categorised into two groups (≤ 50 g/day and > 50 g/day), in line with thresholds reported in previous literature9,10.

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v22, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Duzce University (date: December 4, 2023, approval no: 2023/192).

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Regarding participants’ exercise habits, 29.6% (n = 56) reported regular physical activity, whereas 70.4% (n = 133) reported lower activity levels (Table 1). Based on the descriptive questionnaire, none of the participants in the final cohort reported following specialized dietary patterns (such as vegetarian, vegan, or gluten-free diets).

Hepatic steatosis was present in 119 participants (63.0%) (Table 1). Among them, 70 (58.8%) had Grade 1, 44 (37.0%) Grade 2, and 5 (4.2%) Grade 3 steatosis.

Snack foods were the most commonly selected first option when participants felt hungry (57.1%), and daily fructose intake was significantly higher among these individuals (p < 0.001). No significant association was found with the number of major meals consumed per day (p = 0.444), whereas fructose intake increased in relation to the number of eating occasions beyond major meals (p = 0.028).

Most participants consumed fruit in the evening or at night, rather than in the morning or noon. The likelihood of hepatic steatosis was found to be significantly higher among those consuming fruit in the evening and at night (χ²(3) = 18.94, p < 0.001) (Table 2). To further clarify this association and account for potential confounding factors, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Fruit consumption time was grouped as morning/noon and evening/night. After adjustment for age, BMI, physical activity, and daily fructose intake, evening or night fruit consumption remained independently associated with hepatic steatosis (OR = 4.12, p = 0.004, Nagelkerke R² = 0.63).

When daily fructose intake and exercise habits were examined, median daily fructose intake was lower in individuals reporting regular exercise, and significantly higher in those without regular exercise (p = 0.026).

Participants with higher fructose intake (> 50 g/day) exhibited significantly elevated triglyceride levels (median 154.00 mg/dL, IQR 100.00–193.00) compared to those with daily fructose intake ≤ 50 g (median 112.50 mg/dL, IQR 78.00–153.00; p = 0.003). Similarly, fasting insulin concentrations (median 15.53 µIU/mL, IQR 11.41–19.30 vs. 10.20 µIU/mL, IQR 6.71–15.70) and HOMA-IR values (median 3.70, IQR 2.60–4.60 vs. 2.40, IQR 1.50–3.20) were also higher in the > 50 g/day intake group (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed for HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, fasting glucose, or HbA1c values (p > 0.05).

In the hepatobiliary ultrasound assessment, hepatic steatosis was significantly more prevalent in participants with a daily fructose intake greater than 50 g (79.8%) compared with those with an intake of 50 g/day or less (20.2%) (p < 0.001). Furthermore, individuals in the higher intake group had a larger liver size (median 161.0 mm, IQR 147.0–173.0) compared with those in the ≤ 50 g/day intake group (median 149.5 mm, IQR 139.0–159.0) (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine a specific threshold value that that may be informative for clinical and research contexts by examining the effects of daily fructose intake on metabolic parameters and hepatic steatosis. In our study, groups formed based on a daily threshold of 50 g differed significantly in terms of triglyceride, insulin, and HOMA-IR levels. Notably, the ROC analysis–derived cutoff value of 51.07 g/day, which was associated with an approximately 15-fold increase in the risk of hepatic steatosis, may serve as a preliminary quantitative reference point for evaluating the association between fructose intake and liver health through a clinically meaningful threshold.

In our study, hepatic steatosis was detected in nearly two-thirds of the participants. Globally, the prevalence of hepatic steatosis has been reported to range between 27.3% and 49.4% across different populations11–13. Recent studies conducted in Türkiye have reported prevalence rates ranging from 48.3% to 60.1%14 with rates reaching up to 86.7% among individuals with obesity15. Considering that our study was conducted in a Turkish population consisting predominantly of individuals with obesity, the observed prevalence appears to be within the expected range. This indicates that the observed prevalence is consistent with previously reported data in comparable populations.

HFCS contains a higher proportion of fructose than most natural fructose sources16. Consumption of processed snack foods has been reported to result in substantially higher fructose intake compared with natural fructose sources, largely due to their HFCS content17. These findings support our observation that an increased number of snacks was associated with higher fructose intake.

In our study, fruit consumption was predominantly observed during the evening hours, and hepatic steatosis was significantly more prevalent among individuals with this dietary habit. To our knowledge, few studies have directly examined the relationship between the timing of fruit consumption and hepatic steatosis. However, studies reporting an association between evening snacking habits and higher BMI as well as obesogenic dietary indices18, support the metabolic basis of our findings. While fruit consumption in Western societies tends to be distributed more evenly throughout the daytime18 ,19, our results reflect the common practice of late-evening fruit consumption in the Turkish population20. These findings suggest that the potential effects of meal timing on biochemical pathways warrant further investigation. Nevertheless, the potential influence of total caloric intake, overall late-night eating behavior, and dietary quality as residual confounders cannot be excluded, and this finding should be interpreted with caution pending replication in larger, prospective studies.

In our sample, individuals who engaged in sufficient weekly physical activity had significantly lower fructose intake. This finding may primarily be explained by the tendency of physically active individuals to adopt healthier lifestyles overall, including more favorable dietary choices. In addition, animal studies have shown that aerobic exercise reduces voluntary fructose and sucrose intake under conditions of free access to fructose-containing beverages, potentially through exercise-induced alterations in reward pathways and improved insulin sensitivity21,22. Taken together, these findings suggest that the lower fructose intake observed among physically active individuals in our study might be attributed to healthier lifestyle choices, while potentially aligning with the known biological effects of exercise on appetite regulation and sweet cravings.

The increases in triglyceride, insulin, and HOMA-IR levels observed at daily fructose intakes of 50 g or higher in our study are consistent with the existing literature, despite the use of different cutoff values across studies9, 23. In contrast, studies have reported that increases in LDL cholesterol and total cholesterol typically occur at fructose intakes exceeding 100 g/day24 Accordingly, the 50 g/day cutoff used in our study was not associated with significant changes in cholesterol levels. In parallel with previous findings, HbA1c levels were not found to increase with fructose intake in our study25.

Hepatobiliary ultrasonography revealed significantly higher rates of hepatic steatosis among individuals with fructose intake > 50 g/day compared with those consuming lower amounts. Similarly, case–control studies have reported higher rates of hepatic steatosis at fructose intakes of 52–58 g/day compared with control intakes of 40–41 g/day26. In line with our findings, previous studies have also demonstrated an association between fructose intake and increased steatosis grade among individuals with hepatic steatosis27.

Although studies evaluating daily safe fructose intake limits based on biochemical parameters exist in the literature28, no study has clearly defined a cutoff value associated with ultrasonographically detected hepatic steatosis. In this context, the multivariable logistic regression model developed in our study robustly demonstrated the association between fructose intake and hepatic steatosis.

In conclusion, the multivariable logistic regression model demonstrated strong discriminatory performance for hepatic steatosis, with high sensitivity and specificity. The substantial proportion of explained variance further suggests a strong and consistent association between fructose intake and hepatic steatosis within this study population. Although the identified cutoff value of 51.07 g/day may help address an existing gap in the literature, the cross-sectional design of the study warrants cautious interpretation, and these findings should be regarded as clinically meaningful associations rather than direct evidence of causality.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively modest; therefore, studies with larger populations are needed to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Second, dietary intake was assessed using self-reported questionnaires and seven-day food records, which are inherently subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. In addition, the assessment of dietary intake over a single week represents only a cross-sectional snapshot and may not fully reflect habitual consumption patterns. The cross-sectional design of the study precludes causal inference. Although fructose, energy, and portion sizes were calculated in accordance with current dietary guidelines, minor measurement inaccuracies cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, hepatic steatosis is a multifactorial condition, and while this study focused primarily on fructose intake, the potential influence of other contributing factors may not have been fully captured. Residual confounding by unmeasured dietary or lifestyle factors — including total caloric intake, ultra-processed food consumption, sedentary behavior, comorbidities, and genetic factors — cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, although patients using known steatogenic medications were strictly excluded, the potential independent or interactive effects of other stable chronic diseases and routine general medication use on hepatic steatosis could not be fully excluded or controlled for in our analysis. Furthermore, the association between evening or night-time fruit consumption and hepatic steatosis should be interpreted cautiously, as total caloric intake and broader late-night eating patterns were not fully captured and may represent residual confounders. Finally, the majority of participants had a BMI above 25 kg/m², and the limited representation of normal-weight individuals may have influenced the observed associations and limits extrapolation to normal-weight populations.

Furthermore, the assessment of hepatic steatosis and liver size relied solely on ultrasonography. Although widely used in clinical practice due to its non-invasive nature, cost-effectiveness, and accessibility, ultrasonography has limited sensitivity and reproducibility, particularly in detecting mild steatosis. Modalities such as controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), magnetic resonance imaging-proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF), or liver biopsy would provide a more quantitative and reliable evaluation of hepatic fat content. Similarly, ultrasonography is not the optimal modality for accurate liver size evaluation compared to cross-sectional imaging, as it is subject to operator dependency and anatomical variations.
