Lepton energy scale and resolution corrections based on the minimization of an analytical likelihood: IJazZ2.0
F. Couderc, P. Gaigne, M. Ö. Sahin

Abstract
We present a novel method to determine lepton energy scale and resolution corrections by means of an analytical likelihood maximization applied to Drell–Yan Z → ℓ ​ ℓ Z\to\ell\ell events. The approach relies on an exact analytical treatment of the energy smearing, avoiding random-number-based convolution techniques. This formulation results in a fully differentiable likelihood enabling the use of automatic differentiation algorithms, and thus a substantial reduction in computational cost. The method, implemented in the IJazZ2.0 software, allows the simultaneous extraction of scale and resolution parameters across multiple lepton categories defined by detector or kinematic variables. We validate the technique using toy Monte Carlo studies and realistic Pythia-based simulations, demonstrating unbiased parameter recovery and accurate uncertainty estimates. Particular attention is given to categorizations involving lepton transverse momentum, for which a relative- p T p_{T} strategy is introduced to mitigate biases induced by category migration and kinematic correlations. The method is further adapted to photon-energy scale measurement in Z → μ − ​ μ + ​ γ Z\to\mu^{-}\mu^{+}\gamma decays. Compared to conventional approaches, the analytical method improves numerical stability, robustness of the minimization, and computational performance, making it well suited for large-scale precision calibration tasks at the LHC.



1
Introduction

A precise calibration of the photon and lepton energy scales is a key requirement for precision measurements at high-energy collider experiments. In particular, the measurement of the Higgs boson mass in decay channels involving photons or charged leptons, such as
H\rightarrow\gamma\gamma
and
H\rightarrow ZZ^{*}\rightarrow 4\ell
, is directly sensitive to small biases in the energy or momentum scale. Since electrons and photons share a largely common electromagnetic response in the detector, the energy-scale calibration of electrons is propagated to photons, with additional corrections accounting for differences in shower development and upstream material effects.

Decays of the
Z
boson into charged-lepton pairs,
Z\to\ell\ell
with
\ell\equiv e,\mu
, provide a primary in-situ reference for lepton energy or momentum calibration, owing to their large production rate and the precisely known
Z
-boson mass
[
1
]
. The calibration is typically performed by constraining the reconstructed dilepton invariant-mass distribution to a reference line shape. However, the invariant mass depends simultaneously on the response of both leptons and is affected by the natural width of the
Z
boson, final-state radiation, and detector resolution effects. These features introduce correlations between scale and resolution parameters, complicating their simultaneous extraction in differential calibration schemes.

The calibration strategy is based on a comparison between the lepton-energy scale measured in data and that predicted by a detailed detector simulation. Residual differences between data and simulation are interpreted as corrections to the energy scale and resolution and are extracted using
Z\to\ell\ell
decays. Within the framework we propose, these residual mismodeling effects are assumed to induce approximately Gaussian distortions of the di-lepton invariant-mass distribution, referred to as smearing hereafter.

The core of the method consists of describing the smearing effects using an analytical approach, enabling the construction of a fully analytic likelihood. Automatic differentiation techniques
[
2
]
are then used to efficiently compute exact gradients with respect to all calibration parameters.

This method is implemented in a software tool named
IJazZ2.0
(I Just AnalyZe the Z), which is freely available via a PyPI distribution
[
3
]
and is described in section
2
. The treatment of statistical uncertainties arising from the finite size of the simulated
Z\to e^{+}e^{-}
sample is discussed in section
3
. Additional considerations related to energy-response linearity are presented in section
4
, and the extension of the method to photon energy-scale measurements using
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
decays is described in section
5
.

2
The
IJazZ2.0
method

The aim of
IJazZ2.0
is to measure the differences between Monte Carlo (MC) simulation and data in terms of lepton energy response and resolution. These calibration parameters are usually measured with respect to a set of variables
\vec{X}
describing the lepton properties and detector conditions, such as the polar and azimuthal angles
\theta
and
\phi
, the pseudorapidity
\eta\equiv-\ln[\tan(\theta/2)]
, and shower-shape observables characterizing the lateral and longitudinal development of the electromagnetic cluster in the calorimeter. The transverse momentum
p_{T}
is omitted for the moment as it requires a dedicated treatment as it is already part of the invariant-mass calculation.

The di-lepton invariant mass is defined as:

m_{\ell\ell}^{2}=2p_{T,1}p_{T,2}\left[\cosh(\Delta\eta)-\cos(\Delta\phi)\right],

(1)

where
\Delta\eta
and
\Delta\phi
denote the differences in pseudorapidity and azimuthal angle between the two leptons, and
p_{T,1}
and
p_{T,2}
are their transverse momenta. The angular quantities
\Delta\eta
and
\Delta\phi
are typically extremely well measured in collider experiments; therefore, in the present approach, they are assumed to be perfectly known and not to introduce any bias or smearing in the reconstructed invariant mass.

We note
{r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}(\vec{X})
, respectively the data/MC relative energy scale and data/MC energy smearing. To be more specific, in this method, we correct the energy from the simulation to match the one in data. Therefore,
{r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})
is a correction to be applied to the lepton energy in the simulation:

E_{\mathrm{corrS}}^{\mathrm{mc}}={r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})\times E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{mc}}

(2)

where
E_{\mathrm{corrS}}^{\mathrm{mc}}
is the scale-corrected energy while
E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{mc}}
is the original lepton energy. Usually and conversely, one can correct afterward the lepton energy in the data back to the simulation level with the formula:

E_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{data}}=E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{data}}/{r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})\,.

(3)

Concerning the energy resolution, it is assumed to be always better (
i.e.
smaller) in the simulation, thus the energy resolution in the simulation needs to be degraded (smeared) to its corresponding level in data. Because the simulation already includes most of the effects due to the detector response (energy loss, material…), the modest degradation due to the imperfect modelling of the simulation is assumed to follow a normal distribution. The lepton-energy degradation is done in the simulation with random number trials from a normal distribution. Therefore, for each lepton in the simulation, its energy is drawn from the probabilistic distribution (including scale and smearing corrections):

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{mc}}\!\left(E;\,{r_{\ell}},\,{\sigma_{\ell}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(E;\ {r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})\,E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{mc}},\ {r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})\,{\sigma_{\ell}}(\vec{X})\times E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{mc}}\ \right),

(4)

where
\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma)
denotes a normal distribution with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
.

2.1
Definition of the lepton categories and DY regions

In order to extract the values of
{r_{\ell}}(\vec{X})
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}(\vec{X})
, the phase space defined by the variables
\vec{X}
is first discretized into bins. For simplicity, a one-dimensional binning with
N_{B}
bins is considered, each bin being denoted by
b
. The detector response and smearing are measured independently in each of these bins, resulting in
N_{B}
scale and smearing parameters, collected in the vectors
\vec{{r_{\ell}}}
and
\vec{{\sigma_{\ell}}}
.

Since the
Z
boson decays into a pair of leptons, the calibration must be propagated to the di-lepton invariant-mass scale and smearing, denoted by
{r_{\ell\ell}}
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}
, respectively. These quantities are inferred from the single-lepton parameters
{r_{\ell}}
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}
associated with the two leptons forming the
Z
-boson candidate.

Drell–Yan (DY) events are therefore categorized according to the pair of bins
(b_{1},b_{2})
corresponding to the two leptons in the event, where
b_{i}
denotes the response bin of lepton
i
. Since the configurations
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(b_{2},b_{1})
lead to identical values of
{r_{\ell\ell}}
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}
, they are assigned to the same category. The total number of independent di-lepton categories is thus

N_{C}\equiv\frac{N_{B}\times(N_{B}+1)}{2}.

(5)

As a consequence, the per-event di-lepton scale and smearing parameters,
{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
, associated with each category
c=(b_{1},b_{2})
are given by:

\begin{split}{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}&=\sqrt{{r_{\ell}}_{b_{1}}\times{r_{\ell}}_{b_{2}}}\\
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}&=0.5\times\sqrt{{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b_{1}}^{2}+{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b_{2}}^{2}}\end{split}

(6)

2.2
Definition of the likelihood

In each di-lepton category
c
, the parameters
{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
are determined by comparing the expected scaled and smeared di-lepton invariant-mass distribution predicted by simulation with the corresponding distribution observed in data. The comparison is performed by discretizing the di-lepton invariant-mass spectrum into
N_{I}
bins. Although the number of bins
N_{I}
may depend on the category
c
, this dependence is omitted for simplicity and thisbinning is denoted by
b_{I}
in the following.

The comparison between data and simulation is carried out under the assumption that the predicted scaled and smeared distribution follows a multinomial probability distribution. Consequently, for each di-lepton mass bin
i
in a given category
c
, a multinomial-based likelihood term is computed:

\mathcal{L}(n_{ic};{r_{\ell}}_{b},{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b})=\prod_{c=1}^{N_{C}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{I}}\frac{\left(\sum_{i}n_{ic}\right)!}{\prod_{i}n_{ic}!}\ p_{ic}^{n_{ic}}\ .

(7)

where
p_{ic}
is the expected probability (depending on
{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}
and consequently on
{r_{\ell}}_{b}
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b}
) for a di-lepton event to fall in bin
i
when belonging to category
c
and
n_{ic}
is the observed number of events in data corresponding to the same bin
i
. By definition we have
\sum_{i}p_{ic}=1
.
The likelihood from Eq.
7
can be maximized with respect to the parameters
{r_{\ell}}_{b}
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b}
. In practice, we minimize the negative log-likelihood, defined by:

{nll}(n_{ic};{r_{\ell}}_{b},{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b})=-\sum_{c=1}^{N_{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{I}}n_{ic}\log\left(p_{ic}\right)\ ,

(8)

Additive constants from the multinomial coefficient in Eq.
7
are omitted, as they are independent of the calibration parameters.

2.3
Analytical scale and smearing corrections

As a consequence of Eqs.
4
and
6
, each simulated di-lepton event with invariant mass
m_{\ell\ell}^{\mathrm{mc}}
gives rise to a probabilistic scaled and smeared mass distribution,
\mathcal{M}_{\ell\ell}
, defined as

\mathcal{M}_{\ell\ell}\!\left(m;\,{r_{\ell\ell}},{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(m;\,{r_{\ell\ell}}\,m_{\ell\ell}^{\mathrm{mc}},\,{r_{\ell\ell}}\,{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}\,m_{\ell\ell}^{\mathrm{mc}}\ .\right),

(9)

Since this probabilistic model is compared to data using a binned invariant-mass distribution, the probability
M_{i}
for a simulated event to fall into bin
i
can be predicted as:

M_{i}\!\left(m;\,{r_{\ell\ell}},{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Erf}\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{u}}/{r_{\ell\ell}}-{m_{\ell\ell}}^{\mathrm{mc}}}{\sqrt{2}\,{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}\,{m_{\ell\ell}}^{\mathrm{mc}}}\right)-\mathrm{Erf}\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}/{r_{\ell\ell}}-{m_{\ell\ell}}^{\mathrm{mc}}}{\sqrt{2}\,{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}\,{m_{\ell\ell}}^{\mathrm{mc}}}\right)\right]

(10)

where
\mathrm{Erf}
is the error function,
b_{i}^{\mathrm{u}}
and
b_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}
are respectively the upper and lower bound of bin
i
of the di-lepton invariant mass distribution (
i\in[1,N_{I}]
).

Finally, the invariant-mass distribution in the simulation is binned with a very fine granularity in order to accelerate the computation of the per-event probability
p_{ic}
. To avoid any bias, this binning, denoted
b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{J}
, must be smaller than the expected smearing
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}
; in practice, a bin width below
0.2~\mathrm{GeV}
is recommended. The bins
b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{J}
are indexed by
j
, and the number of simulated events falling into bin
j
of category
c
is denoted
m_{jc}
.

Considering Eq.
10
, the tensor
\alpha_{ijc}
can be used to predict the contribution from bin
j
of the simulation to bin
i
in the likelihood computation. The tensor
\alpha_{ijc}
is defined as:

\alpha_{ijc}({r_{\ell\ell}}_{c},{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{Erf}\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{u}}/{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}-b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}\,{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}\,b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{j}}\right)-\mathrm{Erf}\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}/{r_{\ell\ell}}_{c}-b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}\,{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}_{c}\,b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{j}}\right)\right]

(11)

where
b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{j}
is the center of bin
j
of the binning
b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{J}
. Thus, we can predict the
p_{ic}
probabilities with:

p_{ic}=\frac{\sum_{j}\alpha_{ijc}\ m_{jc}}{\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ijc}\ m_{jc}}\ .

(12)

In Eq.
12
, the normalization factor in the denominator accounts for the fact that the fine binning
b^{\mathrm{mc}}_{J}
can, and should, span a wider di-lepton invariant-mass range than the target binning
b_{I}
, in order to properly account for event migration into and out of
b_{I}
due to the scaling and smearing applied to the simulation.

The validity of the method is illustrated in Fig.
1
, which compares a Breit–Wigner distribution of
10^{4}
events that have been scaled and smeared with
{r_{\ell\ell}}=0.98
and
{\sigma_{\ell\ell}}=0.005
. For each simulated event, a varying number of random trials was generated and compared to the analytical prediction. As the number of trials increases, the agreement improves; with a large number of trials,
n_{\mathrm{smear}}\geq 10{,}000
, the analytical prediction and the Monte Carlo simulation are in perfect agreement.

Figure 1:

Left: the original MC distribution (Breit–Wigner) from 10,000 generated events.
Right: the smeared MC distribution obtained using a random smearing technique, with
n_{\mathrm{smear}}
trials per original MC event, together with the analytical smearing prediction (dashed line).
It can be seen that the analytical smearing accurately reproduces the expected distribution, which is approximated by
n_{\mathrm{smear}}=10{,}000
; the two distributions are indistinguishable.

2.4
Qualitative comparison: analytical vs random smearing techniques

Per-event probabilities
p_{ic}
can be computed using a random smearing method with typically
n_{\mathrm{smear}}=10
trials per simulated event (
e.g.
, see Ref.
[
4
]
). The analytical smearing method presented here provides a CPU-time gain of
\sim 500
for
10^{6}
MC events, up to
\sim 5000
for
10^{7}
events on a laptop with 6 CPU cores.

This gain is further enhanced by automatic-gradient computations implemented via TensorFlow
[
5
]
. With random smearing, the likelihood gradient must be computed numerically, requiring

2\times N_{\mathrm{par}}\times t_{nll}^{\mathrm{smear}},

where
N_{\mathrm{par}}
is the number of fit parameters and
t_{\mathrm{nll}}
is the time to evaluate the likelihood. For the analytical method, the gradient evaluation takes approximately

2\times t_{nll}^{\mathrm{ana}}
.
Overall, the total CPU-time gain for likelihood maximization is several orders of magnitude, and can be further accelerated on GPUs. For example, a minimization with 100 parameters and
2\times 10^{7}
events takes 20–30 min on a laptop, less than one minute on a GPU, whereas the same fit with random smearing would take several days.

2.5
Treatment of statistical uncertainties

The uncertainties arising from the finite size of the data sample are computed using the covariance matrix,
\Sigma
, defined as the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood,
H^{nll}
:

\Sigma^{-1}_{kp}\equiv H^{nll}_{kp}=\frac{\partial^{2}{nll}\!\left(n_{ic};\,\vec{\theta}\right)}{\partial\theta_{k}\,\partial\theta_{p}},

(13)

where
\vec{\theta}
denotes the ensemble of parameters
{r_{\ell}}_{b}
and
{\sigma_{\ell}}_{b}
. Note that this uncertainty does not account for statistical fluctuations due to the finite size of the Monte Carlo simulation, which in some cases may be comparable to that of the data sample.

2.6
Validation with a naive MC approach

The method is first validated by generating
25\times 10^{6}
MC events according to a Cauchy distribution with mean
\mu=m_{Z}
and width
\gamma=\Gamma_{Z}/2
, where
m_{Z}
and
\Gamma_{Z}
are the mass and natural width of the
Z
boson, respectively, taken from Ref.
[
1
]
. In this validation, the full kinematics of the two leptons is not simulated; only a naive
Z
-boson line shape is generated using a standard Breit–Wigner distribution.

For each event, two random numbers are generated to mimic a property
X
of the two leptons from the
Z
-boson decay, with
X
drawn from a uniform distribution over the range
[0,100]
. The detector energy resolution is simulated by generating, for each event, a random number from a normal distribution with mean 1 and standard deviation 0.015. For a subset of
5\times 10^{6}
events, an additional decalibration and smearing depending on
X
is applied, emulating the difference between the true detector response (data) and the nominal simulation (MC). The
IJazZ2.0
method is then used to retrieve these decalibration and smearing parameters, treating the latter part of the sample (with the additional decalibration) as "data" and the rest as "MC".

The results of this validation are shown in Fig.
2
, demonstrating that the fitted parameters agree with the injected ones within statistical precision.

2.7
Adaptive binning
b_{I}

Because the predicted
p_{ic}
enter the
{nll}
function through
\log p_{ic}
, zero probabilities must be avoided. In addition, the invariant-mass spectrum is sharply peaked, and low-statistics tails can bias the fit (
e.g.
by favoring artificially large smearing to populate empty bins). To mitigate this, we use an adaptive binning to determine
b_{I}
: for each category, bin edges are defined so that each mass bin contains the same number of events. In addition, a minimum number of MC events is required per category (100 events by default). The total number of bins in
b_{I}
is inspired by the Freedman–Diaconis rule and is taken to be the minimum between
\left(\sum_{i}n_{ic}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
and
\Delta M_{\ell\ell}/(0.5~\mathrm{GeV})
with
\Delta M_{\ell\ell}
the size of the di-lepton mass window over which the fit is performed, typically
\Delta M_{\ell\ell}=(100-80)~\mathrm{GeV}
, the limit on the total number of bins in
b_{I}
comes from the fact that the typical energy-resolution is larger than
0.5~\mathrm{GeV}
at the
Z
-boson peak, this default behaviour is configurable.

A demonstration of this technique is presented on Fig.
3
. A similar simulation as the one described in section
2.6
is used but we reduce the total number of events to
5\times 10^{4}
for both data and simulation and the property
X
is generated according to a normal distribution so to have some categories with lower statistics, the number of scale and smearing parameters is
N_{B}=3
, consequently giving rise to
N_{C}=6
categories.

Figure 3:
adaptive binning: The bin width is adapted so that the total number of events in the simulation is the same in each bin, the total number of bins in each category depends on the available MC statistics in this category. The points corresponds to the fitted data (in this case a toy MC dataset) while the histogram refers to the fitted prediction from the smeared simulation.

3
Uncertainties due to limited MC statistics

As discussed in Sec.
2.5
, the covariance matrix obtained from the method only accounts for statistical fluctuations due to the finite size of the data sample, assuming that the predicted probabilities
p_{ic}
are computed with infinite precision. In reality,
p_{ic}
are limited by the finite size of the simulation sample. To include this effect, one can study the variation of the
{nll}
minimum induced by fluctuations of the simulated counts
m_{jc}
.

If
m_{jc}
fluctuates by
\delta_{jc}
, the negative log-likelihood becomes
{nll}_{\delta}
, which can be written as

{nll}_{\delta}(\vec{\theta},\delta_{jc})={nll}(\vec{\theta})+\delta{nll}(\vec{\theta},\delta_{jc}),

(14)

where
\vec{\theta}
collectively denotes the response and resolution parameters of the
{nll}
function. To first order in
\delta_{jc}
,

\delta{nll}(\vec{\theta},\delta_{jc})=\frac{\partial{nll}}{\partial m_{jc}}(\vec{\theta})\,\delta_{jc}=\left[\sum_{i}\frac{n_{c}}{p_{c}}\alpha_{ijc}-\sum_{i}\frac{n_{ic}}{p_{ic}}\alpha_{ijc}\right]\delta_{jc},

(15)

with
n_{c}\equiv\sum_{i}n_{ic}
and
p_{c}\equiv\sum_{i}p_{ic}
, the second line being obtained by differentiating Eq.
8
with respect to
m_{jc}
.

Expanding
{nll}_{\delta}
to second order in
\delta\vec{\theta}\equiv\vec{\theta}-{\vec{\theta}}^{*}
, one obtains

{nll}_{\delta}(\vec{\theta},\delta_{jc})={nll}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})+\frac{\partial{nll}}{\partial\vec{\theta}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\,\delta\vec{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\,\delta\vec{\theta}^{T}H_{nll}\,\delta\vec{\theta}+\frac{\partial{nll}}{\partial m_{jc}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\,\delta_{jc}+\frac{\partial^{2}{nll}}{\partial\vec{\theta}\,\partial m_{jc}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\,\delta\vec{\theta}\,\delta_{jc},

(16)

where
{\vec{\theta}}^{*}
denotes the
{nll}
minimum. Differentiating Eq.
16
with respect to
\vec{\theta}
gives the minimum condition:

H_{nll}\,\delta\vec{\theta}+\frac{\partial^{2}{nll}}{\partial\vec{\theta}\,\partial m_{jc}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\,\delta_{jc}=0,

(17)

which implies

\delta\vec{\theta}_{jc}=H_{nll}^{-1}\,\frac{\partial^{2}{nll}}{\partial\vec{\theta}\,\partial m_{jc}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\,\delta_{jc}.

(18)

Since the
\delta_{jc}
fluctuations are independent, the total uncertainty on
{\vec{\theta}}^{*}
due to limited MC statistics is obtained by summing in quadrature:

(\delta{\vec{\theta}}^{*})^{2}=\sum_{j,c}(\delta\vec{\theta}_{jc})^{2}=\sum_{j,c}\left[H_{nll}^{-1}\,\frac{\partial^{2}{nll}}{\partial\vec{\theta}\,\partial m_{jc}}({\vec{\theta}}^{*})\right]^{2}\,\delta_{jc}^{2}.

(19)

For a weighted simulation,
m_{jc}=\sum_{q}w_{jcq}
, where
w_{jcq}
is the weight of event
q
, the corresponding fluctuation is
\delta_{jc}=\sqrt{\sum_{q}w_{jcq}^{2}}
.

To validate this formula, we use the simple simulation described in Sec.
2.6
:
2\times 10^{6}
events are treated as the data sample, and the scale and smearing parameters are measured for 100 independent simulations of
2\times 10^{6}
events each. The standard deviation of these 100 measurements represents the uncertainty due to MC fluctuations. Figure
4
compares these standard deviations to the predictions of Eq.
19
, showing very good agreement.

Figure 4:

Validation of the statistical uncertainties due to limited MC statistics for the response parameters (left) and smearing parameters (right). Triangles show the standard deviation of 100 measurements using the same data but different simulations, while stars show the MC uncertainty predicted by Eq.
19
. The dashed line indicates the statistical uncertainty from the data alone; since data and MC sample sizes are equal, MC and data uncertainties are of the same order.

4
Categorisation vs
p_{T}
and validation with a Pythia-based MC simulation

As discussed in Sec.
2
, when the lepton energy or transverse momentum is included in the set of properties
\vec{X}
, a special treatment is required. In such cases, the measured scale and smearing parameters may be biased due to category migration and/or correlations between the categorisation and the reconstructed di-lepton invariant mass.

Since these biases are strongly correlated with the lepton
p_{T}
spectrum, we validate the method using a realistic Drell–Yan simulation generated with Pythia
[
6
]
. For each lepton from the
Z
-boson decay, the energy is first smeared according to a normal distribution with mean
\mu_{\mathrm{sim}}=1
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}=1.5\%
, representing the typical energy resolution of the ATLAS or CMS electromagnetic calorimeters. The resulting event properties are denoted as
sim
:
{{m_{\ell\ell}}}_{\mathrm{sim}}
,
{p_{T}}_{\mathrm{sim}}
, etc. A total of
30\times 10^{6}
events are generated in this way. Half of the sample is used as reference simulation, while the other half is additionally decalibrated and smeared according to known (injected) parameters to emulate the difference between the true detector response and the nominal simulation.

We then measure the scale and smearing parameters using a lepton-
p_{T}
dependent categorisation. The results are shown in Fig.
5
. A small bias is observed in the measured scale parameters (below
0.1\%
), while a larger bias affects the smearing parameters, consistent with expectations from category migration effects.

Figure 5:

Measured scale (left) and smearing (right) parameters using the Pythia-based DY simulation. The dashed line corresponds to the injected parameters, while the shaded bands indicate the contribution from limited MC statistics to the total uncertainty. A
p_{T}
-dependent categorisation introduces small biases in the measured scale and larger biases in the smearing parameters.

To better understand these biases, we select events in which one lepton satisfies
45~\mathrm{GeV}<{p_{T}}_{\ell}<50~\mathrm{GeV}
and the other
50~\mathrm{GeV}<{p_{T}}_{\ell}<60~\mathrm{GeV}
. The reconstructed di-lepton invariant-mass distribution,
{m_{\ell\ell}}
, for these events exhibits a double-peak structure, as shown in Fig.
6
a. This arises from a threshold effect induced by the
p_{T}
requirements. Furthermore, the two peaks show different correlations with the original simulated mass,
{{m_{\ell\ell}}}_{\mathrm{sim}}
: the peak near the
Z
-boson mass is largely uncorrelated, whereas the secondary peak exhibits a strong correlation. These correlations are illustrated in Fig.
6
b. When the two peaks mix (for instance, for symmetric
p_{T}
requirements), biases in the measured scale and smearing parameters can arise.

These biases can be mitigated by selecting events based on the relative transverse momentum, defined as
r_{p_{T}}={p_{T}}_{\ell}/{m_{\ell\ell}}
. Using this criterion instead of the absolute
p_{T}
, we select one lepton with
45/m_{Z}<{p_{T}}_{\ell}/{m_{\ell\ell}}<50/m_{Z}
and the other with
50/m_{Z}<{p_{T}}_{\ell}/{m_{\ell\ell}}<60/m_{Z}
, where
m_{Z}
is the
Z
-boson mass
[
1
]
. Figs.
6
c and
6
d show the reconstructed
{m_{\ell\ell}}
distribution and its correlation with the simulated mass under this selection, demonstrating that the double-peak structure and the related correlations disappear with the relative
p_{T}
requirement.

Figure 6:

Left: reconstructed di-lepton mass for the absolute
p_{T}
selection (top) and relative
p_{T}
selection (bottom). Right: correlation between the reconstructed-to-simulated di-lepton mass ratio (
{m_{\ell\ell}}/{{m_{\ell\ell}}}_{\mathrm{sim}}
) and the simulated mass. Using the relative
p_{T}
selection removes the double-peak structure and associated correlations observed with the absolute
p_{T}
selection.

In light of the properties of the relative
p_{T}
categorisation, we adopt the following procedure to measure the scale and smearing parameters. The lepton-
p_{T}
categorisation is replaced by a relative
p_{T}
categorisation using, instead of
p_{T}
, the variable
r_{p_{T}}=p_{T}/{m_{\ell\ell}}
. The relative
p_{T}
binning is defined by dividing the original
p_{T}
bin edges
[p_{T1},p_{T2},\dots,p_{Tn}]
by the
Z
-boson mass,
m_{Z}
.

A first fit is performed to extract the energy-scale parameters, after which the lepton
p_{T}
and the di-lepton mass
{m_{\ell\ell}}
are corrected using Eq.
3
. A second fit is then performed to measure the energy-smearing parameters. Indeed, the relative
p_{T}
categorisation introduces correlations between the two leptons, so the scale must be set to 1 in order to properly extract the smearing parameters.

However, in order to correct the scale or smear the lepton energies in the simulation, an absolute lepton
p_{T}
criterion is required. Therefore, the relative
p_{T}
binning is transformed back into an absolute
p_{T}
binning. In each relative
p_{T}
bin
b
of
b_{I}
, the average absolute transverse momentum,
\bar{p}_{Tb}
, is computed and used to define the absolute
p_{T}
bin edges as

[p_{T1},0.5(\bar{p}_{T1}+\bar{p}_{T2}),\dots,0.5(\bar{p}_{Tb_{k}}+\bar{p}_{T_{k+1}}),\dots,p_{Tn}].

It is observed that this recasted
p_{T}
binning closely reproduces the original
p_{T}
categories used as input.

The results of this procedure are shown in Fig.
7
, where the
x
-axis corresponds to the recasted absolute
p_{T}
binning. The injected parameters shown in the figure are measured in the recasted
p_{T}
categories, meaning they correspond to the scale and smearing parameters that would be applied to correct the lepton energies in data/simulation.

Note that this two-step fit procedure does not improve the smearing parameters in the scenario of Fig.
5
. For the absolute
p_{T}
binning case, the second fit converges to the same smearing parameters as the first one, as expected, because no correlations are introduced between the leptons, unlike in the relative
p_{T}
case.

From Fig.
7
, one can see that both the scale and smearing parameters are correctly retrieved within uncertainties, with the exception of the first
p_{T}
bin for the scale parameter, which is slightly biased due to the absolute
p_{T}
cut,
p_{T}>25~\mathrm{GeV}
.

5
Extension to photon energy scale measurement

The method developed for lepton energy scale and smearing determination can be further extended to measure the photon energy scale and smearing in
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
decays.

Following the definition of the scale and smearing parameters introduced in section
2
, we denote
{r_{\gamma}}(\vec{X})
and
{\sigma_{\gamma}}(\vec{X})
as the data-to-simulation relative photon-energy scale and the data-to-simulation photon-energy smearing, respectively. Similarly to Eq.
3
, the photon energy in data can be corrected back to the simulation level by:

E_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{data}}=E_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{data}}/{r_{\gamma}}(\vec{X})\,.

(20)

The method requires specific adaptations for the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process. On the one hand, the presence of a single photon in the final state simplifies the formulation; on the other hand, the photon carries only a fraction of the total energy of the three-body system. To account for this feature, we introduce a new variable,
V_{\mathrm{DY}}
, representing the relative mass carried by the photon in the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
decay. This variable replaces the di-lepton invariant mass in the analytical likelihood, allowing the same fitting procedure as for the lepton case. The method is validated using a Pythia-based simulation.

5.1
Adapting the method to the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process

The squared invariant mass of the three-body system in the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process can be written as

m_{\mu\mu\gamma}^{2}=m_{\mu\mu}^{2}+2\,p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}\left(p_{\mathrm{T}\,\mu^{-}}\,(\cosh\Delta\eta_{\gamma\mu^{-}}-\cos\Delta\phi_{\gamma\mu^{-}})+p_{\mathrm{T}\,\mu^{+}}\,(\cosh\Delta\eta_{\gamma\mu^{+}}-\cos\Delta\phi_{\gamma\mu^{+}})\right),

(21)

where
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\mu^{-}}
,
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\mu^{+}}
, and
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}
are the transverse momenta of the two muons and the photon, respectively, and
m_{\mu\mu\gamma}
and
m_{\mu\mu}
denote the invariant masses of the
\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
and
\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
systems. Considering a variation
\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}
of the photon transverse momentum, the three-body invariant mass can be expressed as

m_{\mu\mu\gamma}\left(\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}\right)=\sqrt{m_{\mu\mu}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}\right)\left(m_{\mu\mu\gamma}^{2}-m_{\mu\mu}^{2}\right)}\,.

(22)

Assuming that
\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}/p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}\ll 1
, the corresponding variation of the invariant mass is given by

\frac{\delta m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}\left(1-\frac{m_{\mu\mu}^{2}}{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}^{2}}\right).

(23)

In the following, we define
\delta{r_{\gamma}}\equiv\delta p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}/p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}
, such that
{r_{\gamma}}=1+\delta{r_{\gamma}}
.

Assuming that the two muons are well calibrated and that the photon energy scale is close to its nominal value (for instance, using electromagnetic calibrations derived from
Z\to e^{+}e^{-}
events), one has
m_{\mu\mu\gamma}\approx m_{Z}
, where
m_{Z}
is the
Z
-boson pole mass. In this case,

\frac{\delta m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}\approx\frac{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{Z}}-1\,.

Combining the above expressions, we define

{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}\equiv\left(\frac{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{Z}}-1\right)\frac{2}{1-\frac{m_{\mu\mu}^{2}}{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}^{2}}},

(24)

which is directly sensitive to residual miscalibration of the photon energy. A relative photon-energy scale miscalibration
\delta{r_{\gamma}}\ll 1
therefore shifts the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distribution by approximately
{r_{\gamma}}-1
, yielding

{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}({r_{\gamma}})\approx{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}({r_{\gamma}}=1)+({r_{\gamma}}-1)\,.

(25)

Figure
8
illustrates a validation of this relation using a Pythia-based simulation of the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process, selecting events with
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}>25~\mathrm{GeV}
. For each event, the photon energy is rescaled according to
{r_{\gamma}}=1+\delta{r_{\gamma}}
, where
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
is a random number drawn from a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}},(1+\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}){\sigma_{\gamma}})
. We use the value
\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}=0.025
which corresponds to a
2.5\%
energy shift, while
{\sigma_{\gamma}}=0.01
mimics a degradation of the photon-energy resolution. The predicted
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distribution obtained from Eq.
25
is found to be in excellent agreement with the simulation.

Figure 8:

Validation of Eq.
25
using a Pythia-based simulation of the
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process. The original distribution represents
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
in the original simulation. The smeared distribution corresponds to
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
after applying the photon energy shift and smearing described in the text. The predicted distribution is obtained from Eq.

, starting from the original distribution. The smeared and predicted distributions are nearly indistinguishable.

5.2
Photon analytical likelihood

The analytical likelihood method developed for the lepton case can be directly applied to the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distributions. For each simulated event, Eq.
25
is used to generate the scaled and smeared distribution of
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}^{\mathrm{mc}}
:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{mc}}\!\left(v;\,{r_{\gamma}},\,{\sigma_{\gamma}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(v;\,{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}^{\mathrm{mc}}+{r_{\gamma}}-1,\;{r_{\gamma}}\times{\sigma_{\gamma}}\right).

(26)

Accordingly, the
\alpha_{ijc}
tensor defined in Eq.
11
becomes

\alpha_{ijc}({r_{\gamma}}_{c},{\sigma_{\gamma}}_{c})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\mathrm{Erf}\!\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{u}}-b_{j}^{\mathrm{mc}}-({r_{\gamma}}_{c}-1)}{\sqrt{2}\,{r_{\gamma}}_{c}\,{\sigma_{\gamma}}_{c}}\right)-\mathrm{Erf}\!\left(\frac{b_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}-b_{j}^{\mathrm{mc}}-({r_{\gamma}}_{c}-1)}{\sqrt{2}\,{r_{\gamma}}_{c}\,{\sigma_{\gamma}}_{c}}\right)\Bigg],

(27)

where
b_{i}^{\mathrm{u,d}}
denote the upper and lower bin edges of the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distribution in data,
b_{j}^{\mathrm{mc}}
are the bin centers in simulation, and
c
labels the event category. This tensor predicts the contribution of each simulation bin
b_{j}^{\mathrm{mc}}
to the predicted probability
p_{ic}
in bin
i
of Eq.
8
,

p_{ic}=\frac{\sum_{j}\alpha_{ijc}\,{{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}_{jc}}{\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ijc}\,{{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}_{jc}}\,.

(28)

The rest of the analytical likelihood procedure remains unchanged with respect to the lepton case, with the only modification being the replacement of the invariant-mass distributions by the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distributions.

5.3
Validation using Pythia-based simulations

The method is validated using a Pythia-based simulation of the
pp\to Z/\gamma^{*}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
process at
\sqrt{s}=13.6

\mathrm{TeV}
. Typical event selections used in collider experiments are emulated by applying the following requirements:
|\eta_{\mu^{\pm}}|<2.5
,
|\eta_{\gamma}|<2.5
,
{p_{\mathrm{T}}}_{\mu}^{\pm}>15

\mathrm{GeV}
and
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}>25

\mathrm{GeV}
. After these selections, the total simulated sample contains approximately
30\times 10^{6}
events. Several tests are performed by varying the injected photon-energy miscalibration parameters. For each test, the full sample is split into two subsamples: one is used to represent the data, while the other serves as the reference simulation. In each event of the data subsample, the photon energy is miscalibrated by
{r_{\gamma}}=1+\delta{r_{\gamma}}
, where
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
is a random number drawn from a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}},(1+\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}){\sigma_{\gamma}})
. Unless otherwise stated, a value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}=1\%
is used.

Since Eq.
25
is only an approximation, an iterative procedure is required to retrieve the injected miscalibration. From Eqs.
24
and
22
, the first-order Taylor expansion of
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
in
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
is given by:

\begin{split}{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}(\delta{r_{\gamma}})&={V_{\mathrm{DY}}}(0)+(1-\epsilon)\,\delta{r_{\gamma}},\ \mathrm{with}\\
\epsilon&\equiv 1-\left.\frac{\partial{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}{\partial\delta{r_{\gamma}}}\right|_{\delta{r_{\gamma}}=0}=\left(1-\frac{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{Z}}\right)\times\frac{3z_{\mu\mu}-2}{z_{\mu\mu}}\end{split}

(29)

where
z_{\mu\mu}\equiv 1-m_{\mu\mu}^{2}/m_{\mu\mu\gamma}^{2}
. Since
m_{\mu\mu\gamma}/m_{Z}\approx 1
, the parameter
\epsilon
is small, with
|\epsilon|<0.10
in practice. Because Eq.
26
is used to smear the simulation, an iterative fitting procedure is required. After the first iteration, the fitted value converges to
{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{fit}}^{1}=(1-\epsilon)\,\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}
. More generally, one can show recursively that after iteration
n
, the fitted value is

{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{fit}}^{n}=\left(1-\epsilon^{n}\right)\,\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}.

The fit therefore converges rapidly to the true value
\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}
, typically within a few iterations.

The convergence is illustrated in Fig.
9
, where
\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}
is varied between
-5\%
and
+5\%
. The fit is performed in the invariant-mass range
80~\mathrm{GeV}<m_{\mu\mu\gamma}<100~\mathrm{GeV}
. The method accurately retrieves the injected values of both
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
and
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
. The accuracy of the
\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}
determination is better than
10^{-4}
. A small residual bias is nevertheless observed in Fig.
9
; its origin is discussed in Section
5.4.1
. This result demonstrates the validity of the full method, including the iterative fitting procedure.

5.4
Intrinsic limitations of the method

If the difference in energy resolution between simulation and data is large, two potential biases may affect the energy scale measurement. These effects are discussed in this section.

5.4.1
Spurious energy shift arising from energy-resolution mismodeling

The second-order Taylor expansion of Eq.
24
can be written as:

{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}(\delta{r_{\gamma}})={V_{\mathrm{DY}}}(0)+(1-\epsilon)\,\delta{r_{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}{\partial\delta{r_{\gamma}}^{2}}\right|_{\delta{r_{\gamma}}=0}(\delta{r_{\gamma}})^{2}\,.

(30)

In this method, the miscalibration
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
between data and simulation is assumed to follow a normal distribution. If we assume that there is no energy-shift between the data and the simulation,
i.e.
when the mean value of
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
vanishes:
\langle\delta{r_{\gamma}}\rangle=\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}=0
, a shift may arise between the mean value
\langle{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{data}}
measured in data and its corresponding value
\langle{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{mc}}
predicted by the simulation due to the presence of the second-order term. By definition, for a centered normal distribution, one has
\langle(\delta{r_{\gamma}})^{2}\rangle={\sigma_{\gamma}}^{2}
. Therefore, in this case, Eq.
30
transforms in:

\langle{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{data}}=\langle{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{mc}}+\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}{\partial\delta{r_{\gamma}}^{2}}\right|_{\delta{r_{\gamma}}=0}{\sigma_{\gamma}}^{2}\,.

(31)

The second-order term reads:

\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}{\partial\delta{r_{\gamma}}^{2}}\right|_{\delta{r_{\gamma}}=0}=2\,\frac{1-z_{\mu\mu}}{z_{\mu\mu}}\left(\frac{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{Z}}-1\right)-\left(1-\frac{3}{4}z_{\mu\mu}\right)\frac{m_{\mu\mu\gamma}}{m_{Z}}\,.

(32)

Thus, even if the data are perfectly calibrated, the fit may assign an apparent energy shift of

{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{fit}}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}}{\partial\delta{r_{\gamma}}^{2}}\right|_{\delta{r_{\gamma}}=0}{\sigma_{\gamma}}^{2}.

Since
m_{\mu\mu\gamma}/m_{Z}\approx 1
, Eq.
32
shows that this shift is always negative. The validity of Eq.
32
has been verified directly using the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distributions.

However, the impact on the fitted parameters differs from a simple shift of the mean, as it depends on the full shape of the
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distribution. This effect is illustrated in Fig.
10
, where a Pythia-based simulation is used without any injected energy-scale shift between data and simulation,
i.e.

\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}=0
, while only the value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
is varied. The prediction of the resulting bias is not straightforward; a naive estimate is shown in Fig.
10
. It is obtained by propagating the shift from Eq.
31
to the mean of the smeared
{V_{\mathrm{DY}}}
distribution using the expected probabilities
p_{ic}
from Eq.
28
.

Fig.
10
shows that this effect can be neglected for
{\sigma_{\gamma}}<0.02
. If required, it can also be calibrated out.

5.4.2
Biases arising from the
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}
selection

In
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
decays, the photon transverse momentum follows a steeply falling spectrum. As a consequence, when a selection such as
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}>c_{\gamma}
is applied, a bias may be introduced. We denote by
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{true}}
the true transverse momentum of the photon and by
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{meas}}
the measured one. Due to the combination of the steeply falling spectrum and energy-resolution effects, more photons with
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{true}}<c_{\gamma}
fluctuate into the selected region
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{meas}}>c_{\gamma}
than photons with
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{true}}>c_{\gamma}
fluctuate out of it. This results in an excess of photons with
\delta{r_{\gamma}}>0
entering the selection compared to photons with
\delta{r_{\gamma}}<0
leaving it. The net effect is therefore a positive bias on
\delta{r_{\gamma}}
in the selected sample.

To illustrate this effect, a Pythia-based simulation is used while varying the energy-resolution miscalibration parameter
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
. The data are assumed to be perfectly calibrated,
i.e.

\overline{\delta{r_{\gamma}}}=0
. A selection
{p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}}_{\mathrm{meas}}>c_{\gamma}
is applied and the fit is performed. The results are shown in Fig.
11
. In this figure, the predicted energy bias corresponds to the sum of the negative bias discussed in section
5.4.1
and the positive bias induced by the
p_{\mathrm{T}\,\gamma}
selection, quantified as
\langle\delta{r_{\gamma}}\rangle_{\mathrm{cut}}
for photons passing the
c_{\gamma}
requirement. An excellent agreement is observed between the predicted and the measured bias.

5.4.3
Considerations on the limitations

These limitations presented in this section are both related to the magnitude of the parameter
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
. In principle, they can be suppressed by smearing the simulation with the fitted value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
and repeating the fit to extract the energy scale with an excellent precision. However, this procedure is not implemented in the current version of the software, and the analyst must carefully assess the origin of fitted value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
before correcting this bias.

In practice, the smearing parameter can be precisely calibrated using
Z\to e^{+}e^{-}
decays with the method described in Section
2
, after which the simulation should be smeared accordingly. If the value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
extracted from
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
decays is significant, it may indicate additional sources of smearing affecting the
m_{\mu\mu\gamma}
invariant-mass distribution, potentially related to muon momentum calibration and/or photon angular resolution effects. In such cases, the parameter
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
effectively absorbs these mismodeling effects. This does not, however, introduce the biases discussed above, which are specifically associated with genuine photon energy-resolution mismodeling.

The interpretation of a large fitted value of
{\sigma_{\gamma}}
is therefore left to the analyst, who may choose either to correct for these effects by smearing the simulation prior to performing the fit, if necessary, or to assign a corresponding systematic uncertainty.

6
Conclusion

The method presented in this paper provides a new framework for the determination of lepton energy scale and resolution corrections based on an analytical treatment of detector smearing effects. By expressing the comparison between data and simulation through a fully differentiable likelihood, the approach eliminates random smearing techniques and enables an efficient use of automatic differentiation for likelihood minimization.

The method is implemented in the
IJazZ2.0
software and validated using toy Monte Carlo studies and realistic Pythia-based Drell–Yan simulations, demonstrating an accurate recovery of scale and resolution parameters within uncertainties. The impact of finite simulation statistics is consistently propagated to the fitted parameters through an analytical uncertainty estimate.

Lepton categorisations involving transverse momentum are shown to introduce biases due to kinematic correlations and category migration. A relative-
p_{T}
categorisation strategy is proposed to mitigate these effects and enables unbiased measurements in realistic conditions. The approach is further extended to the determination of the photon energy scale using
Z\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\gamma
events.

Thanks to its analytical formulation and compatibility with modern machine-learning frameworks, the proposed approach significantly improves numerical stability and reduces the computational cost by several orders of magnitude compared to traditional random smearing methods, making it well suited for large-scale precision calibration tasks at the LHC. The
IJazZ2.0
software is freely available to the community via a PyPI distribution
[
3
]
including relevant documentation and examples.
