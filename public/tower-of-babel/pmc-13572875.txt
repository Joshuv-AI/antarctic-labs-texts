From policy to practice: field stations as engines of tropical conservation
Authors: Jordan Karubian, Virginia Zaunbrecher, Ailin Blasco, Luis Carrasco, Fernando Castillo, Matthew Cassetta, Colin A Chapman, Jennifer Levine, Benneth Obitte, Brian O’Donnell, Gonzalo Rivas-Torres, Iroro Tanshi, Patricia Wright, Felipe Zapata, Thomas B Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLess than five years remain until 2030 deadlines for the Paris Agreement, the Sustainable Development Goals, and the Global Biodiversity Framework. Yet, the gulf between lofty commitments and actual progress grows wider each year. Field stations have tremendous, largely untapped potential to turn these targets into on-the-ground achievements. This is particularly true in the tropics, where biodiversity and carbon mitigation opportunities are highest. Strengthening tropical field stations creates powerful opportunities to partner with locals, support sustainable livelihoods at the interface of wildlands and protected areas, and integrate Indigenous and traditional knowledge with scientific inquiry to develop informed conservation strategies grounded in context-specific evidence. If we invest in them now, tropical field stations can become the connective tissue between global ambition and local action, translating policy into practice. We summarize the benefits and potential of tropical field stations and recommend ways to maximize their capacity to promote inclusive, effective environmental initiatives.Keywords: biodiversity, community engagement, conservation, policy, research infrastructure, sustainable development goals

Tropical biological field stations are vital hubs for the research that shapes global conservation targets. Yet scientific research alone is insufficient to reach and maintain these goals. Both the Global Biodiversity Framework and the Sustainable Development Goals underscore the need for equitable approaches that meaningfully engage and empower local communities. Strengthening tropical field stations creates powerful opportunities to partner with local people, support sustainable livelihoods, integrate indigenous and traditional knowledge with scientific inquiry, and advance conservation strategies grounded in robust, context-specific evidence.

The clock is ticking. With just four years remaining until the 2030 deadlines for the Paris Agreement, the Sustainable Development Goals, and the Global Biodiversity Framework, the distance between promise and performance is becoming impossible to ignore. Each year, most governments reaffirm their commitments. Each year, the planet falls further behind. In 2024, for example, forest destruction exceeded the levels recoded when 140 countries pledged to end deforestation by 2030 (Forest Declaration Assessment Partners 2025).

The core ambitions are well known: protect 30% of the planet, manage biodiversity for both people and nature, and ensure that communities have a real voice in decisions that shape their futures (Sarkar et al. 2019). Yet without a credible pathway to implementation, these goals risk becoming yet another set of aspirational slogans. At the Aichi 2010 Convention on Biodiversity, 20 biodiversity targets were adopted with great fanfare. A decade later, only four showed meaningful progress, whereas twelve are moving decisively in the wrong direction (Rounsevell et al. 2020). If the world is serious about meeting its 2030 commitments, incremental change will not suffice. The question we must confront—urgently and honestly—is how to turn global targets into action on the ground, at the scale and speed the crisis demands.

Field stations have tremendous, largely untapped, potential to tip the balance (figure 1). This is particularly true for field stations located in the tropics where biodiversity and carbon mitigation risks and opportunities are highest. If we invest in tropical field stations now, they can become the connective tissue between global ambition and local action, translating policy into practice (table 1). They can ensure that conservation strategies are anchored in real evidence and involve shared responsibility and equitable partnerships. They are also well-positioned to support on-the-ground implementation and adaptive refinement of these initiatives through meaningful local community involvement and evaluation. In a rapidly closing window of opportunity, the world cannot afford to ignore these quiet, but powerful, engines of change.

Biological field stations offer an effective approach to synthesizing community engagement, research, and conservation efforts in tropical areas, where proven successes remain limited. Field stations are uniquely suited to connect local, regional, and international stakeholders, promoting three-way flow of information between local perspectives, policymakers, and researchers across the domains of capacity building, research, livelihoods, and conservation.

Benefits and potential

Biological field stations in the tropics serve as crucial platforms for basic science, including fundamental breakthroughs in ecology, evolution, and climate science. In this age of increasing reliance on sensor-based data collection (e.g., motion active cameras, automated recording units) and analysis existing data sets, they engage biologists with the natural world (Soga and Gaston 2025). They also enable long-term data collection, providing unique insights into the effects of climate change, habitat fragmentation, and other biotic and abiotic phenomena. Expanding and sustaining these and other forms of biodiversity monitoring is crucial to supporting the formulation of key science-based conservation decisions, including the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services global assessment and the Global Deal for Nature proposal that provided the scientific foundation for the Global Biodiversity Framework, the formulation of the 30 × 30 targets, and global restoration targets.

By connecting scientific research with local knowledge, field stations have the unique opportunity to generate the range of information and perspectives needed to support robust evidence-based decision-making. They can enable effective engagement with local communities and interweave local perspectives and traditional ecological knowledge through community-engaged research, which can improve both science’s quality and its societal impact (Karubian et al. 2025). Most importantly, they train the next generation of environmental leaders and scientists, building urgently needed in-country capacity and amplifying local knowledge.

Stations are responsive, agile partners with a unique capacity to meet emerging research needs (Stuble et al. 2021). For example, the Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project, initiated in 1979, experimentally created forest fragments that have been continuously monitored for nearly 50 years (Laurance, 2018). Similarly, stations can serve as living laboratories to develop, test, and scale conservation solutions. For example, landscape scale experiments assessing the efficacy of emerging habitat restoration and agroforestry methodologies have been implemented at stations in Ecuador, Costa Rica, and Brazil, providing much-needed information on “what works” to conserve biodiversity and maximize return on investment (Holl et al. 2020). (Laurance, 2007)

Field stations directly conserve habitat at rates comparable with protected areas (Eppley, 2024), Torres-Romero et al. 2025). They minimize mining, deforestation, and hunting (Chapman et al. 2025). For example, in Taï National Park, Cote d’Ivoire, density of primates was 100 times higher near a field station than in the remainder of the park where hunting pressure was severe (N’Goran et al. 2012). Field stations also provide tangible socio-economic benefits including direct employment and other livelihoods linked to conservation (Torres-Romero et al. 2025). They offer “hands-on” education and training opportunities for scientists that strengthen local and international conservation and research capacities and collaborations, whereas also cultivating an appreciation for tropical ecosystems among non-scientists and promoting the importance of conservation among the general public.

Priorities moving forward

Currently, a suite of inter-connected challenges constrains tropical field stations from realizing their full potential.

More, better supported stations

Most stations rely on limited, episodic, and unpredictable fees from visiting scientists and field courses for financial support, bolstered by tenuous and often declining resources from academic institutions, often in the northern hemisphere. Others are managed by smaller, independent NGOs susceptible to funding shortfalls, political instability, or shifting governmental policies. This insecurity places many tropical biological field stations at risk and limits their ability to support long-term research, implement and evaluate direct conservation measures, engage local communities, and train scientists.

Tropical field stations require increased, stable, and diversified funding to realize their full potential as engines and laboratories of conservation. Governments and foundations should continue and increase support, and donors should invest in field stations as conservation actors. Long-term partnerships, especially with the private sector and individual donors, can foster novel discoveries that shape future conservation decision-making and outcomes (Deblauwe et al. 2025). Crucial technologies, such as carbon flux towers and molecular laboratories, are scarce in the tropics relative to northern hemisphere, and unevenly distributed. Field stations need access to endowments like the Global Legacy Landscapes fund and the Latin American Reserve Stewardship Initiative to support the operational, maintenance, and administrative costs that are often difficult for stations to fund. At the same time, stations must work to strengthen and diversify income streams, including increasing research and training programs like field courses and, in some cases, carefully managed ecotourism.

Models of connectivity and collaboration

Many tropical field stations operate in relative isolation, with limited engagement with local stakeholders, broader research networks, and the wider public. This disconnect is often compounded by inadequate resources for connectivity at the local level, inadequate cyberinfrastructure, and insufficient laboratory and communications infrastructure for synthesizing and disseminating findings, particularly to policymakers and communities. Increased reliance on sensors and analysis of existing data sets is reducing opportunities for meaningful long-term presence in the field. Field stations also reflect broader structural challenges in academia, including limited integration across disciplines, yet this integration is critical to promote successful conservation efforts. Most are primarily focused on the natural and physical sciences, with minimal engagement from the social sciences, humanities, or local knowledge systems, constraining the quality of evidence-based decision-making and hence the effectiveness of conservation strategies.

Tropical field stations are natural conveners for impactful gatherings for conservation forums and working groups, leveraging clear advantages to directly engage local communities, experts, and policymakers in situ. Researchers and funders should actively encourage and devote resources to interdisciplinary and community-engaged approaches (e.g., participatory action research) to communicate critical enhance the impact of scientific discoveries and findings to policymakers and engage press and communicators to ensure biodiversity is taken into account in land management and conservation.

There should also be support for formation of regional field station networks with active exchange of information and personnel, which would reduce isolation and promote best practices. For example, complementary projects by Fundacion para la Conservación de los Andes Tropicáles (FCAT) in Ecuador and the Ebony Project in Cameroon are achieving important restoration gains with an increasing degree of coordination (figure 2), and the Makerere University Biological Field Station in Kibale, Uganda supports large restoration and tree-planting efforts, with more than two million trees planted, creating valuable opportunities for collaboration.

Leading equitable and inclusive conservation efforts

Field stations have a critical role in reversing a legacy of colonialism, in which local residents and national scientists have been and, often still are, excluded from research and conservation activities (Raby 2017). This legacy is particularly acute in the Global South. Field stations are uniquely designed to both train and employ the next generation of in-country conservation leaders. To meet this challenge, they must actively involve local institutions, researchers, and residents in the field research process; increase the number of researchers at all levels, particularly local ones, and host intensive, hands-on training; and move decisively to make their management and operational structure more equitable. Visiting researchers can strengthen these efforts by proactively integrating local scientists into their projects as collaborators and equitable financial partners. Unfortunately, few academic funders support this sort of integration, leaving a critical funding gap. Donors and investors should increase support for capacity building, the research of local scientists, and the development of new, locally led, tropical field stations to address global disparities.

Conclusion

The global conservation community must change course—now—if it is to meet the fast-approaching 2030 targets. Tropical field stations hold exceptional promise as two-way conduits of knowledge and action between policymakers and local stakeholders. This model is already working at several tropical field stations, exemplifying what twenty-first-century conservation must become: effective, locally grounded, and built to last. Scaling up the number and capacity of supported tropical field stations is essential to closing the gap between global ambition and local action, and to ensuring that environmental policies are evidence-based, sustainable, impactful, and implementable.
