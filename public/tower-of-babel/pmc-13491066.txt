Epigenetic Rearrangements Driven by Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 Are Linked to Impaired Tissue Regeneration in Neonatal Necrotizing Enterocolitis
Authors: Jean-Pierre Etchegaray
Published: 2026

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a life-threatening gastrointestinal disorder that primarily affects premature infants. The pathogenesis of NEC involves intestinal injury, inflammation, and necrosis, which can progress to ulceration and systemic illness affecting multiple organs, resulting in neonates’ mortality.1 A prominent feature of NEC is the inability to repair the intestinal epithelia undergoing cellular apoptosis, necrosis, and destruction of tight junctions, which disrupts the intestinal barrier, causing severe systemic inflammation. The epigenetic mechanisms underlying NEC remain largely unclear, especially in terms of causality, cell-type specificity, and tissue regeneration. Using a multiomics approach based on single-nucleus RNA sequencing, single-nucleus assay for transposable-accessible chromatin sequencing, and Cleavage Under targets and Tagmentation for enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) and trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3), and spatial transcriptomics by multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization, Xiong et al2 show that intestinal epithelial damage in NEC disrupts the recruitment of EZH2, a histone methyltransferase within the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which is an epigenetic regulator required for transcriptional silencing.3 More specifically, EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 functions as an epigenetic repressive mark for the establishment and maintenance of cell fate identity during development.4 Xiong et al2 found that the enrichment of H3K27me3 cobound with EZH2 at promoter regions of genes involved in developmental pathways and antagonists of Wnt-signaling was disrupted in NEC. This resulted in a reorganization of H3K27me3 that negatively affected the expression of genes involved in Wnt-signaling and stem cell function, including Axin2, Smoc2, and Lgr5, which are fundamental for tissue maintenance and repair. Consequently, the cell fate identity of leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 (LGR5)+ intestinal stem cells (ISCs) was compromised, which, in turn, impaired regeneration of the intestinal epithelia in NEC. Additionally, H3K27me3 rearrangements in NEC correlated with the activation of proinflammatory genes, including IL6 and TNFα, known to sustain chronic inflammation and tissue damage by driving immune cell infiltration, leading to necrosis.

An interesting finding from Xiong et al2 is that H3K27me3 was not fully reduced in ISCs from their mouse model with an Ezh2-specific deletion in LGR5+ ISCs. Possibly, this could be explained by noncatalytic and noncanonical EZH2 functions independent of PRC2, as shown in other contexts.5 It is accepted that the function of EZH2 is complex and context-dependent in terms of its molecular partners and cellular states.4,6 Alternatively, Xiong et al2 proposed a compensatory effect by EZH1, the paralogue of EZH2 within PRC2, to account for the not fully reduced H3K27me3 in their Ezh2 knockout mouse model. All-and-all, the findings by Xiong et al2 showed an EZH2-mediated epigenetic mechanism that affects the expression of genes involved in stem cell maintenance and proinflammatory responses, leading to impaired tissue repair and chronic inflammation in NEC. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors include the necessary reduction in sample size to achieve higher resolution multiomics profiling and the use of a single-mouse model of NEC, a highly heterogeneous disease. Thus, this study may have missed rare cell subtypes due to single-cell analyses and other epigenetic dynamics associated with NEC. This gastrointestinal disease exhibits a complex multifactorial pathogenesis, including immature intestinal barrier, dysbiosis, and a disproportionate innate immune response, resulting in bacterial leakage, tissue injury, and necrosis. Consequently, the development of NEC mouse model systems in combination with genome-wide approaches are technically challenging to establish and consolidate. Future experimental approaches to further unravel the role of EZH2 in NEC may include comparisons of diverse mouse models with EZH2 depletion vs catalytically dead EZH2 mutants in LGR5+ ISCs. The potential compensatory effect from EZH1 could be experimentally addressed in mice models with Ezh1/Ezh2 double deletions in LGR5+ ISCs. Lastly, the use of human samples will be instrumental to validate and further unravel the function of EZH2 and H3K27me3 in NEC.
