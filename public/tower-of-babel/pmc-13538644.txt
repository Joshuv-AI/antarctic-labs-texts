The impoverishing effect of tobacco expenditures
Authors: Elvina Merkaj, Drini Imami, Edvin Zhllima, Jeffrey Drope
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tobacco consumption is a major source of health and financial burden, yet evidence on its association with household poverty remains limited. This study examines how tobacco-related expenditures are associated with both the incidence and depth of poverty in Albania. Using nationally representative data from the Household Budget Survey, we estimate poverty indicators under three alternative definitions of household resources: observed expenditure, expenditure net of tobacco spending, and expenditure net of both tobacco and smoking-attributable healthcare costs. The results show that accounting for tobacco-related expenditures is associated with higher poverty levels. Under the most comprehensive scenario, approximately 13,000 additional households (representing nearly 60,000 individuals, including over 10,000 children) fall below the poverty line because of related tobacco expenditures. The headcount ratio increases from about 27% to 29%, while the poverty gap widens by around 2% points. Complementary regression analysis further indicates that higher tobacco expenditure shares are associated with a greater likelihood of being poor. These findings suggest that tobacco-related expenditures are closely associated with economic vulnerability and highlight the potential contribution of tobacco control policies to improving financial protection, reducing poverty-related vulnerability, and supporting broader public health and social development objectives.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains one of the leading preventable causes of death, disease, and financial hardship worldwide. It is a major risk factor for non-communicable diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory conditions, and various cancers1, all of which impose substantial costs on individuals, households, and health systems. According to the World Health Organization, more than 1.3 billion people use tobacco, with over 80% living in low- and middle-income countries2. In these settings, countries face a dual challenge: high levels of tobacco consumption alongside limited capacity to manage the resulting health and economic consequences. These include increased health expenditures, reduced productivity, and increased vulnerability to poverty3–7.

From an economic perspective, the link between tobacco use and household welfare can be understood through the opportunity cost approach. Households operate with limited resources and must decide how to allocate them across competing needs in ways that maximise utility8,9. Because these resources are limited, spending more on one good necessarily reduces the amount available for others. In this framework, consumption decisions involve trade-offs between competing uses of income. This trade-off becomes particularly important in the case of addictive goods such as tobacco, where consumption may persist even under tight budget constraints, as suggested by models of rational addiction10. In this sense, expenditure on tobacco represents an opportunity cost, as resources devoted to tobacco could otherwise be allocated to goods that directly enhance household well-being, such as food, education, or healthcare.

A related perspective, the crowding-out approach, looks at how these trade-offs play out in practice. This approach investigates whether households that spend more on tobacco actually reduce spending on essential goods and services. Evidence from a wide range of countries shows that tobacco consumption can divert limited household resources away from basic needs such as food, housing, education, and healthcare11–16. Recent evidence from Albania points in the same direction, showing that tobacco spending reduces expenditure on essential goods while increasing spending on other addictive products such as alcohol17.

Beyond direct spending, tobacco use can also create financial pressure through its health consequences. Smoking-related illnesses often lead to higher out-of-pocket healthcare costs, lower productivity, and longer-term economic strain. In countries where health systems offer limited financial protection, households frequently have to rely on their own resources to cover treatment costs. This can mean borrowing money, selling assets, or delaying care altogether18,19. These challenges tend to be especially severe for households that are already economically vulnerable.

A growing body of evidence across different countries shows that tobacco spending is linked to higher levels of poverty or deeper economic deprivation. Evidence from China suggests that tobacco-related expenditures pushed tens of millions of people below the poverty line20, while research in India estimates that tobacco use is associated with impoverishment of around 15 million people21. Similar patterns have been observed in countries such as Vietnam, Indonesia, Mexico and Montenegro, particularly among lower-income households22–25.

Albania provides a particularly relevant context for examining these dynamics. It is an emerging post-communist economy in the Western Balkans with relatively high smoking prevalence: around 22.5% of adults and 6.3% of young people use tobacco26. Smoking also represents a significant health burden, accounting for a substantial share (22.1%) of premature mortality26. At the same time, tobacco consumption represents a non-negligible financial cost relative to income levels, placing additional pressure on household budgets. On average, a smoker spends approximately 3.8% of GDP per capita to buy 100 packs of the most popular cigarettes annually26.

Economic vulnerability remains an important concern in Albania. In 2022, around 20.6% of the population is at risk of poverty, and 33.2% experience severe material deprivation27. At the same time, the health system provides relatively low financial protection compared to other European countries. Government health expenditure accounts for only 6.6% of total government spending28, and more than half of total health expenditure is financed out-of-pocket by households29. In practice, this means that households facing illness often rely on their own resources to cover medical costs.

Using nationally representative data from the Household Budget Survey (HBS), this study examines how tobacco expenditures and smoking-attributable healthcare costs are associated with poverty outcomes in Albania. Spending on tobacco and tobacco-related health care may reduce the resources available for essential consumption, thereby influencing both the likelihood of falling into poverty and the depth of deprivation. We adopt an accounting-based approach that evaluates poverty under alternative definitions of household resources by sequentially deducting tobacco expenditure and smoking-related health costs.

This allows us to assess how the incidence of poverty (headcount ratio) and its depth (poverty gap) change when these expenditures are treated as a reduction in disposable resources. To complement this approach, we also estimate a logistic regression model to examine how tobacco use is associated with the likelihood of being poor while controlling for household characteristics. This combined framework allows us to distinguish between tobacco participation and expenditure intensity, and to provide a more nuanced picture of how tobacco use is linked to economic vulnerability.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it provides the first nationally representative assessment of the relationship between tobacco use and poverty in Albania, a context where both poverty and out-of-pocket health expenditures remain significant but evidence is limited. Second, it jointly considers both direct tobacco expenditures and smoking-attributable healthcare costs, offering a more comprehensive measure of the economic burden of tobacco at the household level, which is highly relevant to the many countries like Albania with this potential dual burden. Third, the analysis captures both the incidence and depth of poverty under alternative resource definitions. By combining an accounting-based framework with econometric analysis, the study captures both the incidence and depth of poverty and provides complementary evidence on how tobacco use is associated with poverty across households.

Methods

Dataset and variables

This study investigates the impoverishing effects of tobacco use in Albania by analysing how tobacco-related expenditures influence two key poverty indicators: the headcount ratio and the poverty gap. These indicators are computed using data from the 2017 Albanian Household Budget Survey (HBS). HBS, conducted by the Albanian Institute of Statistics (INSTAT), the national statistical office responsible for the production and dissemination of official statistics in Albania, is a nationally representative household survey designed to collect detailed information on consumption, expenditures, income, and living conditions, primarily to support the calculation of Consumer Price Index weights and national accounts estimates. The HBS follows internationally harmonized methodological standards and is part of a broader system of household surveys implemented in many countries.

This ﻿study is based exclusively on anonymized secondary data from the HBS conducted by the INSTAT. The data were provided by INSTAT to the research team upon request for research purposes and contained no personally identifiable information. As the study involved secondary analysis of anonymized survey data and no direct interaction with human participants, ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this research. All analyses were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The survey is based on a stratified probability sampling design, where the population is divided into strata by geographical regions and urban–rural classification, ensuring representativeness across the country. Approximately 7,519 households were successfully interviewed. Information was gathered through a combination of household interviews and expenditure diaries30. This methodological approach ensures that the survey produces reliable and nationally representative estimates of household consumption and socio-economic conditions, while maintaining consistency with similar surveys conducted internationally.

Econometric specification (complementary analysis)

To complement the accounting-based analysis, we estimate a logistic regression model to examine how tobacco use is associated with the likelihood of household poverty. While the accounting approach evaluates how poverty indicators change under alternative definitions of household resources, the regression analysis provides additional evidence on the association between tobacco use and poverty after controlling for household characteristics.

The dependent variable equals one if the household’s per capita expenditure falls below the national poverty line, based on total expenditure before subtracting tobacco-related costs and zero otherwise.

The headcount ratio

We begin by presenting headcount poverty under the three expenditure scenarios described above, focusing on the number of households, individuals, and children living below the poverty line, both in absolute terms and as a share of the population (Table 2).

The baseline scenario reflects observed poverty levels based on total household expenditure, while the subsequent scenarios adjust household resources by excluding tobacco expenditure and, additionally, smoking-attributable health expenditures. The comparison across scenarios provides an assessment of how the incidence of poverty changes when tobacco-related expenditures are deducted from household resources. These figures should be interpreted as a descriptive comparison, indicating how poverty classification changes when tobacco expenditures are excluded, rather than as a causal effect of tobacco consumption.

Over 200,000 households have monthly per capita expenditures below ALL 12,008, which is considered by INSTAT to be the at-risk-of-poverty level for year 2017. They represent almost 27% of households in Albania. Almost one million household members, of which almost 217,000 are children, live below the poverty line (Table 2).

Under Scenario 1, we examine how poverty estimates change when tobacco expenditure is excluded from total household resources. The results show that approximately 11,500 additional households, representing about 52,000 individuals, including around 9,000 children, are found to be below the poverty line when tobacco spending is deducted.

When both tobacco expenditure and smoking-attributable health expenditures are excluded from household resources (Scenario 2), headcount poverty increases further compared to both the baseline and Scenario 1. Under this specification, approximately 12,900 additional households fall below the poverty line relative to the baseline, corresponding to about 59,500 individuals, including around 10,700 children. Tobacco expenditures are associated with an increase of approximately 2% points in the share of the households classified as living below the poverty line.

The poverty gap

We next analyze the poverty gap, which captures the depth of poverty by measuring the average shortfall of household resources from the poverty line, expressed as a percentage. Table 3 presents average per capita expenditure and the corresponding poverty gap among poor smoker households under the three expenditure scenarios. Average shortfall from the poverty line (monthly ALL per capita) is calculated as the difference between the poverty line and average per capita expenditure. In the baseline, average per capita expenditure is 8,966 ALL, implying an average shortfall from the poverty line of 3,042 ALL, or 25.3% of the threshold. When tobacco expenditure is excluded from household resources, mean expenditure declines to 8,766 ALL, and the corresponding shortfall increases to 3,242 ALL, equivalent to 27.0% of the poverty line. This represents an increase of 200 ALL, or 1.7% points in the poverty gap.

When both tobacco and smoking-attributable health expenditures are excluded, mean per capita expenditure declines further to 8,745 ALL, with an average shortfall of 3,263 ALL, corresponding to 27.1% of the poverty line. Relative to the baseline, this implies an increase of 221 ALL in the average shortfall and 1.8% points in the poverty gap.

Robustness and Complementary analysis

To assess the robustness of the main findings, we re-estimate the poverty measures using alternative assumptions on SAF. In addition, we complement the accounting-based analysis with an econometric approach that examines the association between tobacco use and poverty while controlling for household characteristics.

Sensitivity to SAF

The estimation of smoking-attributable healthcare costs relies on a single SAF of 10%, based on country-specific evidence31. This estimate is derived from the same national context as the present study and shows limited variation across macro-regions and between urban and rural areas, supporting its use as a representative average. However, applying a uniform SAF does not capture heterogeneity in smoking behavior, disease risk, or healthcare utilization across households. In addition, the SAF is estimated at the aggregate (population) level and not directly observed at the household level, which may introduce measurement limitations when attributing costs to individual households.

To assess the sensitivity of our results to this assumption, we consider alternative SAF values reported in the literature. Global evidence suggest that smoking accounts for approximately 5% to 15% of total healthcare expenditures, depending on country context35,36. We also include a higher value based on estimates from the Global Burden of Disease study, according to which smoking was responsible for 21.6% of total deaths in Albania in 201931. Although this measure refers to mortality rather than healthcare costs, it provides a useful upper-bound scenario.

Accordingly, we perform robustness checks using SAF values of 5%, 15%, and 21.6% (Table 4). Since the SAF affects the second scenario where both tobacco and related health costs are deducted, we recalculate both the headcount ratio and the poverty gap under this specification and compare the results with the baseline. Estimates based on the 10% SAF are retained for reference.

The results show a clear association between tobacco consumption patterns and poverty status. Households that report tobacco use are, on average, less likely to be poor, suggesting that smoking is more prevalent among relatively better-off households. However, a higher tobacco expenditure share is positively associated with poverty, indicating that households allocating a larger portion of their budget to tobacco tend to face greater economic vulnerability. This contrast highlights the difference between tobacco participation and tobacco expenditure intensity: while smoking itself may be more common among relatively better-off households, devoting a larger share of household resources to tobacco is associated with greater economic vulnerability. This pattern is also confirmed among smoker households, where higher tobacco expenditure intensity remains associated with a greater likelihood of poverty.

Among the control variables, larger households are more likely to be poor, while education is associated with a lower probability of poverty. Urban residence and a higher number of employed household members are associated with a lower probability of poverty, while prefecture fixed effects indicate significant regional variation in living standards. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the data and potential endogeneity concerns, the results should be interpreted with caution, reflecting associations rather than causal effects.

Discussions and conclusions

Albania faces a significant public health and economic challenge due to its relatively high prevalence of tobacco use, especially among adult males. Tobacco consumption remains a leading risk factor for many non-communicable diseases and contributes substantially to preventable morbidity and premature mortality. In addition to the health consequences, tobacco use imposes a severe financial burden on both individual households and for the broader economy.

This study provides the first nationally representative assessment of how tobacco-related expenditures are associated with poverty outcomes in Albania. The findings suggest that approximately 13,000 households—representing nearly 60,000 individuals, including over 10,000 children—fall below the poverty line when tobacco expenditure and related healthcare costs are accounted for. The national headcount poverty rate increases from 27% to 29% when these smoking-related costs are considered. These households are classified as unable to meet basic needs (such as food, housing, and healthcare) when tobacco-related expenditures are accounted for in household resources.

In addition to being associated with a higher incidence of poverty, tobacco-related expenditure is also linked to greater depth of poverty among those already below the poverty line. The poverty gap increases by approximately 2% points, indicating that existing poor households are further deprived by tobacco-related spending. This aligns with similar findings in other low- and middle-income countries23,37, where tobacco use has been shown to significantly erode household welfare and exacerbate economic vulnerability.

Moreover, regression results indicate that households allocating a larger share of their budget to tobacco tend to have a higher likelihood of being poor, including within the subsample of smoker households. Overall, these findings suggest that the association between tobacco-related expenditures and poverty is robust to alternative assumptions and model specifications.

Our findings have direct implications for public health policy and fiscal strategies. Previous evidence has shown that price-based interventions, such as increased tobacco excise taxes, can effectively reduce smoking prevalence, particularly among poorer populations who are more price-sensitive38. At the same time, tobacco taxation policies should be accompanied by measures that strengthen financial protection within the health system, particularly for low-income households facing high out-of-pocket expenditures related to smoking-attributable diseases. Expanding access to smoking cessation services including counselling, and nicotine replacement therapies may further reduce both the health and financial burden of tobacco use by helping smokers to quit altogether. Such services should be especially targeted at the most vulnerable populations, including those with low income. In terms of health equity, although higher tobacco taxes may disproportionately affect poorer smokers who struggle to quit in the short term, evidence from around the globe demonstrates that these smokers’ larger price responsiveness—i.e., higher likelihood of quitting or cutting down—means that they are also more likely to benefit from reduced consumption and the corresponding improved long-term welfare outcomes. By framing tobacco control as both a public health and poverty alleviation strategy, this study supports integrated policy approaches aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities). Understanding the economic impact of tobacco use at the household level is therefore crucial to designing holistic interventions that promote both health equity and social protection.

Finally, increasing health awareness and literacy is essential to improve the effectiveness of tobacco control policies. Public health campaigns (particularly those targeting adolescents and economically disadvantaged communities) must be made more accessible and culturally relevant to enhance understanding and impact39–41. Education-based prevention strategies, when combined with fiscal tools like taxation, can play a critical role in reducing tobacco use and breaking the link between smoking and poverty.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the analysis relies on the 2017 HBS, as more recent comparable household microdata were not accessible. Although the 2017 HBS remains nationally representative and methodologically consistent with international standards, the use of pre-pandemic data may not fully capture more recent changes in tobacco consumption patterns, income dynamics, prices, or health expenditures, particularly following COVID-19 and subsequent economic shocks. Besides, the lack of individual-level tobacco use data, prevents analysis of intra-household differences, such as variation by gender or age. These limitations restrict a more detailed understanding of the mechanisms linking tobacco use and household welfare.

Household resources are limited, so spending on tobacco is associated with reduced resources available for other types of consumption. However, this does not mean that, in the absence of tobacco use, these resources would necessarily be spent on goods that improve well-being, as household decisions are also influenced by preferences, addiction, and other constraints. Moreover, the analysis cannot directly observe how such resources would be reallocated. Nevertheless, existing evidence shows that tobacco spending is often linked to lower expenditure on essential goods, supporting the relevance of the patterns observed in this study. Finally, because the analysis relies on expenditure-based accounting exercises and cross-sectional associations, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than causal relationships of the effect of tobacco use on poverty.
