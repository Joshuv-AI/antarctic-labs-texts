Fast food: GPS tracking reveals behavior-specific habitat selection and cattle farm subsidies of three sympatric neotropical vulture species
Authors: Christopher Beirne, Enzo Basso, Eduardo Fabrizio Tubelli, Sarah Wicks, Eleanor Flatt, Diego Rolim Chulla, Rosio Vega Quispe, Caleb Jonatan Quispe Quispe, Flor Maria Perez Mullisaca, Sara Campos Landázuri, Priscila Peralta-Aguilar, Andrew Whitworth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding species responses to anthropogenic disturbance is fundamental to ecology and conservation. However, behaviour and inter-individual variation in habitat selection can complicate our understanding of population-level responses to human disturbance, and the initiatives we design to address them. We use a dataset of 601,000 locations from 42 GPS tracked individuals of three sympatric species - king (n = 32), turkey (n = 5) and black vultures (n = 5) - in Costa Rica to explore behaviour-specific habitat selection in a mixed forest-agriculture landscape. We separate the movement data into three key behaviours (flight, feeding and roosting), and then compare their habitat selection preferences using a species-specific generalised mixed modelling framework. We find that the disturbance sensitive king vultures typically prefer mature forest to anthropogenically modified habitats when flying or roosting, however, the difference in relative strength of selection disappears in relation to feeding. These patterns likely reflect king vultures selecting to feed in agricultural landscapes where dead livestock such as cattle are abundant. We find strong evidence for individual heterogeneity in the degree to which they select for livestock pasture as feeding sites, suggesting repeatable individual-variation in foraging tactics. We also found a strong reduction in habitat selection for all behaviours as the distance to contiguous high-quality habitat increases, showing the reliance on tracts of contiguous mature forest for this species. In comparison, the disturbance tolerant species showed idiosyncratic responses to modified habitats, whereby black vultures selected mature forests for flight, but disturbed forest and grassland margins for feeding and roosting, and turkey vultures selected disturbed forest over mature forest and grassland margins for all movement behaviours. These findings represent the first GPS derived habitat selection study of king vultures, the apex obligate scavengers in lowland neotropical habitats and the first multispecies, guild-level, habitat selection analysis of vultures in the lowland neotropics. As expected, we find that king vultures have a stronger affinity to mature forests than their disturbance tolerant counterparts, however cattle farming is likely strongly subsidising some individuals’ diets. Despite this, the marked reduction in king vulture habitat use with increasing distances from contiguous mature forests suggests the existence of a disturbance threshold, whereby the carrying capacity of king vulture populations may be reduced in highly anthropogenically modified landscapes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00610-0.Keywords: Behaviour, Conservation, Individual-variation, Landscape fragmentation, Movement ecology, New world vultures, Scavengers, Selection

Introduction

Anthropogenic habitat conversion and fragmentation have been identified as the main cause of long-term deleterious effects on biodiversity and ecological processes [1]. These changes have been primarily driven by the expansion of agriculture and urban spaces leading to the loss of more than a third of global forest cover in the last half century [2]. The impacts of such changes are expected to be exacerbated in the coming decades [3–5], particularly as climate change drives further alterations to Earth’s existing ecosystems. Characterising how wildlife populations respond to anthropogenic habitat conversion to safeguard their futures is crucial in a rapidly changing world.

The ‘winners and losers’ paradigm offers one way to characterise how ecological communities respond to anthropogenic disturbance by acknowledging that whilst changes will be detrimental to many species, some will benefit [6]. Patterns consistent with this paradigm have been found across multiple taxonomic groups and ecosystems, including: terrestrial mammals in central Canada [7]; mammal and bird assemblages in south Australia [8]; and pioneer plants and understory birds in the Amazon basin [9]. However, anthropogenic disturbances are typically heterogenous at the landscape scale, with patches of natural habitat interspersed with anthropogenic land uses [10] and as such variation in the spatial configuration of disturbed landscapes also has the potential to alter how different populations and individuals respond [11]. One of the fundamental ways in which individuals respond to changing landscapes is through altering their movement behaviour and consequently their habitat selection (i.e., the differentiated use of habitat in relation to its availability) [12]. Individuals must satisfy a diverse array of competing demands to survive and reproduce to maintain healthy and stable populations, and the effects of anthropogenic disturbance on these different demands may differ. For example, it is possible that selection for habitats with the greatest availability of resources may also be linked to direct or indirect costs (e.g. higher mortality risk [13]). Furthermore, it is possible that habitat fragmentation may have different implications for different behavioural activities, or strategies, (e.g. [14]) and even for different individuals within the same species [15]. Given that the sum of such changes ultimately drives population-level distribution and abundance responses to anthropogenic disturbance, species- and behaviour-specific responses are important to consider [16].

Whilst terrestrial species have been determined to be particularly sensitive to anthropogenically impacted landscapes [17], it has been proposed that flying wildlife could buffer the loss of ecosystem services in fragmented landscapes due to their ability to cross terrestrial barriers [18]. Despite this, many large-bodied birds which provide essential ecosystem services are currently displaying significant population declines - particularly vultures [19]. Vulture population declines have often been associated with poisoning, persecution, and anthropogenic habitat change [20]. In New World vultures, evidence suggests that the size and direction of response to habitat fragmentation is driven from a species-specific life-history perspective. For instance, previous work has suggested at least two species appear to benefit from anthropogenic disturbance (a.k.a. ‘winners’), the black vulture (Coragyps atratus) and the turkey vulture (Cathartes aura). They have adapted well to habitat fragmentation, finding communal roosting and feeding grounds within urban matrix habitat [21]. Larger species (e.g. California condor (Gymnogyps californianus) and the Andean condor (Vultur gryphus)) appear to ‘lose’ in response to increasing anthropogenic disturbance as they have shown a preference for undisturbed areas for flying and roosting [22, 23]. However, regular consumption of farm animals and foraging in landfills by larger species within a suburban matrix (in the case of the Andean condor) complicates this picture [24–26] and highlights the importance of performing both within and between species assessments of behaviour-specific habitat selection in mixed-use landscapes.

Here we principally focus on the king vulture (Sarcoramphus papa), the apex obligate scavenger typically found in lowland neotropical rainforest habitats and a charismatic keystone species with a vital role in nutrient cycling processes [27]. The species has a wide distribution in the Americas, from central Mexico to northern Argentina and Uruguay, and is also found throughout the Amazon basin [28]. Although its conservation status is currently of “Least Concern” according to the IUCN Red List [29], it is typically considered to be associated with undisturbed habitats, and population declines have been documented in Central America and extirpations occurring in the northernmost part of its range in Mexico [28]. Whilst such declines have been suggested to be linked to habitat loss and degradation, empirical research assessing this remains scarce [30]. Crucially, despite the importance of king vultures to lowland neotropical ecosystems, there has been no research to date on the role of anthropogenic disturbance on their habitat selection preferences [20]. Therefore, it is a priority to characterise how king vultures respond - both at the individual and the population level - to the rapid transformation of their habitat due to human activities, such as deforestation and agricultural expansion. We contrast the King vulture with the wide ranging, disturbance tolerant black and turkey vulture species, whose patterns of habitat selection have received substantially more research attention, albeit largely geographically focused on north America [20].

Specifically, we explore the ‘winners and losers’ paradigm by examining the interaction between anthropogenic disturbance and habitat-selection behaviour of these three co-occurring vulture species in a mixed-use landscape in southern Pacific Costa Rica. We use GPS tracking devices [31] to facilitate quantification of behaviour-specific (e.g. [32]) and individual-specific responses [33–35] to different land uses. We first take advantage of the larger sample size of king vultures (32 individuals; 486,000 locations) to model habitat selection across three different behaviours: flying, feeding, and roosting and then explore the implications of individual variation in habitat selection choices through correlating selection strength with an index of individual condition (scaled-mass index) and an index of range quality (home range size). We then contrast general patterns in king vulture habitat selection with the two disturbance tolerant vulture species - black (5 individuals; 53,000 locations) and turkey vultures (5 individuals; 61,000 locations). Considering that our study is in an ecologically diverse area in terms of both conserved and anthropogenically altered habitats, we predicted that the king vultures would select for intact and mature forest patches over anthropogenically disturbed habitats. In contrast, given their status as ‘winners’ under anthropogenic disturbance scenarios, we expected black and turkey vultures to exhibit a preference for anthropogenically modified habitats.

Materials and methods

Study site

The study is focused in and around the Puntarenas province, situated on the southern Pacific slope of Costa Rica, which encompasses an area of 11,276 km2 and is the largest of the seven provinces in the country. The landscape of the region (Fig. 1A) includes a matrix of forest, and anthropogenically created habitats including grasslands for cattle farming, crop plantations (e.g. palm oil, pineapple) and urban areas. The region supports three major national parks: La Amistad International Peace Park in the highlands - a large trans-boundary high-elevation (200 - 3549 masl) world heritage site encompassing 4000 km2 - and Corcovado and Piedras Blancas National Parks which combined represent 564 km2 - the largest remaining tract of lowland wet forest in Pacific Mesoamerica [36]. The region experiences a tropical climate, with marked rainy and dry seasons (May to November and December to April respectively) [37].

Animal capture and GPS data collection

Vulture capture efforts were conducted at three locations (see Supplementary Information S1) within two principal periods: from the 16th of February 2022 to the 24th of March 2022 and from the 23rd of December 2022 to the 17th June 2023. During the capture efforts, two walk-in box traps were deployed side-by-side. These were built of steel tubes painted green for camouflage and covered in plastic poultry mesh. The dimensions of the traps were 2 m high ×3 m wide ×3 m deep, with a door 2 m high ×1.5 m wide following the recommendations of Barber & Bildstein [38]. The vertical sliding doors were activated by a rope leading to a hide approximately 30 m away to minimise disturbance. Whole or eviscerated pig (Sus scrofa domestica) carcasses certified by the Servicio Nacional de Sanidad Animal (SENASA) were used as bait. During the trapping efforts we captured and attached satellite tracking devices to 43 individuals, comprising of king vulture (Sarcoramphus papa; n = 33), black vulture (Coragyps atratus; n = 5) and turkey vulture (Cathartes aura; n = 5). All individuals were fitted with light-weight, solar-panelled, e-obs GPS-GPRS tags (http://www.e-obs.de), which included a tri-axial accelerometer and altimeter (king vulture tag weight = 42 g; black and turkey vulture tag weight = 27 g). The tags were attached with Teflon thoracic (X-strap) harnesses [39–41]. The total weight (GPS tags + harness) was 49 g and 34 g, representing approximately 1.3% and 2.2% of the body mass of king vultures and, black and turkey vultures, respectively. The e-obs tags were set to acquire locations every 30 minutes when battery levels were low and every 10 minutes when battery levels were high, from 05:00 to 19:00 local time. The e-obs tags contained GSM receivers, which uploaded data daily when passing cellphone towers. All movement data was stored on Movebank [42]roject name ‘OC Vultures’). We established the tags had negligible calibration error of 3.6 m (95% CI 3.3–3.8 m [43]; which we determined through the collection of 72 h of locations from a single fixed location.

Data analysis

Movement data filtering

We filtered out the first seven days post-device deployment to minimise the potential for the capture process to influence movement behaviour and habitat selection. We then visually inspected the data to remove any overt location outliers and removed any locations which the individual would have to obtain an impossible speed to reach (using a minimum speed ms−1 vs. interval duration plot; threshold = 50 ms−1). As the algorithms used to detect diurnal feeding events and perform the habitat selection analyses (detailed below) can be scale dependent, we subsampled our data to only include days captured in with good coverage - which we defined as days where there is at least one location in each of the hours between 5 am and 7 pm – and where the sampling frequency was ‘moderate’ (see [44]) - which we defined as > 2 per hour and < 7 per hour (mean number of locations per individual-day after data filtering = 59). The upper threshold was used to remove a 10 day window where each individual was followed at 1 minute frequency. This step resulted in the complete removal of one king vulture from the data set as this device did not function effectively after deployment. This filtering step resulted in the following species-specific dataset sizes: king vulture = 32 individuals and 8060 unique individual-days of data (17.2% of ‘low resolution’ days removed); black vulture = 5 individuals and 1069 unique individual-days of data (11.6% of ‘low resolution’ days removed); turkey vulture = 5 individuals and 1074 unique individual-days of data (13.7% of ‘low resolution’ days removed).

Behavioural designation

We further classified our movement data into three categories: flying, feeding/resting and roosting. We describe the definitions of each in turn below. ‘Flying’ was defined as any location with an instantaneous ground speed of 4 ms−1 or higher (king vulture = 486,554 locations; black vulture = 14,272 locations; turkey vulture = 25,924 locations), which has been previously used as a reliable indicator of flight behaviour in these species [45]. To separate roosting from feeding/resting behaviour, we used the ‘GPSeqClus’ package in R [46]. This package takes location data and clusters points which occur in the same time and space using user defined parameters. We used a search radius of 75 m, a temporal window of 14.5 hours, and a minimum number of locations in the cluster of 4 (representing at least 1 hour in duration). We then post-hoc classified these clusters into ‘feeding/resting’ and ‘roosting’ using the following criteria: ‘roosting’ = a cluster which includes one or more nighttime periods without moving (king vulture = 15,517 occasions; black vulture = 807; turkey vulture = 791); ‘feeding/resting’ = any cluster which involves a displacement from the previous nights roosting location, and is not used for roosting the subsequent night (king vulture = 7,746 occasions; black vulture = 2,101; turkey vulture = 1,745). We use the geographical centroid of each of these designated clusters as the location for extracting habitat selection variables.

Habitat selection variables

To classify habitat types we use a modified version of the discrete ESA WorldCover 10 m v200 [47] raster layer (Fig. 1A). As this dataset does not include palm oil, we first replaced any cells which are classified as palm oil plantation in the ‘BIOPAMA/GlobalOilPalm/v1’ [48] dataset with a new habitat - ‘palm oil’. Given that forest quality is also not accounted for in the WorldCover designations, we then used the ‘ETH Global Sentinel-2 10 m Canopy Height’ model [49] to split “forest” into two new categories reflecting their ecological integrity and disturbance history: ‘mature forest’ (representing a pixel average height > 25 m) and ‘disturbed forest’ (representing a pixel average height ≤ 25 m) − 25 m represented a natural inflection point in the height data for the focal region (Supplementary Information S2). Finally, given that vultures use trees and covered areas alongside cattle pasture when feeding within disturbed landscapes (as determined through field visits to cluster locations; summarised in Supplementary Information S3), we converted ‘grassland’ and any forest habitat which falls within 50 m of a grassland patch to a new habitat termed ‘grassland margins’ (Fig. 1A). As vultures flew over the open ocean very rarely, and it was not possible to roost or feed there, we excluded all locations which did not occur on land (n = 225; 0.2% of flying locations; Supplementary Information S4). The majority of habitat within the area of interest (95.4%) was comprised of four habitat types: ‘mature forest’ (34.1%), ‘disturbed forest’ (13.7%), ‘grassland margins’ (42.6%) and ‘palm oil’ (5.0%), and as such we focus our analyses on these. The remaining habitats (mangrove, water bodies, cropland, urban, and wetland) were collapsed into an ‘other’ category (4.6%).

We also created three continuous layers reflecting continuous variables hypothesised to influence vulture habitat selection (see Table 1): i) ‘elevation’ - the focal vulture species are known to be lowland specialists absent from high elevation locations; ii) ‘terrain roughness’ - as a proxy for the availability of thermals for more efficient flight [50]; and distance to contiguous patch of ‘mature forest’ - to capture the overall degree of anthropogenic disturbance in the landscape. Elevation data were acquired using the ‘elevatr’ package [51, Fig. 1B] which was then processed into terrain roughness using the ‘terrain’ function of the ‘terra’ package [52]; Fig. 1C). Distance to contiguous mature forest patches was created by subsampling the ‘mature forest’ habitat type to 5 km2 resolution, then only retaining cells with > 75% ‘mature forest’ cover. The distance to the ‘mature forest’ cells was then determined using the ‘distance’ function in ‘terra’ (Fig. 1D). The resolution of all data layers is 10 m unless otherwise stated.

To characterise the habitat available for selection by each individual we created ‘habitat availability’ polygons representing the estimated 99% potential utilisation distribution (UD) using the area-corrected autocorrelated kernel density estimator (AKDc), following the workflow proposed for the ‘ctmm’ package (see [53]). In areas with hard boundaries, kernel density estimation methods may overestimate the UD, because they do not ignore these large areas of unused habitat [54], thus we constrained these polygons to only include terrestrial habitats (as open water was very rarely used; Supplementary Information S4) and to only include locations where we have verified location data (using a 5 km buffer around all available locations) as two individuals showed exploratory behaviours and therefore had unrealistically vast estimated habitat availability polygons (see Supplementary Information S5).

Statistical analyses

We first conducted a sensitivity analysis to determine the number of random “habitat availability” locations required for estimates of selection strength to stabilise and found that we need at least 50. Consequently, we conservatively used 100 random locations within each constrained ‘habitat availability’ polygon for each behaviour-specific location, and extracted the covariates detailed above. We first checked all covariates for collinearity and found all pairwise correlations between continuous predictor variables to be low ( < 0.45; Supplementary Information S6). Before model fitting, we standardised all continuous covariates to have a mean of zero and standard deviation of one using the ‘MuMIn’ package [55]. We also created dummy variables for each categorical habitat covariate to aid model fit.

i) Does king vulture habitat selection vary by behaviour?

To determine the relative effects of our covariates on habitat selection on each vulture species we used the glmmTMB package [56] to construct species-specific generalised linear models with a binomial error distribution and a logit-link function [35]. We included the continuous variables (see Table 1) as linear terms. To account for the non-independence of the data derived from the same individual and to assess the presence of inter-individual variation in habitat selection behaviours, we included a random intercept for individual identity and random slopes for habitats. To satisfy the assumptions of the inhomogeneous point process model, we assigned weights of 5000 to random locations and a weight of one to all used locations [57, 58]. We fit a separate habitat selection model for each of the three behaviours and compare the resultant coefficients. We used ‘mature forest’ as the intercept, thus all other categorical effects are represented as contrasts from this habitat type, with negative regression coefficients representing lower relative selection strength than ‘mature forest’ and positive representing stronger relative selection strength. To determine if there was support for individual variation in selection strength, we performed model selection based on Akaike Information Criterion corrected for small sample size (AICc) for the random slope model versus a null random intercept only model (Supplementary Information S7).

ii) Is there evidence for individual variation in king vulture habitat selection?

To examine if individual variation in the selection preferences for grassland margin habitats represented a subsidy or a cost to king vulture individuals, we estimated two indices of individual quality: scaled mass index (SMI) and home range. We defined SMI using the index described in [59] whereby we extracted the individual specific, residual deviation from a regression of individual body mass (kg) against tarsus length (mm). Considering the aforementioned limitations of kernel density methods (in our study area), we estimated individual home ranges (95% UD) for range-resident individuals using the dynamic Brownian bridge movement model (dBBMM) approach with the move R package [60]. Technically, the dBBMM estimates the occurrence distribution, and in this case it is well suited to our purpose, as it best estimates the space used by vultures within an area with hard boundaries (e.g., the Osa Peninsula; see Fig. 1). To estimate UDs, we use a window size of 31 locations and a margin of 11 locations (see details in [60]). We then correlated the individual-specific random intercept value of each individual (higher = stronger selection for grassland margins when feeding; lower = weaker) with each metric of individual quality using a linear model. Statistical support for individual selection strength being positively correlated with SMI and/or negatively correlated with home range size would suggest increased selection of grassland habitat representing a resource subsidy, the inverse would suggest a cost.

iii) How does king vulture habitat selection compare to habitat generalists?

To compare king vulture habitat selection to known generalist (disturbance winner) species, we applied the same models described in section i) to black and turkey vultures. Given the low sample sizes for black and turkey vultures (five of each), we did not fit habitat-specific random slope terms for these species, but we do include a random intercept to account for repeated measures from the same individuals. We then compare the selection strength coefficients for each species, taking lack of overlap in the 95% confidence intervals as indication of potential habitat selection differences between species.

Results

i) Does king vulture habitat selection vary by behaviour?

For each of the three behaviours assessed here (flying, roosting, feeding), mature forest was the preferred habitat (Fig. 2A). However, there was strong statistical support for habitat-specific selection patterns which varied by behaviour (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Information S8). When flying, the relative reduction in selection strength compared to mature forest was relatively modest for disturbed forest (~24% lower) and grassland margins (~21% lower), but larger for palm oil and ‘other’ habitats (~70 and 85% lower respectively). When roosting, there was stronger avoidance of all habitats relative to mature forest, with disturbed forest and grasslands being used 76 and 73% less respectively, and palm oil and other habitats even less so ( > 0.99% less). Finally, when feeding there was strong support for king vultures selecting mature forest over disturbed forest (57% less), palm oil (97% less) and other ( > 0.99% less) habitats, however there was no statistical support for a difference between mature forest and grassland margins (12% less). Of the continuous variables assessed, there was a positive effect of terrain roughness on habitat selection strength, and a negative effect of both elevation and distance to contiguous forest for all behaviours (Fig. 2B).

ii) Is there evidence for individual variation in king vulture habitat selection?

For all three behaviours examined, there was strong support for individual variation in habitat selection (statistical support for the inclusion of habitat-specific random slope terms under model selection; Supplementary Information S7). Accounting for individual variation in habitat selection strengths revealed that some individuals selected grassland margin habitat to feed more frequently than mature forest (maximum increase 250%), whereas some individuals selected it less (maximum decrease 50%; Fig. 3A). There was no statistical support for grassland selection strength being related to individual condition (p = 0.72; Fig. 3B), and there was weak (albeit non-significant) support for a positive relationship between home range size and grassland selection (p = 0.32; Fig. 3C).

iii) How does king vulture habitat selection compare relative to disturbance ‘winner’ species?

Interspecies comparisons in habitat selection reveal marked variation in habitat selection profiles for each behaviour: flight showed the least interspecies variation, whereas roosting and feeding patterns diverged substantially (Fig. 4). For flight behaviour, king and black vultures showed similar habitat selection profiles, with a weak preference for ‘mature forest’ over ‘disturbed forest’ and ‘grassland margins’, whereas turkey vultures showed stronger relative selection for ‘disturbed forest’ and ‘palm oil’ over ‘mature forest’ (Fig. 4A). For roosting (Fig. 4B), whereas the King vulture showed strong preference for ‘mature forest’, both black vultures and turkey vultures showed a stronger preference for ‘disturbed forest’ relative to ‘mature forest’, and black vultures also preferred ‘grassland margins’ and the ‘other’ habitat. These selection profiles were echoed in feeding behaviour, with black vultures favouring ‘disturbed forest’, ‘grassland margins’ and ‘other habitats’ over ‘mature forest’, and turkey vultures preferring ‘disturbed forest’ (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

King vulture habitat selection varied markedly both between the three different movement behaviours assessed and in comparison to two sympatric disturbance tolerant (‘winner’) vulture species. Whilst the overall preference for mature forest across all behaviours is consistent with king vultures as a disturbance sensitive specialist, their equal preference for feeding in grassland margins both challenges this perception and highlights the complexity of characterising human-wildlife interactions in mixed-use landscapes. Furthermore, the strong relative avoidance of anthropogenically disturbed habitats and decrease in habitat selection strength as distance from contiguous forest patches increases, highlights the potential costs of further expansion of monoculture in this landscape. Below, we discuss how these findings contribute to our general understanding of the interactions between wildlife and anthropogenically modified landscapes, and then specifically consider the implications of these findings for vulture ecosystem services provision and conservation in this region and beyond.

Winners and losers

The response of biodiversity to expansion of anthropogenic footprints have often been characterised as occurring on a “winners and losers” continuum [7, 9, 61], with large-bodied disturbance sensitive ‘specialists’ losing out to disturbance tolerant ‘generalist’ species. Given the perception of king vultures as a large-bodied species with a strong affinity for primary forest, it is perhaps not surprising that we find that the relative habitat selection strength for highly forested habitat is the greatest across all movement behaviours assessed. Consistent with this, comparison with two sympatric disturbance tolerant species revealed they had consistently stronger relative selection strengths for degraded habitats over mature forest across most of the behaviours assessed. These patterns suggest that black and turkey vultures may represent “winners” in response to increasing anthropogenic disturbance in this landscape. Black vultures in particular hit the criteria for ‘hyper abundant generalist’ designation [62] - they are edge adapted, with gregarious social structure, omnivorous diet, rapid reproduction and high tolerance to anthropogenic disturbance. Alarmingly, the process of habitat specialists being replaced by generalists has been documented to be occurring across multiple guilds throughout Mesoamerican protected areas [63], with potentially negative effects on ecosystem function [64]. Future research examining the interspecies interactions at carcass sites in different disturbance contexts (e.g. through carcass provisioning experiments) would provide deeper understanding of inter-scavenger dynamics, resource monopolization and their implications [65].

Crucially, the lack of statistical support for a difference in the relative selection strength between grassland margins and mature forest for king vulture feeding events complicates the simplified ‘winners’ versus ‘losers’ concept, and suggests that ranching and farming activities may be subsiding king vulture diets in this region. To confirm this, we visited 52 potential king vulture feeding clusters located in grassland margins and found they were dominated by domestic cattle and horse carcasses (representing 83% of identifiable animal remains; see Supplementary Information S9). Dietary replacement of native fauna by domestic species is perhaps not surprising given that it has been documented extensively in other large-bodied obligate scavengers, such as the Andean and California condor (e.g. [24, 66]). These anthropogenic foraging opportunities could replace or even potentially surpass the resource availability of equivalently sized contiguous patches of mature forests [66], and could be increasing the carrying capacity for king vultures in this landscape. It is important to note however, that there may be hidden costs to foraging on anthropogenic sources, such as exposure to antimicrobial pharmaceuticals or pesticides which may negatively impact individual health [67] or elevated mortality in areas with higher human disturbance such as collisions with infrastructure, electrocution or poisoning [68]. Thus, research is urgently required to determine the implications of domestic livestock availability on neotropical vulture species.

Individual variation

One powerful way to explore the potential benefits and/or costs associated with a resource is to find natural inter-individual variation in its use, then compare indices of individual state (e. [69]). We found no overt statistical support for variation in selection of grassland margin habitat acting as either a ‘subsidy’ or ‘ecological trap’ for king vultures in the two indices of individual state assessed here (scaled mass index and home range size). It is possible that the suite of individuals followed in this population are either all already adapted to living in mixed-use landscapes, or that the degree of variation in grassland margin use was not substantial enough to reveal the potential benefits or costs - particularly if the capture method used (cages baited with livestock) under represented individuals with different foraging tactics (e.g. through age structure [70] or social dominance [71]). It is also possible that the costs or benefits related to selection of anthropogenically derived food sources may only be apparent when contrasting populations living in disparate landscapes with marked different levels of anthropogenic disturbance. The costs could also be cryptic, whereby measures of chronic stress (e.g. corticosteroid hormone [72]) or other more sensitive indicators of individual condition may be more informative. For example, in Europe vultures feeding on medicated livestock carcasses showed a high prevalence of oral mucosal lesions [69], and that such lesions were more prevalent in populations which fed in areas with greater agricultural intensity [69]. To address the potential for vultures in this landscape to be exposed to antibiotics, we interviewed 28 farmers in the region to determine if medication of livestock with antibiotics was prevalent (see Supplementary Information S9 for survey details). Of these, 86% of farmers self-reported using at least one type of antibiotic, with six different types reported in total. This suggests that there is a high potential of exposure of vultures feeding in grassland margins to antibiotics. Furthermore, if livestock drive unnaturally large congregations of vultures at single carcasses, these could also represent hotspots for between individual disease transmission (e.g. [73–75]). Future work could focus on assessing the evidence of cryptic costs of feeding on anthropogenic sources and performing landscape comparisons between areas with marked differences in disturbance and landscape compositions.

Conservation and management implications

The resilience of landscapes which have undergone extensive human modification relies on species which can effectively move between patches of habitats of different disturbance histories (termed ‘mobile links’), and as such alterations in their movement behaviour can have cascading effects on ecosystem dynamics and function [76–79]. Within fragmented landscapes, spatiotemporal scale and matrix quality may have a direct or indirect effect on patch-dependent species occurrence and space use (e.g., by facilitating dispersal movements between patches or providing a temporary trophic subsidy) and, consequently, their understanding from a ecological and conservation perspective is urgently needed [80]. While we agree that flying fauna may buffer the loss of ecosystem services in fragmented landscapes [18], our results suggest that the quality of habitat patches may condition functional connectivity. Here we found that as the distance from larger contiguous blocks of good quality forest increases, the relative strength of king vulture habitat selection decreases. This suggests that whilst they can act as a mobile link at landscapes with low-intermediate disturbance levels, there is likely a disturbance anthropogenic threshold within this population after which king vultures are displaced from the landscape. Consistent with this, the lack of forest cover in western Panama also explains why the King vultures tracked in this study largely avoid forays into that region (Supplementary Information S10). Furthermore, despite previous accounts documenting king vultures feeding on palm nuts [81], their relative avoidance of palm plantation habitats was particularly marked across all behaviours assessed, highlighting that continued spread of large-scale monoculture activities may compromise their population size and stability. Finally, there are three further lines of evidence suggesting that the resident king vulture population may be isolated from other king vulture populations with their broader range. Firstly, none of the individuals tracked moved/dispersed beyond the wider Puntarenas region during the period assessed - although this may be due to the fact that we only tagged adult individuals. Second, the ground surface elevation reached by a king vulture in this study was 1650 m, suggesting the mountains within La Amistad to the north (maximum height 3800 m) may represent a major barrier to king vulture movements. Finally, the king vultures observed here very rarely fly over open water (0.2% of all locations and never > 2.1 km from the shore), suggesting large water bodies may also represent significant movement barriers for this species. Crucially, we have documented patterns of behaviour-specific habitat selection for three different vulture species, it remains to be seen if habitat-specific variation in carrion availability per se underpins the patterns observed. Future work directly quantifying the availability of carrion in different areas of different land uses [82] would facilitate empirically testing this explanation.

Conclusions

The vulture assemblage-level habitat selection analyses presented here reveal nuanced responses to anthropogenic disturbance that challenge simplified “winner-loser” frameworks. While King vultures demonstrated strongest selection for mature forest consistent with disturbance-sensitive specialists, they showed equal preference for feeding in grassland margins where domestic livestock carcasses likely provide significant dietary subsidies. Their marked avoidance of palm plantations and decreasing habitat selection strength with increasing distance from contiguous forest patches suggests they function as mobile links only below critical disturbance thresholds, even within a relatively low disturbance forest-agricultural matrix lacking major urban population centres. In contrast, sympatric black and turkey vultures exhibited stronger selection for degraded habitats, positioning them as clear “winners” from landscape modification. Although no overt costs of using agricultural subsidies were detected here, potential remains for cryptic fitness impacts arising from pharmaceutical exposure, infrastructure collisions, and disease transmission at livestock carcasses which warrant further investigation. These findings highlight how continued agricultural expansion could alter neotropical vulture assemblages and underscores the need for empirical quantification of landscape carrion availability to validate the habitat selection patterns observed and further inform conservation strategies in fragmented tropical landscapes.
