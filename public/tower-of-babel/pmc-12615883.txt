Epigenetic regulation of TIPE3 in nasopharyngeal carcinoma and its impact on the hedgehog signaling pathway
Authors: Zhongqiang Cheng, Zhijie Fang, Kaiyue Yue, Yanxun Guo, Lixian Huang, Yanshu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the epigenetic regulation of TIPE3 in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and its role in tumor progression, with a focus on enhancer elements and associated histone modifications, as well as the involvement of the Hedgehog signaling pathway.MethodsThe regulation of TIPE3 was explored through chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays to identify histone modifications, specifically H3K27ac and H3K4me3, associated with enhancer elements upstream of the TIPE3 promoter. CRISPR-Cas9 technology was employed to delete these enhancer regions in NPC cell lines. The effects on TIPE3 expression, cell proliferation, and metastasis were assessed both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, the roles of the histone-modifying enzymes KAT2A and SETD7 in modulating these enhancer marks were examined. The activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway by TIPE3 was also investigated.ResultsTIPE3 was found to be regulated by enhancer elements marked by H3K27ac and H3K4me3. Deletion of these enhancers resulted in significant downregulation of TIPE3, leading to reduced proliferation and metastasis of NPC cells. KAT2A and SETD7 were identified as critical regulators of TIPE3 expression, acting through these enhancer-associated histone modifications. Moreover, TIPE3 was shown to activate the Hedgehog signaling pathway, contributing to NPC progression.ConclusionThe study reveals that TIPE3 is epigenetically regulated by enhancer elements, which are modulated by KAT2A and SETD7, and that TIPE3 promotes NPC progression through activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway. These findings suggest that targeting the enhancer-mediated regulation of TIPE3 and the associated signaling pathways could offer new therapeutic strategies for NPC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05924-1.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, KAT2A, SETD7, TIPE3, Enhancer, Epigenetic

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor originating from the epithelium of the nasopharynx, with a particularly high prevalence in Southeast Asia and Southern China [1, 2]. Despite advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, NPC remains a significant clinical challenge due to its high propensity for local invasion, distant metastasis, and development of resistance to standard therapies such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy [3–5]. These challenges underscore the need for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving NPC progression, with a particular focus on the regulatory networks that control gene expression in this malignancy [6–8].

Epigenetics, the study of heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve alterations in the DNA sequence, plays a crucial role in the regulation of gene activity and cellular identity [9–11]. Key epigenetic mechanisms include DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin remodeling, all of which contribute to the dynamic regulation of gene expression in normal and diseased states [12]. In the context of cancer, epigenetic dysregulation is often a driving force behind oncogenesis, affecting genes involved in cell cycle control, apoptosis, DNA repair, and metastasis [12, 13]. Histone modifications are among the most well-studied epigenetic mechanisms [14]. These modifications, which include methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination of histone proteins, influence chromatin structure and accessibility, thereby regulating gene transcription [15]. Specific histone marks, such as H3K27ac and H3K4me3, are associated with active enhancer regions—genomic elements that enhance the transcription of target genes by facilitating the recruitment of transcriptional machinery and promoting chromatin looping to bring enhancers into close proximity with promoters [16, 17]. The dysregulation of these enhancer elements has been implicated in the aberrant activation of oncogenes across various cancers, including NPC [18].

TIPE3 (TNFAIP8L3) is a gene that has emerged as a potential player in cancer biology, particularly in its role in modulating cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis [19]. Previous studies have reported elevated TIPE3 expression in several types of cancer, including NPC, where its overexpression is correlated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcomes [20–23]. However, the precise mechanisms leading to the upregulation of TIPE3 in NPC remain poorly understood. Given the critical role of enhancers in gene regulation, it is plausible that epigenetic modifications at enhancer regions upstream of the TIPE3 promoter contribute to its dysregulation in NPC.

This study seeks to elucidate the epigenetic regulation of TIPE3 in NPC, with a focus on the role of enhancer elements marked by histone modifications. We employed a combination of ChIP-seq analysis, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated enhancer deletion, and gene expression assays to investigate how enhancer regions influence TIPE3 expression. Additionally, we examined the role of histone acetyltransferase KAT2A and histone methyltransferase SETD7 in modulating the histone marks associated with TIPE3, providing insight into the broader epigenetic landscape that governs its expression. These results highlight the importance of epigenetic mechanisms in NPC progression and suggest that targeting the enhancer-mediated regulation of TIPE3 could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for NPC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) cell lines HNE1, C666-1, and 5–8 F, as well as normal human nasal epithelial cells (HNEpC), were cultured under standard conditions. The cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Genomic and epigenomic analysis

The genomic location of TIPE3 and its associated cis-regulatory elements were analyzed using the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/). The data were visualized for histone modification marks including H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and H3K4me1 in regions upstream of the TIPE3 promoter. ChIP-seq data for H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K9me3, and H3K27ac modifications were obtained from the ENCODE database (https://www.encodeproject.org/). These data were specifically analyzed for the NPC cell line C666-1 and compared with normal nasal tissue to identify differential histone modifications near the TIPE3 promoter region.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and then quenched with 125 mM glycine. Chromatin was sheared using micrococcal nuclease, followed by sonication. The sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated using specific antibodies against H3K4me3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9751), H3K27ac (Abcam, #ab4729), and the corresponding control IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729). The precipitated DNA was purified using the ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, USA) and analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primers specific to the TIPE3 promoter region were used to assess enrichment. Data were normalized to input DNA and analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

CRISPR-Cas9 mediated enhancer deletion and stable overexpression

To interrogate the functional role of upstream regulatory elements of TIPE3, we designed CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNAs to delete three putative enhancer regions (designated A, B, and C) located within 5 kb upstream of the TIPE3 transcription start site (TSS; hg38 chr15:51,025,949). Specific sgRNA sequences, PAMs, target strands, and genomic coordinates are provided in Table 1. Each sgRNA was cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2 vector (Addgene #52961). Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells by co-transfection with packaging plasmids psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). NPC lines HNE1 and C666-1 were infected with lentivirus and selected with 1 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 7 days. Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen), and deletion events were confirmed by PCR and Sanger sequencing with primers flanking the targeted enhancer regions (Supplementary File 1).

SgRNAs sequences

For stable overexpression, full-length cDNAs of KAT2A and SETD7 were cloned into the pLenti-CMV-Puro vector (Addgene). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfecting expression vectors with packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000. TIPE3-KO cells were transduced and selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 5 days to establish stable cell lines. Western blotting confirmed sustained overexpression prior to colony formation assays.

TIPE3 knockdown cell line establishment

To investigate the functional role of TIPE3, we generated TIPE3-knockdown cell lines by transfecting shRNAs targeting TIPE3 into NPC lines HNE1 and C666-1. Briefly, specific shRNA oligonucleotides were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector and packaged into lentiviruses using HEK293T cells. Viral supernatants were collected and used to infect HNE1 and C666-1 cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 48 h, cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 5–7 days to establish stable knockdown lines. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR.

RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System. Primers specific to TIPE3, MIR4713HG, and housekeeping gene GAPDH were used. Relative gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. Western blot

Protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, USA). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (MilliporeSigma, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TIPE3 (Proteintech, #25501-1-AP), KI67 (Abcam, #ab15580), PCNA (Cell Signaling Technology, #13110), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, #5174). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074), the bands were visualized using the ECL Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from TIPE3-knockdown C666-1 and control cells using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina [NovaSeq 6000] platform to generate paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed and quality-checked using fastp and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Gene-level read counts were calculated with featureCounts, and differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes with an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >1 were considered differentially expressed. The significantly upregulated and downregulated genes were separately subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the enrichKEGG function from the clusterProfiler package, with the organism set to Homo sapiens (organism = “hsa”). The enrichment results were visualized using bar plots, in which pathways enriched among upregulated and downregulated genes were displayed in opposite directions for comparative interpretation.

Cell proliferation analysis

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded at a density of 2,000 cells/well in 96-well plates and treated as indicated. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

For colony formation assays, cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis and senescence

Apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, USA).

Cellular senescence was assessed by β-galactosidase staining using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were stained overnight at 37 °C and observed under a light microscope for the presence of blue-stained cells, indicative of senescence.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect cleaved caspase-3 (Cleaved-Cas3) and the nuclear translocation of GLI2 and GLI3. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS. Primary antibodies against Cleaved-Cas3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9664), GLI2 (Abcam, #ab26056), and GLI3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-515771) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope and analyzed with NIS-Elements software (Nikon, Japan).

In vivo tumorigenesis and metastasis assays

For in vivo tumorigenesis assays, 5 × 106 HNE1 or C666-1 cells with stable KAT2A or SETD7 overexpression or enhancer knockout were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Charles River Laboratories, USA). Tumor volume was measured every 5 days using a caliper, and tumors were harvested 28 days post-injection for further analysis. For metastasis assays, 2 × 106 HNE1 or C666-1 cells were injected intravenously via the tail vein into nude mice. After 28 days, mice were sacrificed, and liver and lung tissues were collected. The number of metastatic foci was counted under a microscope after hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of our hospital.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m). For cellular experiments, data were collected from a minimal of three independent experiments. For animal experiments, each group contained six mice. Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Bonferroni post hoc correction for multi-group comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

TIPE3 is an enhancer-regulated gene in NPC

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms driving the high expression of TIPE3 in NPC, we utilized the UCSC Genome Browser, which revealed that TIPE3 is located on chromosome 15 (chr15:51,056,626 − 51,107,073) and exhibits significant H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and H3K4me1 modifications approximately 5 kb upstream of its promoter (Fig. 1A). Given these findings, we hypothesized that the elevated expression of TIPE3 is associated with these “Enhancer signatures.” To test this hypothesis, we analyzed ChIP-seq data for H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K9me3, and H3K27ac modifications in the NPC cell line C666-1 and normal lung tissue, sourced from the ENCODE database. Our analysis demonstrated that H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications near the TIPE3 promoter were significantly higher in C666-1 cells compared to HNEpC cells (nasal epithelial cells) (Fig. 1B). We further validated these findings through ChIP-qPCR in HNEpC, and NPC cell lines HNE1, C666-1, and 5–8 F cells. The results showed a pronounced increase in H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications near the TIPE3 promoter in the NPC cell lines relative to HNEpC cells (Fig. 1C).

To directly assess whether TIPE3 is an enhancer-regulated gene, we employed CRISPR-Cas9 technology to segment the upstream 15 kb region of the TIPE3 promoter into three distinct regions (A, B, and C), each 5 kb in length. Using specific sgRNAs, we constructed enhancer-deleted HNE1 and C666-1 cell lines (Fig. 1D). Deletion of enhancer segments A and C leads to significant downregulation of TIPE3 expression, with segment C showing the greatest reduction at both mRNA and protein levels, indicating its critical role in TIPE3 transcriptional regulation (Fig. 1E-F). Additionally, we found that the expression level of MIR4713HG, another gene located nearby, was unaffected by the deletion of the enhancer (Fig. 1G). These results strongly suggest that TIPE3 is indeed regulated by an enhancer element.

Inhibition of NPC cell growth and metastasis following enhancer deletion

To further elucidate the role of the enhancer in NPC cell growth and metastasis, we conducted cell viability assays (CCK-8) and colony formation assays on HNE1 and C666-1 cells with gRNA C-targeted enhancer deletion (C-KO, briefly designated as enhancer-KO). The results revealed a significant suppression of cell viability in enhancer-KO cells (Fig. 2A-B). Flow cytometry analysis showed an increase in both early (PI−Annexin V+) and late (PI+Annexin V+) apoptotic cells in enhancer-KO lines (Fig. 2C), accompanied by an elevated proportion of senescent cells (Fig. 2D). Additionally, cleaved caspase-3 (Cleaved-Cas3) fluorescence intensity was markedly increased in the enhancer-KO cells (Fig. 2E). Further investigation of cell migration and invasion using scratch assays and Transwell assays indicated that the migratory ability of enhancer-KO cells at 24 h and the number of cells invading the basolateral chambers were significantly reduced (Fig. 2F-G). Moreover, we observed an increase in the epithelial markers ZO-1 and CDH1, alongside a decrease in the mesenchymal markers VIM1 and Snai1 in enhancer-KO cells, indicating a reversal of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Fig. 2H). Additionally, to confirm the oncogenic role of TIPE3 in NPC, shRNAs targeting TIPE3 were administered to the two NPC cell lines. The successful gene knockdown was confirmed by qPCR analysis (Fig S1A). Importantly, CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that the proliferation and colony formation capacities of cells were substantially reduced following TIPE3 knockdown (Fig S1B-C). Transwell assays confirmed the reduction in cell migration and invasion abilities (Fig S1D-E). The EMT in cells was reduced by TIPE3 knockdown as well, as marked by reduced expression of Snail 1 and VIM1 whereas increased levels of ZO-1 and CDH1 (Fig S1F).

In vivo studies involved subcutaneous injection of HNE1 or C666-1 cells into nude mice to generate xenograft tumors. The growth rate of the enhancer-KO tumors was significantly reduced (Fig. 3A-C), with diminished staining intensities for TIPE3, KI67, and PCNA in the tumor tissues (Fig. 3D-F). Furthermore, metastasis studies using tail vein injection of HNE1 or C666-1 cells into nude mice showed a significant reduction in metastatic foci in the lung tissues in enhancer-KO mice (Fig. 3G-H). Immunohistochemical analysis of lung metastatic foci revealed decreased staining intensity for VIM1 and Snai1 in enhancer-KO cells (Fig. 3I-J). These results demonstrate that TIPE3 is an enhancer-regulated gene, and knockout of the enhancer reduces TIPE3 expression, thereby inhibiting NPC cell growth and metastasis.

KAT2A and SETD7 positively regulate TIPE3 expression

Previous findings have established that TIPE3 is regulated by H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications. To identify the specific histone modification enzymes involved, we treated HNE1 and C666-1 cells with various histone acetyltransferase and methyltransferase inhibitors. Among them, the KAT2A-specific inhibitor Butyrolactone 3, combined with SETD7 treatment, resulted in the most significant reduction in TIPE3 expression levels (Fig. 4A-B). To confirm the involvement of KAT2A and SETD7 in TIPE3 regulation, we performed ChIP-qPCR, which revealed enrichment of TIPE3 enhancer fragments in complexes precipitated with anti-KAT2A or anti-SETD7 antibodies (Fig. 4C). Overexpression of KAT2A or SETD7 in C666-1 and HNE1 cells significantly upregulated TIPE3 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4D-E). To further substantiate the regulatory axis involving KAT2A, SETD7, and TIPE3, we designed and synthesized a pGL3 luciferase vector containing the TIPE3 promoter (Fig. 4F). Co-transfection of this vector with KAT2A or SETD7 into HEK293T cells led to a significant increase in luciferase activity (Fig. 4G). Analysis of the TCGA-HNSC database confirmed a strong positive correlation between the expression levels of KAT2A and SETD7 with TIPE3 (Fig. 4H). Additionally, transfection of HNE1 or C666-1 cells with increasing doses of KAT2A or SETD7 overexpression vectors resulted in a dose-dependent increase in TIPE3 expression (Fig. 4I). These findings suggest that TIPE3 is positively regulated by KAT2A and SETD7 through histone modification.

KAT2A and SETD7 overexpression enhances NPC cell growth in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether KAT2A and SETD7 modulate NPC cell malignancy through TIPE3 regulation, we overexpressed KAT2A and SETD7 in HNE1 and C666-1 cells with stable TIPE3 knockdown. The overexpression restored TIPE3 levels (Fig. 5A-B) and promoted cell proliferation (Fig. 5C-D), while reducing apoptosis and senescence (Fig. 5E-G). Furthermore, scratch and Transwell assays showed that KAT2A and SETD7 overexpression enhanced the migratory and invasive capabilities of NPC cells (Fig. 5H-I). Collectively, these results indicate that KAT2A and SETD7 regulate TIPE3 expression via H3K27ac and H3K4me3 modifications, promoting NPC progression.

To elucidate the downstream signaling pathways regulated by TIPE3, we analyzed differentially expressed genes between C666-1 cells with stable TIPE3 knockdown or not. The subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the downregulated genes were predominately enriched in Neurodegenerative disease, Energy metabolism, and Hedgehog signaling pathways (Fig. 7A-C). Additionally, we identified genes with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.3 with TIPE3 using the UALCAN database. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using DAVID 6.8 revealed significant enrichment in the Hedgehog, PI3K/AKT, and Ras signaling pathways (Fig. 7D). The intersection analysis of the two sets of genes led to the identification of genes enriched in the Hedgehog signaling pathway (Fig. 7E). Subsequent qPCR analysis of Hedgehog pathway markers PTCH1, GLI2, and SMO in HNE1 and C666-1 cells showed that these markers were significantly downregulated following TIPE3 knockdown, but their expression was markedly restored by overexpression of KAT2A or SETD7 (Fig. 7F). Discussion

Epigenetic dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of NPC oncogenesis, with aberrant DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling contributing to the silencing of tumor suppressor genes and the activation of oncogenes [24, 25]. Previous studies have highlighted the role of epigenetic changes in NPC, often involving the silencing of key tumor suppressor genes through promoter hypermethylation [26–28]. Enhancers marked by H3K27ac and H3K4me3 are known to drive the overexpression of oncogenes such as MYC in breast cancer and BCL2 in colorectal cancer [29, 30]. In this study, the role of enhancers in these contexts parallels the observations in NPC, where TIPE3, a gene associated with tumor progression, is similarly regulated by enhancer elements. The significant reduction in TIPE3 expression following enhancer deletion is in line with findings in other cancers, where targeting enhancers has effectively downregulated oncogene expression and inhibited tumor growth. This underscores the pivotal role of enhancer elements in sustaining the aberrant gene expression profiles that fuel cancer progression.

TIPE3 has been implicated in several other cancers, where its overexpression is linked to enhanced cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), for instance, TIPE3 has been shown to promote tumor progression by facilitating lipid metabolism and oncogenic signaling pathways [31, 32]. Similarly, in lung cancer, TIPE3 overexpression has been associated with resistance to apoptosis and increased invasive potential [20, 33, 34]. The current study extends these findings to NPC, suggesting a conserved role for TIPE3 in promoting aggressive tumor behavior across different cancer types. While the regulation of TIPE3 in other cancers has predominantly been attributed to genetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms, the present research emphasizes the importance of epigenetic regulation, particularly through enhancer elements, in driving TIPE3 overexpression in NPC. Besides, TIPE3, through enhancer-mediated mechanisms, highlighting the dual role of epigenetics in NPC—both in gene silencing and in the aberrant activation of oncogenes. The identification of KAT2A and SETD7 as regulators of these enhancer elements further emphasizes the complexity of epigenetic regulation in NPC and suggests that targeting these enzymes could disrupt the epigenetic landscape that supports tumor growth. In the context of NPC, the activation of the Hedgehog pathway by TIPE3 provides a mechanistic link between TIPE3 overexpression and the aggressive behavior observed in NPC cells. This finding aligns with other studies that have identified the Hedgehog pathway as a key driver of cancer progression [35–37].

KAT2A and SETD7, the histone-modifying enzymes identified as regulators of TIPE3, have been implicated in the regulation of oncogenes in various cancers. KAT2A, a histone acetyltransferase, acetylates H3K27ac, promoting the activation of enhancers and subsequent transcription of target genes in cancers such as leukemia and prostate cancer [38–40]. Similarly, SETD7, a histone methyltransferase, is responsible for methylating H3K4me3, a mark associated with active transcriptional initiation [41–43]. This study is among the first to explore the roles of KAT2A and SETD7 in NPC, demonstrating that these enzymes are crucial for maintaining TIPE3 expression through their modulation of enhancer-associated histone marks. Importantly, we observed that overexpression of either KAT2A or SETD7 partially rescued TIPE3 mRNA expression in C666-1 and HNE1 cell lines stably administered the TIPE3 shRNA, which consequently restored malignant properties of these cells. It is well known that shRNA silences gene expression post-transcriptionally by guiding the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to degrade target mRNA or inhibit translation, increasing mRNA degradation rates without directly affecting transcription [44]. When transcription rates exceed degradation rates, RNA concentrations can increase [45]. Moreover, while higher transcript abundance can enhance RNAi relative knockdown efficiency, the absolute residual mRNA increases due to elevated baseline production [46]. Additionally, RNA silencing efficiency also depends on the siRNA-to-target mRNA ratio; high transcription rates can saturate RISC complexes, leading to incomplete silencing and higher residual expression [47]. Our data demonstrate that KAT2A/SETD7-driven enhancer activation partially counteracts shRNA-mediated silencing, restoring TIPE3 expression and its tumor-promoting functions to a limited extent. These observations also suggest that KAT2A and SETD7 may function similarly in NPC as in other cancers, contributing to tumor progression by enhancing the transcriptional activation of oncogenes.

The findings provide a comprehensive analysis of the epigenetic regulation of TIPE3 in NPC, offering insights into the broader mechanisms of NPC oncogenesis. By elucidating the role of enhancer elements and histone modifications in driving TIPE3 overexpression, potential therapeutic targets in KAT2A and SETD7 have been identified. Future research should explore the therapeutic potential of targeting these enzymes, as well as investigate the role of other epigenetic regulators in NPC. Additionally, given the emerging importance of enhancers in cancer biology, further studies should aim to map the enhancer landscape in NPC to identify additional regulatory elements that may contribute to the disease. Nevertheless, there are limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the raw PCR and Sanger sequencing data for enhancer deletion in cells are no longer available, as the sequencing runs were conducted some time ago, and unfortunately, the data were not preserved in a retrievable format. limitation could raise concerns regarding the specificity and completeness of the enhancer deletions. Additionally, the experimental design of performing KAT2A/SETD7 overexpression in NPC cells with TIPE3 shRNA-mediated silencing may appear counterintuitive. An alternative approach, such as applying sh-TIPE3 in KAT2A/SETD7-overexpressing cells, could have further clarified the dynamics. However, financial constraints prevented us from doing so. We would like to address these gaps in future investigation endeavors and provide more insightful understanding. Supplementary file Fig S1.
