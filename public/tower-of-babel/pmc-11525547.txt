Association of METS-IR index with psoriasis in US adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hongwei Liu, Changxing Liu, Tianyi Wang, Dianwei Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is linked to insulin resistance (IR). Nevertheless, the applicability of the METS-IR index, a new IR evaluation tool, for evaluating changes in insulin sensitivity in psoriasis populations is currently unknown. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the METS-IR index and psoriasis in a US adult population. This cross-sectional study utilized data from adults aged 20 to 80 years from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2003–2006 and 2009–2014. The associations between the METS-IR index and psoriasis were examined using multivariate logistic regression and smoothed curve fitting. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to verify the stability of the association within the population. This study included 5,966 participants, of whom 182 had psoriasis. In the fully adjusted model, the METS-IR index was positively associated with psoriasis, showing a 1.7% increase in psoriasis prevalence for each one-unit increase in the METS-IR index (Model 2: OR 1.017, 95% CI 1.006–1.028). Participants in the highest quartile group were 91.9% more likely to develop psoriasis compared to those in the lowest quartile group (OR = 1.919, 95% CI 1.180–3.118). Smooth curve fitting revealed a nonlinear association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis, with an inflection point of 41.675. This positive association was more pronounced in females, non-obese individuals, those with light alcohol consumption, comorbid coronary heart disease and hyperlipidemia, non-hypertensive and non-diabetic individuals. The results of the study suggest that higher METS-IR scores are associated with an increased likelihood of psoriasis among U.S. adults. The METS-IR index is specifically recommended as a clinical indicator for the management and treatment of psoriasis in women, non-obese individuals, light alcohol consumers, individuals with comorbid coronary artery disease andhyperlipidemia, non-hypertensive and non-diabetic individuals. However, Considering the many known and unknown covariates that may be associated with psoriasis and influence theresults of the study, we remain cautious about the results obtained and look forward to the addition of subsequent studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77784-x.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a non-infectious severe disease influenced by genetic, environmental, and immunologic factors, characterized by erythematous, papular, and scaly lesions1. Psoriasis affects approximately 2–3% of the global population2 and between 0.91% and 8.5% of adults3. Notably, skin damage is not the only characteristic of psoriasis. Statistics indicate that the prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) in psoriasis patients is between 20% and 50%, with the risk of MetS being at least twice as high as in the non-psoriasis population4. Additionally, MetS-related comorbidities increase the burden on patients, jeopardizing their quality of life and posing a significant public health threat that should not be overlooked in clinical practice.

Insulin resistance (IR) is widely recognized as the primary cause of MetS pathogenesis. IR alters the catabolic and secretory activities of adipose tissue, affecting glucose uptake and triggering changes in blood glucose and lipid levels5. Additionally, IR has been found to alter the differentiation and proliferation of keratinocytes, thereby disrupting epidermal homeostasis and potentially promoting the onset and progression of psoriasis6. It isnoteworthy that abnormally secreted inflammatory factors in psoriasis are involved in mechanisms that interfere with insulin signaling, which may induce or exacerbate IR in the body7. This complex interaction may cause psoriasis and insulin resistance (IR) to mutuallyexacerbate or worsening the overall condition. Therefore, Monitoring fluctuations in IR is essential for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of psoriasis.

However, clinical monitoring of insulin resistance (IR) is challenging, and there has long been a demand for a convenient and reliable IR index among clinicians.Compared with the gold and silver standards of IR assessment, like the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) and the frequently sampled intravenous glucose tolerance test (FSIVGTT), the METS-IR index is accessible to individuals with basic clinical skills, without incurring significant time or financial costs. Additionally, operators do not need proficiency in specialized software such as MINMOD to process the data8,9. Compared with other indices for assessing insulin resistance, the METS-IR Index does not rely on insulin measurements and offers better assay stability than the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) and the Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI)10. The study found that the METS-IR index exhibits a stronger correlation with gold standard measures, showing higher diagnostic accuracy and reduced variability in assessing metabolic risk factors11. Related research suggests that the METS-IR index effectively detects insulin resistance levels associated with psoriasis risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and coronary heart disease12–15. Critically, the METS-IR index accurately reflects visceral obesity levels, enabling it to predict metabolic risk in high-risk populations11. Given the complex relationship between psoriasis and insulin resistance, the METS-IR index could serve as a crucial tool for early screening and prevention of psoriasis.

Until now, the association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis remains unknown. This study will be the first to investigate the association between the METS-IR index and the prevalence of psoriasis in US adults, aiding in the prevention and management of psoriasis.

Methods

Study population

NHANES collects data from an extensive national survey of the general U.S. population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to provide a comprehensive assessment of citizens’ health status, including demographic, health, economic, and other types of data16. All NHANES subjects signed informed consent forms and were approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. Therefore, no further ethics committee review was required. The entire dataset is publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/. This study utilized the NHANES database and employed a cross-sectional study approach to analyze data from five cycles (2003–2004, 2005–2006, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, and 2013–2014), including a total of 50,938 participants. The exclusion criteria were: (1) participants younger than 20 years, (2) pregnant women, (3) participants with missing data on fasting glucose, HDL, triglycerides, and body mass index, (4) participants with missing data on psoriasis diagnosis, and (5) participants with missing data on other covariates such as education level, marital status, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Ultimately, 5,966 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Participant screening flowchart.

Assessment of psoriasis

The diagnosis of psoriasis was determined using self-report questionnaires, including either a dermatologic or a medical condition questionnaire. Participants diagnosed psoriasis by answering the following questions: (1) Have you ever been told by a healthcare provider that you had psoriasis (sore-eye-asis)? (2) Have you ever been told by a doctor or other healthcare professional that you had psoriasis (sore-eye-asis)? Participants gave a yes or no answer to determine their psoriasis diagnosis. Participants who refused to answer or did not know were excluded from the study17.

Assessment of METS-IR

In this study, the METS-IR index was used as the exposure variable, calculated as follows: METS-IR = Ln [(2 × fasting glucose) + fasting triglycerides] × body mass index / [Ln (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol)]. Fasting glucose and triglyceride levels were measured after an 8.5-hour fasting period using an automated biochemical analyzer. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing an individual’s weight (kg) by the square of their height (m).

Assessment of covariates

To understand the effect of confounding factors in multivariate adjustment models on the relationship between the METS-IR index and psoriasis, we considered the following covariates based on existing literature and clinical knowledge. The variables included age (years), gender (male/female), and race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other race). Family PIR was defined as low (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium (PIR > 1.3–3.5), and high (PIR ≥ 3.5) based on U.S. government reports (WWEIA, 1953). Obesity was defined by body mass index (BMI), with BMI < 30 as non-obese and BMI ≥ 30 as obese. Education level was categorized as less than high school, high school or equivalent, and college or above. Marital status was classified as cohabitation or solitude. Alcohol intake was categorized as light (< 1 drink per week), moderate (1–3 drinks per week), and heavy (≥ 4 drinks per week). Smoking status was defined as never smokers (smoked < 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), current smokers (smoked > 100 cigarettes and still smoking), and ex-smokers (smoked > 100 cigarettes but quit). Coronary heart disease is defined by a physician’s diagnosis of coronary heart disease, angina, or a heart attack. High blood pressure was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) diagnosed by a doctor or health professional, (2) use of antihypertensive medication, (3) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on four measurements. Diabetes mellitus was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: glycosylated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), self-reported physician diagnosis, or self-reported use of insulin18. Stroke and cancer were defined by a physician’s diagnosis of these conditions. Any of the following criteria defined Hyperlipidemia: (1) triglycerides (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dL, (2) serum total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 200 mg/dL, low-density lipoproteins (LDL) ≥ 130 mg/dL, and high-density lipoproteins (HDL) < 40 mg/dL (males) or < 50 mg/dL (females), or (3) taking lipid-lowering drugs.

Statistical analysis

Categorical characteristics were analyzed using chi-square tests and expressed as proportions (n) and percentages (%). Continuous characteristics were analyzed using t-tests and expressed as mean and standard error (SE). Multiple logistic regression models were used to assess the association between the METS-IR index, its quartiles, and psoriasis, expressed as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Three multivariate models were constructed to understand the effect of confounding variables. The crude model was unadjusted. Model 1 adjusted for gender, age, and race. Model 2 adjusted for household PIR, BMI, education level, marital status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and medical history (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, cancer, and hyperlipidemia) based on Model 1. A generalized additive model was used for smooth curve fitting to test linear and nonlinear associations between the METS-IR index and psoriasis. For nonlinear associations, a log-likelihood ratio test determined the threshold effect between the METS-IR index and psoriasis. We also analyzed associations by sex (male or female), age (< 40, 40–60, or ≥ 60), BMI (< 30 or ≥ 30), alcohol intake (light, moderate, and heavy), smoking status (never smoker, current smoker, ex-smoker), coronary heart disease (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), diabetes mellitus (yes or no), and hyperlipidemia (yes or no) for stratification and interaction. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.1) or Empowerstats (version 4.1). Two-sided tests were used, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Related statement

The research involving human participants underwent a thorough review and received approval from the Research Ethics Review Board of the NCHS. All patients or participants gave their written informed consent to be part of this study. We confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the essential characteristics of the participants. Among the 5966 individuals, 3166 (53.07%) were male, and 2800 (46.93%) were female, with a mean age of 48.49 ± 16.28 years. A total of 182 participants (about 3%) had psoriasis, consistent with previous epidemiological findings. The psoriasis group had a higher proportion of non-Hispanic whites compared to the non-psoriasis group. The psoriasis group also had a higher BMI, a more significant proportion of past smokers, and was more likely to have comorbid coronary heart disease, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. The METS-IR index was 46.2 ± 13.4 in the psoriasis group, higher than in the non-psoriasis group at 43.5 ± 12.9.

Relationship between METS-IR index and psoriasis

To better discuss the relationship between the METS-IR index and the prevalence of psoriasis, we analyzed three multivariate logistic regression models. We consistently found a significant positive correlation between the METS-IR index and psoriasis, regardless of model adjustments (Crude model: OR = 1.015, 95% CI 1.004–1.025; Model 1: OR = 1.016, 95% CI 1.006–1.026; Model 2: OR = 1.017, 95% CI 1.006–1.028). In the fully adjusted model, each unit increase in the METS-IR index was associated with a 1.7% increase in the risk of psoriasis. Further analysis of the METS-IR index by quartiles showed that this relationship persisted. In the fully adjusted model, the quartile 4 group had a 91.9% increase in the prevalence of psoriasis compared to the lowest quartile group (OR = 1.919, 95% CI 1.180–3.118), as presented in Table 2.

Subgroup analysis

In order to evaluate the influence of potential confounding factors on the relationship between the METS-IR index and psoriasis, we performed subgroup analyses and interaction tests by dividing participants into 9 subgroups based on sex, age, BMI, alcohol intake, smoking, coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. We found a positive association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis in females, non-obese individuals (BMI < 30 kg/m2), those with mild alcohol consumption, and those with combined coronary heart disease and hyperlipidemia, as well as in non-hypertensive and non-diabetic individuals (Fig. 3). The interaction tests revealed no statistically significant interactions in any of the groupings (all p-values for interaction were > 0.05).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study of 5,966 participants demonstrated a nonlinear association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis among U.S. adults. Individuals exhibiting high METS-IR levels had a greater likelihood of developing psoriasis. This correlation remained stable across subgroups based on sex, age, BMI, alcohol intake, smoking, coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. However, it may be more applicable to females, non-obese individuals, light alcohol consumers, those with comorbid coronary heart disease and hyperlipidemia, and non-hypertensive and non-diabetic individuals.

Due to hormonal changes in the menstrual cycle, women are more sensitive to insulin. Previous studies suggest that postmenopausal women lose their protective effect against IR due to estrogen deficiency19. However, some studies found that progesterone levels, rather than endogenous estrogens and androgens, influence insulin sensitivity in premenopausal women. Insulin sensitivity significantly reduces in the luteal phase20,21, with progesterone affecting insulin signaling in adipocytes22. Additionally, psoriasis has a slightly higher prevalence in females than in males23. The METS-IR index significantly correlates with the distribution of intra-visceral, intra-hepatic, and intra-pancreatic fats, examined using MRI or CT scans, reflecting visceral obesity levels11. Since BMI does not reflect body fat distribution, visceral obesity indicates the proper level of obesity. VAT levels are closely related to insulin resistance and diabetes. Obese individuals with low VAT often exhibit metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), while non-obese groups with high VAT tend to exhibit metabolic abnormalities and are at high risk for MetS24,25. Observational studies have shown a positive association between the METS-IR index and disease risk in non-T2D and non-hypertensive populations. The highest quartile group had increased OR and HR values of 0.95 and 2.91 compared to the lowest quartile group [26 − 11]. T2D and hypertension are risk factors for psoriasis. Glucose-lowering and certain antihypertensive medications may affect insulin sensitivity, making the association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis in these populations not significant27. Patients with coronary artery disease and hyperlipidemia have lipid metabolism disorders. Excess-free fatty acids’ toxicity toβ-cells induces hyperinsulinemia and IR. In these subgroups, the METS-IR index showed a significant positive correlation with psoriasis28. Consuming alcohol hinders the insulinsignaling system in the liver, resulting in disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism29. In the present study, this trend was more significant in light drinkers. The presence ofconfounding variables may have affected the relationship between the METS-IR index andpsoriasis in moderate to heavy drinkers.

Studies on the association between psoriasis and insulin resistance (IR) have a long history. An observational study by Pereira et al. found a significant correlation between psoriasis and IR, with a ratio of abnormal glucose homeostasis in psoriasis patients of 2.6330. A similar finding was reported by Gyldenløve et al.31. Another study observed that the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) was significantly associated with insulin secretion, serum resistin, and HOMA-IR levels32. The risk of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease is higher in psoriasis patients compared to non-psoriasis patients33. Clinically, biologics may improve metabolic levels in psoriasis patients. For instance, anti-IL-17 antibodies have been shown to raise HDL and lower LDL levels in patients34. Hagino et al. found that TNF-α inhibitors progressively corrected hyperuricemia and dyslipidemia in psoriasis patients35. Additionally, their study on serum biomarkers in response to TNF-α inhibitor therapy in psoriasis patients reinforced the association between psoriasis and IR. They demonstrated that TNF-α inhibitors effectively reduced serum neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP), all of which are positive indicators of IR36,37. Furthermore, diabetes medications like pioglitazone and rosiglitazone have been effective in slowing psoriasis progression, which side-steps this association38.

The exact mechanism of the association between IR and psoriasis remains incompletely known. However, further research has provided several explanations for this relationship. First, the sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines plays a vital role in this relationship. Physiologically, insulin attaches to its receptor and stimulates the addition of phosphate groups to tyrosine residues on the insulin receptor substrate (IRS), facilitating glucose uptake and utilization by cells39. In contrast, the chronic inflammatory environment of psoriasis disrupts the insulin signaling pathway. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-ɣ, and IL-1β induce serine phosphorylation of IRS through activation of JNK or p38MAPK, causing circulating and keratinocyte IR, leading to epidermal overproliferation and angiogenesis within plaques39–41. Additionally, when insulin resistance is present, adipokine secretion is dysregulated42,43. Elevated production of pro-inflammatory substances such as leptin, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 drives psoriatic inflammatory stress centered on interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A), increasing the risk of psoriasis44–47. Secondly, IR may contribute to abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism in psoriasis. IR promotes lipolysis and gluconeogenesis, elevates circulating glucose, cholesterol, and triglyceride (TG) levels, increases HDL clearance, and promotes subcutaneous and visceral fat accumulation48–51. These factors are associated with the development of psoriasis and MetS. Finally, shared alleles and loci may underlie the association between psoriasis and IR. For example, CDKAL1 gene expression affects both psoriasis and type 2 diabetes mellitus52, Genetic variations in the IL-12B, IL-23 A, and IL-23R genes have been linked to a higher susceptibility to psoriasis and diabetes mellitus, and coronary heart disease53,54. In summary, the METS-IR index encompasses the underlying risk factors for psoriasis and MetS, reflecting the potential association between the METS-IR index and psoriasis.

This study has several advantages. First, the enormous sample size of NHANES enabled us to thoroughly evaluate the correlation between the METS-IR index and psoriasis on a national level, enhancing the dependability of the results through suitable covariate modification. Second, the large sample size enabled us to stratify the study population, further validating the stability of the results. This stratification also allowed us to personalize and fine-tune the management of psoriasis patients in a more detailed manner.

Nevertheless, our study is subject to certain constraints. First, cross-sectional studies cannot clarify the causal relationship between insulin resistance and psoriasis. Additionally, the self-reported questionnaire for psoriasis diagnosis may have potential recall bias. Second, despite adjusting for many covariates, some confounders with a significant impact on outcomes may not have been included. Finally, NHANES did not specify the type or name of prescription medications used by participants. This lack of detail may have led to interference with exposure variables and affected outcomes in specific subgroups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals a significant correlation between the METS-IR index and psoriasis, suggesting it as a valuable tool for diagnosing psoriasis. We suggest that extra attention be paid to METS-IR scores in females, non-obese individuals, those with mild alcohol consumption, comorbid coronary heart disease and hyperlipidemia, non-hypertensive, and non-diabetics individuals for early diagnosis and prevention.However, it must be acknowledged that psoriasis is a multifactorial disease, with numerous known and unknowncovariates beyond the scope of our analysis that may influence the observed results. Therefore, while our findings provide valuable insights, they should be interpreted with caution.
