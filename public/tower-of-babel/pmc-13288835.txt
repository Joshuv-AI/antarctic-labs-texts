Personal, cultural and socio-economic determinants of childbearing reluctance in small families: a field-based study from Iran
Authors: Behnam Honarvar, Mohsen Bayati, Maliheh Alimondegari, Ahmad Kalateh Sadati, Matin Bidares, Fatemeh Rafiee, Mohammad Jafar Pooreisa, Hamid Maghami, Fatemeh Jamali, Fatemeh Bita, Kamran Bagheri Lankarani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecreasing population growth (PG) and aging have been appeared as major national problems in Iran. In this study, reluctance to childbearing and determinants, as an index of PG was investigated to guide policymakers for setting appropriate strategies and interventions. This research addresses childbearing reluctance and its personal, cultural and socio-economic determinants among small families, which represent 45–50% of Iranian households.MethodsSubjects who did not have any children or had only one child been selected through a cross-sectional study using systematic random sampling from the list of marriage public counselling centers of Fars province, Iran in 2024. They filled out a valid online questionnaire that included demographic characteristics, personal (22 items), socio-cultural (10 items), and economic (6 items) factors. Univariable and binary logistic regression were performed using SPSS software Version 25.Results280 subjects from 29 counties of Fars province included 137(48.9%) women and 143(51.1%) men with mean of age 35 ± 5.7-year-old participated in this survey. 145(51.8%) did not have any child, while 135(48.2%) had only one child. Only 98(35%) had a high tendency to have more than one child. Regarding commonest reasons, among personal factors, 80.4% believed that future and children’s quality of life is more important than their numbers. Among socio-cultural factors, 71.1% and of economic factors, 76.4% believed that they did not have any confidence or hope for the improvement of these situations in the country. Univariable analysis showed that, reluctance to childbearing was related to living place, personal, socio-cultural and economic factors (p < 0.05). Multivariable analysis showed that co-agreement with spouse about childbearing (OR:4.1), socio-cultural factors (OR:1.7), personal factors (OR:1.4) and economic factors (OR:1.2) were the main determinants of reluctance to childbearing, respectively.ConclusionsTwo-thirds of the marrieds do not have a high desire for childbearing, and several factors were revealed to be related to this issue. Therefore, strategies to cover both partners, improving socioeconomic conditions and establishment of counselling centers to address personal and cultural challenges for expanding family sizes and increasing fertility rate are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09207-w.

Introduction

Global statistics indicate a rise in the newly emerging phenomenon of childlessness among couples, which is now among the main challenges of modern societies [1]. World population annually growing rate peak was in the mid-1960s, when it was around 2% then it has been declined continuously and reached to the 0.87% in 2024 [2]. Assuming no net migration and unchanged mortality, a total fertility rate (TFR) of 2.1 children per woman ensures a broadly stable population. Together with mortality and migration, fertility is an element of population growth, reflecting both the causes and effects of economic and social developments. The reasons for the dramatic decline in birth rates during the past few decades include postponed family formation and child-bearing and a decrease in desired family sizes [3–6].TFR in Iran has been declined sharply from 6.9 in 1984 to 1.7 in 2022, below the worldwide TFR of 2.1 necessary for population replacement [7].This trend, combined with increasing life expectancy, signals an aging population that is expected to rise from under 10% in 2015 to over 30% by 2050 [8].

In term of reasons for childlessness, higher access to contraceptive methods, involvement of women in the labor markets, increasing levels of education, globalization and digitalization, increasing cohabitation, increasing individualization, economic uncertainty and instability, fertility postponement, lack of partner and biological reasons were reported [9]. However, in addition to the factors mentioned above, theories like the second demographic transition, preference theory, rational choice theory, uncertainty theory, and gender equity should also be considered as possible reasons for childlessness [9]. According to a grounded theory-based study, the causal conditions of childlessness included the high-risk society and adverse experiences. Contextual and intervening conditions included social problems, social support, transitional society, natural resources, social presence of women, and marriage age. The strategies and interactions observed in childless couples included the suppression of feelings, conflicts, rationality, and malingering. The core phenomenon was individualism [1].

This study aimed to investigate the factors associated with reluctance to have children among small families with zero or one child. Understanding these factors contributes to the setting and prioritization of appropriate strategies and implement evidenced-based interventions. This research addresses childbearing reluctance while assessed a wide verity of personal, cultural and socio-economic determinants among small families, which represent 45–50% of Iranian households.

Methods

Statistic population

This study was conducted in Fars province in southwest of Iran. This province is the fourth populated and fourth vast province of Iran. It has five million population (6% of country population), with a nearly the same ratio of males and females, and an annual population growth rate 1.08% (1.8% in the urban regions and − 0.6% in the rural regions). About 70% of population are living in the urban areas and 30% in the rural areas. It consists of 29 cities and different cultures. Population more than 19 years consists 75% of total population in this province and mean of the family size in the urban areas is 3.3 and in the rural areas is 3.4. Out of all males and females > 15 years in this province, 28.3% and 19.4% never married, respectively. Two members (without child) and three members (with one child) families consist 18% and 27% of families, respectively [10].

Study design, sample size and sampling method

This field-based analytic cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024. Sample size was calculated as 323 using the z2pq/d2 formula, considering 70% reluctance-to-childbearing [11], 95% confidence interval (CI), and 5% margin of error. For sampling, firstly, marrieds who were registered from 2010 to 2022 by marriage public counselling center (Bahar-e-Neku Institute) of Fars province were defined (n = 475746). This public institute was established in 2010 to register and holding an obligatory short course of counselling for all couples before their official marriage. Then, for reaching to above calculated sample size, a subsample (n = 10000) was selected by a proportional-based systematic random sampling method and equally from both genders (men = 5000, women = 5000) from 29 counties of this province. Our rational for selecting 10,000 persons, was presumptions of divorce rate 50% [12], prevalence of small family size (2–3 members) 45% [12], up to 45% of responding rate to the online-based surveys [13], incomplete answers, internet’s low penetration/disconnections and effect size: 2 due to multi stage sampling.

Pilot test and development of questionnaire

At first, a draft of questionnaire was developed consisting of extracted items from literature review and opinions of experts. Then, it was sent to an expert team (included 11 subjects from disciplines of psychology, psychiatry, sociology, demographics, health economy, community medicine, general medicine and health education. We held several in-person and online meetings, as well as email correspondence, with them. Additionally, we used a modified Delphi-like method, where we gathered their opinions through multiple rounds of feedback and revisions. This iterative process helped refine the questionnaire until we reached the final version. These experts were wanted to put each question under one category (personal, socio-cultural and economic). These categories were also matched with the principal component analysis (PCA) of findings in the pilot test. Furthermore, content validity ratio (CVR) was calculated according to Lawshe Formula [CVR=[(ne)-N/2)] /N/2] and the related Table [14]. Except one question, other questions had a CVR above 80%; that is more than the threshold level 59%. Content validity index (CVI) of questionnaire (Sum CV of all questions/total number of questions) was 82% and for its personal part was 98%, for socio-cultural part was 82% and for economic part was 91%. For checking the reliability of questionnaire, after initial phone contact, the link of questionnaire was sent to 21 marrieds who were selected by systematic randomization of the available list, however 19 subjects filled the questionnaires (14). Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.70 for the personal domain, 0.74 for socio-cultural, 0.74 for economic part and 0.83 for the questionnaire as a whole. Finally, the questionnaire consisted of background information, and 22 personal items (scoring:0–22), 10 socio-cultural items (scoring:0–10), and 6 economic items (scoring:0–6), considering score 1 for answer “yes” and 0 for answer “no” to each item. These factors were considered as independent variables in the analysis of data. They were also reported as percentage to make comparisons between group with one and group without any child. On the other hand, and as the dependent variable, moderately to very much desire for having more than one child was encoded as 1 and little to none tendency regarding this issue was encoded as 0. As a brief, background information covered items about gender, age, years passed from marriage, number of children, birthplace, living place, level of education, religion, kinship relationship with spouse, job status, and the main source of income for family. Cultural differences with spouse, role in decision making and opinion’s similarity with spouse about family size, alongside preference to have daughter or son were also queried. Personal factors included preferences, priorities, taking parental responsibilities, past childbearing experiences, and infertility. Socio-cultural factors consisted of influence of families, friends, colleagues, social media and social condition on disinterest in having children. Studied Economic factors involved income and job problems, housing and economic perspectives.

Data collection and inclusion and exclusion criteria

After initial phone contacts and explanation about this study, a mobile-based message containing the link of an electronic questionnaire (called Porsline) was sent to the selected ones who agreed to participate in this study. Inclusion criteria were designed as filtering questions. Initially, we asked “would you like to participate in the study?” If the answer was yes, the next question was, “are you married?” If the answer was yes again, the following question was, “do you have no children or only one child?” If participants answered yes to this, they continued with the survey. Those who did not meet these criteria were excluded from the study. Exclusion criteria included who themselves or their spouses were non-Iranian or those who were singles (divorced/widowed).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (ver.25). After quality checking of data, descriptive statistics were extracted. Shapiro-Wilk test was done for checking normal distribution of continuous variables. Then univariable analysis (with t-test, chi-squared and spearman correlation) was done. Next, variables with p-value < 0.2 were selected and entered into the multivariable analysis (binary logistic regression: backward LR method). P-value < 0.05 was considered significant in the final analysis.

Results

Table 1 shows the demographic, socioeconomic, and marital characteristics of the studied participants. Findings showed that, 280 of 323 selected subjects filled the questionnaires completely; showing 87% responding rate. Participants included 137(48.9%) women and 143(51.1%) men with the mean of age 35 ± 5.7-year-old (min:19, max: 53, median:35). 194(69.2%) had an education level up to 12 years and 256 (91.4%) were living in the cities, including 172(61.4%) in the Shiraz (the capital city of Fars province). 247(88.2%) had been born in cities and 200(71.4%) did not have any kindship relationship with their spouses. Mean years that passed from marriage till attending this study was 5.9 ± 3.6 years (min:2, max: 21, median: 5). 177(63.2%) had a kind of job with income and 272(97.1%) themselves and/or their spouses were their source of income. 274(97.9%) were belonged to Shia and 5 (1.8%) were belonged to Sunni branches of Moslems.145(51.8%) did not have any child, while 135(48.2%) had only one child. 248(88.6%) said that both themselves and their spouses were the decision makers about childbearing and 208 (74.3%) had a high to very high co-agreement with their spouses about this issue. Gender of child was a matter of importance for 64(22.9%) of participants. Only, 98(35%) mentioned that had a much to very much tendency to have more than one child, while two-third had a moderate to low desire regarding it. We did not find any significant difference regarding tendency to childbearing between those without (53.6%) and those with one child (46.4%) (χ2 = 0.35, p = 0.5) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study revealed that at least two-thirds of the marrieds without or with one child, did not have a high desire for childbearing or childbearing more, while co-agreement with spouse about childlessness, socio-cultural factors, personal factors and economic factors were the main determinants of reluctance to childbearing in the studied marrieds, respectively. Among these factors prioritizing the quality of life of children over their numbers, lack of confidence in improving social conditions, and financial challenges were remarkable.

Background factors

We found that urban inhabitants had a stronger childbearing reluctance compared to the rural ones. This finding is similar to the other studies’ results which highlighted a more rate of childbearing reluctance in urban or modern socities than rural or traditional socities [6].This finding may addresses higher costs of housing, childcare and education, higher employment rate of mothers, full time employment of couples, different role, values and perspevtives of families and especilly children, and also different level of fertility literacy, higher age of marriage and higher eonomic, political and social uncertainities in the urbal comparing to the rural areas. In this study, we did not find any significant association between other studied background variables (age, gender, level of education, number of children, relativity with spouse, years passed from marriage, importance of gender of child) and childbearing tendency. However, role of age, education and gender of respondents and number of children in childhaving desire was more or less confirmed in some other studies [11, 15–17]. These results underscore the need for tailored interventions addressing these items especially in the urban families and also need to other researches to address fertility concerns across diverse ethnic populations and contexts.

Personal preferences, priorities, experiences

Individual priorities, such as the desire for autonomy, career aspirations, and lifestyle preferences, emerged as critical determinants in childbearing desire in our study. This is consistent with prior studies that highlighted the role of personal goals, peace, psychological well-being, marital satisfaction, taking parental responsibility, career concerns, emigration and educational pursuits, individualism and adherence to universal values in desire to have children [18, 19]. Our research revealed that a significant proportion of newly marrieds prioritize the relationship with their spouses over expanding their family sizes, similar to another study which emphasized on such influence in fertility decisions [11].

We also found that personal preferences had a significant association with lack of desire for childbearing compared to those had such desires. This mirrors the perceived costs and values of childbearing, as denoted by another study [20].

Some participants in our study believed that having children disrupts their personal peace. This view was in line with a study that reported psychological challenges and stressors diminish childbearing desires [21].

Additionally, our research showed the role of professional aspirations and work-life balance in shaping desire toward childbearing. Many participants expressed concerns that childbearing could hinder their professional duties. This was in line with another study’s findings [22].

A significant portion of reluctant participants in our study, noted for difficulties in making balancing between child-rearing responsibilities and career obligations. Educational and personal development priorities were also emerged prominently, with some childless participants focusing on continuing education compared to the group desiring to have more children. This observation was in line with another study that identified modern parenting ideals as pivotal in shaping fertility intentions [23].

Moreover, we found that emigration aspirations further illustrate the prioritization of personal goals over traditional family structures, with many childless participants emphasizing this factor compared to those desiring children. Role of emigration in having less child was also emphasized by another study [16].

Fear of negative effect of childbearing on woman’s appearance and body in who without desire to have children compared to who had such desire, was another finding in our study which was in line with another study [24].

Alongside these personal considerations, our study highlighted the critical role of spousal agreement and family stability in childbearing decisions. These factors foster marital collaboration and create a supportive environment for addressing fertility challenges, as it was remarked in other studies which revealed the importance of harmonized perspectives, marital satisfaction, trust, and role of supportive family policies in family planning [15, 25, 26].

Above findings show the importance and necessity of policy making and interventions by government toward implementation of work-life balance, creating accessible and affordable educational opportunities, empowering of couples to overcome their challenges, cearting a supportive, peaceful, trustful and hopeful condition in the country (for decreasing the emigration desire), addressing career and income concerns and holding free fertility counsultation services for couples in our country.

Socio-cultural factors

Our research identified a complex interplay of societal, familial, and peer influences that contribute to childbearing reluctance among married couples. These evidences are in line with existing researches that concluded the importance of comprehensive policies to address societal, and cultural barriers to achieving reproductive goals [11, 16, 17, 22, 27–33].

A significant aspect and frequently cited reason for childlessness by participants in our study was their attitude about societal instability/risky society and their lack of confidence in improved social conditions. Familial and peers’ influences was also emerged as critical determinants of less desire for having children in our study, with many participants referencing familial norms or peers influence that discourage them for making larger families. This finding was compatible with another study that demonstrated a dual role of cultural norms and social participation in either encouraging or discouraging fertility [11]. Furthermore, the role of media and especially social networks which was identified by some participants in this study in reinforcing them to childbearing reluctance, adds an additional layer to the socio-cultural framework’s effects on this issue. While this specific factor is less prominently discussed in existing literature, it suggests a growing need to examine the media’s role in shaping public perceptions and behaviors regarding family planning. Role of social networks and its mechanisms including social pressure, social contagion, social learning and social support in childbearing intention was assessed by one study and it was showed that family-centered social networks have the most probability of going to a higher parity progression. This probability for non-supportive, kin-centered, and friend-centered social networks were 5.1, 2.8 and 2.2 times more, respectively [34]. Another study found a positive association between men’s attitude towards childbearing and amount of internet usage [28]. These evidences highlight the role of socio-cultural norms and familial and social networks in shaping individual and couple-level reproductive choices. Therefore, these evidences emphasized the need for targeted public awareness campaigns and community-based programs that address the socio-cultural barriers to childbearing, while emphasizing on the positive aspects of family size extension and reducing the current stigma which is associated with having more children in some parts of our country.

Economic factors

Economic challenges are universally recognized as critical factors influencing childbearing decisions, and both our findings and existing literature corroborate this issue. Our study revealed the multifaceted economic pressures such as lack of steady income, job instability, housing crisis and cost of infertility treatment as reasons of childlessness. This aligns with the findings of other studies who similarly highlighted the dominance of financial barriers in fertility decisions [11, 15, 17, 23, 29, 35, 36]. The participants’ economic pessimismistic outlook and doubts about its improvement, which was identified in our research, expressing a broader context for the observed financial anxieties in childless groups. Economic policies aimed at reducing financial insecurity, such as affordable housing, stable employment opportunities, and accessible and affordable fertility treatments, could alleviate economic pressures and support individuals and couples in making childbearing decisions.

Implications for policy and intervention

This study remarked evidences at macro, meso and micro levels of policymaking that is necessary for setting strategies, making guidelines and implement multifaceted interventions for increasing childbearing intentions at society. This study showed that main efforts should be focused on establishment of counselling facilities to make co-agreement of both partners about childbearing and also toward interventions on demographic characteristics (e.g., urban versus rural location, younger couples), personal factors (e.g., personal preferences, career aspirations, work-life balance), socio-cultural items (as societal and familial influences, media impact, social networks), and economic factors (e.g., income, job stability, housing crisis, infertility treatment costs). However, in all steps of policymaking and interventions, reversible groups of voluntary and nonvoluntary childless and modifiable factors should be prioritized on irreversible/non-modifiable counterpart groups.

Strengths, limitations and recommendations

As strength, this study was among population-based and comprehensive studies that assessed nearly all possible determinants of reluctance to childbearing in small families (marrieds without or with only one child) in both urban and rural areas. As a limitation, the researchers could not conduct this study among interviewees and their spouses as a couple-based survey. Therefore, conducting future studies assessing both partners is proposed. Furthermore, this study was a cross-sectional survey and defined correlates of childlessness, however to define causes, cohort and in-depth qualitative studies is necessary.

Conclusion

This study found that two-thirds of the marrieds had a moderate to low desire for childbearing, and several factors were revealed to be related to this issue. To increase childbearing, effective strategies must address both partners’ perspectives. Policymakers should also focus on improving socioeconomic conditions through income stabilization and improvement, housing affordability, and allocate subsidies for childcare and infertility. Training and employing professional reproductive counsellors and establishing accessible and affordable and culturally sensitive counseling facilities is crucial to help couples navigate their personal and cultural barriers about childbearing. These interventions, combined with efforts to rebuild social capital, societal trust and hope and promote supportive family policies, can create an environment that empowers couples to pursue their family aspirations while respecting their priorities and challenges. The focus of priorities should be those couples who desire for childbearing but because of obstacles cannot expand their family sizes.
