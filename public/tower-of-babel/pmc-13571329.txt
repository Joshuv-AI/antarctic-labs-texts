Atypical STEMI in the Young
Authors: Tomoyuki Ishinaga, Naoko Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Idiopathic ascending aortic thrombus is an exceptionally rare entity, often difficult to distinguish from acute aortic dissection on imaging and only infrequently recognized as a cause of acute coronary syndrome. Because the vast majority of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) cases arise from atherosclerotic plaque rupture,1 considering alternative etiologies in the acute phase is inherently challenging. The case reported in this issue of JACC: Case Reports by Melo-González et al2 describes a highly unusual presentation in which a thrombus located along the anterior aspect of the ascending aorta completely occluded the right coronary artery ostium, resulting in STEMI. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating patients with atypical clinical features.

A multimodality diagnostic approach combining coronary angiography and cross-sectional imaging clarified that the thrombus was situated adjacent to the coronary ostium. The patient was relatively young, had no underlying aortic disease, and lacked identifiable prothrombotic conditions—features consistent with an idiopathic process. Emergency surgical intervention was selected, a decision justified not only by the need for right coronary artery revascularization but also by the potential risk of systemic embolization given the location, size, and possible mobility of the thrombus. This report provides clinically valuable insights into diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making in a condition for which no established guidelines exist, emphasizing the need for individualized management strategies.

Although the pathogenesis of idiopathic ascending aortic thrombus remains uncertain, prior literature has proposed several mechanisms, including localized flow disturbances, subtle endothelial injury, and transient hypercoagulability.3 Notably, the right coronary cusp (RCC) is a frequent site of thrombus formation.3,4 The predilection for thrombus formation along the anterior ascending aorta and the RCC may be explained through Virchow's triad. First, the RCC vortex forms rapidly and forcefully but is characterized by frequent secondary vortices that dissipate abruptly during diastolic deceleration, causing the vortex core to shift markedly within the sinus. This inherent “instability” may promote localized stasis.5,6 Second, both the RCC and the anterior ascending aorta occupy the most anterior anatomical position and are directly exposed to the systolic jet from the left ventricle. As a result, these regions experience high wall shear stress.7,8 Moreover, oscillatory shear stress, which involves directional changes in shear forces, is also pronounced in these areas and has been associated with endothelial injury in some hemodynamic studies.9,10 When these factors are accompanied by conditions that induce transient hypercoagulability—for instance, arrhythmia, inflammation, dehydration, malignancy, or hormonal imbalance—the elements of Virchow's triad may converge to promote thrombus formation.

This case highlights the renewed importance of multimodality imaging in the prompt evaluation of a critically ill patient presenting with chest symptoms. In the emergency department, clinicians must rapidly identify life-threatening conditions such as acute coronary syndrome, type A aortic dissection, and massive pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiography, although sometimes limited by image quality, remains invaluable for quick assessment. Identification of regional wall motion abnormalities, an intimal flap in the ascending aorta, aortic regurgitation, pericardial effusion, masses within the sinuses of Valsalva or aorta, and estimated right ventricular pressure can significantly narrow the differential diagnosis and guide urgent additional diagnostic testing and treatment.

Computed tomography angiography and transesophageal echocardiography provide high-resolution evaluation of thrombus morphology, attachment site, and mobility—key determinants of embolic risk and treatment strategy. These imaging modalities enable tailored decision-making between anticoagulation and surgical intervention. Furthermore, the management of atypical STEMI and complex aortic pathology requires seamless collaboration among cardiology, cardiac imaging, and cardiac surgery teams. This case exemplifies the value of such multidisciplinary coordination.
