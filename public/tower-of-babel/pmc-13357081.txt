Development and internal validation of a SEER-based prediction model for survival in poorly and undifferentiated colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms
Authors: Qiqiang Huang, Lilin Lin, Minmin Chen, Rui Huang, He Huang, Suyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoorly and undifferentiated colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms (CR-NENs) are rare and highly aggressive tumors with limited individualized prognostic assessment. Conventional staging systems are insufficient to capture heterogeneity, and dedicated prognostic models for this subgroup remain lacking. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a multivariable prediction model for survival in patients with CR-NENs.MethodsPatients diagnosed with poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database spanning 2000–2019. Eligible cases were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts (7:3). Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were defined as study endpoints. Candidate variables including demographic and clinicopathological factors were analyzed using Cox regression to identify independent predictors, which were incorporated into nomograms. Model performance was evaluated by concordance index (C-index), time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with area under the curve (AUC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on predicted risk scores, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed.ResultsA total of 904 patients were included. The cohort was characterized by a high proportion of advanced disease, with approximately 80% of patients presenting with T3–4 tumors, about 75% having lymph node involvement, and over 40% with distant metastases. Multivariate analysis identified lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis as independent risk factors, while female sex, married status, and surgical treatment were protective factors (all P<0.05). The nomograms demonstrated moderate discriminative ability, with C-indexes of 0.70 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.67–0.72] for OS and 0.71 (95% CI: 0.68–0.73) for CSS in the training cohort, and comparable performance in the validation cohort. For OS prediction, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUC values were 0.76, 0.82, and 0.85 in the training cohort and 0.72, 0.80, and 0.80 in the validation cohort; for CSS prediction, the corresponding AUC values were 0.76, 0.81, and 0.84, and 0.76, 0.86, and 0.88, respectively. Calibration curves showed good agreement, and DCA indicated clinical net benefit. Risk stratification based on the nomograms effectively distinguished patients with significantly different survival outcomes (P<0.001).ConclusionsWe developed and internally validated a SEER-based prediction model for OS and CSS in poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs. The model may assist in individualized risk stratification, although further external validation is required before clinical application.Keywords: Colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms (CR-NENs); prognosis; nomogram; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database (SEER database); risk stratification

Key findings

• This study was based on the data of 904 patients with poorly and undifferentiated colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms (CR-NENs) from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database [2000–2019], and developed and internally validated a prognostic prediction model for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) of patients with poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs.

What is known and what is new?

• Poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs are rare, highly aggressive tumors, characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and extremely poor prognosis. Existing prognostic assessments mainly rely on tumor-node-metastasis staging and World Health Organization grading, which are limited in capturing biological heterogeneity and providing individualized survival predictions.

• The first dedicated prediction tool for poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs: we developed and internally validated nomograms for OS and CSS specifically for this high-risk subgroup, filling a gap in individualized prognostic modeling. Based on the model-derived scores, we further established a risk stratification system that effectively discriminates patients into high- and low-risk groups. The model provides individualized estimates of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities, offering a more refined framework for prognostic assessment and supporting clinical decision-making and risk-adapted management strategies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The nomogram can assist clinicians in quickly assessing the long‑term survival probability of patients with poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs and identifying high‑risk individuals to optimize follow‑up and management.

• The model quantifies the associations between survival outcomes and clinical factors, including surgery and demographic characteristics, offering insight into prognostic differences and potential implications for individualized treatment considerations.

Introduction

Colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms (CR-NENs) are a rare group of tumors originating from neuroendocrine cells, accounting for less than 1% of all colorectal tumors (1,2). Despite their low incidence, CR-NENs exhibit remarkable heterogeneity in biological behavior and clinical outcomes (3-6). According to the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, NENs are graded based on mitotic count and Ki-67 index into G1, G2, and G3. G3 tumors are further divided into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs; G3) and poorly differentiated or undifferentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) (7). Previous studies have shown that G1–3 NENs generally have relatively favorable prognoses, whereas colorectal NECs are highly aggressive, characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and extremely poor outcomes, with a median survival of only 5–11 months and a 5-year survival rate below 10% (2,8). At present, the optimal treatment strategy for CR-NENs remains unclear, and no standardized clinical management guidelines have been established (3,8,9). With increasing awareness of colorectal diseases, implementation of cancer screening programs, and widespread use of endoscopic techniques, the incidence of CR-NENs has been rising (1,2). Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop effective prognostic assessment tools to guide clinical decision-making.

Previous studies have confirmed that the degree of tumor differentiation is a critical prognostic determinant for gastrointestinal NENs (10,11). Compared with well-differentiated tumors, patients with poorly differentiated or undifferentiated neoplasms typically exhibit stronger aggressiveness, a higher risk of distant metastasis, and markedly shortened survival, with median overall survival (OS) often less than 1 year (11). Furthermore, because patients with poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs experience rapid disease progression and have a narrow therapeutic window, their clinical management depends heavily on early risk stratification and individualized decision-making. Although prognostic prediction models have been developed for well- and moderately differentiated populations (12), there is still a lack of individualized prognostic tools specifically designed for poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs based on large-scale patient cohorts.

Due to the overall low incidence of CR-NENs, particularly the poorly differentiated and undifferentiated subtypes, prospective studies in this field are extremely limited. Most of the available evidence is derived from small-sample, single-center retrospective studies, which often combine NENs from different gastrointestinal sites or with varying degrees of differentiation (13-15). This heterogeneity limits a more in-depth understanding of colorectal-specific high-risk populations and hampers the development of robust, site-specific prognostic evidence. At present, prognostic assessment of CR-NENs in clinical practice primarily relies on the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system and the WHO grading classification. However, these conventional approaches have several inherent limitations. The TNM staging system mainly reflects the anatomical extent of the tumor and fails to adequately capture its biological aggressiveness (16). Meanwhile, The WHO grading system relies on the Ki-67 index and mitotic count; however, in real-world settings, it is often affected by missing data and inconsistencies in assessment (17). In addition, the evaluation of the Ki-67 index is subject to intertumoral staining heterogeneity, as well as methodological limitations in counting (18).

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, established by the National Cancer Institute, covers approximately 28% of the United States (U.S.) population and integrates high-quality clinical, pathological, and follow-up data. It represents an important resource for investigating the epidemiology and prognosis of rare tumors (10,19-21). Compared with the traditional classification system relying solely on TNM staging and WHO pathological grading, the SEER database allows the incorporation of more comprehensive clinical characteristics, demographic data, and follow-up outcomes, thereby overcoming the limitation of existing classification systems that only enable pathological stratification but fail to provide individualized survival prediction. Based on this, the present study utilized the SEER database to specifically focus on patients with poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs. We systematically analyzed prognostic factors and developed an individualized survival prediction model (nomogram), with the aim of providing more precise quantitative support for clinical risk stratification and personalized management, as well as offering insights for the optimization of future stratification and treatment strategies for CR-NENs. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0561/rc).

Methods

Data source

The patient cohort for this study was extracted from the SEER cancer database of the National Cancer Institute using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.5). Eligible patients were identified from “SEER Research Plus Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2021 sub [2000–2019]” using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.5). “Site and Morphology, Behavior recode for analysis” was limited to “Malignant”, “Site and Morphology, Site recode International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-O-3/WHO 2008” was limited to “Colon and rectum”. As patient data in the SEER database are de-identified, ethical approval and informed consent were waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria include (I) histology types based on ICD-10 morphology codes: 8012–8013, 8041, 8240–8246, 8249; and (II) patients with poorly differentiated (G3) or undifferentiated (G4) colorectal NET. Exclusion criteria comprised patients with: (I) excluding the “appendix, cecum, and large intestine (NOS)”; (II) lacked positive histological confirmation; (III) follow-up time less than 1 month; (IV) missing therapy management information; (V) unconfirmed or unknown T, N, and M staging data; and (VI) lacked tumor size details. A flowchart detailing the patient screening process and study design is presented in Figure S1.

Variables and definitions

Variables included age (≤65/>65 years), sex (male/female), race (White/Black/others), marital status (single/married/other), tumor stage [American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) I/II/III/IV], tumor size (≤20/>20 mm), primary tumor site (colon/rectum), and treatment, categorized as surgery (endoscopic/surgical procedures/non-surgery), radiotherapy (yes/no), and chemotherapy (yes/no). Age was treated as a categorical variable and dichotomized at 65 years. Tumor size was also categorized according to clinically relevant thresholds in NENs, with 2 cm used as the cutoff, given its relevance to treatment strategy and surgical decision-making. The TNM staging data were reclassified according to the 9th edition AJCC cancer staging manual. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause, and cancer-specific survival (CSS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death attributed to the cancer, with both OS and CSS serving as the study endpoints. The follow-up time was calculated directly based on the SEER-reported survival time variable, which is derived from the interval between diagnosis date and either death date or last known follow-up date in the registry. During follow-up, a total of 690 OS event and 599 CSS events were observed in the entire cohort. As the final multivariable Cox regression model included 5 independent predictors, the events per variable (EPV) ratio was 138 for OS and 119.8 for CSS. Both EPV values exceeded 10, confirming the robustness of our multivariable analysis.

Model construction and validation

A total of 904 eligible patients were randomly assigned to training and testing cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. To ensure reproducibility of the random split, a fixed random seed was set during the data partitioning process. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed on the training cohort to identify independent prognostic factors associated with OS and CSS. In the training cohort, univariate Cox analysis was first used to screen potential prognostic variables, and those with P value <0.05 were subsequently entered into multivariate Cox regression to identify independent prognostic factors. The final independent predictors were used to construct nomograms to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS.

Model performance was evaluated in both training and validation cohorts. Discriminative ability was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points. An AUC value >0.7 was considered to indicate acceptable to moderate predictive performance. The concordance index (C-index) was used to further quantify model discrimination. Calibration curves based on 1,000 bootstrap resamples were generated to assess agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility and net benefit of the model. Risk stratification was performed using optimal cut-off values, and survival differences between risk groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test. Model performance was further compared with the AJCC 9th edition staging system using ROC analysis and C-index estimates.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). Continuous variables were analyzed as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed using the survival package to identify prognostic factors, and results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The R packages survival, rms, timeROC, survminer, and rmda were used for Cox regression, nomogram construction, time-dependent ROC analysis, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and DCA, respectively. X-tile software was applied to determine optimal cut-off values for risk stratification. Survival differences among groups were examined using Kaplan-Meier survival analyses (the log-rank test). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The detailed baseline clinical characteristics of all the patients are presented in Table 1. A total of 904 patients were included in the OS analysis, with 636 randomly assigned to the training set and the remaining to the validation set. For the CSS analysis, 898 patients were included, with 630 in the training cohort and 268 in the validation cohort. No significant differences were observed between the variables in the training and validation sets (P>0.05), indicating successful randomization and comparability of the groups. In this study, the majority of patients were white (approximately 80%). Males and females were roughly equally represented, as were elderly (>65 years) and younger (≤65 years) patients. Colonic NETs were more prevalent (approximately 70%) than rectal tumors (approximately 30%). Most tumors exceeded 20 mm in size, accounting for roughly 90% of cases. The majority of patients presented with advanced disease, with approximately 80% classified as T3–4 stage. Lymph node involvement was observed in about 75% of patients, and distant metastases were present in over 40%, highlighting a high proportion of advanced-stage disease within the cohort. Regarding treatment, approximately 80% of patients underwent surgical intervention, nearly 60% received chemotherapy, and fewer than 20% received radiotherapy.

Nomogram variable screening

As shown in Table 2, univariate Cox regression analysis in the training cohort identified seven variables significantly associated with OS (P<0.05), including sex, marital status, tumor size, T stage, N stage, M stage, and surgery. For CSS, the significant variables were largely consistent, except that T stage was not retained (P>0.05), while age was additionally identified as a significant factor. In the multivariate analysis, lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis emerged as independent predictors of poorer survival. Patients with N1 disease had significantly worse OS (HR =1.69; 95% CI: 1.339–2.132; P<0.001) and CSS (HR =1.969; 95% CI: 1.550–2.502; P<0.001), while patients with distant metastasis also had markedly inferior OS (HR =3.044; 95% CI: 2.493–3.715; P<0.001) and CSS (HR =3.261; 95% CI: 2.637–4.033; P<0.001). Conversely, female sex and married status were associated with favorable outcomes. Female patients demonstrated improved OS (HR =0.753; 95% CI: 0.623–0.909; P=0.003) and CSS (HR =0.806; 95% CI: 0.659–0.987; P=0.04), while married patients also had better OS (HR =0.678; 95% CI: 0.532–0.863; P=0.002) and CSS (HR =0.697; 95% CI: 0.537–0.903; P=0.006). In addition, surgical treatment was significantly associated with improved OS (HR =0.698; 95% CI: 0.552–0.884; P=0.003) and CSS (HR =0.678; 95% CI: 0.534–0.862; P<0.001).

Nomogram construction and validation

Based on the statistically significant variables, two nomogram models were constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS in patients with poorly or undifferentiated CR-NENs using the training cohort (Figure 1). In the training set, the C-index was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.72) for OS and 0.71 (95% CI: 0.68–0.73) for CSS, representing apparent model performance. In the validation set, the C-index was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.64–0.71) for OS and 0.69 (95% CI: 0.65–0.73) for CSS, reflecting internal validation performance. In the training set, the nomograms yielded AUC values of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.73–0.80), 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.86), and 0.85 (95% CI: 081–0.89) for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively (Figure 2A), while in the validation set, the corresponding AUC values were 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78), 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.86), and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.73–0.87) (Figure 2B). For CSS prediction, the AUC values were 0.76 (95% CI: 0.72–0.80), 0.81 (95% CI: 0.77–0.85), and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.89) in the training cohort (Figure 2C), and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70–0.82), 0.86 (95% CI: 0.80–0.91), and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.83–0.93) in the validation cohort (Figure 2D). Calibration was assessed using bootstrap resampling (B=1,000), and the bias-corrected calibration curves indicated reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS in both the training and validation cohorts (Figure 3A-3D). Moreover, DCA suggested that the two models may provide a potential net clinical benefit for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and CSS across both the training and validation cohorts, as the model curves were higher than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies within a range of threshold probabilities (Figure 4 and Figure S2). Collectively, these findings suggest that the nomograms achieved moderate discriminative ability and reasonable calibration, with potential clinical utility.

Risk stratification for CR-NENs

Risk scores were calculated based on the nomograms and used to stratify patients into two risk groups (low and high) according to the optimal cut-off points determined by the maximally selected rank statistics for OS (optimal cut-off points =0.1) and CSS (optimal cut-off points =0). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a clear separation of survival curves, with OS and CSS progressively decreasing from the low- to high-risk groups in both the training and validation cohorts (Figure 5A-5D), confirming the discriminatory ability of the nomogram-based risk stratification.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by nomogram-derived risk groups: (A) OS in the training set; (B) OS in the validation set; (C) CSS in the training set; (D) CSS in the validation set. CSS, cancer-specific survival; OS, overall survival.

To compare with the 9th edition AJCC staging system, ROC analysis showed that the nomogram-based risk groups had higher predictive accuracy, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs outperforming those of AJCC in OS. In the training set, AUCs were 0.74 (95% CI: 0.70–0.77), 0.79 (95% CI: 0.75–0.83), and 0.81 (95% CI: 0.76–0.85) for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, respectively, and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.64–0.76), 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.85), and 0.79 (95% CI: 0.72–0.86) in the validation set. In CSS, the corresponding AUCs were 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69–0.77), 0.77 (95% CI: 0.73–0.82), and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.85) in the training set, and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.71–0.82), 0.86 (95% CI: 0.80–0.91), and 0.87 (95% CI: 0.81–0.93) in the validation set (Figure S3). Similarly, the C-index of the nomogram-based stratification was superior than AJCC in OS (C-indextraining set: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.65–0.70; C-indexvalidation set: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.63–0.70) and CSS (C-indextraining set: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.66–0.71; C-indexvalidation set: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.66–0.73).

Discussion

The major strength of this study lies in the utilization of the SEER database to systematically evaluate prognostic factors in patients with poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs for the first time, and to construct corresponding nomogram models. In this poor-prognosis subgroup, five factors (sex, marital status, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and surgical intervention) were identified as independent predictors of OS and CSS. The nomogram demonstrated moderate discrimination, calibration, and predictive accuracy, and showed better discrimination than AJCC staging system, highlighting its potential utility for risk stratification and individualized management support in clinical decision-making.

Gastrointestinal NENs are relatively uncommon, with NECs—the subgroup associated with the poorest prognosis under current classification systems—being even rarer. As a result, many studies have analyzed gastroenteropancreatic NENs in aggregate (22). Nevertheless, NENs arising from different anatomical sites demonstrate marked heterogeneity in molecular features, prognostic trajectories, and therapeutic responses. For example, ileal NETs typically follow an indolent course, are frequently characterized by chromosome 8 loss, and lack well-defined driver mutations, whereas pancreatic NENs are often associated with MEN1 and DAXX/ARTX mutations (7). Substantial progress has been made in elucidating the prognostic factors of pancreatic NENs (23-26) and gastric NENs (27,28), yet evidence regarding CR-NENs remains limited. Prior studies have reported that up to 85% of CR-NENs originate in the rectum, of which the majority (88.2%) are detected by colonoscopy and 94.6% can be resected endoscopically, leading to favorable overall outcomes (29). By contrast, 61.0% of colonic NETs require surgical resection. Distinct from these well-differentiated entities, our study focused on poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs. These tumors were found to arise predominantly in the colon, with most patients presenting at advanced stages and frequently exhibiting distant metastases, consistent with previous reports (30), thereby may partly explain their dismal prognosis (10). It is noteworthy that case selection in the present study was based on the “grade of differentiation” variable within the SEER database. The included poorly differentiated and undifferentiated CR-NENs shared clinical and biological characteristics highly similar to NECs defined by the WHO classification; however, owing to the absence of Ki-67 index or mitotic count, they cannot be regarded as entirely equivalent and should instead be interpreted as a closely related but not completely overlapping subgroup.

In terms of demographic factors, our study identified marital status and sex as independent prognostic indicators. Married patients exhibited superior survival compared with their unmarried counterparts, a pattern that has been validated not only in CR-NENs (31,32) but also in NENs at other anatomical sites (33,34) and various solid tumors (35). Potential explanations include stronger social support, higher treatment adherence, and greater psychological stability (36). Sex differences were also significant, with female patients consistently demonstrating better prognosis across multiple NEN studies (37-39). Moreover, a study based on the United Kingdom National Cancer Registration and Analysis Service database indicated a survival advantage for females across several solid tumors (40). Possible underlying reasons include lower screening adherence and delayed diagnosis in males (38), as well as potential protective effects of estrogen or progesterone receptor expression in NENs (39). Nonetheless, the precise biological mechanisms warrant further investigation.

In this study, surgical intervention demonstrated a significant protective effect. Surgery remains the primary treatment for patients with NENs, applicable to those with localized disease, who may undergo either endoscopic resection or surgical excision, as well as to patients with locally advanced disease, who typically require radical surgery (30,41). Beyond its therapeutic role, surgery provides supplementary clinical staging information, offering additional objective data to guide prognostic assessment and subsequent management (41). However, the observed survival benefit of surgery may reflect not only the treatment effect but also favorable baseline characteristics, such as limited tumor burden and absence of distant metastasis. In this study, the surgical cohort included patients treated endoscopically, who were often identified through early screening; their favorable outcomes may partially result from indolent tumor biology and early timing of intervention. Conversely, patients who did not receive any surgical treatment frequently presented with rapidly progressing disease, high tumor burden, or significant comorbidities. Approximately 40% of patients in our cohort had distant metastases, for whom chemotherapy constituted the primary treatment and prognosis was generally poor. Therefore, in prognostic analyses, surgery reflects both therapeutic efficacy and, indirectly, disease severity, warranting cautious interpretation in modeling. Nonetheless, surgery was incorporated into the nomogram and demonstrated independent prognostic value in multivariable analyses. The strength of the nomogram lies in its integration of multidimensional variables, which may assist in individualized risk assessment. By including surgical modality as one of the input variables, the model can rapidly estimate patient-specific survival probabilities in clinical practice, facilitating risk stratification and treatment decision support. Even if surgery partially reflects baseline disease severity, its inclusion enhances the model’s discriminative performance and clinical utility.

Notably, neither chemotherapy nor radiotherapy demonstrated a significant improvement in OS or CSS. This finding likely reflects limitations of the database and patient characteristics rather than a lack of clinical efficacy. First, the SEER database does not provide detailed information regarding chemotherapy or radiotherapy, such as drug type, dosage, regimen, or combination strategies, recording only their presence or absence, which limits precise assessment of treatment intensity and adherence. Second, patients receiving chemoradiotherapy were predominantly high-risk individuals with advanced-stage disease or distant metastases. In this cohort, over half of the patients received chemotherapy, suggesting a predominance of metastatic or WHO G3 cases. Previous studies have reported a median OS of only 6.7 months for patients with metastatic colorectal NEC (42), indicating that advanced stage and highly aggressive tumor biology may partially obscure the true therapeutic benefit. Additionally, CR-NENs generally exhibit limited sensitivity to conventional chemoradiotherapy, which may further attenuate observable overall efficacy. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of complete ineffectiveness of chemoradiotherapy. Instead, they underscore the need for future studies incorporating more detailed treatment information and prospective designs to clarify true treatment effects. Moreover, exploring novel approaches, such as immunotherapy and molecularly targeted therapies, may improve outcomes in this high-risk patient subgroup.

The N and M stages reflect tumor burden and aggressiveness and represent core prognostic indicators for CR-NENs. Currently, CR-NENs are primarily staged according to the AJCC 9th edition gastroenteropancreatic NEN staging system (41). However, for high-risk patients with poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumors, including colorectal NECs, conventional staging may not fully capture the actual risk. To address this, we developed a nomogram-based prognostic model integrating demographic, clinical, pathological, and treatment-related variables. Compared with reliance on AJCC staging alone, this model demonstrated superior discrimination (C-index) and predictive accuracy (AUC), allowing better differentiation of outcomes among patients within the same stage. It is important to note that, due to the lack of Ki-67 or mitotic count data in the SEER database, patient grouping in this study was based on pathological differentiation, and therefore could not fully adhere to WHO classification criteria. Nevertheless, differentiation is the most stable and commonly used pathological variable in real-world databases, and poorly/undifferentiated tumors inherently represent the subgroup with the worst prognosis (10). Thus, this study may serve as a risk stratification tool for this high-risk subgroup of poorly/undifferentiated CR-NENs and offers a reference for individualized clinical decision-making. In the absence of Ki-67 information, the model may serve as a supplementary tool in research and clinical risk assessment contexts. The purpose of this study is not to replace WHO classification but to provide clinicians with a feasible tool for risk assessment in database research and real-world clinical scenarios, with future validation and optimization expected using molecular and pathological data.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective database analysis, it is inherently susceptible to selection bias and missing data. Second, the SEER database lacks detailed information on treatment, including intraoperative resection margins involvement, chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens, doses, and courses, which limits the ability to perform more granular efficacy analyses. Third, key pathological indicators, such as Ki-67 index and mitotic count, were unavailable, restricting direct alignment with WHO classification. Finally, the predictive model developed in this study has not yet been externally validated in independent cohorts. Future prospective, multicenter studies integrating clinical and pathological data are warranted to further optimize and validate this prognostic framework.

Conclusions

In summary, this study focused on the rare and high-risk subgroup of poorly and undifferentiated CR-NENs within the SEER database, identified key prognostic factors, and developed nomogram-based models for predicting OS and CSS. Compared with conventional staging systems, these models demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, prognostic discrimination, and clinical utility. Although Ki-67 and other important pathological indicators were not available in the dataset, our findings may provide preliminary evidence for risk stratification in this specific patient population and could potentially assist clinical decision-making. Further studies incorporating WHO classification, molecular pathology, and external validation are warranted to confirm and extend these findings.
