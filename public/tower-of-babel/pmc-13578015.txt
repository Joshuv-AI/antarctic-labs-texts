Metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis from diffuse breast cancer liver metastasis: a case report
Authors: Takahiro Komori, Norihide Yoneda, Kanako Terada, Kazuto Kozaka, Hirofumi Terakawa, Hiroko Ikeda, Kenichi Harada, Satoshi Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis is a rare condition associated with several malignancies. It is characterized by diffuse hepatic tumor infiltration, leading to rapid progression to liver failure. We report a case of metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis caused by diffuse hepatic metastasis from breast cancer and describe the computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and histopathological findings. A woman in her 50s who had undergone left mastectomy with reconstruction for breast cancer 9 years prior presented with fatigue and abdominal distension. Following the initial surgery, she received adjuvant hormone therapy. Five years prior to presentation, a local recurrence at the surgical site was detected and surgically resected; thereafter, she was followed up without additional treatment. At presentation, non-contrast CT revealed massive ascites, prompting further evaluation with contrast-enhanced dynamic CT and gadolinium-ethoxybenzyl-diethylenetriamine-pentaacetic acid (Gd-EOB-DTPA)-enhanced MRI. Imaging showed a cirrhosis-like morphology, suggesting pseudocirrhosis. However, considering the absence of previously documented hepatic metastases and no history of chemotherapy, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis was suspected. A transjugular liver biopsy confirmed the diagnosis, revealing extensive tumor infiltration of the portal areas accompanied by fibrosis and scar formation that dissected the hepatic parenchyma. In patients with malignancies who develop rapidly progressive liver dysfunction and cirrhosis-like imaging findings, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Additionally, recognizing this entity that represents a pathophysiology distinct from that of pseudocirrhosis is crucial, despite their similar morphological appearances on imaging.Keywords: Metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis, Breast cancer, Pseudocirrhosis, Magnetic resonance imaging, Diffuse hepatic metastasis

Introduction

Metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis is a rare condition characterized by diffuse hepatic infiltration of tumor cells, leading to rapidly progressive liver failure and portal hypertension [1–3]. It has been associated with several malignancies, most frequently including breast cancer, as well as lung and gastric cancer [2–6]. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG-PET/CT), diagnosing metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis remains challenging. Unlike typical hepatic metastases, this condition progresses without forming discrete nodular lesions, often leading to rapid clinical deterioration before a definitive diagnosis is established. Consequently, only a limited number of reports have detailed its radiological and pathological features. Pseudocirrhosis is a well-recognized condition characterized by cirrhosis-like morphological changes that develop during or after chemotherapy in patients with breast cancer. However, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis represents a distinct pathophysiological process driven by diffuse tumor infiltration and associated stromal fibrosis, rather than treatment-related remodeling. Herein, we report a case of metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis caused by diffuse hepatic metastases from breast cancer, focusing on its CT and MRI findings.

Case report

A woman in her 50s who had undergone a left mastectomy with reconstruction for breast cancer 9 years prior presented with progressive fatigue and abdominal distension. The primary tumor was a mucinous carcinoma, for which she received adjuvant hormonal therapy. Five years later, a local recurrence at the surgical site was detected and surgically resected; histopathological examination confirmed mucinous carcinoma. She received no additional treatment and was followed up clinically. At the current presentation, the patient visited a local clinic for her symptoms. Abdominal ultrasonography and CT revealed massive ascites and cirrhosis-like changes, prompting referral to our institution. Laboratory evaluation confirmed progressive hepatic dysfunction, with elevated liver enzymes and impaired coagulation parameters (aspartate aminotransferase 163 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase 97 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase 126 IU/L, γ-glutamyl transpeptidase 252 IU/L, total bilirubin 2.5 mg/dL, albumin 3.1 g/dL, and prothrombin time-international normalized ratio 1.44). Tumor markers were markedly elevated, with a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 800 ng/mL and a cancer antigen 15 − 3 (CA15-3) level of 44.2 U/mL. In contrast, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II) levels were within normal range.

On CT, the liver demonstrated blunted margins and an irregular surface, consistent with cirrhosis-like morphology, and was accompanied by massive ascites. Non-contrast CT revealed heterogeneous low attenuation of the hepatic parenchyma with patchy hypoattenuating areas. Contrast-enhanced CT showed heterogeneous parenchymal enhancement without identifiable discrete nodules or mass-forming lesions (Fig. 1). MRI demonstrated a cirrhosis-like morphology. On pre-contrast fat-suppressed T1-weighted images, the hepatic parenchyma showed heterogeneous low signal intensity. Dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging revealed numerous hypovascular areas in the early arterial phase, with gradual enhancement in the transitional phase. In the hepatobiliary phase of gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI, no definite hypointense lesions were identified; however, delayed parenchymal uptake was observed, potentially reflecting impaired hepatic function. On fat-suppressed T2-weighted images, the hepatic parenchyma showed mild diffuse hyperintensity. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrated diffuse hyperintensity throughout both hepatic lobes without a definite reduction in the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. FDG-PET/CT showed no abnormal FDG uptake suggestive of hepatic metastases (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, pseudocirrhosis was initially suspected; however, given the overall clinical context, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis was considered. Therefore, transjugular liver biopsy was performed.

Histological examination revealed diffuse infiltration of tumor cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, arranged in trabecular and nested patterns. The tumor cells exhibited eccentric nuclei and mucin-containing cytoplasm; however, stromal mucin deposition was minimal. This atypical morphology was more consistent with invasive ductal carcinoma than with mucinous carcinoma. Nodular metastatic foci were predominantly identified in the centrilobular region, with additional sinusoidal and portal infiltrations. These infiltrative growth patterns were associated with diffuse stromal fibrosis throughout the hepatic parenchyma, consistent with metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed positivity for estrogen receptor (> 95%, score 3b), negativity for progesterone receptor (score 0), and a HER2 immunohistochemistry score of 1+. The tumor cells were also positive for GATA3, suggesting a breast cancer origin. The Ki-67 labeling index was 19%. Expression of organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B3 (OATP1B3) was preserved in hepatocytes (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

Metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis is a rare but aggressive manifestation of diffuse hepatic metastasis characterized by sinusoidal and portal infiltration of tumor cells, leading to rapidly progressive liver failure [1–3]. Breast cancer is the most frequently documented primary tumor [2–6]. In our case, no discrete hepatic nodules were identified on CT or MRI, and FDG-PET/CT demonstrated no abnormal FDG uptake despite extensive tumor infiltration. This apparent false-negative FDG-PET finding may be explained by the infiltrative growth pattern of metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis. Diffuse sinusoidal and portal infiltration result in a relatively low tumor cell density per imaging voxel and a lack of mass-forming lesions, potentially limiting detectable FDG accumulation. Furthermore, extensive stromal fibrosis and sinusoidal obstruction may impair hepatic microcirculation, thereby reducing tracer delivery. These factors may contribute to FDG-PET/CT limited sensitivity in this setting. A similar mechanism may also explain the absence of a marked decrease in ADC values on diffusion-weighted imaging. The hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging may be partly attributable to the T2 shine-through effect [7]. However, the relatively mild T2 hyperintensity suggests that this alone may not fully explain the findings. Diffuse infiltration of tumor cells into the hepatic parenchyma, along with the relatively low tumor cellularity associated with this process, may also contribute to less pronounced diffusion restriction. Previous studies have reported a moderate inverse correlation between ADC values and tumor cellularity across various tumor types [8].

Differentiating this entity from pseudocirrhosis is clinically important. Pseudocirrhosis typically develops in patients with known liver metastases undergoing chemotherapy, and is thought to result from tumor regression, scarring, fibrosis, and hepatic parenchymal regeneration [9]. Its disease course is generally slow, and imaging frequently demonstrates clustered fibrotic scars corresponding to treated metastases. In contrast, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis arises from diffuse tumor infiltration associated with stromal fibrosis [10]. Discrete metastatic lesions are often not clearly identified, and the condition is characterized by rapid morphological changes accompanied by progressive liver dysfunction. In this case, histological examination confirmed extensive tumor infiltration of the portal tracts and hepatic sinusoids with prominent fibrosis dissecting the parenchyma, supporting the diagnosis. The prognosis for metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis is extremely poor. Hoshina et al. reported that more than 60% of patients with breast cancer–related metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis died within 30 days of diagnosis [11]. Severe hepatic dysfunction often precludes systemic chemotherapy, significantly limiting the treatment options. The rapid clinical deterioration observed in our patient aligned with these observations.

In conclusion, metastatic carcinomatous cirrhosis should be considered in patients with malignancies who rapidly develop progressive liver dysfunction and cirrhosis-like morphological changes on imaging, even in the absence of detectable hepatic metastases. Recognizing this entity, which reflects diffuse tumor infiltration rather than treatment-related hepatic remodeling, is essential, as it is associated with an extremely poor prognosis.
