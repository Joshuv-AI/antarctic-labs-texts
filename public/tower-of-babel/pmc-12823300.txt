Population Pharmacokinetics of Asundexian in People at Risk for Thromboembolic/Cardiovascular Events
Authors: Ashraf Yassen, Friederike Kanefendt, Jochen Zisowsky, Astrid Broeker, Hardi Mundl, Peter Vis, Dirk Garmann, Jan Berkhout
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAsundexian is a potent, selective, and reversible inhibitor of activated clotting Factor XI currently under development for secondary prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke in the ongoing Phase III OCEANIC‐STROKE study (NCT05686070). Here, we report the development of a population pharmacokinetic (popPK) model for asundexian. Plasma concentration data were available from 2914 participants enrolled in nine Phase I and II studies of asundexian. The pharmacokinetics (PK) of asundexian were well described by the popPK model. Within the investigated dose range of asundexian 10–100 mg once daily, the PK of asundexian was dose‐proportional. The systemic apparent clearance (CL/F) of asundexian was estimated to be 2.25 L/h and the central volume of distribution (V C/F) was 35.3 L. Body weight, age, sex, concomitant administration of cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) inhibitors, and renal function were identified as statistically significant covariates influencing the PK of asundexian. After accounting for differences in the distribution of these covariates, the PK of asundexian was comparable in healthy participants and participants at risk for thromboembolic/cardiovascular events. Similarly, no significant differences in PK were noted among participants with atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, or acute myocardial infarction. No clinically relevant covariates were identified that would warrant dose adjustments in various special populations of interest, including those defined by body weight, age, sex, and renal function, for the prevention of secondary ischemic strokes.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, clinical trials, covariates, phase I, phase II, population pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Factor XI (FXI) is an intrinsic coagulation pathway protein with growing evidence suggesting that it may have an essential role in thrombosis [1, 2]. Unlike coagulation factors, which are part of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, recent research suggests that FXI has a minor role in primary hemostasis compared with its role in thrombus formation [2]. Human congenital FXI deficiency is generally associated with mild and injury‐related bleeding, which suggests that the FXI pathway is less important for normal hemostasis in vivo [3]. In addition, some studies have shown that elevated plasma levels of FXI may contribute to thromboembolic disease [4, 5, 6], and that inhibition of activated FXI (FXIa) has antithrombotic efficacy in various animal models of arterial, venous, and cerebral thrombosis [7, 8, 9]. The PACIFIC‐STROKE trial enrolled patients with acute non‐cardioembolic ischemic stroke who were receiving standard antiplatelet therapy to evaluate the efficacy and safety of asundexian. In the exploratory analyses, asundexian 50 mg reduced the relative risk of recurrent symptomatic ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA) compared with placebo by 36%. In addition, asundexian 50 mg also reduced the composite of recurrent ischemic stroke and TIA compared with placebo in the subgroup of participants with any degree of extracranial or intracranial atherosclerotic disease by 61% [10]. Furthermore, exposure–response analyses from the three PACIFIC studies [10, 11, 12] demonstrated no significant association between asundexian exposure and major or clinically relevant non‐major bleeding [13]. In addition, beneficial effects of inhibition of FXI or FXIa in the prevention of venous thromboembolic events in people undergoing total knee replacement have been reported [14, 15, 16, 17]. Therefore, there is an increasing interest in developing and testing novel anticoagulants that target FXIa, with the aim of establishing a new treatment option for the prevention and/or treatment of thromboembolic diseases with lower bleeding risk compared with the currently available therapeutic options.

In particular, people who require oral anticoagulation treatment are at risk of bleeding; for example, those with multiple comorbidities, people with renal or hepatic impairment, or the elderly (≥ 75 years of age). For these populations, some challenges may arise from the need for dose adjustments or even the requirement to stop anticoagulant therapy. This may be due to variability in pharmacokinetics (PK), which is driven by demographic and clinical characteristics and the use of concomitant medications [18, 19]. Asundexian is a novel, selective oral inhibitor of FXIa currently under investigation for the prevention of secondary ischemic stroke in people on standard antiplatelet therapy following an acute non‐cardioembolic ischemic stroke or high‐risk TIA [20]. In this context, the clinical PK properties of asundexian are characterized by near‐complete oral bioavailability with a dose‐proportional increase in the exposure of asundexian over doses of 25–100 mg once daily (OD) in healthy volunteers [21, 22, 23]. In addition, the PK of asundexian seems to be independent of time after multiple dosing [24]. Asundexian is eliminated from plasma with a mean terminal half‐life (t 1/2) of approximately 13.9–19.2 h after oral administration in healthy participants [21, 25], with a low apparent plasma clearance (CL/F) after oral dosing of about 3.19–4.21 L/h estimated based on non‐compartmental analysis [26]. Primary metabolic pathways for asundexian involve hydrolysis via carboxylesterase 1 (~47%) and, to a lesser extent, oxidative biotransformation via cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4; ~13%). Asundexian is predominantly excreted via the hepatobiliary pathway (80%), with 28% and 9% excreted as unchanged drug via feces and urine, respectively. In plasma, asundexian is the main entity and only the pharmacologically inactive metabolite M10 was considered a major metabolite [25]. Renal clearance of asundexian is almost in the range of the product of fraction unbound in plasma and the glomerular filtration rate, assuming glomerular filtration or a well‐balanced sum of excretion and reabsorption to be the main mechanism for renal elimination [27].

The clinical pharmacology program for asundexian encompasses a broad range of Phase I studies in special populations that have provided valuable insights into the effect of age, sex, and renal and hepatic function, as well as the effect of ethnicity on the PK of asundexian [23, 24]. In addition to various Phase I studies, plasma concentration data from participants at risk of thromboembolic/cardiovascular events enrolled in the PACIFIC Phase II clinical development program [10, 11, 12] were included in this integrated population PK (popPK) analysis, with the aim to assess potential covariates that may influence the PK of asundexian in participants who experienced non‐cardioembolic ischemic strokes.

Methods

Study Population

This popPK analysis was conducted using data pooled from nine clinical studies (Table S1): six Phase I studies in healthy participants and volunteers with relevant medical conditions, and three Phase II studies in (a) participants who experienced an acute non‐cardioembolic ischemic stroke (PACIFIC‐STROKE), (b) participants with a recent acute myocardial infarction (PACIFIC‐AMI), and (c) participants with atrial fibrillation (PACIFIC‐AF). Across all studies, oral doses of asundexian ranged from 10 to 100 mg. Extensive PK sampling was conducted in all Phase I studies and sparse PK data were collected in the PACIFIC Phase II studies. Details on the dosing regimens, number of subjects, and PK sampling schemes for all studies included in this analysis are provided in Table 1. All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of Good Clinical Practice. Study protocols and amendments were approved by independent ethics committees. All participants provided written informed consent prior to study enrollment.

Asundexian Assay

Plasma concentrations of asundexian were measured using validated high‐pressure liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry detection [28]. The chromatographic separation method was transferred and cross‐validated for use in different laboratories. The calibration range for asundexian was from 5.00 μg/L (lower limit of quantification) to 5000 μg/L (upper limit of quantification). Mean inter‐assay accuracy varied between 96.0% and 103.5% and precision was ≤ 7.0%. Quality control samples in the concentration range from 15.0 to 3750 μg/L and from 15.0 to 20,000 μg/L were determined with an accuracy of 96.0%–105% and a precision of 1.6%–10.3%, respectively, for asundexian across studies (Table S2). There were 1.62% observations below the lower limit of quantification (Table S3). These observations were excluded from the analysis dataset.

Data Analysis and popPK Modeling Approach

This popPK analysis was performed by means of non‐linear mixed effects modeling as implemented in the NONMEM software package (version 7.5, Icon Development Solutions, Ellicott City, Maryland, USA) in combination with Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN) (version 5.3.0). GFortran (version 9.4.0) was used as a compiler [29, 30, 31]. Diagnostic graphics, exploratory analyses, and post‐processing of NONMEM outputs were performed using R (version 4.2., The R foundation for Statistical Computing), within RStudio (version 2022.07.1 + 554, RStudio Inc., Boston, USA) [32].

Base Model Development

To characterize variability in PK and evaluate the effect of relevant covariates in participants treated with asundexian, an integrated popPK model was developed. The starting model was a one‐compartment linear disposition model with first‐order absorption. Various disposition and absorption models were considered, including zero and/or first‐order absorption models, first‐order with lag time, and transit model compartments. Model development, including the selection of random effect structure, was performed by the criteria described in the Model Qualification section (section 2.7) and was guided by graphical exploration (e.g., individual and population‐level concentration–time plots, dose‐normalized plots, or specific visualizations focusing on the observed absorption phase) of the available data. The random inter‐individual variability (IIV) was modeled assuming an exponential (lognormal) variance model. The residual error was modeled as additive (on a log scale).

Forward Inclusion and Backward Deletion Procedure

Following the development of the base popPK model, the covariate analysis was performed. All pre‐defined covariates (age, body weight [BW], body mass index, race, sex, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR; calculated using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration formula {CKD‐EPI}], serum creatinine, bilirubin, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, CYP3A4 inhibitors, CYP3A4 inducers, history of myocardial infarction, and history of heart failure) were added individually to the base model, and tested on CL/F and apparent central volume of distribution (V C/F). The continuous covariates were incorporated in a power model, normalized to a reference value of a typical subject, and categorical covariates were incorporated using indicator variables, in which each category had its own fixed‐effects parameter value.

Covariates decreasing the objective function value (OFV) by less than 6.63 (p < 0.01; 1 degree of freedom [df] for Chi‐squared [χ 2] distribution) were considered non‐significant and not included in the covariate modeling procedure. As a second step, remaining covariates were ranked according to the size of the OFV drop observed during the first step. These covariates were added to the model. When strongly correlated covariates (including size‐related covariates) were present, those demonstrating the largest reduction in the OFV were selected for inclusion in the model, while other correlated variables were excluded. If adding a covariate caused the OFV to decrease by at least 6.63 points, it remained in the model. If the decrease was smaller than 6.63 points, the covariate was excluded, and the next covariate in order was tested. This process continued until all significant covariates from the univariate analysis were tested, resulting in the final full covariate model. As a third step, a backward deletion was performed, in which all covariates that had been added during the forward inclusion procedure were removed sequentially from the full covariate model, starting with the covariate that had been included last. Statistical significance was assessed using a stricter significance level: each covariate was retained if, after its removal, the increase in OFV was more than 10.8 (p < 0.001, χ 2, 1 df). This was repeated until each covariate caused an increase of at least 10.8 points on removal.

Clinical Relevance of Covariates

The clinical relevance of statistically significant covariate effects was assessed through the summarization and/or graphical representation of individual‐specific exposure metrics, such as (dose‐normalized) area under the curve at steady state (AUCss), maximum plasma concentration at steady state (C max,ss), and trough plasma concentration at steady state (C trough,ss), as well as individual model parameter values versus covariates. To characterize the impact of the expected effect of covariates on asundexian exposure, simulations were performed based on the final integrated popPK model for asundexian 50 mg OD. For the simulations, 10,000 virtual subjects were generated, which were different from the reference subject (typical subject with median [continuous] covariates and/or most prevalent [categorical] covariates) by changing one covariate value and including parameter uncertainty. Finally, AUCss and C max,ss were derived and divided by AUCss or C max,ss, respectively, corresponding to the reference subject, resulting in 10,000 geometric mean ratios for which summary statistics were calculated. To illustrate the impact of covariates, forest plots of the geometric mean ratios relative to the reference subject and 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of AUCss and C max,ss were constructed.

Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR)

To evaluate the uncertainty of the popPK parameter estimates, the SIR method implemented in PsN was applied [33]. A total of 1000 importance samples were generated from the parameter posterior distribution, followed by 500 resamples to construct empirical CIs. The SIR procedure allowed robust estimation of parameter uncertainty, including IIV and residual error, without relying on asymptotic approximations. Parameter summaries, including the median and 95% CIs, were derived from the resampled distributions.

Results

Population Characteristics

The dataset for the popPK analysis contained 16,599 asundexian PK observations collected from the 2914 participants enrolled in the nine clinical studies (Table S3). Of the 2914 participants, 69.4% were male. The age and BW of all participants ranged from 21 to 92 years and from 34.6 to 178 kg, respectively. There were differences in the age distribution across the multiple study populations. Participants in the PACIFIC‐AF study were older (mean age of 73.3 years) than participants from the PACIFIC‐STROKE and the PACIFIC‐AMI studies (mean age of 66.7 and 66.6 years, respectively; Table 2). Participants in the Phase I multiple‐dose study [22] were younger (mean age of 33.5 years) than participants enrolled in the PACIFIC program. In the PACIFIC‐AF study, the mean eGFR value (62.3 mL/min/1.73 m2) was lower than in participants in the PACIFIC‐STROKE (79.2 mL/min/1.73 m2) or PACIFIC‐AMI (74.6 mL/min/1.73 m2) studies (Table 2). In addition, there was a difference in mean BW between non‐Asian and Asian participants in the PACIFIC Phase II studies (83.5 kg in non‐Asian vs. 65.3 kg in Asian participants; Table S4). PopPK Model Development

Asundexian plasma concentration data were adequately described by a linear PK model. The transit absorption model described the observed delayed and prolonged absorption pattern of asundexian. Accordingly, a two‐compartment PK model with two transit absorption compartments, first‐order absorption, and linear elimination was selected as the final model (Table S5). To address model instability due to overparameterization (due to the imbalance in the number of PK samples across the different studies), the major popPK model refinement assumed equal volumes of distribution of the central and peripheral compartments. In addition, the popPK model included IIV terms on systemic apparent clearance CL/F, V C/F, and absorption rate constant (K a). Table 4 lists the parameter estimates of the final popPK model along with the means, relative standard error, shrinkage (as obtained from NONMEM), and median and 95% CIs derived from SIR. All fixed and random‐effect parameters were estimated with good precision (relative standard error < 24%) and with low‐to‐moderate variability, except for the covariance between IIV‐V C and IIV‐K a. Goodness‐of‐fit plots showed good correlation between observed concentrations versus population and individual predicted concentrations of asundexian. Conditional weighted residuals were randomly distributed around zero without specific trends across the range of time and population‐predicted concentrations (Figures S1–S10). pcVPC plots for the PACIFIC studies showed that median, 5th percentile, and 95th percentile profiles of observed data were well captured within the 95% CIs of the respective profiles of predicted data, which further supported the finding that the final model described the data and variability well in PACIFIC participants (Figure 1). In addition, the pcVPC plots showed that the popPK model adequately predicted the elimination phase following single and multiple dosing, but slightly underestimated C max following multiple‐dose administration in healthy volunteers (Figure 2).

Covariate Model

BW, age, and sex were found to be statistically significant predictors of CL/F and V C/F, with typical CL/F and V C/F values increasing with higher BW and decreasing with participant age. Females had a lower CL/F by 12.1%, and a 14.0% lower V C/F than males. eGFR and concomitant use of combined CYP3A4 inhibitors (predominantly weak and moderate) also had a statistically significant impact on asundexian CL/F.

In healthy participants receiving asundexian 50 mg OD, the geometric mean AUCss was 13,800 μg h/L [22], while in the PACIFIC‐STROKE study, the geometric mean AUCss was 23,941 μg h/L following administration of asundexian 50 mg OD (Table 5). Differences in the exposure between healthy participants and study participants with underlying disease can be explained by differences in BW, age, sex, eGFR, and concomitant use of CYP3A4 inhibitors. Asundexian CL/F increased with higher BW. A typical participant who weighed 110 kg (95th BW percentile) had a 13.8% higher CL/F than a participant who weighed 79 kg (median BW). For a participant with a BW of 55 kg (5th BW percentile), the CL/F was 13.5% lower than with a participant with a BW of 79 kg. Age also had a statistically significant effect on CL/F. Compared with a 68‐year‐old participant (median age), CL/F was 7.7% lower in older participants (82 years of age; 95th age percentile) and 16% higher in younger participants (48 years of age; 5th age percentile). Compared with a typical subject with a median eGFR of 77 mL/min/1.73 m2, CL/F was 10.4% lower in participants with an eGFR of 42.4 mL/min/1.73 m2 (5th eGFR percentile) and 5.7% higher in participants with an eGFR of 104 mL/min/1.73 m2 (95th eGFR percentile). Concomitant use of CYP3A4 inhibitors decreased CL/F by 5.3% and no statistically significant improvement was observed when moderate and weak inhibitors were tested separately. In addition to these covariates, there was a small absolute difference in CL/F observed between study participants with underlying disease compared with healthy participants (0.04 L/h lower, p‐value of < 0.05) with a decrease in IIV of 0.5%. Despite this statistically significant difference, the 2% reduction in CL/F is considered not clinically relevant (e.g., > 25% change) and was therefore not accounted for in the final popPK model.

Comparison of the popPK post hoc estimates for asundexian exposure (AUCss, C max,ss, and C trough,ss) showed that the post hoc exposure estimates across the three PACIFIC studies (Figure S11) were variable. Similarly, geometric mean C max,ss and C trough,ss were higher in PACIFIC‐AF than in PACIFIC‐STROKE and PACIFIC‐AMI. The exposure in PACIFIC‐AMI was closer to PACIFIC‐STROKE, with a geometric mean AUCss of 23,185 μg h/L (Table 5). The study population was not a statistically significant covariate and PK variability between the three PACIFIC studies could be explained by BW, age, sex, and eGFR. No other covariates, including ethnicity, history of heart failure, or myocardial infarction, were identified as statistically significant sources of IIV in the PK of asundexian.

The estimated t 1/2 also showed slight differences between healthy participants and study participants with underlying disease. Among non‐Asian healthy participants from the Phase I studies, the mean t 1/2 (95% CI) was 15.8 h (12.3–19.3), while in participants from the PACIFIC‐STROKE, it was 22.4 h (15.2–33.7) (Table S6).

Discussion

The PK of asundexian in healthy volunteers, as well as participants included in the Phase II studies, was linear and adequately described with a two‐compartment PK model.

This popPK analysis confirmed that age, sex, BW, and renal function significantly influenced the PK of asundexian, consistent with results from Phase I clinical pharmacology studies [22, 23, 24]. In the Phase I age and sex study, elderly participants showed a 28.8% higher AUC than healthy younger participants (18–45 years of age), aligning with a 23.7% increase in CL/F with older age in the popPK analysis [24]. In the same study, an 8.4% higher AUCss was observed in female participants compared with healthy male participants [24]. In addition, in healthy participants, the popPK‐derived post hoc mean estimate for CL/F was 3.68 L/h, closely matching the non‐compartmental analysis results from the Phase I single and multiple‐dose studies, which estimated the CL/F to be between 3.19 and 4.21 L/h [22, 26]. These findings highlight the predictive performance of the popPK across different populations, including both healthy participants as well as those at risk for thromboembolic or cardiovascular events.

The impact of renal function on asundexian clearance observed in this study also corroborates findings from earlier Phase I studies involving participants with varying degrees of renal impairment [24]. To this end, in Phase I studies, total AUC increased, on average, by 7.8% and 38.6% in participants in the eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 and eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 categories, respectively, compared with those with an eGFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 [24]. Similarly, the covariate analysis in this study showed an average decrease in CL/F of 23.9% and 34.4% for participants with eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 and eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, compared with those with an eGFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Among participants with atrial fibrillation (AF), the calculated AUCss was approximately two‐fold higher than with healthy participants. The Phase I population was mainly younger, healthy male participants and the population with AF was typically older and had a lower renal function compared with participants who experienced a recent acute non‐cardioembolic ischemic stroke or an acute myocardial infarction [10, 11, 12]. In addition, in the PACIFIC‐AF study, a higher percentage of females was included compared with the PACIFIC‐AMI study (Table 3) [11, 12]. While this analysis revealed differences in AUCss and C max,ss between healthy participants and study participants with underlying disease, these differences are not indicative of fundamental PK differences between younger healthy people and older people with comorbidities. These differences in asundexian exposure between Phase I and II populations, as well as across the Phase II trials, can be largely attributed to differences in the distribution of PK‐relevant covariates. Thus, the difference in exposure is more reflective of participant characteristics than disease etiology‐driven changes in the PK of asundexian.

Based on Phase I studies, 22.7% and 29.0% higher dose‐normalized AUC was observed in healthy male Chinese and Japanese participants, respectively, than in healthy male non‐Asian participants [23]. In the PACIFIC program, there was also a small difference in exposure between Asian and non‐Asian participants. AUC exposures were approximately 7% higher in the PACIFIC‐AMI and PACIFIC‐STROKE studies, and 22% higher in the PACIFIC‐AF study in Asian compared with non‐Asian participants (Figure 3). Covariate analysis did not reveal any ethnic difference in the PK of asundexian between Asian and non‐Asian participants, suggesting that differences in exposure may be explained by differences in BW and age in both healthy participants and Phase II study participants. While the predicted asundexian exposure on average was higher in Asian versus non‐Asian participants, the individual estimated exposure values for AUCss were similar between Asian and non‐Asian participants when differences in BW were considered. These findings highlight the robustness of these covariates across different study phases and populations. As participants in race categories described as “Black or African American”, “American Indian/Alaska Native”, or “other” individually constituted less than 1% of the total population in the PACIFIC program, identification of robust covariate relationships for individual race categories was not feasible. Therefore, all non‐Asian participants were contained within the “White” race group for the covariate analysis.

The popPK analysis could not completely confirm the magnitude of the effect of concomitant use of CYP3A4 inhibitor(s) as seen in the Phase I drug–drug interaction studies. In the human mass balance study, 13% of asundexian excretion was attributed to CYP3A4‐mediated metabolism, while 37% was consequent to the excretion of unchanged drug with P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) contribution [25]. In Phase I clinical pharmacology studies, 103% and 75.6% increases in AUC were observed when asundexian was co‐administered with combined P‐gp and strong or moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors, respectively, whereas no effect was observed for moderate CYP3A4 inhibition only [27]. Although concomitant use of weak, moderate, or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors during the study was evaluated as a covariate in this popPK analysis, the magnitude of the covariate effect was small (5.3% reduction in CL/F). Reasons that may explain the differences in the magnitude of the effect of CYP3A4 inhibitors between the Phase I and II studies include: (i) that the environment of Phase II studies is generally less well controlled than Phase I drug–drug interaction cross‐over studies, that is, unlike Phase I studies, in which dose schedules and sample collection times are optimized to maximize the likelihood of capturing the full extent of a potential drug–drug interaction, Phase II studies typically lack detailed documentation on the exact timing of concomitant medication intake. Also, the exact clock time of co‐medication is not documented in Phase II studies, and Phase I studies are designed to show the maximum effect based on optimized co‐administration (time/exposure) of “perpetrator” and “victim”; (ii) only a few participants in the PACIFIC Phase II studies received strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, as these drugs were part of the participant exclusion criteria in the PACIFIC Phase II studies; and (iii) pooling moderate and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors together with weak CYP3A4 inhibitors into a single covariate category. The decision was made due to the low number of participants receiving strong (0.62%) or moderate (10.5%) CYP3A4 inhibitors in the PACIFIC studies, in which more than 50% received weak CYP3A4 inhibitors, for which no relevant effect was expected.

In conclusion, no relevant PK differences were observed between healthy participants and Phase II study participants with different underlying diseases. Differences in asundexian exposure can be primarily attributed to variations in key PK‐relevant covariates, with participants in the Phase II studies generally being older and having reduced renal function compared with healthy participants. Importantly, participants in the Phase II trials with different underlying etiologies for thromboembolic events demonstrate similar PK profiles once differences in the PK‐relevant covariate distributions are accounted for, underscoring the role of demographic and physiological factors in driving PK variability observed for asundexian rather than disease‐specific etiology. Age, sex, BW, and renal function explain differences in exposure between participants; however, the changes in exposure are less than 25% relative to a typical person for each of the individual covariates and are, therefore, unlikely to necessitate dose adjustments of asundexian in subpopulations of special interest.
