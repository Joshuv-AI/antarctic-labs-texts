On the dimension of pullback attractors in recurrent neural networks
Muhammed Fadera 1

Abstract
Recurrent neural networks trained via the reservoir computing paradigm have demonstrated remarkable success in learning and reconstructing attractors from chaotic systems, often replicating quantities such as Lyapunov exponents and fractal dimensions. It has recently been conjectured that this is because the reservoir computer embeds the dynamics of the chaotic system in its state space before learning. This conjecture has been established for reservoir computers with linear activation functions and remains open for more general reservoir systems. In this work, we employ a non-autonomous dynamical systems approach to establish an upper bound for the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor, a subset of the reservoir state space that is approximated during training and prediction phases. We prove that the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor is bounded above by the box-counting dimension of the space of input sequences with respect to the product topology. In particular, for input sequences originating from an N in N_{\text{in}} -dimensional smooth dynamical system or their generic continuously differentiable observations, the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor is bounded above by N in N_{\text{in}} . The results obtained here highlight the fact that, while a reservoir computer may possess a very high-dimensional state space, it exhibits effective low-dimensional dynamics. Our findings also partly explain why reservoir computers are successful in tasks such as attractor reconstruction and the computation of dynamic invariants like Lyapunov exponents and fractal dimensions.

Reservoir computing (RC) framework has offered a computationally efficient way to train recurrent neural networks by randomly generating the internal weights and keeping them fixed while focusing training only on the output layer

. Since a large class of RCs are universal approximators of fading memory mappings on left infinite input sequences

[

,

[

[

[

Beyond the theory of universal approximations, it is unclear how RCs are able to achieve such performance on these tasks. In the past few years, efforts have been made to understand the decision-making process of such systems by studying them as input-driven dynamical systems
[

,
7

14

[

,
8

that echo state networks (ESNs) trained with output feedback take advantage of stable fixed points and their relative locations to solve such tasks. As well as in the continuous output case, errors in ESNs trained on such tasks have been identified with the emergence of spurious/untrained attractors
[

,
8
]
. Transitions to such attractors may cause errors over short time steps in classification problems with discrete-valued inputs and over longer time steps in continuous output problems
[
14
]
. Even with such erroneous transitions, it is known, for example, in tasks such as attractor reconstruction, that RCs can maintain the general dynamics of the system they were trained on well beyond when their predictions start to diverge. This property of RCs allows them to learn dynamic invariants, such as Lyapunov exponents and fractal dimensions, of the system they were trained on
[

,

,

Although it is known that the asymptotic dynamics of a reservoir computer synchronises with those of the driving input dynamics
[

, this synchronisation may not be sufficient for explaining the performance of reservoir computers

. In
[

[

In this paper, we focus on establishing bounds on the dimension of the synchronised dynamics of the reservoir when driven with a certain class of input sequences. In particular, we investigate the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor in the nonautonomous dynamical system arising from a large class of RCs of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}[n+1]=g(\mathbf{u}[n+1],\mathbf{x}[n])

(1)

2.4
RNNs as nonautonomous dynamical system

The input-driven dynamics of RNNs are best understood in the lens of nonautonomous dynamical systems
[
29
]
. There are two main ways of dealing with nonautonomous dynamical systems: as a process or a skew product dynamical system
[
29
]

The dynamics in the space of input sequences

\displaystyle\pi_{n}:\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\to U,

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}[n].

Let

\displaystyle\theta:\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{u}[n]\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\to\{\mathbf{u}[n+1]\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

be the left shift operator. The left shift operator is continuous (with respect to the product topology) since each of its projections

to

Furthermore, it is also invertible with inverse

Thus

\displaystyle\theta^{m+n}(\mathbf{u})=\theta^{m}\circ\theta^{n}(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\>\text{and for all }\>\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

Now define the skew product flow on

\displaystyle\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x):=g(\pi_{0}(\theta^{n}(\mathbf{u})),\Phi(n-1,\mathbf{u},x))=g(\mathbf{u}[n],\Phi(n-1,\mathbf{u},x))\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

\mathbf{\Psi}:C(\mathcal{U},X)\to C(\mathcal{U},X)
be the map
H\to\Phi(1,\theta^{0}(\cdot),H(\theta^{-1}(\cdot))
. We will show that
\mathbf{\Psi}
is a
\mu
-contraction on
C(\mathcal{U},X)
. Let
F,H\in C(\mathcal{U},X)
. Then

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{\Psi}(F),\mathbf{\Psi}(H))

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}||\Phi(1,\mathbf{u},H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))-\Phi(1,\mathbf{u},F(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))||

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}||g(\mathbf{u}[1],\Phi(0,\mathbf{u},H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))))-g(\mathbf{u}[1],\Phi(0,\mathbf{u},F(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))||

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}||g(\mathbf{u}[1],H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))-g(\mathbf{u}[1],F(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))||

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\mu\cdot||H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))-F(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))||

\displaystyle=\mu\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}||H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))-F(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))||

\displaystyle=\mu\rho(F,H).

So
\mathbf{\Psi}
is a
\mu
-contraction on
C(\mathcal{U},X)
. Since
C(\mathcal{U},X)
is a complete metric space, by Banach fixed-point theorem,
\mathbf{\Psi}
has a unique fixed point in
C(\mathcal{U},X)
. That is, there exists a unique continuous map
H\in C(\mathcal{U},X)
such that
\mathbf{\Psi}(H)=H
.
∎

Now we will show that the sequence
\mathbf{x}=\{H(\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is the unique entire solution associated to
\mathbf{u}
. Consequently, by (
12
),
\mathbf{A}=\{H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u}))\}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}
is the unique pullback attractor of the cocycle
(\theta,\Phi)
. This also means that when training RCs, for any choice of initial condition, the dynamics of the reservoir will become close to the fibres of the pullback attractor after a long washout. The rate of contraction to the pullback attractor is controlled by
\mu
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
2.1
, the sequence
\mathbf{x}=\{H(\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is the unique entire solutions associated to
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Fix
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and let
n,m\in\mathbb{Z},n\geq m
. We note that

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{u})=\Phi(1,\mathbf{u},H(\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})))

by Theorem
2.1
. We first show that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}[n]=\Phi(k,\theta^{n-k}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}[n-k]).

(14)

We argue by induction on
k
. The case
k=0
follows from the definition of a cocycle. For
k=1
,

\displaystyle\Phi(

\displaystyle 1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}[n-1])

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),H(\theta^{n-2}(\mathbf{u}))),

\displaystyle=H(\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}))=\mathbf{x}[n].

Assume (
14
) holds for some
k\geq 0
. Let
\mathbf{z}=\{H(\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{v}))\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be the entire solution associated to
\mathbf{v}=\theta^{-1}(\mathbf{u})
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi(k+1,\>

\displaystyle\theta^{n-(k+1)}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}[n-(k+1)])

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),\Phi(k,\theta^{n-(k+1)}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}[n-(k+1)])\quad\text{by the cocycle property }(\ref{eq:cocycle_property}),\>

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),\Phi(k,\theta^{n-(k+1)}(\mathbf{u}),H(\theta^{n-1-(k+1)}(\mathbf{u})),

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),\Phi(k,\theta^{n-k}(\mathbf{v}),H(\theta^{n-k-1}(\mathbf{v})))),

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}),\Phi(k,\theta^{n-k}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{z}[n-k])),

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\theta^{n}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{z}[n])\quad\text{by the inductive hypothesis,}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{z}[n+1],

\displaystyle=H(\theta^{n}(\mathbf{v}))=H(\theta^{n-1}(\mathbf{u}))=\mathbf{x}[n].

Choosing
k=n-m
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}[n]=\Phi(n-m,\theta^{m}(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}[m]).

∎

3
Dimension of the pullback attractor

It is usually insightful to study properties of the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
in relation to the corresponding property of the driving input sequence or the space of input sequences. For example, it has been recently shown that the echo index of an input-sequence, a generalisation of ESP, can be related to the minimal number of repetitions in the input sequence
[
6
,
3
]
. It has also been shown that in training a reservoir computer to reconstruct the Lyapunov spectrum of a dynamical system, the most negative conditional Lyapunov exponent of the reservoir dynamics should be more negative than the most negative Lyapunov exponent of the driving dynamical system
[
21
]
. It was shown in
[
20
]
that a related condition is sufficient for the GS to be continuously differentiable. RCs have also been shown to display multifunctionality when trained on data from separate attractors
[
14
,
39
]
. In this section, we link the dimension of the pullback attractor of
(\theta,\Phi)
to the dimension of the space of input sequences. In particular, we establish upper and lower bounds for the dimension of the pullback attractor of
(\theta,\Phi)
based on the dimension of the state input sequences
\mathcal{U}
and its subsets, with a particular focus on subsets of the type
\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
.

We will use the box-counting dimension as it provides an upper bound for the other notions of dimension such as Hausdorff dimension
[
43
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Box-counting dimension)
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a compact metric space. For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
N(Z,\varepsilon)
be the minimum number of balls of radius
\varepsilon
needed to cover
Z
with centres in
Z
. Note that
N(Z,\varepsilon)
is finite for each
\varepsilon>0
since
Z
is compact. The (upper) box-counting dimension is defined as

\displaystyle\text{dim}_{B}Z=\lim\sup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log_{2}N(Z,\varepsilon)}{-\log_{2}\varepsilon}.

The box-counting dimension satisfies the following properties

1.

box-counting dimension of a singleton is
0
,

2.

the box-counting dimension of an open subset of an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space is
n
.

3.

if
(X,d_{X})
and
(Z,d_{Z})
are metric spaces and
f:X\to Z
is a Lipschitz continuous function with

\displaystyle d_{Z}(f(x_{2}),f(x_{1}))\leq Cd_{2}(x_{2},x_{1}),

then
\dim_{B}f(X)\leq\dim_{B}X
. In particular, if
f:X\to Z
is bi-Lipschitz, then
\dim_{B}f(X)=\dim_{B}X
.

3.1
Upper bound for finite dimensional input sequence spaces

There are various results on upper bounds for the dimension of the pullback attractor that can be applied to finite dimensional input sequence spaces
[
10
,
5
]
. Lower bounds, in general, require specific information about the the driving dynamical system such as the existence of a “physical measure”
[
52
]
or the existence of saddle-like fixed points
[
9
]
, the dimension of whose unstable manifold provides the lower bound. For our upper bound, we will use the result of Vishik and Chepyzhov
[
9
, Chapter IX, Theorem 1.2]
. Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{u}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}

(15)

be all points on the pullback attractor of the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
with input sequences in
\mathcal{V}
. We refer to the set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}
as the pullback attractor corresponding to input sequences in
\mathcal{V}
. We start with the statement of the result of Vishik and Chepyzhov and then prove that our skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
satisfies its assumptions if
g
is continously differentiable.

Theorem 3.1

(Vishik and Chepyzhov
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite-dimensional subset of
(\mathcal{U},d^{w})
. Suppose the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
satisfies the following assumptions.

VC1

Finite time Lipschitz continuous
on
\mathcal{V}

i.e
there exist a function
C:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for all
x\in X

\displaystyle||\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)-\Phi(n,\mathbf{v},x)||\leq C(n)d^{w}(\mathbf{u}_{[0,n]},\mathbf{v}_{[0,n]})

where

\mathbf{u}_{[0,n]}
is the projection of
\mathbf{u}
onto
U^{n+1}
i.e.
\mathbf{u}_{[0,n]}[i]=\mathbf{u}[i]
for
0\leq i\leq n
and
\mathbf{u}_{[0,n]}[i]=0\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{in}}}
otherwise.

VC2

Uniform quasidifferentiability
on
\mathcal{V}
: the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
is uniformly quasidifferentiable on
\mathcal{V}
if there exist a parameterised family of bounded linear maps
\{L\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)\}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V},n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},x\in X}
from
\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
such that

\displaystyle||\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)-\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},z)-L\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)(x-z)||\leq\gamma(n,x-z)||x-z||

where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V}
,
x,z\in X
and
\gamma(n,s)\to 0
as
||s||\to 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. The function
\gamma(n,s)
is independent of
x,z\in X
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V}
.

Then

\displaystyle\text{ dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V}}\text{dim}_{B}\>\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{u}}+\text{dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{V}.

If the skew product flow satisfies the echo state property, the following is a simple corollary of the above result and the fact that the box-counting dimension of a singleton is
0
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a finite dimensional subset of
\mathcal{U}
, and that the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
satisfies the ESP, then

\displaystyle\text{ dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}\leq\text{dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{V}.

Remark 2
.

The upper bound in Corollary
3.2
would also apply to the case when
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{u}}
is finite for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{V}
. Alternatively, the dimension of
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{u}}
can be bounded above by the singular values of the matrix of the linear map
L\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)

[
9
,
50
]
. In particular, suppose
M(n,\mathbf{u},x)
is the matrix of the linear map
L\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)
and
\alpha_{1}(n,\mathbf{u},x)\geq\alpha_{2}(\mathbf{u},n,x)\ldots\geq\alpha_{N_{r}}(\mathbf{u},n,x)\geq 0
are its singular values i.e. the square root of the eigenvalues of
M^{T}M
. Let the numbers
\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u})
,
j=1,\ldots,N_{r},
be defined as

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u})=\sup_{x\in X}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\alpha_{j}(\mathbf{u},n,x).

(16)

If
m(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the smallest integer such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m(\mathbf{u})+1}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u})<0
and
\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{u})\geq\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{u})\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{N_{r}}(\mathbf{u})
, then

\displaystyle\dim_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{u}}\leq m(\mathbf{u})+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m(u)}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u})}{-\lambda_{m(\mathbf{u})+1}(\mathbf{u})}.

If the
\limsup
in (
16
) can be replaced with a limit, the numbers

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u},x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\alpha_{j}(\mathbf{u},n,x),\>j=1,\ldots,N_{r},

are the so-called conditional Lyapunov exponents of
(\theta,\Phi)
when driven with
\mathbf{u}

[
50
,
42
]
.

We will now show that assumption
VC2
and
VC1
are satisfied by the skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
if
g
is continuously differentiable on
U\times X
and there exist
\mu,\eta>0
such that

\displaystyle||g(u,x)-g(v,z)||\leq\eta||u-v||+\mu||x-z||,\>\text{ for all }\>x,y\in X\>\text{and}\>u,v\in U.

(17)

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
g=(g^{1},\ldots,g^{N_{r}})
is continuously differentiable on
U\times X
and satisfies condition (
17
). Then skew product flow
(\theta,\Phi)
satisfies assumption
VC1
and assumption
VC2
.

Proof.

Starting with assumption
VC1
, it follows by the cocycle property and assumption (
17
) that

\displaystyle||\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x)-\Phi(n,\mathbf{v},x)||

\displaystyle\leq\eta||\mathbf{u}[n]-\mathbf{v}[n]||+\mu||\Phi(n-1,\mathbf{u},x)-\Phi(n-1,\mathbf{v},x)||,

\displaystyle\leq\eta||\mathbf{u}[n]-\mathbf{v}[n]||+\mu\eta||\mathbf{u}[n-1]-\mathbf{v}[n-1]||+\mu^{2}||\Phi(n-2,\mathbf{u},x)-\Phi(n-2,\mathbf{v},x)||,

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu^{i}||\mathbf{u}[n-i]-\mathbf{v}[n-i]||\>\text{ since}\>\Phi(0,\mathbf{u},x)=\Phi(0,\mathbf{v},x)=x,

\displaystyle\leq\eta\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\mu^{n-i}}{w_{i}}\right)\max_{j=0,\ldots n}w_{j}||\mathbf{u}[j]-\mathbf{v}[j]||,

\displaystyle=C(n)d^{w}(\mathbf{u}_{[0,n]},\mathbf{v}_{[0,n]}).

As for assumption
VC2
, we note that there is nothing to prove for the case
n=0
since
\Phi(0,\mathbf{u},x)=x
. Now, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the map
\Phi(n,\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{U}\times X\to X
is equivalent to the map
F:U^{n}\times X\to X
where

\displaystyle F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},x)=g(\mathbf{u}[n],g(\mathbf{u}[n-1],...,g(\mathbf{u}[1],x),\ldots))=\Phi(n,\mathbf{u},x),

\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}
,
x\in X
and
\underline{\mathbf{u}}=(\mathbf{u}[1],\ldots,\mathbf{u}[n])
. Since
g
is continuously differentiable on

,

is also continuously differentiable on

by the chain rule. We will take the linear map

to be
D_{2}F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},x)
, the partial derivative of

with respect to
x
. The function
\gamma(n,\xi)
can be defined as

\displaystyle\gamma(n,\xi)=\sup_{\underline{\mathbf{u}}\in U^{n}}\sup_{0<||x-z||\leq\xi}\frac{||F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},x)-F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},z)-D_{2}F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},x)(x-z)||}{||x-z||}.

Note that
\gamma
is well defined since both
U^{n}
and
X
are compact.

∎

Remark 3
.

If, in addition to
g
being continuously differentiable, both
X
and
U
are convex, then the Lipschitz condition (
17
) holds.

Theorem
3.1
can be applied to the input sequence space
\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
to get an upper bound on the box-counting dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}}
. This result is based on the fact that if
\phi
is bi-Lipschitz, then there exists a bi-Lipschitz embedding of
U
into
\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose there exist
C>1
such that for all
u,v\in U

\displaystyle||\phi(u)-\phi(v)||\leq C||u-v||\>\text{ and }\>||\phi^{-1}(u)-\phi^{-1}(v)||\leq C||u-v||.

Assume
(\theta,\Phi)
satisfy the ESP and
g
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
, then

\displaystyle\dim_{B}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}}\leq\dim_{B}U

Proof.

We will show that there exists a weighting sequence
w=\{w_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle\dim_{B}\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}=\dim_{B}U

and the result will follow by Theorem
3.1
, Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
.
Let
u\in U
and define
\Psi:U\to\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
such that

\displaystyle\Psi(u)=(\ldots,\phi^{-n}(u),\ldots,\phi^{-1}(u),u,\phi(u),\ldots,\phi^{n}(u),\ldots).

The map
\Psi
maps
u
to its trajectory with
\Psi(U)=\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
.
\Psi
is injective and continuous (since each projection is continuous) and thus a homeomorphism onto its image since
U
is compact. The inverse
\Psi^{-1}:\Psi(U)\to U
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{u}[0].

We define the weighting sequence
w:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to(0,1)
as
w_{n}=C^{-n}
.
Then

\displaystyle d^{w}(\Psi(u),\Psi(v))=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{|n|}d(\phi^{n}(u),\phi^{n}(v))

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{|n|}C^{|n|}d(u,v)=d(u,v).

The inverse
\Psi^{-1}:\Psi(U)\to U
also satisfies

\displaystyle d(\Psi(\mathbf{u}),\Psi(\mathbf{v}))=d(\mathbf{u}[0],\mathbf{v}[0])\leq d^{w}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

for any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}
. Thus
\Psi
is a bi-Lipschitz map from which we conclude that

∎

3.2
Lower bound and an infinite-dimensional input sequence space

As mentioned earlier, lower bound for the dimension of attractors, even for autonomous systems, usually require specific information about the dynamics of the system. We will attempt to establish a lower bound for the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor corresponding to the space of periodic input sequences of sufficiently large period in
\mathcal{U}=U^{\mathbb{Z}}
and by extension for
\mathcal{U}
itself. We prove in Theorem
3.7
that if the
U
interior of
U
is non-empty
i.e

U
contains an open set, then the pullback attractor corresponding to input sequences of period
m
will also contain an open set for
m
sufficiently large. Thus the pullback attractor has box-counting dimension
N_{r}
. This results is perhaps not surprising for the set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{U}}
since from (
13
),
H(\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{U}}
depends on infinite past histories of
\mathbf{u}
from the first term, where
\mathbf{u}
takes all possible values in an infinite-dimensional metric space
\mathcal{U}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.7
is based on the implicit function theorem
[
2
, Theorem 17.3]
and the local surjectivity Theorem
[
1
]
, which we now state.

Theorem 3.5

(Implicit function theorem)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open sets and
G:U\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuously differentiable function on
U\times X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Suppose
G(u_{0},x_{0})=0
for some
u_{0}\in U
and
x_{0}\in X
, and the Jacobian
D_{2}G(u_{0},x_{0})
is invertible. Then there exist an open set
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
u_{0}
and a unique continuously differentiable function
h:V\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

1.

h(u_{0})=x_{0}
and
G(u,h(u))=0
for every
u\in V
,

2.

The Jacobian of
h
is given by

\displaystyle Dh(u)=-[D_{2}G(u,h(u))]^{-1}[D_{1}G(u,h(u))].

Theorem 3.6

(Local surjectivity theorem)
.

Let
n\geq m
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuously differentiable function. Suppose there exists
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
Dh(u)
is surjective i.e. it has rank
m
. Then there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
u
and
W
of
h(u)
such that
h:U\to W
is surjective. In particular, if
V
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
Dh(u)
is surjective for all
u\in V
. Then
h
is an open mapping on
V
.

Remark 4
.

The local surjectivity theorem is related to the fact that submersions are locally projections and thus are open mappings. It has been recently shown that it holds for an even larger class of functions
[
45
]
so long as its set of critical values and critical points are not too big.

Now we state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
U
,
X
and
g
be as in Definition
2.1
. Additionally, we assume that
\interior\>U\neq\emptyset
and
g:U\times X\to X
is continuously differentiable. Let

be such that
mN_{\text{in}}\geq N_{r}
. Let
\underline{\mathbf{u}}=(\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{2},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m})\in U^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{\text{in}}}
and the function
F:U^{m}\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
defined by

\displaystyle F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},x)=g(\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{1},g(\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{2},\ldots,g(\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m},x)\ldots)),

(18)

satisfies the following conditions

G1

||D_{2}g(u,x)||_{2}<1
for all
(u,x)\in U\times X
.

G2

there exist
z\in X,\underline{\mathbf{v}}\in U^{m}
such that
z=F(\underline{\mathbf{v}},z)
and the linear map
D_{1}F(\underline{\mathbf{v}},z):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{\text{in}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}}
is surjective
i.e
it has rank
N_{r}
.

Let
(U^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}
be set of all
m
-periodic input sequences of the form

\displaystyle(\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m},\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m-1},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m},\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{m-1},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots).

Then
\mathcal{H}_{(U^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}}
contains an open set and thus has box-counting dimension
N_{r}
.

Remark on the conditions of Theorem
3.7
.

Condition
G1
implies that, for each
\underline{\mathbf{u}}\in U^{m}
fixed,
F(\underline{\mathbf{u}},\cdot)
is a contraction on

. Thus, by the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique fixed point

corresponding to
\underline{\mathbf{v}}
, i.e.

. Condition

requires that for some
\underline{\mathbf{v}}\in U^{m}
, we have that the partial derivative of

with respect to
\underline{\mathbf{u}}
has full rank when evaluated at
(\underline{\mathbf{v}},z)
. These two conditions are, in general, independent of each other.

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Let
y
be the unique solution to
F(\underline{\mathbf{w}},y)=y
for some fixed
\underline{\mathbf{w}}\in U^{m}
. From Theorem
2.2
,
y=H(\theta^{km}(\mathbf{w}))
for each

where
\mathbf{w}
is the periodic input sequence

\displaystyle(\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{m},\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{m-1},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{m},\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{m-1},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\ldots).

So for any subset
\mathbf{V}\subseteq U^{m}
, the set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}
,
\mathcal{V}=\mathbf{V}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of all such

’s as
\underline{\mathbf{w}}
takes values in
\mathbf{V}
is contained in
\mathcal{H}_{(U^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}}
. In particular,
\text{dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{H}_{(U^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}}\geq\text{dim}_{B}\>\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}

5
Discussions and conclusions

In this paper, we provide two important results for the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor of reservoir systems. First, using a result of Vishik and Chepyzhov
[
9
]
, we demonstrate that the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor of a driven reservoir system is bounded above by the box-counting dimension of the space of input sequences in the product topology. We use this result to show that, in particular, the pullback attractor corresponding to input sequences coming from a trajectory of bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
\phi
on a compact set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{in}}}
is bounded above by
\dim_{B}U
. It would be interesting to establish a lower bound for the box-counting dimension of the pullback attractor in this case. The main preliminary result used to prove lower bounds on the dimension of sets is Frostman Lemma
[
36
]
and its variants. The Frostman Lemma is based on a well-defined probability (or
\sigma
-finite) measure. However, it is unclear how one can define a measure on the pullback attractor. As is often the case, we can work in the extended state space
\mathcal{V}_{(U,\phi)}\times X
to get an autonomous dynamical system. Even then, an invariant measure on the input dynamics may be required for any well-defined product measure on the product space. Our experiments with the Lorenz and Rössler attractors suggest that, if
\mu
is the invariant measure on each of these attractors, the invariant measure on the extended space of the input and the reservoir is just a product of
\mu
with itself.

The second main result of this paper is that, if
U
contains an open set, the pullback attractor corresponding to input sequences in
U^{\mathbb{Z}}
always contains an open set, and so has dimension equal to that of the driven reservoir. The proof technique relied on a subset of input sequences composed of periodic input sequences whose period is larger than the dimension of the reservoir. This essentially reduces the problem to a fixed-point problem, where tools such as the implicit function theorem and the local surjectivity theorem can be applied.

A significant amount of work has already been done in training reservoirs on dynamical systems and utilising autonomous dynamics in the prediction phase to infer properties of the dynamical system, such as fractal dimension and Lyapunov exponents
[
41
]
, bifurcation and tipping points
[
40
,
31
]
, persistent homology
[
20
]
etc. The accuracy of these methods and how it relates to the dimension or number of neurons in the reservoir, as well as the hyperparameters of the network, such as the spectral radius, is yet to be studied. Although nonautonomous dynamical systems theory offers a way to link the dynamics of the input to those of the reservoir, it is not as fully developed as the theory of autonomous dynamical systems. Thus, tackling these questions in generality is a very challenging task. This reason is part of the motivation for the research conducted in this paper. Results of Section
4
suggest that for specific quantities, such as the box-counting dimension, it is not necessary to train the reservoir to obtain reasonable estimates. Indeed, if generalised synchronisation is an embedding, this can be done for any dynamics invariant. One of the main consequences of computing these quantities before training is that we can study the regime where their estimates have very high precision, and thus use this as a means to inform how hyperparameters, such as the spectral radius, should be chosen before training.
