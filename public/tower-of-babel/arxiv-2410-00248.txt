Strength is bounded linearly by Birch rank Thanks: BB was supported by NSF grant DMS-2101075 Thanks: AL was supported by NSF grant DMS-2402041
Benjamin Baily, Amichai Lampert

Abstract
. Let f f be a homogeneous polynomial over a field. For many fields, including number fields and function fields, we prove that the strength of f f is bounded above by a constant multiple of the Birch rank of f . f. The constant depends only on the degree of f f and the absolute transcendence degree of the field. This is the first linear bound obtained for forms of degree greater than three, partially resolving a conjecture of Adiprasito, Kazhdan and Ziegler. Our result has applications for the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. The circle method yields an asymptotic formula for counting integral zeros of (collections of) homogeneous polynomials, provided the Birch rank is sufficiently large – a natural geometric condition. Our main theorem implies that these formulas hold even if we only assume a similar lower bound on the strength of the (collection of) homogeneous polynomials – an arithmetic condition which is a priori weaker. This answers questions of Cook-Magyar [ CM14 ] and Skinner [ Ski97 ] , and also yields a new proof of a seminal result of Schmidt [ Sch85 , Theorem II] as a consequence of Birch’s earlier work [ Bir62 ] . Over finite fields we obtain a quasi-linear bound for partition rank of tensors in terms of analytic rank, improving Moshkovitz-Zhu’s state of the art bound [ MZ22 ] .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Let
K
be a field and
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d.
In 1962, Birch
[
Bir62
]
introduced the following notion of rank.

Definition 1.1

(Birch rank)
.

The
Birch rank
of
f
is

\textnormal{Brk}(f)=\codim_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\nabla f(x)=0).

For
K=\mathbf{Q},
Birch obtained an asymptotic formula for the number of integer zeroes of
f
of bounded height, provided that
\textnormal{Brk}(f)
is sufficiently large. We will call such a result a
Birch-type result
. Birch-type results have since been obtained in many other settings, including number fields
[
Ski97
]
, function fields
[
Lee11
]
, collections of forms of different degrees
[
BH17
]
and for solutions whose entries are all prime
[
CM14
]
. In the context of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method, or more generally when estimating exponential sums involving
f
, large Birch rank is a natural hypothesis as it allows good control of these sums.

In
1985,
Schmidt
[
Sch85
]
defined another notion of rank for polynomials.

Definition 1.2

(Strength)
.

The
strength
of
f
is

\textnormal{str}(f)=\min\left\{s:f=\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}h_{i}\right\},

where
g_{i},h_{i}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of degree less than
d
.

Remark0.

Strength is sometimes known as Schmidt rank in the literature. The term ‘strength’ was introduced by Ananyan and Hochster
[
AH20
]
.

If
f=g_{1}h_{1}+\dots+g_{r}h_{r}
, then
\{x:\forall i,g_{i}(x)=h_{i}(x)=0\}\subseteq\{x:\nabla f(x)=0\}
, so
\textnormal{Brk}(f)\leq 2\cdot\textnormal{str}(f)
is an easy consequence of Krull’s height theorem. The converse implication, however, is far from obvious and is the subject of this paper. Schmidt proved that when
K=\mathbf{Q}
it is sufficient to assume only large strength in order to obtain an asymptotic formula for the number of integer solutions. We will call such a result a
Schmidt-type result
. We emphasize that Schmidt did
not
prove his result by showing that large strength implies large Birch rank, but instead by some ingenious alternative arguments. Because strength is defined via forms of lower degree, Schmidt’s result lends itself naturally to inductive arguments with consequences for the number of integer zeros of
arbitrary
forms.

In the following decades, strength has found myriad applications ranging from additive combinatorics to commutative algebra. In 2009, motivated by the inverse conjecture for the Gowers norm, Green and Tao
[
GT09
]
proved that polynomials of high strength over finite fields have equidistributed values. Their result was extended and improved by many others, see e.g.
[
KL08
,
HS10
,
TZ12
,
Mil19
,
Jan20
,
BL23
,
CM23
,
LZ24a
,
MZ24
]
. In 2020, Ananyan and Hochster
[
AH20
]
proved Stillman’s conjecture regarding the projective dimension of ideals in polynomial rings. Strength played a central role in their proof, as well as in subsequent related work, such as
[
ESS19
,
Dra19
,
BDE19
,
ESS21
]
.

In the original context of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method, however, Schmidt’s result has not been extended to other settings. The methods used in
[
Sch85
]
have proven difficult to adapt elsewhere, and the analogous Schmidt-type results are explicitly left open in the work of Skinner
[
Ski97
]
and Cook-Magyar
[
CM14
]
.

1.2.
Results

Our main result bridges the gap between Birch-type results and Schmidt-type results by showing that
\textnormal{str}(f)\ll_{d}\textnormal{Brk}(f)
in all the extensions of Birch’s theorem mentioned above. This converts all of the Birch-type results above into Schmidt-type results, with the bound increasing by only a constant factor. As stated in the abstract, this answers questions raised by Skinner and Cook-Magyar, and recovers Schmidt’s seminal result as a consequence of Birch’s theorem. See section 2 for details.

Bounds for strength in terms of Birch rank were obtained for cubics in
[
Der22
]
,
[
AKZ21
]
, and
[
CM22
]
; for quartics in
[
KP22
]
; and for arbitrary degree in
[
LZ24
]
, albeit only for some fields. More general results were obtained in
[
Bik+25
]
and
[
BDS24
]
. Adiprasito, Kazhdan and Ziegler conjectured in
[
AKZ21
]
that linear bounds hold for all degrees.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Suppose that
\textnormal{char}(K)=0
or
\textnormal{char}(K)>d.
Then

\textnormal{str}(f)\ll_{d}\textnormal{Brk}(f).

So far, this conjecture has been proven only for
d\leq 3.
Our main result partially resolves conjecture
1.3
. To state it, we first define

r=r(K)=\begin{cases}\textnormal{trdeg}(K/\mathbf{Q})+1\ ,\ &\textnormal{char}(K)=0,\\
\max(\textnormal{trdeg}(K/\mathbf{F}_{p}),1),\ &\textnormal{char}(K)=p>0.\end{cases}.

Our result is then as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
\textnormal{char}(K)=0
or
\textnormal{char}(K)>d.

(1)

If
K
is infinite then
\textnormal{str}(f)\leq C(d,K)\cdot\textnormal{Brk}(f),
where

C(d,K)=(2^{d-1}-1)(d-1)^{r+1}\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}.

(2)

If
K
is finite then
\textnormal{str}(f)\ll_{d}\textnormal{Brk}(f)\cdot\log(\textnormal{Brk}(f)+1).

Theorem
1.4
for finite fields complements the results of Moshkovitz-Zhu
[
MZ24
]
to improve their current record bound for partition rank in terms of analytic rank for tensors. The relevant definitions are given in §2. We obtain the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose
F
is a multi-linear map of degree
d
over a finite field. Then

\textnormal{prk}(F)\ll_{d}\textnormal{ark}(F)\cdot\log(\textnormal{ark}(F)+1).

Remark0.

Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
for finite fields were recently proved independently in
[
MZ24
]
based on recent results in
[
CY24
,
MZ24a
]
. The results of
[
CY24
,
MZ24a
]
are obtained by a different method than ours and lead to slightly weaker bounds.

Layout of the paper

In §2 we state our results for multi-linear forms in detail and describe some applications of theorem
1.4
for the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. In §3 we recall known results and use these to show that our results all follow from theorem
2.5
. We then describe the structure of the proof of this theorem. In §4 we make some reductions regarding the fields we must consider, and establish results relating point counting to dimension - including an asymptotic characterization of geometric rank. In §5 we prove theorem
2.5
in positive characteristic and in §6 we prove theorem
2.5
in characteristic zero.

2.
Multi-linear results and applications of theorem
1.4

2.1.
Multi-linear results

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over
K
and let
F:V^{d}\to K
be a multi-linear map, i.e. a tensor of degree
d.
The analogue of Birch rank in the multi-linear setting is
geometric rank
, which was defined by Kopparty, Moshkovitz and Zuiddam
[
KMZ20
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Geometric rank)
.

The
geometric rank
of
F
is
\textnormal{grk}(F)=\codim_{V^{d-1}}S_{F},
where

S_{F}=\left((x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})\in V^{d-1}:F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},\cdot)\equiv 0\right).

There is also a multi-linear analogue of strength, called
partition rank
. This was introduced by Naslund in
[
Nas20
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Partition rank)
.

We say that
F
has
partition rank one
if there exists a subset
\varnothing\neq I\subsetneq[d]
such that
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=G((x_{i})_{i\in I})\cdot H((x_{j})_{j\in[d]\setminus I})
, where
G:V^{|I|}\to K
and
H:V^{d-|I|}\to K
are nonzero multi-linear forms. In general, the
partition rank
of
F
is

\textnormal{prk}(F)=\min\{r:F=F_{1}+\ldots+F_{r}\},

where the
F_{i}:V^{d}\to K
are multi-linear forms of partition rank one.

The multi-linear version of theorem
1.4
can then be stated as follows.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
r=r(K)
be as in theorem
1.4
.

(1)

For infinite
K
we have

\textnormal{prk}(F)\leq(2^{d-1}-1)(d-1)^{r}\textnormal{grk}(F),

(2)

For finite
K
we have

\textnormal{prk}(F)\ll_{d}\textnormal{grk}(F)\cdot\log(\textnormal{grk}(F)+1).

Remark0.

Note that there is no restriction on the characteristic in the above theorem.

All of our results will follow from a novel technical result relating geometric rank and another notion –
analytic rank
. For finite fields, analytic rank is an arithmetic version of geometric rank, introduced by Gowers and Wolf in
[
GW11
]
. We extend their definition to infinite fields.

Definition 2.4

(Analytic rank)
.

(1)

For
K=\mathbf{F}_{q},
the
analytic rank
of
F
is

\textnormal{ark}(F)=\log_{q}\left(\frac{q^{(d-1)\dim V}}{|S_{F}(K)|}\right).

(2)

For infinite
K,
the
analytic rank
is

\textnormal{ark}(F)=\codim_{V^{d-1}}\overline{S_{F}(K)},

where the closure is taken with respect to the Zariski topology.

We now state our main technical result, which is of independent interest. Informally, we show that varieties defined by systems of multi-linear equations are full of
rational points.

Theorem 2.5
.

(1)

For infinite
K
we have
\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq(d-1)^{r}\cdot\textnormal{grk}(F),

where
r=r(K)
is defined as in theorem
1.4
.

(2)

For finite
K
we have
\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq(d-1)\cdot\textnormal{grk}(F).

We will explain how this result implies theorems
1.4
,
1.5
and
2.3
in §3.

2.2.
Applications for the Hardy-Littlewood circle method

Let
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
be a collection of homogeneous forms of degree
d
in
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}].
All of the results we will discuss in this subsection apply also for systems of forms of varying degrees, but we stick to one fixed degree for clarity of exposition. We now need Birch rank for tuples of forms.

Definition 2.6

(Birch rank for tuples)
.

The
Birch rank
of
\underline{f}
is

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})=\codim_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\textnormal{rk}(J(x))<s),

where
J(x)
is the matrix of partial derivatives.

When
K
is a number field or a function field, we adopt Schmidt’s terminology from
[
Sch85
]
and say that
\underline{f}
is a
Hardy-Littlewood system
, or HLS for short, when the number of common integer zeroes of
\underline{f}
obeys the asymptotic formula predicted by the Hardy-Littlewood circle method.

Example 2.7.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}.
If
\underline{f}
is an HLS, then

\{x\in[-P,P]^{n}\cap\mathbf{Z}^{n}:f_{i}(x)=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq s\}=\mathfrak{J}\mathfrak{S}P^{n-ds}+o(P^{n-ds}),

where
\mathfrak{J},\mathfrak{S}
are the
singular integral
and
singular series
respectively. We note that
\mathfrak{J}\mathfrak{S}>0
whenever the vanishing locus of
\underline{f}
contains a nonsingular rational point. For further details on the case
K=\mathbf{Q}
see
[
Sch85
, Section 3]
; for the case of a general number field see
[
Ski97
, Lemma 6]
.

The following result was first proved by Birch for
K=\mathbf{Q}

[
Bir62
]
and then extended by Skinner to number fields
[
Ski97
]
and Lee to function fields
[
Lee11
]
.

Theorem 2.8

(Birch, Skinner, Lee)
.

Let
K
be a number field or a function field
\mathbf{F}_{q}(t)
with characteristic
>d.
If
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>s(s+1)(d-1)2^{d-1}
then
\underline{f}
is a HLS.

Cook and Magyar
[
CM14
]
proved the striking result that for
K=\mathbf{Q}
and
d\geq 2
a similar hypothesis implies an asymptotic for the number of integer solutions whose entries are all
prime
numbers, with an appropriately modified asymptotic formula (For
d=1
they require a different notion of rank, since for example the smooth linear form
x_{1}
has no zeros with all entries prime, no matter how large
n
is). When such a formula holds, we call
\underline{f}
a
prime HLS
.

Theorem 2.9

(Cook-Magyar)
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}
and
d\geq 2.
There exists a constant
\alpha(d,s)
such that if
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>\alpha(d,s)
then
\underline{f}
is a prime HLS.

We say that the above results are of
Birch-type
, since the hypothesis is a lower bound on the Birch rank.

In a celebrated result
[
Sch85
]
, Schmidt extended Birch’s original theorem to the setting where we assume only a lower bound on the strength of
\underline{f}.
To state Schmidt’s result, we first define strength for tuples of forms.

Definition 2.10

(Strength for tuples)
.

The
strength
of
\underline{f}
is

\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})=\min\left\{\textnormal{str}(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s}):a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in K\textnormal{ not all zero}\right\},

where the zero form has strength zero.

Schmidt’s result is as follows.

Theorem 2.11

(Schmidt)
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}.
There exists a constant
\beta=\beta(d)
such that if
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})\geq\beta s^{2}
then
\underline{f}
is a HLS.

Recalling the easy inequality
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})\leq 2\textnormal{str}(\underline{f}),
we see that Schmidt’s theorem implies Birch’s theorem, albeit with a bound which has worse dependence on
d.
The true utility of Schmidt’s result, however, lies in the fact that forms not satisfying the hypotheses can by definition be decomposed into forms of lower degree. This inductive process allowed him to deduce results about asymptotic counts of integer zeroes for
arbitrary
systems of forms. As we mentioned earlier, Schmidt did
not
deduce his result from Birch’s theorem by showing that
\textnormal{str}(f)
is bounded above by some function of
\textnormal{Brk}(f),
but instead by a novel alternative argument. We call theorem
2.11
a
Schmidt-type
result, since the hypothesis is a lower bound on strength.

Schmidt’s argument has proven difficult to adapt to other settings, and to our knowledge there are no further Schmidt-type results in the literature. Both Skinner
[
Ski97
]
and Cook-Magyar
[
CM14
]
speculate about the possibility of obtaining Schmidt-type results in their settings and discuss the challenges this would present.

Theorem
1.4
directly implies that all of the Birch-type results above yield a Schmidt-type result with a comparable bound. Before stating this, we need an extension for tuples of forms.

Lemma 2.12
.

When
K
has characteristic zero we have

\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})\leq Cs\cdot(\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})+s-1),

where
C=C(d,K)
is the constant of theorem
1.4
.

Proof.

This follows by an argument which we learned from A. Polishchuk. First, note that

\{x\in\overline{K}^{n}:\textnormal{rk}(J(x))<s\}=\bigcup_{\underline{a}\in\mathbb{P}_{\overline{K}}^{s-1}}\{x\in\overline{K}^{n}:\nabla(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s})(x)=0\}.

Consequently, we have

(1)

\min_{\underline{a}\in\mathbb{P}_{\overline{K}}^{s-1}}\textnormal{Brk}(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s})\leq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})+s-1.

Now, suppose the minimum is obtained for some
0\neq\underline{a}\in\overline{K}^{s}
and write
\eta=\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{a}\cdot\underline{f}).
We claim that there exists
0\neq\underline{b}\in K^{s}
with
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{b}\cdot\underline{f})\leq s\eta.
Theorem
1.4
and inequality (
1
) then imply

\textnormal{str}(\underline{b}\cdot\underline{f})\leq C\cdot s\eta\leq Cs\cdot(\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})+s-1),

completing the proof. To prove the claim about the existence of such a
\underline{b},
let
\mathcal{F}
be the subspace of
\textnormal{sp}_{\overline{K}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
spanned by the Galois conjugates of
\underline{a}\cdot\underline{f}.
By Galois descent, this has a basis consisting of forms in
\textnormal{sp}_{K}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}).
Let
\underline{b}\cdot\underline{f}
be one of these basis elements. Since
\dim(\mathcal{F})\leq s,
we can write
\underline{b}\cdot\underline{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}(\underline{a}\cdot\underline{f})
where
\beta_{i}\in\overline{K}
and the
\sigma_{i}
are field automorphisms. Clearly we have
\textnormal{Brk}(\sigma_{i}(\underline{a}\cdot\underline{f}))=\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{a}\cdot\underline{f})=\eta
for all
1\leq i\leq s.
Birch rank is subadditive, so we get
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{b}\cdot\underline{f})\leq s\eta
as claimed.
∎

Now we can convert the above Birch-type results to Schmidt-type results. In particular, this yields a new proof of Schmidt’s theorem
2.11
.

Proposition 2.13
.

There exists a constant
\gamma=\gamma(d)
such that the following holds:

(1)

If
K
is a number field and
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})>\gamma\cdot s^{3}
then
\underline{f}
is a HLS.

(2)

Suppose that
K=\mathbf{Q}
and
d\geq 2.
If
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})>\gamma\cdot\alpha(d,s)
then
\underline{f}
is a prime HLS, where
\alpha(d,s)
is any constant such that theorem
2.9
holds.

(3)

Suppose that
K=\mathbf{F}_{q}(t)
has characteristic
>d
and that
s=1.
If
\textnormal{str}(f)>\gamma
then
f
is a HLS.

Proof.

This follows immediately by combining theorems
2.8
and
2.9
with lemma
2.12
.
∎

Remark0.

Two remarks are in order here. The first is that we get a result over function fields only for a single form. This is due to the absence of a suitable analogue of lemma
2.12
. The second is that the above proposition reproves Schmidt’s theorem
2.11
but with a cubic dependence on
s
rather than quadratic. However, the quadratic dependence can also be recovered by using a refinement of Birch’s theorem due to Dietmann
[
Die14
]
and independently Schindler
[
Sch15
]
. We do not give a proof of this here, but the interested reader should have no difficulty filling in the details.

3.
Reduction to theorem
2.5
and proof sketch

In this section we assume theorem
2.5
holds and use it to deduce our other results: theorems
1.4
,
1.5
and
2.3
. Then we discuss the plan of the proof of theorem
2.5
.

3.1.
Proof of theorem
1.4

We begin by explaining how theorem
1.4
follows from its multi-linear analogue theorem
2.3
. Suppose that
V
is a finite-dimensional vector space over
K
and that
f\in\text{Sym}^{d}V.
There is an associated symmetric multi-linear form
\tilde{f}:V^{d}\to K
given by

\tilde{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})=\nabla_{h_{1}}\ldots\nabla_{h_{d}}f(0),

which satisfies
\tilde{f}(x,\ldots,x)=d!f(x).
It is not difficult to see that the strength of
f
and the partition rank of
\tilde{f}
are closely related.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
LZ24
, claim 3.2]
)
.

If
K
has characteristic zero or
>d
then

\textnormal{str}(f)\leq\textnormal{prk}(\tilde{f})\leq\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\textnormal{str}(f).

We need the following key lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
KLP24
]
, theorems 1.3 and A.1)
.

(1)

Suppose that
K
is
algebraically closed
and that the characteristic of
K
does not divide
d.
Then

\frac{\textnormal{Brk}(f)}{2}\leq\textnormal{str}(f)\leq(d-1)\cdot\textnormal{Brk}(f).

(2)

Suppose that
K
is infinite and that
F:V^{d}\to K
is multi-linear. Then

\textnormal{grk}(F)\leq\textnormal{prk}(F)\leq(2^{d-1}-1)\cdot\textnormal{ark}(F)

Proof of theorem
1.4
assuming theorem
2.3
.

Let
\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f)
denote the strength of
f
in
\overline{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}],
where
\overline{K}
is the algebraic closure of
K.
Similarly, let
\overline{\textnormal{prk}}(\tilde{f})
denote the partition rank of
\tilde{f}
when considered as a multi-linear map over
\overline{K}.
By lemmas
3.2
and
3.1
we get

\textnormal{grk}(\tilde{f})\leq\overline{\textnormal{prk}}(\tilde{f})\leq\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\cdot\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f)\leq(d-1)\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\cdot\textnormal{Brk}(f).

By theorem
2.3
and lemma
3.1
we have

\textnormal{str}(f)\leq\textnormal{prk}(\tilde{f})\leq(2^{d-1}-1)(d-1)^{r+1}\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\cdot\textnormal{Brk}(f)

when
K
is infinite and

\textnormal{str}(f)\leq\textnormal{prk}(\tilde{f})\ll_{d}\textnormal{Brk}(f)\cdot\log(\textnormal{Brk}(f)+1)

when
K
is finite.
∎

3.2.
Proof of theorem
2.3

Suppose first that
K=\mathbf{F}_{q}
is a finite field. For
l\geq 1
we write
F_{l}
for the multi-linear map
F\otimes\mathbf{F}_{q^{l}}:(V\otimes\mathbf{F}_{q^{l}})^{d}\to\mathbf{F}_{q^{l}}.
Note the following simple inequality.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
CM23
]
, proof of corollary 1)
.

We have
\textnormal{prk}(F)\leq l\cdot\textnormal{prk}(F_{l}).

The heavy lifting will be done by the following result of Moshkovitz and Zhu.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
MZ24
]
, theorem 1.6 for
\varepsilon=1
)
.

There exists a constant
C=C(d)
such that the following holds: For any
a\geq 1,
if
\textnormal{ark}(F_{l})\leq a
and
q^{l}\geq C(a+1)^{d-2}
then
\textnormal{prk}(F_{l})\leq 2^{d-1}a.

Proof of theorem
2.3
for finite fields.

Let
r=\textnormal{grk}(F).
Theorem
2.5
implies that
\textnormal{ark}(F_{l})\leq(d-1)r
for all
l\geq 1.
Let

l=\lceil\log C+(d-2)\log\left((d-1)r+1\right)\rceil,

where
C=C(d)
is the constant of proposition
3.4
. Note that
l\ll_{d}\log(r+1).
Our choice of
l
guarantees that
q^{l}\geq 2^{l}\geq C[(d-1)r+1]^{d-2}
so we may apply proposition
3.4
with
a=(d-1)r
to obtain

\textnormal{prk}(F_{l})\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)r.

Applying lemma
3.3
gives

\textnormal{prk}(F)\leq l\cdot 2^{d-1}(d-1)r\ll_{d}r\log(r+1).

∎

The case of an infinite field is comparatively immediate.

Proof of theorem
2.3
for infinite fields.

By theorem
2.5
and lemma
3.2
, we have

\textnormal{prk}(F)\leq(2^{d-1}-1)\cdot\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq(2^{d-1}-1)(d-1)^{r}\cdot\textnormal{grk}(F).

∎

3.3.
Proof of theorem
1.5

Again,

We have

Proof of theorem

By lemma

Structure of the proof of theorem

We begin by applying Weil restriction to reduce to the case of purely transcendental extensions of either
\mathbf{Q}
or
\mathbf{F}_{q}.
By choosing a basis for
V
we may assume without loss of generality that
F
has coefficients in
A,
where
A=\mathbf{Z}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}]
or
A=\mathbf{F}_{q}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}],
respectively. The quotients of

by maximal ideals are finite fields, and we consider the reductions of

over these finite fields. We prove a characterization of geometric rank via the asymptotic count of singular points for these reductions.

The chief novel technique in our proof lies in the next step. We employ a scaling inequality for zeroes of systems of multi-linear equations to deduce that many of these singular points have small height and therefore
lift to genuine singular points of

F
. For finite fields, there is one final step consisting of estimating the size of the fibers of the “evaluation at zero” map, which sends singular points with coefficients in
\mathbf{F}_{q}[t]
to singular points with coefficients in
\mathbf{F}_{q}.

Lemma 5.1

(Scaling)
.

Let

be finite abelian groups and
G:H^{d-1}\to H^{\prime}
a multi-linear map. Suppose
\varphi:H\to L
is a group homomorphism and define

N^{y}(G)=|\{x\in H^{d-1}:G(x)=0,\varphi(x_{i})=y_{i}\}|

for any
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d-1})\in L^{d-1}.

Then

(7)

N^{y}(G)\leq N^{0}(G).

In particular, for any subgroup

we have

|\{x\in H^{d-1}:G(x)=0\}|\leq[H:H_{0}]^{d-1}\cdot\left|\{x\in H_{0}^{d-1}:G(x)=0\}\right|.

Proof.

The statement about subgroups is deduced by taking
\varphi
to be the projection
\varphi:H\to H/H_{0}
and then summing over all

We prove inequality (

For the base case

there are two possibilities. If

, then there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, there exists some

with

and

In this case,

N^{y}(G)=\left|x_{0}+\{x\in H:G(x)=0,\varphi(x)=0\}\right|=N^{0}(G).

For general

we have

\displaystyle N^{y}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in H,\ \varphi(x_{d-1})=y_{d-1}}N^{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-2})}(G(\cdot,x))\leq\sum_{x\in H,\ \varphi(x_{d-1})=y_{d-1}}N^{0}(G(\cdot,x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in H^{d-2},\ \varphi(x^{\prime}_{i})=0}N^{y_{d-1}}(G(x^{\prime},\cdot))\leq\sum_{x^{\prime}\in H^{d-2},\ \varphi(x^{\prime}_{i})=0}N^{0}(G(x^{\prime},\cdot))=N^{0}(G),

where the first inequality follows from the inductive hypothesis and the second one from the base case
d=2.

in characteristic zero

In this section,
F

d
-tensor over

for some

. Using the results of the previous section, we will derive the bound
\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq(d-1)^{s+1}\textnormal{grk}(F)
.

(1)

First, change bases so that
F
has coefficients in
A=\mathbf{Z}[t_{1},\dots,t_{s}]
. The variety

\mathfrak{S}_{F}=(F(x,\cdot)\equiv 0)\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{A}^{n(d-1)}

has generic fiber
S_{F}
, giving an integral model for
S_{F}
over
A
.

(2)

For
s=0
, the argument is essentially the same as the case of
\mathbf{F}_{q}[t]
in the previous section, so we assume
s\geq 1
for the remainder of this outline.

(3)

For all but finitely many primes

, the form
F\otimes_{A}A/p
has the same geometric rank as
F
. By
Proposition

5.3
, the variety
S_{F\otimes_{A}A/p}
has many rational points of small height. Similarly to the previous section, many of these points lift to
A
-points of
\mathfrak{S}_{F}.

(4)

By Noether normalization, the abundance of
A
-points proves the desired upper bound
\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq(d-1)^{s+1}\textnormal{grk}(F)
.

We begin with a semigroup analogue of lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 6.1

(Scaling)
.

Let
B
be an abelian group and let
G:(\mathbf{Z}^{n})^{d-1}\to B
be a multi-linear map. For a positive integer
R
set

\displaystyle N_{R}(G)

\displaystyle=|\{x\in(\mathbf{Z}^{n})^{d-1}:G(x)=0,\ 0\leq x_{j}<R\ \forall j\in[n(d-1)]\}|

\displaystyle Z_{R}(G)

\displaystyle=|\{x\in(\mathbf{Z}^{n})^{d-1}:G(x)=0,\ -R<x_{j}<R\ \forall j\in[n(d-1)]\}|.

Then for any positive integer
L
we have

N_{LR}(G)\leq L^{n(d-1)}Z_{R}(G).

Proof.

By induction on
d.

We begin with the base case
d=2
. First note that for any
b\in B
the number of integer solutions to
G(x)=b
in
[0,R)^{n}
is bounded above by
Z_{R}(G).
This follows by subtracting a single solution from each of the others. Therefore

\displaystyle N_{LR}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq x_{i}<LR}1_{G(x)=0}=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1,\ldots,L-1\}^{n}}\sum_{0\leq x_{i}<R}1_{G(x+R\omega)=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1,\ldots,L-1\}^{n}}\sum_{0\leq x_{i}<R}1_{G(x)=-G(R\omega)}\leq\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1,\ldots,L-1\}^{n}}Z_{R}(G)=L^{n}Z_{R}(G).

For general
d>2,
we have

\displaystyle N_{LR}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}^{n},0\leq x_{i}<LR}N_{LR}(G(x,\cdot))\leq\sum_{x\in\mathbf{Z}^{n},0\leq x_{i}<LR}L^{n(d-2)}Z_{R}(G(x,\cdot))

\displaystyle=L^{n(d-2)}\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Z}^{n(d-2)},-R<y_{i}<R}N_{LR}(G(\cdot,y))

\displaystyle\leq L^{n(d-1)}\sum_{y\in\mathbf{Z}^{n(d-2)},-R<y_{i}<R}Z_{R}(G(\cdot,y))=L^{n(d-1)}Z_{R}(G),

where the first inequality follows from the inductive hypothesis and the second one from the base case
d=2.

∎

Definition 6.2
.

Let
A=\mathbf{Z}[t_{1},\dots,t_{s}]
and let
K=\mathbf{Q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{s})
. Let
F:(A^{n})^{d}\to A
be a
d
-tensor. Define
G:(A^{n})^{d-1}\to\Hom_{A}(A^{n},A)
by
G(x)=F(x,\cdot)
. Let
A_{\geq 0}
be the additive sub-semigroup of
A
in which the coefficients of all monomials are non-negative. For
x\in(A^{n})^{d-1}
, let
\lVert x\rVert\in\mathbf{Z}^{\geq 0}
denote the largest absolute value of any integer coefficient appearing in any term of

. For

, we define

N_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}}(G)=|\{x\in(A_{\geq 0}^{n})^{d-1}:G(x)=0,\ \lVert x\rVert<L,\ \deg_{t_{i}}(x)<R_{i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq s\}|

We then define the following invariants of

:

\gamma_{0}(F)=n(d-1)-\limsup_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}\to\infty}\frac{\log_{L}Z_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}}(G)}{R_{1}\dots R_{s}}.

Additionally, let

denote the map
(A^{n})^{d-1}\xrightarrow{G}A^{n}\to(A/L)^{n}
. We also define

\delta_{0}(F)=n(d-1)-\limsup_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}\to\infty}\frac{\log_{L}N_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}}(G_{L})}{R_{1}\dots R_{s}}.

Note that this definition makes sense even if

, as the empty product equals 1.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

be as in

. Then

.

Assuming this lemma, we are now able to prove

for fields of characteristic zero.

Proof.

A standard application of Noether normalization (

A.2
) shows that
\textnormal{ark}(F)\leq\gamma_{0}(F)
.
By

6.3
, it suffices to prove that
\delta_{0}(F)\leq(d-1)^{s}\textnormal{grk}(F)
. For

, this follows from

]

, we note by

. Consequently, the bound

for all

.
∎

To conclude this section, we now prove

6.3
.

Proof.

Choose
L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}\in\mathbf{Z}^{+}
such that

N_{L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s}}(G_{L})\geq L^{R_{1}\dots R_{s}(n(d-1)-\delta_{0}(F)-\varepsilon)}.

For

Appendix A

The results contained within this appendix are standard and similar to existing results. Nevertheless, we detail their proofs here for the sake of self-containedness and completeness.

A.1.
A Consequence of Noether Normalization

Lemma A.1

(Height point growth in positive characteristic)
.

Let
A=\mathbf{F}_{q}[t_{1},\dots,t_{r}]
and
K=\mathbf{F}_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
. Suppose
X\subset\mathbf{A}^{n}
is an affine variety defined over
K.
We define a height function on points of
A^{n}
by
H(x)=(\deg_{t_{1}}x,\ldots,\deg_{t_{r}}x).

For
R_{1},\dots,R_{r}\in\mathbf{N}
, set

X(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})=\{x\in X(A):H(x)_{i}<R_{i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq r\}.

Then

\dim(X)\geq\limsup_{R_{1},\dots,R_{r}\to\infty}\frac{\log_{q}|X(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})|}{R_{1}\dots R_{r}}.

Proof.

The key is that we have

|\mathbf{A}^{b}(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})|=|\mathbf{A}^{1}(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})|^{b}=q^{bR_{1}\dots R_{r}}.

By Noether normalization, there exists a finite map
\pi:X\to\mathbf{A}^{b}
for
b=\dim X
. Since
A
is infinite, this map can be taken linear with coefficients in
A
. Consequently, there exists
C>0
such that

\pi(X(R_{1},\dots,R_{s}))\subseteq\mathbf{A}^{b}(R_{1}+C,\dots,R_{r}+C).

Taking cardinalities of both sides, we obtain

|X(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})|\leq\deg(\pi)q^{b(R_{1}+C)\dots(R_{r}+C)}.

Taking
\log_{q}
, dividing by
R_{1}\ldots R_{r}
and then taking the limit
R_{1},\dots,R_{r}\to\infty
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Height point growth in characteristic zero)
.

Let
A=\mathbf{Z}[t_{1},\dots,t_{s}]
and
K=\mathbf{Q}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
. Suppose
X\subset\mathbf{A}^{n}
is an affine variety defined over
K.
We define a height function on points of
A^{n}
by
H(x)=(L,\deg_{t_{1}}x,\dots,\deg_{t_{s}}x)

For

The key is that

|\mathbf{A}^{b}(L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s})|=|\mathbf{A}^{1}(L,R_{1},\dots,R_{s})|^{b}=(2L-1)^{bR_{1}\dots R_{s}}\leq(2L)^{bR_{1}\dots R_{s}}.

Taking

, dividing by

∎

A.2.

In

Lemma A.3
.

Let
A=\mathbf{F}_{q}[t]
. Let
G(X)\in A[X]
be a non-constant function. Then
G
has a solution mod
\pi
for infinitely many prime elements
\pi\in A
.

Proof.

Cassels gives an elementary proof of this result in
[
Cas76a
]
for
A=\mathbb{Z}
.
The proof for
\mathbf{F}_{q}[t]
is identical, with only one claim requiring special justification in this case.

Claim
.

Let
P
be a finite set of primes in
\mathbf{F}_{q}[t]
and suppose
G(0)\neq 0
. Then there exists
c
divisible by all
\pi\in P
such that
G(G(0)c)
is not a unit multiple of
G(0)
.

To prove this claim, it suffices to take

for

.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
A=\mathbf{F}_{q}[t],\ K=\mathbf{F}_{q}(t)
and let

be a finite extension. Let
e\geq 0
such that
L^{p^{e}}\subseteq K^{\text{sep}}
. Then there are infinitely many primes
\pi\in A
which admit a field embedding
\alpha_{\pi}:L\to K_{\pi}
extending the Frobenius iterate
F^{e}:A\to A
.

Proof.

Start with the case that
L/K
is separable and write
L=K(\beta)
using the primitive element theorem. Suppose
\beta
satisfies minimal equation
H(z)=H_{s}z^{s}+\dots+H_{0}
over
K
. Clear denominators so that
H_{i}\in A
. Let
P^{\prime}
denote the set of prime elements
\pi\in A
such that
H(z)=0
has a solution mod
\pi
. Remove from
P^{\prime}
all primes which divide
(H_{s}\Delta(H))
. By
lemma

A.3
, the resulting set

is still infinite. Let
\pi\in P
. By the fact that

\Delta H\not\equiv 0\mod\pi,

it follows that
H
has a
simple
root mod
\pi
. By Hensel’s lemma, it follows that
H
has a root
\eta\in A^{\wedge\pi}
. We then define
\alpha_{\pi}(\beta)=\eta
, which gives a well-defined embedding
\alpha_{\pi}:L\to K_{\pi}
extending the identity map
A\to A
. In the case that
L/K
is inseparable, we apply the above argument to
L^{p^{e}}/K
for some

such that
L^{p^{e}}\subseteq K^{\text{sep}}
and precompose with
F^{e}:L\to L^{p^{e}}
.
∎
