Genome-wide identification, characterization, and expression analysis of the DMP gene family in Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa ssp. pekinensis)
Authors: Xianghan Ge, Jia Shi, Xuerui Li, Shengjie Dai, Ping Xu, Xiaohua Wang, Imran Khan, Hongxia Zhang, Zhenning Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe DMPs (DOMAIN OF UNKNOWN FUNCTION 679 membrane proteins) constitute a plant-specific protein family that plays essential roles in reproductive development, senescence, and diverse physiological processes. Despite their importance, limited information is available on DMP genes in Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa). To address this, we conducted a genome-wide identification and characterization of the DMP gene family in Chinese cabbage, followed by analyses of their structural features, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns.ResultsA total of 16 BrDMP genes were identified, unevenly distributed across 10 chromosomes. Most BrDMP genes contained a single exon without introns. Evolutionary analyses revealed 3 tandem and 6 segmental duplication events, while phylogenetic analysis classified the family into five subfamilies, with Subfamily III being the largest (6 members). BrDMP proteins displayed distinct monocot- and dicot-specific clustering patterns. The qRT-PCR demonstrated pronounced organ-specific expression, particularly in root, stamen, and big bud. Moreover, the majority of BrDMP genes were differentially regulated under abiotic stresses (drought, salt, cold, heat) and by exogenous plant hormones (ABA, tZ, NAA). Notably, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B (Subfamily IV) shared strong similarity with haploid induction genes reported in Arabidopsis thaliana, maize, and Brassica napus. These genes showed elevated expression in big bud and stamen, localized to the cell membrane, and were able to partially rescue the seed abortion phenotype in the A. thaliana dmp8 dmp9 double mutant.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the DMP gene family in Chinese cabbage. Our findings not only advance understanding of BrDMP gene functions but also identify BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B as strong candidates for developing DMP-based haploid inducer lines, offering direct applications for accelerating doubled-haploid breeding in Brassica crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07960-2.Keywords: Chinese cabbage, DMP, Gene family, Phylogeny, Gene expression, Haploid induction

Background

Membrane proteins are indispensable in vital biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, signal transduction, and material transport [1]. For instance, sperm membrane proteins GCS1/HAP2 and GEX2 are essential for sperm attachment and double fertilization, while egg cell-secreted EC1 regulates sperm-egg recognition and fusion competency [2, 3]. The DUF679 domain is a conserved motif primarily localized to the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum, with its core function hypothesized to involve the regulation of membrane trafficking or intercellular signaling [4]. This domain serves as a defining feature of the DMP family, which constitutes a class of membrane proteins exclusively found in green plants, typically containing 4–5 transmembrane domains with cytoplasmic N- and C-termini. These proteins participate in plant reproductive development, senescence processes, and various physiological regulatory mechanisms [3, 5]. Given the vital roles of the DMP family in plant biological processes, its distribution and characteristics in different plant species have been extensively studied.

In A. thaliana, ten genes belonging to the DMP family have been identified, each displaying tissue-specific expression characteristics. Among them, AtDMP1, AtDMP2, AtDMP3, AtDMP4, and AtDMP7 are associated with different forms of programmed cell death, including organ and leaf senescence, silique dehiscence, and floral organ abscission [6]. AtDMP8 and AtDMP9 facilitate gamete fusion during double fertilization [5, 7]. Seventeen CaDMP genes were identified across three pepper varieties [8]. Following various stress treatments, CaDMP expression levels showed differential upregulation, indicating their functional importance in pepper growth and development. Similarly, a total of 33 members of the oat DMP gene family have been identified, among which AsDMP1, AsDMP19, and AsDMP22 potentially play a role in regulating seed aging in oats [9]. Furthermore, analysis of four cotton varieties revealed 58 DMP genes whose expression patterns also suggest involvement in reproductive development, plant senescence, and stress responses [10].

Recent progress in plant reproductive biology has firmly established DMP genes as central regulators of haploid induction (HI) across crops [11]. The concept originated from the pioneering work of Zhong et al., who identified ZmDMP as the causal HI gene in the maize Stock6 genotype, demonstrating that CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of ZmDMP triggers efficient haploid formation [12]. Subsequent work demonstrated that mutations in A. thaliana homologs AtDMP8 and AtDMP9 could induce maternal haploid production [13]. This breakthrough has been extended to various dicot species, including tomato, alfalfa, B. napus, Brassica oleracea, tobacco, potato, and watermelon [7, 14–17]. In B. oleracea, for example, BoC03.DMP9 was shown to function as a pollen-abundant HI gene whose knockout induces maternal haploids irrespective of genetic background [18]. In B. napus, Zhong et al. characterized four DMP genes and achieved a 2.4% HI rate through multiplex CRISPR/Cas9 knockout [7]. Parallel work by Li et al. on the Westar cultivar demonstrated HI rates reaching 2.53% across T0-T2 generations [14]. Both groups observed a dosage compensation effect among the four BnaDMP paralogs, with progressively higher HI rates in double, triple, and quadruple mutants. Research suggests that DMP knockdown disrupts sperm-egg plasma membrane fusion, leading to haploid embryo formation [19]. The evolutionary conservation of ZmDMP-like proteins across dicots and in specific monocots provides a strong biological basis for the expanding use of DMP-based HI systems across diverse crops.

Chinese cabbage, originating from China, is one of the most important vegetable crops worldwide with high yield and good quality [20]. While the DMP gene family has been extensively studied in various crops, no reports exist for Chinese cabbage, and the molecular mechanisms underlying its haploid induction remain unclear. Our study revealed 16 DMP family genes in the ‘Chiifu’ genome of Chinese cabbage. By examining their physicochemical features, conserved motifs, evolutionary relationships, promoter cis-elements, and expression dynamics, we generated a resource that can aid in functional characterization and in pinpointing promising candidates for haploid induction.

Materials and methods

Identification and physicochemical characterization of the DMP gene family in Chinese cabbage

A. thaliana DMP protein sequences were retrieved and used as seed sequences to perform BLAST P searches in BRAD (http://brassicadb.cn/) [6] with -evalue1e-5 as the parameter to screen candidate proteins. To ensure no candidates were missed, a secondary BLAST P search was conducted against the Chinese cabbage genome database using the identified DMP protein sequences. Finally, conserved domains of the candidate proteins were analyzed using the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [21], NCBI Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) [22], and the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [23], to further validate the accuracy of DMP protein identification. For B. napus and B. oleracea, candidate DMP genes were identified by performing BLAST P searches in BRAD using BrDMP protein sequences as seeds, followed by conserved domain analysis and redundancy removal.

Additionally, the molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric point (pI) of the DMP proteins were predicted using the ExPASy tool (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/) [24]. Subcellular localization of BrDMPs was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolfpsort.html), TargetP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TargetP-2.0/) [25] and Predotar (https://urgi.versailles.inrae.fr/Tools/Predotar) [26]. Transmembrane domain (TM) distribution and quantity were predicted using the TMHMM2.0 software (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0) [27].

Analyses of DMP gene sequences and phylogenetic relationships

A study of the phylogenetic relationships between DMP genes was conducted. We constructed an evolutionary tree using DMP protein sequences from A. thaliana, Chinese cabbage, rice, tomato, maize, B. napus, and B. oleracea. DMP protein sequences for A. thaliana, rice, tomato, and maize were obtained from published literature [6].

To compare the DMP protein sequences, we applied MUSCLE for multiple sequence alignment within the MEGA 7.0 software package. The aligned sequences were subsequently analyzed to generate a neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree using the Poisson correction model, pairwise deletion, and 1,000 bootstrap replications [28].

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of the DMP genes family

The exon-intron structures of BrDMPs genes were analyzed using the GSDS website (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/) [29]. Conserved motifs in DMP protein sequences were predicted using the MEME suite (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) and visualized with TBtools-II (v2.310) [30], with modifs arrangements illustrated. Further multiple sequence alignment of Chinese cabbage protein sequences was conducted using ClustalW in MEGA 7.0 [28].

Chromosomal localization, cis-acting element analysis, and collinearity analysis of BrDMP genes

Genomic and annotation files for Chinese cabbage, B. napus, and B. oleracea were obtained from the BRAD database. Based on the physical positions of DMP genes on chromosomes and chromosome lengths, we mapped the genes using MapInspect software. Gene duplication events were analyzed using TBtools-II, Selective pressure was assessed by calculating rates of synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) substitutions, along with the Ka/Ks ratio. Divergence time (MYA, million years ago) was calculated using the formula: MYA = Ks/(2 × 1.5 × 10 − 8) ×10− 6 [31].

The 2,000 bp sequence upstream of the ATG transcriptional initiation site of the DMP gene was extracted using TBtools-II. The cis-acting regulatory elements related to abiotic stress and plant hormones were predicted using the PlantCARE online analysis website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html) [32] and visualized by TBtools-II.

Plant materials

The B. rapa subsp. pekinensis inbred line ‘Chiifu-401-42’ was kindly provided by Professor Xiaolin Yu. The A. thaliana dmp8 dmp9 mutant (Col-0 background) was obtained from Dr. Xiongbo Peng. A. thaliana wild-type (Col-0) and Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco) wild-type seeds were collected and maintained in our own lab.

Plant growth, treatment, and sampling

B. rapa subsp. pekinensis line ‘Chiifu 401 − 42’ was used as the experimental material. All seedlings were cultivated under controlled conditions in a growth chamber with a 16/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod at 22 ± 1 °C. When the plants reached flowering and pod-setting stages, samples were collected from diverse organs and tissues, including roots, leaves, stem, flowers, small siliques, medium siliques, large siliques, sepals, stamens, petals, pistils, as well as buds of three size categories: small (< 1.6 mm), medium (1.6–2.8 mm), and large (> 2.8 mm) [33]. At least 10 individual plants were harvested per biological replicate for each tissue, and samples were pooled accordingly. To examine gene expression patterns, we analyzed diverse tissues and treatments. For drought treatment, watering was ceased for the three-week-old seedlings, allowing them to gradually dry out. Based on the methodology described by Liu et al [33], regarding the treatment of leaf drought severity, samples were taken at four time points: 0 d, 3 d, 5 d, and 7 d after treatment. For the treatments of temperature stress, seedlings were subjected to cold and heat stress by incubation at 4℃ and 32℃ under a light growth chamber, respectively. Salt stress was imposed by watering with a nutrient solution containing 200 mM NaCl. Samples were collected at 0 h, 3 h, 8 h, 24 h, and 48 h post-treatment, with normally cultivated seedlings acting as controls. Phytohormone treatments involved spraying 21 days-old seedlings with 100 µM Abscisic acid (ABA), 100 µM trans-Zeatin(tZ), and 100 µM 1-Naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), with distilled water as a control. Sampling occurred at 0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h after treatment. To minimize sample variability, root tissues were collected only for drought and salt treatments, whereas for all other treatments, the second true leaf was sampled. Each treatment consisted of at least 18 seedlings per biological replicate, with three biological replicates performed. Samples were pooled for each replicate, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection, and stored at -80℃ in an ultra-low temperature freezer [34].

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR analysis

Plant RNA was obtained using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, A1120A). The concentration and quality of the RNA were checked with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and by agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, cDNA was generated using the Takara PrimeScript FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser(RR092S), and primers for qRT-PCR assays were designed using Primer 5.0. Specificity was confirmed by PCR band size and melting curve peaks. Table S2 contains a list of PCR primers used in this study. The reference genes were BrCyp for organ expression analysis and BrUBC30 for abiotic stress and plant hormone treatments [35]. Reactions used YALEPIC® Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR MasterMix (YQ51005). Amplification was carried out on a LightCycler® 480II (Roche) with cycling conditions of 95 °C for 15 s, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s. Gene expression was analyzed by the 2−△△Ct approach, with fold changes transformed to Log₂ scale [36]. Final expression data were visualized as heatmaps using TBTools-II software employing the average linkage clustering method. Each gene’s average Ct value was derived from three biological replicates.

Subcellular localization of BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B

The constructs 35Spro::BrDMP9A-eGFP-NOST-1300 and 35Spro::BrDMP9B-eGFP-NOST-1300 were generated and chemically transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Table S6 contains primer sequences for the construction of vectors. Agrobacterium containing either the 35Spro::BrDMP9A-eGFP-NOST-1300 or 35Spro::BrDMP9B-eGFP-NOST-1300 plasmid was then mixed in equal quantities with Agrobacterium harboring the cell membrane localization marker PIP2-mCherry, and the mixture was injected into tobacco leaves. After being incubated at 25 °C for 3 days, the samples were observed for fluorescence signals using an LSM710 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Heterologous expression of BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B in A. thaliana

The vectors AtDMP9pro::BrDMP9A-NOST-1300 and AtDMP9pro::BrDMP9B-NOST-1300 were constructed and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains GV3101 by chemical transformation. These strains were used to transform A. thaliana dmp8, dmp9 double mutants via the floral dip method [37]. Positive strains were screened using screening marker gene herbicides and further verified by the PCR method. Wild-type and dmp8, dmp9 mutants served as controls. The number of siliques per plant in T1 generation was recorded and statistically analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS software. After importing the data, normality tests were first conducted, followed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test and Dunnett’s T3 test to evaluate the significance of differences between groups [38].

Results

Identification and functional profiling of DMP genes in Chinese cabbage

Using A. thaliana DMP protein sequences as query sequences, A total of 16 DMP genes were identified in the Chinese cabbage genome through BLAST P alignment, redundancy removal, and conserved domain validation. These genes were named based on their phylogenetic relationships with A. thaliana DMP genes. Except for AtDMP8, each AtDMP gene had two corresponding homologous genes in Chinese cabbage. In BrDMP genes, open reading frames (ORFs) ranged from 609 to 1464 bp, encoding polypeptides of 165 to 244 aa and theoretical isoelectric points (pI) ranging from 5.14 to 9.17. According to subcellular localization prediction, the DMP protein in Chinese cabbage is localized to the cell membrane. Transmembrane domain predictions revealed that most BrDMPs possess four transmembrane regions, suggesting structural conservation within the family (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis of plant DMP genes

To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships between BrDMP genes and DMP genes from other species, we aligned DMP protein sequences and generated a phylogenetic tree based on data from seven species, the species examined were A. thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), Chinese cabbage, B. oleracea, and B. napus. The phylogenetic tree divided the DMP proteins into five subgroups (I–V) (Fig. 1). The third subfamily, which contains the highest number of DMP genes, consists of 37 genes, of which 6 are identified as BrDMP genes. Following this, subfamilies I, II, V, and VI comprise 27, 21, 18, and 11 genes, respectively, with BrDMP representations of 4, 4, 0, and 2. Each subgroup included both monocot and dicot DMP genes, with distinct clustering patterns specific to monocots or dicots. Notably, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B from subgroup IV clustered with the reported haploid-inducing genes AtDMP9, AtDMP8, and ZmDMP (ZmDMP11), suggesting their potential functional conservation and candidacy for haploid induction in Chinese cabbage.

DMPs in different species are marked with different geometric shapes, and subfamily branches are marked with different colors. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method with Poisson correction, pairwise deletion, and 1000 bootstrap replicates

Gene organization and conserved motif pattern of BrDMP gene

In order to explore the organization and conserved motif patterns of the BrDMP gene family, phylogenetic trees, exon-intron structures, and protein motif distributions were visualized based on the Chinese cabbage genome and annotation files (Fig. 2). Most BrDMPs exhibited a single large exon structure without introns, while the remaining six BrDMPs contained only one intron. Comparative analysis of gene structures and phylogenetic trees revealed that closely related genes shared similar exon-intron architectures. MEME software analysis identified four conserved motifs (motifs 1–4) prevalent among BrDMP family members, indicating their functional conservation and potential regulatory significance (Figure S2).

Chromosomal localization of BrDMP genes

The BrDMP genes were found to be unevenly distributed on the chromosomes across six of the ten chromosomes in Chinese cabbage (Fig. 3). Most genes clustered on chromosome 1, while chromosomes 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8 contained only one or two genes.

Collinearity analysis of BrDMP genes

Analysis of duplication events in BrDMP genes identified three tandem and six segmental duplication pairs (Fig. 4A). In all duplicated gene pairs, the Ka/Ks ratio is < 1, suggesting purifying selection. The Ks values ranged from 0.280659 to 1.771761, corresponding to divergence times of 9.36–59.06 million years ago (MYA), suggesting that BrDMP gene evolution and expansion occurred over millions of years rather than at a single time point (Table S3). To further explore the evolutionary history of BrDMP genes, synteny analyses were conducted among A. thaliana, Chinese cabbage, and B. oleracea. 14 pairs of segmental duplicated genes were identified between A. thaliana and Chinese cabbage, while 21 pairs were found between Chinese cabbage and B. oleracea (Table S4 and Fig. 4B). In comparison, the relationship between Chinese cabbage and B. oleracea is closer, and evolution is more conservative. Ka/Ks analysis confirmed purifying selection for most duplicated genes, except for BolDMP4B/BrDMP3B, where Ks was not applicable due to extensive synonymous substitutions and distant evolutionary divergence.

Cis-regulatory element analysis of DMP gene promoters in Chinese cabbage

In order to further study the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms and potential biological functions of Chinese cabbage DMP genes, we used the PlantCARE website to predict cis-acting elements within the promoter region (2,000 bp upstream of the transcriptional start site ATG). Our results showed that the promoter sequences of BrDMP genes contained numerous cis-acting elements that are related to abiotic stress conditions (e.g., drought, low temperature, wounding) and plant hormones (e.g., auxin, gibberellin, jasmonic acid) (Fig. 5 and Table S5). This indicates that DMP genes in Chinese cabbage may be involved in resistance to abiotic stresses and plant hormone signaling pathways.

Expression profiles of BrDMP genes across different organs and tissues

Using Chinese cabbage ‘chiifu’ as experimental material, the expression profiles of BrDMP genes were examined across various organs and tissues (Fig. 6 and Figure S5-1). Due to their high sequence similarity and the inability of qRT-PCR to distinguish their transcripts, BrDMP2A and BrDMP2B, BrDMP4A and BrDMP4B, BrDMP7A and BrDMP7B, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B were each analyzed as single genes. Except for BrDMP5A, which is potentially a pseudogene as no expression was detected, expression data are available for the other 15 genes. The DMP genes of Chinese cabbage showed tissue or organ-specific expression. BrDMP1A had a very high expression level in stamens, possibly participating in stamen development. BrDMP1B, BrDMP3A, and BrDMP3B had the highest relative expression levels in roots. BrDMP6B had relatively high expression in little buds, and the expression gradually decreased during the middle and big bud stages. BrDMP9A&9B genes had the highest expression levels in big buds and stamens, indicating that they might play an important role in stamen maturation. Moreover, previously reported haploid induction genes are primarily expressed in the stamen and flower bud [7], suggesting that they may have similar functions and could serve as candidate genes for haploid induction.

Expression profiling of BrDMP genes in response to abiotic stress

To assess responses to abiotic stress, BrDMPs expression was examined under various stress treatments (Fig. 7). Most genes displayed an initial upregulation followed by downregulation. Under drought, BrDMP1B, BrDMP6B, and BrDMP9A&9B clustered together and were upregulated, whereas BrDMP2A&2B, BrDMP3A, BrDMP5A, BrDMP6A and BrDMP3B were downregulated (Fig. 7A, Figure S5-2). Under salt stress, BrDMP4A&4B, BrDMP2A&2B, BrDMP5Aand BrDMP6B were upregulated, while others were downregulated (Fig. 7B, Figure S5-3). Under heat stress, most genes were upregulated at 3 h but downregulated at 8 h and 24 h, with partial recovery at 48 h. BrDMP1A and BrDMP3A were consistently downregulated (Fig. 7C, Figure S5-4). Under cold stress, BrDMP1A and BrDMP6B clustered together with declining expression, while others showed transient upregulation (Fig. 7D, Figure S5-5). Overall, these patterns reveal that BrDMP genes exhibit diverse and stress-specific transcriptional responses, indicating that different family members may play distinct roles in adaptive reactions to drought, salinity, heat, and cold.

Expression patterns of BrDMP genes under phytohormone treatments

NAA, tZ, and ABA are key phytohormones involved in stress responses. Under exogenous NAA treatment, BrDMP genes clustered into two major branches (Fig. 8A, Figure S5-6). The first branch (BrDMP7A&7B, BrDMP3A, BrDMP1A, and BrDMP3B) exhibited initial upregulation followed by downregulation, with BrDMP3A showing significant induction at 0.5 h and 1 h. The second branch displayed gradual downregulation. In contrast, tZ treatment elicited more complex expression patterns (Fig. 8B, Figure S5-7). BrDMP6B expression decreased during 0–1 h but sharply increased at 2–4 h, while BrDMP4A&4B were consistently downregulated. Under ABA treatment, most BrDMP genes showed initial downregulation followed by recovery, particularly BrDMP3B, BrDMP1A, and BrDMP3A (Fig. 8C, and Figure S5-8). Overall, these results indicate that BrDMP genes exhibit highly dynamic and hormone-specific transcriptional responses, suggesting their involvement in multiple hormone-mediated regulatory pathways.

BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B locates in cell membrane

35Spro::BrDMP9A-eGFP and 35Spro::BrDMP9B-eGFP fusion proteins, constructed as shown as (Figure S3), emitted green fluorescence signals on the cell membrane, colocalizing with red fluorescent membrane markers (Fig. 9), confirming cell membrane localization, which is consistent with WoLF PSORT predictions, supporting their potential functional importance at the cell membrane.

BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B could partially rescue the seed abortion phenotype of A. thaliana dmp8, dmp9 double mutant

A complementation assay was conducted to determine whether BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B could rescue the double-fertilization defect in dmp8, dmp9 haploid-inducing mutants. Using the dmp8, dmp9 A. thaliana background, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B genes were expressed under the control of the AtDMP9 promoter, and the seed number and silique length were observed in the T1 generation. As shown in the figure, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B genes can partially compensate for the functions of AtDMP9A and AtDMP9B genes during double fertilization. This finding is consistent with the results presented by [7], where DMP-HI candidate genes from species such as B. napus rescued the phenotypes of A. thaliana dmp mutants (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Bioinformatics analysis identified 16 DMP genes in Chinese cabbage, unevenly distributed across 10 chromosomes. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using DMP protein sequences from seven species A. thaliana, Chinese cabbage, rice, tomato, maize, B. napus, and B. oleracea revealing five distinct subgroups, with subgroup III containing the most members. The clustering patterns of DMP proteins displayed monocot- or dicot-specific associations, suggesting a common ancestral origin predating the divergence of monocots and dicots. Gene structure analysis indicated that each BrDMP gene contains a conserved DUF679 domain and 3–5 transmembrane domains, with most BrDMPs consisting of a single large exon, implying specialized functional roles. The Ka/Ks ratios (< 1) for duplicated BrDMP gene pairs indicate purifying selection during Chinese cabbage evolution. The results of subcellular localization prediction indicate that the DMP protein in Chinese cabbage is localized to the cell membrane, consistent with observations in sweetpotato [39], while AtDMPs were predicted to localize to tonoplasts and endoplasmic reticulum, aligning with their known functions [5]. Gene duplication events, particularly segmental and tandem duplications, play crucial roles in gene family expansion and functional diversification, which may lead to redundancy [40]. Evolutionary analysis revealed three tandem-duplicated and six segmentally-duplicated BrDMP gene pairs, with segmental duplication being the primary driver of BrDMP family expansion, attributable to three whole-genome duplication events in Chinese cabbage [41].

qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated functional roles of DMPs in plant growth and development, though expression levels varied across tissues, reflecting functional specialization. BrDMP1A showed predominant expression in stamens, suggesting involvement in stamen development, while BrDMP1B, BrDMP3A, and BrDMP3B exhibited the highest expression in roots, indicating potential roles in root growth. Promoter analysis identified numerous cis-acting elements related to abiotic stress and phytohormone responses in BrDMP genes. Under conditions of abiotic stress and hormonal treatment, most DMP genes exhibited an initial upregulation followed by downregulation in expression levels. Zhang et al. observed that in pepper subjected to drought, osmotic, cold, and heat stresses, all DMP genes were uniformly upregulated, with no evidence of negative regulation [8]; This contrasts with our findings in Chinese cabbage, where several BrDMP genes showed both positive and negative regulation. Such differences likely reflect biological variation arising from the type of tissues sampled (e.g., reproductive vs. vegetative organs, early vs. late developmental stages), since stress-responsive transcription can be highly tissue-specific. Additionally, differences in genetic background, growth conditions, and stress treatment severity or duration may influence hormonal signaling and stress perception pathways, ultimately leading to distinct transcriptional patterns between species and experiments. Notably, BrDMP6B expression progressively increased under drought, osmotic, hormonal, and heat stresses while decreasing under cold stress, suggesting that they may play a positive regulatory role in the resistance of Chinese cabbage to drought, osmotic, hormonal, and heat stresses.

Recent studies demonstrate successful haploid inducer (HI) line creation via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of DMPs in crops such as maize, rice, wheat, tomato, tobacco, B. napus, and B. olerace [12, 14, 18, 42–44]. However, BrDMPs’ functions remain underexplored. In this study, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B (subgroup IV) clustered phylogenetically with known HI genes (AtDMP9, AtDMP8, ZmDMP), implying conserved roles. qRT-PCR revealed the highest BrDMP9A&9B expression in large flower buds and stamens, suggesting importance in stamen maturation. Subcellular localization confirmed cell membrane localization, consistent with reported ZmDMP, SlDMP, AtDMP8, and AtDMP9.Studies on AtDMP8 and AtDMP9 show that DMP mutations impair sperm-egg fusion, suggesting BrDMPs may participate in gamete recognition or fusion. Complementation assays indicated partial rescue of dmp8, dmp9 fertilization defects, supporting a role in pollination and fertilization. This is consistent with the results of Zhong et al. using a complementation strategy involving A. thaliana dmp8,dmp9 to identify DMP genes with conserved reproductive functions [7]. Thus, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B are potential HI candidates in Chinese cabbage.

Conventional pure line development in B. rapa requires multiple generations of selfing, whereas haploid breeding enables doubled haploid (DH) production within two generations, significantly accelerating breeding cycles [45]. While parthenogenesis induction via ectopic expression of key genes BABY BOOM and PARTHENOGENESIS and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated HI systems have been reported, limitations persist. BBM1, an AP2/ERF superfamily member specifically expressed in sperm cells, serves as a key embryogenesis trigger. Khanday et al. demonstrated that egg cell-specific BBM1 expression induces parthenogenesis in rice [46]. Similarly, Underwood et al. identified the PAR gene in apomictic dandelion and achieved parthenogenesis in lettuce via PAR transgenesis [47]. However, the requirement for transgene overexpression and T-DNA integration limits their agricultural application [47, 48]. Uniparental chromosome elimination represents another HI mechanism. CENH3 (centromere-specific histone 3), essential for centromere assembly and chromosome segregation, can induce haploidy when mutated, though the difficulty in obtaining loss-of-function mutants has hindered its widespread use [49, 50]. Alternatively, elevated ROS levels or mutations in ROS-related genes (MTL/NLD/PLA1, POD65, PLD3) can trigger sperm DNA fragmentation and HI. MTL (MATRILINEAL), also termed NLD or PLA1, encodes a pollen-specific phospholipase. Wang et al. achieved apomictic seed production in rice by simultaneously knocking out PAIR1, REC8, OSD1, and MTL. However, MTL’s monocot-specific conservation limits its dicot application [48, 51]. Peroxidase ZmPOD65 knockout yielded 7.7% HI rates in maize [52], while phospholipase ZmPLD3 editing confirmed HI induction, though mechanistic differences likely exist between monocots and dicots, given sequence divergence. Nuclear membrane fusion interference represents another HI strategy. ECS1/ECS2 deficiency causes gamete fusion defects and maternal haploidy, as demonstrated in rice [53]. Disrupting sperm-egg plasma membrane communication (KPL) or fusion (DMP) also induces haploids. KOKOPELLI (KPL), a sperm-specific nat-siRNA in A. thaliana, leads to single fertilization events when mutated [54]. Zhong et al. first cloned ZmDMP from Stock6 inducer lines - this DUF679(DOMAIN OF UNKNOWN FUNCTION 679)-containing membrane protein, when knocked out, induces 0.1–0.3% haploidy [7, 13]. The conserved presence of DMPs across angiosperms enables HI system development in dicots.

In conclusion, DMP genes currently represent the most promising HI candidates in dicots, with successful applications in horticultural crops. While BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B-based HI systems show great potential in Chinese cabbage, further experimental validation is required. Our comprehensive analysis nominates BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B as prime HI candidate genes for subsequent functional studies. Future research will focus on generating CRISPR/Cas9 knockout mutants of BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B in Chinese cabbage to directly evaluate their haploid induction efficiency, and further explore the molecular mechanisms by which these genes regulate gamete recognition and fertilization processes.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified 16 BrDMP genes in Chinese cabbage. Based on a comprehensive analysis of phylogenetic tree, tissue expression patterns, subcellular localization, and heterologous expression in A. thaliana, BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B genes could serve as candidate genes for haploid induction in Chinese cabbage. This study preliminarily analyzed the biological functions of the DMP gene family in Chinese cabbage, laying a theoretical foundation for subsequent research on haploid breeding of Chinese cabbage. Future work will involve generating CRISPR/Cas9 knockout mutants of BrDMP9A and BrDMP9B to directly validate their haploid induction capability.
