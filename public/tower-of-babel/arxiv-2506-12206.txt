1.1.
Related works

While it is not necessary for our result to hold, a natural example to keep in mind throughout the paper is when the coefficient distribution
\xi
is supported on the integers. In that case, the discriminant is also an integer, as it is a polynomial in the coefficients with integer coefficients, and serves as an algebraic invariant that helps studying the roots of a polynomial.

Our main motivation for studying the discriminant of a random Kac polynomial is the work of Bary-Soroker and Kozma
[
5
]
, and in particular a numerical study described in
[
5
, Section 4]
. That work studies irreducibility of random polynomials with integer coefficients, and in particular establishes that
f_{n}
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
with high probability as
n\to\infty
, for
\xi
which is uniformly distributed on
\{1,\ldots,240\}
. See also
[
8
]
for a stronger result in this direction, assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis. The irreducibility of
f_{n}
is established by showing that (with high probability) the Galois group of
f_{n}
contains the alternating group. In fact, in
[
5
, Section 4]
Bary-Soroker and Kozma conjecture that with high probability the Galois group of
f_{n}
is the full symmetric group, which in view of the above is equivalent to
\Delta(f_{n})
not being a square. In particular, based on numerical evidence, the work
[
5
]
predicted that
\log|\Delta(f_{n})|
is asymptotically normal with linear mean and variance; our main result shows that in fact the first order term grows like

n\log n
rather than
linearly. Motivated by this numerical study,
[
5
]
suggests the following heuristic: the discriminant of a random polynomial is a large random integer, and hence it is unlikely for it to be a square. Theorem
1
of this paper is a modest justification for the numerical observation from
[
5
, Section 4]
, that the discriminant of a random polynomial is typically a very large number which concentrates on a linear scale. We also mention the recent work by Hokken
[
13
]
, which computed the probability that the discriminant is a square in a related but different model of random skew-reciprocal polynomials.

One motivation for the previously mentioned works
[
5
,
8
]
is work on the van der Waerden conjecture. If one considers a random monic polynomial
P
of degree
d
where all non-leading coefficients are integers chosen independently and uniformly at random in
[-H,H]
, van der Waerden conjectured in 1936 that the probability
P
is reducible is
O(H^{-1})
as
H\to\infty
, and furthermore the probability the Galois group is not the symmetric group is asymptotically equal to the probability
f
is irreducible. A breakthrough work of Bhargava
[
6
]
proved the former statement. Further, the work reduced the latter, stronger conjecture to showing that the probability the Galois group is the alternating group is
o(H^{-1})
. This remains an open problem. This model for a random polynomial is often called the “box model”, and we refer the reader to the works
[
3
,
4
,
12
]
for more information about the discriminant of random polynomials sampled from the “box model”.

For Gaussian random polynomials (that is, when
\xi
is Gaussian), the behavior of
\log|\Delta(f_{n})|
can be analyzed more precisely. Indeed, in
[
18
]
the authors studied a variant of this problem on the Riemann sphere, with a different random ensemble of Gaussian polynomials. Importing the techniques from
[
18
]
, we suspect one can show that for
\xi
Gaussian we have that
\text{Var}\big(\log|\Delta(f_{n})|\big)
grows linearly in
n
and that the fluctuations are asymptotically normal. As this direction is quite technical and not so much related to the main motivation, we do not pursue this here.

1.2.
Heuristic explanation for the leading term

At first sight, it is not at all obvious why the leading term for
\log|\Delta(f_{n})|
should be
2n\log n
, or even why it should be universal in the choice of random coefficients. Before going into technical details, we give a heuristic explanation of why this should be the case. First, recall a classical fact about the roots of random Kac polynomials: with high probability, as
n\to\infty
, the roots of
f_{n}
cluster uniformly near the unit circle. In fact, this clustering occurs at the scale
n^{-1}
, meaning that there are
n\big(1-o(1)\big)
roots present in the annulus

\Big\{1-\frac{\omega(n)}{n}\leq|z|\leq 1+\frac{\omega(n)}{n}\Big\}\,,

as soon as
\omega(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, see
[
14
,
20
]
. Furthermore, the angular distribution of the roots is approximately uniformly distributed, as follows from the classical Erdős-Turán inequality
[
10
]
. Denoting by
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
the roots of
f_{n}
, we have that

|\Delta(f_{n})|=|\xi_{n}|^{n-2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})|

(see the proof of Claim
2
below). Hence, by splitting the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
into

{\tt I}=\Big\{|z|<1-\frac{\omega(n)}{n}\Big\}\,,\qquad{\tt II}=\Big\{1-\frac{\omega(n)}{n}\leq|z|\leq 1+\frac{\omega(n)}{n}\Big\}\,,\qquad{\tt III}=\mathbb{C}\setminus({\tt I}\cup{\tt II})\,,

we have that
2
2

2

Here and everywhere,
\alpha
represents a symbolic root of
f_{n}
, and a sum or a product over
\alpha
’s represents the respective sum or product over roots.

(4)

\log|\Delta(f_{n})|=\Big(\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt I}}+\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt II}}+\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt III}}\Big)\log|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|+n\log|\xi_{n}|+O(1)\,.

In the domain
{\tt I}
, it is not hard to see that
|f_{n}^{\prime}|
grows at most polynomially, and since there are
o(n)
roots in
{\tt I}
we get that the corresponding sum is asymptotically negligible and does not contribute to the leading term. On the other hand, for
z\in{\tt II}
a simple variance computation shows that typically

|f_{n}(z)|\approx\sqrt{n}\,,\qquad|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|\approx n^{3/2}\,.

Since there are
n(1-o(1))
roots in
{\tt II}
, we get that

\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt II}}\log|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\approx n\log(n^{3/2})=\frac{3}{2}n\log n\,.

Finally, to deal with roots from the domain
{\tt III}
, we note that typically, the derivative
f_{n}^{\prime}
grows like its leading coefficient, and we get

\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt III}}\log|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|+n\log|\xi_{n}|\approx\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt III}}\log|\xi_{n}n\alpha^{n-1}|+n\log|\xi_{n}|\approx n\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt III}}\log|\alpha|+n\log|\xi_{n}|\,.

We note that the distribution of roots in the domain
{\tt III}
is
not
universal. For instance, if
\xi\in\{-1,1\}
then there are never roots with
|\alpha|>2
, but if
\xi
is Gaussian then there is a positive probability of having a root with
|\alpha|>2
. Interestingly, the term
n\log|\xi_{n}|
saves the day.
Indeed, by applying
Jensen’s formula
[
1
, p.207]

(5)

\int_{0}^{1}\log|re^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta}-\alpha|\,{\rm d}\theta=\begin{cases}\log r&|\alpha|\leq r\,,\\
\log|\alpha|&|\alpha|>r\,,\end{cases}

we see that

n\sum_{\alpha\in{\tt III}}\log|\alpha|+n\log|\xi_{n}|=n\int_{0}^{1}\log|f_{n}(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})|\,{\rm d}\theta\approx n\log(n^{1/2})=\frac{1}{2}n\log n\,.

Plugging the above heuristic computation into (
4
) gives

\log|\Delta(f_{n})|\approx\Big(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Big)n\log n=2n\log n\,.

1.3.
Possibility of double roots

We briefly comment on our assumption that the coefficient distribution
\xi
has no atom at the origin. This assumption is natural, particularly because the discriminant is not consistently defined across polynomials of differing degrees. However, it is not essential for our result.
Indeed, the recent work
[
19
, Corollary 1.2]
(see also
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
) implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(f_{n}\ \text{has a double root}\big)=\big(\mathbb{P}(\xi_{0}=0)\big)^{2}\,.

Furthermore, it is apparent from (
2
) that
\Delta(f_{n})=0
if and only if
f_{n}
has a double root. In the course of the proof of Theorem
1
, we will in fact prove that with high probability as
n\to\infty
, we have

|\Delta(f_{n})|=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $f_{n}$ has a double root,}\\
n^{2n}e^{-n{\sf D}_{\ast}(1+o(1))}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

That is, if we do not assume anything about
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)
, then the asymptotic (
3
) holds unless some event with (possibly) positive probability occurs. To avoid this technicality, we shall adopt the assumption
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{0}=0)=0
throughout the paper. We also remark that the proof works equally well to obtain an analogous statement if one forces
\xi_{0}\equiv 1
(or equivalently
\xi_{n}\equiv 1
), which is another model sometimes considered, but we will not comment on this further.

1.4.
Sketch of proof

For a polynomial
P(z)=A\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\alpha_{j})
, let
P^{r}(z)=z^{n}P(z^{-1})
denote its reciprocal polynomial. Then
\Delta(P)=\Delta(P^{r})
and for the random polynomial (
1
),
f_{n}
and
f_{n}^{r}
have the same law. The first key step towards the proof of Theorem
1
is a convenient representation of the discriminant, which more visibly “sees” this distributional symmetry. Recall that the
Mahler measure
of a polynomial
P
is given by

(6)

M(P)=A\prod_{j=1}^{n}\max\{1,|\alpha_{j}|\}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log|P(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})|\,{\rm d}\theta\right)

where the last equality is Jensen’s formula (
5
). Although it is called a “measure”, the Mahler measure is in fact a multiplicative height function on polynomials, which is another well-studied algebraic invariant; see the book
[
17
]
for more background and references. Its relevance to our study of the discriminant is due to the following simple claim, which is a “symmetrized” version of (
4
).

Claim 2
.

For any polynomial
P
we have

|\Delta(P)|=\Big(\prod_{|\alpha|\leq 1}|P^{\prime}(\alpha)|\Big)\cdot\Big(\prod_{|\alpha|>1}\left|\frac{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}}\right|\Big)\cdot M(P)^{n-2}\,,

where the product is over all roots of
P
.

Proof.

For
P(z)=A\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-\alpha_{j})
, we have for any root
\alpha_{j}
that

P^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})=A\prod_{i\not=j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})\,.

Using the definition of the discriminant we see that

|\Delta(P)|=|A|^{2n-2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\prod_{i\not=j}|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\Big)=|A|^{n-2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|P^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})|

This is nothing but the representation of the discriminant as the resultant of the polynomials and its derivative. The claim now follows immediately from (
6
).
∎

The main advantage of this representation for the discriminant is that for the random polynomial
f_{n}
the variables

(7)

\prod_{|\alpha|<1}|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\prod_{|\alpha|>1}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}}\Big|

have the same law. Though perhaps not immediately obvious, (
7
) is a consequence of the reciprocal symmetry, and is proved in Claim
6
below.
Therefore, with Claim
2
we can rewrite
\log|\Delta(f_{n})|
as a sum of three random terms (see Lemma
5
below), two of which are equal in law, and our goal is the prove concentration for each term on the linear scale, as
n\to\infty
. First, we state the concentration result for the Mahler measure term:

Proposition 3
.

Let
f_{n}
be the random polynomial (
1
). Then

\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\,{\rm d}\theta\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,,

where
\gamma\approx 0.5772
is Euler’s constant.

Stated more explicitly, Proposition
3
shows concentration for the Mahler measure of random polynomials, namely that

M(f_{n})=\sqrt{n}\,e^{-\gamma/2}\big(1+o(1)\big)\,,

with high probability as
n\to\infty
.
We remark that a special case of Proposition
3
, when
\xi
takes the values
\{-1,+1\}
with equal probability, was already proved as an auxiliary result in
[
21
, Lemma 1.3]
. In Section
6
we will provide an alternative simple proof of Proposition
3
, which uses a result of Cook-Nguyen
[
9
]
and allows for more general distributions of coefficients.

The bulk of the technical work in this paper is to prove the corresponding concentration result for the terms (
7
). For
t\geq 0
we set

(8)

\Phi(t)=\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\cdot\log\bigg(\frac{\big(1+2t^{2}-\cosh(2t)\big)\cdot\big(1-\coth(t)\big)}{2t^{3}}\bigg)\,.

Here,
\sinh,\cosh
and
\coth
are the hyperbolic sine, cosine and cotangent, respectively. Obviously,
\Phi
is a continuous function and a simple computation shows

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\Phi(t)=-\frac{\log 3}{3}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{t^{2}}{\log t}\,\Phi(t)=-3\,.

Therefore,
\Phi
is an integrable function on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and we can set

(9)

{\sf c}_{\ast}=1-\gamma+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(t)\,{\rm d}t\,.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f_{n}
be the random polynomial (
1
) and assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=0
. Then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

where
{\sf c}_{\ast}
is given by (
9
).

We note that for
z
close to the unit circle, the standard deviation of
f_{n}^{\prime}(z)
is of order
n^{3/2}
, and hence the normalization appearing in Proposition
4
is natural. In view of the above, we can read out an explicit formula for the limiting constant
{\sf D}_{\ast}>0
appearing in Theorem
1
. It is given as

(10)

{\sf D}_{\ast}=\frac{\gamma}{2}-2(1-\gamma)-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(t)\,{\rm d}t\,,

where
\gamma\approx 0.5772
is Euler’s constant and
\Phi
is given by (
8
). A numerical approximation of the integral appearing above gives that
{\sf D}_{\ast}\approx 5.92947
.

To handle the sum from Proposition
4
we recall that with high probability as
n\to\infty
, the roots of
f_{n}
cluster uniformly around the unit circle at scale
n^{-1}
.
Therefore, it is reasonable to guess that

(11)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1-\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\,,

which is indeed the case, see Lemma
10
and Lemma
12
below. In view of (
11
), Proposition
4
boils down to showing that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1-\log^{3}n/n<|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

which we prove in Section
4
.

A key step in the analysis is to “remove the singularity” caused by the derivative being small at the roots. For instance, if the random polynomial
f_{n}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists\alpha\,;\,f_{n}(\alpha)=f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)=0\big)>0\,,

as is often the case for discrete coefficients distribution
\xi
, then the sum in Proposition
4
has infinite mean. To establish concentration, we therefore need to isolate the contribution of roots on which
f_{n}^{\prime}
is atypically small, and show that it is asymptotically negligible. This is done by importing some technical results from our work
[
19
]
, see Section
3
for more details. After that, we have reduced the proof of Proposition
4
to proving that

(12)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1-\log^{3}n/n<|\alpha|<1}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{polylog}(n)}\Big\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,.

To study the sum in (
12
), we apply a net argument. More explicitly, we construct a net
\mathcal{N}
of mesh size
n^{-1-\beta}
(for some small, fixed
\beta>0
) in the annulus
\{1-\log^{3}n/n\leq|z|\leq 1\}
. Each net point
z\in\mathcal{N}
is associated with a polar rectangle
\mathcal{R}_{z}
of area
n^{-1-2\beta}
. We then approximate the event that a root lies in
\mathcal{R}_{z}
by an event involving the linear approximation of
f_{n}
at
z
. This approximation enables us to transfer the analysis from the sum (
12
) over roots to a similar sum over the net
\mathcal{N}
. A key feature of the net
\mathcal{N}
is its relatively coarse width. For
z\in\mathcal{N}
, the probability that
\mathcal{R}_{z}
contains a root is approximately
n^{-2\beta}
. If we take
\beta
to be sufficiently small, this allows us to use a standard application of the Berry–Esseen theorem to compare the probabilities of these events with those arising from the ‘Gaussian version’ of
f_{n}
, i.e. when the coefficients are Gaussian random variables. Through this Gaussian comparison, we show that the sum in (
12
) is close in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
to the corresponding sum over
\mathcal{N}
, and that the variance of the latter tends to zero as
n\to\infty
.

The sketch described in the paragraph above establishes the concentration (
12
), and it remains to identify the actual limit by computing the limit of expectations. Indeed, the constant
{\sf c}_{\ast}
arises from an explicit computation of the expected sum in the case where
f_{n}
has Gaussian coefficients. In that setting, a Kac–Rice type formula yields an exact expression for the expected sum over roots. After that, a straightforward asymptotic analysis of the formula gives the limiting expectation
{\sf c}_{\ast}
, which (by the Gaussian comparison) also governs the limiting expectation in the general (non-Gaussian) case.

2.1.
Preliminaries

As we already mentioned in the introduction, the starting point of the proof is a convenient representation for the logarithm of the discriminant, which follows from Claim
2
in the introduction. This representation will allow us the “see” the concentration on scale
n
, as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
P
be a polynomial of degree
n
with no double roots and no root on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\log|\Delta(P)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|P^{\prime}(\alpha)\right|+\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}}\right|+(n-2)\int_{0}^{1}\log|P(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})|\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|+\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{P^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}n^{3/2}}\right|+(n-2)\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{P(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\,{\rm d}\theta+(2n-1)\log n\,,

where the sums are over roots of
P
.

Proof.

If
P
has no double roots, then
P^{\prime}(\alpha)\neq 0
for all roots
\alpha
. We may then take the logarithm of both sides of the equality from Claim
2
, which in view of (
6
) proves the first equality. Adding and subtracting
(2n-1)\log n
and distributing
\frac{3}{2}\log n
to each of the
n
roots, as well as
(n-2)\log\sqrt{n}
to the Mahler measure term completes the proof.
∎

We note that Proposition
3
shows concentration for the integral (i.e. the Mahler measure term) on the right-hand side of Lemma
5
, while Proposition
4
shows concentration for the first sum appearing in this expression. Next on the agenda, we want to show that for random polynomials, both sums in fact have the same law.

Claim 6
.

Let
f_{n}
be the random polynomial (
1
), then the random variables

\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}n^{3/2}}\right|

have the same distribution.

Proof.

Define
g_{n}(z)=z^{n}f_{n}(z^{-1})=\xi_{0}z^{n}+\xi_{1}z^{n-1}+\ldots+\xi_{n}
. Then
g_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}f_{n}
, and every root of
f_{n}
corresponds to a reciprocal root of
g_{n}
(and vice-versa). Denoting by
\beta
a symbolic root of
g_{n}
, we have

g_{n}^{\prime}(\beta)=n\beta^{n-1}f_{n}(\beta^{-1})-\beta^{n-2}f_{n}^{\prime}(\beta^{-1})=-\beta^{n-2}f_{n}^{\prime}(\beta^{-1})\,.

We get that

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{|\beta|<1}\log\left|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(\beta)}{n^{3/2}}\right|=\sum_{|\beta|<1}\log\left|\beta^{n-2}\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\beta^{-1})}{n^{3/2}}\right|=\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}n^{3/2}}\right|\,,

as desired.
∎

2.2.
Proof of the main result

After collecting the necessary preliminaries, we now show how the proof of Theorem
1
follows from Proposition
3
and Proposition
4
. To apply Lemma
5
with
P=f_{n}
, we need to know that with high probability there are no roots of
f_{n}
on the unit circle itself. While proving this fact is not very difficult, we state a quantitative statement, deduced from a much stronger statement due to Cook and Nguyen
[
9
]
, which we will also use when analyzing the Mahler measure of a random polynomial.

Claim 7
.

Let
f_{n}
be the random polynomial (
1
), then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\min_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f_{n}(z)|\geq\frac{1}{n}\Big)=1\,.

In particular, we also have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\,\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\Big)=0\,.

Proof.

The claim follows from a universality result on the minimum modulus on the unit circle of random polynomials
[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
(building on the same result for Gaussian polynomials
[
22
]
). It states that

\sqrt{n}\min_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f_{n}(z)|

converges in law as
n\to\infty
to a non-trivial exponential random variable. In particular

\mathbb{P}\Big(\,\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sqrt{n}\min_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\Big)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\,,

as claimed.
∎

We are ready to prove the main result of the paper.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By
[
19
, Corollary 1.2]
we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\,f_{n}\ \text{has a double root}\,\Big)=0\,.

Hence, in view of Claim
7
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T})=1-o(1)
as
n\to\infty
, where

\mathcal{T}=\Big\{\text{$f_{n}$ has no double roots and no root on $\mathbb{S}^{1}$}\Big\}\,.

On the event
\mathcal{T}
, we can apply Lemma
5
and get that

(13)

\frac{1}{n}\Big(\log|\Delta(f_{n})|-2n\log n\Big)\\
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}n^{3/2}}\right|+\frac{n-2}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\,{\rm d}\theta+o(1)\,.

By Proposition
3
we have that

\frac{n-2}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\,{\rm d}\theta\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,,

whereas by Proposition
4
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,.

Finally, by Claim
6
both sums on the right-hand side of (
13
) have the same law, and another application of Proposition
4
shows that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|>1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n-2}n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,.

Altogether, as
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}^{c})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
, relation (
13
) yields that

\frac{1}{n}\Big(\log|\Delta(f_{n})|-2n\log n\Big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+2\,{\sf c}_{\ast}\,.

It remains to note that

-\frac{\gamma}{2}+2\,{\sf c}_{\ast}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_of_c_ast}}}{{=}}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+2(1-\gamma)+2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(t)\,{\rm d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:intro_def_of_D_ast}}}{{=}}-{\sf D}_{\ast}\,,

which is what we wanted to show.
∎

2.3.
Control on the maximum

We conclude this section by collecting some basic analytic facts that will be helpful in the proofs of Proposition
3
and Proposition
4
. We start by showing how our sub-Gaussian assumption on the random coefficients allows us to control the maximum of the random polynomial
f_{n}
(and its first two derivatives) via the Salem-Zygmund inequality (see
[
15
, Chapter 6]
). Crucially, the maximal values are
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
away from the typical values, which is the context of our next lemma.

Lemma 8
.

For the event

(14)

\mathcal{G}=\bigcap_{\ell=0}^{2}\Big\{\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\big|f_{n}^{(\ell)}\big((1+1/n)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\big)\big|\leq n^{\ell+1/2}\log n\Big\}\,,

there exists
c>0
so that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G})\geq 1-e^{-c\log^{2}n}
.

Proof.

For
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{2\ell}\big(1+1/n\big)^{2k}\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{2\ell}\lesssim n^{2\ell+1}\,.

Therefore, for some
c>0
small enough we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\big|f_{n}^{(\ell)}\big((1+1/n)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\big)\big|\geq n^{\ell+1/2}\log n\Big)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\big|f_{n}^{(\ell)}\big((1+1/n)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\big)\big|\geq c\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{2\ell}\big(1+1/n\big)^{2k}\Big)^{1/2}\log n\bigg)\leq\exp\Big(-c\log^{2}n\Big)\,.

The second inequality in the above display is the Salem-Zygmund inequality
[
15
, Chapter 6, Theorem 1]
. By union bounding the above over
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
we get what we want.
∎

We will also make use of a Jensen-type bound on the number of roots of an analytic function, in terms of its growth.

Claim 9
.

Suppose
f
is an analytic function in
\mathbb{D}
, continuous up to the boundary
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and such that
f(0)\not=0
. Then for every
r\in(0,1)
we have

\#\big\{|\alpha|\leq r\,:\,f(\alpha)=0\big\}\leq\frac{1}{1-r}\log\Big(\frac{M_{f}}{|f(0)|}\Big)\,,

where
M_{f}=\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f(e^{{\rm i}\theta})|
.

Proof.

By Jensen’s formula we have

\int_{0}^{1}\log|f(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})|\,{\rm d}\theta-\log|f(0)|=\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\Big)\,.

Clearly

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\log|f(e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta})|\,{\rm d}\theta\leq\log M_{f}\,,

and furthermore

\sum_{|\alpha|<1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\Big)\geq\#\big\{|\alpha|\leq r\,:\,f(\alpha)=0\big\}\log\Big(\frac{1}{r}\Big)\,.

Altogether, we get the inequality

\#\big\{|\alpha|\leq r\,:\,f(\alpha)=0\big\}\leq\frac{1}{\log(1/r)}\log\Big(\frac{M_{f}}{|f(0)|}\Big)\,,

and by bounding
\log(1/r)\geq 1-r
for
r\in(0,1)
we get the claim.
∎

3.
Negligible contributions away from the annulus

The goal of this section is to prove (
11
) from the introduction. That is, to show that roots which are sufficiently far away from the unit circle do not contribute to the sum in Proposition
4
. We will show (
11
) in two steps. We first handle the sum of roots with
|\alpha|\leq 1-\delta
for some fixed but small
\delta>0
, for which we lean on our recent result
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
about double zeros of infinite random power series. After that, we turn to handle roots in the annulus
\{1-\delta\leq|\alpha|\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n\}
, where we again lean on our work
[
19
]
to show it is uncommon to have a root in this annulus on which the derivative is small.

3.1.
Roots from inside the unit disk

In this section we deal with roots with
|\alpha|\leq 1-\delta
, for some small but fixed
\delta>0
.

Lemma 10
.

For each fixed
\delta>0
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1-\delta}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\,.

For a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\xi_{k}
, all with common sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
, we set

(15)

f_{\infty}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}z^{k}\,.

By the Borel-Cantelli lemmas, it is not hard to see that almost surely
f_{\infty}
defines a random analytic function in
\mathbb{D}
. As this analytic function is also the distributional limit of
f_{n}
in the disk
\{|z|\leq 1-\delta\}
as
n\to\infty
, the following theorem from our work
[
19
]
becomes relevant.

Theorem 11

(
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
f_{\infty}
be given by (
15
), and assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=0
. Then almost surely
f_{\infty}
does not have a double zero in
\mathbb{D}
.

Proof of Lemma
10
.

We will in fact prove a slightly stronger statement: let
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables, and let
f_{n}
and
f_{\infty}
be the corresponding Kac polynomial (
1
) and infinite Taylor series (
15
), respectively. As a consequence of the convergence of (
15
), we have that almost surely
f_{n}
converges to
f_{\infty}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
, and in particular in
\{|z|\leq 1-\delta\}
. We will show that

(16)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|<1-\delta}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}0\,,

with respect to this coupling with
f_{\infty}
. As almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability, Lemma
10
will follow once (
16
) is established. Indeed, by Hurwitz’s theorem the number of roots of
f_{n}
in
\{|z|\leq 1-\delta\}
is eventually at most the number of roots of
f_{\infty}
in
\{|z|\leq 1-\delta/2
} which is also almost surely finite by the identity theorem for analytic functions. Hence

\frac{\log n}{n}\,\#\big\{|\alpha|\leq 1-\delta\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\big\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}0\,.

It remains to deal with a potential singularity of
\log|f_{n}^{\prime}|
at a root. We already asserted that almost surely
f_{\infty}
has only finitely many zeros in
\{|z|\leq 1-\delta\}
. By Theorem
11
, all of these zeros have multiplicity one, and hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<1-\delta\\
f_{\infty}(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log|f_{\infty}^{\prime}(\alpha)|

is almost surely finite. By Hurwitz’s theorem, almost surely for
n
large enough
f_{n}
has no double roots, and we get that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<1-\delta\\
f_{n}(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<1-\delta\\
f_{\infty}(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log|f_{\infty}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\,,

and in particular

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|<1-\delta\\
f_{n}(\alpha)=0\end{subarray}}\log|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}0\,.

This proves (
16
), and hence also the lemma.
∎

3.2.
Roots on which the derivative is small I

The next thing on our agenda is to get rid of intermediate roots, i.e. those roots in the annulus
\{1-\delta\leq|z|\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n\}
for
\delta>0
small and fixed.

Lemma 12
.

There exists
\beta>0
and
C>0
so that for all
\delta>0
we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\ \Bigg|\sum_{1-\delta\leq|\alpha|\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n}\log\bigg|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\bigg|\,\Bigg|\geq\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\,\Bigg)\leq C\delta^{\beta}\,.

To prove Lemma
12
, we will need the following claim, which is implicit in
[
19
]
.

Claim 13
.

There exists
\beta>0
small enough and
C>0
so that for all
\delta>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\Big\{1-\delta\leq|z|\leq 1-\frac{\log^{3}(n)}{n}\Big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq 1\Big)\leq C\delta^{\beta}

for all
n
large enough.

Proof.

Let
N=\lfloor\log_{2}\big(\delta n-\log^{3}(n)+1\big)\rfloor+1
. For
0\leq k\leq N
we set

\rho_{k}=1-\frac{\log^{3}(n)+2^{k}-1}{n}\,,

and so,
\rho_{0}=1-\log^{3}(n)/n
and
1-2\delta\leq\rho_{N}\leq 1-\delta
. By
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
, there exists
\beta>0
small so that

(17)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\big\{\rho_{k+1}\leq|z|\leq\rho_{k}\big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq 1\Big)\lesssim(1-\rho_{k})^{\beta}\,.

Summing (
17
) over
0\leq k\leq N
, we get by the union bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\Big\{1-\delta\leq|z|\leq 1-\frac{\log^{3}(n)}{n}\Big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq 1\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{N}(1-\rho_{k})^{\beta}\lesssim\Big(\frac{2^{N}}{n}\Big)^{\beta}\lesssim\delta^{\beta}\,,

as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
12
.

Recall the definition (
14
) of the event
\mathcal{G}
, and consider the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\exists\alpha\in\Big\{1-\delta\leq|z|\leq 1-\frac{\log^{3}(n)}{n}\Big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq 1\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\{|\xi_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\Big\}\,.

On the event
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{B}_{1}^{c}
, for each root
\alpha\in\{1-\delta\leq|z|\leq 1\}
we have

1\leq|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq n^{3/2}\log n\,.

Furthermore, by Claim
9
, on the event
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}^{c}
we have

\#\Big\{|\alpha|\leq 1-\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\Big\}\lesssim\frac{n}{\log^{3}n}\,\log\Big(\frac{n}{|\xi_{0}|}\Big)\lesssim\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\,.

Hence, on the event
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{B}_{1}^{c}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}^{c}
we have

\Bigg|\sum_{1-\delta\leq|\alpha|\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n}\log\bigg|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\bigg|\,\Bigg|\lesssim\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\log n\leq\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}}

for all
n
large enough. To conclude the proof, it remains to note that as
n\to\infty
we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})=o(1)
by Lemma
8
,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{2})=o(1)
by our assumption that
\xi
has no atom at
0
, and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{1})\leq C\delta^{\beta}
by Claim
13
.
∎

3.3.
Roots on which the derivative is small II

By combining Lemma
10
and Lemma
12
, we have essentially proved (
11
). Therefore, to complete the proof of Proposition
4
, we want to show that

(18)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1-\log^{3}n/n<|\alpha|<1}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

which we do in Section
4
below. Denote by

(19)

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{z\,:\,1-\frac{\log^{3}(n)}{n}\leq|z|\leq 1\Big\}\,.

Before we move on, we collect another lemma, which basically shows that we can restrict the sum in (
18
) only to those roots on which the derivative is not too small.

Lemma 14
.

We have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\log_{-}\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\boldsymbol{1}\left\{\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\right\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\,.

We again separate out a claim which imports the relevant bound from
[
19
]
.

Claim 15
.

With
\mathcal{A}
given by (
19
), we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)=0\,.

Proof.

We again yield to
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
, and argue similarly as in the proof of Claim
13
. Indeed, for
k=0,1,\ldots,\widetilde{N}
with
\widetilde{N}=1+\lfloor\log\big(\log^{3}n\big)\rfloor
, we denote by

\widetilde{\rho}_{k}=1-\frac{2^{k}}{n}\,.

Then
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}=1-n^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\rho}_{\widetilde{N}}\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n
.
By
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]

we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\big\{\widetilde{\rho}_{k+1}\leq|z|\leq\widetilde{\rho}_{k}\big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\big\{\widetilde{\rho}_{k+1}\leq|z|\leq\widetilde{\rho}_{k}\big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{(1-\widetilde{\rho_{k}})^{-5/4}}{\log n}\Big)\lesssim\frac{1}{\log n}\,.

Furthermore, another application of
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
(the case
k=-1
therein) shows that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\Big\{1-\frac{1}{n}\leq|z|\leq 1\Big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{4}n}\,.

By union bounding over the concentric annuli we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists

\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\Big\{1-\frac{1}{n}\leq|z|\leq 1\Big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k=0}^{\widetilde{N}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\alpha\in\big\{\widetilde{\rho}_{k+1}\leq|z|\leq\widetilde{\rho}_{k}\big\}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{4}n}\Big)\lesssim\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\,,

as desired.
∎

Denote by

(20)

\mathcal{S}=\Big\{z=re^{{\rm i}\theta}\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,|\theta|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\quad\text{or}\quad|\theta-\pi|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big\}\,.

Before we proceed to prove Lemma
14
, we state another claim which basically says that roots in the “almost real” sector
\mathcal{R}
do not contribute to the sum (
18
).

Claim 16
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be given by (
20
), then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\#\{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\}\leq\sqrt{n}\,(\log n)\Big)=1\,.

Proof.

By the Erdős-Turán inequality
[
10
, Theorem 1]
we have

\#\{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{n}+16\sqrt{n\,\log\bigg(\frac{\max_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f_{n}(z)|}{\sqrt{|\xi_{0}||\xi_{n}|}}\bigg)}\,.

Therefore, on the event
\mathcal{G}\cap\{|\xi_{0}|\geq n^{-1}\}\cap\{|\xi_{n}|\geq n^{-1}\}
we have

\#\{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\}\lesssim\sqrt{n\log(n^{3/2}\log n)}\lesssim\sqrt{n\log n}\,,

which yields the claim, in view of Lemma
8
and our assumption of no atom of
\xi
at the origin.
∎

Proof of Lemma
14
.

Combining Claim
15
and Claim
16
, the lemma will follow once we show that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{-}\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\boldsymbol{1}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log^{4}n}\leq\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\right\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\,.

which, in turn, would immediately follow from

(21)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\#\Big\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log^{4}n}\leq\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\bigg]\lesssim\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\,.

This bound is established via a net argument. Let
\kappa=n^{-5/4}\cdot(\log n)^{-6}
and let
{\sf N}
be a
\kappa
-net in
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
|{\sf N}|\leq 10\,m(\mathcal{A})\,\kappa^{-2}\lesssim n^{3/2}\log^{15}n
. We first note that, on the event
\mathcal{G}
, there cannot be two distinct roots from the left-hand side of (
21
) which are at distance
\leq\kappa
. Indeed, suppose that
\alpha\not=\alpha^{\prime}
are two such roots, then Taylor’s theorem implies that

f_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})=f_{n}(\alpha)+(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)+O\Big(\frac{|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|^{2}}{2}n^{5/2}\log n\Big)\,,

and hence

|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\lesssim\kappa n^{5/2}\log n=\frac{n^{5/4}}{\log^{5}n}\,.

That is, such pairs
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
are not counted in (
21
), and we get that

(22)

\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\cdot\#\Big\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log^{4}n}\leq\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\\
\leq\sum_{z\in{\sf N}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{D}(z,\kappa)\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\,.

We now estimate the probabilities appearing on the right-hand side of (
22
). Indeed, on the event
\mathcal{G}
, if there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{D}(z,\kappa)
with
f_{n}(\alpha)=0
and
|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq n^{3/2}/\log^{10}n
, then

|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|\leq|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|+O\big(\kappa n^{5/2}\log n\big)\leq 2\,\frac{n^{3/2}}{\log^{10}n}

and

|f_{n}(z)|\leq\kappa n^{3/2}\log n\leq\frac{n^{1/4}}{\log^{2}n}\,.

We see that

\mathcal{G}\cap\Big\{\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{D}(z,\kappa)\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\\
\subset\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}(z)|}{n^{1/2}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log^{2}n}\,,\ \frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\,,

and a standard small-ball bound for
\big(f_{n}(z),f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)
(e.g.
[
19
, Claim 3.5]
) implies that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{|f_{n}(z)|}{n^{1/2}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log^{2}n}\,,\ \frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\bigg)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\log^{24}n}\,.

Plugging the above into (
22
) yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\#\Big\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}\,:\,f_{n}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\log n}\leq\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\lesssim n\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})+|{\sf N}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\log^{24}n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma}~\ref{lemma:Salem-Zygmund}}}{{\lesssim}}n\,e^{-c\log^{2}n}+\frac{n}{\log^{7}n}\,.

Hence, (
21
) is proved (with a
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
room to spare) and the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

4.
Law of large numbers for sum in the annulus

The goal of this section is to complete the proof of Proposition
4
, which we will do except for the actual computation of the limit, which we postpone to Section
5
. Recall that
\mathcal{A}
the the annulus (
19
) and that
\mathcal{S}
is the “almost real” sector (
20
). In view of reductions preformed in Section
3
above, we restrict our attention to the sum

(23)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\,.

Let
\mathbb{H}=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\text{Im}(z)>0\big\}
be the upper half-plane. Since
f_{n}
has real coefficients, the sum (
23
) consists of two equal contributions, from
\mathbb{H}
and from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{H}
, and it is equal to

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{H}\cap\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\,.

We will show that the sum above is in fact close (in
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
) to a discrete sum over a net in the annulus
\mathcal{A}
. We will then show, by comparing the original polynomial to a Gaussian polynomial in the annulus, that the corresponding net sum is concentrated as
n\to\infty
.

4.1.
Setting up the net argument

We will take
\beta>0
to be a sufficiently small constant (
\beta=10^{-3}
will be good enough). For
\eta=n^{-1-\beta}
and
r_{0}=1-\log^{3}n/n
we set

M_{1}=\lfloor 10\eta^{-1}\rfloor\,\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{2}=\lfloor 10\,\eta^{-1}\log(r_{0})\rfloor\,.

The net
\mathcal{N}
is defined via

\mathcal{N}=\bigg\{\exp\Big(\frac{2b-1}{2M_{2}}\cdot\log(r_{0})+{\rm i}\pi\frac{2a-1}{2M_{1}}\Big)\ :\quad{\Large\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\{\lfloor n^{-1/2}M_{1}\rfloor,\ldots,M_{1}-\lfloor n^{-1/2}M_{1}\rfloor\}\\[2.87996pt]
b\in\{1,\ldots,M_{2}\}\end{subarray}}\,\bigg\}\,.

In words,
\mathcal{N}
is an
\eta
-net in
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, and furthermore
|\mathcal{N}|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{1+2\beta})
. For each
z\in\mathcal{N}
we denote the associated polar rectangle by

\mathcal{R}_{z}=\left\{z\cdot\exp\left(\frac{t}{2M_{2}}\cdot\log(r_{0})+{\rm i}\pi\frac{s}{2M_{1}}\right):t,s\in[-1,1]\right\}\,.

We also define

\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}=\big\{w:\mathrm{dist}(w,\mathcal{R}_{z})\leq n^{-1-3\beta/2}\big\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}=\big\{w\in\mathcal{R}_{z}:\mathrm{dist}(w,\mathcal{R}_{z}^{c})\geq n^{-1-3\beta/2}\big\}\,.

For
z\in\mathcal{N}
, we will consider the events

(24)

\mathcal{C}_{z}^{\pm}:=\left\{z-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}\in\mathcal{R}_{z}^{\pm}\right\}\cap\left\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10\mp 1}n}\right\}\,.

Roughly speaking, the event (
24
) occurs if the linear approximation of
f_{n}
at the point
z
predicts a root in the rectangle
\mathcal{R}_{z}
, while the derivative is just of typical size. The next claim is a simple consequence of this observation.

Claim 17
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)=\widetilde{O}(1)\,.

The proof of Claim
17
is postponed to Section
5
below, where it is derived as a consequence of a more general statement (see Lemma
24
below). We note that in fact this sum is
O(1)
(the proof of Lemma
23
implicitly shows this), but for its application in the next Lemma, a bound of
\widetilde{O}(1)
is good enough. The next lemma shows how the sum over the net points captures the asymptotic size of (
23
). We note that the factor of two appearing in the net sum is due to the fact that
\mathcal{N}
is a net in the upper half plane and the contribution from the lower half plane is identical.

Lemma 18
.

On the event
\mathcal{G}
given by (
14
), for
\bullet\in\{-,+\}
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}+O(n^{-\beta/2})\,,

and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\geq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}}+O(n^{-\beta/2})\,.

Proof.

We start with the upper bound. Let
\mathbb{H}=\big\{z\,:\text{Im}(z)>0\big\}
denote the upper half-plane. Clearly, each root
\alpha\in(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S})\cap\mathbb{H}
is contained in
\mathcal{R}_{z}
for some
z\in\mathcal{N}
. We first show that, on the event
\mathcal{G}
, the existence of a root
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{z}
with
|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\geq n^{3/2}/\log^{10}n
implies that
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}
holds. Obviously

(25)

|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|\geq|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|-\widetilde{O}(\eta n^{5/2})\geq|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\,\big(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-\beta})\big)\,.

Furthermore, Taylor’s theorem implies that

0=f_{n}(\alpha)=f_{n}(z)+(\alpha-z)f_{n}^{\prime}(z)+\widetilde{O}(\eta^{2}n^{5/2})\,,

and hence (
25
) shows that

z-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}=\alpha+\widetilde{O}(n\eta^{2})=\alpha+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1-2\beta})\,.

That is, the event
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}
holds. It remains to note that, on the event
\mathcal{G}
, there are no two distinct roots
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
from the sum (
23
) in the same
\mathcal{R}_{z}
. Otherwise, we would have

f_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})=f_{n}(\alpha)+(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)+\widetilde{O}\big(n^{5/2}|\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha|^{2}\big)\,,

which gives
|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|=\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2-\beta})
, which is a contradiction. Altogether, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\big(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-\beta})\big)\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}+\widetilde{O}\Big(\frac{n^{-\beta}}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Claim}~\ref{claim:sum_of_probabilities_over_net_points_is_bounded}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}+\widetilde{O}(n^{-\beta})\,.

To prove the corresponding lower bound, we will first show that if
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}
occurs, then necessarily there exists
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}_{z}
with
f_{n}(\alpha)=0
. Indeed, define the linear approximation
L_{z}(\zeta)=f_{n}(z)+(\zeta-z)f_{n}^{\prime}(z)
around
z
and note that
L_{z}(\zeta_{0})=0
, where

\zeta_{0}=z-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}.

On the event
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}
, we have
\zeta_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}
. To show there is actually a root of
f_{n}
nearby, we will turn to Rouché’s theorem. Indeed, let
\gamma=n^{-1-7\beta/4}=\eta n^{3\beta/4}
and note that

|L_{z}(\zeta_{0}+\gamma e^{{\rm i}\theta})|=\gamma|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(\gamma n^{3/2})=\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{1/2-7\beta/4})\,

for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. On the event
\mathcal{G}
, Taylor’s theorem allows us to bound the difference

\big|L_{z}(\zeta_{0}+\gamma e^{{\rm i}\theta})-f_{n}(\zeta_{0}+\gamma e^{{\rm i}\theta})\big|\leq\widetilde{O}(\eta^{2}n^{5/2})=\widetilde{O}(n^{1/2-2\beta})\,.

Hence, Rouché’s theorem implies that
f_{n}
and
L_{z}
both have a single root in
\mathbb{D}(\zeta_{0},\gamma)\subset\mathcal{R}_{z}
. It remains to perform a simple Taylor expansion (the same one as in (
25
)) to show that at this root, we have
|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\geq n^{3/2}/\log^{10}n
. Since the interiors of
\{\mathcal{R}_{z}\}_{z\in\mathcal{N}}
are disjoint, we arrive at

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\big(1+\widetilde{O}(n^{-\beta})\big)\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}+\widetilde{O}(n^{-\beta})

which is the desired lower bound, and we are done.
∎

4.2.
Gaussian comparison

With Lemma
18
, we see that to prove concentration of (
23
) we need to understand the corresponding net sums over
\mathcal{N}
. This is achievable as we know that the random polynomial
f_{n}
(and its derivative) evaluated at finitely many separated points is roughly Gaussian. To better explain this point, let

(26)

g_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma_{k}\,z^{k}\,,

be a random Kac polynomial with
\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
being i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian random variables
3
3

3

We say that
Z=X+{\rm i}Y
is a standard complex Gaussian if
X,Y
are independent Gaussian random variables with mean zero and variance
1/2
.
. We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{g},\,\mathbb{E}_{g}
, the corresponding probability measure and expectation operator. The next lemma is a consequence of the Berry-Esseen theorem:

Lemma 19
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, then

\sup_{K\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(f_{n}(z),f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)\in K\Big)-\mathbb{P}_{g}\Big(\big(g_{n}(z),g_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)\in K\Big)\Big|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})

where the supremum is over all convex sets
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Furthermore, for
z,w\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}
, we have

\sup_{K\subset\mathbb{C}^{4}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(f_{n}(z),f_{n}^{\prime}(z),f_{n}(w),f_{n}^{\prime}(w)\big)\in K\Big)-\mathbb{P}_{g}\Big(\big(g_{n}(z),g_{n}^{\prime}(z),g_{n}(w),g_{n}^{\prime}(w)\big)\in K\Big)\Big|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})

where here the supremum is over all convex sets
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{4}
.

Lemma
19
is deduced from a standard, sufficiently general Berry-Esseen theorem in Section
7
. As a first application, we prove the following simple claim, showing that the events
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}
and
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}
are close.

Claim 20
.

For all
z\in\mathcal{N}
we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}\big)\leq n^{-9\beta/4}\,.

Proof.

We first note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(z-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}\in\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}\in n\log^{2}n\cdot\big(\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}-z\big)\Big)+e^{-c\log^{2}n}\,,

where the inequality is just Lemma
8
. We bound the Lebesgue measure of the set in this event by

m\Big(n\log^{2}n\cdot\big(\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}-z\big)\Big)\lesssim n^{2}\log^{4}n\,\big(n^{-1-\beta}\big)^{2}n^{-\beta/2}\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-5\beta/2})\,.

Covering this set with a constant number of disks with radii
r=\widetilde{O}(n^{-5\beta/4})
, we can apply Lemma
19
and the union bound to get

\mathbb{P}\Big(-\frac{f_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}\in n\log^{2}n\cdot\big(\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{z}^{-}-z\big)\Big)\lesssim\sup_{x\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}_{g}\Big(\frac{g_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}\in\mathbb{D}(x,r)\Big)+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})=\widetilde{O}(n^{-5\beta/2})

where the last inequality is due to the fact that the density of the Gaussian
g_{n}(z)/\sqrt{n}
is
\widetilde{O}(1)
.
∎

A simple corollary of the above is a bound on the
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
distance between the sum (
23
) and the sum over net points.

Lemma 21
.

For
\bullet\in\{-,+\}
we have

\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg|\lesssim n^{-\beta/5}\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
8
, we can bound the expectation on the event
\mathcal{G}
, as both random variables have
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
growing at most polynomially in
n
, while
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\leq\exp(-c\log^{2}n)
. On the event
\mathcal{G}
, Lemma
18
shows that the
L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
distance is bounded by

n^{-\beta/2}+\frac{\log n}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{z}^{-}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Claim}~\ref{claim:difference_between_C_+and_C_-_small}}}{{\lesssim}}n^{-\beta}+\frac{\log n}{n}\,|\mathcal{N}|\,n^{-9\beta/4}\lesssim n^{-\beta/5}\,,

as desired.
∎

4.3.
Mean and variance of the net sum

As another application of the Gaussian comparison (Lemma
19
), we can show that the sum over net points is is concentrated. This is a consequence of the next claim, which states that the net sum decorrelates for separated points.

Claim 22
.

For
z,w\in\mathcal{N}
such that
|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}
we have

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\cdot\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\Big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]\bigg|\leq n^{-1/20}\,,

for both
\bullet\in\{-,+\}
.

As we already mentioned, Claim
22
is a simple consequence of Lemma
19
and is proved in Section
7
below. Yet another consequence of the Gaussian comparison is that we can compute the limiting constant for the sum over the net via the Kac-Rice formula. This will be performed in Section
5
, but for now we can state the following lemma:

Lemma 23
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]={\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

where
{\sf c}_{\ast}
is given by (
9
).

4.4.
Combining the ingredients: Proof of Proposition
4

We conclude this section by proving

(27)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

which is exactly the statement of Proposition
4
. As we are proving convergence in probability, Lemma
8
asserts that we may assume throughout the event
\mathcal{G}
holds.
Indeed, towards (
27
) we first show that

(28)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0\,.

Fix some
\delta>0
and observe that for all
{\varepsilon}>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\,\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\bigg|\geq{\varepsilon}\bigg)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\,\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq 1-\delta}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\bigg|\geq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\bigg)+\mathbb{P}\bigg(\,\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1-\delta\leq|\alpha|\leq 1-\log^{3}n/n}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\bigg|\geq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\bigg)\,.

By Lemma
10
, the first term in the sum tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
, and Lemma
12
shows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\,\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\mathcal{A}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\bigg|\geq{\varepsilon}\bigg)\lesssim\delta^{\beta}\,.

As this is true for all
\delta>0
, we conclude that (
28
) holds. By combining this observation with Lemma
14
, it will be sufficient to show that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,,

Moreover, recalling that the sector
\mathcal{S}
is given by (
20
), Claim
16
asserts that with high probability the number of roots in
\mathcal{S}
is
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
. Hence, with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}}\bigg|\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\bigg|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}=\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})

and we have reduced (
27
) to showing that

(29)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log\left|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\right|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|f^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}{\sf c}_{\ast}\,.

In view of Lemma
21
, concentration of the sum in (
29
) is an immediate consequence of

(30)

\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg)\lesssim n^{-5\beta}\,.

After (
30
) is established, the limiting constant is identified via Lemma
23
. It remains to prove (
30
) holds. Indeed, we denote by
\mathcal{N}^{2}=\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
the Cartesian product of the net points, and let

\mathcal{N}_{g,2}=\Big\{(z,w)\in\mathcal{N}^{2}\,:\,|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}\Big\}\,.

We further set
\mathcal{N}_{b,2}=\mathcal{N}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{g,2}
, and note that

|\mathcal{N}_{b,2}|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{1+2\beta}\cdot n^{1/2+2\beta})=\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2+4\beta})\,,

while
|\mathcal{N}^{2}|=\widetilde{O}(n^{2+4\beta})
. We have

\displaystyle\text{Var}\bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{(z,w)\in\mathcal{N}^{2}}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\cdot\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\Big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{2}}\,|\mathcal{N}_{b,2}|+n^{-1/20}\,\frac{|\mathcal{N}^{2}|}{n^{2}}\lesssim n^{-5\beta}\,,

where the second inequality follows from Claim
22
(and a trivial bound for
(z,w)\in\mathcal{N}_{b,2}
). We have thus proved the bound (
30
), and with that we get (
27
).
∎

5.
Computing the mean

Recall that
g_{n}
is the Gaussian random polynomial (
26
), which is the (complex) Gaussian counterpart to the Kac random polynomial
f_{n}
we consider throughout the paper. The goal of this section is to prove Lemma
23
, which we will do by first showing that, as
n\to\infty

(31)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{g}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]+o(1)\,.

For Gaussian polynomials, we have additional tools to compute the limiting expectation in (
31
). Indeed, we shall apply below the Kac-Rice formula (see Section
5.2
below) to compute the Gaussian expectation, and with that complete the proof of Lemma
23
.

5.1.
Approximating the event
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}

Recall that for
z\in\mathcal{N}
the event
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}
is given by (
24
). The key step towards the proof of (
31
) is to show that
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}
has the same first-order asymptotic probability under
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}_{g}
, as
n\to\infty
. A slightly more general formulation of this fact is given by the next lemma.

Lemma 24
.

For
z\in\mathcal{N}
we have

(32)

\Big|\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+})-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+})\Big|\leq n^{-1/20}\,.

Furthermore, for both
\bullet\in\{-,+\}
we have

(33)

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\Big]\,\bigg|\leq n^{-1/20}\,.

Finally, for
z,w\in\mathcal{N}
such that
|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}
and for
\bullet\in\{-,+\}
we have

(34)

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\Big]\,\bigg|\leq n^{-1/20}\,.

Proof.

We will only prove (
34
) as both (
32
) and (
33
) have very similar (and simpler) proofs. We will also just consider the case
\bullet=+
, as the complementary case
\bullet=-
is identical. We can intersect both expectations with the event
\mathcal{G}
given by (
14
), since Lemma
8
guarantees that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\log_{\bullet}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{c}}\Big]\leq\widetilde{O}\big(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})^{1/2}\big)\lesssim e^{-c\log^{2}n}\,,

which we can safely absorb in the error term. For
z\in\mathcal{N}
, we set

(35)

\mathcal{E}_{z}=\Big\{(X,Y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,:\,\frac{X}{nY}\in\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}-z\,,\,\frac{1}{\log^{9}n}\leq|Y|\leq\log^{2}n\,,\,|X|\leq\log^{2}n\,\Big\}\,.

That is,
\mathcal{E}_{z}
is the subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
which corresponds to the event
\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{G}
, with

X=\frac{f_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y=\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\,.

Recalling that
\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+}
is a polar rectangle around
z
with side lengths
\widetilde{O}(n^{-1-\beta})
, we get that
m(\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+})=\widetilde{O}(n^{-2-2\beta})
and hence

(36)

m(\mathcal{E}_{z})\leq\widetilde{O}\big(n^{2}m(\mathcal{R}_{z}^{+})\big)=\widetilde{O}(n^{-2\beta})\,.

We further denote by
\mu
the law of

\Big(\frac{f_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{f_{n}(w)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)\qquad\text{on }\ \mathbb{C}^{4}\,,

and we get that

(37)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{+}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\log_{+}\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{C}_{w}^{+}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\Big]\\
=\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X_{1},Y_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{z}\\
(X_{2},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{w}\end{subarray}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})\,.

We will also denote by
\mu_{g}
the corresponding (Gaussian) law of

\Big(\frac{g_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{g_{n}(w)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)\qquad\text{on }\ \mathbb{C}^{4}\,.

In view of (
37
), we want to show that

(38)

\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X_{1},Y_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{z}\\
(X_{2},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{w}\end{subarray}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})\\
=\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X_{1},Y_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{z}\\
(X_{2},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{w}\end{subarray}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu_{g}(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})+O(n^{-1/20})

which we do in what follows. Let
\kappa=\beta>0
be a small parameter, and consider the tiling of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
parametrized by the lattice
(n^{-\kappa}\,\mathbb{Z})^{4}
, namely

\mathcal{Q}=\Big\{p+\big[0,n^{-\kappa}]^{4}\,:\,p\in(n^{-\kappa}\,\mathbb{Z})^{4}\Big\}\,.

Then all cubes in
\mathcal{Q}
have mutually disjoint interiors and their union is
\mathbb{R}^{4}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
. For
z\in\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{E}_{z}
given by (
35
) we set

\Gamma_{z}=\big\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}\,:\,Q\subset\mathcal{E}_{z}\big\}\,,\quad\Gamma_{z}^{b}=\big\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}\,:\,Q\cap\mathcal{E}_{z}\not=\emptyset\,,\ Q^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{z}\not=\emptyset\big\}\,,\quad\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}={\Gamma}_{z}\cup{\Gamma}_{z}^{b}\,,

and note that since
\mathcal{E}_{z}
is a Lipschitz domain, (
36
) implies that

(39)

|{\Gamma}_{z}|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{4\kappa-2\beta})\quad\text{and}\quad|{\Gamma}_{z}^{b}|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{3\kappa-2\beta})\,.

To prove (
38
), we need to compare between the probability measures
\mu
and
\mu_{g}
. Indeed, for any pair of cubes
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{Q}
, Lemma
19
implies that

(40)

\mu\big(Q_{1}\times Q_{2}\big)=\mu_{g}\big(Q_{1}\times Q_{2}\big)+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})\,.

Furthermore, since
\mu_{g}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{C}^{4}
with a bounded (Gaussian) density, we also have

(41)

\mu_{g}\big(Q_{1}\times Q_{2}\big)=\widetilde{O}(n^{-8\kappa})\,,

so by taking
\kappa>0
sufficiently small (
41
) also applies for
\mu
in place of
\mu_{g}
. Since
\log_{+}|\cdot|
has uniformly bounded variation in
\mathbb{C}
, an immediate consequence of (
40
) is that

(42)

\iint_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu\\
=\iint_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu_{g}+\widetilde{O}(n^{-8\kappa})

for any
Q_{1}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}
and
Q_{2}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{w}
. We get that

\displaystyle\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X_{1},Y_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{z}\\
(X_{2},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{w}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q_{1}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}}\sum_{Q_{2}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}}\iint_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle(\text{by}~\eqref{eq:comparison_of_measures_with_logarithmic_integral})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q_{1}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}}\sum_{Q_{2}\in\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}}\iint_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu_{g}+\widetilde{O}\big(|\overline{{\Gamma}}_{z}|^{2}n^{-8\kappa}\big)

\displaystyle(\text{by}~\eqref{eq:bound_on_caridinality_of_Gamma})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q_{1}\in{\Gamma}_{z}}\sum_{Q_{2}\in{\Gamma}_{z}}\iint_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu_{g}+\widetilde{O}\big(n^{-4\beta}+n^{-2\kappa-2\beta}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X_{1},Y_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{z}\\
(X_{2},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{w}\end{subarray}}\log_{+}|Y_{1}|\cdot\log_{+}|Y_{2}|\,{\rm d}\mu_{g}(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2})+\widetilde{O}(n^{-4\beta})\,,

which proves the upper bound in (
38
), for
\beta>0
sufficiently small. The lower bound also follows by switching between
\mu
and
\mu_{g}
is the above derivation, and altogether we have proved (
38
), which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

With Lemma
24
, we give a simple proof which we owe from the previous section.

Proof of Claim
17
.

By Lemma
24
, item (
32
) we have

\bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}_{g}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)\bigg|\lesssim n^{-1/20}\,\frac{|\mathcal{N}|}{n}\lesssim n^{3\beta-1/20}\,.

Therefore, it is enough to prove the corresponding bound for the Gaussian measure
\mathbb{P}_{g}
. Let
\nu=\nu_{z}
denote the law of

\Big(\frac{g_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)

induced on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Borrowing the notation from the proof of Lemma
24
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{g}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\cap\mathcal{G}\big)=\nu(\mathcal{E}_{z})\,.

As
\nu
has uniformly bounded density we get from (
36
) that

\mathbb{P}_{g}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-2\beta})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-2\beta})\,,

and hence

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}_{g}\big(\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}\big)=\widetilde{O}\Big(n^{-2\beta}\,\frac{|\mathcal{N}|}{n}\Big)=\widetilde{O}(1)\,.\qed

5.2.
Application of the Kac-Rice formula

In light of Lemma
24
, we can now turn our attention to compute the relevant expectation with respect to the Gaussian polynomial
g_{n}
given by (
26
). For
t\geq 0
we set

(43)

G_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,g_{n}(e^{-t/n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\gamma_{k}\,e^{-tk/n}\,,

and

(44)

\psi_{n}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\,.

Lemma 25
.

With
\psi_{n}
given by (
44
) and
\displaystyle T_{n}=-n\log\Big(1-\frac{\log^{3}n}{n}\Big)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]=\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t+o(1)

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemma
24
, item (
33
), we have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{g}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]+O(n^{-1/20+2\beta})

which is precisely (
31
). Having proven that, we now ‘unwind’ the Gaussian expectation back to the original sum over roots in
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, so that we can apply the Kac-Rice formula. Indeed, combining the above with Lemma
21
(applied to the Gaussian polynomial
g_{n}
) yields that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{g}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|g_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\bigg]+o(1)\,,

where we recall that
\mathcal{A}
is given by (
19
) and
\mathcal{S}
is given by (
20
). Since
g_{n}
is a Gaussian process which is (almost surely) analytic, a sufficiently general Kac-Rice formula, see for instance
[
2
, Theorem 6.4]
(see also
[
18
, Appendix A]
for a related derivation) yields

(45)

\mathbb{E}_{g}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\frac{|g_{n}^{\prime}(\alpha)|}{n^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\Big\}\bigg]\\
=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|g_{n}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|g_{n}^{\prime}(z)|\geq\frac{n^{3/2}}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \mid g_{n}(z)=0\Big]}{\pi\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|g_{n}(z)|^{2}\big]}\,{\rm d}m(z)\,.

Our goal now is to simplify the integral on the right-hand side of (
45
). First, we note that
g_{n}
is rotation invariant (since the complex Gaussian distribution is such), so this integral is in fact equal to

\frac{2}{n}\int_{1-\log^{3}n/n}^{1}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|g_{n}^{\prime}(r)|^{2}\log\Big|\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(r)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|g_{n}^{\prime}(r)|\geq\frac{n^{3/2}}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \mid g_{n}(r)=0\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|g_{n}(r)|^{2}\big]}\,r\,{\rm d}r\,.

By applying the change of variables
r=e^{-t/n}
and plugging in the definition (
43
) of
G_{n}
, the above integral is equal to

2\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\,e^{-2t/n}\,{\rm d}t\,.

Plugging this into (
45
) yields that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]\\
=\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\geq\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\,e^{-2t/n}\,{\rm d}t+o(1)\,.

To conclude the proof, it remains to note that
\psi_{n}
given by (
44
) is uniformly bounded in
t\in[0,T_{n}]
, since the numerator is uniformly bounded from below and the denominator is uniformly bounded from above. In particular, we have

\frac{\Big|\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|<\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]\Big|}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\lesssim\frac{\log\log n}{\log^{20}n}\,,

and hence

\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{\Big|\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|<\frac{1}{\log^{10}n}\big\}\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]\Big|}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\,{\rm d}t\lesssim\frac{\log^{3}n}{\log^{19}n}=o(1)\,.

Since we also have
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{n}}|e^{-2t/n}-1|\,{\rm d}t=o(1)
, we arrive at

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]\\
=\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}\,{\rm d}t+o(1)\,.

and we are done.
∎

It remains to compute the leading order in the Gaussian integral from Lemma
25
. We document this simple computation in the next claim, which will be proved in the next sub-section.

Claim 26
.

Let
\psi_{n}(t)
be given by (
44
), then for every
t\geq 0
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(t)=\Psi(t)\,,

where

(46)

\Psi(t)=\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\cdot\Big(\frac{\log S(t)+1-\gamma}{2}\Big)\,,

and

S(t)=\frac{\big(1+2t^{2}-\cosh(2t)\big)\cdot\big(1-\coth(t)\big)}{2t^{3}}\,.

Furthermore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}t\,.

We note by passing that the pre-factor

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}

in the limiting density
\Psi
is, up to normalization, the limiting radial density for the expected number of roots of random Kac polynomials in the disk, see
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. As we shall see below, Claim
26
follows from a simple Gaussian computation of the density (
44
). Before we perform this computation we show how Lemma
23
is derived, and in particular pin down the relation between
\Psi
defined in (
46
) and
\Phi
from Theorem
1
.

Proof of Lemma
23
.

By combining Lemma
25
with Claim
26
, we see that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\,\frac{2}{n}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{z}^{+}}\bigg]=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}t

with
\Psi
given by (
46
). Observe that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\,{\rm d}t=\Big[-\frac{1}{t}+\coth(t)\Big]_{t=0}^{t=\infty}=1\,,

which implies that

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\log S(t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=1-\gamma+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(t)\,{\rm d}t

where

\Phi(t)=\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\cdot\log\bigg(\frac{\big(1+2t^{2}-\cosh(2t)\big)\cdot\big(1-\coth(t)\big)}{2t^{3}}\bigg)\,.

This limit matches the definition (
9
) of
{\sf c}_{\ast}
, and hence we are done.
∎

5.3.
Computing the limiting integral

Recall the definition (
43
) for the normalized Gaussian random polynomial
G_{n}
. Our goal here is to prove Claim
26
, that is compute the limit of the density
\psi_{n}
given by (
44
) as
n\to\infty
and also justify that the corresponding integral converges. Denote by

(47)

s_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{g}[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-2tk/n}\,.

Claim 27
.

For all
t\geq 0
and
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
we have

(48)

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}^{(\ell)}(t)=(-2)^{\ell}\int_{0}^{1}{\lambda}^{\ell}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}=e^{-2t}\times\begin{cases}\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2t}&\ell=0\,,\\
\frac{1+2t-e^{2t}}{2t^{2}}&\ell=1\,,\\
\frac{e^{2t}-1-2t-2t^{2}}{t^{3}}&\ell=2\,.\end{cases}

Furthermore, we have

(49)

\sup_{\ell\in\{0,1,2\}}\,\sup_{n\geq 2}\,\sup_{t\in[0,M]}|s_{n}^{(\ell)}(t)|=O_{M}(1)\,.

Lastly, for all
t\in[M,\log^{4}n]
for with
M
being sufficiently large but fixed we have

(50)

|s_{n}^{(\ell)}(t)|\in\Big(\frac{0.1}{t^{\ell+1}},\frac{10}{t^{\ell+1}}\Big)

for all
n
large enough.

Proof.

Note that

s_{n}^{(\ell)}(t)=\frac{1}{n^{\ell+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-2k)^{\ell}e^{-2tk/n}

which is a Riemann sum for the limiting integral (
48
), with
n+1
equally spaced points in
[0,1]
. Hence, the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula implies that

s_{n}^{(\ell)}(t)=(-2)^{\ell}\int_{0}^{1}{\lambda}^{\ell}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}+\widetilde{O}(n^{-1})

uniformly for
t\in[0,\log^{4}n]
, from which both (
48
) and (
49
) follow immediately. To prove (
50
), we only need to observe that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{1+\ell}\int_{0}^{1}{\lambda}^{\ell}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}x^{\ell}e^{-2x}\,{\rm d}x\in\Big[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\Big]\,.\qed

Claim 28
.

Let
Z
be a mean-zero complex Gaussian with
\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2}]=s
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big[|Z|^{2}\log|Z|\big]=\frac{s}{2}\big(\log s+1-\gamma\big)

where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{Z}=Z/\sqrt{s}
and note that
\widehat{Z}
is a standard complex Gaussian. We have

\mathbb{E}\big[|Z|^{2}\log|Z|^{2}\big]=s\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\log|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{Z}|^{2}]\cdot\log s\Big)=s\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\log|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\big]+\log s\Big)\,.

It remains to note that

\mathbb{E}\big[|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\log|\widehat{Z}|^{2}\big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\log x)\,x\,e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x=1-\gamma\,.\qed

Combining the above claims, we are ready to conclude the computation for the limiting constant.

Proof of Claim
26
.

Recall that
\psi_{n}
is given by (
44
). Its denominator is equal to
s_{n}
, given by (
47
). We can compute the numerator in
\psi_{n}
as well. Indeed, the conditional of
G_{n}^{\prime}(t)
given that
G_{n}(t)=0
is a mean-zero complex Gaussian with variance given by the Schur complement, i.e.

\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\big]-\frac{\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[G_{n}(t)\overline{G_{n}^{\prime}(t)}\big]\Big|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\big[|G_{n}(t)|^{2}\big]}=s_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{(s_{n}^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{s_{n}(t)}\,.

Therefore, by Claim
28
we have

\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\log|G_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\ \big|\ G_{n}(t)=0\Big]=\frac{\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)}{2}\Big(\log\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)+1-\gamma\Big)\,,

and altogether

(51)

\psi_{n}(t)=\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)\,\frac{\log\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)+1-\gamma}{2s_{n}(t)}\,.

We are now ready to take the limit
n\to\infty
. Claim
27
and some algebra gives that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{1}{\lambda}^{2}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}-4\,\frac{\Big(\int_{0}^{1}{\lambda}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}\Big)^{2}}{\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2t{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8e^{-4t}}{e^{2t}-1}\bigg(\frac{(e^{2t}-1)(e^{2t}-1-2t-2t^{2})}{2t^{3}}-\frac{(1+2t-e^{2t})^{2}}{4t^{3}}\bigg)

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(1+2t^{2}-\cosh(2t)\big)\cdot\big(1-\coth(t)\big)}{2t^{3}}=S(t)\,.

Furthermore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{s}_{n}(t)}{s_{n}(t)}=\frac{2t}{1-e^{-2t}}\,S(t)=\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\,.

Plugging (
5.3
) and the above into (
51
), we finally arrive at

(53)

\Psi(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(t)=\Big(\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(t)}\Big)\cdot\Big(\frac{\log S(t)+1-\gamma}{2}\Big)\,,

which is what we wanted to show. To justify the limit of integrals, we note that Claim
27
together with (
51
) shows that

|\psi_{n}(t)|\lesssim\frac{|\log t|}{1+t^{2}}\,,

uniformly as
n\to\infty
. Thus, we can apply the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem together with (
53
) and get that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T_{n}}\psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}t\,,

as desired.
∎

6.2.
Proving concentration

We denote by

(57)

By Claim

Claim 30
.

For the complex Gaussian random polynomial
g_{n}
given by (
26
) we have

\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\big|\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\big|\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]<\infty\,,

and furthermore, for all

has the standard complex Gaussian law. Then it is immediate to check that
\mathbb{E}[\big|\log|Z|\big|]<\infty

\mathbb{E}[|\log|Z|]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|z|\,e^{-|z|^{2}}\,{\rm d}m(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\log s)\,e^{-s}\,{\rm d}s=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,,

∎

Lemma 31
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\,{\rm d}\theta\Big)\Big]\bigg)=0\,.

Proof.

We first note that for each
n\geq 2

\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\big|\,\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\Big]<\infty\,.

Therefore, we can apply Fubini’s theorem and observe that, for all fixed
M\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\big]\,{\rm d}\theta

(58)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{n^{-3/2}}^{M}\log x\,{\rm d}\big(\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\big)\,{\rm d}\theta

By the Esseen bound on small-ball probability of non-degenerate random variables
[
11
]
(see also
[
19
, Lemma 2.4]
), we have for
x\geq n^{-1/2}
,

(59)

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\lesssim x

uniformly for
\theta\in[0,1]
. Hence, we have

\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{n^{-3/2}}^{n^{-1/2}}\log x\,{\rm d}\big(\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\big)\,{\rm d}\theta\bigg|\lesssim\log n\cdot\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq n^{-1/2})\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

and (
6.2
) yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\Big[\log x\cdot\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\Big]_{x=n^{-1/2}}^{x=M}-\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)}{x}\,{\rm d}x\bigg)\,{\rm d}\theta+o(1)

\displaystyle=\log M\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M)\,{\rm d}\theta-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)}{x}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\theta+o(1)\,.

By repeating the same computation for the Gaussian polynomial, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]

(60)

\displaystyle=o(1)+\log M\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq M)\Big)\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\qquad-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)}{x}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\theta\,,

so we want to show that the right-hand side of (
6.2
) tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. By Claim
29
we have

\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\big|\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\big|}{x}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\theta\leq\widetilde{O}\Big(n^{-1/2}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x}\Big)\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})\,,

and by recalling that
\mathcal{I}
is given by (
56
) we also have

\int_{\mathcal{I}}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\big|\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq x)\big|}{x}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\theta\lesssim\log n\int_{\mathcal{I}}{\rm d}\theta\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,.

That is, the second integral on the right-hand side of (
6.2
) tends to zero. To deal with the first integral, observe that

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq M)\big|=\big|\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)>M)-\mathbb{P}_{g}(\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)>M)\big|\lesssim e^{-cM^{2}}\,,

where the inequality is due to the sub-Gaussian assumption on the coefficients of

6.2
) and get that

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]\bigg|\\
\lesssim o(1)+e^{-cM^{2}}\,.

Furthermore, an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality shows that for all

\mathbb{E}\big[|\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq M\}\big]\lesssim\big(\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq M)\big)^{1/2}\lesssim e^{-cM^{2}}\,,

and hence we get that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]\bigg|\lesssim e^{-M^{2}}

for all

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]\bigg)=0\,.

To conclude the lemma, it remains to observe that

\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]\bigg|\\
=\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{B}}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{g}_{n}(\theta)\,{\rm d}\theta\Big]\bigg|\lesssim\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B})^{1/2}\,,

where the inequality follows from Claim

the right-hand side of the above display tends to zero as

∎

Lemma 32
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Var}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)=0\,.

Proof.

We start by showing that regions where the Gaussian comparison is invalid asymptotically do not contribute to the variance. Indeed, (
59
) shows that

(61)

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-3/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq n^{-1/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\bigg|^{2}\lesssim(\log n)^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq n^{-1/2}\big)\,{\rm d}\theta\Big)^{2}\lesssim\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\,.

Furthermore, for all
M\geq 1
large but fixed an application of Cauchy-Schwarz shows that

(62)

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\bigg|^{2}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\log^{2}\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq M\big\}\Big]\,{\rm d}\theta\leq e^{-cM^{2}}\,,

where the last inequality follows from the sub-Gaussian assumption on the coefficients of
f_{n}
. Recall that
\mathcal{I}
is given by (
56
) and has measure
O(n^{-1/2})
. Then clearly

(63)

\bigg|\int_{\mathcal{I}}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-1/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\bigg|\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,.

By combining (
61
), (
62
) and (
63
) we see that

(64)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\text{Var}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)\\
\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\text{Var}\Big(\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-1/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)+e^{-cM^{2}}\,,

for all
M\geq 1
. To conclude the lemma, it remains to show that the variance on the right-hand side of (
64
) tends to zero as
n\to\infty
, for all fixed
M\geq 1
. For that, let us denote

X_{n}(\theta)=\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{n^{-1/2}\leq\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big\}

for
\theta\in[0,1]
. An application of Fubini shows that

(65)

\text{Var}\Big(\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}X_{n}(\theta)\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)=\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\text{Cov}\Big(X_{n}(\theta),X_{n}(\varphi)\Big)\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}\varphi\,.

As in the proof of Lemma
31
, we can integrate by parts and get that

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(\theta)]=(\log M)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M\big)-\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x\big)}{x}\,{\rm d}x\,,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle X_{n}(\theta)X_{n}(\varphi)]

\displaystyle=\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\log x\log y\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}y}\Big(\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq y\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\log x\bigg[\log M\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq M\big)-\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq y\big)\,\frac{{\rm d}y}{y}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(\log M)^{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq M\big)-\log M\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq M\big)}{x}\,{{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\log M\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq M,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq y\big)}{y}\,{{\rm d}y}+\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\int_{n^{-1/2}}^{M}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\leq x,\,\widetilde{f}_{n}(\varphi)\leq y\big)}{xy}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,.

Note that all these computations apply equally well when the underlying polynomial is
\widetilde{g}_{n}
. Hence, Claim
29
allows us to compare the covariance to the covariance of independent Gaussians and observe that, for all
\theta,\varphi\in[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}
such that
|\theta-\varphi|\geq n^{-1/2}
, we have

\Big|\text{Cov}\Big(X_{n}(\theta),X_{n}(\varphi)\Big)\Big|\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})\,.

By Cauchy-Shwarz, we also have the trivial bound

\Big|\text{Cov}\Big(X_{n}(\theta),X_{n}(\varphi)\Big)\Big|\leq\widetilde{O}(1)\,,

and by plugging into (
65
) we see that

\displaystyle\text{Var}\Big(\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}X_{n}(\theta)\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\int_{[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{I}}\Big|\text{Cov}\Big(X_{n}(\theta),X_{n}(\varphi)\Big)\Big|\Big(\boldsymbol{1}_{\{|\theta-\varphi|\leq n^{-1/2}\}}+\boldsymbol{1}_{\{|\theta-\varphi|>n^{-1/2}\}}\Big)\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}\varphi

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{O}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{|\theta-\varphi|\leq n^{-1/2}\}}{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y+n^{-1/2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2}).

In view of (

), this shows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{ Var}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\log\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\big\{\widetilde{f}_{n}(\theta)\geq n^{-3/2}\big\}\,{\rm d}\theta\,\Big)=0\,,

as desired.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

Recall the definition (
57
) of the event
\mathcal{B}
. As we want to prove convergence in probability, Claim

implies that we can restrict to the complement event

By Lemma

7.
Gaussian comparison proofs

The goal of this section is to prove Lemma
19
, as a consequence of the Berry-Esseen theorem for random vectors (see, e.g.
[
7
, Corollary 17.2]
). To do so, we will need to understand the covariance structure of the random vector

(66)

\Big(\frac{f_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{f_{n}(w)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)

for
z,w\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}
. In particular, as we identify
\mathbb{C}^{4}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{8}
, we need to lower bound the least singular value of the
8\times 8
covariance matrix obtained by taking the real and imaginary parts of (
66
). The relevant covariances are simple functions of trigonometric sums, and the next basic bound will be key in the analysis.

Claim 33
.

For
r\in[0,1]
,
\theta\in[n^{-1/2},\pi-n^{-1/2}]
and
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
we have

\qquad\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k}k^{\ell}\cos(k\theta)\bigg|\leq 8n^{1/2+\ell}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{k}k^{\ell}\sin(k\theta)\bigg|\leq 8n^{1/2+\ell}\,.

Proof.

For
z=re^{{\rm i}\theta}
we have

\Big|\sum_{k=0}^{n}z^{k}\Big|=\Big|\frac{1-z^{n+1}}{1-z}\Big|\leq\frac{2}{|1-z|}\leq 4\sqrt{n}\,.

The cases
\ell=1
and
\ell=2
follow from the identities

\sum_{k=0}^{n}kz^{k}=-\frac{nz^{n+1}}{1-z}+z\frac{1-z^{n+1}}{(1-z)^{2}}

and

\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{2}z^{k}=-\frac{n^{2}z^{n+1}}{1-z}-\frac{2nz^{n+1}}{(1-z)^{2}}+\frac{z(z+1)(1-z^{n})}{(1-z)^{3}}\,,

together with the triangle inequality to yield the desired bound.
∎

To study the covariance structure, we will use the following standard trigonometric identities:

\displaystyle 2\cos(\theta)\cos(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta-\varphi)+\cos(\theta+\varphi)

(67)

\displaystyle 2\cos(\theta)\sin(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta+\varphi)-\sin(\theta-\varphi)

\displaystyle 2\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta-\varphi)-\cos(\theta+\varphi)\,.

Denote by
\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma)=\min_{\|v\|=1}\|\Sigma v\|
the least singular value of a matrix
\Sigma
. We start by giving a lower bound on the least singular value of the covariance matrix evaluated at a single point
z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.

Claim 34
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
and set

\Sigma_{z}=\operatorname{Cov}\bigg(\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}(z)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}(z)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)}{n^{3/2}}\bigg)

Then
\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{z})\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(1)
.

Proof.

Write
z=re^{{\rm i}\theta}
and note that
z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
implies that

r\in\Big[1-\frac{\log^{3}n}{n},1\Big]

and
|\theta|\in[n^{-1/2},\pi-n^{-1/2}]
. Write

\qquad s_{\ell}=\frac{1}{n^{1+\ell}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}r^{2k}k^{\ell}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-tb}t^{\ell}\,{\rm d}t

with
b=-2n\log(r)\in[0,\log^{3}n]
. The Euler-Maclaurin summation formula (used exactly as in the proof of Claim
27
) implies that
|s_{\ell}-S_{\ell}|\leq O(n^{-1/2})
for
\ell\in\{0,1,2\}
. Combining this observation with Claim
33
and the trigonometric identities (
7
) implies that

(68)

\Sigma_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}&0&0&s_{1}\\
0&s_{0}&s_{1}&0\\
0&s_{1}&s_{2}&0\\
s_{1}&0&0&s_{2}\end{pmatrix}+O(n^{-1/2})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&0&0&S_{1}\\
0&S_{0}&S_{1}&0\\
0&S_{1}&S_{2}&0\\
S_{1}&0&0&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}+O(n^{-1/2})\,.

We further have

\sigma_{\min}\left(\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&0&0&S_{1}\\
0&S_{0}&S_{1}&0\\
0&S_{1}&S_{2}&0\\
S_{1}&0&0&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sigma_{\min}\bigg(\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}\\
S_{1}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg)\,.

It remains to note that the operator norm of the above matrix is uniformly bounded, while

\det\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}\\
S_{1}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}=S_{0}S_{2}-(S_{1})^{2}=\frac{1+e^{-2b}-2e^{-b}-b^{2}e^{-b}}{b^{4}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log^{12}n}\,.

We get that

\sigma_{\min}\bigg(\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}\\
S_{1}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg)\geq\det\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}\\
S_{1}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\bigg\|\begin{pmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}\\
S_{1}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\|_{\text{op}}^{-1}\,\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log^{12}n}

which, in view of (
68
), gives the claim.
∎

The next claim boosts the above to a similar least singular value bound for separated points.

Claim 35
.

For
z,w\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
|z-w|\geq n^{-1/2}
, we set

\Sigma_{z,w}=\operatorname{Cov}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}(z)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}(z)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(z)\big)}{n^{3/2}}\\
\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}(w)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}(w)\big)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\text{Re}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(w)\big)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{\text{Im}\big(f_{n}^{\prime}(w)\big)}{n^{3/2}}\end{pmatrix}

Then
\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{z,w})\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(1)
.

Proof.

Applying the trigonometric identities (
7
) together with Claim
33
shows that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{z,w}
asymptotically factors as

(69)

\Sigma_{z,w}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{z}&0\\
0&\Sigma_{w}\end{pmatrix}+O(n^{-1/2})\,,

and the claim follows at once from Claim
34
.
∎

The proof of the desired Gaussian comparison now easily follows.

Proof of Lemma
19
.

Both statements in the lemma follow immediately from the Berry-Esseen theorem for a sum of independent random vectors with uniformly bounded third moment, as it appears in
[
7
, Corollary 17.2]
. For a pair of separated points
z
and
w
we apply Claim
35
to show that the covariance matrix is non-singular (up to a tolerable
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
loss), while for a single point
z\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{S}
we apply Claim
34
instead.
∎

We conclude with a proof that we owe from Section
4
.

Proof of Claim
22
.

By Lemma
24
item (
34
), it suffices to prove the claim for the Gaussian random polynomial
g_{n}
. Indeed, the corresponding statement for Gaussians follows at once from (
69
), as it shows that the difference between the joint density of

\Big(\frac{g_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}},\frac{g_{n}(w)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)

and the product of the densities of

\qquad\Big(\frac{g_{n}(z)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Big(\frac{g_{n}(w)}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(w)}{n^{3/2}}\Big)

is
O(n^{-1/2})
. This shows that the difference of expectations is
\widetilde{O}(n^{-1/2})
and we are done.
∎
