Concomitant antithrombotic therapy and substance use in older adult trauma patients: a secondary evaluation of an EAST Multicenter Trial
Authors: Abby Sheffield, Arnav Mahajan, Sharven Taghavi, Danielle Tatum, Marie L Crandall, Brian K Yorkgitis, on behalf of the EAST Multicenter Prospective Observational Study of Trauma Patients on Antithrombotic Agents Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe concurrent use of antithrombotic agents and intoxicants in older trauma patients (≥65) poses a potential risk for adverse outcomes. As the prevalence of substance use among older adults rises, understanding the impact of these combined factors on trauma outcomes is crucial. This study evaluates the impact of combined intoxication and antithrombotic use on trauma outcomes in older adults.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of data from an Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma-sponsored multicenter prospective observational study involving 15 Level I and II trauma centers from February 2018 to June 2019. Included patients were on antithrombotic therapy and had blood alcohol concentration (BAC) and/or urine toxicology (UT) samples collected at trauma evaluation. Intoxication was defined as BAC >0.08 mg/dL or a positive UT. Patient demographics, injury characteristics, and outcomes were analyzed using linear and logistic regression models.ResultsOf 2793 trauma patients on antithrombotics, 862 met inclusion criteria, with 155 (18.0%) testing positive for intoxicants. Positive tests were associated with younger age (74 years vs 79 years, p<0.001), lower Charlson Comorbidity Index (4 vs 5, p<0.001), and similar Injury Severity Scores (6 vs 9, p=0.38). Patients with positive tests had more penetrating mechanisms of injury (11.0% vs 3.7%, p=0.01) and similar hospital stays (3 days vs 4 days, p=0.311). ICU admission rates were lower (32.9% vs 42.2%, p=0.041), with no difference in ICU length of stay or mortality. Higher ICU admissions (p=0.039) and increased ICU stays were seen in those on two substances (OR: 5.26, 95% CI 0.76 to 9.76, p=0.022).ConclusionsOlder trauma patients on antithrombotic therapy with concurrent intoxicant use did not exhibit worse clinical outcomes, despite higher rates of penetrating injuries and polysubstance use. These findings underscore the importance of tailored discharge planning and comprehensive care strategies for this vulnerable population.Level of evidenceLevel III, prognostic/epidemiological.Keywords: Anticoagulants, Substance-Related Disorders, geriatrics

Introduction

Antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents offer clinical benefits in treating several prothrombotic conditions in older adults.1 2 The highest fatality complication associated with their use is intracranial hemorrhage.3 4 Older adults form an increasing segment of the trauma population, and the use of anticoagulants in this group, when coupled with injury, leads to higher mortality and progression of traumatic brain injury.5 6

In the past decade, alcohol-related emergency department visits among older adults have increased by 7.5% annually, whereas younger populations’ admissions have plateaued.7 Despite this, older adults are less frequently screened for substance misuse, contributing to underdiagnosis and underreporting.8 9 Intoxicant use is known as a predisposing risk factor for injury, with alcohol-related or drug-related visits to trauma centers comprising a pooled estimate of 32.4% to 37% of all trauma patients.10 11

Despite numerous studies on the implications of alcohol misuse on traumatic injury outcomes, the growing number of older adults with intoxication who are likely on antithrombotic raises concerns about the combined impacts of these factors on trauma outcomes.8 12 Our hypothesis is that older trauma patients on antithrombotic therapy who are also intoxicated will have worse outcomes compared with those who are not intoxicated. The objective of this study is to evaluate the association between combined intoxication and antithrombotic use and in-hospital outcomes.

Methods

Multicenter study recruitment

This study was a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data in an Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma sponsored multicenter, observational study of 15 Level 1 and Level II trauma centers between February 2018 and June 2019. Details on the inclusions of this study have been previously published.6 Eligible patients were on antithrombotic medications prior to injury.

Study population

Subjects were included if they were: taking an antithrombotic agent prior to injury and had a blood alcohol concentration (BAC) or urine toxicology (UT) sample collected during trauma evaluation. We included patients aged 65 years or older, as this age threshold aligns with the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma guidelines.13 Data on patient demographics, injury characteristics, antithrombotic agents, and clinical outcomes were gathered from electronic medical records. For each patient, we recorded age, gender, comorbidities, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) Score. Injury mechanisms were categorized into blunt, penetrating, and mixed (blunt and penetrating) and further subdivided into specific mechanisms such as motor vehicle collisions and falls.

Data collection and variables

Alcohol intoxication was defined as having a BAC of >0.08 mg/dL. Patients were subsequently divided into the intoxicated (>0.08 mg/dL) and non-intoxicated (<0.08 mg/dL) groups. UT screening tested for substances of interest, including cocaine, phencyclidine, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opiates, and amphetamines, with a binary result of positive or negative. Those with a positive test were defined as those with active intoxicant use. These data were then further evaluated for the presence of polysubstance positivity on UT as well as concomitant alcohol intoxication. Antithrombotic agents included in our data collection were aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, dipyridamole, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, betrixaban, and other. Variables were collected through chart review, and there was no change in the therapeutic or diagnostic interventions for included patients.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient population. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with IQRs and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Univariate comparisons between groups were performed using the χ² test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. We employed both linear and logistic regression models to assess the impact of substance use on clinical outcomes. The linear regression models were used to analyze continuous outcomes of hospital length of stay (LOS) and intensive care unit (ICU) LOS, whereas logistic regression models were used for the binary outcome of mortality. Covariates included in the models were selected based on clinical relevance and findings from univariate analyses. GCS Score was excluded from the regression models due to the high risk of collinearity, as obtunded patients may be more likely to undergo BAC and UT testing. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.3.1). The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were adhered to in the preparation of this article.14

Results

In the trial, 2793 trauma patients on antithrombotic medications were studied. Of these, 862 (30.9%) met this study’s inclusion criteria of taking an antithrombotic agent and having a BAC and/or UT sample collected. Of these patients, 155 (18.0%) tested positive for at least one intoxicant. Regarding disposition, 43.4% were discharged home and 47.2% required extended care facility placement. Common comorbidities included coronary artery disease (38.1%), cerebrovascular disease (14.2%), and myocardial infarction (10.3%). Dementia was present in 9.0% of patients intoxicated patients.

Comparison between patients who tested positive (n=155) and patients who tested negative (n=704) for intoxicants showed that tested patients were younger (74 vs 79, p<0.001) and male (60% vs 52.7%, p=0.12). Tested patients also had a lower frequency of head Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) Scores ≥3 (17.8% vs 26.6%, p=0.034) and a lower Charlson Comorbidity Index Score (4 vs 5, p<0.001). Penetrating injuries were more common in those with positive tests (table 1).

Substance use and polysubstance combinations

Of the 155 patients who tested positive, 127 (82.0%) were positive for one substance, 26 (16.8%) for two substances, and 2 (1.2%) for three or more substances. Opiates were the most frequently detected substance, with 82 patients (52.9%) testing positive. Polysubstance use was high among patients positive for cocaine (100%), methamphetamine (100%), and benzodiazepines (54.5%). Polysubstance use was lower in patients with positive tests for alcohol (15.2%) and opiates (24.4%) (table 2).

Polysubstance use

Among polysubstance users, those on three or more substances were younger (75 years for 1 substance, 71 years for two substances, 69 years for three or more substances, p<0.001) and tended to be more often male (57.5% vs 73.1% vs 50.0%, p=0.178). GCS Scores were higher for patients on one substance (p=0.025). Falls were less common among polysubstance users (81.1% vs 57.7% vs 0.0%, p=0.042). Gunshot wounds were more common in those on three or more substances (0.0% vs 3.8% vs 50.0%, p<0.001), as well as stab wounds (0.8% vs 7.7% vs 50.0%, p<0.001).

Outcomes of interest

Hospital LOS (3 days vs 4 days vs 8 days, p=0.083) and mortality (5 (4.0%), 1 (4.0%), 0 (0.0%), p=0.752) did not differ significantly among this group. ICU admission rates were highest for those on three or more substances (67.7% vs 69.2% vs 100%, p=0.039) with a dose-response relationship, though ICU LOS and ventilator requirements did not increase (table 4). Linear regression demonstrated that having three or more substances was associated with no significant change in hospital LOS (OR: 3.26, 95% CI −5.77 to 12.3, p=0.479), whereas two substances showed increased ICU LOS (OR: +5.26, 95% CI 0.76 to 9.76, p=0.022). No significant differences were found for mortality (table 5).

Discussion

Our study identifies older trauma patients on antithrombotic therapy at risk due to concomitant intoxicant use, primarily younger men with fewer comorbidities. Around 18% of patients in our cohort tested positive for intoxicants, with 18% of this group demonstrating polysubstance misuse. Although we did not find the combination of antithrombotic and intoxicant to worsen clinical outcomes, it underscores the need for heightened awareness and careful management of this vulnerable population. As the prevalence of substance use among older adults continues to rise, this finding is crucial for informing future trauma care strategies.

Discharge planning for older adults who are intoxicated and on antithrombotic therapy is complex and necessitates a multidisciplinary approach. It is known that patients with an alcohol-related admission are at more than twice the risk for future injuries, which is critical for those on antithrombotic therapy due to the increased risk of subsequent bleeding events.15 Our findings demonstrated that even when adjusted for injury severity, patients with substance use had similar hospital and ICU lengths of stay, though ICU admission rates were lower in the intoxicated group. This finding contradicted our initial hypothesis that combined antithrombotic and intoxicant use would worsen outcomes. However, this observation aligns with previous studies that have found lower mortality rates and shorter hospital stays in populations with high rates of traumatic injury, suggesting that alcohol may have a protective effect in patients with brain injuries.16 17 Of note, we found that patients with two substances showed increased ICU LOS, suggesting a potential dose-dependent effect of polysubstance use on critical care needs. These patterns raise important questions about the possibility that these patients might face unaddressed risks postdischarge. Factors such as weaker structures in geriatric trauma care, provider bias, ageism, and fear of offending older adults may contribute to these outcomes.18 19 Given these findings, tailored discharge plans that address the potential for substance misuse and ensure robust follow-up care are critical to mitigate future injury risk and manage ongoing health needs effectively within this demographic.

There is an overlooked link between intoxicant use and penetrating injuries in trauma patients. Our study finds a higher incidence of penetrating injuries among patients who tested positive for intoxicants, particularly in those with polysubstance use, where we observed increased rates of both gunshot wounds and stabbings among those with multiple substances. This contrasts with literature that lacks a consistent relationship between drug use and trauma severity.20,23 Our study shows a link between intoxicant use and more penetrating injuries, challenging the assumption that such injuries are confined to younger individuals and suggesting the need for vigilance in this older group.

There is potential in rethinking prescribing and reversal strategies for older trauma patients using antithrombotics. Prior studies link alcohol and drug use to increased injury risk, but our findings suggest that concomitant use of antithrombotic agents does not worsen clinical outcomes.5 This presents a conflicting analysis as prior evidence has demonstrated risks of these factors independently in traumatic injury.5 6 10 11 Our results, being the first study of its kind to examine this unique population, imply that introducing antithrombotic agents in patients with a history of alcohol use may not be as dangerous as previously thought, with other risk factors potentially posing greater danger. Paralleling this, as antithrombotic medications become increasingly omnipresent in trauma care, understanding the implications of reversal will be critical as our evidence currently suggests that outcomes between these groups are similar.24

This study is not without limitations. Although the study occurred at 15 centers, enrolment bias in the voluntary study can be considered, as some patients meeting inclusion criteria may not have been captured. As universal BAC and UT are lacking among participating trauma centers, there may be selection bias introduced. Using multicenter data does introduce the possibility of data entry variability for determination of CCI and ISS/AIS between centers, which is inherent in multicenter trials. Although some of the substances have medical uses, obtaining information on those with a valid prescription for medical use was outside the scope of the data set. However, many of these drugs are listed as potentially inappropriate per the Beers Criteria, and caution should be exercised in their prescribing.25 This is particularly concerning as over 25% of the toxicology testing was positive for more than one substance. Due to differing half-lives of intoxicating substances, acute intoxication at the time of test collection is unknown. The age of the patients in the negative intoxicant group is higher, and falls are common in older adult populations.26 27 Although all cohorts in this study were taking an antithrombotic agent, comparison to patients not taking antithrombotic medications is outside the scope of this work and could be investigated in the future. Although our study examined older adults defined by their age, we did not assess frailty scores, which could provide additional insights into outcomes in this population. The median ISS in our cohort was low (four in positive tests vs eight in negative tests). These findings may not extend to severely injured populations where antithrombotic and intoxicant effects could differ.

Conclusion

Our study identifies a significant risk group among older trauma patients on antithrombotic therapy with concomitant intoxicant use. Although 18% of these patients tested positive for intoxicants, and a notable proportion showed polysubstance use, clinical outcomes were generally similar between groups, with the exception of increased ICU LOS in those with two substances. This highlights the need for targeted discharge planning and management, given the increased risk of future injuries. The link between intoxicant use and penetrating injuries in older adults, particularly in those with polysubstance use, challenges existing assumptions and calls for a nuanced approach to trauma care. Our findings suggest that antithrombotic use in this context may not increase clinical risks, but comprehensive care strategies are essential to address ongoing health needs and mitigate injury risks. Future research should explore the long-term outcomes of these patients, the effectiveness of tailored discharge plans, and the role of antithrombotic reversal protocols in trauma care, and address potential biases and data variability in multicenter studies.
