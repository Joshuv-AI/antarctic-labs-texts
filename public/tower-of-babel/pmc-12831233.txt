Harnessing ChatGPT for abstract screening in health-related scoping reviews: the role of structured eligibility criteria
Authors: Kim Nordmann, Florian Fischer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScoping reviews, especially the screening phase, are time-consuming. Due to an exponential increase in the number of scientific articles related to health issues being published each year, the time burden is also expected to rise. In parallel, there is a gap in machine learning tools specifically tailored to support screening processes within scoping reviews. Large language models, such as ChatGPT, have shown promise in accelerating this process. Given the reliance of artificial intelligence on structured inputs, this study aims to refine eligibility criteria and analyse ChatGPT’s performance in abstract screening for a scoping review on digital tools supporting interprofessional interactions in healthcare.MethodsWe conducted a thematic analysis of ChatGPT 4.0’s explanations based on a previously conducted scoping review and developed three refined sets of eligibility criteria (narrow, wide, and balanced). ChatGPT reassessed these criteria using the human reviewers’ decisions as the gold standard. We calculated performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. Additionally, we combined decisions using majority voting and human conflict resolution to assess their combined performance.ResultsRephrasing the eligibility criteria using the Population-Concept-Context framework revealed challenges in each category: the complexity of healthcare provider interactions (population), ambiguities in defining digital tools (concept), and difficulties in identifying the healthcare setting (context). For example, ChatGPT was sometimes overinclusive, including individuals such as politicians, while at other times applying overly rigid boundaries, excluding healthcare groups like palliative care providers. The wide set of eligibility criteria achieved the highest sensitivity, while the narrow set had the highest specificity, with the original and balanced sets performing between these extremes. Combining decisions provided a good balance between sensitivity and specificity, effectively identifying ambiguous abstracts for manual review. The combination of balanced and narrow criteria resulted in a lower overall workload compared to other combinations.ConclusionRefining and structuring the eligibility criteria as well as combining different sets improved ChatGPT’s decision-making accuracy, highlighting the importance of well-defined and well-formulated criteria for such tasks. The formulation of eligibility criteria directly impacted screening outcomes: broad criteria maximized sensitivity at the expense of specificity, while narrow criteria produced the opposite effect. Combining different approaches may enable screening by one human reviewer accompanied by an AI-assisted screening for the major part and to include a second human reviewer in cases of discrepancies. However, challenges such as trust and transparency persist and need to be addressed for full integration into the review process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13901-4.Keywords: Scoping review, Artificial intelligence, Abstract screening, Large language models

Background

Scoping reviews are widely used in research to map scientific evidence and identify evidence gaps across various scientific disciplines, such as medicine and public health, with the goal of informing research, practice, and policy [1]. However, conducting these reviews is labour-intensive and time-consuming, often taking anywhere from two weeks to 20 months [2]. Given the sharp increase in publication rates in recent years, this process is expected to become even more burdensome [1, 3]. The first appraisal phase, which involves screening a large number of abstracts by at least two independent reviewers is particularly time-consuming and prone to errors, yet it is crucial to the quality and validity of the review [4–8]. To address these challenges, artificial intelligence (AI) has been explored as a supporting tool – predominantly for screening in systematic reviews [9, 10]. While AI has been shown to reduce the workload involved in systematic reviews by at least 30%, its benefits for scoping reviews are less clear [11–13]. Scoping reviews tend to focus on broader concepts, as opposed to specific interventions and comparators, which makes it more difficult for semi-automated screening tools to yield the same level of efficiency as in systematic reviews [1, 12].

Large language models, such as the one OpenAI’s ChatGPT is based on, have shown promising results for systematic reviews in pilot studies, demonstrating their ability to rapidly analyse large volumes of unstructured data [6, 7, 14–16]. An initial trial using ChatGPT for a scoping review in the context of healthcare also yielded encouraging results [17]. These large language models operate on the basis of natural language task specifications, known as prompts, which guide the model’s output [18]. To produce high-quality outputs, a highly reliable prompt is essential [8, 9, 19, 20]. Previous studies have shown that prompts are sensitive to subtle modifications in syntax (such as length and instance order) and semantics (such as wording or instructions) [18, 21, 22]. Finding the ‘perfect’ prompt is challenging, as there is currently no established method for their design and selection [23, 24]. However, through prompt engineering – a process in which prompts are critically evaluated, iteratively adjusted, and optimized – the performance can be improved [21, 25, 26]. While prompts are composed of several elements, a key component in abstract screening is the input, particularly the eligibility criteria. As with human researchers, these criteria guide the AI tool in the screening process and determine the validity of the results [27].

There are several approaches to structuring eligibility criteria in order to ensure accuracy and reliability in reviews. For systematic studies on intervention studies, the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcome) framework and its extensions, such as S (Study type) and T (Time) are commonly used [28]. In contrast, the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework is typically applied for scoping reviews which demonstrate broader research questions and wider search and screening criteria compared to systematic reviews [1, 29, 30]. Given AI’s preference for structured prompts, it stands to reason that AI could similarly benefit from a structured format, thus potentially reducing false negatives and false positives and ensuring inclusion of relevant studies while minimizing researchers’ workload. While studies assessing AI performance in abstract screening commonly evaluate along standard performance metrics such as sensitivity, this study conducts an in-depth analysis of ChatGPT’s explanations for including or excluding an abstract with the goal of optimizing the formulation of eligibility criteria [6, 7, 17]. Specifically, we aimed to address two key questions: (1) How should inclusion and exclusion criteria be structured to be sufficiently inclusive and adequately exclusive for AI-assisted screening procedures? (2) How does ChatGPT perform when applying the adapted eligibility criteria?

Methods

Underlying material: description of the scoping review

In 2022/2023, we conducted a scoping review on digital tools supporting interprofessional interaction in healthcare in order to identify factors that impact the development and deployment, adaptation, and sustained use of the tools. Additionally, we explored, analysed, and synthesized implicit definitions, dimensions, and concepts pertaining to these tools for healthcare provider interaction. To capture all available scientific literature on this topic, all types of primary research approach and study design, including meta-analysis from any healthcare setting and geographic location, published in English, French, German, Portuguese, or Spanish were included in the study. We included a manuscript if it featured a digital tool facilitating interaction between a minimum of two healthcare workers (1) from different professional backgrounds, or (2) with the same background but working in different organizations. Abstracts, book chapters, and literature with the main focus on interaction in an educational setting or between healthcare providers and patients were excluded. The protocol detailing the methodology is published elsewhere [31].

The database search yielded 15 306 unique results, the abstracts of which were screened by two blinded researchers, with a third reviewer resolving any conflicts, ultimately resulting in 188 inclusions. These screening results are defined as the gold standard and “correct” decisions for comparing the performance of humans versus AI (Fig. 1). In order to do so, we retrospectively trained ChatGPT 4.0 in a follow-up study on a subset of 100 abstracts of this scoping review [17]. ChatGPT was chosen due to its widespread use in various domains and strong performance across tasks [8, 32, 33]. The model’s decisions were based on a prompt detailing the instructions, followed by the inclusion and exclusion criteria (context), and the abstract of the respective manuscript (Fig. 2). For each decision made by ChatGPT 4.0, we obtained an explanation of its reasoning. ChatGPT 4.0 demonstrated high accuracy (68%), specificity (67%), sensitivity (88–89%), and a negative predictive value of 99%.

Data analysis

In-depth analysis of ChatGPT’s responses and the gold standard error rate

In order to better understand ChatGPT’s decisions, we conducted an in-depth analysis of its explanations, with the ultimate goal of using these results to refine the prompts to improve ChatGPT’s decisions. In the chosen scoping review, ChatGPT correctly included 594 (true positives, TP) and excluded 9859 entries (true negatives, TN). It incorrectly excluded 80 (false negatives, FN) and included 4773 (false positives, FP) entries.

Correct decisions (TN/TP): We randomly sampled 10% of all correctly classified (included and excluded) abstracts and qualitatively evaluated the correctness of the explanations. For TP (n = 60), we compared the explanations with the inclusion reasoning provided by human researchers. For TN (n = 986), we coded ChatGPT’s exclusion reasons into ten categories based on a thematic analysis. This was done by two independent researchers. The categories were devised a priori from the scoping review’s exclusion criteria and included: no digital tool, no interaction between healthcare professionals, interaction between healthcare professionals with the same background and/or same organization, focus on students/education, conference abstract, editorial, book or chapter, abstract unavailable, other reason. Missing information in ChatGPT’s explanation was interpreted as meeting the inclusion criteria and/or failing to meet the exclusion criteria. In cases where ChatGPT used tentative language such as “could imply,” “potentially” or “seems”, these explanations were classified as meeting the respective inclusion and/or exclusion criteria. ChatGPT’s exclusion reasons were then compared with those provided by human researchers.

Correct decisions (TN/TP): We randomly sampled 10% of all correctly classified (included and excluded) abstracts and qualitatively evaluated the correctness of the explanations. For TP (n = 60), we compared the explanations with the inclusion reasoning provided by human researchers. For TN (n = 986), we coded ChatGPT’s exclusion reasons into ten categories based on a thematic analysis. This was done by two independent researchers. Incorrect decisions (FP/FN): We inductively analysed 113 false positives (2% of FP) and all 80 false negatives (100% of FN), identifying common error patterns and the underlying assumptions that led to misclassification. Two researchers, one of whom was not involved in the scoping review, conducted a joint and inductive analysis of the first 30 FP. Subsequently, each researcher independently analysed randomly assigned abstracts, with regular meetings to discuss findings and to resolve any coding discrepancies. Data saturation was reached after 113 FP, as no new error patterns emerged or were expanded further. Using the same coding tree, one researcher coded all FN afterwards.

ChatGPT 4.0 screened the abstracts twice, producing slightly different results across the two rounds (interrater-reliability (Cohen’s kappa): 0.76, standard error: 0.01, 95% confidence interval: 0.75–0.78). To understand the discrepancies (1648 abstracts), we analysed all FN-TP pairs (25 abstracts), i.e., where an abstract was falsely excluded in the first rating but correctly included in the second, and a subset of 3% of FP-TN pairs (26 abstracts). These mismatches were analysed using the same coding tree as employed in the previous analysis.

In this secondary analysis, we also investigated the error rate of the initial scoping review, focusing on omissions and incorrect inclusions by human reviewers.

Derivation of the new prompts and their performance analysis

Based on the thematic analysis above, we reformulated the original inclusion and exclusion criteria in three refined prompts, using the “Population-Concept-Context” (PCC) scheme suggested by the Joanna Briggs Institute (Table 1) [30]. In order to evaluate the performance of these refined prompts, we constructed a subset of abstracts comprising 25% of each category – 2465 for TN, 149 for TP, 20 for FN, 1193 for FP – and obtained ChatGPT 4.0’s decisions. To obtain the 25% subset, we assigned a random number in Excel to each abstract within the categories (FN, TP, FN, and FP), sorted them in ascending order, and selected the first 25%. The responses were gathered via an application programming interface in Excel (Microsoft Corporation), using version 6.9 of an Excel macro (ListenData, 2023) and the ‘AIAssistant’ function, which elicited responses from ChatGPT without recalling prior conversations. We did not enforce a word limit and set the temperature – a parameter that controls the randomness of the response – to 0.7. A temperature of 0.0 favours the selection of the most probable words, whereas a temperature of 1 or higher enables more creative outputs [34]. We set the temperature to 0.7 to allow for a limited stochastic variability in ChatGPT’s outputs, given that scoping reviews aim to respond broader research questions than systematic reviews and demonstrate broader eligibility criteria. Using Stata version 16 (StataCorp LLC), we calculated several key performance metrics for each prompt, based on the human researchers’ original decisions as the gold standard. The metrics included: (1) sensitivity, (2) specificity, (3) accuracy, (4) balanced accuracy, (5) positive predictive value, (6) negative predictive value, (7) F1 score, (8) false negative rate, (9) proportion missed, (10) false positive rate, 11) Matthews correlation coefficient, and 12) the workload savings (Table 1) [6–8, 35].

We combined ChatGPT’s decisions from each prompt using two approaches: (1) conflicts between the prompts were left undecided to be potentially resolved by a human researcher, or (2) conflicts were resolved through majority voting. We then calculated performance metrics for the combined prompts. In approach 1), conflicts that required human resolution were excluded from the final evaluation.

Results

Part 1: Results of the in-depth analysis of chatgpt’s explanations to rephrase the inclusion and exclusion criteria

The qualitative analysis of ChatGPT’s explanations provided in-depth insights about how it dealt with (“interpreted”) the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The analysis of FP and FN primarily informed the rephrasing of the inclusion criteria, while the analysis of TN informed the adaptation of the exclusion criteria. The inductive analysis led to a reorganization of the original inclusion criteria according to the PCC framework (appendix 1), recommended by Joanna Briggs Institute, which is detailed below [30].

Population

ChatGPT frequently used the term “different healthcare professionals” in its explanations, often specifying roles such as nurses or physicians when explicitly mentioned in the abstract. The in-depth analysis identified three main categories: broad understanding, implicit understanding, and inclusiveness.

Broad understanding: ChatGPT often mistakenly included abstracts that used vague terms such as “interdisciplinary,” “whole health community,” and “multiprofessional” without explicit mention of specific healthcare providers. These terms were sometimes scattered throughout the abstract and disconnected from the digital tool. Conversely, some abstracts were wrongly excluded because they lacked explicit references to healthcare provider interactions, despite containing broad terms such as “integrated care”, “interdisciplinary team members”, “team communication”, and “interprofessional collaborative practice”.

Implicit understanding: ChatGPT occasionally wrongly inferred the presence of healthcare providers based on context, such as the assumed properties of the tool (e.g., “integrated care system” or “integrated care programs”), references to settings (e.g., “a GP practice” or “healthcare services”), and mentions of interprofessional involvement in data collection. Concept

ChatGPT effectively recognized digital tools, often mentioning them explicitly in its explanations. The in-depth analysis revealed four main categories related to the concept: broad understanding, misinterpretation, inclusiveness, and focus.

Broad understanding: In some falsely included abstracts, ChatGPT identified abstract terms such as “information and communication technology”, “digital health transformation”, and “digital innovations” as relevant digital tools. These terms were sometimes interpreted out of context and appeared throughout the abstract, particularly in the results and conclusion section. Conversely, other abstracts were incorrectly excluded despite referencing these abstract terms, such as “health information technology”, “social media”, and “videoconference”.

In some cases, additional participants were involved in the tool’s use, potentially overshadowing interprofessional interaction aspects.

Context

With regard to context, only one subcategory emerged: Wrong setting. Some abstracts were wrongly included despite being outside the healthcare sector (e.g., “smart clothing”).

Exclusion criteria1

While ChatGPT falsely excluded two abstracts due to insufficient information (both having fewer than 70% of the average word count), it accurately identified 100% of references without abstracts. It also correctly recognized the presence or absence of the exclusion criteria “conference abstract”, “editorial”, and “book” in over 99% of abstracts. However, ChatGPT incorrectly identified two abstracts as not being conference-related and mistakenly categorized three as conference-related based on the words “workshop” and “conference”. ChatGPT correctly detected the presence or absence of the exclusion criteria “students/education” in over 98% of abstracts. Nevertheless, it miscategorized 16 abstracts based on terms such as “workshop” and “student-run clinic”, and falsely excluded abstracts briefly mentioning words related to education. The exclusion reason “same healthcare providers in the same organization” posed challenges to ChatGPT, which incorrectly identified 22 abstracts under this category mainly due to words such as “same department”, “network”, and “(interdisciplinary) team”, while only four abstracts genuinely met this criterion. The “patient-healthcare provider interaction” criterion was similarly challenging: ChatGPT correctly identified the presence or absence of this criterion in 91.4% but generated 79 false positives, broadly interpreting terms such as “collaboration between patients and research groups” or patients being part of an interdisciplinary team as meeting the criteria. It further falsely excluded abstracts briefly mentioning patients.

Summary

The analysis of FP suggested a need for narrower inclusion criteria, while the FN indicated that a broader definition of the inclusion criteria was necessary. TN helped to refine the exclusion criteria.

Abstracts that were inconsistently classified by ChatGPT revealed ambiguity due to implicit references to healthcare provider interactions, an implicit understanding of digital tools, and the varying importance placed on the primary focus of the abstract. Based on these insights, we established both narrow and broad phrasings of the criteria (in terms of prompts for ChatGPT) as well as a potential combination of the two (see Table 2) and re-evaluated a subset of the scoping review using all three versions.

Part 2: Performance of the rephrased inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individual prompt

The wide prompt identified the highest number of TP (n = 158) but also produced the highest number of FP (n = 1365). It only missed eleven studies (FN). The narrow prompt, on the other hand, generated the fewest FP (n = 502) and the most TN (n = 3156) at the cost of missing 52 studies (FN) and identifying only 117 TP. These findings are reflected in the performance metrics. The wide prompt yielded the highest sensitivity (0.935), the lowest specificity (0.627), and the highest false positive rate (0.373) (Fig. 3b). Conversely, the narrow prompt achieved the highest specificity (0.863) and the lowest sensitivity (0.692), displaying the highest false negative rate (0.308) (Fig. 3c). The sensitivity and specificity for the original and balanced prompts fell between these values. Notably, the narrow prompt displayed a higher positive predictive value (0.189) compared to the other prompts, while all prompts had a negative predictive value above 0.984.

Given the highly imbalanced nature of the dataset (imbalance ratio, i.e. number of excluded abstracts divided by the number of included abstracts, at 21.709), the accuracy of the prompts differed, while the balanced accuracy, i.e. the proportion of correctly predicted decisions, ranging from 0.778 to 0.782 across all prompts. The narrow prompt achieved the highest F1 score (0.297), indicating a good balance between positive predictive value and sensitivity. Equally, it showed the highest Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) at 0.310, reflecting a fair performance, while the wide prompt had the lowest MCC at 0.236. Under the hypothesis that all abstracts evaluated as positive require human review, the narrow prompt provided the highest workload savings (0.825). In contrast, the wide prompt resulted in a higher manual screening load (workload savings: 0.599).

Combined prompts

The prompts were combined in two ways: either by resolving conflicts through human intervention or through majority voting. This resulted in 930 undecided abstracts (24%) for the combination of the narrow and wide prompt (NW), 710 (19%) for narrow and balanced (NB), 422 (11%) for balanced and wide (BW), and 1031 (27%) for the combination of all three prompts (NBW) (Fig. 4a). In the majority voting approach, no undecided abstracts remained, as the majority decision was adopted. Majority voting identified as many TP as the original prompt (149) but with fewer FP (1067 vs. 1193). NBW and BW had the lowest number of FN (9), followed by NW with 10. NBW had the lowest number of FP with 476, followed by NB with 482 and NW with 490.

In terms of performance metrics, BW demonstrated the highest sensitivity (0.942), followed by NBW and NW (0.927 and 0.921, respectively). While BW had a lower specificity (0.678), NW and NBW maintained higher specificity (0.823 and 0.822) (Fig. 4b). NBW, NW and NB demonstrated high positive predictive values (0.193, 0.191, and 0.193), the highest balanced accuracy (0.862, 0.872, and 0.848), and the highest Matthews correlation coefficient (0.376, 0.373, and 0.359) (Fig. 4c). NBW, BW, and NW presented with the lowest proportion of missed inclusions (0.004, 0.004, and 0.004).

Despite the positive results, the true workload savings of these combinations were lower due to the high number of undecided abstracts requiring manual review. The NBW (majority voting) performed largely similarly to the original prompt, showing a slightly higher specificity (0.708 vs. 0.674), a slightly different positive predictive value (0.123 vs. 0.111), a higher balanced accuracy (0.795 vs. 0.778), and a higher Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.260. Discussion

This study illustrates the importance of meticulously formulating eligibility criteria for scoping reviews; particularly when the chosen wording is to be used in prompts for AI support in the screening process. Reorganizing the inclusion criteria using the Population-Concept-Context framework revealed challenges in each category. A key challenge within the population concept was the complexity of healthcare provider interactions, the individuals involved, and how implicitly these interactions were mentioned in the abstracts. For the concept, difficulties arose in defining what constituted a digital tool, the interactions it facilitates (often not explicitly described), and the relevance of the abstract’s focus. With regard to the context, identifying the appropriate healthcare setting was a major obstacle. The in-depth analysis results prompted the derivation of three refined criteria: narrow, balanced, and wide. ChatGPT’s performance with the refined criteria highlighted a trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across individual prompts. Understandably, the wide prompt achieved the highest sensitivity, while the narrow prompt had the highest specificity, with the original and balanced prompt performing between these two extremes. Combining prompts achieved a good balance between sensitivity and specificity, effectively identifying ambiguous abstracts that required manual screening. Although NW and NBW performed similarly in terms of Matthews correlation coefficient and F1 score, NW resulted in a lower overall workload, indicating the potential of combining prompts to enhance the quality of AI-assisted screening procedures.

This leads to important implications in evidence synthesis: As some authors suggest leaving borderline cases to human researchers’ judgement, one possibility for identifying these cases might be to prompt tools based on large language models to use a rating scale from least relevant to most relevant, similar to the review-automation tool Rayyan [7, 11, 12, 16, 36]. However, this study showed that combining differently tailored eligibility criteria, such as the narrower and wider set, effectively identified borderline cases (represented as undecided abstracts). ChatGPT’s performance without the borderline cases was good in terms of sensitivity and specificity.

Large language models such as ChatGPT have proven to perform well in the screening phase of systematic reviews, with their use honing the consistency of selection and streamlining the review process, and thus ultimately improving the quality of the review [6, 9, 14]. Our study demonstrated that ChatGPT has a high specificity, effectively excluding irrelevant papers and reducing time burden [6]. This is especially beneficial in time-sensitive or resource-constrained settings, where shortcuts such as using one screener or limiting the search strategy are often employed [6, 8]. By reducing the number of abstracts human reviewers need to screen, large language models might help to improve the concentration and motivation of human researchers [37]. This in turn may make it possible to allocate human resources more efficiently, e.g., due to solo-screening, and help to reduce human error rates [8, 37]. A possible screening mode might be to proceed with solo-screening on those abstracts that were included when combining the decisions, e.g., of the narrow and wide prompt and apply the gold-standard screening of two reviewers on those abstracts that resulted in differing decisions, i.e. those undecided.

The secondary analysis revealed that human researchers falsely included 3.56% of abstracts and falsely excluded 0.36%. These findings align with prior studies and underscore an inherent error rate in human decisions [16, 38]. The low false exclusion rate and high false inclusion rate underline that human researchers tend to be overinclusive [11, 35]. Other reasons for human error may include limited or excessive experience with the topic, which can lead to bias, or differing interpretations of the eligibility criteria [13, 35, 39, 40]. These results indicate that, while the human-driven gold standard is used for AI training, it is not perfect [8]. Relying on imperfect training data can lead to a misaligned model and negatively affect the tool’s performance metrics [11, 38].

Given the degree of human error in screening decisions, it is important to establish the performance metrics at which AI tools can be considered “on a par” with human researchers; especially since a major barrier for tool uptake is the expectation of the scientific society that these tools must match or outperform human performance [9, 40]. Another significant obstacle is the lack of transparency of these tools [40]. An inherent element of AI tools is that their training and processes are – despite research efforts on explainable AI – essentially a black box [16]. This lack of transparency hinders trust in AI tools. In our study, eliciting explanations for ChatGPT’s decisions not only improved the prompt but also provided insight into the tool’s inference process, potentially fostering trust [41]. Regardless of the promising results in this study, it needs to be emphasized that human oversight is – at the current point in time – still paramount.

Limitations

Key strengths in our study lie in the combination of qualitative and quantitative methods for an in-depth analysis, with performance evaluation using the gold standard of two independent human reviewers as a comparator for ChatGPT’s performance [35]. However, the study is not without limitations. First, our results are based on a single scoping review, thus additional research is required to explore the applicability of the results to other topics and disciplines. Second, for practical reasons, we conducted the study with one chatbot only, whereas multiple chatbots are continuously evolving, which may limit replication efforts [7, 24]. Additionally, since ChatGPT is not open source, its training and model data, including model biases, remain undisclosed [24, 42]. As large language models – such as the one underlying ChatGPT – continue to evolve, our findings reflect ChatGPT’s performance at the time of the study and may not generalize to future iterations. Future research should additionally compare results with other large language models to assess generalizability. Lastly, while we explored many prompt combinations during refinement, we only tested one framework for eligibility criteria [7]. Applying more structured prompt-engineering and testing other frameworks could potentially yield even better results [43].

Conclusion

With an ever-growing body of research, reviews are becoming more time-consuming, highlighting the need for reliable support tools such as large language models. Our study revealed challenges in ChatGPT’s understanding of eligibility criteria. Refining and structuring these criteria led to more accurate decisions for individual prompts. This accuracy was further improved by combining ChatGPT’s decisions across differently scoped eligibility criteria and effectively identifying borderline cases requiring human judgement. We demonstrated that clear and well-structured eligibility criteria can enhance performance. This finding may also be applicable to reviews in other fields and disciplines, such as the wider medical sector, engineering and social science. Integrating AI into the review process offers the potential to reduce time burdens for researchers and improve the consistency of decisions, provided that challenges such as trust and transparency are addressed. Human oversight remains essential, and further research on the integration and behaviour of large language models is necessary to fully integrate them into research practice.
