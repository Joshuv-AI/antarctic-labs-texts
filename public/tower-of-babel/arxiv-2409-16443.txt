Asymptotic Lower Bounds for the Feedback Arc Set Problem in Random Graphs
Harvey Diamond, Mark Kon, Louise Raphael

Abstract
Given a directed graph, the Minimal Feedback Arc Set (FAS) problem asks for a minimal set of arcs in a directed graph, which, when removed, results in an acyclic graph. Equivalently, the FAS problem asks to find an ordering of the vertices that minimizes the number of feedback arcs. This is considered an algorithmic problem of central importance in discrete mathematics, with varied applications to problems in computer science and operations research. Berger and Shor, in 1990, developed upper bounds for the FAS problem in general directed graphs. Here we find asymptotic lower bounds for the FAS problem in a class of random graphs given by the Erdős-Rényi model G(n,M), with n vertices and M (undirected) edges, the latter randomly chosen. Each edge is then randomly given a direction to form our directed graph. Our interest is in developing a lower bound for the minimal feedback arc set that holds with probability 1 as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty . We show that P ​ r ​ ( Y ∗ ≤ M ⁡ ( 1 2 − log ⁡ n 2 ​ Δ a ​ v ) ) Pr\left(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq M\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}\right)\right) approaches zero exponentially in n n , with Y ∗ \textbf{Y}^{*} the (random) size of the minimal feedback set and Δ a ​ v = M / n \Delta_{av}=M/n the average vertex degree. We subsequently apply our lower bounds to a set of experimental FAS data on related random graphs, as developed by Kathrin Hanauer. Not only does our formula provide a reasonably close lower bound for the minimal set, but the approximation that lies midway between our lower bound and the obvious upper bound of M / 2 M/2 is remarkably close to the computed FAS data over a range of experiments, suggesting that this approximation may in fact be asymptotic to the minimal number of feedback arcs, for large n n , and an excellent estimate even for moderate values.



1
Introduction

In a directed graph (digraph)
\textbf{D}=(V,E)
with
V
the set of vertices and
E
the set of directed arcs, the Minimal Feedback Arc Set (FAS) problem is to find a minimal set of arcs
E^{\prime}
such that removing these arcs produces a digraph
\textbf{D}^{\prime}=(V,E\setminus E^{\prime})
that is acyclic.

The FAS problem is longstanding in the field of graph theory and its applications. While originating as a problem in circuit theory in a paper by
Younger [9], algorithmic interest as a graph theoretic problem can be dated from its identification as an NP-complete problem in Richard Karp’s landmark 1972 paper [7] containing a compilation, with proofs, of important NP-complete combinatorial problems. We note in this regard the recent survey text by Kudelić [8] of results over the subsequent 50 years, primarily concerning the development of practical algorithms for this problem. In the introductory chapter, Kudelić ([8], page 11) provides a listing, with references, of over 30 applications in computing, operations research, and other application areas. The recent paper by J. Fox, Z. Himwich, and N. Mani [3], mainly concerned with algorithms and upper bounds for the FAS problem in classes of digraphs, also contains a brief background discussion and references to several applications of this problem.

As an NP-complete problem, a minimal feedback arc set is difficult to find, so that it is useful to determine how far a feedback arc set might be from its optimal size. In the seminal work by B. Berger and P. Shor [1] in 1990 and following, the authors obtain upper bounds applicable to general directed graphs, along with random algorithms. Here we consider the problem of lower bounds for the FAS problem for an important family of random graphs, associated with the so-called G(n,M) model. While an upper bound tells you to keep working if the size of your feedback set exceeds it, a lower bound tells you when it may not be effective to keep working, if you have already obtained a set not much larger in size than that bound.

In conceptualizing this problem we find it convenient to work with the adjacency matrix
A(\textbf{D})
of a directed graph
D
. This is a
0/1
matrix where a
1
in the
(i,j)
position corresponds to an arc from vertex
i
to vertex
j
. Hence a
1
where
j>i
above the diagonal indicates an arc from a vertex
i
to a higher-numbered vertex
j
. A
1
below the diagonal corresponds to an arc from a higher-numbered vertex to a lower numbered vertex. The latter are called feedback arcs. As noted in the classic text of Harary [5], a digraph is acyclic if and only if it is possible to order the vertices of
D
so that every directed edge goes from a lower-numbered to a higher-numbered vertex. The FAS problem is then to find a renumbering of the vertices that results in a minimal number of
1^{\prime}s
below the diagonal. These
1^{\prime}s
identify the feedback arcs to be eliminated.

As discussed in the papers [1] and [3], in which various bounds and algorithms are considered, we will use
oriented directed graphs
, which are undirected graphs for which each edge is assigned a direction. Equivalently, these are directed graphs without 2-cycles. Our goal in this paper is to obtain
lower
bounds on the size of the minimal arc set, that apply with high probability, asymptotically increasing to 1 with the number of vertices, in a class of oriented directed, random graphs.

The graphs we consider will be random oriented directed graphs on
n
vertices generated from the Erdős-Rényi
G(n,M)
model, in which we have
n
vertices and then
M
randomly chosen undirected arcs between the vertices. Each such arc is then randomly (with probability 1/2) assigned a direction, resulting in an oriented directed graph
D
. Considered as a random experiment, starting with a zero
n\times n
matrix we are choosing
M
locations above the diagonal in which to insert a
1
, and then each such
1
located in the
(i,j)
position,
j>i
, is relocated to the
(j,i)
position below the diagonal with probability
1/2
. We note that in a previous arXiv paper [2]
the authors considered the model referred to there as
D
(n,p) where directed graphs are constructed so that each possible directed arc is included in the graph with probability p. Probability bounds similar to those of Section 2 were obtained there. However the model G(n,M) used here is more germane to research in the field, and we also have available, as discussed in Section 4, a collection of related experimental results on the size of the feedback arc set that can be readily compared with the theoretical results presented in Section 3. We would remark finally that the the random graphs considered here, with their statistically uniform structure, are not typical of practical examples, but they can serve nevertheless as a useful reference example and are worth considering for their mathematical interest.

In Section 2 we develop some basic probabilistic inequalities in considering the
n!
vertex orderings. Separately, we obtain inequalilties that apply to the tails of the binomial distribution
B(M,1/2)
giving the number of feedback arcs in our graphical construction. In Section 3 we obtain our lower bound for the size of the minimal feedback arc set.

In Section 4, we compare our lower bound with experimental results from FAS algorithms applied to a suite of random graphs, obtained by Kathrin Hanauer in her Master’s thesis [4]. We discover that a small modification of our lower bound produces a direct formula approximating the minimal FAS size that graphically matches the experimental data over a range of edge densities and number of nodes. This formula, we conjecture, is actually an asymptotic formula for the size of the minimal feedback arc set for the class of random graphs under consideration.

2
Basic Inequalities for Random Graphs

We begin with some notation and terminology. In our random graph construction, we define the random outcome as the adjacency matrix
A
with
D(A)
as the associated directed graph, and the random variables
X(A)
and
Y(A)
as the number of 1’s above and below the diagonal, respectively. We observe that
Y
is a binomial random variable with binomial distribution
B(M,1/2)
and
\textbf{X}+\textbf{Y}=M
. We refer to a realizable arrangement of 0’s and 1’s under this construction as a
configuration
. Boldface generally indicates the outcome of a random experiment, while normal typeface e.g.
A,X,Y

refer to particular cases. By a permutation of an adjacency matrix
A
we mean a matrix
P^{T}AP
for some permutation matrix
P
, or equivalently, the adjacency matrix of some vertex reordering of the directed graph equivalent to
A
.

For any given directed graph considered here (whether random or not), with adjacency matrix
A
we define
A^{*}(A)
as an adjacency matrix that results from a solution of the FAS problem, with corresponding numbers
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
of ones above and below the diagonal respectively with
X^{*}+Y^{*}=M
. Thus
A^{*}=P^{T}AP
for some
n\times n
permutation matrix
P
and and
Y^{*}
is the minimum number of ones that appear below the diagonal among all such permutations. Correspondingly, given an adjacency matrix
A^{*}
with a minimal set of feedback arcs, the adjacency matrices
\{P^{T}A^{*}P\}
are precisely the adjacency matrices that have
A^{*}
as an optimal reconfigured feedback arc set. The matrix entries of
A^{*}
we refer to as an
optimal configuration

We now develop the basic inequalities used in the next section to obtain our lower bound. We begin with a simple lemma providing a bound on the probability that the minimal feedback arc set has no more than
k
edges, i.e., the event
Y^{*}\leq k
. Because there are
n!
permutations of the vertex ordering, a minimal feedback arc set with no more than
k
arcs can have originated from any of the
n!
permutations of its corresponding adjacency matrix. If we enlarge this set of adjacency matrices to those with
Y\leq k
, the set of
n!
permutations of this set cannot be any smaller. This gives rise to a simple inequality:

Lemma 2.1
.

For our random graph construction, we have the following:

Pr(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq k)\leq n!Pr(\textbf{Y}\leq k)
.

Proof.

First we show that the sets
E^{*}=\{A:Y^{*}\leq k\}
and
E=\{A:\exists P,Y(P^{T}AP)\leq k\}
are the same. Clearly
E^{*}\subset E
by choosing
A=A^{*}
and
P=I
in the definition of
E
. On the other hand, every adjacency matrix
A
in
E
has a reordering with at most
k
feedback arcs, so that
Y^{*}(A)\leq k
. Now the set
E
consists of all the permutations of matrices
A
for which
Y(A)\leq k
since every permuation is invertible via a permutation. Hence
E
consists of the union, over the
n!
different permutations
P
, of the set
\{A:Y(A)\leq k\}
, namely the set
E=\bigcup_{P}\{P^{T}AP:Y(A)\leq k\}
. Each such set in the union has the same probability and by subadditivity property of probability of a union, we have
Pr(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq k)\leq n!Pr(\textbf{Y}\leq k)
.
∎

We note that our sets are not in general disjoint since
Y^{*}(A)<Y(A)
will be true in some cases, and the same matrix
P^{T}AP
may arise from two different
A,P
for which

Y(A)\leq k
. The utility of the result is that
Pr(\textbf{Y}(\textbf{A})\leq k)
can be readily estimated and provides the inequality we are looking for.

Under the random construction, the random variable
Y
has the binomial distribution
B(M,1/2)
. We are interested in the probability that
\textbf{Y}\leq k
for some
k<M/2
, as our question is how small can we expect the minimal feedback arc set to be under this random construction.

Theorem 2.2
.

Pr(\textbf{Y}\leq M(\frac{1}{2}-t))\leq\exp(-2Mt^{2})
.

Proof.

This result follows from Hoeffding’s theorem [6]:
Let
\textbf{X}_{1},..,\textbf{X}_{n}
be independent random variables satisfying
a_{i}\leq\textbf{X}_{i}\leq b_{i}
almost surely. Set
\textbf{S}_{n}=\textbf{X}_{1}+...+\textbf{X}_{n}
. Then

Pr(\textbf{S}_{n}-E[\textbf{S}_{n}]\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

The theorem applies as well to the left tail, namely to
Pr(\textbf{S}_{n}-E[\textbf{S}_{n}]\leq-t)
.
Applying Hoeffding’s theorem in this form to our case, where
\textbf{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\textbf{X}_{i}}
, with
0\leq X_{i}\leq 1
and
E[Y]=M/2
we obtain

Pr(\textbf{Y}-M/2\leq-t)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{M}\right)\text{,}

and then replacing
t
by
Mt
, the result follows.
∎

Remark: The variance of
Y\sim B(M,p)
, with
p=1/2
is
M/4
. The normal approximation of the binomial distribution would suggest that

Pr(Y-M/2\leq-s\sqrt{M/4})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{-s}\exp(-s^{2}/2)ds

while Theorem 2.2 provides the inequality (using
t=s/\sqrt{4M}
)

Pr(Y-M/2\leq-s\sqrt{M/4})\leq\exp(-s^{2}/2)

so the Hoeffding bound gives us the ”correct exponent” even for small values of
s
and the expressions differ only by an algebraic factor
O(1/s)
that will not significantly affect the results were we to seek a more sophisticated inequality for the tails.

3
Asymptotic Lower Bounds

In [1], Berger and Shor developed a randomized algorithm demonstrating an
upper
bound on the minimal feedback arc set of at most
M(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{C}{\sqrt{\Delta}})
, where
M
is the number of edges,
\Delta
is the maximum vertex degree and
C
is a constant that depends only on
\Delta
. Here we develop for our class of random graphs a probabilistic
lower
bound of a similar form, with probability conveging exponentially to one, asymptotically as the number of vertices
n
increases to infinity. As we noted above, in assessing the outcome of an algorithm for the FAS problem, an upper bound tells you to keep working if your feedback arc set exceeds that size, and a lower bound provides some guidance on whether you have sufficiently reduced the size.

From Lemma 2.1 we have
Pr(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq k)\leq n!Pr(\textbf{Y}\leq k)
and Theorem 2.2 provides the inequality
Pr(\textbf{Y}\leq M(\frac{1}{2}-t))\leq\exp(-2Mt^{2})
from which it follows that

Pr\left(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq M(\frac{1}{2}-t)\right)\leq n!\exp(-2Mt^{2}).

(1)

The interplay between
n
,
M
, and
t
provides an opportunity to develop conditions for which
Pr(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq M(\frac{1}{2}-t)))\rightarrow 0
. One such set of conditions is the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\Delta_{av}=\frac{M}{n}
denotes the average vertex degree, we have

Pr\left(\textbf{Y}^{*}\geq M\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}\right)\right)\geq 1-3\sqrt{n}e^{-n}\text{.}

Proof.

Stirling’s formula provides that
\log(n!)\leq n\log n-n+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi n)+\frac{1}{12n}
. In Equation (1) we express
n!=\exp(\log(n!))
, and then using Stirling’s formula, we choose the value of
t
that will cancel the
n\log n
term in the exponent, namely
t=\sqrt{\dfrac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}
. We then have

Pr\left(\textbf{Y}^{*}\leq M(\frac{1}{2}-t)\right)\leq\sqrt{2\pi n}\exp\left(-n+\frac{1}{12n}\right)\leq 3\sqrt{n}e^{-n}\text{,}

rounding up to a bound
3
for the constant factor. Finally, we reverse the inequality, obtaining the Theorem in terms of a lower bound that holds asymptotically with probability converging to
1
exponentially, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

∎

We observe that the lower bound in Theorem 3.1 is clearly only useful when
M
increases with
n
in such a way that
\sqrt{\dfrac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{n\log n}{2M}}
is less than
1/2
, and in most practical cases we would like
\log n=o(\Delta_{av})
. In particular, it is quite clear for dense graphs in which
\Delta_{av}>\epsilon n
uniformly for some
\epsilon>0
the number of feedback arcs will approach
M/2
as
n\rightarrow\infty
with probability 1.

An Application to Experimental Results and a Conjecture

In her Masters thesis [4], Kathrin Hanauer investigated the experimental performance of eight FAS algorithms on random graphs, applying further post-processing algorithms in some cases to reduce the size of the set obtained. Among the graphs considered were those generated from the random construction referred to in the thesis as
ER(n,p)
, in which the undirected Erdős-Rényi model
G(n,p)
is the starting point, with each possible arc included with probability
p
, after which a direction is assigned with equal probability. We compare these results with the lower bounds predicted here for the model
G(n,M)
, choosing for
M
the expected number of edges, namely
M=p\binom{n}{2}=p\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
. Hanauer ran 20 examples for each
(n,p)
pair so the average size for the feedback arc set is reasonably compared with our lower bound as calculated from this expected number.

Using Hanauer’s figures from her thesis, we plot the curves

\Bigl(n,M\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}\bigr)\Bigr)

for the given value of
p
, where
M=p\binom{n}{2}
and
\Delta_{av}=M/n
. Somewhat by chance, we observed that by halving the deviation from
M/2
given by
M\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}
we create a direct heuristic approximation for the size of the feedback arc that is nearly indistinguishable from Hanauer’s experimental minimal FAS curves in most cases, and we include this heuristic curve as well. The theoretical lower bound is plotted in orange and the heuristic approximation is plotted in red. We plot directly on the figures from [4], which include the original captioning.

In Figure 1, the left figure involves the random graphs
ER(n,p)
for
p=1/2

M=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}

reflecting our lower bound and our heuristic approximation, respectively. For these experimental results, the thesis notes there was additional post-algorithmic optimization carried out. On the right in Figure 1 is the case of
p=1
meaning that we start with every possible (undirected) arc on
n
vertices. In Figure 2 the left figure involves
p=.1
and so reflects relatively low density. In the final figure (lower right), we see feedback arc sets for different values of
p
when applied to a fixed number

These plots, applied across a range of arc densities and number of verticies, are very suggestive of a conjecture that our heuristic approximation actually represents asymptotic behavior, formulated as follows.

Conjecture
:
For the random oriented directed graphs considered here, constructed on
n
vertices with
\frac{M}{\binom{n}{2}}\rightarrow p>0\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty
, the minimum feedback arc set satisfies, with probability approaching
1
, the asymptotic formula

M/2-\textbf{Y}^{*}\sim\frac{M}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\Delta_{av}}}\quad.

It is interesting that the graphs show that the conjecture works well even for reasonably small values of
n
. The case of
p=.5
corresponds to each vertex having roughly in-degree and out-degree each of about
n/4
so these are relatively dense graphs. In terms of proving the conjecture, these large random graphs will, at least in expected value, have the name number of arcs going in and out, so the effect of reordering vertices will be second order, depending on probabilistic fluctuations in the locations of the arcs, which have an asymptotic regularity for large values of
n
. Should this conjecture prove to be true, it would be of interest to see at what rate
p\rightarrow 0
could be established so that the result would still be true.

5
Summary

In contemplating the Minimal Feedback Arc Set problem, it is useful to know how effective reordering the vertices of an oriented directed graph would be in reducing the number of feedback arcs. We have provided here an estimate for a lower bound of the minimal feedback arc set that applies with high probability, effective for large values of the average degree, within a sample space for which each oriented directed graph of
M
arcs is equally likely. In particular, this includes large graphs with a uniformly positive fraction of possible arcs. In this case we conjecture an asymptotic formula for the minimal number of feedback arcs, as
n\rightarrow\infty
suggested by a set of previous experiments in the literature.
