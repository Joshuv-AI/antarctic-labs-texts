Scoping review mapping the implementation and adaptation of life skills training programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder: contexts, frameworks, interventions and outcomes
Authors: Sirirat Nitayawan, Mary C Leamy, Natthapon Inta, Patraporn Bhatarasakoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo map the implementation and adaptation of life skills training programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder across diverse settings, identifying frameworks, intervention components, outcomes and implementation strategies.DesignA scoping review following Arksey and O’Malley’s methodology and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews extension for Scoping Reviews reporting guidelines.Data sourcesSix electronic databases (MEDLINE (Ovid), PubMed (National Library of Medicine), PsycINFO (APA PsycNET), CINAHL (EBSCO), ThaiJo (Thai Journals Online) and TCI-Thailand (Thai-Journal Citation Index) were searched from 1970 to 10 December 2024, supplemented by handsearching reference lists and relevant organisational websites.Eligibility criteriaPrimary studies targeting life skills training for adults with schizophrenia spectrum disorder, including at least three life skills components (medication management, social skills, communication, organisation/planning, transportation, financial management) and providing intervention details.Data extraction and synthesisData were extracted using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication checklist. Outcomes were analysed using Kirkpatrick’s four-level training evaluation framework. Implementation adaptation, barriers and enablers were identified through narrative synthesis.Results33 studies from 7 countries (France, Italy, Spain, Canada, USA, Turkey and China) met inclusion criteria. Three major programme frameworks emerged: University of California, Los Angeles Social and Independent Living Skills Programme, Functional Adaptation Skills Training and Cognitive-Behavioural Social Skills Training. Cultural adaptations were crucial for implementation success, with programmes demonstrating adaptability across diverse settings while maintaining core therapeutic components. Implementation barriers included cognitive deficits, transportation difficulties and workforce limitations; enablers included structured formats, diverse teaching methods and family involvement. Most studies showed positive behavioural changes, but only one-third reported broader systemic outcomes.ConclusionsLife skills training programmes had been reported to be implemented across diverse settings when appropriately adapted to cultural contexts and resource constraints. Most programmes combine structured learning with real-world practice, accommodate cognitive limitations through diverse teaching methods and engage families in the intervention process. Future research should focus on implementation strategies enhancing skill generalisation, addressing resource limitations in low-income and middle-income countries, and evaluating longer-term outcomes.

Introduction

Schizophrenia spectrum disorder affects approximately 24 million people worldwide, representing about 1% of the global population.1 This severe mental disorder is characterised by disorganised thinking, perception, emotions, language, sense of self and behaviour, including hallucinations, delusions and negative symptoms such as diminished emotional expression and avolition.2 The impact extends beyond the individual, creating significant burdens for families, communities and healthcare systems. Globally, the economic burden of schizophrenia spectrum disorder is estimated at US$155.7 billion annually, with direct healthcare costs accounting for approximately 30%–40% and indirect costs related to lost productivity, premature mortality and carer burden comprising the remainder.3 4 The disability burden is equally substantial, with schizophrenia spectrum disorder ranking among the top 10 causes of disability worldwide, contributing to dependency, carer strain, stigmatisation and social exclusion.5 The condition often leads to severe functional impairments, with up to 60%–70% of individuals experiencing significant deficits in daily living skills, interpersonal relationships and occupational functioning.6

The standard treatment approach for schizophrenia spectrum disorder globally involves a combination of antipsychotic medications, psychosocial interventions and community-based support.7 Despite advances in pharmacotherapy, significant limitations persist, including medication non-adherence (30%–60% of patients), treatment-resistant symptoms (approximately 30% of cases), and substantial side effects that impact quality of life.8 Psychosocial interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy, family interventions and social skills training show modest usefulness but face implementation challenges due to resource constraints, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries.9 The global mental health landscape reflects these challenges with significant variations in service availability; the median number of mental health workers ranges from below 1 per 100 000 population in low-income countries to over 70 in high-income countries.10 While many countries have implemented mental health policies that include psychosocial interventions, significant gaps exist in accessibility and quality, with an estimated treatment gap of 50%–90% for severe mental disorders like schizophrenia spectrum disorder.11 Cultural factors, including stigma and varying beliefs about mental illness, further complicate treatment engagement and recovery outcomes across different contexts.12

Liberman13 describes the pivotal role of Life Skills Training (LST) in the evolution of psychiatric rehabilitation programmes, particularly during the mid-20th century. By the 1970s, Dr Robert Liberman had developed structured social skills training programmes specifically designed to improve the functional capacities of individuals with severe mental illnesses. This marked a significant advancement in the systematic application of LST programmes in psychiatric contexts. LST programmes for individuals with chronic psychotic disorder encompass structured interventions designed to enhance functional capabilities in domains such as medication management, social skills, communication skills, organisation and planning, transportation and financial management.14 These programmes typically incorporate components of psychoeducation, skills training, behavioural practice and environmental supports to promote recovery and independence.

Evidence supports the efficacy of life skills interventions in improving functional outcomes, reducing hospitalisation rates and enhancing quality of life for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder.15 16 For example, Functional Adaptation Skills Training (FAST) has been shown to improve everyday functioning in middle-aged and older adults with schizophrenia.17 Similarly, individualised LST programmes improve functional outcomes, particularly in areas such as money management, self-care and communication.18 Despite promising findings, significant gaps remain. The generalisation of learnt skills to real-world settings often falls short, highlighting a need for interventions like in vivo amplified skills training to bridge this gap.19 The relationship between LST and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) warrants clarification: IADL are the complex functional activities required for independent living (eg, medication management, financial management, transportation use), while LST programmes are the structured interventions designed to teach these competencies. In psychiatric rehabilitation, 'life skills’ often encompasses both basic ADL such as personal hygiene and more complex IADL, though this review focuses primarily on programmes targeting IADL given their greater complexity and importance for community integration.14 Furthermore, in two systematic reviews, interventions targeting IADL for individuals with schizophrenia have shown moderate to strong evidence in improving independent functioning and reducing relapse rates.20 IADL encompass complex activities necessary for independent community living, such as managing finances, using transportation, shopping and managing medications. LST programmes specifically target these IADL competencies through structured interventions, with the terms often used interchangeably in psychiatric rehabilitation literature. While IADL represent the functional activities themselves, LST programmes are the structured interventions designed to teach and enhance performance of these activities. Tungpunkom et al21 acknowledged the importance of life skills in promoting independence but noted inconsistent and low-quality evidence regarding their effectiveness in improving functional outcomes. However, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the optimal frameworks, components and implementation strategies for these programmes across diverse cultural and resource contexts.

Existing research has predominantly originated from high-income Western settings, with limited investigation of adaptations for diverse global contexts.22 Moreover, the field lacks systematic mapping of implementation frameworks that guide the translation of life skills interventions from controlled research settings to real-world practice environments.23 While some programmes emphasise cognitive remediation approaches, others prioritise practical skills acquisition or peer support models, yet the comparative of these different frameworks remains unclear.24 Furthermore, there is limited synthesis of implementation strategies that address contextual barriers such as resource constraints, workforce limitations and cultural factors that influence programme sustainability and scalability.25

The substantial gaps in knowledge regarding the implementation of LST programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder necessitate a comprehensive mapping of existing approaches. A scoping review methodology is particularly appropriate for this topic given the heterogeneity of interventions, settings and implementation strategies in the literature.26 While previous reviews have examined the efficacy of life skills interventions,20 21 this is the first scoping review to systematically map implementation approaches, cultural adaptations and training evaluation outcomes across diverse global contexts using structured frameworks (Template for Intervention Description and Replication, TIDieR and Kirkpatrick).

Review question

How have LST programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder been implemented across diverse settings, and what are their defining frameworks, intervention components, implementation strategies and outcomes?

Method

Study design

This review followed the guidelines proposed by Arksey and O’Malley,26 which involves five key steps: developing the research question; identifying relevant studies; selecting studies according to inclusion criteria; charting the data; collating, summarising and reporting the results and considering the implementations of the study findings for policy, practice and research. Reporting of the findings of this review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) proposed by Tricco et al.27

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on the PCC (population, concept and context) criteria28 from the PRISMA-ScR. Included studies had to target LST programmes for individuals with schizophrenia or studies with the majority of participants with schizophrenia aged over 18 years. Studies focusing on carers or family members, non-psychotic disorders or organic disorders affecting brain function were excluded.

For the concept criteria, studies had to include LST programmes with at least three components targeting independent daily living (from medication management, social skills, communication skills, organisation and planning, transportation and financial management). Studies had to report intervention components and implementation strategies with training details provided either in the study or by contacting authors.

For context criteria, primary studies targeting LST in hospital, clinic, home or community-based services were included.

All primary research studies (including randomised controlled trial (RCT), quasi-experimental, observational and case studies) were included. Excluded study designs were systematic reviews, meta-analyses, protocols, editorials, commentaries and opinion pieces. Searches were limited to English and Thai languages. English was selected as the primary language of international scientific publication, while Thai was included given the research team’s linguistic capacity and the importance of capturing regional implementation approaches from Southeast Asia, a region underrepresented in global mental health literature.

Search strategy

Considering the broad uncertainties on its use and various terms used to describe LST programme, a pilot search was conducted to establish a comprehensive background understanding. The pilot search revealed that studies with LST programme as a training intervention did not always include ‘LST programme’ in the title or abstract. Therefore, the review adopted a two-step approach: (1) use a variety of terms searching LST programme, and then (2) hand search the papers identified in step one to identify training intervention studies.

The comprehensive search was conducted within six electronic databases (MEDLINE (Ovid), PubMed (National Library of Medicine), PsycINFO (APA PsycNET), CINAHL (EBSCO), ThaiJo (Thai Journals Online), and TCI-Thailand (Thai-Journal Citation Index) from 1970 to 10 December 2024. The year 1970 was selected as the starting point since this was when the term “life skills training” was coined in American rehabilitation programme for people with severe mental illnesses by Dr. Robert Liberman.13 Additionally, Google Scholar was searched using simplified terms ‘Schizophrenia life skills training’ and 'Schizophrenia life skills programme’ to identify grey literature and additional studies not indexed in traditional databases. The refined search strategy involved: (i) traditional database searching using comprehensive search terms (see online supplemental file A for complete search strategies including all filters and limits); (ii) internet search engines; and (iii) grey literature by hand-searching reference lists, searching relevant organisational websites and key journals.

Screening and selection

After removal of duplicates, titles/abstracts were screened in duplicate using Covidence software. Disagreements and uncertainties were resolved by the first and the third authors (SN and NI) with reference to the title and abstract. Full-text articles were retrieved if inclusion criteria were met or if the abstract contained insufficient information. The first and the third authors (SN and NI) independently did full-text screening and conflicts were discussed and resolved with input from the second author (ML) and the fourth (PB), and the inclusion or exclusion criteria were regularly checked.

Data extraction

Data extraction included: details of different LST programmes; general study characteristics (author, year, country of study, aim, design, outcome and key findings); summary of current training programme and outcomes using the TIDieR)checklist29 by providing a structured format which describes interventions in a clear and detailed manner; and outcome measures of the training. The TIDieR checklist guided extraction of underlying theory (why), materials and procedures used (what), the intervention provider (who), the mechanism of delivery (how), the location (where), intervention schedule and intensity (when and how much), whether adaptations were used (tailoring), and the intervention adherence (how well).

Data analysis

An analytical descriptive method was used to synthesise the extracted data and identify patterns across LST programmes. A narrative synthesis approach was employed to draw comparisons between different programmes, examine contextual and process-oriented information and identify common implementation strategies and adaptations. Kirkpatrick’s Four Levels of Training Evaluation framework30 was used to categorise and analyse outcomes systematically. The model includes: 1) Reaction: how participants feel about the intervention; 2) Learning: the degree to which they acquire intended knowledge or skills; 3) Behaviour: how well they apply what was learnt in real life; and 4) Results: the broader clinical or systemic outcomes resulting from the intervention.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The initial search returned 24 335 records. Screening of titles and abstracts excluded 23 385 articles, leaving 167 as potentially eligible. Screening of full-text items excluded an additional 134 articles, for the following reasons: studies not retrieved (n=33; primarily articles behind paywalls and inaccessible dissertations), wrong intervention (n=5), wrong study design (n=15; systematic reviews, protocols and commentary papers), not in English or Thai language (n=9), wrong patient population (n=15), no training details provided (n=14), and fewer than three life skills components for training (n=43). The final review included 33 eligible articles, all primary research studies. LST programmes have been conducted across seven countries: Turkey, USA, France, Italy, Canada, Spain and China. Of the 33 included studies, most were conducted in the USA (n=17, 51.52%) and China (n=6, 18.18%), followed by Turkey (n=5, 15.15%). A total of 22 studies (66.67%) were conducted in high-income countries (USA, France, Italy, Canada, Spain) and 11 studies (33.33%) in upper-middle-income countries (Turkey, China) according to World Bank classifications.31

Sample sizes across studies ranged from single-case studies involving one participant to trials with up to 378 participants.32 Participants primarily included individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia (21 studies, 63.64%), schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (10 studies, 30.3%), schizophrenia or psychotic mood disorder (one study, 3.03%), and schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder or schizophreniform disorder (one study, 3.03%) recruited from outpatient clinics (n=18, 54.55%), inpatient psychiatric units (n=7, 21.21%), community mental health centres (n=7, 21.21%), and patient’s home (n=1, 3.03%). The majority of studies utilised RCT designs (n=22, 66.67%), followed by quasi-experimental designs (n=7, 21.21%), single-case experimental designs (n=2, 6.06%) and descriptive/observational studies (n=2, 6.06%). Intervention durations most commonly ranged from 8 weeks to 6 months, though some were as brief as 1–2 weeks while others extended over multiple months.

LST programmes primarily targeted psychosocial functioning, everyday living skills, social functioning, symptom management and community reintegration. Common assessment tools included Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, Social Functioning Scales, Social Disability Screening Schedule, University of California, San Diego (UCSD) Performance-based Skills Assessment (UPSA), FAST, and various scales measuring quality of life, self-efficacy and independent living skills. Key findings across studies consistently indicated significant improvements in social functioning, daily living skills and reduced psychiatric symptoms (particularly negative symptoms). However, the transfer of skill improvements to real-world functioning varied, and outcomes related to self-efficacy, quality of life and employment showed inconsistent results. Studies incorporating cognitive-behavioural approaches and comprehensive LST reported more robust and sustained improvements in functional outcomes compared with control conditions. Eight studies (24.24%)1419 3237 reported follow-up evaluations, ranging from 3 months to 2 years, demonstrating varying degrees of sustained intervention effects.

Review findings

LST programmes

The most used LST programme in training evaluations studies was the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Social and Independent Living Skills Programme,38 the FAST,14 the Cognitive-Behavioural Social Skills Training (CBSST)39 and other programmes. A summary of the review findings is attached in the online supplemental file B.

UCLA social and independent living skills programme

This programme is a psychosocial intervention designed for individuals with severe and persistent mental illness, primarily schizophrenia. Based on social learning theory and behavioural techniques, the programme comprises structured modules addressing essential life skills, including medication management, symptom management, substance abuse management, community re-entry, recreation for leisure, basic conversation, friendship and intimacy, and workplace fundamentals. Typically delivered over 3–6 months with sessions held 1–3 times weekly, lasting approximately 90–120 min per session, and delivered through group sessions that include focused instructions, motivational enhancement, observational learning, role-playing, behavioural rehearsal, positive reinforcement, problem-solving and homework practice assignments. The programme emphasises active learning and repeated practice to accommodate participants’ cognitive deficits and facilitate the generalisation of learnt skills to real-world settings.

The UCLA Social and Independent Living Skills Programme is one of the most frequently reported psychosocial interventions for schizophrenia in the literature, with implementations documented across diverse cultural contexts. Cross-cultural adaptations have been reported in China, where modifications incorporated Chinese family systems and collective responsibility.3537 In Turkey, modifications emphasised family education and community integration aligned with Turkish cultural values.40 In Spain, the programme was reformatted for in-home delivery to accommodate family-centred care traditions.41 In France, it was integrated with existing French psychiatric rehabilitation frameworks.42 These adaptations modified delivery methods, family involvement strategies and practical examples to align with local cultural contexts while maintaining the programme’s core therapeutic components.

Functional Adaptation Skills Training

FAST is a structured psychosocial intervention based on social-cognitive theory and independent living skills approaches. This programme is designed to improve everyday functional abilities in patients with schizophrenia, addressing persistent impairments that remain despite medication treatment.14 17 34 The programme targets six core domains: medication management, social skills, communication, organisation/planning, transportation and financial management through structured modules delivered in small face-to-face groups. Sessions typically last 90–120 min, occurring weekly or twice weekly over 12–24 weeks, and incorporate behavioural modelling, role-playing, homework and reinforcement strategies to promote skill acquisition. FAST is delivered by master’s or doctoral-level therapists, sometimes alongside paraprofessionals, with rigorous fidelity monitoring through manualised protocols and supervision.17 43 The intervention demonstrates consistent improvements in functional skills measured by the UCSD UPSA, though transfer to real-world settings can be limited.14 17 44

Cultural adaptations have been developed, such as Programa de Entrenamiento para el Desarrollo de Aptitudes para Latinos (PEDAL), which was adapted from the FAST programme for Latino patients with schizophrenia in the USA.35 Patterson et al conducted this adaptation in the USA through three stages: (1) careful Spanish translation and back-translation; (2) incorporating Latino cultural components like familiar foods, songs and telenovelas and (3) aligning with Mexican cultural values of familismo (family prioritisation), respeto (respect for elders) and personalismo (emphasis on close relationships). Practical modifications included focusing on cash management rather than cheques (financial management), emphasising family-assisted transportation and framing medication adherence as benefiting the family system rather than promoting independence (organisation/planning). The programme incorporated three dedicated family sessions based on focus group feedback, gender-appropriate activities and adapted materials for lower educational backgrounds. These adaptations were implemented with Latino patients with chronic psychosis in the USA.35

Cognitive-Behavioural Social Skills Training

CBSST is a structured psychosocial intervention targeting functional impairments and negative symptoms in schizophrenia that persist despite medication. The programme consistently incorporates three modules: Cognitive Skills using the ‘3C’s’ method (Catch It, Check It, Change It) to challenge defeatist thoughts, Social Skills through role-playing exercises to improve communication, and Problem-Solving using the ‘SCALE (Specify, Consider, Assess, Lay out, Execute)’ approach for real-life challenges. Delivered face-to-face in small group sessions by master’s or doctoral-level therapists with specialised training, CBSST typically spans 24–36 weekly sessions with supplemental individual goal setting.36 39 45 Studies consistently demonstrate significant improvements in functioning measured by the Independent Living Skills Survey, negative symptoms, defeatist attitudes and educational engagement.36 37 46 Implementation challenges include transportation difficulties and variable retention rates (54%–86%), while success facilitators include workbooks, peer support, flexible entry points and community-based delivery.

Other programmes

Several other innovative programmes were identified in the review. The individualised LST programme developed in Turkey18 addresses functional challenges through a structured 8-week programme with 16 face-to-face sessions (90 min each), progressing through four phases: goal setting, building awareness, skills development and real-life application. A Turkish ‘I Can Ride the Storm’ programme47 follows nine modules addressing disease awareness, medication adherence, self-care, household management, social communication, activity engagement, thought management, behaviour control and helping others. The Social Skills and Neurocognitive Individualised Training (SSANIT) programme in Italy48 integrates rehabilitation with social skills training and cognitive remediation through weekly group sessions and computerised tasks. The Group Patient-Led LST programme in China49 is unique in its patient leadership approach, where patients with better life skills lead structured training sessions, promoting peer learning and self-efficacy. The SOSKITRAIN programme (Spain)50 employs virtual reality to provide simulated environments for practising complex social interactions, representing one approach using technology-based methods.

Intervention components and delivery

LST programmes vary significantly in their components and delivery methods. (See online supplemental table 2 for a summary of life skills programmes according to the TIDieR checklist.) The TIDieR framework captures key intervention components including underlying theory (‘why’), materials and procedures (‘what’), providers (‘who’), delivery mechanism (‘how’), location (‘where’), schedule and intensity (‘when and how much’), adaptations (‘tailoring’), and implementation fidelity and participant adherence (‘how well’). Analysis using the TIDieR framework revealed the following patterns and is described below.

Theoretical foundations

LST programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder are theoretically grounded in several established approaches. Social Learning Theory forms the foundation of the UCLA Social and Independent Living Skills Programme, emphasising observational learning, behavioural rehearsal and reinforcement.13 19 Cognitive-behavioural theory underpins programmes like CBSST, focusing on challenging defeatist thoughts and building cognitive skills through structured modules.36 39 Social-cognitive theory provides the theoretical basis for the FAST programme, addressing skill acquisition through demonstrations and guided practice.14 17 Some programmes, particularly in China, incorporate behavioural theory with token economies and positive reinforcement systems.51 These theoretical foundations recognise that despite pharmacological treatment, individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder continue to experience significant functional impairments requiring targeted psychosocial interventions.

Materials and procedures

Most programmes use comprehensive training manuals with step-by-step modules, particularly evident in the UCLA programme and FAST.13 14 Materials include participant workbooks, visual aids, role-play scripts and assessment tools adapted for cognitive limitations common in schizophrenia.36 52 Procedural components consistently include skill demonstration (modelling), behavioural rehearsal through role-play, structured feedback, problem-solving techniques and homework assignments for skill generalisation.33 39 Key skill domains addressed across programmes include medication management, social skills, communication, organisation and planning, transportation, financial management, self-care and community navigation.14 16

Training providers

The review found psychiatrists involved in seven studies,1843 48 49 5355 general nurses in eight studies,1417 40 48 5457 psychiatric nurses in four studies,515860 occupational/rehabilitation therapists in seven studies, psychologists in seven studies and social workers in three studies.41 48 54 Provider qualifications varied across studies and likely reflected country-specific workforce requirements and regulations. In US-based studies, FAST and CBSST were typically delivered by master’s or doctoral-level clinicians,17 39 consistent with US licensing requirements where master’s level training is the minimum qualification for independent clinical practice in many mental health disciplines. Paraprofessionals were used in eight studies,14 17 19 32 33 41 50 52 often working alongside professionals to increase implementation capacity.33 50 Innovative approaches emerged in resource-limited settings, such as the Chinese patient-led LST programme where high-functioning patients were trained as peer facilitators.56 Most programmes (25 out of 33) specified specialised training for providers, highlighting the importance of skill development beyond basic professional qualifications.

Mechanism of delivery and location

Group format was most common (30 studies), typically involving 6–12 participants and leveraging social learning and peer support. Individual formats (9 studies) allowed for personalised skill development and targeted intervention. Some programmes (9 studies) combined both approaches, using groups for skill acquisition and individual sessions for personalisation. Regarding location, outpatient settings dominated (19 studies), typically in community mental health centres, while inpatient psychiatric units (7 studies) focused on transition skills. Community settings (studies) included residential facilities and real-world environments to enhance skill generalisation. One notable innovation was Moriana’s41 home-based delivery of an adapted UCLA programme in Spain, which accommodated family-centred care traditions and overcame transportation barriers.

Training schedule and intensity

Duration ranged from brief interventions (1–8 weeks in 19 studies) to medium-term programmes (9–30 weeks in 9 studies) and extended interventions (31–60 weeks in 6 studies). Session frequency and number showed similar variation, with most programmes (26 studies) providing 1–40 sessions, while more intensive approaches (7 studies) delivered 41–60 sessions. The most common format was weekly group sessions lasting 60–120 min, though some inpatient programmes implemented daily sessions, such as Brown & Munford’s49 approach with 4-hour sessions 5 days weekly. Several longer-term programmes incorporated booster sessions to maintain gains, as seen in Zhou’s33 30-month intervention with monthly maintenance sessions following intensive training.

Cultural adaptation

Cultural adaptations were documented across multiple implementation contexts and can be organised into three main themes. Family engagement adaptations modified programmes to align with local family structures, including incorporation of collective responsibility dynamics (China35 36), addition of dedicated family sessions (PEDAL for Latino patients in USA),35 emphasis on family education (Turkey)40 and home-based delivery to accommodate family involvement (Spain).41 Content and delivery modifications addressed local practices and preferences, such as shifting from check-writing to cash management (PEDAL,35 transforming relaxation training into culturally acceptable ‘passive’ music therapy with headphones (China51), and emphasising community integration activities aligned with local values (Turkey).40 Technology and individualisation approaches included automatic difficulty adjustments in computerised components (SSANIT, Italy)48 and systematic needs assessment for content tailoring (Turkey).18 These adaptations maintained core programme components while modifying delivery methods, examples and engagement strategies to align with local contexts.

Programme adherence and fidelity

As shown in ‘How well’ column (see online supplemental table 2), intervention fidelity and participant adherence were reported in 26 of 33 studies, though measurement approaches varied considerably. Implementation fidelity was supported through manualised protocols with standardised materials, provider training and certification, regular supervision and monitoring via fidelity checklists.17 36 Participant adherence was tracked through attendance records, homework completion monitoring and skill practice documentation, with retention rates varying across studies. CBSST studies reported rates between 54% and 86%.36 39 Some programmes implemented structured measures to enhance adherence, such as Li and Wang’s58 token economy system that increased motivation through tangible rewards. Eight studies did not report clear fidelity or adherence measures, representing a notable methodological gap potentially limiting evaluation of implementation quality.

Implementation barriers and enablers

Implementation factors were identified at three distinct levels, with clear contrasts between barriers and facilitating strategies:

Patient Level: Barriers included cognitive deficits limiting skill acquisition, negative symptoms affecting motivation and difficulties generalising skills to real-world settings.19 34 High dropout rates (40% in one Turkish programme)47 and transportation difficulties39 further challenged participation. Corresponding enablers addressed these barriers through motivation enhancement strategies (token economies,58 workbooks for home practice), peer support in group formats and flexible programme entry allowing participants to join ongoing groups.

Provider Level: Barriers centred on workforce limitations, including shortage of trained staff and time-intensive intervention requirements. Facilitating strategies included task-sharing with paraprofessionals,14 17 19 32 33 41 50 52 use of peer facilitators,56 structured manuals enabling consistent delivery across providers and cotherapist models distributing workload.47

System Level: Barriers included fragmented services with poor inpatient-outpatient transitions, short admission periods limiting intervention delivery and insufficient funding. System-level facilitators involved integration with existing psychiatric services,51 family involvement reducing service burden41 and adaptation to local infrastructure constraints through home-based41 or community-based delivery.

Implementation implications: The documented strategies suggest that task-sharing with non-specialists, peer-led models and family engagement approaches may address workforce constraints, the most pervasive barrier identified. These strategies warrant systematic evaluation, particularly in resource-limited settings where traditional specialist-delivered models are not feasible.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive mapping of LST programmes for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder across diverse global contexts. The findings reveal important patterns in implementation frameworks, intervention components and adaptation strategies and outcomes that can inform future research and practice.

Theoretical foundations and programme components

LST programmes are grounded in well-established theoretical frameworks, primarily social learning theory, cognitive-behavioural theory and social-cognitive theory. These frameworks recognise that despite pharmacological treatment, individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorder continue to experience significant functional impairments requiring targeted psychosocial interventions.13 Three structured programmes dominated the literature: the UCLA Social and Independent Living Skills Programme (based on social learning theory), FAST (based on social-cognitive theory), and CBSST (based on cognitive-behavioural theory). Despite different theoretical foundations, these programmes shared core components including medication management, social skills, communication, organisation/planning, transportation and financial management.

The review identified that programmes commonly incorporated structured, manualised formats with clear lesson plans, diverse teaching methods accommodating cognitive limitations and progressive skill-building from knowledge acquisition to real-world application.33 39 These components align with previous findings that structured approaches are particularly effective for individuals with cognitive deficits characteristic of schizophrenia.15

Implementation context and cultural adaptations

A key finding is the significant variation in implementation contexts, ranging from high-income Western settings to resource-constrained environments. While high-income countries typically used specialised professionals (psychiatrists, psychologists) for delivery, middle-income countries demonstrated innovative approaches, such as peer-led delivery in China49 and home-based family interventions in Spain.41 These adaptations reflect pragmatic responses to workforce limitations that characterise global mental health systems.11

Cultural adaptations were frequently reported across implementations. The UCLA modules were implemented across diverse settings such as China, Turkey, Spain and France, with documented modifications including incorporating family systems and collective responsibility in China35 36, emphasising family education in Turkey,40 reformatting for in-home delivery in Spain41 and integrating with existing psychiatric rehabilitation frameworks in France.42 These adaptations modified delivery methods, family involvement strategies and practical examples to align with local cultural contexts. While some systematic reviews have examined the effectiveness of cultural adaptations in mental health interventions,12 this scoping review mapped the types and frequency of cultural modifications reported in LST programmes without evaluating their impact.

Implementation barriers and facilitators

Implementation challenges identified in this review mirror broader issues in global mental health implementation science. Patient-level barriers included cognitive limitations, negative symptoms affecting motivation and difficulties generalising skills to real-world settings.19 34 These findings align with Harvey and Strassnig’s6 observation that cognitive deficits significantly predict functional disability in schizophrenia spectrum disorder. System-level barriers included workforce limitations, insufficient funding and poor continuity between inpatient and outpatient services reflecting the widespread resource constraints that characterise mental health systems globally.25

Key facilitators of identified implementation included structured, manualised formats, diverse teaching methods accommodating different learning styles, motivation enhancement strategies and family involvement. These enablers align with established principles of implementation science that emphasise the importance of intervention adaptability, stakeholder engagement and infrastructure support.23

Implications for research and practice

This review highlights several priorities for future research. First, more studies using standardised outcome measures with longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the durability of effects. Second, research on cost-effectiveness across different delivery models would provide valuable information for policy decisions, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Third, more implementation studies examining the transition from controlled research to real-world practice would help identify strategies for scaling evidence-based interventions. Additionally, research exploring innovative delivery approaches, such as digital adaptations or peer-led models, could address workforce limitations in underserved areas. Finally, more studies from low-income countries are needed to understand implementation challenges and opportunities in the most resource-constrained environments. Future research should prioritise implementation studies in low-resource settings, where the treatment gap for schizophrenia spectrum disorder remains highest (50%–90% in low-income and middle-income countries)10 11 but evidence on LST programmes is scarce, as demonstrated by this review’s limited representation from such settings. Addressing the identified implementation barriers, participant cognitive deficits, transportation difficulties, limited availability of trained staff and challenges creating adequate real-life practice opportunities requires innovative approaches that should be systematically developed and evaluated. Implementation research should employ more rigorous frameworks to understand contextual factors affecting programme success, with particular attention to fidelity assessment, cost analyses and sustainability planning, which were notably absent in most included studies.

This review identified variations in programme delivery that may inform future implementation efforts. Studies documented the involvement of non-specialist providers, including peer facilitators56 and paraprofessionals,14 17 19 32 33 41 50 52 suggesting possible alternatives to specialist-only delivery models. However, without quality appraisal or effectiveness data, these findings should be considered exploratory rather than prescriptive.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review offers several methodological strengths compared with previous research. Unlike earlier reviews that focused primarily on efficacy outcomes, this review’s comprehensive mapping of implementation frameworks across diverse contexts provides a more nuanced understanding of how LST programmes function in real-world settings. The detailed extraction using the TIDieR checklist represents a more structured approach than previous studies that often lacked systematic documentation of intervention components. The inclusion of studies from both high-income and middle-income countries distinguishes this review from previous literature that predominantly examined Western contexts. For instance, while Tungpunkom et al21 noted inconsistent evidence regarding effectiveness, they did not explore the critical role of cultural adaptations that emerged as central to implementation success in this review. The systematic analysis using Kirkpatrick’s evaluation framework revealed important differences in outcome patterns compared with previous research. Where earlier reviews like John et al20 focused primarily on clinical outcomes, this review identified that most studies demonstrated positive behavioural changes (level 3), but only one-third reported broader systemic outcomes (level 4), suggesting a significant gap in understanding long-term effectiveness that was not highlighted in previous research.

A notable limitation is the exclusive focus on published literature in English and Thai, potentially missing relevant studies in other languages, particularly problematic given the global scope of the review. While Asher et al9 included studies from low-income settings, this review lacks representation from such contexts, limiting comparative analysis across the full spectrum of resource environments. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in search strategies and inclusion criteria. Asher et al examined broader ‘community-based psychosocial interventions’ which encompassed various intervention types beyond structured LST programmes, while our review required studies to specifically report at least three life skills components with detailed training information. Additionally, our language restrictions (English and Thai only) may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages that are more common in low-income settings. Furthermore, our requirement for detailed intervention descriptions (per TIDieR criteria) may have inadvertently excluded studies from resource-limited settings where publication standards or journal constraints limit methodological detail.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the exclusive focus on published literature in English and Thai potentially missed relevant studies. Second, the heterogeneity of outcome measures prevented direct comparisons between studies. Third, and inherently, the scoping review methodology maps evidence without quality appraisal or effectiveness assessment. While this approach successfully identified implementation patterns, cultural adaptations and research gaps, determining which programmes or adaptations are most effective requires systematic review and meta-analysis, a logical next step given the evidence base mapped here.

Conclusions

This scoping review reveals critical gaps in the global evidence base for LST programmes in schizophrenia spectrum disorder. The absence of studies from low-income countries, where 50%–90% of individuals with schizophrenia lack access to treatment, represents a fundamental limitation in understanding implementation across the full spectrum of resource contexts. The predominance of specialist-delivered, resource-intensive programmes questions their relevance for settings where mental health workers number less than 1 per 100 000 population.

Three dominant programmes (UCLA Social and Independent Living Skills, FAST, CBSST) emerged from high-income settings, each requiring 12–24 weeks of structured sessions delivered by trained professionals. While cultural adaptations were documented across seven countries, the review cannot determine whether these modifications enhanced outcomes without quality appraisal. The identified adaptations, family integration, peer-led delivery and simplified content directly respond to workforce constraints, yet their effectiveness remains unexamined.

Study limitations

Language restrictions to English and Thai likely excluded relevant evidence from francophone Africa, Latin America and other regions where different implementation models may exist. The requirement for detailed intervention descriptions may have systematically excluded studies from resource-limited settings where publication constraints prevent comprehensive reporting. These methodological choices potentially perpetuate the evidence gap from contexts where implementation innovations are most needed.

Policy and practice implications

The documented use of non-specialist providers (peers, family members, paraprofessionals) across multiple studies suggests task-sharing approaches warrant systematic investigation rather than continued reliance on specialist-only models. The 40% dropout rates and transportation barriers identified indicate that current programme structures may be fundamentally misaligned with patient circumstances. Digital adaptations and peer-led models, documented in only three studies, directly address the primary implementation barriers yet remain peripheral to the evidence base.

Future research should prioritise implementation studies in low-income settings using pragmatic designs that accommodate local publication capacities. Rather than adapting Western programmes, developing context-specific interventions with communities may yield more sustainable solutions.
