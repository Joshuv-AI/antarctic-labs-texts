Simply Connected Wandering Domains of Small Order Entire Functions
Adi Glücksam, Leticia Pardo-Simón

Abstract
We show that any bounded, simply connected domain with analytic boundary can be realised as a wandering domain of an entire function of any prescribed order in ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) . Extending results of Boc Thaler, our construction simultaneously prescribes the domain and the exact order of the map. In particular, we produce the first examples of entire functions with bounded simply connected wandering domains of each order in ( 0 , 1 2 ) \bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr) .

Introduction

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an entire function and denote by
f^{n}
its
n
-th iterate,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
Fatou set
,
F(f)
, consists of the points
z\in\mathbb{C}
for which the family
\{f^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is normal in a neighbourhood of
z
, and the
Julia set
is its complement,
J(f):=\mathbb{C}\setminus F(f)
. We refer to the surveys
[
7
,
41
,
43
,
14
]
for background on iteration of entire functions. A connected component of the Fatou set,
U\subset F(f)
, is called a
wandering domain
if
f^{n}(U)\cap f^{m}(U)=\emptyset
whenever
n\neq m
. By Sullivan’s no-wandering-domain theorem,
[
44
]
, rational maps, and, in particular, polynomials, admit no wandering domains, i.e., this phenomenon is inherently transcendental.

The first example of an entire map with a wandering domain was constructed by Baker in
[
2
]
. Since then, many examples have been produced by various methods. Notably, constructions based on the Eremenko–Lyubich approximation scheme
[
18
]
—rooted in classical approximation theorems of Runge and Arakelyan—offer significant flexibility. This flexibility allows one to prescribe features such as the family of iterates having finite limit functions (yielding
oscillating
wandering domains) or even prescribe the exact shape of the domains, e.g.
[
16
,
35
]
. The trade-off of these methods, however, is that typically they provide little quantitative information about the map itself—most prominently about its set of singular values (singularities of the inverse) or its
order of growth
, defined by

\rho(f)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log M_{f}(r)}{\log r},\qquad M_{f}(r):=\max_{\left|{z}\right|=r}\left|{f(z)}\right|.

By contrast, constructions based on quasiconformal techniques (e.g.
[
30
,
10
,
33
,
13
]
),
lifts
of periodic components (e.g.
[
26
,
21
]
), or infinite products (e.g.
[
4
,
11
,
43
]
) can produce entire functions with wandering domains and bounded singular sets and/or finite order; however, they typically offer limited control over the geometry of the domains themselves.

In keeping with a central theme of transcendental dynamics—identifying minimal transcendental features, such as growth, that still permit non-rational behaviour (here, wandering domains)—a natural goal is to reduce the order of growth as much as possible without losing geometric control. In this work we achieve simultaneous control on both the growth-rate and the geometry: we prescribe both the analytic boundary of any bounded simply connected wandering domain and the (arbitrarily small) order of growth of the corresponding entire function.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\alpha\in\left({0,1}\right)
and every bounded simply connected domain,
U
, with analytic boundary there exists an entire function,
f
, of order
\alpha
for which
U
is a wandering domain and the iterates,
f^{n}|_{U}
, are univalent.

We now place the result within its broader context. Wandering domains for entire maps may be simply connected or multiply connected. The multiply connected case is relatively well understood: multiply connected wandering domains are necessarily bounded, their iterates form a nested family of annuli tending to infinity and the dynamics are power-map-like, i.e., broadly speaking, they can really only posses one type of geometry and one type of dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
for more details). Furthermore, building on his original example, Baker showed in 1985 that for every
\rho\in[0,\infty]
there exists an entire function of order
\rho
with a multiply connected wandering domain
[
4
]
. Bishop later gave, via an infinite product, an example of an entire map of order
0
with multiply connected wandering domains whose boundaries form a countable collection of
C^{1}
curves, being this the first example of an entire function with Julia set of Hausdorff dimension
1
,
[
11
]
.

By contrast, simply connected wandering domains display a much wider range of phenomena. For example, they could be bounded or unbounded (
see
, e.g.,
[
26
,
32
]
), the set of limit functions of
\left\{{f^{n}|_{U}}\right\}
may include finite constant ones, i.e., they can be
oscillating
(first example in
[
18
]
), and they arise for functions in class
\mathcal{B}
(entire functions with
a
bounded singular set), see
[
10
]
followed by
[
31
,
22
,
33
]
. Moreover, their internal dynamics admit several distinct types
as seen in

[
6
,
19
,
17
]
; see also
[
20
]
for analogous results in the meromorphic setting.

The possible geometries of simply connected wandering domains were recently unravelled by Boc Thaler
[
16
]
. They constructed for any bounded, simply connected, regular open set,
U
, an entire function realising
U
as an escaping (or oscillating) univalent wandering domain. Building on these ideas, Martí-Pete, Rempe and Waterman
[
35
]
further broadened the class of admissible shapes by realising escaping wandering domains for any bounded simply connected domain
D
such that
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{D}
has an unbounded connected component
W
with
\partial W=\partial D
, thus supporting highly intricate geometries such as Lakes of Wada continua; see also
[
34
]
for meromorphic analogues. However, none of these constructions provides any bound on the growth-rate of the entire map.

Theorem
1.1
requires the analyticity of
\partial U
.
Such sets are bounded, simply connected, regular open sets
, i.e., they fall within Boc Thaler’s setting in
[
16
]
because of the conformal extendability of the Riemann map across
\partial U
(see
[
37
, Prop. 3.1]
). In particular,
[
16
]
shows that boundaries of bounded simply connected wandering domains can be made arbitrarily well-behaved, in contrast to invariant Fatou components, whose boundaries are typically highly non-smooth, see
[
1
,
12
]
. Theorem
1.1
demonstrates that this contrast in boundary regularity between wandering domains and invariant Fatou components persists even for functions of very slow growth.

Looking for the lowest possible growth-rate of entire maps admitting simply connected wandering domains, Sixsmith
[
42
]
constructed an entire function of order zero, defined via an infinite product, that possesses a simply connected wandering domain. For higher growth, Baker
[
3
, Theorem 5.2]
produced simply connected wandering domains for every order
\rho\geq 1
, and Martí-Pete–Shishikura constructed functions in class
\mathcal{B}
of orders
\frac{p}{2}
for all

, in particular of order
\frac{1}{2}
—the smallest possible within class
\mathcal{B}
. To the best of our knowledge, our result gives the first examples with
bounded
simply connected wandering domains for all orders strictly between
0
and
1
, thereby filling the gaps between the order-
0
construction of Sixsmith, the order-
\frac{1}{2}
class-
\mathcal{B}
examples of Martí-Pete and Shishikura, and Baker’s examples for
\rho\geq 1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

For every
\rho\in[0,\infty]
there exists and entire function of order
\rho
with a bounded simply connected wandering domain.

This is the simply connected analogue of Baker’s theorem for multiply connected wandering domains
[
4
]
.

We remark that, in the
unbounded
case, Baker’s 1981 conjecture
in

[
5
]
asserts
the non-existence
of unbounded wandering domains for functions of order
<\tfrac{1}{2}
; this remains open. Examples of order
>\tfrac{1}{2}
appeared in
[
17
]
; see also
[
36
]
for the case of real functions with real zeros.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
, built on Hörmander’s
\bar{\partial}
-techniques, combines the approximation paradigm of
[
16
,
35
]
, with the approximation tools first used in
[
17
]
that allowed adding the quantitative control needed
to prescribe
small order. In the spirit of Boc Thaler, we construct a sequence of entire maps converging locally uniformly and use designated sets of points, distributed in ‘layers’ accumulating at
\partial U
, whose forward images are mapped into attracting basins in order to shape the wandering domain. The two key new ingredients are
using a method that allows us to simultaneously control the approximation
error and the
growth
, and a
geometric
quantitative ‘straightening-to-a-disk’ step near almost round analytic boundaries. A carefully chosen parameter scheme and a diagonal convergence argument then yield the desired map with prescribed order and wandering dynamics.

In order to obtain the prescribed set as a wandering domain, we need to precisely preserve the distinction between images of the set and its complement under iterations. We must therefore use conformal maps to map our wandering domain forward. On the other hand, there is no conformal map that changes the modulus of annuli. This implies that the distance between iterations of different ‘layers’ surrounding the set, which are mapped to an attracting basin, and iterations of the set, which are translated forward, decreases, which naturally increases the growth-rate. It requires meticulous analysis to balance between the growth-rate bounds and the error accumulated along different iterates, resulting in a highly technical and long proof.

Structure of the article.

Section 2 sets notation and recalls the
\bar{\partial}
–approximation framework, collecting the subharmonic tools (gluing and puncture lemmas) and quantitative Riemann–mapping estimates used later. Section 3 proves Lemma 3.1, a ‘straightening and separating’ lemma, which, for a given bounded simply connected domain with boundary that is nearly round, yields an entire function that uniformly ‘straightens’ the domain with derivative control, sends selected small disks near the boundary into a tiny neighbourhood of a fixed far point, and satisfies an explicit global growth bound. Section 4 assembles the sequence: we fix the parameter scheme, carry out the base ‘straightening’ step, perform the inductive step, control the attracting basin, and derive the growth estimates. Section 5 passes to the limit and completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.2
The approximation method

We shall construct our desired entire functions
using
an approximation argument based on Hörmander’s
\bar{\partial}
-theorem. We use the following one-dimensional version
of

[
28
, Theorem 4.2.1]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Hörmander)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a subharmonic function. Then, for every
g\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
there exists
\beta\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
solving
\bar{\partial}\beta=g
in the sense of distributions such that

(2.1)

\iint_{\mathbb{C}}\left|{\beta(z)}\right|^{2}\frac{e^{-u(z)}}{\left({1+\left|{z}\right|^{2}}\right)^{2}}dm(z)\leq\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{C}}\left|{g(z)}\right|^{2}e^{-u(z)}dm(z)=:{\mathcal{I}^{2}},

provided that
{\mathcal{I}^{2}}
is finite.

We use this theorem to approximate a model map that prescribes different dynamics in different regions—when done correctly, the approximation inherits the same dynamical behaviour.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\{\Omega_{k}\}
be a collection of pairwise
4\varepsilon
–separated planar domains, i.e.,
\operatorname{dist}(\Omega_{j},\Omega_{k})\geq 4\varepsilon
for all
j\neq k
. There exists a numerical constant,
C>0
, and a smooth function,
\chi:\mathbb{C}\to[0,1]
, such that:

1.

\chi\equiv 1
on each
\Omega_{k}
.

2.

\chi\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\bigcup_{k}\Omega_{k}^{+\varepsilon}
.

3.

\operatorname{supp}\nabla\chi\subset\bigcup_{k}\big(\Omega_{k}^{+\varepsilon}\setminus\Omega_{k}\big)
and
\|\nabla\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}
.

For details on the construction of such maps, see for example
[
24
, Proposition 2.5 and Observation 2.6]
.

The technique we now describe was previously used in various contexts (e.g.,
[
40
]
,
[
23
,
8
,
24
,
25
]
). In particular, we previously introduced this technique (jointly with Evdoridou in
[
17
]
) to construct fast escaping wandering domains. We prescribe the desired dynamics via a
model map
,
h
, that is holomorphic on the union of pairwise separated domains,
\bigcup_{k}\Omega_{k}^{+\varepsilon}
,
without assuming any global holomorphic extension. We then glue these local pieces with the cut-off
function

\chi
, described in Proposition
2.2
, and correct the resulting
\bar{\partial}
–error by solving the equation
\bar{\partial}\beta=\bar{\partial}(\chi h)=(\bar{\partial}\chi)\cdot\,h
using Theorem
2.1
with a carefully designed subharmonic weight,
u
. The separation obtained by multiplying by
\chi
, ensures that
(\bar{\partial}\chi)
is supported in thin bands around
\Omega_{k}
, where
the subharmonic function,
u
, will be large, which makes the integral in (
2.1
),
\mathcal{I}^{2}
, finite and the resulting error estimates small. The following proposition summarizes the application of the method.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\{\Omega_{j}\}
be a collection of planar domains satisfying the separation hypothesis of Proposition
2.2
, and denote by
\chi
the smooth function constructed in Proposition
2.2
. Suppose
h
is holomorphic on
\bigcup_{j}\Omega_{j}^{+\varepsilon}
, and let
u:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
be a subharmonic function satisfying

\mathcal{I}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{C}}\left|{(\bar{\partial}\chi)\,h}\right|^{2}e^{-u(z)}dm(z)}<\infty.

Let
\beta
be Hörmander’s solution to the
\bar{\partial}
-equation
\bar{\partial}\beta=\bar{\partial}(\chi h)=(\bar{\partial}\chi)\cdot\,h
provided by Theorem
2.1
, and define the map
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

f(z):=\chi(z)\,h(z)-\beta(z).

Then
f
is entire and satisfies:

(A_{1})

(Error bound) For every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and every
r\in\left(0,\max\left\{{1,\frac{\left|{z}\right|}{3}}\right\}\right]
for which
\chi|_{B(z,r)}\equiv 1
,

\left|{f(z)-h(z)}\right|\leq\frac{2+2\left|{z}\right|^{2}}{{r}}\cdot{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\underset{w\in B(z,r)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)}.

(A_{2})

(Growth bound) For every
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\left|{f(z)}\right|\leq\underset{w\in B(z,1)}{\max}\left|{h(w)}\right|+\frac{2+2\left|{z}\right|^{2}}{{\sqrt{\pi}}}\cdot{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\underset{w\in B(z,1)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)}.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
,
\bar{\partial}\chi
is bounded, and contained in a region where
h
is holomorphic, which implies that
g\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
. This, combined with the proposition assumptions, means that all hypotheses of Hörmander’s theorem are satisfied. Observe that
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
, since
\chi
is bounded, and
\beta\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{C})\subset L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
. Moreover, since the equation
\bar{\partial}\beta=g
in the statement of Hörmander’s theorem holds in distribution, we obtain that

\overline{\partial}f(z)=\overline{\partial}\left({\chi(z)\cdot h(z)-\beta(z)}\right)=\overline{\partial}\chi(z)\cdot h(z)-\overline{\partial}\beta(z)=g(z)-g(z)=0

in distribution. By Weyl’s lemma, weak solutions for the Laplace equation are smooth solutions, see e.g.
[
29
, p. 67, Remark 57]
, therefore the equality above holds point-wise, i.e.,
f
is entire.

In order to prove property

(

A
1

)

, note that for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
for which
\chi(w)=1
for all
w\in B(z,r)
,
\beta(w)=f(w)-h(w)
, i.e.,
\beta
is holomorphic on
B(z,r)
. Applying Cauchy’s integral formula for the function
\beta=f-h
and using Hörmander’s theorem,

\displaystyle\left|{f(z)-h(z)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\beta(z)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\left|{\iint_{B(z,r)}\beta(w)dm(w)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{{\sqrt{\pi}r}}\sqrt{\iint_{B(z,r)}\left|{\beta(w)}\right|^{2}dm(w)}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{{r}}\underset{w\in B(z,r)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)}\left({1+\left|{w}\right|^{2}}\right)\sqrt{\iint_{B(z,r)}\left|{\beta(w)}\right|^{2}\frac{e^{-u(w)}}{\left({1+\left|{w}\right|^{2}}\right)^{2}}dm(w)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{{r}}\underset{w\in B(z,r)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)}\left({1+\left|{w}\right|^{2}}\right)\cdot\mathcal{I}\leq\frac{2+2\left|{z}\right|^{2}}{{r}}\cdot{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\underset{w\in B(z,r)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)},

where the second inequality is due to Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality holds since for
r\leq\frac{\left|{z}\right|}{3}

\underset{w\in B(z,r)}{\max}\left(1+\left|{w}\right|^{2}\right)\leq 1+\left({\left|{z}\right|+r}\right)^{2}\leq 2\left({1+\left|{z}\right|^{2}}\right).

To see property

(

A
2

)

, arguing similarly, with
r=1
and using Minkowski inequality (
the triangle inequality of the
L^{2}
norm
),

\displaystyle\left|{f(z)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\left|{\int_{B\left({z,1}\right)}{f(w)}d{m(w)}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\int_{B\left({z,1}\right)}{\left|{\chi(w)\cdot h(w)-\beta(w)}\right|^{2}}d{m(w)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\underset{w\in B(z,1)}{\max}\left|{h(w)}\right|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\int_{B\left({z,1}\right)}{\left|{\beta(w)}\right|^{2}}d{m(w)}}

\displaystyle\leq\underset{w\in B(z,1)}{\max}\left|{h(w)}\right|+\frac{2+2\left|{z}\right|^{2}}{{\sqrt{\pi}}}\cdot{\mathcal{I}}\cdot\underset{w\in B(z,1)}{\max}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}u(w)},

concluding the proof.
∎

2.3
Subharmonic functions

As we saw in the previous subsection, subharmonic functions play a central role in our construction: they make the
L^{2}
integral finite and keep the error and growth bounds under control where needed. We include two of the main results we shall use, and refer the reader to
[
38
,
27
]
for basic definitions and standard results.

We require our subharmonic
function
to satisfy different properties in different regions. Specifically, where we require good approximation, the function needs to be small (potentially negative), but in ‘gluing’ regions (i.e., where
\partial\chi
is supported), the function needs to be large. We shall often use the following well-known ‘gluing lemma’ and its corollary.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
38
, Theorem 2.4.5]
)
.

Let
u
be a subharmonic function on an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
, and let
v
be a subharmonic function on an open subset
V
of
U
such that

\limsup_{z\to\zeta}v(z)\leq u(\zeta)
for
\zeta\in U\cap\partial V.

Then
\tilde{u}
is subharmonic on
U
, where

\tilde{u}=\begin{cases}\max\left\{{u,v}\right\}&\text{on }V,\\
u&\text{on }U\setminus V.\end{cases}

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
u
and
v
be subharmonic functions defined on open sets
U,V\subset\mathbb{C}
respectively, such that
U\cap V\neq\emptyset
. If

\limsup_{z\to\zeta}v(z)\leq u(\zeta)\text{ for }\zeta\in U\cap\partial V\quad\text{ and }\quad\limsup_{z\to\zeta}u(z)\leq v(\zeta)\text{ for }\zeta\in V\cap\partial U,

then
w
is subharmonic on
U\cup V
, where

w=\begin{cases}u&\text{on }U\setminus V,\\
\max\left\{{u,v}\right\}&\text{on }U\cap V,\\
v&\text{on }V\setminus U.\end{cases}

The following result allows
one
to modify a given subharmonic function by
essentially
assigning the value
\left({-\infty}\right)
to prescribed points, which
gives rise to
a new subharmonic function taking very negative values in neighbourhoods of such points, see
[
25
, Figure 1]
.
Such modification is
particularly useful when such points are in the regions where we want our entire map to approximate the model map
closely, for example
part

(

A
1

)

of Proposition
2.3

could become
arbitrarily small around them.

Lemma 2.6

(The Puncture Lemma
[
25
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
u\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous subharmonic function and, for
N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, let
\left\{{B_{k}=B(z_{k},r_{k})}\right\}_{k=1}^{N}
be a collection of pairwise disjoint disks. Assume that for every
k
,
\underset{z\in B_{k}}{\inf}\;\Delta u>0
. Then there exists a subharmonic function
v\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

1.

u(z)=v(z)
whenever
z\not\in\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{N}B_{k}
;

2.

For every
\delta\in\left({0,1}\right)
, and
k
,

\underset{z\in B(z_{k},\delta\cdot r_{k})}{\max}\;v(z)\leq\underset{z\in B_{k}}{\max}\;u(z)-c\cdot r_{k}^{2}\cdot\left(\underset{z\in B_{k}}{\inf}\;\Delta u\right)\cdot\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

where
c>0
is some numerical constant.

2.4
Conformal maps

One of the key ingredients in our proof is repeatedly straightening the iterates of
U
back to the unit disk. We do so by using a Riemann map while keeping quantitative control of how close the straightening is to the identity when the these iterates are nearly a disk.
The following result of Warschawski becomes useful for this goal.

Lemma 2.7

(Warschawski,
[
45
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
G\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain satisfying that

(2.2)

\partial G\subset\mathbb{A}\left(\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon},1+\varepsilon\right)\quad\text{ for some }\varepsilon>0.

Let
h:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow G
be the Riemann map normalised so that
h(0)=0
and
\arg(h^{\prime}(0))>0
. Then for every
r<1

\underset{\left|{z}\right|\leq r}{\max}\;\left|{h(z)-z}\right|\leq r\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon}\left({1+\frac{2}{\pi}\log\left({\frac{1+r}{1-r}}\right)}\right).

Note that Lemma
2.7
deals with bounds on maps from the disk to a domain. Our construction requires an inverse form, given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
G\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain satisfying (
2.2
) for some
\varepsilon\in\left({0,e^{-2}}\right)
. Then

\left|{z}\right|\;<\;1-3\,\varepsilon\log\left({\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)\Rightarrow\left|{h^{-1}(z)-z}\right|\;<\;3\,\varepsilon\log\left({\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right).

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to understand what is the image of
R\cdot\mathbb{D}
under
h^{-1}
, and use Warschawski’s lemma, Lemma
2.7
, on the new radius, i.e., if
h^{-1}(R\cdot\mathbb{D})\subset B(0,\rho(R))\subseteq\mathbb{D}

then

by inclusion of the domains and monotonicity of function on the right.

(2.3)

\displaystyle|w-h(w)|\leq\left|{w}\right|\varepsilon e^{\varepsilon}\left({1+\frac{2}{\pi}\log\left({\frac{1+\left|{w}\right|}{1-\left|{w}\right|}}\right)}\right)\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\log\left({\frac{1}{1-\left|{w}\right|}}\right).

is conformal, for every
