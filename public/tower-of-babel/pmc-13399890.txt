Arthroscopic Partial Repair and Arthroscopic Debridement Improve Pain and Functional Outcomes in Older Patients With Massive Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tears: A Systematic Review
Authors: Dave Osinachukwu Duru, Andrew Kailin Zhou, Rahul Geetala, Andrew Metcalfe, Salma Chaudhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the current evidence on patient‐reported outcomes and complications after arthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement for massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (MIRCTs) in patients aged 65 years or older.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted through September 2025 according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and case series (>10 patients) reporting outcomes of arthroscopic partial repair or debridement in patients aged ≥65 years with minimum 12‐month follow‐up. Two reviewers independently extracted data and assessed study quality using Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) and Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tools.ResultsTwenty‐six studies (n = 942 patients) were included. Both interventions led to clinically meaningful improvements in pain and function. Mean visual analog scale pain score improvements ranged from 2.0 to 5.8 with partial repair and 4.0 to 6.5 with debridement. Mean Constant‐Murley score gains ranged from 15.0 to 33.6 with partial repair and 16.4 to 31.4 with debridement. Retear rates after partial repair were high (41.7%‐87.3%) but not consistently associated with worse outcomes. Rates of infection and conversion to reverse shoulder arthroplasty were low across both interventions.ConclusionsArthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement are both effective joint‐preserving options for managing massive irreparable rotator cuff tears in older patients, resulting in significant improvements in pain and function, with low complication rates.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, systematic review of Level I to IV studies.

Massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (MIRCTs) remain a challenging clinical problem, particularly in older patients. 1 Irreparable tears are commonly associated with pain and progressive functional impairment, and may ultimately lead to cuff tear arthropathy. 2 With an ageing population and increasing expectations for activity and independence in later life, the prevalence of MIRCTs is rising, making optimal management in older patients an important clinical issue.

In patients aged 65 years and older, treatment decisions for MIRCTs are influenced by age‐related biological and functional factors, including increased fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy, diminished bone quality, and a higher burden of comorbidities. 3 In this population, treatment goals often prioritize pain relief and preserving activities of daily living. 4 These considerations affect the choice between joint‐preserving arthroscopic procedures and arthroplasty.

Reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) is an effective treatment for MIRCTs associated with pseudoparalysis or cuff tear arthropathy, providing reliable pain relief and functional improvement. 5 , 6 Compared with joint‐preserving arthroscopic procedures, RSA is more invasive with higher upfront procedural costs. 7 , 8 Consequently, joint‐preserving interventions may be considered in select patients. This could include older patients with intact deltoid function, lower functional demands, or a preference to defer arthroplasty.

Arthroscopic partial repair and debridement represent two common joint‐preserving strategies for MIRCTs. 9 Partial repair aims to restore rotator cuff continuity as far as possible through tendon mobilization, margin convergence, or medialization; this attempts to restore stability by rebalancing the remaining anterior and posterior components of the rotator cuff. 10 , 11 , 12 In contrast, arthroscopic debridement involves removal of degenerative tendon tissue and subacromial pathology to reduce pain and improve mobility. 13

Despite widespread use of arthroscopic partial repair and debridement, the relative benefits of these interventions in older patients with MIRCTs remain uncertain. Individual studies report improvements in pain and function with both procedures, but outcomes vary considerably, and direct comparative data are limited. A systematic review reported similar short‐ to mid‐term outcomes following partial repair and debridement for irreparable rotator cuff tears. 14 However, the review did not specifically focus on older patients, include comparative studies, or synthesize data on retear rates or conversion to RSA. 14

The purpose of the systematic review was to evaluate the current evidence on patient‐reported outcomes and complications after arthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement for MIRCTs in patients aged 65 years or older. The hypothesis was that both arthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement would result in significant improvements in pain and function in older patients with MIRCTs.

METHODS

This review was conducted and structured in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The methodology was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251027913).

Comparative or observational studies reporting outcomes for arthroscopic partial repair or debridement in the target population were included. Eligible designs were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case‐control studies, and case series (>10 patients). Nonrandomized and noncomparative studies were included due to the paucity of direct comparative evidence.

Excluded studies included: (a) patients cohorts with a mean or median age less than 65 years old, unless outcomes for the older patients subset were reported separately; (b) focused solely on surgical procedures other than partial repair or debridement (e.g., RSA or tendon transfer); (c) with a follow‐up period of less than 12 months; (d) case reports or case series with less than 10 patients; and (e) conference abstracts, editorial expert opinions, and review articles. Only articles written in English were included, and no restrictions were placed on the publication year.

The review focused on older patients, aged ≥65 years, with MIRCTs. Each study's definition of “massive” or “irreparable” cuff tear was used. Included studies examined arthroscopic partial rotator cuff repair or debridement of a MIRCT. As partial repairs often follow from debridement, studies involving debridement and an attempted cuff repair were categorized as “partial repairs.” “Debridement” studies involved removal of torn tendon edges, subacromial decompression, tuberoplasty, bursectomy, and/or biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, without attempted cuff repair. Partial repair was compared with debridement when comparative data were available; otherwise, single‐arm studies of either intervention were included.

A comprehensive search was conducted across 3 databases: MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Ovid), and Cochrane Library (CENTRAL). The search spanned from each database's inception to September 2025. Combinations of keywords and MeSH terms were used for “partial rotator cuff repair”, “arthroscopic debridement”, “subacromial decompression”, “massive rotator cuff tear”, “irreparable rotator cuff tear”, “reverse shoulder arthroplasty”, and “shoulder replacement”, joined using Boolean operators AND/OR. Reference lists of relevant articles and prior reviews were searched to identify additional studies. No date limits were applied, and only English articles were included.

All search results were imported into Rayyan (Cambridge, MA), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (D.O.D., R.G.) independently screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. Two reviewers (D.O.D., A.K.Z.) then examined full‐text articles to determine final inclusion, with reasons documented for exclusions. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (S.C.).

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (D.O.D., A.K.Z.) using a standardized form designed a priori. The following variables were collected: (1) study details: author names, year of publication, country, single‐center vs. multicenter, study design, and level of evidence. (2) Patient population: sample size, patient demographics (e.g., mean age and sex), and clinical characteristics (presence of pseudoparalysis, dominant arm affected, relevant comorbidities, prior shoulder surgeries). (3) Tear characteristics: how the study defined a “massive” or “irreparable” tear, tendons involved, approximate tear size, chronicity, and any classification used (e.g., Hamada grade). (4) Intervention details: partial repair or debridement, including specific surgical techniques (e.g., subacromial decompression, biceps tenotomy/tenodesis, or margin convergence). (5) Follow‐up duration: the mean or median length of post‐operative follow‐up for outcome assessment. (6) Outcomes: The primary outcomes of interest were patient‐reported outcome measures of shoulder function and pain. When reported, preoperative baseline scores and follow‐up scores were collected for these measures. Pain outcomes were captured, typically reported as a visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, pre‐ and post‐operatively. Secondary outcomes extracted included range of motion (e.g., active forward flexion/elevation and external rotation) and strength. The number of patients who required conversion to RSA were recorded, as well as the time interval to conversion, if provided. In accordance with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines, 15 sex‐disaggregated outcome data were extracted where available. If outcome data were only presented in graphs, values were approximated using plot digitization. Authors were contacted for additional data.

Two reviewers (D.O.D., A.K.Z.) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool for randomized studies and the Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool for nonrandomized studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (S.C.).

A quantitative meta‐analysis was considered a priori. However, formal pooling was not performed due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity (quantified using the I 2 statistic). Most studies were single‐arm case series without comparator groups. Outcome reporting was inconsistent, with variable outcome scales, differing follow‐up time points, and incomplete reporting of standard deviations. As a result, a formal meta‐analysis was not feasible. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the data.

RESULTS

Our search yielded 1577 records after removing duplicates. Twenty‐six studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The studies comprised 4 RCTs, 1 nonrandomized prospective cohort study, 7 nonrandomized retrospective cohort studies, 3 prospective case series, 9 retrospective case series, and 2 case‐control studies (Table 1).

Of the 26 included studies, 2 studies were comparative (arthroscopic partial repair versus debridement). 7 , 23 Twenty‐four had a single relevant arm: 15 evaluated partial repair and 9 evaluated debridement. Partial repair often involved medialization or margin convergence to partially approximate the cuff to the anatomic footprint. 24 , 40 In most studies evaluating partial repair, arthroscopic debridement was performed concurrently as part of the procedure. Debridement techniques varied and included removal of degenerative tendon tissue, bursectomy, subacromial decompression, acromioplasty, tuberoplasty, and biceps tenotomy or tenodesis, applied according to study‐specific or patient‐specific indications. The extent and combination of these adjunctive procedures were inconsistently reported across studies, and procedural detail was frequently insufficient to allow stratification of outcomes by individual surgical components.

Postoperative rehabilitation protocols generally followed a staged progression. Partial repair studies employed an initial protection phase using an abduction sling or brace for approximately 4 to 8 weeks, with passive range of motion initiated either immediately or after a short delay, followed by active motion between 6 and 10 weeks postoperatively. 16 , 17 , 18 , 39 Strengthening exercises were usually introduced after 6 to 12 weeks, with return to heavier activities delayed until at least 6 months. 25 , 27 , 32 , 33 , 36 In contrast, studies evaluating debridement more frequently permitted immediate active and passive motion as tolerated, often without formal immobilization. 13 , 38

Patient Demographics

The 26 studies consisted of 942 shoulders from 942 patients. Five hundred ninety‐seven patients underwent partial repair, and 345 underwent debridement. In the partial repair group, 45% were female. The debridement group was 45% female, suggesting similar sex demographics.

For partial repair, mean age ranged from 65 to 72 years. For debridement, mean age ranged from 65 to 76. Mean follow‐up ranged from 12 to 79 months for partial repair group and 12 to 145 months for debridement. Mean preoperative Hamada grade ranged from 1 to 1.5 for partial repair (reported in 6 studies) and 1 to 1.2 for debridement (reported in 2 studies).

Thirteen studies reported whether the dominant arm was treated. Seven evaluated arthroscopic partial repair, and 5 evaluated arthroscopic debridement. One study directly compared partial repair and debridement. 23 The dominant arm was treated at a similar rate for both treatments, ranging from 62% to 85% in partial repair studies and 59% to 86% in debridement studies.

Tear Characteristics

Most partial repair and debridement studies defined a massive rotator cuff tear as >5 cm and/or involving 2: or more tendons. An irreparable tear was defined as a tear that could not be restored to the anatomical footprint. In both partial repair and debridement cohorts, supraspinatus (97.4%‐100% of cases) and infraspinatus (66.7%‐100% of cases) were most affected. Subscapularis was involved in 31.4%‐77.4% of cases.

Outcome Measures

Fourteen different outcome measures were reported in the included studies. The most common were VAS, Constant‐Murley (CM), and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores.

Pain

Both partial repair and debridement studies reported significant reductions in pain and improvements in function from baseline (Tables 2 and 3). Pain was primarily assessed via VAS scores, reported in 12 studies evaluating partial repair and 5 studies evaluating debridement. In studies investigating partial repair, mean improvement in VAS at final follow‐up ranged from 2.0 to 5.8. In studies evaluating debridement, the mean improvement ranged from 4.0 to 6.5. The 2 comparative studies showed identical mean changes in VAS comparing partial repair to debridement. 7 , 23 Two studies evaluating partial repair showed an initial short‐term improvement in VAS at 12 month follow‐up, which was not maintained at final follow‐up. 17 , 36 Many of the studies showed statistically significant improvements in VAS scores from baseline to final follow‐up. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for VAS was analyzed in 4 studies, all evaluating partial repair, not debridement. The percentage of partial repair patients reaching the MCID for VAS ranged from 66.7% to 100%.

Function

Function was primarily assessed via CM scores (Table 2). Mean changes in CM scores were reported in nine studies evaluating partial repair and four evaluating debridement. In studies investigating partial repair, mean improvement in CM scores at final follow‐up ranged from 15.0 to 33.6. In studies evaluating debridement, mean improvement ranged from 16.4 to 31.4. Many of the studies showed statistically significant improvements in CM scores from baseline to final follow‐up. Three partial repair studies assessed MCID achievement for CM scores, ranging from 82.6% to 95%. Additional functional measures were used (e.g., ASES), but limited use complicated comparisons.

In one study comparing partial repair and debridement directly, there was no significant difference in change in pain (VAS) or function (qDASH and CM) from preoperative to final follow‐up (P > .05). 23 However, in the other comparative study, partial repair led to significantly higher increases in CM and UCLA scores compared with debridement (P < .05). 7

Two studies investigated CM scores at multiple follow‐up times: one evaluated partial repair and the other evaluated debridement. 30 , 37 In both cases, there was improvement in CM scores at each follow‐up (Table 3); baseline patient characteristics such as age, sex, and smoker status were not significantly different between the 2 study groups, supporting comparison. An exploratory mean‐difference meta‐analysis of postoperative CM score improvement across the 2 comparative studies showed substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 82.4%).

Four studies reported sex‐specific differences in functional outcomes. Three studies observed superior functional outcomes in male patients compared with female patients following debridement. 13 , 22 , 30 One study reported improved functional outcomes in male patients compared with female patients following partial repair. 37 In contrast, 6 studies found no significant association between sex and postoperative functional outcomes. 20 , 23 , 24 , 33 , 34 , 41

Range of Motion (ROM)

ROM was measured by degree of forward flexion, external rotation, and abduction. ROM scores were reported in nine studies evaluating partial repair and five studies evaluating debridement. ROM improved significantly with either partial repair or debridement. One comparative study found partial repair improved range of motion more than debridement alone. 7

Muscle Strength

Muscle strength outcomes were reported in 8 studies, 6 evaluating partial repair and 2 evaluating debridement; heterogeneity in scoring measures limits comparisons across single series. In 1 comparative study assessing muscle strength, partial repair showed improved outcomes compared to debridement. 7 The other comparative study reported significantly increased strength with both partial repair and debridement, but did not report whether one conferred greater improvement than the other. 23

Acromiohumeral Distance (AHD)

AHD was reported in 13 studies: 8 evaluating partial repair and 5 evaluating debridement. Among studies assessing partial repair, mean change at final follow‐up in AHD ranged from −1.9 to +0.4 mm. Studies assessing debridement reported a similar reduction in AHD, with mean changes typically ranging from −1.3 to −0.6 mm. One debridement study reported a mean change of −4.0 mm; however, this study had the longest final follow‐up (145 months), whereas others were <48 months. One comparative study reported AHD remained relatively stable with partial repair, but decreased significantly with debridement, suggesting increased superior humeral head migration. 7

Complications

Eleven studies reported complications, whereas 6 explicitly reported no complications. In the 6 partial repair studies, 4 patients acquired infections and 5 experienced pseudoparalysis reversion. In the 5 debridement studies, 1 patient developed a seroma, 1 developed a hematoma, 3 developed complex regional pain syndromes, 11 experienced pain exacerbations, and 2 developed adhesive capsulitis.

Six studies evaluating partial repair reported re‐tears, with prevalence ranging from 41.7% to 87.3%.

In 7 partial repair studies that reported conversion to RSA, a total of 11 patients were revised with RSA. 17 , 21 , 23 , 25 , 37 Where reported in 3 partial repair studies, the mean durations for conversion to RSA were 25, 33, and 36 months. Six studies evaluating debridement reported reversion to RSA, where 14 patients were revised with RSA; the mean durations for conversion to RSA were 6, 15, and 63 months.

Patient Satisfaction

Across 10 studies reporting patient satisfaction, both partial repair (5 studies) and debridement (5 studies) resulted in moderate to high satisfaction at final follow‐up. Reported satisfaction rates ranged from 51.6% to 100% following partial repair and 67% to 87% following debridement.

Risk of Bias

Cochrane RoB‐2 (RCTs)

The risk of bias among the 4 included RCTs was generally low, with minor variability across domains (Figure 2). Bias arising from the randomization process (D1), deviations from intended interventions (D2), missing outcome data (D3), and selection of the reported result (D5) were well addressed in all studies, each receiving a “Low risk” rating for these domains. However, bias in the measurement of outcomes (D4) showed slight inconsistency. One study was rated as having ‘Some concerns’ due to the potential for detection bias, as outcome assessors may not have been blinded to group allocation. 37 The remaining studies were rated as “Low risk” in all 5 domains. 7 , 22 , 30 Overall, the risk of bias ranged from low to moderate across the included trials, with 3 studies at low risk and one study with some concerns due to outcome assessment procedures.

Risk‐of‐bias assessment of randomized studies using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.

ROBINS‐I (Nonrandomized Studies)

Nonrandomized studies were assessed using the ROBINS‐I tool, which evaluates 7 domains of potential bias (Figure 3). The overall risk of bias across the 22 nonrandomized studies was predominantly rated as “serious.” The most prominent concern was bias due to confounding (D1), rated “Serious” in 18 of the 22 studies. This domain was particularly compromised by the large number of case series, which by design lacked control groups and did not adjust for baseline characteristics. Without control groups, it was not possible to isolate the effects of the intervention, making confounding a substantial threat to internal validity. Even in the few cohort studies, statistical adjustment for confounders was either absent or insufficiently detailed. Bias in selection of participants (D2) was also frequently rated as “Serious” or “Moderate,” often due to nonconsecutive recruitment, poor reporting of inclusion criteria, or surgeon‐driven selection based on intraoperative judgment. This raised concerns about potential selection bias. In contrast, bias due to classification of interventions (D3) and bias due to deviations from intended interventions (D4) were generally rated as “Low” across studies, as surgical techniques were adequately described and no major deviations from intended treatment were identified. Bias due to missing data (D5) showed moderate variability. Several studies had incomplete reporting of follow‐up duration or patient attrition and rarely used imputation or sensitivity analyses. Bias in measurement of outcomes (D6) ranged from “Low” to “Moderate”; although validated outcome measures were commonly used, the lack of assessor blinding in most studies may have introduced detection bias. Bias in selection of the reported result (D7) was typically rated as “Low.” Overall, most nonrandomized studies in this review were subject to serious risk of bias, chiefly due to confounding and participant selection. The predominance of uncontrolled case series without comparator groups substantially limited causal inference. These limitations should be carefully considered when interpreting the clinical outcomes and drawing conclusions from this body of evidence.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review of MIRCTs, both arthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement resulted in significant improvements in pain and function, with low complication and conversion to RSA rates.

Studies evaluating arthroscopic partial repair also included concomitant debridement as part of tendon preparation. Interventions were therefore categorized according to the primary surgical intent: attempted restoration of rotator cuff continuity (partial repair) versus symptom‐directed tissue removal without cuff repair (debridement). This distinction was necessary given the variability in intraoperative techniques, which may limit the interpretability of outcome differences between interventions.

Pain outcomes were most assessed by VAS score, and functional outcomes were most assessed by the CM score. Both outcomes improved with partial repair and debridement, reaching MCID when reported in partial repair studies. Debridement studies did not report MCID for pain or function, limiting conclusions regarding clinical importance of the data and comparison to partial repair.

Pertaining to sex differences, when reported, males showed improved outcomes following partial repair or arthroscopic debridement compared with females; this may reflect the sex‐based difference in patient‐reported experiences. 42

In 1 study comparing partial repair and debridement, there was no statistically significant difference in CM scores. 23 In a more recent comparison study, partial repair showed significantly higher improvement in mean CM compared with debridement. 7 However, the comparative studies differed in mean follow‐up (45 vs. 14 months). Consistent with this, an exploratory mean‐difference meta‐analysis of postoperative CM score improvement across the 2 comparative studies showed substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 82.4%). In 3 studies evaluating partial repair, CM scores were reported at 12 months and final follow‐up. 17 , 32 , 36 These studies consistently showed a substantial improvement in CM scores within the first 12 months postoperatively, followed by a decrease in the relative CM score improvement at subsequent follow‐up. This trend aligns with the findings of a systematic review and meta‐analysis by Khatri et al. (2018), which showed that with rotator cuff repair, most functional improvement occurs within the first year. 43

Similar improvements in pain and functional outcomes following partial repair or debridement for MIRCTs were reported in a previous systematic review by Hyde et al. 14 However, that review did not specifically stratify outcomes for older patients or investigate retear rates.

Retears were frequent after partial repair but often did not impact functional outcomes; included studies found no significant difference in CM and UCLA scores between retear and no‐retear groups that underwent partial repair. 7 , 23 , 24 This suggests that meaningful functional improvement can occur despite structural failure.

Partial repair offers the theoretical advantage of restoring coronal and sagittal force couples. Even partial restoration of the cuff footprint has been proposed to allow functional compensation by deltoid and preserve shoulder kinematics. 12 Conversely, debridement is seen as a safe and valid option for low demand older patients with MIRCT, particularly in a “palliative” setting. 38 It removes inflamed tissue to improve pain and enhance motility. Also, debridement is associated with lower upfront procedural costs than partial repair. Based on NHS tariff–based analyses, the estimated cost per patient for debridement (£1240.55) is lower than that for partial repair (£4052.76). 44 , 45 , 46 Operative time follows a similar pattern, with debridement generally requiring shorter duration and less technical complexity than partial repair. 13 Compared with RSA, both arthroscopic procedures are less invasive and incur lower initial costs. 47 , 48

However, these estimates reflect initial procedural costs, not accounting for longer‐term cost‐effectiveness, including quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs), revision surgery, or subsequent conversion to RSA. Data evaluating quality‐adjusted life years following arthroscopic treatment of MIRCTs in older patients are limited, and none of the included studies performed a formal cost‐utility analysis. Consequently, economic considerations should be interpreted alongside treatment durability, likelihood of revision, and patient‐specific functional goals, rather than upfront cost alone.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Many included studies were case series rated as Level IV evidence and only 2 studies directly compared partial repair and debridement; the baseline characteristics of the 2 study populations were similar with respect to age and sex, but other baseline factors were sparsely reported, limiting comparison. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 Next, few studies stratified outcomes by tendon involvement, tear chronicity, Hamada grade, or degree of fatty infiltration. Also, the surgical techniques, follow‐up times, concomitant procedures, and rehabilitation protocols were heterogeneous and inconsistently reported, limiting comparisons. Additionally, outcome measures varied and were infrequently reported across multiple time points, precluding meta‐analysis.

Despite the benefits of partial repair and debridement, clinical uncertainty persists regarding the optimal management of MIRCTs in older patients. 53 Inconsistencies exist not only in the choice between surgical strategies but also in the specific techniques used.

CONCLUSIONS

Arthroscopic partial repair and arthroscopic debridement are both effective joint‐preserving options for managing MIRCTs in older patients, resulting in significant improvements in pain and function, with low complication rates.
