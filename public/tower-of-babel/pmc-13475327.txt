Male partner’s involvement in prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) of HIV and associated factors in Gofa Zone, Southern Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Amare Maru Endalew, Yebeltal Kassa, Efrata Girma Tufa, Worku Mimani Minuta, Abdulbasit Sherfa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMale partner involvement is recognised as an important strategy to boost prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) of HIV. However, in Ethiopia, data on male perspectives and the role of legal and service-level factors that influence involvement are limited.MethodWe conducted a community-based cross-sectional study in Gofa Zone from 17 June to 21 August 2021. Using stratified multistage random sampling, 596 male partners whose female partners were pregnant or had given birth within the previous 6 months were interviewed with a structured questionnaire. A 6-item index measured male involvement; we used bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with involvement (reported as adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CI).ResultMale involvement (score ≥4/6) was 32.2% (192/596; 95% CI 28.5 to 36.1). Higher education (secondary or above: AOR 2.9; 95% CI 1.35 to 6.35) and good PMTCT knowledge (AOR 5.51; 95% CI 3.11 to 9.77) were positively associated with involvement. Beliefs that pregnancy is solely a woman’s responsibility (AOR 0.43; 95% CI 0.24 to 0.76), perception that PMTCT clinics serve only women (AOR 0.37; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.65), long waiting times (AOR 0.34; 95% CI 0.19 to 0.61) and perceived absence of legal or policy support encouraging male involvement (AOR 0.31; 95% CI 0.17 to 0.57) were negatively associated with involvement.ConclusionMale involvement in PMTCT in this setting remains suboptimal. Interventions to increase knowledge, address sociocultural norms and make services more male-friendly may improve male involvement in HIV PMTCT services.Keywords: Infectious Disease Transmission, Vertical; HIV; Prevalence; Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Globally, maternal-to-child transmission (MTCT) of HIV accounts for the vast majority of new HIV infections among children aged 0–14 years.1 The condition remains an important public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programme is designed to reduce HIV transmission during pregnancy, delivery and breastfeeding, and male partner involvement is widely recognised as one of the strategies that may strengthen uptake of PMTCT services and improve maternal and child health outcomes.2,3

Male involvement in HIV PMTCT refers to the participation of pregnant women’s husbands in HIV counselling and testing (HCT) alongside their wives during antenatal care (ANC) visits to support PMTCT services.4,5 Husband participation in HIV PMTCT is essential, as it helps to reduce the risk of new HIV infections, decreases women’s fear of disclosing their HIV status, improves couples’ communication and joint decision-making regarding healthy sexual practices and family planning, and enhances uptake of PMTCT services and adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) among seropositive women.6,7

In developed countries, husbands are strongly encouraged to participate with their wives in HIV PMTCT services during ANC, alongside other MTCT prevention measures. As a result, vertical transmission of new HIV infections among children is approaching elimination.8 Studies conducted in Brazil and Thailand reported that 54% and 46% of men, respectively, participated in HCT for PMTCT together with their wives.9,10

In developing African countries, the benefits of male involvement in PMTCT services are comparable to those observed in developed settings.11,12 Nevertheless, the implementation of HIV PMTCT programmes has largely remained women-centred, with limited emphasis on engaging male partners. Consequently, many husbands remain insufficiently informed about their critical role in promoting and safeguarding both family health and their own well-being.13 Evidence revealed that the proportion of male participation in HIV PMTCT services alongside their wives ranged from 7% to 28.1%.14 As a result, the region continues to experience a substantial HIV burden, particularly with respect to new MTCT infections among children aged 0–14 years, who remain highly vulnerable to HIV acquisition.15

In Ethiopia, favourable conditions for male partner involvement in HIV PMTCT services, including participation in HIV HCT with their wives during pregnancy, childbirth and the postnatal period, still exist.16 However, evidence indicates that only 20%–53% of men participate in PMTCT services alongside their wives and use HCT services for HIV PMTCT.5,17–22 Several factors have been identified as barriers to male participation in PMTCT services, including limited awareness and knowledge of PMTCT, cultural perceptions that PMTCT is solely a woman’s responsibility, long waiting times at health facilities and inadequate space to accommodate male partners during service provision.5,17,18,23

Furthermore, in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area, evidence regarding men’s perspectives on involvement in PMTCT services remains limited. In addition, there is insufficient attention to important public health policy and implementation issues that may substantially influence and strengthen male involvement in PMTCT programmes. Therefore, this study aimed to assess male involvement in HIV PMTCT services and associated factors in the Gofa Zone, Southern Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Study setting and period

The research was carried out in the Gofa Zone, situated in the Southern Ethiopia Regional State of Ethiopia. Geographically, the zone is situated at 6°18′ N latitude and 36°53′ E longitude, with an elevation of 1395 m above sea level. According to zonal administrative data, the overall population of the zone was expected to be 658 005, with 322 423 men and 335 582 women. Among the female population, 153 315 were of reproductive age (15–49 years), with an estimated annual birth rate of 5305. PMTCT/ANC service coverage in the zone was reported to be 49%.24 The study was conducted from 17 June 2021 to 21 August 2021.

Study design and study population

A community-based cross-sectional study was performed among male partners living in the Gofa Zone, whose female partners were either pregnant or had delivered during the 6 months prior to the survey and had attended at least one ANC visit. The study participants were selected from randomly chosen kebeles.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using Epi Info V.7.2.4.0 statistical software, incorporating a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.5 and a proportion of 52.1% for male participation in HIV PMTCT services, as derived from a prior study conducted in Bishoftu, central Ethiopia.25 The total sample size of 633 individuals was calculated after accounting for a 10% non-response rate.

Sampling technique and procedure

A stratified multistage sampling technique was employed to select the study participants. From the seven rural woredas and two town administrations in the Gofa Zone, two rural woredas (Geze Gofa and Demba Gofa) and one town administration were selected using a simple random sampling technique. The study area was further stratified into urban and rural settings. Subsequently, one urban kebele from Bulki Town, four rural kebeles from Geze Gofa Woreda and five rural kebeles from Demba Gofa Woreda were selected randomly.

The sample size was proportionally allocated to each selected kebele based on the number of eligible households. Lists of eligible male partners were obtained from family folders and registration books maintained by health extension workers, and a sampling frame was prepared using household identification numbers and husbands’ names. Finally, study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique (figure 1).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Male partners whose female partners were pregnant or had given birth within the previous 6 months, had attended at least one ANC visit and resided in the randomly selected kebeles were included in the study.

The score for each participant ranged from 0 (no participation) to 6 (participation in all six PMTCT measurement variables). Respondents who answered with a total score of 0–3 were considered no participation in PMTCT and 4–6 were considered participating in PMTCT.2,17,21,26

Male partners are men who have a sexual relationship with a woman who is pregnant or giving birth to him.20

Knowledge of PMTCT for HIV

Comprehensive knowledge of PMTCT was assessed using 10 structured knowledge-related items adapted from previously published similar studies. The items addressed awareness of PMTCT services, modes of MTCT of HIV and methods for the prevention of HIV transmission. Each correct response was assigned a score of one, whereas incorrect responses were assigned a score of zero. The scores for all items were summed to generate a composite knowledge score, which was subsequently converted into a percentage. Respondents who scored more than 60% on the knowledge assessment items were categorised as having good knowledge of PMTCT, whereas those who scored 60% or below were categorised as having poor knowledge of PMTCT.2,17

Household wealth index

The household wealth index was used as a composite indicator of the cumulative living standard of households. It was constructed using household asset variables adapted from the 2019 Demographic and Health Survey and modified to fit the local context. The indicators included ownership of assets such as a television, bicycle or car; housing characteristics, including roofing, wall and flooring materials; livestock ownership; and farmland ownership. Principal component analysis was conducted using SPSS to generate household wealth scores. Based on these scores, households were categorised into five wealth quintiles: first quintile (lowest), second quintile, third quintile (middle), fourth quintile and fifth quintile (highest).27

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with male partners at their households using a pretested structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published similar studies.5,18,20 Initially prepared in English, the questionnaire was translated into the local Goffa language by a language expert and then back-translated into English by an English language expert to ensure consistency and accuracy. The questionnaire consisted of six sections: male involvement in PMTCT of HIV, sociodemographic characteristics, individual-related factors, sociocultural factors, healthcare system-related factors and public health law-related factors. Data were collected from male partners whose female partners were either pregnant or had given birth within the previous 6 months and had attended at least one ANC visit. For respondents who were unavailable during the initial household visit, at least three repeat visits were made before considering them non-respondents.

Data quality control

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data collection process, data collectors and supervisors received 1 day of training prior to the commencement of data collection. The training focused on the objectives of the study, the contents of the questionnaire, data collection procedures and ethical considerations. Throughout the data collection period, supervisors and investigators regularly reviewed the completed questionnaires for completeness, accuracy and consistency. Appropriate feedback was provided to the data collectors to maintain data quality.

Data processing and analysis procedures

The data were checked for completeness, coded, entered into Epi-Data V.3.1, and exported to SPSS V.21 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the study variables, and bi-variable logistic regression was performed to identify candidate variables. Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all VIF values were below the accepted threshold. Variables with a p value of less than 0.25 in bi-variable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs were reported, and statistical significance was declared at p<0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 596 respondents participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 94.2%. The mean age of the participants was 31.05 years (SD±6.87 years). The majority of respondents (93.3%) were rural residents. Approximately half of the participants (51.2%) had attained at least elementary-level education. In terms of occupation, farmers accounted for the largest proportion of respondents (23.8%), followed by government employees (19.8%). The distribution of household wealth status was relatively even across quintiles, with 22.7% of participants belonging to the middle (third) wealth quintile (table 1).

Knowledge of male partners on HIV PMTCT services

Most respondents were aware that HIV can be transmitted from mother to child (78.7%) and recognised the preventive role of ART for mothers and infants (72.3%). However, knowledge of specific PMTCT preventive practices was limited, with only 31.1% correctly identifying caesarean delivery and exclusive breastfeeding as preventive measures. Nearly half of the respondents (49.2%) had heard about PMTCT services, mainly through their female partners (49.7%) and health professionals (33.0%). Overall, 36.2% of respondents demonstrated good knowledge of PMTCT services, while 63.8% had poor knowledge (online supplemental table 1, table 2).

Sociocultural and healthcare service factors

Sociocultural barriers toward male involvement in PMTCT services were common. About 28% of respondents considered pregnancy-related issues to be solely women’s responsibilities, and 29% believed PMTCT clinics were intended only for women. Although most respondents had access to nearby health facilities (90%), only 37% had attended PMTCT clinics with their spouses. Long waiting times (75%) were the most commonly reported barrier. In addition, only 17% had received direct invitations from health workers, and 15% had encountered PMTCT promotional messages through mass media (table 2).

Public health law factors influence male involvement in PMTCT services

Most respondents (88%) were unaware of legal provisions promoting male involvement in PMTCT programmes. About one-quarter (24.7%) believed that the absence of legal enforcement reduced male participation in PMTCT/ANC services, while 42.8% reported that lack of formal invitations from health professionals limited male involvement (table 2).

Male partner’s involvement in PMTCT of HIV services

Nearly one-third of respondents (32.2%) answered ‘yes’ to four or more PMTCT-related questions and were classified as involved in PMTCT services. Only 36.1% were aware of their wives’ PMTCT appointments. More than half of the respondents (57%) reported discussing HCT with their wives during pregnancy; however, only 37.1% had accompanied their wives to a PMTCT clinic, and 32.4% had jointly undergone HCT at the clinic. Although counselling was provided by health professionals, less than half of the respondents (43.3%) expressed willingness to use condoms during their wives’ pregnancy (table 3).

Factors associated with male involvement in the PMTCT service

Age, educational status, household wealth index, PMTCT knowledge, belief that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility, perception that PMTCT clinics serve only women, belief that pregnant women can be tested for HIV without husband permission, long waiting time and perceived legal or policy support for male involvement were associated with the dependent variable in the bi-variable binary logistic regression analysis at p<0.25. However, in the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, educational status, PMTCT knowledge, belief that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility, perception that PMTCT clinics serve only women, long waiting time and perceived legal or policy support for male involvement remained statistically significantly associated with male involvement in PMTCT at p<0.05. Although other studies have reported a significant association with household wealth index, this variable was not significantly associated in the present study. This may be due to differences in study settings and the relatively similar wealth distribution among participants.

Male partners with primary education and secondary education or higher had higher odds of involvement in PMTCT than those who could not read and write, with adjusted ORs of 2.3 (95% CI 1.10 to 5.04) and 2.9 (95% CI 1.35 to 6.35), respectively. Participants with good PMTCT knowledge also had higher odds of involvement in PMTCT (AOR=5.51; 95% CI 3.11 to 9.77). In contrast, men who believed that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility and that PMTCT clinics serve only women had lower odds of participating in PMTCT services (AOR=0.43; 95% CI 0.24 to 0.76 and AOR=0.37; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.65, respectively). Similarly, those who perceived long waiting times and limited policy support for male involvement had lower odds of being involved in PMTCT services (AOR=0.34; 95% CI 0.19 to 0.61 and AOR=0.31; 95% CI 0.17 to 0.57, respectively) (table 4).

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the level of male partners’ involvement in PMTCT of HIV services during ANC and to identify factors associated with such involvement in Gofa Zone, Southern Ethiopia. The findings showed that only about one-third of male partners were involved in PMTCT services. Educational status and PMTCT knowledge were positively associated with involvement, whereas sociocultural factors, such as the belief that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility and health service-related factors, such as long waiting times and perceived lack of policy support for male involvement, were negatively associated with involvement.

In this study, approximately 32.2% (95% CI 28.5% to 36.1%) of male partners were involved in PMTCT of HIV services. This finding is comparable to reports from Lemo District in the Hadiya Zone (30.9%),18 Hawassa 29.8%28 and Eastern Tigray (33.1%).29 However, the level of involvement observed in the current study was higher than findings reported from Georgia (12%),30 Tanzania (24.7%),31 Addis Ababa (28.1%),17 North West Ethiopia (26.1%),2 Gondar Town (20.9%),32 Debre Tabor Town (21.2%)20 and Fentale district in eastern Ethiopia (14%).33 Conversely, the finding was lower than studies conducted in Brazil (54%),9 Thailand (46%),10 Malawi (64.1%),34 South Africa (44.1%)35 and several Ethiopian settings, including Debre Markos Town (52.4%),36 Bishoftu (52.1%),25 Ambo Town (42.5%),19 Arba Minch Town and Zuria Woreda (53%)21 and Delanta district in south Wolo (53.7%).37

The observed variations across studies may be attributed to differences in study design, study setting, sociodemographic characteristics, level of urbanisation, accessibility of healthcare services, availability of health information and exposure to mass media campaigns promoting male involvement in maternal and child health services.

In this study, male partners’ involvement in PMTCT services was associated with educational attainment, with higher levels of education associated with greater participation. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa,38 Namibia39 and Ethiopia, including North West Ethiopia,2 Lemo district in the Hadiya zone18 and Gondar Town.32 The result may be explained by the fact that more educated male partners are likely to have greater exposure to health information, including information about PMTCT services, and may better understand the benefits of participating in HIV prevention and mother-to-child transmission services.

Higher PMTCT knowledge among male partners was associated with greater, fivefold, participation in PMTCT services with their spouses. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Tanzania,31 Kenya23 and Ethiopia, including Debre Markos Town,36 Ambo Town,19 North West Ethiopia,2 Arba Minch Town and Zuria Woreda.21 A possible explanation is that men with better PMTCT knowledge are more aware of HIV transmission and prevention, the main components of PMTCT, and the benefits of discussing HCT with their wives during pregnancy.

Perceived long waiting times at PMTCT HIV clinics were associated with lower male partner participation. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa,38 Kenya,23 Namibia39 and Debre Tabor Town, Ethiopia.20 A possible explanation is that long waiting times, competing work responsibilities and women-centred clinic environments may discourage male partners from attending PMTCT services. From a gender-power perspective, PMTCT clinics are often socially perceived as spaces primarily intended for women, which may limit men’s willingness to participate in maternal health services. In addition, unfriendly clinic settings, lack of privacy and limited male-inclusive service organisation may further reduce male involvement. Similar barriers have been reported in previous PMTCT studies.12,40

Concerning sociocultural influences, beliefs that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility and that PMTCT clinics serve women only were associated with lower male partner participation in PMTCT services. This finding is consistent with the review reported for Africa14 and Sub-Saharan Africa,38 as well as studies conducted in Addis Ababa17 and Goba Town,5 Ethiopia. These results suggest that sociocultural barriers continue to limit male partners’ participation in HIV PMTCT services and that efforts to improve involvement remain constrained by deeply rooted cultural norms and gender expectations.41,42 In addition, these findings may reflect gender-power relations that position pregnancy and HIV-related reproductive care as women’s responsibilities, thereby limiting men’s sense of ownership and participation in PMTCT services.12

Perceived limited legislative or policy support for male involvement in PMTCT HIV services was associated with lower male partner involvement. This finding could be comparable with a study conducted in Goba Town, Ethiopia, in which male partners who did not agree on legal enforcement of male partners to be tested were less likely to take HCT as a part of PMTCT of HIV.5 The result may reflect limited awareness of national and local policies that encourage male involvement, which may reduce men’s engagement in PMTCT services with their spouses. This interpretation is supported by Ethiopia’s national PMTCT guideline, which recommends promoting male involvement in PMTCT and delivering gender-sensitive services, as well as the national comprehensive HIV care guideline, which calls for ensuring male involvement in PMTCT services.43,44

Strengths and limitations of the study

Compared with earlier studies that relied on information obtained from women, this study provides more direct evidence on male involvement in PMTCT services because the data were collected directly from male partners through a community-based approach. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and does not allow establishment of temporal relationships between explanatory variables and male involvement in PMTCT services. Second, the use of self-reported data may have introduced social desirability and recall biases. In addition, some eligible male partners may have been missed during household visits due to work-related absence or other reasons, potentially leading to non-response bias. Furthermore, the operational definitions and predefined cut-off points used to measure knowledge and involvement may not have fully captured the complexity of these constructs. Finally, the absence of qualitative data limited a deeper understanding of the social and contextual factors influencing male involvement in PMTCT services.

Conclusion

The findings showed that male involvement in PMTCT services was low. Educational status and good knowledge of PMTCT services were positively associated with involvement, whereas socio-cultural beliefs, including the perception that pregnancy is primarily a woman’s responsibility, long waiting times at health facilities and perceived lack of policy support for male participation were negatively associated with involvement. Improving male involvement in PMTCT services requires coordinated efforts at both community and health-system levels. Community engagement and gender-transformative approaches are needed to address sociocultural barriers, while health-system reforms aimed at creating more male-friendly services, reducing waiting times, offering flexible consultation schedules and strengthening supportive healthcare provider practices may enhance male participation in PMTCT services.
