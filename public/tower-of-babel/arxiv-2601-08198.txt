Triplets Better Than Pairs: Towards Stable and Effective Self-Play Fine-Tuning for LLMs
Yibo Wang, Hai-Long Sun, Qing-Guo Chen, Zhao Xu, Weihua Luo, Kaifu Zhang, Lijun Zhang

Abstract
Recently, self-play fine-tuning ( 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{SPIN} ) has been proposed to adapt large language models to downstream applications with scarce expert-annotated data, by iteratively generating synthetic responses from the model itself. However, 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{SPIN} is designed to optimize the current reward advantages of annotated responses over synthetic responses at hand, which may gradually vanish during iterations, leading to unstable optimization . Moreover, the utilization of reference policy induces a misalignment issue between the reward formulation for training and the metric for generation. To address these limitations, we propose a novel T riplet-based S elf- P lay f I ne-tu N ing ( 𝚃 ​ - ​ 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN} ) method that integrates two key designs. First, beyond current advantages, 𝚃 ​ - ​ 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN} additionally incorporates historical advantages between iteratively generated responses and proto-synthetic responses produced by the initial policy. Even if the current advantages diminish, historical advantages remain effective, stabilizing the overall optimization. Second, 𝚃 ​ - ​ 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN} introduces the entropy constraint into the self-play framework, which is theoretically justified to support reference-free fine-tuning, eliminating the training-generation discrepancy. Empirical results on various tasks demonstrate not only the superior performance of 𝚃 ​ - ​ 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN} over 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{SPIN} , but also its stable evolution during iterations. Remarkably, compared to supervised fine-tuning, 𝚃 ​ - ​ 𝚂𝙿𝙸𝙽 \mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN} achieves comparable or even better performance with only 25 % 25\% samples, highlighting its effectiveness when faced with scarce annotated data.

Formally, it can be framed as a two-player game between a main player and an opponent player, each of which corresponds to an instance policy of the same LLM. In this game, the main player aims to distinguish expert-annotated responses from generated ones, while the opponent player strives to produce high-quality synthetic responses that can deceive the main player.

Based on this framework,

introduce the seminal method named
\mathtt{SPIN}
, which is designed to optimize the relative reward advantages of annotated responses over generated ones, and employs the policy from previous iteration as a reference for reward computations. However, with the evolution of LLM, the relative advantage between two types of data gradually diminishes over time. When the relative advantage is eliminated, the optimization objective of
\mathtt{SPIN}
degenerates into a constant independent of the policy, allowing
any policy
to become an optimal solution and thus leading to
unstable optimization
. Moreover, the incorporation of reference policy in
\mathtt{SPIN}
causes a
misalignment
between the reward formulation used for training and the log likelihood that guides response generation. Empirical evidences in Figure
3
reveal that although annotated responses yield higher rewards than generated responses, their log likelihoods become lower than synthetic samples during iterations.
In other words, higher rewards do not necessarily lead to higher generation priority.

Preliminary

In this section, we provide a brief overview of three fine-tuning strategies (i.e., supervised fine-tuning, self-play fine-tuning, and our
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
) for LLMs. The comparisons are also illustrated in Figure
1
.

2.1
Supervised fine-tuning

A common strategy to adapt a pretrained model to downstream tasks is fine-tuning it on high-quality annotated data. Specifically, given a prompt
\mathbf{x}
and its corresponding annotated response
\mathbf{y}
, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) aims to align the model with
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. Formally, the goal of SFT is to learn a policy
\pi_{\textrm{SFT}}
by maximizing the log-likelihood of high-quality annotated data:

\pi_{\textrm{SFT}}=\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\right],

(1)

where
q(\cdot)
and
\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
denote the distributions of prompts and annotated responses, respectively. Mathematically, (
1
) is equivalent to the solution of minimizing the KL divergence between the target distribution
\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
and the policy
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
:

\pi_{\textrm{SFT}}=\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})||\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))\right].

(2)

It can be verified that the solution (
2
) exactly matches the target distribution of annotated responses, i.e.,
\pi_{\textrm{SFT}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})=\pi_{\text{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. Unfortunately, achieving the optimal policy typically demands extensive high-quality samples, resulting in high costs for data curation and annotation, and thereby limiting the practical applicability of supervised fine-tuning
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Tan et al. 2024
)
.

2.2
Self-play fine-tuning

Self-play fine-tuning has emerged as a promising way to address the scarcity of expert-annotated data when adapting LLMs to downstream tasks
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Yuan et al. 2024a
)
. Different from SFT that runs over a fixed annotated dataset, self-play fine-tuning employs both annotated samples and self-generated responses as inputs, with the latter continuously refined as the model evolves.

Specifically, at each iteration
t+1
, the model first generates a synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
for a given prompt
\mathbf{x}
by sampling from the policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. Then, the model utilizes the prompt
\mathbf{x}
and pairwise samples (i.e., the annotated response
\mathbf{y}
and the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
) to obtain the policy
\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}
. Let the reward for a response be formulated by the log ratio of the likelihood between the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the previous policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}

(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. Then, the objective of
\mathtt{SPIN}

(
Chen et al. 2024b
)
is to maximize the gap (referred to as the relative advantage) between the reward of annotated response
\mathbf{y}
and that of synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}=\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\left(\lambda\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\lambda\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\right)\right],\end{split}

(3)

where
\lambda>0
denotes a hyper-parameter,
\ell(\cdot)
is a convex and monotonically decreasing function, and the expectation operation is taken over the distributions
\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. In the following, we elaborate two major limitations of
\mathtt{SPIN}
:
unstable optimization
during iterations and
misalignment
between training and generation.

Unstable optimization.

The instability of
\mathtt{SPIN}
arises from the inherent structure of the objective in (
3
). To be precise, we consider the case where the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
becomes identical to the annotated response
\mathbf{y}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}
). In this case, the advantage of
\mathbf{y}
over
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
vanishes, causing the objective (
3
) to degenerate into a constant that is independent of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
. As a result,
any
policy
\pi_{\theta}
becomes optimal to (
3
), leading to unstable optimization or even performance degradation.

Misalignment between training and generation.

Given the reference policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
from the iteration
t
, the objective (
3
) is designed to optimize the reward gap between two types of responses, where the reward for a response
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
is defined as
r(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{y}})=\lambda\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{x})-\lambda\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{x})
. However, it is important to note that the formulation of
r(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{y}})
differs from the principle used for generation, i.e.,
\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{x})
. In other words, due to the incorporation of reference policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
, a higher reward, i.e.,
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})>r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
for two responses
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
, does not necessarily imply a higher generation probability, i.e.,
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})>\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
may not hold. This demonstration has been empirically verified by our experiments, as illustrated in Figure
3
.

3
Triplet-based self-play fine-tuning

In this section, we introduce our triplet-based self-play fine-tuning (
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
), a stable and reference-free method for fine-tuning LLMs. Building upon the self-play framework, our
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
involves two players: a main player and an opponent player, both of which are different policy instances from the same LLM. For clarity, we describe the update rules for two players separately.

The update rule for main player.

Recall that the goal of the main player is to distinguish among the annotated response
\mathbf{y}
, the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
generated from the previous policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
and the proto-synthetic response
\mathbf{y}_{0}
produced by the initial policy
\pi_{\theta_{0}}
. Conceptually, the triple responses are expected to satisfy the relation
\mathbf{y}\succeq\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\succeq\mathbf{y}_{0}
, where the operator
\succeq
indicates that the main player has greater preference for one response than in another. In other words,
\mathbf{y}_{0}
serves as an anchor that helps to evaluate how far the current policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
has progressed. This design is partially inspired by the schema-equilibration process in cognitive development, where knowledge builds upon and refines pre-existing cognitive anchors
(
Piaget 1952
)
. Formally, we are motivated by the integral probability metric (IPM)
(
Müller 1997
)
to establish the objective of the main player. In detail, let
c(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{y}})
denote the confidence with which the main player believes a response
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
is sampled from the real-world data distribution
\pi_{\text{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. Then, the goal of main player is to find a confidence function

\begin{split}c_{t+1}=\argmin_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\left(c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right)+\beta\ell\left(c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{0})\right)\right],\end{split}

(4)

where
\beta\geq 0
denotes a trade-off hyper-parameter, and
\mathcal{C}
denotes a function class that will be specified later, and
\ell(\cdot)
denotes a monotonically decreasing function, e.g., the logistic function
\ell(x)=-\log\sigma(x)
and the exponential function
\ell(x)=\exp(-x)
. The expectation operation here is taken over the distributions
\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{y}_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. Given the triple responses
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
, and
\mathbf{y}_{0}
, the objective in (
4
) consists of two terms:

•

The first term
\ell\left(c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right)
measures the gap between the real response
\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{\text{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
and the synthetic one
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
, reflecting the relative advantages of
\pi_{\text{data}}
over
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
;

•

The second term
\ell\left(c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{0})\right)
compares the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
to the proto-synthetic response
\mathbf{y}_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
, capturing historical advantages of
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
over
\pi_{\theta_{0}}
during iterations.

During the self-play process, the opponent player evolves over time, generating synthetic responses
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
that progressively approximate the real response
\mathbf{y}
. As a result, the current advantage, i.e., the first term in (
4
), gradually diminishes. In contrast, since
\mathbf{y}_{0}
remains fixed during iterations, the historical advantage, i.e., the second term in (
4
), remains meaningful even when the current advantage vanishes, ensuring that (
4
) does not degenerate to a constant independent of
\pi_{\theta}
.

The update rule for opponent player.

In this part, we suppose that the main player has learned a confidence function
c_{t+1}
capable of effectively distinguishing between
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
. Then, we proceed to elaborate on the update rule for the opponent player. Note that the goal of the opponent player is to generate a response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
that misleads the main player to the best of its ability. Therefore, with the given confidence function
c_{t+1}
, the opponent player aims to find a policy
\pi_{\hat{\theta}}
that maximizes the expected confidence score over synthetic responses
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
generated from itself:

\begin{split}\pi_{\hat{\theta}}=&\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right]+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))\right],\end{split}

(5)

where
\alpha>0
denotes the regularization parameter, and
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
denotes the entropy constraint. In (
5
), the first part reflects the confidence that how the main player believes that
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
is sampled from the real-world distribution
\pi_{data}
, and the second part serves to stabilize the optimization. It is worth noting that (
5
) has a closed-form solution, as stated below.

Proposition 1
.

Given a general confidence function
c_{t+1}
, the optimal policy of the opponent player in (
5
) takes the form of

\pi^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})=\frac{\exp(c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})/\alpha)}{\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})/\alpha)}

where
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the set that contains all possible responses for the prompt
\mathbf{x}
.

Note that due to the generality of
c_{t+1}
, the closed-form solution
\pi^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
may not be attainable in the policy space of LLM. To resolve this issue, we need to choose a specific form of the function class
\mathcal{C}
such that the closed-form solution becomes
tractable
. Specifically, we hope to find a policy
\pi_{\theta}
that satisfies
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})=\pi^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
with the confidence
c_{t+1}
learned from a certain class
\mathcal{C}
. To this end, according to Proposition
1
, we can obtain
\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})/\alpha)
, which implies
c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
. In other words, we can choose the function class

\mathcal{C}=\left\{\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})|\theta\in\Theta\right\}

(6)

where
\Theta
denote the parameter space of LLM, and optimize (
4
) to obtain the confidence
c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
where the policy
\pi_{\theta}
is exactly the optimal solution of (
5
). Therefore, we do not need to solve (
5
) explicitly. Moreover, since (
6
) depends solely on the policy
\pi_{\theta}
and does not rely on
any
reference policy, it inherently avoids the training-generation misalignment issue suffered by
\mathtt{SPIN}
.

Comparisons between
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
and
\mathtt{SPIN}
.

Both methods are designed to handle the limited available data when adapting LLMs to downstream tasks, but they fundamentally differ in (i) optimization principles, (ii) implicit reward formulation, and (iii) computational overhead, as detailed below:

•

\mathtt{SPIN}
only optimizes the current advantage between the annotated response
\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
and the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
in (
3
), which may result in the instability issue during iterations.
In contrast,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
optimizes not only the current advantage of
\mathbf{y}
over
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
, but also the historical advantage of
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
over the proto-synthetic response
\mathbf{y}_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
, ensuring a stable evolution and thereby mitigating the unstable optimization;

•

In
\mathtt{SPIN}
, the reward function for a response
\mathbf{z}
is formulated as
r_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=\lambda\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})-\lambda\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
. This formulation misaligns with the generation metric (i.e., the log-likelihood), resulting in potential suboptimal performance. By contrast, the reward function in
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
(also referred to as the confidence function under the self-play framework) is defined as
r_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})=\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
, which matches the generation metric and thus resolves the misalignment issue;

•

Compared to
\mathtt{SPIN}
,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
incurs only a marginal increase in computational and memory costs. Specifically,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
performs a one-time generation of
\mathbf{y}_{0}
at the beginning, which is not repeated in subsequent iterations. In practice, the costs of this generation is negligible compared to the iterative training process. Moreover, while
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
expands each training instance from a pair of
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
to a triplet of
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{0})
, the overall memory costs remains nearly unchanged, as it eliminates the need for a reference model, which is necessary in
\mathtt{SPIN}
. Consequently, the additional overhead of
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
is minimal compared to
\mathtt{SPIN}
.

Gradient analysis.

To mechanistically understand the impact of
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
on the training process, we examine the gradient of (
7
) with respect to the parameter
\theta
, as presented in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Define
u(\pi_{\theta})=\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
and
v(\pi_{\theta})=\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{0}|\mathbf{x})
. Then, given a monotonically decreasing function
\ell(x)
, the gradient of (
7
) with respect to
\theta
is

\begin{split}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}}(\theta)=\alpha\mathbb{E}\Big[&\ell^{\prime}(\alpha u(\pi_{\theta}))\cdot\left(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\right)\\
&+\beta\ell^{\prime}(\alpha v(\pi_{\theta}))\cdot\left(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{0}|\mathbf{x})\right)\Big]\end{split}

(8)

where
\ell^{\prime}(\cdot)
denotes the derivative of
\ell(\cdot)
, and the expectation is taken over the distributions
\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{data}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})
. With a specific choice of
\ell(x)
, we can derive more explicit forms of the gradient. Detailed derivation are provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Note that the function
\ell(x)
is monotonically decreasing, leading to a non-positive derivative
\ell^{\prime}(x)\leq 0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, during the training, the gradient in (
8
) tends to increase the likelihood of
\mathbf{y}
and decrease the likelihood of
\mathbf{y}_{0}
with weights corresponding to the current advantage
u(\pi_{\theta})
and the historical advantage
v(\pi_{\theta})
, respectively. For the synthetic response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
, the update direction is influenced by both current and historical advantages, with the latter weighted by hyperparameter
\beta
.

Aligning training rewards and generation log-likelihoods.

We then show that
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
is able to alleviate the misalignment issue suffered by
\mathtt{SPIN}
. We plot the training dynamics and generation statistics of
\mathtt{SPIN}
and
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
on Zephyr-7B at the iteration
1
in Figure
3
(a) and Figure
3
(b), respectively. We observe that in
\mathtt{SPIN}
, annotated responses yield higher rewards than synthetic responses, but their log-likelihoods are lower than those of synthetic responses, shown in the left and middle panels of Figure
3
(a). The reason lies in that the reward function of
\mathtt{SPIN}
incorporates both the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the reference policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
from the previous iteration, expressed as
r_{\mathtt{SPIN}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\lambda\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})-\lambda\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
for a response
\mathbf{z}
. This formulation differs from the generation principle that solely relies on
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})

3
(a). The results indicate that nearly half of the samples exhibit higher rewards but lower log-likelihoods. By contrast,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
effectively mitigates the mismatch issue by aligning the reward formulation for training and log-likelihood used for generation, i.e.,
r_{\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})
where
\alpha=1
in this experiment. Empirical evidence, shown in the left and middle panels of Figure
3
(b), demonstrates that both the rewards and log-likelihoods of real responses are consistently higher than those of generated responses. We also examine the generation statistic of two types of responses in terms of rewards and log-likelihoods, with similar trends observed at the right panel of Figure
3
(b).

. In contrast,

Reference-free reward function.

To address the training-generation misalignment issue in
\mathtt{SPIN}
,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
introduce an entropy constraint into the self-play framework as shown in (
5
), from which we theoretically deliver a reference-free reward function for
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
. To investigate its effectiveness, we compete our
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
with a variant, namely
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}_{\mathtt{ref}}
, that still optimizes with triplet inputs, but uses the reward function with reference policy. The loss function of
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}_{\mathtt{ref}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}_{\mathtt{ref}}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\left(\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\right)+\beta\ell\left(\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}-\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{0}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{y}_{0}|\mathbf{x})}\right)\right].

(9)

We report the average performances of
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
and
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}_{\mathtt{ref}}
on all tasks in Figure
4
(b). From the results, we observe that
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
demonstrates superior performance compared to
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}_{\mathtt{ref}}
over all iterations. This can be attributed to that using the reference-free reward function preserves the alignment between training and generation, which in turn facilitates performance improvement.

\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
involves two hyper-parameters: (i) the trade-off parameter
\beta
that balances the current and historical advantages, and (ii) the regularization coefficient
\alpha
that controls the entropy penalty. To investigate the robustness of these parameters, we conduct experiments at iteration
1
with varying
\alpha
and
\beta
. We select values for
\alpha
and
\beta
from the set
\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0\}
, and report the average performances over all

\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
is relatively insensitive to a wide range of
\alpha
and
\beta
with diverse values. Accordingly, we use

and

as the default values in our experiments.

5
Related work

Self-play fine-tuning for LLMs.

The self-play learning framework originates from TD-Gammon
(
Tesauro 1995
)
, and has produced profound impacts in various fields, such as multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
Lanctot et al. 2017
;
Muller et al. 2019
;
Bansal et al. 2018
)
and game-playing
(
Silver et al. 2017a
;
Silver et al. 2017b
;
Anthony et al. 2017
)
. The central idea of this framework is to train the model by playing against its previous version for progressive improvements. Recently, self-play fine-tuning has been introduced into the investigations for LLMs
(
Chen et al. 2024b
)
, which then leads to a series of subsequent works
(
Ren et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024b
;
Yuan et al. 2024b
;
Rosset et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Pang et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. For example,
Gao et al. 2024a
introduce the self-play mechanism into the recommendation systems to calibrate item-level predictions and suppress the bias issue, and
Yuan et al. 2024a
incorporate the idea of self-play into diffusion models, resulting in a novel diffusion-based method. Different from the above studies that apply self-play fine-tuning to specific domains of LLMs, our work follows the seminal study of
Chen et al. 2024b
, investigating the fundamental nature of self-play fine-tuning, and aims to resolve the unstable evolution and misalignment issues in the original method.

Data generation in LLMs.

Large language models are typically data-driven, heavily relying on the high-quality annotated data
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Tan et al. 2024
)
. However, the available data is often limited for LLM training, and the costs of human expert annotation are prohibitively expensive. For this reason, recent efforts have been made to employ advanced LLMs, such as GPT series
(
OpenAI 2023
;
OpenAI 2024
;
OpenAI 2025
)
, Gemini series
(
Google 2023
;
Google 2024
;
Google 2025
)
, and Claude series
(
Anthropic 2024a
;
Anthropic 2024b
;
Anthropic 2025
)
, to generate synthetic data for LLM fine-tuning
(
Josifoski et al. 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2023
;
Long et al. 2024
;
Qin et al. 2025
)
. It has been shown that in most domains, advanced expert LLMs are capable of generating high-quality data that can benefit others
(
Ubani et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Choi et al. 2024
;
Zhang 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
)
. Compared to these studies, our self-play fine-tuning method focuses on using data generated by the current policy model to train itself to achieve the progressive performance improvements, and offers an alternative to expert-LLM-based strategies.

6
Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we investigate self-play fine-tuning for LLMs and identify two issues of the existing method
\mathtt{SPIN}
:
unstable optimization
during iterations and
misalignment
between training and generation. To address these issues, we propose a novel self-play fine-tuning method named
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
with two key designs. First, our
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
simultaneously optimizes the current advantage of the annotated response
\mathbf{y}
over the newly generated response
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
, and the historical advantage of
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
over the proto-synthetic response
\mathbf{y}_{0}
. When current advantages vanish, historical advantages can still guide the fine-tuning process, avoiding the unstable optimization. Second,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
incorporates the entropy constraint into the self-play framework, which eliminates the need for a reference policy in the reward computations, thus aligning the reward formulation and the generation metric. Empirically, we conduct extensive experiments and examine the performances of
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
on a variety of tasks. Experimental results show that
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
maintains stable evolution and achieves better performance than its contenders, and outperforms supervised fine-tuning by leveraging only
25\%
annotated data.

Limitations and future work.

There are several promising directions for future research. First, at each iteration,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
needs to re-generate synthetic responses for all prompts, although some of them may no longer contribute to further evolutions. Consequently, a carefully designed filter strategy is necessary to choose valuable samples before generation. Second, in
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
, the expert-annotated data is fixed during iterations. But, in real-world applications, the target distribution may shift over time, and hence the fixed annotated samples may not be suitable for online fine-tuning. For this reason, self-play fine-tuning for non-stationary target distributions is another interesting direction, for which we believe that some advanced techniques from online learning may offer valuable insights
(
Hazan 2016
;
Orabona 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2024e
)
.

Appendix A
Mathematical derivations

In this section, we provide mathematical derivations for the propositions and theorems.

A.1
Proof of Proposition
1

In this part, we proceed to derive the optimal solution of the following objective:

\pi_{\hat{\theta}}=\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right]+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))\right].

(10)

under a general confidence function
c_{t+1}
with the entropy constraint
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
. The proof is inspired by
Rafailov et al. 2023
, converting (
10
) into a KL-divergence minimization problem. Specifically, by the definition of entropy, we have:

\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\right].

(11)

Combining (
10
) and (
11
), we have:

\begin{split}&\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right]+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))\right]\\
=&\argmax_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})-\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\right]\\
=&\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{\alpha}c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\right]\\
=&\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{x})}\exp(\frac{1}{\alpha}c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}))}-\log Z(\mathbf{x})\right].\end{split}

(12)

where
Z(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\frac{1}{\alpha}c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}))
denotes the partition function for normalization. Note that
Z(\mathbf{x})
is a function of the prompt
\mathbf{x}
, and independent of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
. We define

\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{x})}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),

which is a valid policy, since
\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for any
\mathbf{y}
and
\sum_{\mathbf{y}}\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=1
. Next, we can rewrite (
12
) as:

\begin{split}&\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}-\log Z(\mathbf{x})\right]\\
=&\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})||\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}))-\log Z(\mathbf{x})\right],\end{split}

from which we can observe that the optimal policy is exactly
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
. Therefore, the opponent policy is

\pi_{\hat{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{x})}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}c_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

In this part, we derive the gradient of (

\theta

\begin{split}u(\pi_{\theta})=&\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})\\

In Section
4.2
, we have reported the log-likelihood dynamics of two self-play methods at iteration
1
. In the following, we examine the trend of log-likelihood changes across different iterations. Specifically, we track the log-likelihoods of annotated samples
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
and synthetic samples
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
during policy training at iterations
0
,
1
and
2
, and calculate the gap between them, defined as
\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})
. The results are presented in Figure
7
. It is observed that at iteration
0
, for both methods, the log-likelihoods of annotated samples are higher than that of synthetic samples, resulting in positive gaps between them. However, at iterations
1
and
2
,
\mathtt{SPIN}
demonstrates an unexpected pattern: the log-likelihoods of annotated samples fall below those of synthetic samples, resulting in negative gaps. This reversal stems from the misalignment between the reward function employed in
\mathtt{SPIN}
and the log-likelihood. Specifically, the objective function (
3
) of
\mathtt{SPIN}
maximizes the reward gap between annotated and synthetic samples. Due to the incorporation of a reference policy, the reward is no longer equivalent to the log-likelihood. As a result, optimizing for high reward during training does not guarantee an increase in log-likelihood. Importantly, since log-likelihood underlies the generation behavior of LLM and reflects its preference for certain responses, it is undesirable that
\mathtt{SPIN}
assigns higher log-likelihoods to synthetic samples than to annotated ones. In contrast,
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
exhibits consistent behavior in terms of log-likelihood: across all iterations, the log-likelihoods of annotated samples remain higher than those of synthetic samples. This desirable property stems from
\mathtt{T}\mbox{-}\mathtt{SPIN}
’s elimination of the reference policy in its reward function, which ensures alignment between the reward and log-likelihood.
