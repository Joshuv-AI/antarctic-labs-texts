Monitoring of Static Fairness
Thomas A. Henzinger, Mahyar Karimi, Konstantin Kueffner, Kaushik Mallik

Abstract
Machine-learned systems are in widespread use for making decisions about humans, and it is important that they are fair , i.e., not biased against individuals based on sensitive attributes. We present a general framework of runtime verification of algorithmic fairness for systems whose models are unknown, but are assumed to have a Markov chain structure, with or without full observation of the state space. We introduce a specification language that can model many common algorithmic fairness properties, such as demographic parity, equal opportunity, and social burden. We build monitors that observe a long sequence of events as generated by a given system, and output, after each observation, a quantitative estimate of how fair or biased the system was on that run until that point in time. The estimate is proven to be correct modulo a variable error bound and a given confidence level, where the error bound gets tighter as the observed sequence gets longer. We present two categories of monitoring algorithms, namely ones with a uniform error bound across all time points, and ones with weaker non-uniform, pointwise error bounds at different time points. Our monitoring algorithms use statistical tools that are adapted to suit the dynamic requirements of monitoring and the special needs of the fairness specifications. Using a prototype implementation, we show how we can monitor if a bank is fair in giving loans to applicants from different social backgrounds, and if a college is fair in admitting students while maintaining a reasonable financial burden on the society. In these experiments, our monitors took less than a millisecond to update their verdicts after each observation.



1
Introduction

Runtime verification complements traditional static verification techniques, by offering lightweight solutions for checking properties based on a single, possibly long execution trace of a given system
[
8
]
.
We present new runtime verification techniques for the problem of bias detection in decision-making software.
The use of software for making critical decisions about humans is a growing trend; example areas include judiciary
[
14
,
20
]
, policing
[
23
,
53
]
, banking
[
52
]
, etc.
It is important that these software systems are unbiased towards the protected attributes of humans, like gender, ethnicity, etc.
However, they have often shown biases in their decisions in the past
[
20
,
50
,
59
,
62
,
63
]
.
While there are many approaches for mitigating biases before deployment
[
20
,
50
,
59
,
62
,
63
]
, recent runtime verification approaches
[
3
,
34
]
offer a new complementary tool to oversee
algorithmic fairness
in AI and machine-learned decision makers during deployment.

To verify algorithmic fairness at runtime, the given decision-maker is treated as a
generator
of events with an unknown model.
The goal is to algorithmically design lightweight but rigorous
runtime monitors
against quantitative formal specifications.
The monitors observe a long stream of events and, after each observation, output a quantitative, statistically sound estimate of how fair or biased the generator was until that point in time.
While the existing approaches
[
3
,
34
]
considered only static decision-makers whose inputs and outputs are fully observable, we present monitors for when the monitored stochastic processes are Markov chains whose state spaces are either fully or partially observable.

Monitoring algorithmic fairness involves on-the-fly statistical estimations, a feature that has not been well-explored in the traditional runtime verification literature.
As far as the algorithmic fairness literature is concerned, the existing works are mostly
model-based
, and either minimize decision biases of machine-learned systems at
design-time
(i.e., pre-processing)
[
44
,
71
,
11
,
70
]
, or verify their absence at
inspection-time
(i.e., post-processing)
[
33
]
.
In contrast, we verify algorithmic fairness at
runtime
, and do not require an explicit model of the generator.
On one hand, the model-independence makes the monitors trustworthy, and on the other hand, it complements the existing model-based static analyses and design techniques, which are often insufficient due to partially unknown or imprecise models of systems in real-world environments.

We first present monitoring algorithms for when the monitored systems are modeled using partially observed Markov chains (POMC) with unknown transition probabilities, and the properties are specified as the so-called bounded specification expressions (BSE) that are able to express many common algorithmic fairness properties from the literature, like demographic parity
[
21
]
, equal opportunity
[
33
]
, and disparate impact
[
26
]
.
The difficulty of monitoring BSEs on POMCs comes from the fact that a random
observation
sequence that is visible to the monitor may not follow a Markovian pattern, even though the underlying
state
sequence is Markovian.
We argue that this makes it impossible to establish if the given BSE is fulfilled or violated based on one single observation trace of the system.
To circumvent this, we propose to assume that the POMC starts in the stationary distribution, which in turn guarantees a certain uniformity in how the observations follow each other.
We argue that the stationarity assumption is fulfilled whenever the system has been running for a long time, which is suitable for long term monitoring of fairness properties.
With the help of a few additional standard assumptions on the POMC, like aperiodicity and the knowledge of a bound on the mixing time, we can compute PAC estimates on the limiting value of the given BSE over the distribution of all runs of the system from a single monitored observation sequence.

While POMC and BSE provide us a very general and unifying framework for building the foundations of monitors, we also consider the special case when the POMCs have fully observable state spaces, i.e., they are Markov chains (MC), and the BSE specifications are restricted to a special fragment called PSE that is able to only express arithmetics over the unknown transition probabilities.
For this special class of problems, we present monitors that require significantly less assumptions on the monitored system and yet are significantly more accurate.

For both classes of problems, POMCs with BSEs and MCs with PSEs, the basic schemes of the monitoring algorithms are similar.
In both cases, the monitor observes one long random execution sequence from the generator, and after each new observation outputs an updated PAC-style estimate of the value of the given specification.
Each PAC estimate at time
t
consists of two parts, namely a real interval
[l_{t},u_{t}]
and a probability value
p_{t}\in[0,1]
.
We offer two separate families of monitoring algorithms, and they cover two different correctness interpretation of the monitors’ outputs
[l_{t},u_{t}]
and
p_{t}
:
First, the pointwise sound monitors guarantee that at every
t
, the true fairness value lies within
[l_{t},u_{t}]
with probability at least
p_{t}
.
Second, the uniformly sound monitors guarantee that with probability at least
p_{t}
, for every
t
—including the future time points—the true fairness value lies within
[l_{t},u_{t}]
.
It is easy to show that, uniformly sound monitors are more conservative and their intervals would always contain the intervals generated by the pointwise sound monitors.
In other words, every uniformly sound monitor is also a pointwise sound monitor, but not the other way round.
Our monitoring algorithms combine statistical tools to design pointwise sound and uniformly sound monitors for POMCs with BSEs and MCs with PSEs.

In short, our contributions are as follows:

1.

Monitoring POMCs with BSEs:

We propose a general framework for monitoring algorithmic fairness of unknown AI decision-makers with partially observable state space.

2.

Monitoring MCs with PSEs:

We also consider the special case when the underlying state space of the monitored system is fully observable.
This special case is practically relevant and provides tighter probabilistic correctness bounds.

3.

Pointwise sound monitoring algorithms:

We formalize pointwise soundness as a kind of (probabilistic) correctness requirement of monitors, where the monitors’ outputs must be correct at each step with high probability.

4.

Uniformly sound monitoring algorithms:

In addition to pointwise soundness, we formalize uniform soundness as a second type of correctness requirement, where the monitors’ outputs must be correct at all time on the entire trace with high probability.

5.

Empirical evaluations:

We empirically demonstrate the usefulness of our monitors on a canonical examples from the fairness literature such as the bank loan and the admission example from D’Amour
[
17
]
.

This paper unifies our previous papers that cover Item 1 and Item 2 but exclusively for pointwise sound monitors (Item 3).
We additionally extend our previous results to the novel class of uniformly sound monitors (Item 4).
Our uniformly sound monitors utilise the powerful martingale-based tools developed by Howard
[
39
]
to compute tight interval estimates.
Furthermore, all the proofs that were omitted from our previous papers are now included.

1.1
Motivating Examples

We first present two real-world examples from the algorithmic fairness literature to motivate the problem; these examples will later be used to illustrate the technical developments.

The lending problem
[
52
]
:

Suppose a bank lends money to individuals based on certain attributes, like credit score, age group, etc.
The bank wants to maximize profit by lending money to only those who will repay the loan in time—called the “true individuals.”
There is a sensitive attribute (e.g., ethnicity) classifying the population into two groups
g
and
\overline{g}
.
The bank will be considered fair (in lending money) if its lending policy is independent of an individual’s membership in
g
or
\overline{g}
.
Several
group fairness
metrics from the literature are relevant in this context.

Disparate impact

[
26
]
quantifies the
ratio
of the probability of an individual from
g
getting the loan to the probability of an individual from
\overline{g}
getting the loan, which should be close to
1
for the bank to be considered fair.

Demographic parity

[
21
]
quantifies the
difference
between the probability of an individual from
g
getting the loan and the probability of an individual from
\overline{g}
getting the loan, which should be close to
0
for the bank to be considered fair.

Equal opportunity

[
33
]
quantifies the
difference
between the probability of a
true
individual from
g
getting the loan and the probability of a
true
individual from
\overline{g}
getting the loan, which should be close to
0
for the bank to be considered fair.
A discussion on the relative merit of various different algorithmic fairness notions is out of scope of this paper, but can be found in the literature
[
67
,
46
,
16
,
22
]
.
We show how we can monitor whether a given group fairness criteria is fulfilled by the bank, by observing a sequence of lending decisions.

The college admission problem
[
57
]
:

Consider a college that announces a cutoff of grades for admitting students through an entrance examination.
Based on the merit, every truly qualified student belongs to group
g
, and the rest to group
\overline{g}
.
Knowing the cutoff, every student can choose to invest a sum of money—proportional to the gap between the cutoff and their true merit—to be able to reach the cutoff, e.g., by taking private tuition classes.
On the other hand, the college’s utility is in minimizing admission of students from
\overline{g}
, which can be accomplished by raising the cutoff to a level that is too expensive to be achieved by the students from
\overline{g}
and yet easy to be achieved by the students from
g
.
The
social burden
associated to the college’s cutoff choice is the expected expense of every student from
g
, which should be close to
0
for the college to be considered fair (towards the society).
We show how we can monitor the social burden, by observing a sequence of investment decisions made by the students from
g
.

1.2
Related Work

There has been a plethora of work on algorithmic fairness from the machine learning standpoint
[
55
,
21
,
33
,
49
,
45
,
64
,
10
,
68
,
12
,
71
,
40
,
47
]
.
In general, these works improve algorithmic fairness through de-biasing the training dataset (pre-processing), or through incentivizing the learning algorithm to make fair decisions (in-processing), or through eliminating biases from the output of the machine-learned model (post-processing).
All of these are interventions in the design of the system, whereas our monitors treat the system as already deployed.

Recently, formal methods-inspired techniques have been used to guarantee algorithmic fairness through the verification of a learned model
[
2
,
9
,
66
,
31
,
56
]
, and enforcement of robustness
[
42
,
6
,
30
]
.
All of these works verify or enforce algorithmic fairness
statically
on all runs of the system with high probability.
This requires certain knowledge about the system model, which may not be always available.
Our runtime monitor dynamically verifies whether the current run of an opaque system is fair.

Our monitors are closely related to the seminal work of Albarghouthi et al.
[
3
]
, where the authors build a programming framework that allows runtime monitoring of algorithmic fairness properties on programs.
Their monitor evaluates the algorithmic fairness of repeated “single-shot” decisions made by functions on a sequence of samples drawn from an underlying unknown but fixed distribution, which is a special case of our more general Markov chain model of the generator.
Moreover, we argue and empirically show in Sec.
6.4
that our approach produces significantly tighter statistical estimates than their approach on most PSEs.
On the flip side, their specification language is more expressive, e.g., it is capable of specifying individual fairness criteria
[
21
]
.
We only consider group fairness, and monitors for individual fairness will independently appear in separate works
[
32
]
.
Also, they allow logical operators (like boolean connectives) in their specification language.
However, we obtain tighter statistical estimates for the core arithmetic part of algorithmic fairness properties (through PSEs), and point out that we can deal with logical operators just like they do in a straightforward manner.

Shortly after our first paper on pointwise sound monitors for MCs with PSEs
[
35
]
, we published a separate work for monitoring long-run fairness in sequential decision making problems, where the feature distribution of the population may dynamically change due to the actions of the individuals
[
34
]
.
Although this other work generalizes our current paper in some aspects (support for dynamic changes in the model), it only allows sequential decision making models (instead of Markov chains), and only offers pointwise sound monitors and lacks support for uniformly sound monitors.
We also developed runtime active intervention tools for
enforcing fairness
by altering decisions of the AI agent if needed
[
13
]
.
In contrast, the monitors presented in the current paper are passive entities that can only raise warnings when fairness is violated.
A limitation of our fairness enforcement tools is that they are applicable to sequential decision-makers, and not probabilistic generators such as POMCs and MCs.
Furthermore, they require precise knowledge of the probability distributions over the inputs and outputs of the sequential decision-making agent.

Traditional runtime verification techniques support mainly temporal properties and employ finite automata-based monitors

[
65
,
43
,
25
,
54
,
19
,
7
,
5
]
.
In contrast, runtime verification of algorithmic fairness requires checking statistical properties, which is beyond the limit of what automata-based monitors can accomplish.
Although there are some works on quantitative runtime verification using richer types of monitors (with counters/registers like us)
[
29
,
37
,
60
,
38
]
, the considered specifications usually do not extend to statistical properties such as algorithmic fairness.
One exception is the work by Ferrère et al.
[
27
]
, which monitors certain statistical properties, like mode and median of a given sequence of events.
Firstly, they do not consider algorithmic fairness properties.
Secondly, their monitors’ outputs are correct only as the length of the observed sequence approaches infinity (asymptotic guarantee), whereas our monitors’ outputs are
always
correct with high confidence (finite-sample guarantee), and the precision gets better for longer sequences.

Although our work uses similar tools as used in statistical verification
[
4
,
69
,
15
,
18
,
1
]
, the goals are different.
In traditional statistical verification, the system’s runs are chosen probabilistically, and it is verified if any run of the system satisfies a boolean property with a certain probability.
For us, the run is given as input to the monitor, and it is this run that is verified against a quantitative algorithmic fairness property with statistical error bounds.
To the best of our knowledge, existing works on statistical verification do not consider algorithmic fairness properties.

2.1
Basic Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}^{+}
be the set of natural numbers excluding zero, and
\mathbb{R}
be the set of real numbers. For a given subset
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we define
I(R)\coloneqq\{[a,b]\subseteq R\mid a,b\in R:a<b\}
as the set of all real intervals over
R
. Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a<b
.
We define
[a;b]\coloneqq\{a,a+1,\dots b\}
as the interval from
a
to
b
over the natural numbers and as a shorthand we will use
[b]\coloneqq[1;b]
.

Sequences:

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a given alphabet.
A word of length
n
over
\mathcal{W}
is a sequence of characters
\vec{w}\coloneqq(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathcal{W}^{n}
.
We will write
|\vec{w}|
to denote the length of the word
\vec{w}
, where the length can be either finite or infinite.
We denote the set of all finite and infinite words over
\mathcal{W}
as
\mathcal{W}^{*}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\omega}
, respectively, and we denote the set of all words as
\mathcal{W}^{\infty}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}^{*}\cup\mathcal{W}^{\omega}
.
For every
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{\infty}
and every
m<n<|\vec{w}|
, we will write
\vec{w}_{m:n}
to denote the infix
w_{m}\ldots w_{n}
of
\vec{w}
from
m
to
n
, and will use the shorthand
\vec{w}_{n}
in place of
\vec{w}_{1:n}
, called the prefix of length
n
, where
\vec{w}_{0}
will denote the empty word.
Let
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{U}
be a given pair of alphabets and
f\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{U}
be a mapping.
We will lift
f
to map every word
\vec{w}
over
\mathcal{W}
to an equally long word
u
over
\mathcal{U}
by writing
u=f(w_{1})f(w_{2})\ldots
.

2.2
Markov Chains, with full or partial state observation

We use partially observed Markov chains (POMC) as sequential randomized generators of events.

Syntax.

A POMC is a tuple
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda,\mathcal{O},\ell\right)
, where

\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{N}^{+}
is a countable set of states,

M
is a stochastic matrix of dimension
|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|
, called the
transition probability matrix
,

\lambda
is a probability distribution over
\mathcal{S}
representing the
initial state distribution
,

\mathcal{O}
is a countable set of
observation labels
, and

\ell\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{O}
is called the
labeling function
mapping every state to an observation.

Semantics.

Every POMC
\mathcal{H}
induces a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)
over the generated state and observation sequences.
For every finite state sequence
\vec{s}=s_{1}s_{2}\ldots s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, the probability that
\vec{s}
is generated by
\mathcal{H}
is given by
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\vec{s})=\lambda_{s_{1}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}M_{s_{i}s_{i+1}}
.
Every finite state sequence
\vec{s}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
for which
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\vec{s})>0
is called a finite
internal path
of
\mathcal{H}
; we omit
\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda
if it is clear from the context.
It is known that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)
can be extended to the set of infinite paths.
The set of every infinite internal path will be denoted as
\mathit{Paths}(\mathcal{H})
.

Observed Sequences.

An external observer can only observe the observable part of an internal path of a POMC.
Given an internal path
\vec{s}=s_{1}s_{2}\ldots\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
, we write
\ell(\vec{s})
to denote the observation sequence
\ell(s_{1})\ell(s_{2})\ldots\in\mathcal{O}^{\infty}
.
For a set of internal paths
S\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
, we write
\ell(S)
to denote the respective set of observation sequences
\{\vec{w}\in\mathcal{O}^{\infty}\mid\exists\vec{s}\;.\;\vec{w}=\ell(\vec{s})\}
.
An observation sequence
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{O}^{\infty}
is called an
observed
path (of
\mathcal{H}
) if there exists an internal path
\vec{s}
for which
\ell(\vec{s})=\vec{w}
.
As before, we use
\mathit{OutPaths}(\mathcal{H})
to denote the set of every finite observed path.
For every observed path
\vec{w}
, let
\ell^{-1}(\vec{w})
be the set
S
of every internal path such that
\ell(S)=\{\vec{w}\}
.
We lift the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)
to the set of observed paths in the usual way, namely, for every measurable set
\mathcal{U}
of observed paths,
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{U})=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\{\vec{s}\in\mathit{Paths}(\mathcal{H})\mid\exists\vec{w}\in\mathcal{U}\;.\;\vec{s}\in\ell^{-1}(\{\vec{w}\})\}\right)
.

Markov Chains.

A POMC
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda,\mathcal{O},\ell\right)
is called a (fully observed)
Markov chain
(MC) if
\ell
is a bijection.
In the special case when the state and observation labels coincide, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{O}
and
\ell\colon q\mapsto q
for every
q\in\mathcal{S}
, we will drop the observations and observation function and represent the MC as the tuple
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda\right)
.
Clearly, for MCs, the concept of internal and observed paths coincide, and they will be referred to simply as
paths
and the set of all infinite paths of an MC
\mathcal{M}
will be denoted as
\mathit{Paths}(\mathcal{M})
.

2.3
Register Monitors

Our register monitors are adapted from the polynomial monitors of Ferrère et al.
[
28
]
, and were also used in our previous work (in a more general randomized form)
[
35
]
.

Syntax.

Let
R
be a finite set of integer variables called registers.
A function
v\colon R\to\mathbb{N}
assigning concrete value to every register in
R
is called a valuation of
R
.
Let
\mathbb{N}^{R}
denote the set of all valuations of
R
.
Registers can be read and written according to relations in the signature
S=\langle 0,1,+,-,\times,\div,\leq\rangle
.
We consider two basic operations on registers:

•

A
test
is a conjunction of atomic formulas over
S
and their negation;

•

An
update
is a mapping from variables to terms over
S
.

We use
\Phi(R)
and
\Gamma(R)
to respectively denote the set of tests and updates over
R
.

Counters
are special registers with a restricted signature
S=\langle 0,1,+,-,\leq\rangle
.

Definition 1

(Register monitor)
.

A register monitor is a tuple
\left(\Sigma,\Lambda,R,v_{\mathsf{in}},f,T\right)
where

\Sigma
is a finite input alphabet,

\Lambda
is an output alphabet,

R
is a finite set of registers,

v_{\mathsf{in}}\in\mathbb{N}^{R}
is the initial valuation of the registers,

f\colon\mathbb{N}^{R}\to\Lambda
is an output function, and

T\colon\Sigma\times\Phi(R)\to\Gamma(R)
is the transition function such that
for every
\sigma\in\Sigma
and for every valuation
v\in\mathbb{N}^{R}
, there exists a unique
\phi\in\Phi(R)
with
v\models\phi
and
T(\sigma,\phi)\in\Gamma(R)
.

We refer to register monitors simply as monitors, and we fix the output alphabet
\Gamma
as the set of every real interval.

Dynamics.

A
state
of a monitor
\mathcal{A}
is a valuation of its registers
v\in\mathbb{N}^{R}
; the initial valuation
v_{\mathsf{init}}
is the initial state.
The monitor
\mathcal{A}

transitions
from state
v
to another state
v^{\prime}
on input
\sigma\in\Sigma
if there exists
\phi
such that
v\models\phi
, there exists an update
\gamma=T(\sigma,\phi)
, and if
v^{\prime}
maps every register
x
to
v^{\prime}(x)=v(\gamma(x))
.
The transition from
v
to
v^{\prime}
on input
\sigma
is written as
v\xrightarrow{\sigma}v^{\prime}
.
A
run
of
\mathcal{A}
on a word
w_{1}\ldots w_{t}\in\Sigma^{*}
is a sequence of transitions
v_{1}=v_{\mathsf{init}}\xrightarrow{w_{1}}v_{2}\xrightarrow{w_{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{w_{t}}v_{t+1}
.

Semantics.

The
semantics
of the monitor is the function
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\Lambda
that maps every finite input word to the last output of the monitor on the respective run.
For instance, the semantics of
\mathcal{A}
on the word
\vec{w}
is
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket(\vec{w})=f(v_{t+1})
.

3
Algorithmic Fairness as Statistical Properties

We formalize algorithmic fairness properties as statistical properties. Statistical properties are defined as the limit of an arithmetic expressions consisting of empirical averages of atomic functions. We specify statistical properties using the Bounded Specification Expressions (BSE) introduced by Henzinger et al.
[
36
]
.
In this section, we motivate the representation of algorithmic fairness properties as statistical properties, present the syntax of BSE, and introduce a new path-based semantics. We highlight two useful fragments and show their applicability to existing fairness measures. In addition, we pove that our new path-based semantics generalises the existing model-based semantics by first showing their equivalence on a subclass of POMCs and then demonstrating that the path-based semantics is well-defined for a much more general class of POMCs.

Model-based perspective.

The simplest example of fairness as a statistical property is a coin. Assume we want to assess the fairness of an infinite sequence of independent coin flips.
We can quantify the fairness of the sequence by looking at the bias
p
of our coin.
We assess the fairness of the coin by comparing the probability of obtaining heads with the probability of obtaining tails. One comparison measure is demographic parity, which considers the difference between these two probabilities, i.e.,
p-(1-p)
.
This is the model-based perspective on fairness, which can be generalised to more complex stationary systems, specifically irreducible and positively recurrent POMCs. Example
3
illustrates how this can be achieved by utilising the stationary distribution of such systems.

Example 2
.

Suppose there are two coins
A
and
B
, where
A
comes up heads with probability
0.9
and
B
comes up tails with probability
0.9
.
At each step, either coin
A
or coin
B
is chosen uniformly at random and tossed. We can observe that the biases of the individual coins balance out in expectation.
We can quantify the expected fairness value of the process using a version of demographic parity computed from the initial state, i.e.,
0.5(p_{A}-(1-p_{A}))+0.5(p_{B}-(1-p_{B}))
.

We used this notion of fairness in one of our previous papers
[
36
]
. This definition is sensible because the system admits a stationary distribution, i.e., the long run average of the time spent with each coin is equal to the initial random choice, i.e.,
0.5
for each coin. Therefore, for every stationary distribution
\pi
we have the unique fairness value
\pi_{A}(p_{A}-(1-p_{A}))+\pi_{A}(p_{B}-(1-p_{B}))
.
This fails for non-stationary systems. We illustrate this in the example below.

Example 3
.

As before, we consider two coins
A
and
B
, where
A
comes up heads with probability
0.9
and
B
comes up tails with probability
0.9
.
Different to before, we start by selecting either coin
A
or coin
B
with equal probability, after which we continuously toss the chosen coin.
If we compute the expected fairness value as before, we obtain the same result, i.e., the system is fair in expectation. However, every possible run of this system will show a bias, i.e.,
0.8
in one scenario and
-0.8
in the other.

Path-based perspective.

Example
3
demonstrated that a system may be fair in expectation at the beginning, but its actual behaviour is severely unfair for every realisation.
We can address the problems with non-stationarity by adopting a path-based perspective.
We propose to avoid quantifying fairness using the limit of the empirical mean. Formally, for the sequence of coin tosses
(w_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
we use

\displaystyle\hat{p}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}w_{i}

to compute demographic parity, i.e.,
\hat{p}-(1-\hat{p})
. This measure evaluates to
0.5
in Example
2
and
0.8
or
-0.8
in Example
3
.

3.1
Bounded Specification Expressions

We specify statistical properties using the rich bounded specification expressions (BSE), which are interpreted over POMCs.
In this section, we introduce a path-based semantics that unifies and generalises the model-based semantics introduced in two previous works
[
35
,
36
]
.

Syntax.

Given a POMC
\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda,\mathcal{O},\ell\right)
, statistical properties use atomic functions of the form
\nu\colon\mathcal{O}^{n}\to[a,b]
mapping finite words of a fixed length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
into a bounded interval over the reals
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
The specified word length
n
for any atomic function
\nu
is called the
arity
of
\nu
. We define the set
\Sigma
as the set of all atomic functions.
A BSE contains arithmetic connectives to express complex value-based properties of an underlying POMC. The syntax is given as:

\displaystyle\text{(BSE)}\qquad\varphi

\displaystyle\Coloneqq\kappa\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ \nu\in\Sigma\ |\ \varphi+\varphi\ |\ \varphi\cdot\varphi\ |\ 1\div\varphi\ |\ (\varphi).

(1)

We note that atomic functions of arity
0
are equivalent to real-valued constants
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. We have made the constant explicit to simplify notation.
We use
V_{\varphi}
to denote the set of variables appearing in the expression
\varphi
.
The
size
of an expression is the total number of operators (
+,-,\cdot,\div,\land,\neg
) in the expression.
Next, we present two examples to illustrate atomic functions.

Example 4
.

Consider the MC
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{lend}}
introduced in Ex.
1
, and suppose we are interested in checking whether two individuals from different groups but with the same credit score were treated differently (one was granted a loan and the other one was not) in the last
10
rounds.
We can express this property using the boolean atomic function
\nu_{10}
such that for every
\vec{s}=s_{1}\ldots s_{10}\in\mathcal{S}^{10}
,
\nu_{10}(\vec{s})=1
iff there exist
k,l\in[1;9]
and
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
with
s_{k}=(A,i)
,
s_{l}=(B,i)
, and
s_{k+1}\neq s_{l+1}
(i.e., one of
\{s_{k+1},s_{l+1}\}
is
Y
and the other one is
N
).
Observe that the function
\nu_{10}
defined above is invalid for
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{po\mathchar 45\relax lend}}
, because the observation labels do not contain
(A,i)
-s and
(B,i)
-s.

Example 5
.

Consider the POMC
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{po\mathchar 45\relax lend}}
introduced in Ex.
1
, and suppose we are interested in calculating the difference between the ratios of accepted individuals from the two groups in the last
10
rounds, assuming we have seen at least one individual from either group.
We can formalize this property using the atomic function
\nu_{10}
such that for every
\vec{w}=w_{1}\ldots w_{10}\in\mathcal{O}^{10}
,

\displaystyle\nu_{10}(\vec{w})=\left|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{9}\mathbb{1}\{w_{i}=A,w_{i+1}=Y\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{9}\mathbb{1}\{w_{i}=A\}}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{9}\mathbb{1}\{w_{i}=B,w_{i+1}=Y\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{9}\mathbb{1}\{w_{i}=B\}}\right|.

Path-based semantics.

The path-based semantics of a BSE
\varphi
is defined as follows. Let
\nu_{n}\in\Sigma
be an atomic function of arity
n
.
The infinite semantics of BSE formulas is built using their finitary semantics stated below.
For every finite prefix
\vec{w}_{t}\in\mathcal{O}^{t}
of length
t\geq n
from an arbitrary infinite word
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{O}^{\omega}
, and
k\coloneqq n-1
:

\displaystyle\llbracket\kappa\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=\kappa

(2)

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu_{n}\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=\frac{1}{t-k}\sum_{i=1}^{t-k}\nu_{n}(w_{i}\ldots w_{i+k})

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi+\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})+\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi\cdot\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})\cdot\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})

\displaystyle\llbracket 1\div\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=1\div\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})\quad(\text{provided }\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})\neq 0)

and the actual semantics of a BSE
\varphi
on the entire infinite word
\vec{w}
is obtained by taking the limit over the length of the prefix, i.e.,

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{w})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t}).

(3)

This limit does not exist in general. However, it exists for all POMCs whose the state space can be decomposed into a finite transient component and a finite number of irreducible and positively recurrent components, e.g., the system in Example
3
. Under those conditions, the semantic value of a
BSE
is well-defined.
This generalizes the model-based semantics introduced by Henzinger et al.
[
36
]
, which is restricted to irreducible and positively recurrent POMCs, e.g., Example
2
.

3.2
Useful Fragments

We present useful fragments of the BSE language. The fragments are characterized syntactically; however, we give an intuition about their semantic value.
Moreover, we demonstrate that some of those fragments can be used to express existing fairness measures.

Polynomial and division free.

An expression is called
division-free
if it does not contain the division operator “
\div
”.
An expression is called
polynomial
if it is a weighted sum of monomials. A monomial is a product of powers of variables with integer exponents
1
1

1

Although monomials and polynomials usually only have positive exponents, we take the liberty of using the terminology even when negative exponents are present.

Probabilistic specification expressions (PSEs).

In our prior work
[
35
]
, we introduced PSEs to model algorithmic fairness properties of Markov chains with a fully observable state space.
PSEs are arithmetic expressions over atomic variables of the form
\rho(r|q)
, where
q
and
r
are states of the given Markov chain. The semantic value of such a variable equals the transition probability from
q
to
r
. Therefore, the semantics of a PSE is the valuation of the expression obtained by plugging in the respective transition probabilities.
We can express PSEs using BSE formulas as follows.
Every variable
\rho(r|q)
appearing in a given PSE is replaced by the subformula

\displaystyle\rho(r|q)\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{1}\{qr\}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{1}\{qs\}}

(4)

where
\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function, i.e.,
\vec{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
,
\mathbb{1}\{xx^{\prime}\}(\vec{w}_{2})=1
if
\vec{w}_{2}=xx^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, BSE formulas are strictly more expressive than PSEs. For instance, unlike PSEs, they can express properties such as the probability of transitioning from one observation label to another, and the average number of times a given state is visited on any finite path of a Markov chain.

Probabilities of sequences.

We consider a useful fragment that expresses the probability that a sequence from a given set
U\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{*}
of finite observation sequences appears at any point during the infinite observed path.
We assume that the length of every sequence in
U
is uniformly bounded by some integer
n
.
Let
\overline{U}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{n}
denote the set of extensions of sequences in
U
up to length
n
, i.e.,
\overline{U}\coloneqq\{\vec{w}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}\mid\exists\vec{u}\in U.\;\vec{u}\text{ is a prefix of }\vec{w}\}
.
Then the desired property will be expressed simply using an atomic function with
\nu\colon\mathcal{O}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
being the indicator function of the set
\overline{U}
, i.e.,
\nu(\vec{w})=1
if and only if
\vec{w}\in\overline{U}
.
For a set of finite words
U\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{*}
, we introduce the shorthand notation
\rho(U)
to denote the probability of seeing an observation from the set
U
at any given point in time.
Furthermore, for a pair of sets of finite words
U,V\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{*}
, we use the shorthand notation
\rho(U\mid V)
to denote
\nicefrac{{\rho(UV)}}{{\rho(U)}}
, which represents the conditional probability of seeing a word in
V
after we have seen a word in
U
.

Fairness and BSE.

In the following examples, we demonstrate that the BSE specification language is sufficiently expressive to cover an array of common fairness properties.

Example 6

(Group fairness.)
.

Consider the setting in Ex.
1
.
We show how we can represent various group fairness properties using BSE-s.
Demographic parity
[
21
]
quantifies bias as the difference between the probabilities of individuals from the two demographic groups getting the loan, which can be expressed as
\rho(Y\mid A)-\rho(Y\mid B)
.
Disparate impact
[
26
]
quantifies bias as the ratio between the probabilities of getting the loan across the two demographic groups, which can be expressed as
\rho(Y\mid A)\div\rho(Y\mid B)
.

Example 7

(Social fairness.)
.

Consider the setting in Ex.
1
, except that now the credit score of each individual is observable along with their group memberships, i.e., each observation is a pair of the form
(X,i)
with
X\in\{A,B\}
and
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
There may be other non-sensitive features, such as age, which may be hidden.
We use the social fairness property
[
34
]
quantified as the difference between the expected credit scores of the groups
A
and
B
.
To express this property, we use the unary atomic functions
\nu_{1}^{X}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
, for
X\in\{A,B\}
, such that
\nu_{1}^{X}\colon(Y,i)\mapsto i
if
Y=X
and
0
otherwise.
The semantics of
\nu_{1}^{X}
is the expected credit score of group
X
scaled by the probability of seeing an individual from group
X
.
Then social fairness is given by the BSE
\varphi=\frac{\nu_{1}^{A}}{\rho(A)}-\frac{\nu_{1}^{B}}{\rho(B)}
.

Example 8

(Quantitative group fairness.)
.

Consider a sequential hiring scenario where at each step the salary and a sensitive feature (like gender) of a new recruit are observed.
We denote the pair of observations as
(X,i)
, where
X\in\{A,B\}
represents the group information based on the sensitive feature and
i
represents the salary.
We can express the disparity in expected salary of the two groups in a similar manner as in Ex.
7
.
Define the unary functions
\nu_{1}^{X}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
, for
X\in\{A,B\}
, such that
\nu_{1}^{X}\colon(Y,i)\mapsto i
if
Y=X
and is
0
otherwise.
The semantics of
\nu_{1}^{X}
is the expected salary of group
X
scaled by the probability of seeing an individual from group
X
.
Then the group fairness property is given by the BSE
\varphi=\frac{\nu_{1}^{A}}{\rho(A)}-\frac{\nu_{1}^{B}}{\rho(B)}
.

3.3
Equivalence to Model-based Semantics

In this subsection, we show that the model-based semantics for both BSE and PSEs, defined respectively by Henzinger et al.
[
36
]
and Henzinger et al.
[
35
]
are equivalent to the new path-based semantics for the class of irreducible and positively recurrent POMCs.
This class is formally specified in Assumption
1
.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that the POMCs are irreducible and positively recurrent.

Model-based semantics.

Under the model-based semantics introduced by Henzinger et al.
[
36
]
, the semantic value of an atomic function is equivalent to the expected value of the atomic function w.r.t. the POMC’s stationary distribution.
Formally, the model-based semantics of a BSE
\varphi
is defined as follows.
For every atomic function
\nu_{n}
with arity
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a POMC
\mathcal{H}
satisfying Assumption
1
, we have

\displaystyle\llbracket\kappa\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=\kappa

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu_{n}\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\nu_{n}(W_{1},\dots,W_{n}))

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi+\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})+\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi\cdot\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\cdot\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\llbracket 1\div\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=1\div\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\qquad(\text{provided }\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\neq 0)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\nu_{n}(W_{1},\dots,W_{n}))\coloneqq\sum_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\nu_{n}(\ell(s_{1}),\dots,\ell(s_{n}))\cdot\pi(s_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}.

Equivalence.

We show the equivalence between the path- and model-based semantics
of BSE for POMCs satisfying Assumption
1
. This is a direct consequence of the ergodic theorem
[
58
]
.
First, the existence of the stationary distribution is guaranteed by the irreducibility and positive recurrence of the POMC. Second, the stationary distribution
\pi
of a POMC is a distribution over its states that remains constant over time, i.e.,
\pi=M\pi
. Hence, the semantic value of the BSE is time-invariant.
Third, in the limit the sample average of a function converges to its expected value computed w.r.t.
\pi
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
be an irreducible and positively recurrent POMC (Assumption
1
) and let
\nu_{n}
be an atomic function of arity
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu_{n}\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\llbracket\nu_{n}\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\quad a.s.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda,\mathcal{O},\ell\right)
. Let
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda)
be the irreducible and positive recurrent Markov chain of
\mathcal{H}
, with unique stationary distribution
\pi
. We construct a
n^{\text{th}}
order Markov chain.
The chain
\mathcal{M}^{(n)}=(\mathcal{S}^{n},M^{(n)},\lambda^{(n)})
is defined by its transition matrix
M^{(n)}
where
for
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
s_{n+1}\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle M_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}),(s_{2},\dots,s_{n+1})}^{(n)}=M(s_{n},s_{n+1})

and
0
everywhere else, and its initial distribution

\displaystyle\lambda^{(n)}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\coloneqq\lambda(s_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}.

This Markov chain represent a
n
sliding window of the original.
We define the distribution
\pi^{(n)}
such that for every
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\pi^{(n)}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\coloneqq\pi(s_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}.

This distribution is the stationary distribution of the augmented Markov chain, i.e., for every
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\pi^{(n)}(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\cdot M_{(r_{1},\dots,r_{n}),(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\pi^{(n)}(r,s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1})M_{s_{n-1},s_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(r)\cdot M_{r,s_{1}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}M_{s_{n-1},s_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\pi(r)\cdot M_{r,s_{1}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\pi(s_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\pi^{(n)}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}).

The first equality is because any non-zero entry in
M^{(n)}
must be between two states overlapping on
n-1
terms.
For
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
we define
f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\coloneqq\nu_{n}(\ell(s_{1}),\dots,\ell(s_{n}))
.
By the ergodic theorem (Theorem 1.10.2
[
58
]
) we have almost sure convergence for
\vec{X}^{(n)}\sim\mathcal{M}^{(n)}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}f(X_{i}^{(n)})=\sum_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\pi^{(n)}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\cdot f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}).

By construction we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}f(X_{i}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\nu_{n}(\ell(X_{i}),\dots,\ell(X_{i+n-1}))=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\nu_{n}(W_{i},\dots,W_{i+n-1})

and that

\displaystyle\sum_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\pi^{(n)}(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\cdot f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\pi(s_{1})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}M_{s_{i},s_{i+1}}\cdot\nu_{n}(\ell(s_{1}),\dots,\ell(s_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\nu_{n}(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})).

Hence, we prove our claim by concluding that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t-(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{t-(n-1)}\nu_{n}(W_{i},\dots,W_{i+n-1})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\nu_{n}(W_{1},\dots,W_{n}))\quad a.s..

∎

From Lemma
1
we can establish the equivalence of the semantics by induction.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
be an irreducible and positively recurrent POMC (Assumption
1
) and let
\varphi
be a BSE. Then

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\quad a.s.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on the structure of the expression. The induction hypothesis is
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})
. The induction hypothesis implies that the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{t}(\vec{W})
exists almost surely.
There are two base cases. The first,
\varphi=\kappa
, holds by definition
\llbracket\kappa\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\kappa=\llbracket\kappa\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})
. The second,
\varphi=\nu_{n}
, follows from Lemma
1
.
The induction step is identical for every
\star\in\{+,\cdot,\div\}
and follows from the limit laws.
Let
\varphi=\psi\star\chi
then by induction hypothesis
\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})
and
\llbracket\chi\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\llbracket\chi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})
. This implies that the limits of
\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{W})
and
\llbracket\chi\rrbracket(\vec{W})
exist almost surely. Hence, the limit of
\llbracket\psi\star\chi\rrbracket(\vec{W})
exists, resulting in

\displaystyle\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\llbracket\psi\star\chi\rrbracket(\vec{W})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\vec{W})\star\lim_{t\to\infty}\llbracket\chi\rrbracket(\vec{W})

\displaystyle=\llbracket\psi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})\star\llbracket\chi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\mathcal{H}).

∎

PSE model-based semantics.

Under the model-based semantics introduced by Henzinger et al.
[
35
]
, the semantic value of
\rho(r|q)
, where
q
and
r
are states of the MC, is equivalent to the transition probability from
q
to
r
as given by
M_{qr}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall q,r\in\mathcal{S}\colon\llbracket\rho(r\mid q)\rrbracket(\mathcal{M})=M_{qr}.

Equivalence between path-based and model-based semantics of PSEs.

We show the equivalence between the path- and model-based semantics of PSE for MCs satisfying Assumption
1
. This is a direct consequence of Theorem
2
.
We demonstrate the equivalence by showing that the encoding in Equation
4
evaluates to the transition probabilities under the model-based semantics of general BSE.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{M}
be an irreducible and positively recurrent Markov chain (Assumption
1
). For all states
q,r\in\mathcal{S}
we have
\llbracket\rho(r|q)\rrbracket(\vec{W})=M_{qr}
almost surely.

Proof.

Let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{M}
. From Theorem
2
we know

\displaystyle\llbracket\rho(r|q)\rrbracket(\vec{W})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{1}\{qs\}(\vec{W}_{t}))}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{1}\{qr\}(\vec{W}_{t}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(X_{1}=q,X_{2}=s)}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(X_{1}=q,X_{2}=r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(X_{1}=q,X_{2}=s)}{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(X_{1}=q)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(X_{2}=s\mid X_{1}=q)

\displaystyle=M_{qs}.

∎

3.4
Generalisation of Model-based Semantics

We show that the path-based semantics for BSE is not well-defined in general. However, it is well-defined over the class of POMCs in which the state space can be decomposed into a finite transient component and a finite number of irreducible and positively recurrent components.
This class is formally specified in Assumption
2
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the state space of a POMCs can be decomposed into a finite set of transient states and a finite set of irreducible and positively recurrent components. Formally, let
\mathcal{H}
be a POMC, then

Let

4
Monitoring Statistical Properties

Informally, our goal is to build monitors that observe randomly generated observation sequences of increasing length from a given unknown POMC, and, after each observation, will generate an updated estimate of how fair or biased the system is.
Since the monitor’s estimate is based on statistics collected from a finite path, the output may be incorrect with some probability.
That is, the source of randomness is from the fact that the prefix is a finite sample of the fixed but unknown POMC.

Soundness.

For a subset of POMCs
\mathcal{P}
, a given BSE
\varphi
, and a given
\delta\in(0,1)
, we define a
problem instance
as the tuple
\left(\mathcal{P},\varphi,\delta\right)
.
Given a problem instance, we first formalize two notions of soundness for monitors.

Definition 2

(Pointwise soundness of monitors.)
.

Suppose
\left(\mathcal{P},\varphi,\delta\right)
is a problem instance.
A monitor
\mathcal{A}
with output alphabet
\{[l,u]\mid l,u\in\mathbb{R}\;.\;l<u\}
is called
\delta
pointwise sound for
\varphi
over
\mathcal{P}
iff for every POMC
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathcal{P}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})\in\mathcal{A}(\vec{W}_{t})\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Definition 3

(Uniform soundness of monitors.)
.

Suppose
\left(\mathcal{P},\varphi,\delta\right)
is a problem instance.
A monitor
\mathcal{A}
with output alphabet
\{[l,u]\mid l,u\in\mathbb{R}\;.\;l<u\}
is called
\delta
uniformly sound for
\varphi
over
\mathcal{P}
iff for every POMC
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathcal{P}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\forall t\in\mathbb{N}:\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})\in\mathcal{A}(\vec{W}_{t})\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Intuitively, the verdict of a given monitor is
correct
, if its output interval includes the true semantic value of the BSE on the monitored POMC.
A pointwise sound monitor guarantees that at every time point its verdict is correct with probability
1-\delta
. In contrast, a uniformly sound monitor guarantees with probability
1-\delta
that there is not a single incorrect verdict in the (potentially) infinite sequence of verdicts generated by the monitor. Every uniformly sound monitor is trivially pointwise sound.
However, a direct consequence of the law of the iterated logarithm is that the other direction is not true, as shown in the following example.

Example 10
.

Consider a simple POMC
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda,\mathcal{O},\ell)
where
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{a,b\}
,
\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\{-1,1\}
,

\displaystyle M\coloneqq\small\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\
0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\coloneqq\small\begin{pmatrix}0.5\\
0.5\end{pmatrix},\;\text{and}\quad\ell(q)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if}\;\;q=a\\
-1&\quad\text{if}\;\;q=b\end{cases}.

The resulting sequence of observations
\vec{W}\coloneqq(W_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is equivalent to a sequence of i.i.d. Radermacher random variables.
Moreover,
(\varphi,\delta)
be a problem instance where, the BSE is the identity function, i.e.,
\varphi\coloneqq\mathrm{id}
.
We construct the monitor
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{O}^{*}\to I(\mathbb{R})
such that for all
\vec{w}\in\mathcal{Q}^{*}
with
t=|\vec{w}|

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\vec{w})\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}w_{i}\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{t}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}.

For simplicity, we define
\bar{w}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{t}w_{i}
.
We show that the monitor
\mathcal{A}
is pointwise sound but not uniformly sound for the set of POMC
\{\mathcal{H}\}
. To be specific we show that for
\mathcal{H}
the statement

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbb{P}\left(\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})\in\mathcal{A}(\vec{W}_{t})\right)\geq 1-\delta

holds, while at the same time

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\forall t\in\mathbb{N}:\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{W})\in\mathcal{A}(\vec{W}_{t})\right)=0.

We establish pointwise soundness using Hoeffding’s inequality.
That is, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(0\in\frac{1}{t}\bar{W}_{t}\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{t}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\frac{1}{t}\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{t}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

At the same time we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{1}{t}\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{t}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|}{\sqrt{2t\log(2/\delta)}}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|}{\sqrt{2t\log(2/\delta)}}\geq 1\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup\frac{\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|}{\sqrt{2t\log(2/\delta)}}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(ii)}}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup\frac{\left|\bar{W}_{t}\right|}{\sqrt{2t\log\log t}}\geq 1\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(iii)}}}{{=}}1.

The first inequality (i) follows from the fact that the limsup will always be smaller than or equal to the supremum. The second inequality (ii) follows from the fact that
\log(2/\delta)\leq\log\log t
for
t\geq e^{2/\delta}
. The equality (iii) follows from the law of iterated logarithm.
The proof concludes by taking the complement and applying De Morgan.

Problem 1
.

The

that we consider are: given a problem instance
\left(\mathcal{P},\varphi,\delta\right)

5
Monitoring BSEs on POMCs

In this section we present a pointwise and a uniformly sound monitor for monitoring general
BSE
over irreducible, positively recurrent, and aperiodic POMCs, which start in their stationary distribution and have a known upper bound on their mixing time.
This class of POMCs, as specified below, is a more restrictive than the class of POMCs specified in Assumptions
1
.

Assumption 3
.

We assume that the unknown POMC
\mathcal{H}
generating the sequence
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
is from the set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{POMC}}
of all irreducible, positively recurrent, and aperiodic POMC starting in their stationary distribution.
Additionally, we assume knowledge of an upper bound on the mixing time
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
of
\mathcal{H}
.

When given a realization of an unknown POMC from this class of POMCs, our monitor will be able to provide converging confidence intervals for any BSE under the assumption that it is provided with an upper bound on the POMC’s mixing time.
Intuitively, this is because irreducibility and positive recurrence guarantee that the monitor observes every state infinitely many times and that there exists a stationary distribution reflecting the proportion of time the POMC will spend in a particular state. The mixing time and aperiodicity is required by the monitor to compute the confidence interval.

5.1
Monitoring Algorithm

We start by presenting the point estimate for atomic functions, which we then combine to obtain a monitor for general
BSE
using union bounds and interval arithmetic.

5.1.1
Monitoring Atomic Functions

A monitor for each individual atomic function is called an atomic monitor, which serves as the building block for the overall monitor.
The output confidence interval of each atomic monitor on a given finite observed path is constructed by first computing a point estimate of the semantic value of the atom based on the observed path, and second computing the estimation error for the given confidence level using a McDiarmid-style concentration inequality
[
61

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{w}_{t})=\frac{1}{t-(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{t-(n-1)}\nu(w_{i},\dots,w_{i+(n-1)}).

(5)

5.2
Technical Proofs of Correctness

In this sub-section we present the proofs of Theorem

We start with Theorem
4

1

7

Here we rely on the fact that the Markov chain starts in its stationary distribution. Second, we demonstrate that the interval estimate constructed around the point estimate captures the true value of the property with high probability. We accomplish this by leveraging a McDiarmid-style concentration inequality for Markov chains
[
61
]
, which directly generalizes to POMCs (see Corollary 2.17
[
61
]
). We restate the Theorem for Markov chains below.

Theorem 6

(
[
61
]
)
.

Let
\vec{S}_{n}\coloneqq S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
be an ergodic Markov chain
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq(\mathcal{S},M,\pi)
with countable state space
\mathcal{S}
, unique stationary distribution
\pi
, and finite mixing time bounded by
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
.
Suppose that some function

with artiy
n
satisfies s.t.
\forall\vec{x},\vec{y}\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle|f(\vec{x})-f(\vec{y})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathbb{1}\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with positive entries. Then for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}\left(\left|f(\vec{S_{n}})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{M}}(f(\vec{S_{n}}))\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}\cdot 9\cdot\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}}\right).

This is a generalization of the classical McDiarmid’s inequality which bounds the distance between the sample value and the expected value of a function satisfying the bounded difference property when evaluated on independent random variables. The inequality we us is only one of many generalizations
[
61
,
24
,
48
]
.
The particular theorem assumes that the partially observed Markov chain has a unique stationary distribution and requires a known bound on mixing time
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
of the Markov chain. Moreover, it requires that our estimator is unbiased and that it satisfies the bounded difference property, which we demonstrate in Lemma
7
and Lemma
8
respectively. This establishes the pointwise soundness of the atomic monitor. We lift this to uniform soundness by performing a union bound across time. We show that Theorem
5
is a consequence of Theorem

Unbiasedness of the estimator

We start our work toward applying Theorem
6
by establishing the unbiasedness of the estimator
\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\cdot)
. Here we leverage the fact that the hidden Markov model is in the stationary distribution.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{POMC}}
with stationary distribution
\pi
, let
\nu\colon\mathcal{O}^{n}\to[a,b]
be a function for fixed
n
,
a
, and
b
. For
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
let
\vec{S}
be the underlying MC. Then for every

we have
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))=\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W})
.

Proof.

Let
N=t-n+1
. By definition and linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu(\vec{W}_{i:i+n-1})\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\nu(\vec{W}_{i:i+n-1})\right).

By stationarity we know that for every

that
W_{i}\sim\pi M^{i-1}=\pi
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\nu(\vec{W}_{i:i+n-1})\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\nu(\vec{W}_{n})\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(\nu(\vec{W}_{n})\right)=\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}).

∎

As a simple corollary we obtain the same result for the estimators of the atoms

Proof.

This follows directly from Prop.

Establishing the Bounded Difference Property.

To apply Theorem

We start by upper-bounding the maximal deviation of the atomic function estimator on almost identical inputs.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\nu\colon\mathcal{O}^{n}\to[a,b]
be a function with fixed
n
,
a
, and
b
,
t\geq n
be a constant,
\vec{x},\vec{y}\in\mathcal{O}^{\omega}
be a pair of observation sequences such that
\vec{x}_{t}
and
\vec{y}_{t}
differ at position
k\in[t]
only.
Then

\displaystyle|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{x}_{t})-\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{y}_{t})|\leq\frac{b-a}{N}\min(k;t-(k-1);n;t-(n-1)).

Proof.

Let
N=t-(n-1)
By definition we have

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{x}_{t})-\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{y}_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu(x_{i},\dots,x_{i+n-1})-\nu(y_{i},\dots,y_{i+n-1}).

The value of
\nu(x_{i},\dots,x_{i+n-1})-\nu(y_{i},\dots,y_{i+n-1})
is
b-a
if
k\in[i;i+n-1]
and
0
otherwise. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{x}_{t})-\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{y}_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in I}b-a=\frac{b-a}{N}\min(k;t-(k-1);n;t-(n-1)).

where
I=[\max(0,k-(n-1)),\min(k+(n-1),N)]
. The interval
I
can be encoded using the
\min(n;t-(n-1);k;t-(k-1))
.
The first term takes into account that, if the string is long enough we know that the symbols at position
k
can be evaluated at most
n
times, once for each input position of the function. The second term takes into account that, if the string is short we know that the symbols at position
k
can be evaluated at most
t-(n-1)
times.
However, this is an over approximation if
k
is at the beginning or at the end of the word.
∎

Confidence Interval.

The confidence intervals generated by McDiarmid-style inequalities for Markov chains tighten in relation to the mixing time of the Markov chain.
This means the slower a POMC mixes, the longer the monitor needs to watch to be able to obtain an output interval of the same quality. This relationship is given in Theorem
6
taken from
[
61
]
. We translate this into a confidence interval for BSE atoms.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{POMC}}
with stationary distribution
\pi
,
\nu:\mathcal{O}^{n}\to[a,b]
be a function for a fixed
n
,
a
, and
b
,
t\geq n
be a constant, and let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
. Then for every
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))|\geq\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)\cdot\frac{t\cdot n^{2}\cdot(b-a)^{2}\cdot 9\cdot\tau_{mix}}{2(t-(n-1))^{2}}}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

From Lemma
8
we know that for every
\vec{x}_{t},\vec{y}_{t}\in\mathcal{O}^{t}

\displaystyle|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{x}_{t})-\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{y}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{b-a}{N}\min(i;t-(i-1);n;t-(n-1))\cdot\mathbb{1}\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{n}{N}\cdot(b-a)\cdot\mathbb{1}\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}^{\prime}\}

we conclude that

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(\frac{n}{N}\cdot(b-a)\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{t\cdot n^{2}}{(t-(n-1))^{2}}\cdot(b-a)^{2}

as required by Theorem
6
. This gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon^{2}(t-(n-1))^{2}}{t\cdot n^{2}\cdot(a-b)^{2}\cdot 9\cdot\tau_{mix}}\right).

from that the required bound follows from simple arithmetic.
∎

From this we construct uniform confidence bounds by performing a union bound over all possible time points. We show this standard result in general form in the lemma below.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\vec{X}
be any stochastic process over
\mathcal{X}
, let
h\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
increasing s.t.
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h(i)}\leq 1
,
,let
\varepsilon\colon(0,1)\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an error function such that for all
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}-\mathbb{E}(X_{t})|\geq\varepsilon(\delta,t))\leq\delta

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:|X_{t}-\mathbb{E}(X_{t})|\geq\varepsilon(\delta\cdot h(t)^{-1},t))\leq\delta.

Proof.

First, we set
\delta_{t}\coloneqq\delta\cdot h(t)^{-1}
for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
and then apply union bound to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:|X_{t}-\mathbb{E}(X_{t})|\geq\varepsilon(\delta_{t},t))\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta_{t}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:|X_{t}-\mathbb{E}(X_{t})|\geq\varepsilon(\delta\cdot h(t)^{-1},t))\leq\delta\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}h(t)^{-1}\leq\delta.

∎

We can combine Lemma
9
and Lemma
10
to obtain the uniform bound.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{POMC}}
with stationary distribution
\pi
,
\nu:\mathcal{O}^{n}\to[a,b]
be a function for a fixed
n
,
a
,
b
, and let
\vec{W}\sim\mathcal{H}
. Then for every
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))|\geq\sqrt{\ln(\pi^{2}t^{2}/(3\delta))\cdot\frac{t\cdot n^{2}\cdot(b-a)^{2}\cdot 9\cdot\tau_{mix}}{2(t-(n-1))^{2}}}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

First we set for every
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and every
t\geq n

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{POMC}}^{p}(\delta^{\prime},t)\coloneqq\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta^{\prime})\cdot\frac{t\cdot n^{2}\cdot(b-a)^{2}\cdot 9\cdot\tau_{mix}}{2(t-(n-1))^{2}}}.

Next, we start by using the pointwise bound given by Lemma
9
with
\delta_{t}\in(0,1)
and perform a union bound over all points in time

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall t\geq n\colon|\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}^{\mathcal{H}}(\llbracket\nu\rrbracket(\vec{W}_{t}))|\geq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{POMC}}^{p}(\delta_{t},t)\right)\leq\sum_{t=n}^{\infty}\delta_{t}.

Next we need to set

We choose

as a consequence we get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=n}^{\infty}\delta_{t}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\delta_{t}=\delta\cdot\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}=\delta.

This concludes the proof.
∎

6
Monitoring PSE-s on MCs

In this section we present a pointwise and a uniformly sound monitor for PSE fragment of BSE over irreducible and positively recurrent fully-observed MCs.
When given a realization of an unknown MC from this class, our monitor will be able to provide converging confidence intervals for any PSE.
Intuitively, this is because irreducibility and positive recurrence guarantees that the monitor observes every state infinitely many times. We restate Assumption
1
for MCs below.

Assumption 4
.

We assume that the unknown MC
\mathcal{M}
generating the sequence
\vec{S}\sim\mathcal{M}
is from the set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{MC}}
containing all irreducible and positively recurrent MCs.

By limiting the expressivity of the language and restricting ourselves to perfectly observed MCs we can construct monitors that are significantly more accurate and require less additional information about the monitored process, e.g., mixing time, compared to the general purpose monitors for POMCs and BSE as presented in Section
5
.

Simplification.

For simplicity we restrict our attention to PSE without division operator, i.e.,
division-free
PSE.
This is without loss of any generality, since it can be shown that every arbitrary PSE
\varphi
of size
n
can be transformed into a semantically equivalent PSE of the form
\varphi_{a}+\frac{\varphi_{b}}{\varphi_{c}}
of size
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}2^{n})
, where
\varphi_{a}
,
\varphi_{b}
, and
\varphi_{c}
are all division-free PSE expressions.
From this, we can obtain individual monitors for each of the division-free sub-expressions, whose outputs can then be combined using Algorithm
2
to obtain the monitor for the overall PSE
\varphi
.
Like Section
5
, we will proceed in two steps to obtain the output of the monitor for division-free PSE expressions: first, we will show how to obtain the point estimates, and, second, we will present the calculations for the approximation error required for the confidence interval construction.

6.1
Key Idea

Suppose
\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{S},M,\lambda\right)
be the monitored unknown MC and let
\vec{S}\sim\mathcal{M}
.
For every atomic variable
\rho(j\mid i)\in V_{\varphi}
in the given PSE, where
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
, we introduce a
\mathrm{Bernoulli}{(M_{ij})}
random variable
X^{ij}
with the mean
{M_{ij}}
unknown to us.
We make an observation
x^{ij}_{p}
for every
p
-th visit to the state
i
on a run, and if
j
follows immediately afterwards then record
x^{ij}_{p}=1
else record
x^{ij}_{p}=0
.
This gives us a sequence of observations
\vec{x}^{ij}=x^{ij}_{1},x^{ij}_{2},\ldots
corresponding to the sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\vec{X}^{ij}=X^{ij}_{1},X^{ij}_{2},\ldots
.
For instance, for the run
121123
we obtain
\vec{x}^{12}=1,0,1
for the variable
\rho(2|1)
.
The heart of our algorithm is an aggregation procedure of every sequence of random variable
\{\vec{X}^{ij}\}_{\rho(j\mid i)\in V_{\varphi}}
to a single i.i.d. sequence
\vec{Y}\coloneqq(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
of auxiliary random variables, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
Y_{t}
is an unbiased point estimator of
\varphi
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(Y_{t})=\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{S})
.
In the following, we explain how the aggregation works for different arithmetic operators in division-free PSE sub-expressions.

Sums and differences.

Let
\varphi=\rho(j\mid i)+\rho(l\mid k)
.
We combine
\vec{X}^{ij}
and
\vec{X}^{kl}
as
Y_{p}=X^{ij}_{p}+X^{kl}_{p}
, so that
y_{p}=x^{ij}_{p}+x^{kl}_{p}
is the corresponding observation of
Y_{p}
.
Then
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(Y_{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{ij}_{p}+X^{kl}_{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{ij}_{p})+\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{kl}_{p})=M_{ij}+M_{kl}
, which is equivalent to
\llbracket\rho_{ij}+\rho_{kl}\rrbracket(\vec{S})
. A similar approach works for
\varphi=\rho(j\mid i)-\rho(l\mid k)
.

Multiplications.

For multiplications, the same linearity principle will not always work, since for random variables
A
and
B
,
\mathbb{E}(A\cdot B)=\mathbb{E}(A)\cdot\mathbb{E}(B)

only if

A
and
B
are statistically independent, which will not be true for specifications of the form
\varphi=\rho(j\mid i)\cdot\rho(k\mid i)
.
In this case, the respective Bernoulli random variables
X^{ij}_{p}
and
X^{ik}_{p}
are dependent:

\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{ij}_{p}=1)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{ik}_{p}=1)=M_{ij}\cdot M_{ik}
, but
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(X^{ij}_{p}=1\land X^{ik}_{p}=1)
is always 0 (since
both

j
and
k
cannot be visited following the
s
-th visit to
i
).
To benefit from independence once again, we temporally shift one of the random variables by defining
Y_{p}=X^{ij}_{2s}\cdot X^{ik}_{2s+1}
, with
y_{p}=x^{ij}_{2s}\cdot x^{ik}_{2s+1}
.
Since the random variables
X^{ij}_{2p}
and
X^{ik}_{2p+1}
are independent, as they use separate visits of state
i
, hence we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(Y_{p})=M_{ij}\cdot M_{ik}
.
For independent multiplications of the form
\varphi=\rho(j\mid i)\cdot\rho(l\mid k)
with
i\neq k
, we can simply use
Y_{p}=X^{ij}_{m}\cdot X^{ik}_{m}
. In both cases we obtain
\llbracket\rho_{ij}\cdot\rho_{kl}\rrbracket(\vec{S})

6.2
Monitoring Algorithm

In general, we use the ideas of aggregation and temporal shift on the syntax tree of the division-free PSE
\varphi
, inductively. Thereby, we create a realization of the process
\vec{Y}
obtained by transforming
\vec{S}\sim\mathcal{M}
. The procedure
MC-MonitorDivFree
is presented in Algorithm
3
in detail.
We now show how we can use the process
\vec{Y}
to construct both an unbiased point estimator and a confidence interval for
\varphi
.

Point estimate.

Let
\varphi
be a PSE, let
\vec{S}\sim\mathcal{M}
and let
\vec{Y}
be the process of i.i.d. random variables with expectation
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{S})
obtained from
\vec{S}
.
We claim that the finitary semantic value of the identity function
evaluated over a finite realization
\vec{y}_{t}
of the constructed
\vec{Y}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathrm{id}\rrbracket(\vec{y}_{t})\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}

is an unbiased estimator for the semantic value of
\varphi
, i.e.,
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{S})
. We prove this claim in Lemma
13
.

Confidence interval.

The constructed process
\vec{Y}
consists of a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with mean
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket(\vec{S})
.
Hence, we can apply Hoeffdings inequality to construct the confidence interval required for a pointwise sound monitor and the stitching bound from Howard et al.
[
39
]
to obtain a uniformly sound.
We use
\mathrm{CI}_{\mathrm{MC}}
to denote the estimation error bound, whose value is based on whether a pointwise sound or uniformly sound monitor is required.
Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma=b_{\varphi}-a_{\varphi}
where
a_{\varphi}
and
b_{\varphi}
are the worst-case lower and upper bound of the value of expression
\varphi
.
A sound monitor is obtained by setting
\mathrm{CI}_{\mathrm{MC}}
to

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MC}}^{p}(t,\delta,\sigma^{2})\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2t}\cdot\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}

A uniformly sound monitor is obtained by setting
\mathrm{CI}_{\mathrm{MC}}
to

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{MC}}^{u}(t,\delta,\sigma^{2})\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\sqrt{1.064\max(1,\sigma^{2}t)\left(2\cdot\log\left(\frac{\pi\log(\max(1,\sigma^{2}t))}{\sqrt{6}}\right)+\log(2/\delta)\right)}

Optimizing memory.

Consider a PSE
\varphi=\rho(j\mid i)+\rho(l\mid k)
.
The outcome
y_{p}
for
\varphi
can only be computed when both the Bernoulli outcomes
x^{ij}_{p}
and
x^{kl}_{p}
are available.
If at any point only one of the two is available, then we need to store the available one so that it can be used later when the other one gets available.
It can be shown that the storage of “unmatched” outcomes may need unbounded memory.
To bound the memory, we use the insight that a
random reshuffling
of the i.i.d. sequence
X^{ij}_{1},\dots,X^{ij}_{p}
would still be i.i.d. and thus we do not need to store the exact order in which the outcomes appeared.
Instead, for every
\rho(j\mid i)\in V_{\varphi}
, we only store the number of times we have seen the state
i
and the edge
(i,j)
in counters
c_{i}
and
c_{ij}
, respectively.
Observe that
c_{i}\geq\sum_{\rho(k\mid i)\in V_{\varphi}}c_{ik}
, where the possible difference accounts for the visits to irrelevant states, denoted as a dummy state
\top
.
Given
\{c_{ik}\}_{k}
, whenever needed, we generate in
\vec{z}_{i}
a
random reshuffling
of the sequence of states, together with
\top
, seen after the past visits to
i
.
From the sequence stored in
\vec{z}_{i}
, for every
\rho(k\mid i)\in V_{\varphi}
, we can consistently determine the value of

(consistency dictates
x^{ik}_{p}=1\Rightarrow x^{ij}_{p}=0

Moreover, we reuse space by resetting
\vec{z}_{i}
if no longer needed.
It is shown in the proof of Thm.
12
that the size of every

can be at most the size of the expression. This random reshuffling of the observation sequences is the cause of the probabilistic transitions of the monitor.

6.3
Technical Proofs of Correctness

In this section we prove the correctness and the resource requirements of the algorithm in Theorem

First we establish that the sequence of random variables constructed by the monitor is indeed a sequence of i.i.d. random variables.
Second we use this fact and apply Hoeffding’s inequality to construct the error bound giving us a sound monitor.
The uniform soundness requires the error bounds to be slightly looser than the bounds obtained through Hoeffding. We construct them using a powerful result from Howard et.al.

We conclude this sub-section by analyzing the complexity of the algorithm.
Here we show that all PSE can be transformed into an equivalent PSE in polynomial form.

Pointwise bounds.

Suppose the given PSE is only a single variable

The monitor

Now consider a slightly more complex PSE

One approach to monitor
\varphi^{\prime}

[

Furthermore, we lose all the advantages of having any dependence between the terms in the PSE.
For instance, by observing that

We overcome these issues by estimating the value of the PSE as a whole as much as possible.

Our approach is always at least as accurate as their approach

Uniform bounds.

7.1
Monitoring BSEs on POMCs

We implemented our monitoring algorithm in Python, and evaluate on the real-world lending example
[
17
]
described in Ex.
1
and to a synthetic example called hypercube.
We ran the experiments on a MacBook Pro (2023) with Apple M2 Pro processor and 16GB of RAM.

Setup.

The underlying POMC model of the system (hidden from the monitor) approximately follows the structure illustrated in Fig.
1
, with several modifications.
First, a low-probability self-loop was added to state
S
to ensure aperiodicity.
Second, we restricted the model to only two credit score levels.
Third, the full system contains more hidden states—
171
in total—capturing additional latent variables such as whether an individual repays or defaults on a loan.
We monitor two fairness properties: demographic parity, defined as
\varphi_{\mathsf{DP}}\coloneqq\rho(Y\mid A)-\rho(Y\mid B)
, and its absolute variant
\varphi_{\mathsf{TDP}}\coloneqq\rho(AY)-\rho(BY)
.
The first expresses the difference in loan approval rates between groups
A
and
B
, while the second captures the difference in joint probabilities of selecting and approving individuals from each group.
Neither property can be expressed as a PSE,
\varphi_{\mathsf{DP}}
involves conditioning, and
\varphi_{\mathsf{TDP}}
involves absolute probabilities.

Experimental outcomes.

Upon receiving new observations, the monitors for
\varphi_{\mathsf{DP}}
and
\varphi_{\mathsf{TDP}}
updated their outputs in
47,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicros}$
and
18,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicros}$
on average, respectively.
Across all runs, the update times ranged from
43,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicros}$
to
0.2,$\mathrm{s}$
for
\varphi_{\mathsf{DP}}
and from
12,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicros}$
to
3.2,$\mathrm{s}$
for
\varphi_{\mathsf{TDP}}
, confirming their efficiency in practice.
Figure
4

\delta=0.05
(i.e.,
95\%

For the lending example, we used a conservative upper bound
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}=170589.78
on the mixing time (computed as in
[
41
]
), which results in slow convergence of the estimation error
\varepsilon
.
For example, to reduce
\varepsilon

\varphi_{\mathsf{TDP}}

For
\varphi_{\mathsf{DP}}

We conclude that eliminating these divisions through direct estimation, along with using tighter bounds on the mixing time, would substantially improve long-term accuracy.

8
Conclusion

This work addressed the problem of monitoring algorithmic fairness properties over Markov chains with unknown transition dynamics, under both full and partial observability. For the partially observed case, we introduced a more expressive specification language alongside a corresponding, albeit slower, monitoring algorithm. For the fully observed case, we developed a specialized algorithm tailored to a restricted fragment of the specification language. We evaluate both approaches theoretically and empirically. For the empirical evaluation we utilize examples adapted from commonly used fairness benchmarks
[
17
]
.

Immediate future research directions are: improving the efficiency of monitoring under full observability for the general specification language; developing efficient model checking procedures for fairness properties; and exploring the applicability of statistical model checking techniques for offline fairness evaluation.
