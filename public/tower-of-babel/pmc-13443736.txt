Patient awareness of viral hepatitis infection in Al-Madinah, Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sally Elnawasany, Ahmad Afghani, Ruba Alqahtani, Rahaf Almaimani, Shatha T AlKuhayli, Zainab A AlShanqiti, Saffanah A AlAhmadi, Fatmah A AlSharif, Wafa A AlHarbi, Almas A AlOufi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViral hepatitis is one of the most serious health problems worldwide. Chronic viral hepatitis affects the population’s health in Saudi Arabia, where many people are suffering from liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma and have undergone liver transplants. Prevention of viral hepatitis is promoted by health education on the disease. The study aims to evaluate the level of awareness and knowledge about viral hepatitis infection among patients in Al-Madinah, Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to June 2025 among consecutive patients diagnosed with hepatitis B or C who attended the gastroenterology department at King Fahad Hospital. Patient knowledge was assessed using a validated questionnaire. Scores ≥ 6 are considered adequate knowledge. The knowledge rate was calculated as the percentage of participants with adequate knowledge out of the total study population. Descriptive statistical analysis was used to summarize the findings. The overall knowledge rate among participants was 40.6%, with a mean knowledge score of 5.75 ± 1.36. A significant positive correlation was found between knowledge scores and participants’ educational level (r = 0.26, p-value = 0.01) and duration of HBV or HCV infection (r = 0.2, p-value = 0.041). Longer duration of diagnosis with hepatitis B or C virus (> 5 years) or having previous vaccination with hepatitis A or B vaccine were good predictors (independent predictors) of good knowledge about viral hepatitis among participants. Awareness of viral hepatitis among patients of the study is insufficient, particularly regarding prevention strategies. Strengthening educational efforts may contribute to improving disease outcomes and support national goals for eliminating viral hepatitis.Keywords: Viral hepatitis, Awareness, Health Education, Infection preventionSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Chronic viral hepatitis is one of the most serious health problems in the world. It results in the mortality of 1.4 million patients each year1. It affects the population’s health in Saudi Arabia, where many individuals are suffering from liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and have undergone liver transplants2. Chronic hepatitis B and C are prevalent in Saudi Arabia at a rate of 2.72% and 3.43%, respectively. The percentages of mortality related to hepatitis B and C are 2.2 and 6.61 per 100,000 patients, respectively, and per 100,000 individuals, hepatitis A, B, and C coexist at a rate of 0.23, 14.53, and 5.75, respectively3.

Hepatitis A virus (HAV)

The faecal-oral route is the main mode of transmission of the Hepatitis A virus (HAV). There may be no symptoms, or the patient may suffer from acute severe hepatitis in the form of Jaundice, elevated body temperature, and diarrhoea4.

Hepatitis B virus (HBV)

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) is highly prevalent in the world5. Although it is endemic in Saudi Arabia, the national vaccination program has decreased its prevalence in recent years6,7. HBV is transmitted via contaminated blood and body secretions. Vertical transmission from an infected mother to her baby is another important route8. The most common complications of HBV are liver cirrhosis and HCC9.

Hepatitis C virus(HCV)

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is a major global public health concern, while the WHO has aimed to eradicate it by 203010. Due to its long incubation period, which may be 20 years, some patients are unaware of their illness11. Exposure to infected blood constitutes the main route of transmission12. Direct-acting antivirals (DAA) have reduced the prevalence of HCV in various countries, including Egypt, where HCV prevalence decreased from 10 to 0.38% (< 0.5%) in 10 years13.

Hepatitis D virus (HDV)

At the beginning of this decade, the Hepatitis D virus (HDV) infected about twelve million individuals in the world14. The HDV is a weak virus that needs HBV for hepatocyte infection and proliferation. It shares the HBV route of transmission15. Chronic HDV may lack clinical symptoms or be associated with features of liver insufficiency16.

Hepatitis E virus (HEV)

Hepatitis E virus (HEV) is transmitted via contaminated food or water. It is the cause of most reported cases of jaundice worldwide, with more harm in pregnancy, but sometimes it can be asymptomatic17–19.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2024 and June 2025. This research aimed to evaluate the level of awareness and knowledge of viral hepatitis infection among hepatitis patients in Al-Madinah, Saudi Arabia. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Institutional Review Board (IRB), General Directorate of Health Affairs in Madinah, Ministry of Health, Madinah, KSA, IRB number (24–105).

The study population was the consecutive patients who visited King Fahad Hospital’s gastroenterology department and were diagnosed with viral hepatitis B and C.

Hepatitis A virus (HAV): 1 question, Hepatitis B virus (HBV): 5 questions, Hepatitis C virus (HCV): 3 questions, Hepatitis D virus (HDV): 1 question. Hepatitis E virus (HEV): 1 question.

Each question has 3 answer options: (“Yes,” “No,” and “I don’t know”. For every correct answer, a score of “1” was given, and for every wrong or “I don’t know” answer, a score of “0” was given, leaving a total score of 11 for the knowledge questions. If a participant’s score was less than 60% of the total, the overall awareness score was classified as bad. If the participant’s score was 60% or higher, the overall awareness score was considered good20,21.

Validity and reliability analysis

The present survey’s questionnaire assessed hepatitis patients’ awareness and knowledge about hepatitis in Saudi Arabia. The survey questions were derived from earlier research20–22. A pilot study involving 30 participants was conducted, and the findings were used to assess the questionnaire’s reliability and validity.

Validation analysis

A three-person expert panel evaluated the questionnaire’s validity. The experts modified the initial items to determine whether they were appropriate for examination. The subject matter experts were asked to score each item on a four-point scale based on its applicability and relevance. If the item was acceptable (simple, relevant, and clear), it was given a score of “4”, if it was adequate but needs minor improvement, it was given a score of “3”, if it needed considerable modification, it was given a score of “2”, and if it was not so adequate (may be omitted), it was given a score of “1”. The content validity index (CVI) is the percentage of all items that expert’s rate as 3 or 4. If the CVI was found to be 80%, the score is considered to have strong validity. The CVI of the intended questionnaire was calculated and found to be 93.1%.

Reliability analysis

When the reliability of internal consistency was investigated, a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 was discovered. A Cronbach’s alpha value greater than 0.7 (3) indicates that the scale is internally consistent23.

Data analysis

Data were statistically analysed using SPSS application version 26. To investigate the association between the variables, the Chi-squared test (χ2) was applied to qualitative data that was expressed as numbers and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (Mean ± SD), and correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was done to assess the risk factors (independent predictors) of good knowledge about viral hepatitis among participants. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated at a 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Regarding demographic characteristics of the 96 participants, 55 patients (57.3%) were 50 years old or less. Male participants were 57 (59.4%), and 87 (90.6%) of the patients were from urban areas. Unemployment was documented in 61 patients (63.5%), and 33 (34.4%) individuals recorded a university qualification (Table 1). 76 (79.2%) of the participants had a positive hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg) in the past or were diagnosed with HBV. 7 (7.3%) and 4 (4.2%) had a previous HAV and HEV infection, respectively. 12 (12.5%) had hepatitis C infection in the past or were positive for anti-HCV. Only 8 (8.3%) and 4 (4.2%) had a positive HBV and HAV vaccination history, respectively. (Table 2).

Among HBV and HCV patients in our study (n = 88), 61 (69.3%) had been diagnosed for more than five years, 21 (23.9%) for 1–5 years, and 6 (6.8%) for less than 1 year.

Regarding hepatitis knowledge, 40 (41.7%) participants correctly agreed that eating contaminated food can transmit the HAV. For HBV, 49 (51%) knew that chronic hepatitis B can cause cirrhosis or liver cancer. Almost half 47 (49%) disagreed that shaking hands with an HBV patient causes HBV infection. The majority, 62 (64.6%), knew that HBV can be transmitted from an infected mother to her baby during delivery, and 60 (62.5%) knew that antiviral drugs effectively delay HBV development. Regarding HCV knowledge, 69 (71.9%) knew that sharing razors with others leads to HCV infection, and 49 (51%) knew that HCV patients can be treated completely by antiviral therapy. About 63 (65.6%) knew the importance of doing HCV testing before blood transfusions, tattoos, or ear piercings. Only 9 (9.4%) knew that the mode of transmission of HDV, as well as its effect on the liver, is similar to HBV, and 22 (22.9%) knew that HEV can be transmitted through eating contaminated food. Almost 40 (40.6%) knew that the baby should be vaccinated with the HBV vaccine within 24 h after birth. (Table 3; Fig. 1). The mean knowledge score was 5.3 ± 2.9; a good knowledge rate was recorded in 39 (40.6%) patients (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Chronic viral hepatitis is a serious worldwide problem with high mortality and morbidity. This cross-sectional study assessed viral hepatitis awareness in 96 hepatitis patients.

Hepatitis knowledge rate was relatively low (40.6%). Although this may indicate poor awareness of viral hepatitis and its prevention, the small number of participants and short study duration may explain this low knowledge rate.

A higher, but not statistically significant, knowledge rate was observed among younger males, urban residents, the unemployed, and those with higher education levels. This may indicate that the population under study’s knowledge of hepatitis is not sufficiently determined by demographic factors alone. On the other hand, the educational level demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the knowledge rates, emphasizing how crucial education raises health literacy and knowledge of viral hepatitis. In a similar vein, knowledge rates were positively correlated with longer duration of HBV or HCV diagnosis, which may be explained by greater exposure to medical information, counselling, and follow-up over time, all of which enhanced the disease orientation. Additionally, individuals who had been infected with hepatitis for more than five years and those who had received a hepatitis A or B vaccination were more likely to have higher levels of knowledge. These results suggest that ongoing engagement with healthcare services and preventive measures enhances hepatitis knowledge and comprehension. Special consideration of patients with lower educational attainment and less exposure to medical education is recommended.

Many previous studies reported similar findings. A Chinese study that included 3,626 participants reported 52.34% knowledge rate of viral hepatitis. Higher rates were associated with university education and hepatitis B immunization24. Similarly, a Saudi cross-sectional study that included 549 individuals recorded 42% knowledge rate25. A higher HBV knowledge rate 50.5% was detected in another Saudi study of 402 adults. 69.7% of the respondents were aware that hepatitis B can be prevented by vaccination. Just 9% of the participants possessed good preventive practice, while 46.5% had poor preventive practice. The mean knowledge score was 22.23 ± 4.2226. The explanation of this higher knowledge rate and score may be attributed to a larger sample (402 compared to 96 in our study), which improved representativeness. The participants were mostly educated (60.9% with a bachelor’s degree), which exceeds the percentage in our study. The data collection process was also varied: Our study was directed at hospital patients, while this study used community-based convenience sampling at public places in Riyadh, which is a different culture. Furthermore, this study used a more extensive knowledge and practice scoring system. While we used 11 direct knowledge questions. Moreover, our study assessed all viral hepatitis, not HBV knowledge, which was assessed only in the Riyadh study.

Another study by Bafail et al. (2020) assessed HBV knowledge in 5 thousand public participants of average age 21–36 years in Jeddah. Approximately 65.35% of participants possessed a basic level of awareness regarding the hepatitis B virus (HBV), and 75% acknowledged the existence of a preventive vaccine27. Sample size and community variation from our study explain the differences in knowledge rates. However, both studies agreed that younger age and university-level education are associated with a higher knowledge rate.

On the other hand, higher medical knowledge rates among doctors were reported in many studies. One study assessed 180 primary health care doctors in Al-Jouf for their HBV medical knowledge. The average knowledge score was 62.9%28. Additionally, Alshammari et al. studied HBV knowledge among healthcare workers in 7 Saudi towns and demonstrated a moderate to good level of knowledge. Specifically, 73.7% acknowledged the effectiveness of the vaccine, and 80.1% correctly identified the liver as the main organ affected by the disease. Factors associated with higher knowledge included prior training, the presence of institutional vaccination protocols, awareness of guidelines, and female gender29.

The diversity of the participants in the previous two studies explains the difference in knowledge rates. Our study included patients, mostly with lower educational levels; however, both studies assessed the knowledge in trained healthcare professionals. Moreover, the assessment questions were different. Our study used basic knowledge questions; on the other hand, both studies involved more scientific questions. Overall, while both studies show gaps in knowledge, the gap is wider among the general public. This highlights the need for more health education and vaccination awareness among the communities.

Unlike most other research, this study tested hepatitis patients’ awareness of all forms of viral hepatitis. This provides more depth of viral hepatitis knowledge evaluation.

Many constraints made it difficult to assess the hepatitis knowledge rate in this study. Small sample size, single hospital, and short duration. Furthermore, recruiting individuals just from a hospital context may cause selection bias, as the sample may not accurately reflect the characteristics of the broader population. Further research is necessary to adequately measure public awareness of viral hepatitis, which is an important step in hepatitis prevention.

Conclusions

Awareness and understanding of viral hepatitis among patients of the study are insufficient, particularly regarding prevention strategies. Strengthening educational efforts, especially among groups with lower educational attainment and less exposure to medical education, may contribute to improve disease outcomes and support national goals for eliminating viral hepatitis.
