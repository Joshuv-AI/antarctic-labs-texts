Trends in disease characteristics and premature mortality among hospitalised middle-aged patients in Anhui Southern Area, China, between 2011 and 2020: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yao Yuanying, Chen Lei, Lijuan Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to explore the characteristics of age, disease and premature mortality among middle-aged inpatients.DesignCross-sectional.SettingData were gathered from the electronic medical records system of the hospitals between 2011 and 2020: a regional study.ParticipantsA total of 151 320 patients, aged 45–59 years, underwent hospitalisation.Primary outcome measuresWe analysed the characteristics of age and disease using the database from the electronic medical record system. Moreover, a binary logistic regression model was adopted to investigate the risk factors for premature mortality.ResultsWe identified 151 320 eligible inpatients, 53.0% of whom were female. The proportion of middle-aged inpatients increased alarmingly over a decade from 6.4% in 2011 to 28.3% in 2020. The incidence rate of inpatients aged 45 to 50 years increased perpendicularly, with its peak observed at the age of 50. The prevalence tended to decrease after the age of 50; however, it increased at the age of 59. Overall, cancer had the highest incidence rate (22.7%), followed by accidental injury, digestive system disease, cardiovascular disease and cerebrovascular disease (9.0%, 7.9%, 6.3% and 4.5%, respectively). The overall mortality among the middle-aged inpatients was 0.5%. Logistic regression analysis showed that sex, marital status and comorbidity were the factors contributing to mortality in hospitalised patients, with male, divorced and more than two comorbidities being risk factors for death.ConclusionsA rising trend in the number of middle-aged inpatients was observed during the study period. The number of hospitalisations reaches its peak at the age of 50. Being male, divorced and having more than two comorbidities are risk factors for premature mortality. So prevention, early detection and appropriate treatment of comorbidities are beneficial in improving health and reducing mortality.Keywords: Health informatics, Cross-Sectional Studies, Public Hospitals, Hospitalisation

Introduction

It has long been known that ill health, due to frailty and poor physical conditions, is prevalent in the elderly and accounts for the overwhelming majority of deaths in the ageing population. However, a growing body of research has revealed that the disease tends to strike younger1,3 and middle-aged people aged 45–59, who are the most productive, which may lead to loss of productivity, increased lifetime healthcare use and poor prognosis.4 5 Furthermore, the latest data suggest that the overall mortality rate among middle-aged people has increased in the recent years.6 However, related research on premature mortality is limited and lacks specific explanation of disease features, trends, and risk factors. Therefore, further research is needed to explore the disease and mortality characteristics of middle-aged patients.

There is no strict standard for premature mortality as it is based on the average life expectancy at birth in a given country, which varies from region to region.7 World Health Statistics 2020 shows that the overall life expectancy in China in 2016 was 76.4 years, with 17% premature mortality due to the four major chronic diseases. Without doubt, until now, there are many studies about mortality, for instance, Zhang8 found that the incidence of thyroid cancer increased significantly on a global scale from 1990 to 2017, which was consistent with the trend for cervical cancer in Japan,9 pancreatic cancer with diabetes in Korea10 and colorectal cancer in Sweden.11 However, they focussed mainly on the analyses of overall incidence and mortality, ignoring the causes and characteristics of disease in different age groups, especially middle-aged people, who play an important role in societal productivity. There are, of course, some exceptions. Bajpai12 attempted to elaborate the trends in the age distribution of head and neck cancers in patients less than 40 years of age. Cabral13 explored the trends in the incidence of ischaemic stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage in young people and found a significant increase of 62% in subjects less than 45 years of age. Unfortunately, they only focussed on one disease, and thus, they overlooked analyses of mechanisms, features of disease and risk factors in young adults. A further study evaluating trends in characteristics and premature mortality in middle-aged inpatients is warranted.

We aimed to assess the characteristics of age, disease, premature mortality and its contributing factors to mortality in middle-aged (45≤59 years) patients who were hospitalised from 2011 to 2020 so that a better insight into this phenomenon may be valuable for disease screening in young people, intervention measures for risk factors and formulation of public health policies for the government.

Methods

Setting

Located in central China, Anhui is an important part of the Yangtze River Delta and is at a strategic crossroads in China’s economic development. Located in the southern part of Anhui Province, the South Anhui Region covers a geographical area of 31 200 square kilometres, including Wudang Prefecture, Chizhou Prefecture, Xuancheng Prefecture, Chizhou Prefecture and Wuhu City, and has a population of 18.07 million as of 2019. With three Grade 3A hospitals, as well as numerous general hospitals and community health service organisations, the South Anhui region is rich in healthcare resources and is able to meet the demands for healthcare services of residents in the South Anhui region. The data from the hospitals have some value for middle-aged inpatients in middle-income cities.

Study design

A retrospective study of inpatients aged 45≤59 years was conducted using the electronic medical record system dataset from the hospitals between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2020. Patients who were treated in the emergency/outpatient department or discharged within 24 hours were excluded. All demographic data and most disease-related information were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including sex, age, region (urban and rural), medical payment methods (such as medical insurance for urban employees, medical insurance for urban residents, new rural cooperative medical insurance, poverty relief, all at one’s own expense and all at public expense), marital status, length of hospital stay, year of discharge, the primary diagnosis and discharge situation (cured, improved, not cured, death and others). Surgery was divided into two categories: yes and no. The number of comorbidities was counted based on whether the inpatient had other diagnoses or the number of other diagnoses from the home pages of the medical records.

In our study, we used the code of International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision to analyse the primary diagnostic names representing different types of diseases from the letters A to Z. It was worth noting that, in order to obtain more detailed information, circulatory diseases (I00–I99) were divided into three categories in the statistical process: cardiovascular diseases, cerebrovascular diseases and other. We defined middle-aged adults as those aged 45≤59 years, according to the most recent WHO age classification criteria for life stages.

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct public or patient involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

Statistical analysis

All standard descriptive statistics including percentages and frequencies were obtained by using SPSS statistics V.20 to achieve the research goals. Continuous variables were expressed as means and medians. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Two comparisons of each categorical variable between groups were performed using the χ2 test, which should be noted that a two-sided p value<0.05 was considered to have statistical significance in the statistical analysis. To analyse risk factors for premature mortality, we decided to adopt a binary logistic regression model by including variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis. P values for trends were then determined based on these models.14

Results

Overall trends and general characteristics

We identified 151 320 inpatients aged 45≤59 years who met the inclusion criteria. Overall, the proportion of hospitalised middle-aged patients each year out of the total number of inpatients increased alarmingly from 6.4% in 2011 to 28.3% in 2020 in a decade study period (figure 1). In this study, 53.0% were female, and the male-to-female ratio was 0.89. The number of hospitalised middle-aged women increased 6.19 times from 1991 in 2011 to 14 312 in 2020, compared with 5.35 times for men. The majority of occupations were farm workers and unemployed (27.5% and 25.3%, respectively) (table 1). A total of 150 011 (99.1%) were married, and only 0.2% were divorced. The average length of hospital stay (SE) was 9.6 days (10.692), with an overall decreasing trend from 11.4 (11.606) in 2011 to 8.76 (7.928) in 2020. And approximately a third of middle-aged patients had two or more comorbidities (28.9%) compared with patients with a single disease (44.6%; table 1), with the most common types of comorbidities being endocrine system and cardiovascular system comorbidities (13.22%), cardiovascular system and digestive system comorbidities (10.87%), and cardiovascular system and respiratory system comorbidities (7.31%).

Age traits

The mean age (SE) of the study population was 51.7 (4.059) years (table 1). There were 63 161 patients aged ≤50 years (41.7%), with the most common primary diagnosis being cancer (21.5%). As observed in figure 2, the incidence rate of hospitalised inpatients increased perpendicularly from the age of 45 to 50, peaking at the age of 50. The prevalence decreased year by year after the age of 50 but increased from the age of 59. The general trends mentioned above applied perfectly to men; however, it was a little different for women, in whom the incidence rate peaked at the age of 50 and then declined from age 51 to 59. And in rural areas, 50 was also the peak age for hospitalisation among middle-aged people, and the rate of hospitalisation slowly declined with age.

Disease characteristics

Overall, the highest disease incidence among middle-aged inpatients was cancer (22.7%), followed by accidental injury, digestive system disease, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease (9.0%, 7.9%, 6.3% and 4.5%, respectively; figure 3A). Diseases of the digestive system (8.6%) were the second-most common disease after cancer in urban areas, while it was accidental injury (16.6%) in rural areas. The top five primary diagnoses for men were in line with the total trends (figure 3B). However, in women, the trend was slightly different, and the top five primary diagnoses were cancer (24.2%), digestive system disease (8.3%), accidental injury (6.9%), gynaecological disease (6.8%) and cardiovascular disease (5.6%; figure 3C). During the study period, from 2011 to 2020, the proportion of middle-aged patients suffering from accidental injury, cardiovascular disease, gynaecological disease and digestive disease decreased; on the contrary, cancer and cerebrovascular disease increased overall (figure 4). Cancers of the digestive system were dominant (37.4%), followed by thyroid cancers (21.2%) and gynaecological cancers (15.3%). It was worth noting that the incidence of diabetes increased from 2.2% in 2011 to 3.0% in 2020.

Premature mortality and risk factors

The total all-cause mortality among middle-aged inpatients in 2011–2020 was 0.5%. Males had a higher mortality than females (62.7% vs 37.3%), urban than rural (65.7% vs 34.3%) and divorced than married (5.7% vs 0.5%). Among the patients who died, the mean age (SD) was 52.1 (4.078), the average length of hospital stay (SD) was 13.5 (36.093) and close to half (47.0%) of the inpatients had undergone surgery. Approximately 83.9% of patients who died had two or more comorbidities. Lung cancer (25.7%) accounted for the most common cancer deaths, followed by liver cancer (18.2%), stomach cancer (12.2%) and leukaemia (11.5%; table 2). Taking death as the dependent variable and those with statistical significance in univariate analysis as the independent variables, logistic regression analysis results indicated that sex, marital status and comorbidities were the contributing factors to death in hospitalised patients aged 45≤59 years, among which male, divorce and more than two comorbidities were risk factors (table 3).

Discussion

We document the following trends in the middle-aged patients who were hospitalised in the past decade, which helps us to understand the health status and disease trends of middle-aged inpatients in southern Anhui and provides effective guidance for the government to understand the health needs of middle-aged inpatients and formulate public healthcare policies. While medical technology has become increasingly developed, the morbidity of hospitalised patients has increased more than fourfold between 2011 and 2020. The worsening of air pollution, the destruction of the geographical environment, the increase in stress levels and bad lifestyles have posed a great threat to the health of middle-aged adults. Bajpai12 found an overall increase in the proportional prevalence of oropharyngeal cancer, largely due to the increased incidence in middle-aged patients (40–59). Of course, it should also be emphasised that the development of medicine and healthcare makes people pay more attention to physical health and disease prevention, which contributes to some extent to the increase in the number of inpatients aged 45≤59 years. However, Konat-Bąska15 suggested that breast cancer did not increasingly affect younger women in the Lower Silesian Voivodeship, which was different from our outcomes. During the study period, the mean length of stay for patients generally showed a decreasing trend, which was consistent with the national policy of reducing the length of stay in China. It was beneficial to reduce the in-hospital costs for patients, solve the problem of difficulty in seeing a doctor, improve the efficiency of medical resource utilisation and accelerate the bed turnover rate.

Gender disparities exist among patients.16,18 We found that males accounted for a small proportion of hospitalisations but had higher mortality than females. On the contrary, previous evidence showed that hospitalised young women had higher mortality but lower morbidity than men,7 which may be related to the fact that our study was a regional study rather than a national census. Studies suggest that the incidence rate of some diseases is higher in the female group, which partly leads to more female patients than men. 19Vaccarino found that premature mortality from cerebrovascular disease in Europe was unequal in both sexes. The incidence trend of cervical cancer increased significantly in women aged 20–59 years in Japan.9 Similarly, the incidence rates of thyroid cancer were much higher in females than in males in China during 2005–201520. Notably, due to the heterogeneity of the results presented, the changes in lifestyles, healthcare resources and work stress should be highlighted in the gender subgroups. Smoking, alcohol consumption, life stress and poor psychological resilience in men are major risk factors for mortality; furthermore, loneliness and social isolation have been suggested as risk factors for cancer mortality in a cohort of middle-aged men.21

We found that the age of middle-aged inpatients showed a fluctuating trend during the study period; furthermore, regardless of female and rural area, the age of 50 was the peak incidence of disease in middle-aged adults and an important turning point for physical health, suggesting that healthcare screening for people in their 50s is critical. Unfortunately, there has been little research into why 50 is the most common age for middle-aged disease, so no solid evidence has been found to explain this phenomenon. We believe that, on the one hand, at the age of 50, hormone secretion in the body gradually decreases, resulting in a large loss of calcium and a decline in immunity and resistance to disease. On the other hand, long-term physical inactivity and an unhealthy diet reduce physical quality and even cause problems such as hyperlipidaemia and hyperglycaemia. Therefore, government departments should strengthen healthcare education and implement a series of preferential policies to encourage residents to participate in health check-ups and disease screening, especially those the age of 50, so as to achieve early detection and early treatment of diseases, thereby alleviating the financial burden of diseases.

Our study found that cancer was the biggest threat to human health, which was in line with other studies showing that cancer was the leading cause of death worldwide in countries at all income levels.15 22 The incidence rates of many cancers have increased in many low-and-middle-income countries due to the increase in risk factors typical of Western countries23 related to obesity, kidney, uterine corpus and colorectum cancer,24 but in southern China, the incidence of cardiovascular disease in middle-aged patients is increasing rapidly.25 Although our study is only regional, and there may be differences between countries and regions due to geographical and cultural differences, it provides useful references for us to understand the trends in diseases that threaten the health of middle-aged inpatients. Accidental injuries including bites,26 burns27 and traffic accidents28 are becoming the second leading cause of injury among middle-aged adults, especially traffic accidents resulting from the expansion of roads in all directions, which are responsible for approximately 1.25 million deaths in low-and-middle countries29 and increase the additional economic burden of disease.30 Meanwhile, the prevalence of stroke3 and coronary heart disease and coronary artery disease2 is increasing every year, especially in the young group because of work pressure, irregular daily schedules, and diet and other predisposing factors.31 32 In our study, the incidence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease accounted for 10.8% of all middle-aged patients, so it is necessary to strengthen healthcare education and promote proper nutrition.

Comorbidity is defined as the simultaneous presence of two or more chronic diseases at the same time.33 Our study suggested that comorbidity was an influencing factor for premature mortality in middle-aged inpatients. According to some studies,34 older people have more comorbidities, and comorbidity is reported in 35% to 80% of all ill people. Comorbidity has implications for patient care, treatment planning, assessment of treatment effectiveness35 and family financial burden. Patients with comorbidities are weaker, and they have more complications such as cardiovascular disease and cerebrovascular disease, especially for cancer patients. Our study suggested that the endocrine system and cardiovascular system comorbidities and cardiovascular system and digestive system comorbidities were the most common types, leading to poorer diagnosis and more complex treatment, increasing medical expenses and the risk of death. It indicates that individuals should increase prevention and treatment of comorbidities, doctors should treat patients’ diseases from a multidisciplinary perspective, and the government should increase publicity and education about common comorbidities and encourage middle-aged patients to exercise more and lead a healthy life.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several limitations. The major disadvantage of this study is that the scope of the study is small, so it is limited to southern Anhui, although it represents 50% of middle-aged inpatients, and more hospital data are still needed as a reference for inpatients in regions with different economic levels in China. Moreover, the cross-sectional study can only reflect the status of inpatients at one point in time and cannot track the trend of change and development over time, and it was difficult to determine the causal relationship between risk factors and death. Second, we used the data from hospital information system data as the main analysis database, which is subject to coding errors. In addition, as International Classification of Diseases codes are set for medical payments, there are problems with the validity of the codes as they cannot represent the actual clinical condition.36 Third, we only analysed the group of inpatients aged 45≤59 years but did not compare them with patients aged 60 years and older, so we could not understand the differences in disease characteristics and mortality trends of middle-aged and older hospitalised patients. Despite these limitations, it is valuable to evaluate the characteristics of disease and trends in premature mortality in middle-aged inpatients.

Conclusion

In summary, a rising trend in the number of middle-aged inpatients was found during the study period, which provides an effective guide for the government to understand the health needs of middle-aged inpatients and formulate public healthcare policies. Males account for a small proportion of hospital admissions but have a higher mortality rate than females. Meanwhile, since 50 is the peak age for disease onset in middle-aged adults, it is necessary to strengthen health screening and public health education measures before the age of 50. Cancer becomes the primary disease spectrum in middle-aged patients, which provides useful references for us to understand the trends in diseases that threaten the health of middle-aged inpatients. More comorbidities, being male and divorce are risk factors for premature mortality in people aged 45≤59 years.
