Reprocessing Mass Spectra from Therapeutic Drug Monitoring to Unveil Proton Pump Inhibitor Use and Quantify Phenoconversion of Escitalopram Metabolism in CYP2C19‐Genotyped Patients
Authors: Kristine Hole, Birgit M Wollmann, Julia Carolin Stingl, Espen Molden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscitalopram is a frequently prescribed antidepressant drug. CYP2C19 genotype is a major determinant of escitalopram metabolism and treatment outcome. However, real‐life studies show a high degree of genotype–phenotype mismatch in escitalopram metabolism. The present study aimed to identify comedication with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and quantify phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism in CYP2C19‐genotyped patients by reprocessing high‐resolution mass spectra from therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) analyses. Patients were included from a combined TDM/pharmacogenetics (PGx) laboratory facility in Oslo, Norway. TDM profiles of escitalopram and CYP2C19 genotypes were available in the laboratory database. Reprocessing of high‐resolution mass spectra by exact mass and fragment detection identified possible comedication with pantoprazole, esomeprazole/omeprazole or lansoprazole using mzCloud reference library. Patients were grouped as normal metabolizers (NMs), intermediate metabolizers (IMs), poor metabolizers (PMs), rapid metabolizers (RM), or ultrarapid metabolizers (UM). Primary outcome measure was N‐desmethylescitalopram‐to‐escitalopram metabolic ratio (MR; CYP2C19 biomarker) in UMs vs. PMs. 2818 patients were included, constituting 2373 without and 445 (15.8%) with PPIs. Most patients were females (64.2%). Regarding CYP2C19 phenotype distribution, the cohort comprised 39.3% NMs, 29.3% IMs, 2.6% PMs, 24.6% RMs, and 4.2% UMs. Escitalopram MR was 4.5‐fold higher in CYP2C19 UMs vs. PMs without PPIs (P < 0.001), while this difference was 2.8‐fold in pantoprazole users (P < 0.001), and 1.8‐fold in esomeprazole/omeprazole users (P = 0.2). In conclusion, PPIs show significant phenoconversion of CYP2C19‐mediated escitalopram metabolism with esomeprazole/omeprazole exhibiting a stronger effect than pantoprazole. The novel method for identification of interacting comedications in TDM samples illustrates the innovative potential of high‐resolution mass spectrometry in precision medicine.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Implementation of genotype‐guided drug dosing is one of the actions facilitating precision medicine. For some enzymes within the cytochrome P450 (CYP) family, for example, CYP2C19, genotype is a major predictor of metabolizer phenotype. For drugs metabolized by the respective enzymes, preemptive genotyping may predict dosing to the target plasma concentration range. However, in real‐world populations, there is a substantial genotype/phenotype mismatch, which reduces the validity of CYP genotyping in precision medicine. Multiple factors may underlie the mismatch between genotype and metabolizer phenotype, but comedication with drugs causing enzyme inhibition or induction is regarded as an important issue.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

Escitalopram is one of the commonly prescribed antidepressant drugs showing large variability in treatment response, which is significantly attributed to genetic polymorphisms of CYP2C19, the major enzyme involved in escitalopram metabolism. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) exhibit CYP2C19‐inhibitory action to different degrees, so comedication may cause phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism. Using an innovative high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) method, we searched to unveil PPI comedication in therapeutic drug monitoring samples of escitalopram and quantify possible phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism in a population comprising 2818 patients with known CYP2C19 genotype.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Comedication with PPIs was disclosed in ~ 15% of the escitalopram‐treated patients. Significant phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism was seen both for omeprazole/esomeprazole (most) and pantoprazole (least). In patients with genotype‐predicted rapid or normal CYP2C19 metabolism, comedication with omeprazole/esomeprazole caused escitalopram metabolism close to a genotype‐predicted CYP2C19 poor metabolizer.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

In real‐life clinical settings, information on comedications is required for successful application of genotype‐guided dosing. HRMS can effectively unveil interacting comedications in blood samples, which illustrate the innovative potential of this technology in translational and precision medicine.

Escitalopram is one of the world's most frequently used antidepressant drugs (ADs). In 2023, escitalopram was the 14th most commonly prescribed medication overall in the United States, with over 37 million prescriptions. 1 Meta‐analyses have shown that escitalopram is superior to other ADs for the acute phase treatment of major depressive disorder (MDD) in terms of efficacy, acceptability, and tolerability. 2 , 3 However, the individual variability in clinical response of escitalopram is extensive, as for other antidepressants. 4 This is a multifactorial phenomenon, but pharmacokinetic differences causing interindividual variability in blood levels and brain exposure at equal dosing are of substantial importance.

For escitalopram, variability in CYP2C19 metabolism via N‐demethylation is the main reason underlying interindividual differences in blood concentrations at similar dosing. 5 , 6 The variability in CYP2C19 metabolism is determined by multiple factors, where pharmacogenetics (PGx) plays a prominent role. Regarding PGx of CYP2C19 metabolism, diplotypes of alleles encoding absent, normal, or increased enzyme activity divide populations into metabolizer subgroups, that is poor metabolizers (PMs) being homozygous carriers of noncoding variants (e.g., CYP2C19*2), intermediate metabolizers (IMs) carrying one inactive allele, rapid metabolizers who are heterozygous for the increased function CYP2C19*17 allele, and ultrarapid metabolizers (UMs) being homozygous carriers of CYP2C19*17 7 . Finally, individuals defined as CYP2C19 normal metabolizers (NMs) are homozygous carriers of the wild‐type allele (CYP2C19*1). 7 The proportions of various metabolizer subgroups differ a lot across different ethnic groups; for example, the frequency of CYP2C19 PMs is 2–3% in White Europeans compared with 15–20% in East Asians. 8

CYP2C19 catalyzes the major metabolic N‐demethylation pathway of escitalopram with a minor contribution of CYP2D6. 5 On group levels, CYP2C19 PMs and UMs are associated with a respective 60–70% reduced and 30–40% increased formation of N‐desmethylescitalopram compared with CYP2C19 NMs. 5 A N‐desmethylescitalopram‐to‐escitalopram ratio < 0.24 shows 90–100% sensitivity and specificity in predicting CYP2C19 PMs. 9 Thus, the N‐desmethylescitalopram‐to‐escitalopram ratio is a strong biomarker of CYP2C19 metabolism. In addition to being a major determinant of escitalopram metabolism and systemic exposure, CYP2C19 genotype is significantly associated with the outcome of escitalopram treatment in real‐life settings. 6 , 10 , 11 Without any recommendations on preemptive CYP2C19 genotype‐guided escitalopram dosing, variability in CYP2C19‐mediated metabolism is probably causable for the three to fourfold increased odds of switching from escitalopram to other antidepressants in CYP2C19 PMs and UMs compared with NMs. 6 However, around 10–15% of CYP2C19 NMs/IMs also switch from escitalopram to other drugs, 6 which may reflect the high degree of overlap in escitalopram metabolism between genotype‐predicted PMs and IMs.

The extensive variability in CYP2C19 metabolism within genotype‐predicted subgroups may be caused by many different factors, for example, use of interacting comedications or inflammatory responses modulating/inhibiting enzyme activity. 12 , 13 This phenomenon is called phenoconversion, which is particularly relevant in real‐life settings where patients often use multiple medications. In treatment of psychiatric disorders, polypharmacy often occurs due to disease complexity, frequent adverse effects, and somatic comorbidity. As many antidepressants regulate serotonin levels and signaling in the gastrointestinal tract, comedication with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) is relevant to prevent side effects such as dyspepsia, gastroesophageal reflux, or bleeding. 14 , 15 In addition, several PPIs exhibit CYP2C19‐inhibitory action, 16 , 17 and may cause phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism causing a mismatch in genotype–phenotype translation. This is an important issue if implementing preemptive CYP2C19 genotyping to predict dosing of escitalopram to target blood levels.

While pharmacogenetic (PGx) analyses represent a tool applicable for prediction of dose requirements, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is another tool for dose optimization. 18 , 19 , 20 For PGx to be successful, it is crucial that the analyzed gene variants predict pharmacokinetics with high precision. In comparison, TDM is a tool capturing pharmacokinetic variability reflecting both genetic and nongenetic factors. 19 With state‐of‐the‐art analytical equipment, such as ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) linked to high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) detection, it is also possible to get a picture of metabolites and concomitant drugs present in a blood sample during TDM analysis. This provides great potential for studying the impact of drug interactions, phenoconversion, and pharmacokinetic variability in clinical settings.

Today, PGx and TDM are considered complementary tools in precision medicine of psychiatry. 18 , 19 , 20 Thus, laboratory facilities performing both types of analyses are valuable to assess and manage variability on an individual level. Based on PGx/TDM data from a laboratory service, the present study aimed to identify comedication with PPIs and quantify phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism in CYP2C19‐genotyped patients by reprocessing high‐resolution mass spectra from TDM analyses.

METHODS

Patient inclusion

Patients were included from Center for Psychopharmacology at Diakonhjemmet Hospital, Oslo, Norway, which is a combined TDM/pharmacogenetics laboratory facility. The department annually performs around 50,000 TDM analyses of psychiatric drugs and CYP genotyping of ~ 12,000 patients. Accumulated data from the analyses are stored in the same database. The population comprises a mix of individuals from inpatient and outpatient settings.

In the present study, patients who had performed TDM analyses of escitalopram using a UPLC‐Orbitrap‐HRMS method during 2019–2023, and genotyping of CYP2C19 during 2012–2024, were included. Criteria for inclusion comprised (i) detectable serum concentrations of escitalopram and its major metabolite, N‐desmethylescitalopram, (ii) information on prescribed daily escitalopram dose, (iii) 10–30 hours between last dose intake of escitalopram and blood sampling for TDM, and (iv) age > 18 years. Exclusion criteria were concurrent use of potent CYP3A4/CYP2C19 inducers (carbamazepine, phenytoin, and phenobarbital) or the CYP2C19/3A inhibitors (fluvoxamine and fluoxetine), provided on the TDM requisition form. For patients with more than one TDM event fulfilling the criteria, the last eligible TDM analysis was included in the study.

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Committee (# 482562) and the hospital's investigational research board. As only retrospective data were included from the database without any burden or risk, the study was approved without need of patients' consent.

Genotyping

Genotyping of CYP2C19 had been performed as part of clinical follow‐up of the included patients. All had been genotyped at the Center for Psychopharmacology using TaqMan‐based real‐time polymerase chain reaction assays developed for routine pharmacogenetic analysis. The CYP2C19 genotyping panel included the following variant alleles encoding absent CYP2C19 metabolism: CYP2C19*2 (rs4244285), CYP2C19*3 (rs4986893), and CYP2C19*4 (rs28399504). In addition, the panel comprised the gain‐of‐function CYP2C19*17 (rs12248560) variant allele. In the genotype‐to‐phenotype translations, homozygous carriers of nonfunctional variants were defined as PMs, while heterozygous carriers of nonfunctional variants, CYP2C19*1/*2–4 or CYP2C19*17/*2–4 diplotypes were grouped as IMs. Carriers of the CYP2C19*1/*17 diplotype were defined as RMs, while CYP2C19*17/*17 carriers were classified as UMs. The remaining patients genotyped as CYP2C19*1/*1 were defined as NMs.

UHPLC HRMS assay and reprocessing of mass spectra

The TDM method for determination of escitalopram and N‐desmethylescitalopram was based on an ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography‐high‐resolution (Orbitrap) mass spectrometry system. Serum aliquots of 200 μL were mixed with 400 μL acetonitrile/methanol (9/1) containing labeled internal standards (citalopram‐D6 and N‐desmethylcitalopram‐13C‐D3) for sample preparation. Following protein precipitation and centrifugation, supernatant aliquots of 4 μL were injected onto the chromatographic column. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an XBridge BEH C18 column (2.5 μm, 2.1 · 75 mm) (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) with a gradient mobile phase containing acetonitrile (20–95%) and ammonium acetate (pH = 4.8). The total run time was 3.5 minutes with retention times of 1.69 and 1.63 minutes for escitalopram and N‐desmethylescitalopram, respectively. The compounds were quantified in full‐scan acquisition mode using the accurate mass of the protonated molecules (m/z 325.17107 and 311.15542 for escitalopram and N‐desmethylescitalopram, respectively) within a ± 5 ppm mass tolerance, while accurate data‐dependent MS/MS analysis was simultaneously triggered to permit confirmation of identification. The lower limit of quantification was 7 nmol/L for escitalopram and 5 nmol/L for N‐desmethylescitalopram. The TDM assay was accredited for clinical use showing validation parameters < 11% for intra‐ and inter‐run imprecision and inaccuracy.

To identify possible comedication with PPIs, the TraceFinder 5.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) software was applied for reprocessing of full‐scan HRMS files from previous TDM analyses of escitalopram. First, we searched for potential presence of esomeprazole/omeprazole, pantoprazole, and lansoprazole using a targeted reprocessing approach in a subset of random TDM samples used for method development. Based on searches in a reference library (mzCloud, Thermo Fisher) to match exact masses (Mw tolerance < 5 ppm) corresponding to the protonated molecular ions of esomeprazole/omeprazole (m/z 346.12199), pantoprazole (m/z 384.08241), and lansoprazole (m/z 370.08316), retrospective identification of PPI comedication in the UPLC HRMS data files was enabled (Figure 1 ). In addition, MS/MS fragmentation patterns consistent with those of the respective PPIs in the reference library confirmed their identities (Figure 2 ). Finally, identification of specific metabolites of PPIs in the UPLC‐HRMS chromatograms, by the approach described above, provided supplementary evidence for detection of PPIs in the escitalopram samples (Figure 2 ). Among the random test samples, chromatographic peaks consistent with presence of all PPIs were detected by the routine TDM assay of escitalopram.

MS/MS spectra showing fragmentation patterns of lansoprazole (a), pantoprazole (b), and esomeprazole/omeprazole (c), of respective chromatographic peaks shown in Figure 1. Fragmentation patterns (fingerprints) were assessed using mzCloud as a reference library for compound verification.

The method designed to specifically screen for the three PPIs was applied for reprocessing of full‐scan HRMS files from historic TDM analyses of escitalopram in the whole study population to identify cases with PPI comedication. Due to similar physical properties of R‐ and S‐omeprazole, standard chromatography, as used in this assay, could not differentiate between esomeprazole (pure S‐enantiomer) and racemic omeprazole (R/S mixture). Therefore, cases with chromatographic peaks consistent with the presence of omeprazole were merged into a common subgroup comprising users of either esomeprazole or racemic omeprazole.

Measures and statistics

After reprocessing MS spectra, the included escitalopram‐treated patients were divided into nonusers and users of the various PPIs and further stratified by CYP2C19 genotype. Then, N‐desmethylescitalopram‐to‐escitalopram metabolic ratios (MR) and dose‐adjusted serum concentrations (CD) of escitalopram were compared between CYP2C19 PMs and NMs, IMs, RMs, and UMs within subgroups of nonusers and users of the different PPIs. The main outcome measures were–fold difference in MRs (primary) and CDs (secondary) of escitalopram between CYP2C19 UMs and PMs among nonusers and users of different PPIs.

GraphPad Prism version 10.2.3 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) was used for statistical analyses. Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test was applied for comparisons of MRs and CDs of escitalopram between CYP2C19 subgroups. The same tests were used for comparisons of continuous, descriptive variables within the study population. P < 0.05 defined a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Patient population

A total of 3,245 patients were scrutinized for possible inclusion in the study, whereof 2,818 patients fulfilled the criteria. Among the included patients, 2,373 received escitalopram treatment without PPIs, while the remaining patients (n = 443, 15.7%) were comedicated with pantoprazole (n = 255), esomeprazole/omeprazole (n = 179), or lansoprazole (n = 11).

The following numbers of patients were assigned to various genotype‐predicted CYP2C19 subgroups regardless of PPI comedication: 1106 (39.2%) NMs, 827 (29.4%) IMs, 74 (2.6%) PMs, 693 (24.6%) RMs, and 118 (4.2%) UMs. These frequencies align with populations comprising mainly individuals of Caucasian ethnicity, which is the case in Norway. In the study cohort, the female‐to‐male ratios ranged between 1.5‐ and 2.5‐fold across different subgroups (Table 1 ). Age was generally lower in patients without PPIs (median values 40–45 years) compared with those comedicated with PPIs (median values 48–72 years) (Table 1 ).

Effect of PPIs on escitalopram MR

For patients treated with escitalopram without PPIs, the MR was 4.5‐fold higher in CYP2C19 UMs vs. PMs (Figure 3 a, Table 2 , P < 0.001) and showed a stepwise increase by increasing metabolizer phenotype of CYP2C19 (Figure 3 a ). The same trend, although less steep, was present for escitalopram MRs in patients comedicated with pantoprazole (Figure 3 b ), while in patients using esomeprazole/omeprazole differences were quite similar between PMs and the various subgroups of non‐PMs (Figure 3 c ). The difference in MRs between CYP2C19 UMs and PMs ranged 2.8‐fold (P < 0.001) and 1.8‐fold (P = 0.2) for patients comedicated with pantoprazole and esomeprazole/omeprazole, respectively (Table 2 , Figure 3 b,c ).

Graphs show box plots with median and range indicated. In the UM subgroup, one patient had a MR value above 3 (3.64).

Aligning with the absence of CYP2C19 metabolism in PMs, the escitalopram MR estimates were similar for PM subgroups regardless of PPI comedication (Table 2 ). The MR estimates were similar for CYP2C19 IMs, NMs, RMs, and UMs subgroups within the esomeprazole/omeprazole‐treated subpopulation (Table 2 , Figure 3 c ), reflecting substantial CYP2C19 phenoconversion. For patients comedicated with pantoprazole, escitalopram MR estimates were higher than observed for esomeprazole/omeprazole‐treated patients and did not show the same extent of phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism.

Among the 11 patients (nine females) comedicated with lansoprazole, there were no CYP2C19 PMs, three IMs, four NMs, two RMs, and two UMs. The median values of escitalopram MR observations in the limited number of lansoprazole users were 0.26 for IMs, 0.30 for NMs, 0.29 for RMs, and 0.31 for UMs. Due to the limited number of patients comedicated with lansoprazole, these are excluded from statistical comparisons and assessments of CYP2C19 inhibition.

Effect of PPIs on escitalopram CD ratio

CD ratio of escitalopram ranged 3.2‐fold between CYP2C19 PMs and UMs in patients not using PPIs (Table 2 , Figure 4a ; P < 0001). In pantoprazole‐treated patients, the CD ratio of escitalopram differed 4.4‐fold between PMs and UMs (Figure 4b ; P < 0001) compared with a nonsignificant, 1.4‐fold difference (Figure 4c ; P = 0.6) in patients comedicated with esomeprazole/omeprazole (Table 2 ).

DISCUSSION

The present study represents a unique example where HRMS can be utilized to unveil use of interacting comedications causing phenoconversion and reduced precision of genotype‐predicted drug metabolizer status in real‐life settings. Using the novel method for reprocessing full‐scan mass spectra from TDM analyses of escitalopram to identify comedication with PPIs, data were provided to quantify phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism in relation to CYP2C19 genotype. The findings show that comedication with esomeprazole/omeprazole causes a stronger inhibition of escitalopram metabolism than pantoprazole and that the inhibitory effect is dependent on CYP2C19 genotype, that is, with reductions in escitalopram N‐demethylation ranging from ~ −70% in CYP2C19 UMs to −35% in IMs during comedication with esomeprazole/omeprazole. Aligning with absent metabolism in CYP2C19 PMs, comedication esomeprazole/omeprazole or pantoprazole showed no effect on escitalopram N‐demethylation in this subgroup.

CYP2C19 is the major enzyme involved in N‐demethylation of escitalopram, with only a minor contribution of CYP2D6. 5 It has been shown that this metabolic ratio has a sensitivity of 100% in detecting CYP2C19 PM at a cutoff of < 0.24 providing a specificity of 88%. 9 Thus, the N‐desmethylescitalopram‐to‐escitalopram ratio is an excellent biomarker of CYP2C19 metabolism supporting that the change in metabolic ratio by PPIs is due to CYP2C19 phenoconversion. This phenoconversion by comedication inhibiting CYP2C19 is probably of major importance for the high interindividual variability in escitalopram metabolism observed within CYP2C19 genotype‐predicted metabolizer subgroups in clinical practice, which is more than 10‐fold. 5 , 6 , 21 , 22 The substantial phenoconversion that may occur due to interacting comedications illustrates an important weakness related to the use of CYP genotyping in precision medicine, as a tool for predicting dose requirements of relevant drugs in psychiatry and other areas.

The drug combinations investigated in the present study are indeed of clinical relevance. SSRIs are associated with an increased risk of developing gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), erosive esophagitis, and other related conditions, 23 where escitalopram and sertraline are the least tolerated antidepressants regarding gastrointestinal adverse effects. 24 The increased risks of gastrointestinal side effects are linked to reduced reuptake of serotonin in enterocytes and hence increased serotoninergic activity, which may increase gastric motility and secretion, as well as influence gastric acid and mucus production. 25 , 26 These gastrointestinal side effects of SSRI treatment are probably a reason for the common use of PPIs disclosed in the escitalopram‐treated population of the present study.

The significantly reduced formation of N‐desmethylescitalopram in CYP2C19 PMs was reflected by the 3.2‐fold increase in dose‐adjusted serum concentration of escitalopram compared with UMs in patients without PPI comedication. Despite that the observed daily dose of escitalopram was lower in PMs vs. NMs and UMs (10 vs. 15 mg/day), serum concentration of escitalopram was doubled in PMs. We have previously shown that CYP2C19 metabolism and serum concentration of escitalopram is significantly associated with treatment failure, as measured by switch to other ADs. 6 For CYP2C19 PMs, the odds ratio for escitalopram switch was 3.3‐fold higher vs. CYP2C19 NMs. 6 The present study, which shows that both CYP2C19 IM, NM, RM, and UM patients comedicated with esomeprazole/omeprazole obtain comparable serum concentrations of escitalopram as for CYP2C19 PMs without PPIs. The patient proportion comedicated with esomeprazole/omeprazole comprised 6.4% of the total study population in the present study, implying that phenoconversion of escitalopram metabolism by esomeprazole/omeprazole comedication might be of similar clinical importance for treatment outcome of escitalopram in real‐life settings as the genotype‐predicted CYP2C19 PM phenotype in Caucasian patients (2–3% of the population).

It is known that various PPIs exhibit different CYP2C19‐inhibitory potency. 16 , 17 Comedication with pantoprazole was identified in around 10% of the 2,818 included patients. Pantoprazole is a less potent inhibitor of CYP2C19 metabolism than esomeprazole/omeprazole, 16 , 17 so the higher difference in escitalopram MR between CYP2C19 UMs and PMs for pantoprazole (2.8‐fold) compared with esomeprazole/omeprazole (1.8‐fold) was expected. However, the 2.8‐fold difference in MR between UMs and PMs during use of pantoprazole compared with the 4.5‐fold range in MR between UMs and PMs among patients without PPIs suggests that pantoprazole's CYP2C19‐inhibitory potential could cause interactions of clinical relevance in certain patients. Regarding the CYP2C19‐inhibitory potency of racemic omeprazole vs. esomeprazole (S‐enantiomer), the racemic mixture is reported to exert the strongest inhibition. 16 , 17 This may have caused uncertainty in quantifications and subgroup comparisons of phenoconversion effects among patients comedicated with esomeprazole/omeprazole, as the TDM method used in the present study did detect R‐ and S‐omeprazole separately. To identify whether racemic omeprazole or esomeprazole is being used, enantioselective liquid chromatography would be required.

The significantly, 1.5‐fold higher CD ratio of escitalopram observed during pantoprazole comedication in patients not expressing CYP2C19 (i.e., PMs) might indicate that pantoprazole inhibit non‐CYP2C19 metabolism of escitalopram. Pantoprazole metabolism is itself highly dependent on CYP2C19 phenotype, as reflected by the > 5‐fold higher concentration in CYP2C19 PMs vs. NMs. 27 , 28 However, despite increased exposure and interaction potential in CYP2C19 PMs, limited inhibition of CYP enzymes other than CYP2C19, 29 makes it is unlikely that pantoprazole is capable of inhibiting secondary enzymes involved in metabolism of escitalopram, such as CYP3A4. 30 Although the interaction mechanism(s) underlying the increased CD ratio of escitalopram during pantoprazole comedication, exclusively seen in CYP2C19 PMs, comedicated with pantoprazole is unclear, the example shows the innovative aspect of considering drug–drug interactions at a patient level rather than a population level when developing algorithms aiming to predict dose requirements and treatment outcomes in precision medicine.

We were unable to clarify if physicians requesting PGx and TDM analyses of escitalopram were aware of comedication with PPIs, since medication lists are usually not included when analyses are requested. Therefore, information about possible interactions with PPIs is not part of the laboratory assessment reports, which may imply that phenoconversion of CYP2C19 metabolism by PPIs is overlooked in clinical practice. For patients comedicated with omeprazole/esomeprazole, the present study showed that dose‐adjusted escitalopram concentrations did not differ significantly across CYP2C19 genotype groups. When considering genotype‐guided dosing in precision medicine, a mismatch between genotype and phenotype due to esomeprazole/omeprazole comedication would probably be largest in CYP2C19 UMs and RMs, where the physicians may be recommended to prescribe increased doses causing unexpectedly high concentrations and risk of side effects because of phenoconversion.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a study has applied reprocessing of high‐resolution mass spectra from TDM analyses to identify concurrent use of interacting drugs. The concept shows the power of this bioanalytical approach in assessing potential drug‐drug interactions in the setting of polypharmacy and exposure to agents which may affect treatment outcome. However, a limitation of the study was a lack of information on additional factors that may influence metabolism of escitalopram. These factors comprise organ function, disease state, and inflammation, as well as the possible presence of interacting comedications not screened for, which may all cause both intra‐ and intergenotype variability in escitalopram metabolism. Furthermore, clinical information on potential lack of effects and adverse effects would have been favorable, but unfortunately such details are usually missing on the TDM requisition forms filled out by the physicians. However, the large size of the study population outweighs many of these limitations when estimating the effect of CYP2C19 genotype and PPIs on escitalopram metabolism.

In conclusion, comedication with PPIs is frequent among escitalopram‐treated patients. PPIs show significant phenoconversion of CYP2C19‐mediated escitalopram metabolism in a genotype‐dependent fashion, with esomeprazole/omeprazole exhibiting a stronger effect than pantoprazole. The CYP2C19 phenoconversion is probably of major importance for the high interindividual variability in genotype‐predicted CYP2C19 metabolism and treatment outcome of escitalopram in clinical practice. The novel method for identification of interacting comedications in TDM samples illustrates the innovative potential of HRMS in translational and precision medicine.
