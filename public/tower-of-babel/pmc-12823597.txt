Data-driven vehicle stability control via co-simulation of digital twin and constrained MPC
Authors: Mu Lin, Zhengwei Zhang, Min Huang, Yu Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the rapid advancement of intelligent connected vehicles and autonomous driving technologies, vehicle lateral stability under high-speed or emergency steering conditions has become a critical safety concern. Traditional control strategies often exhibit limited performance in complex driving scenarios. This study proposes a novel co-simulation framework integrating digital twin technology with constrained Model Predictive Control (MPC) to enhance lateral stability and torque distribution. A high-fidelity digital twin model was constructed to improve real-time accuracy, while an MPC-based controller was designed to optimize handling under extreme conditions. Experimental results demonstrated improved performance: lateral stability error was reduced by 62.5%, and yaw rate error by 57.1%, compared to traditional methods. The key novelty lies in the dynamic, data-driven integration of the digital twin for real-time MPC optimization. These findings provide a robust theoretical foundation and technical support for intelligent vehicle development.

Introduction

The rapid development of intelligent connected vehicles and autonomous driving has heightened concerns about vehicle lateral stability during high-speed or emergency steering. This issue critically affects both comfort and safety, especially under extreme conditions such as low-adhesion roads or sudden maneuvers, where vehicle dynamics become highly nonlinear. Traditional control methods, including PID and Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR), often struggle in these scenarios due to limitations in handling constraints and nonlinearities1– 2. In contrast, Model Predictive Control (MPC) offers advantages in multi-objective optimization but faces challenges in model accuracy and real-time performance3– 4. Recent advances in digital twin technology enable high-fidelity virtual models that reflect physical systems in real time, allowing data-driven optimization5– 6. However, the co-simulation of digital twin with constrained MPC for vehicle stability control remains underexplored.

In recent years, digital twin technology, as an emerging virtual-physical fusion approach, has demonstrated unique advantages in vehicle dynamics control. By constructing high-fidelity virtual models, this technology enables real-time reflection of physical system dynamics and optimizes control strategies through data-driven methods7. For vehicle lateral stability control, digital twin technology not only enhances model accuracy but also refines control algorithms via real-time data feedback, thereby improving system robustness and responsiveness8. Furthermore, it facilitates optimized torque distribution through virtual-physical interaction, further enhancing stability under complex operating conditions9.

In light of the current research landscape in vehicle lateral stability control, this study is dedicated to integrating digital twin technology with Model Predictive Control, proposing an innovative strategy for vehicle lateral stability control and torque distribution optimization. This study makes three key contributions: (1) the development of a high-fidelity digital twin model for lateral dynamics with real-time data-driven updates; (2) the design of an MPC-based stability controller that optimizes handling under extreme conditions; and (3) the implementation of a torque distribution strategy using the digital twin to enhance stability and efficiency. By leveraging the digital twin for real-time model refinement, our approach addresses the limitations of traditional MPC, offering improved accuracy and adaptability. The integrated control strategy seamlessly switches between PID, LQR, and MPC based on stability criteria, ensuring robust performance. This work provides a comprehensive solution for vehicle stability, with potential applications in intelligent transportation systems.

Vehicle dynamics model and digital twin model construction

Vehicle dynamics model

In the process of constructing the vehicle dynamics model, primary emphasis is placed on the detailed derivation of a nonlinear two-degree-of-freedom (2-DOF) vehicle model10. This model comprehensively captures the two core degrees of freedom governing lateral motion and yaw motion. By establishing a system of differential equations for these degrees of freedom, the model accurately describes the dynamic response characteristics of the vehicle under complex and variable driving conditions. To facilitate controller design and implementation, the nonlinear model is linearized and transformed into a state-space representation. This critical step not only significantly reduces model complexity but also enhances the applicability of modern control theory, laying a solid foundation for in-depth analysis and design.

Subsequently, to meet the practical requirements of digital computation, the linearized state-space equations are discretized, yielding a discrete state-space model11. This discretized model serves as a crucial mathematical framework for the subsequent design of the Model Predictive Control controller. Notably, the predictive model, as the core component of the MPC algorithm, must strike a balance between precision and computational complexity. Here, the nonlinear 2-DOF model is adopted as the predictive model to achieve an optimal trade-off between accuracy and real-time performance. Torque distribution optimization strategy

Tire friction ellipse theory

In the study of torque distribution optimization strategies, the primary task is to gain an in-depth understanding of the theoretical implications of the tire friction ellipse and its critical role in vehicle lateral stability control29-32. The tire friction ellipse, as a geometric model characterizing the combined limits of longitudinal and lateral tire forces under specific road conditions, provides an essential theoretical foundation for vehicle dynamics control. Through systematic analysis of the coupling relationships among longitudinal tire forces, lateral tire forces, and adhesion forces, the physical boundary conditions and potential optimization dimensions for torque distribution are determined, thereby offering theoretical support for subsequent control strategy development.

It is particularly noteworthy that, as the sole force transmission interface between the vehicle and the ground, the multi-dimensional forces and moments within the tire contact patch collectively determine the vehicle’s driving, braking, steering, and load-bearing performance. Therefore, the precise calculation of tire forces is a core element in ensuring the effectiveness of lateral stability control, and its accuracy directly influences the overall performance of the control system. Fundamentals of phase plane stability analysis

Phase Plane Analysis as an effective tool for stability analysis of nonlinear systems, visually reveals the dynamic behavior characteristics of a system by depicting the trajectories of its state variables40. Traditional phase plane stability criterion methods primarily rely on the analysis of equilibrium points and limit cycles, where system stability is evaluated by observing whether the trajectories converge to equilibrium points41. However, when applied to vehicle lateral stability problems, traditional phase plane analysis methods exhibit certain limitations. On one hand, these methods are typically based on linear or weakly nonlinear system assumptions, making it difficult to accurately capture the strongly nonlinear characteristics of vehicles under extreme operating conditions. On the other hand, traditional methods are highly sensitive to system parameters, particularly under complex scenarios such as fluctuating road adhesion coefficients or vehicle load transfer, which may compromise the accuracy of stability assessment.

To address these shortcomings, an Improved Phase Plane Stability Criterion is proposed. This method significantly enhances the precision and robustness of phase plane analysis by introducing dynamic boundary conditions and a nonlinear compensation mechanism. Specifically, the improved criterion dynamically adjusts the boundary conditions of the phase plane based on real-time vehicle operating conditions. This dynamic adjustment is grounded in the Tire Friction Ellipse Theory, where the phase plane boundaries are precisely regulated by calculating the resultant force of tire longitudinal force (Fx) and lateral force (Fy) in real time:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_{x,y}}=\sqrt {F_{x}^{2}+F_{y}^{2}} \leqslant \mu {F_z}$$\end{document}

Herein, µ represents the road adhesion coefficient, and Fz denotes the tire vertical force. By dynamically adjusting the boundary conditions, the proposed method can more accurately reflect the stability margin of the vehicle under extreme operating conditions. The improved phase plane stability criterion method also incorporates a nonlinear compensation mechanism, which enhances the accuracy of phase plane analysis by compensating for the nonlinear characteristics of the system. Integrated control strategy and stability criteria

Improved phase plane stability criteria

In the field of vehicle lateral stability control, the traditional phase plane method, while capable of revealing the stability characteristics of the system to some extent, exhibits evident limitations—particularly in complex operating conditions where it struggles to accurately capture dynamic behavioral changes of the system. In light of this, an improved phase plane stability criterion method is proposed. This approach ingeniously incorporates a digital twin model, enabling real-time updates of the stability region on the phase plane, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of vehicle lateral stability state assessment. This innovation not only improves the general applicability of the stability criterion but also provides a more robust and reliable foundation for the design and optimization of subsequent control strategies.

As an intuitive graphical method for second-order and lower nonlinear systems, the phase plane method offers the advantage of bypassing the need to solve complex nonlinear equations. Deficiencies of traditional phase plane stability criteria

The traditional phase plane analysis method, while offering advantages such as simplicity in implementation and intuitive visualization, still exhibits certain limitations. Specifically, the conventional approach only considers the influence of the sideslip angle β when determining the stability region boundary, while neglecting the amplitude constraint of the yaw rate ω imposed by the tire lateral adhesion limit. This simplification may lead to identified stability regions that include hazardous conditions where ω exceeds safe thresholds.

By connecting these boundary points, the amplitude limit of ω is established, as depicted by the blue curve in Fig. 5. This improved method not only retains the advantages of traditional phase plane analysis but also enhances the accuracy and reliability of stability region delineation by incorporating the amplitude constraint of ω.

The traditional phase plane analysis method exhibits inherent limitations in vehicle stability assessment. Research indicates that this approach may yield scenarios where the sideslip angle β remains within the stable range while the yaw rate ω exceeds safety thresholds, highlighting the necessity for boundary modification of the stability region. Through systematic investigation, the stability area defined by conventional methods can be subdivided into two characteristic subregions (as shown in Fig. 6): Region ① represents stable conditions where β lies within the saddle point boundary, while Region ② corresponds to cases where β exceeds the saddle point boundary yet maintains stability. These two subregions collectively constitute the stability set defined by traditional phase plane analysis.

However, in-depth studies reveal that the stability of Region ② holds only theoretical significance and should not be considered part of the stable region in practical control applications. Phase plane analysis demonstrates that when β is small, the vehicle can effectively suppress the growth of the sideslip angle while maintaining the desired yaw response (ensuring handling performance), thereby achieving better stability constraints. However, when β increases, a significant control conflict arises between handling performance and stability, where pursuing the desired response may compromise vehicle stability.

Quantitative analysis of the phase plane reveals that in Region ①, β is negative, and maintaining the desired response contributes to stability preservation. In contrast, in Region ②, β is slightly positive, presenting an inherent contradiction between handling and stability.

Based on the above analysis, the stability region division of the phase plane is further optimized, and the phase plane is divided into four regions as shown in Fig. 7:

Within the improved phase plane analysis framework, the study area can be divided into four characteristic subregions: Region ② represents a critical zone where stability conditions are satisfied but the yaw rate constraint is exceeded; Region ③ denotes a transitional area that neither meets the coordinated requirements of handling stability nor remains in a stable state; Region ④ explicitly characterizes the unstable state of the system. In contrast, Region ① is identified as the optimal stability zone obtained by rectifying the limitations of the conventional phase plane method. Geometrically, the boundary of Region ① is determined by two saddle points in the phase plane and the critical yaw rate, which can be mathematically expressed as:44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} {\beta _{saddle\_l}} \leqslant \beta \leqslant {\beta _{saddle\_r}} \\ {\omega _{\hbox{min} }} \leqslant \omega \leqslant {\omega _{\hbox{max} }} \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

Research indicates that the stability characteristics of the phase plane are primarily governed by three key parameters: road adhesion coefficient, vehicle speed, and front wheel steering angle. Further analysis reveals that the stability characteristics of Region ① carry distinct physical significance: when the system state lies within this region, both the lateral stability of the vehicle and the desired handling performance can be guaranteed. In contrast, although Region ② satisfies the stability condition, it still poses safety risks in practical applications due to exceeding the yaw rate constraint. Regions ③ and ④ represent transitional instability and complete instability characteristics, respectively. This refined classification method enables a more accurate description of vehicle stability characteristics under different operating conditions, providing more reliable guidance for the design of stability control systems.

To fully exploit the respective advantages of these control algorithms, an innovative integrated control strategy based on an improved phase plane stability criterion is proposed. By introducing the concept of system stability control weighting, this strategy achieves smooth switching between energy-efficient control and stability control under different operating conditions. Specifically, when the vehicle is in a stable state, the system tends to prioritize PID or LQR control algorithms for their superior energy-saving performance. However, once a challenge to the vehicle’s lateral stability is detected, the system automatically switches to the MPC control algorithm to ensure handling stability and driving safety. The integrated control strategy demonstrates notable advantages in enhancing system flexibility and robustness while achieving global optimization of vehicle dynamic performance.

Analysis of control strategy switching smoothness

In the integrated control strategy, the switching logic among PID, LQR, and MPC relies on the stability weight ρ. However, the switching process may induce transient vehicle dynamics responses, such as torque mutations or attitude fluctuations, which adversely affect ride comfort and handling stability. To address this issue, this paper conducts an in-depth analysis of the smoothness of control strategy switching.

Fig. 14Comparison of control strategy switching processes.

Comparison of control strategy switching processes.

First, a switching scenario is designed to simulate an abrupt change in the stability weight ρ from 0.3 to 0.7, corresponding to the transition from PID-dominant to MPC-dominant control modes. A vehicle model is constructed using high-precision simulation tools such as CarSim and Simulink, recording vehicle states during the switching instant, including longitudinal torque, lateral acceleration, yaw rate, and sideslip angle. Simulation results in Fig. 14 demonstrate that the direct switching strategy causes torque fluctuations reaching ± 10 N·m, while the peak lateral acceleration change rate exceeds 0.3 m/s², indicating significant transient impacts during the switching process. To enhance switching smoothness, a smooth transition function such as the Sigmoid function was introduced to filter the stability weight ρ. This function achieves gradual adjustment of control outputs by smoothly varying the ρ value. After improvement, torque fluctuations were reduced to within ± 2 N·m, and the peak lateral acceleration change rate decreased to below 0.1 m/s². Furthermore, the effectiveness of the smooth transition under real vehicle conditions was validated through real-time data acquisition via a digital twin platform (Fig. 15). In double lane-change tests, the root mean square value of the weighted acceleration during switching, calculated according to ISO 2631-1 standard, remained below 0.5 m/s², while the sideslip angle rate was maintained within 5°/s, meeting the comfort and stability requirements for engineering applications.

Application of the digital twin platform

The digital twin platform, serving as the foundational support for cyber-physical integration technology, plays an important role in the implementation of integrated control strategies. By constructing a virtual mirror that maintains high fidelity with the physical vehicle, the platform can capture and reflect the dynamic behavioral characteristics of the vehicle in real time. Leveraging advanced data-driven techniques, the digital twin platform further optimizes the design and execution of control strategies, significantly advancing the development of vehicle control technologies.

The core functionalities of the digital twin platform encompass real-time data acquisition and updating, simulation-based optimization and validation, as well as cyber-physical fusion control. Specifically, through a densely distributed sensor network, the platform can continuously acquire various operational data of the vehicle, including but not limited to key metrics such as lateral acceleration and yaw rate. These data are then used to dynamically update the state parameters of the virtual model, ensuring a high degree of consistency between the virtual model and the physical vehicle. This capability lays a solid foundation for subsequent simulation-based optimization.

During the simulation optimization and validation phase, the digital twin platform enables extensive virtual experiments, where control strategy parameters are adjusted and their effectiveness and robustness are verified. This process not only reduces the costs and risks associated with physical testing but also significantly enhances the efficiency of control strategy optimization. More innovatively, the platform achieves cyber-physical fusion control by deploying the optimized control strategies in real time to the actual vehicle, ensuring that the desired performance is achieved in practical applications. This novel control strategy design workflow not only improves design efficiency but also ensures continuous optimization and adaptation through real-time data feedback and adjustments.

Unlike traditional MPC approaches that often rely on fixed models and lack adaptability, the proposed digital twin-enhanced MPC continuously updates the predictive model using real-world data, leading to better performance under uncertain and varying road conditions. This represents a significant advancement over conventional control methods, which struggle with nonlinearities and real-time constraints.

The current study assumes the availability of high-frequency sensor data and a reliable communication link between the physical vehicle and the digital twin. Model simplifications, such as the use of a 2-DOF vehicle model, may limit accuracy in highly dynamic scenarios. Additionally, the control strategy’s performance depends on the accuracy of tire force estimation and road adhesion identification.

Looking ahead, Several directions warrant further investigation: Enhancing the digital twin model to incorporate more complex vehicle dynamics and environmental factors. Extending the framework to multi-vehicle cooperative control in connected vehicle environments. Integrating artificial intelligence and reinforcement learning for adaptive and predictive stability control. Exploring real-world deployment challenges, such as computational efficiency and robustness under communication delays.
