Non-asymptotic quantum communication on lossy transmission lines with memory
Francesco Anna Mele, Giovanni Barbarino, Vittorio Giovannetti, Marco Fanizza

Abstract
Non-asymptotic quantum Shannon theory analyses how to transmit quantum information across a quantum channel as efficiently as possible within a specified error tolerance, given access to a finite, fixed, number of channel uses. In a recent work, we derived computable lower bounds on the non-asymptotic capacities of memoryless bosonic Gaussian channels. In this work, we extend these results to the non-Markovian bosonic Gaussian channel introduced in F. A. Mele, G. D. Palma, M. Fanizza, V. Giovannetti, and L. Lami IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 70 (12), 8844-8869 (2024), which describes non-Markovian effects in optical fibres and is a non-Markovian generalisation of the pure loss channel. This allows us to determine how many uses of a non-Markovian optical fibre are sufficient in order to transmit k k qubits, distil k k ebits, or generate k k secret-key bits up to a given error tolerance ε \varepsilon . To perform our analysis, we prove novel properties of singular values of Toeplitz matrices, providing an error bound on the convergence rate of the celebrated Avram–Parter’s theorem, which we regard as a new tool of independent interest for the field of quantum information theory and matrix analysis.

II.1
Non-asymptotic capacities of memoryless channels

In the context of quantum Shannon theory
[
43
,
62
]
, the noise affecting a memoryless communication line can be modelled via a single quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
, which acts on each of the transmissions signals. Specifically, a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
is a linear, completely positive, trace-preserving transformation from the set of input quantum states to the set of output quantum states
[
43
,
62
]
. Additionally,
n
uses of such a memoryless communication line are modelled via the tensor-product quantum channel
\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}
. More precisely, any (possibly correlated) input state
\rho^{(n)}
of the first
n
signals are mapped to the (potentially degraded) output state via the following relation:

\displaystyle\rho^{(n)}\mapsto\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}\!\left(\rho^{(n)}\right)\;.

(1)

The quality of the quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
can be characterised using figures of merit tailored to the specific communication task under consideration. In this work, we focus on three communication tasks:

•

Qubit distribution
: The goal is to reliably transmit an arbitrary quantum state, possibly entangled with an auxiliary system, across the quantum channel;

•

Entanglement distribution assisted by two-way classical communication
: The goal is to generate two-qubit Bell pairs (referred to as
ebits
) between two parties connected by the quantum channel, exploiting the additional resource of a two-way classical communication line;

•

Secret-key distribution assisted by two-way classical communication
: The goal is to generate secret keys shared between two parties linked by the quantum channel, exploiting the additional resource of a two-way classical communication line;

For each of these communication tasks, one can define a proper notion of
non-asymptotic capacity

[
43
,
53
]
:

•

the
n
-shot quantum capacity
Q^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
is defined as the maximum number of qubits that can be transmitted up to error
\varepsilon
across
n
uses of
\mathcal{N}
;

•

the
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity
Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
is defined as the maximum number of ebits that one can distil up to error
\varepsilon
using
n
uses of
\mathcal{N}
and arbitrary LOCCs (Local Operation and Classical Communication operations) ;

•

the
n
-shot secret-key capacity
K^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
is defined as the maximum number of secret-key bits that one can distil up to error
\varepsilon
using
n
uses of
\mathcal{N}
and arbitrary LOCCs.

For the exact definitions of the errors
\varepsilon
associated with each of the these quantum communication tasks, as well as rigorous definitions of the capacities
Q^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
,
Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
,
K^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})
,
we refer to Ref.
[
53
]
.

The
asymptotic capacities
are defined by taking the limit of large channel uses (
n\rightarrow\infty
) and vanishing error (
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
) of the regularised version of their non-asymptotic counterparts
[
62
,
43
]
. That is, the
quantum capacity

Q(\mathcal{N})
,
two-way quantum capacity

Q_{2}(\mathcal{N})
, and
secret-key capacity

K(\mathcal{N})
are defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})}{n}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle Q_{2}(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})}{n}\,,

\displaystyle K(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{K^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})}{n}\,.

In other words, the quantum capacity of
\mathcal{N}
is the maximum achievable rate of qubits which can be transmitted with vanishing error in the asymptotic limit of infinitely many uses of the channel. Analogously, the two-way quantum capacity (resp. secret-key capacity) of
\mathcal{N}
is the maximum achievable rate of ebits (resp. secret-key bits) which can be distilled via LOCCs with vanishing error in the asymptotic limit of infinitely many uses of the channel. Note that a simple resource counting argument establishes the following ordering among the non-asymptotic capacities:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\leq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})\leq K^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{N})\;,

(3)

and, in particular, the same holds for their asymptotic counterparts, i.e.

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ(\mathcal{N})

\displaystyle\leq Q_{2}(\mathcal{N})\leq K(\mathcal{N})\;.

(4)

II.2
Non-asymptotic capacities of non-Markovian channels

When memory effects are present, the noise affecting a given input signal depends on the specific state of the previously sent signals
[
11
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. In this context, the input-output relations are no longer governed by a single quantum channel
\mathcal{N}
. Instead, they are described by a family of quantum channels

\displaystyle{\cal F}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{N}^{(1)},\mathcal{N}^{(2)},\cdots,\mathcal{N}^{(n)},\cdots\}\;,

(5)

where
\mathcal{N}^{(n)}
replaces
\mathcal{N}^{\otimes n}
in Eq. (
1
) to model the input-output

n
signals:

\displaystyle\rho^{(n)}\mapsto\mathcal{N}^{(n)}(\rho^{(n)})\;,

(6)

where
\rho^{(n)}
denotes the initial state of the first
n
signals. Although the expressions of the various maps in
{\cal F}
depend upon the specific details of the communication line, they must always satisfy the following consistency requirement, which ensures that future transmissions do not affect past signaling events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{(n)}={\mathcal{T}}_{n+1}\circ\mathcal{N}^{(n+1)}\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(7)

where
{\cal T_{n+1}}
represents the partial trace with respect to the
(n+1)
-th output signal. In this non-Markovian context, the
n
-shot capacities of the communication line can be
formally identified as the one-shot capacities of the quantum channel
\mathcal{N}^{(n)}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q^{(\varepsilon,1)}\!\left(\mathcal{N}^{(n)}\right)\,,

(8)

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}\!\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,1)}\!\left(\mathcal{N}^{(n)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle K^{(\varepsilon,n)}\!\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq K^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\mathcal{N}^{(n)}\right)\,.

Additionally, as before, the asymptotic capacities are defined in terms of the regularised version of the corresponding non-asymptotic capacities in the limit of
large
n
and vanishing
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q^{(\varepsilon,n)}\left({\cal F}\right)}{n}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle Q_{2}\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}\left({\cal F}\right)}{n}\,,

\displaystyle K\left({\cal F}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{K^{(\varepsilon,n)}\left({\cal F}\right)}{n}\,.

II.3
Pure loss channel

Let us briefly give some preliminaries about continuous-variable systems; for further details, we refer to Ref.
[
2
]
. A continuous-variable system with
n
modes is characterised by the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which consists of all square-integrable, complex-valued functions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
2
]
. In quantum optics, such systems are commonly used to describe the electromagnetic field
[
2
,
3
]
. By definition, a state is said to be
Gaussian
if it is a Gibbs state of a quadratic hamiltonian in the position and momentum operators of the system
[
2
]
. Moreover, a
bosonic
Gaussian channel

[
2
]
is a quantum channel that maps the set of Gaussian states into itself.

The most important Gaussian channel is arguably the
pure loss channel
, a single-mode bosonic Gaussian channel that models the phenomenon of photon loss in memoryless optical links
[
2
]
. The pure loss channel, denoted as
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, is characterised by a single parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
, which represents the ratio between the output energy and the input energy of the transmitted signals.
Mathematically,
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
acts on an input single-mode state
\rho
by mixing it in a beam splitter (i.e. a semi-reflective mirror) with an environmental mode
{E}
initialised in the vacuum state
|0\rangle_{E}
:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Tr_{E}\left[U_{\lambda}^{SE}\big(\rho\otimes\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}_{E}\big)(U_{\lambda}^{SE})^{\dagger}\right]\,,

(10)

where
U_{\lambda}\coloneqq\exp\!\left[\arccos\sqrt{\lambda}\left(a^{\dagger}b-a\,b^{\dagger}\right)\right]
denotes the beam splitter unitary of transmissivity
\lambda
, with
a
and
b
being the annihilation operators of the signal and of the environment, respectively, and where
\Tr_{E}
denotes the partial trace with respect to the environment — see Fig.
1
for a pictorial representation.

Note that the pure loss channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
is noiseless for
\lambda=1
(it equals the identity channel), while it is completely noisy for
\lambda=0
(it maps any input state into the vacuum). It is also worth recalling that

]

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{1}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{2}}=\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\qquad\forall\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in[0,1]\;.

(11)

Both the asymptotic and non-asymptotic capacities of the pure loss channel have been extensively analysed

26

]
. Specifically, the (asymptotic) quantum capacity, two-way quantum capacity, and secret-key capacity of the pure loss channel are given by the following simple expressions:

,
26
]

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)&\text{if $\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},1]$ ,}\\
0&\text{if $\lambda\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]$ ,}\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle Q_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=K(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})=\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\right)\,,

Moreover, given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity and the
n
-shot secret-key capacity can be lower bounded as
[
53
]

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptK^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\geq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})\geq nQ_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{6}\,3\,(4-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(2-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\,,

(13)

and upper bounded as
[

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})\leq K^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})\leq nQ_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})+\log_{2}6+2\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\,.

(14)

Additionally, the
n
-shot quantum capacity can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\geq nQ(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{23}(32-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(16-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{6}}\right)\;,

(15)

and, for

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})\leq n\frac{Q(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})}{(1-2\varepsilon)}-\varepsilon\log_{2}\varepsilon-(1-\varepsilon)\log_{2}(1-\varepsilon)\,,

where the latter follows from the fact that the pure loss channel is degradable, together with a general upper bound of the one-shot quantum capacity in terms of the coherent information

II.4
Non-Markovian generalisation of the the pure loss channel

In the absence of memory effects, an optical fibre is commonly modelled by the pure loss channel
{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}
, where the parameter
\lambda
coincides with the effective transmissivity of the fibre. Specifically, the transmission of
n
signals through the fibre is modelled as in Eq. (
1
), where the channel
\mathcal{N}
is identified with
{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}
. The first attempts to describe memory effects in optical fibres arose in Refs.
[
11
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Specifically, the models presented in Refs.
[
11
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
were based on a suitable non-Markovian generalisation of the pure loss channel
[
11
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, which tried to describe memory effects by allowing for interferences between the state emerging from the environmental output port (i.e., port IV of Fig.
1
) of the beam splitter associated with a given input signal and the states of the subsequent input signals
[
11
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. However, as noted in Ref.
[
42
]
, these models are not fully satisfactory as they fail to describe memory effects for long optical fibres; instead, they constitute a good model just for very short optical fibres.

To overcome these limitations, Ref.
[
42
]
introduced a more refined model of optical fibres with memory effects. The peculiar feature of this model is that the interferences between signals occurs continuously along the entire fibre, making it a good model even for arbitrarily long fibres. In order to explain this more refined model, it is necessary to start from the following simple, provoking question: Why the pure loss channel is a consistent model of memoryless optical fibres?

At a first glance, one may be tempted to think that modelling a memoryless optical fibre with a pure loss channel is a too crude approximation, as it would mean to model a possibly very long line of glass (the fibre) via a single short mirror (the beam splitter). Following the same intuition, the pure loss channel appears to be a reasonable model when the fibre is very short (ideally infinitesimal), as the latter essentially becomes a small piece of glass, i.e. a single beam splitter. To see why the pure loss channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
is a good model even for long (memoryless) optical fibres, let us consider an optical fibre of transmissivity
\lambda
. Let us divide such fibre into
M
segments, each of length
L/M
, where
L
denotes the total lenght of the fibre. Since the transmissivity
\lambda
is exponentially decreasing in the length
L
, each segment has a transmissivity of
\lambda^{1/M}
. As
M
approaches infinity, each segment becomes infinitesimally short and thus can be modelled as a beam splitter of transmissivity
\lambda^{1/M}
, coupling the input signals traveling through the fibre with a (single-mode) localised environment initialised in the vacuum state (see Fig.
2
for a pictorial representation). Since the memoryless assumption guarantees that all the
M
environments resets to the vacuum state after every channel use, it follows that the entire optical fibre can be modelled as the composition of
M\rightarrow\infty
pure loss channels, each with transmissivity
\lambda^{1/M}
. By exploiting the composition rule in (
11
), such a composition of
M
pure loss channels turns out to be exactly equal to the pure loss channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
. Hence, we conclude that the pure loss channel is a good model for
memoryless
optical fibres.

We are now ready to explain the model of optical fibre with memory effects introduced in Ref.
[
42
]
. The above-mentioned assumption that each environment resets to the vacuum state after every channel use is precisely what defines the memoryless (i.e. Markovian) regime: the communication channel returns to its initial configuration after each use, meaning that all environments are restored to the vacuum. This resetting can be interpreted as a thermalisation process that counteracts the perturbation induced by the interaction between the input optical signals and the environment via the
M
beam splitters. Under the memoryless assumption, this thermalisation process is perfect — each environment is fully restored to the vacuum state after every channel use. However, when input signals arrive in rapid succession, thermalisation becomes imperfect, causing each new signal to interact with an
M
-mode environment that retains traces of previous signals instead of returning to the
M
-mode vacuum state. Since a pure loss channel provides an effective model for describing the thermalisation of a single-mode system undergoing energy dissipation
[
64
]
, the authors of Ref.
[
42
]
model such a thermalisation process as a pure loss channel of transmissivity
\mu
acting on each of the
M
local environment after every channel use, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. When
\mu=0
,
such a model reduces to the memoryless configuration described by the pure loss channel. As
\mu
increases, the influence of previously transmitted signals on the optical link becomes more pronounced.

By summarising, the model of optical fibre with memory effects introduced in Ref.
[
42
]
is characterised by two parameters: the transmissivity of the fibre
\lambda\in[0,1]
and the memory parameter
\mu\in[0,1]
, which quantifies the extent of memory effects in the optical link. In such a model,
n
uses of the optical fibre are described by the
n
-mode Gaussian channel
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}
whose interferometric representation is depicted in Fig.
3
(for a precise mathematical definition of
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}
, see Ref.
[
42
]
). Using the notation introduced in Eq. (
5
), the communication line associated with this model turns out to be represended by the family of quantum channels

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt{\cal F}_{\lambda,\mu}\coloneqq\{\Phi^{(1)}_{\lambda,\mu},\Phi^{(2)}_{\lambda,\mu},\cdots,\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu},\cdots\}\;,

(17)

where the channel
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}
evolves the first
n
input signals. Finally, note that when the memory parameter vanishes (
\mu=0
), such a channel reduces to the memoryless configuration
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,0}=\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{\otimes n}
described by the pure loss channel
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
— in this sense, such a model constitutes a non-Markovian generalisation of the pure loss channel.

42
]

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ({\lambda,\mu})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)})\,,\qquad\qquad Q_{2}({\lambda,\mu})=K({\lambda,\mu})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)})\,,

(18)

where
Q(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)})
and
Q_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)})
are the capacities reported in Eq. (
12
) of the pure loss channel
\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}
of transmissivity
\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)
, where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)\coloneqq\lambda^{\frac{1-\mu}{1-2\sqrt{\mu}\cos(\theta)+\mu}}\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi]\;.

(19)

Since these capacities are monotonically increasing in the memory parameter
\mu

[
42
]
, it follows that memory effects improve the communication capabilities of optical fibres. Additionally, by exploiting Eq. (
18
) and Eq. (
12
), it easily follows that the quantum capacity
Q({\lambda,\mu})
vanishes if and only if
\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. This implies the following remarkable phenomenon
[
42
]
: for any arbitrarily small non-zero value of the transmissivity
\lambda
, if the memory parameter
\mu
is sufficiently large (so that the condition
\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}>\frac{1}{2}
is satisfied), then qubit distribution across an optical fibre of transmissivity
\lambda
becomes achievable. This is remarkable because, in the absence of memory effects, it is impossible to perform qubit distribution across an optical fibre of transmissivity
\leq\frac{1}{2}
(as its quantum capacity vanishes).

III
Non-asymptotic quantum communication across optical fibres with memory effects

According to Eq. (
8
), the
n
-shot capacities of the model of optical fibres with memory effects of Ref.
[
42
]
are defined in terms of the one-shot capacities of the
n
-mode Gaussian channel
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\coloneqq Q^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}\right)\,,\qquad Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\coloneqq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}\right)\,,\qquad K^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\coloneqq K^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}\right)\,.

(20)

The goal of this paper is to obtain easily computable lower bounds on these quantities. This is important as it allows one to answer the following practical question: How many uses of an optical fibre of transmissivity
\lambda
and memory parameter
\mu
are sufficient to transmit
k
qubits, distil
k
ebits, or generate
k
secret-key bits within a given error tolerance
\varepsilon
? We derive such bounds on the non-asymptotic capacities in the following theorem, proved in Theorems
9
-
11
in the Appendix.

Theorem 1

((Non-asymptotic quantum communication across an optical fibre with memory effects))
.

For all
n\geq 4
and all
\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the
n
-shot capacities of the above non-Markovian model of optical fibre with transmissivity
\lambda
and the memory parameter
\mu
can be lower bounded as:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq nQ({\lambda,\mu})-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{23}(32-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(16-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{6}}\right)\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptK^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq nQ_{2}({\lambda,\mu})-\sqrt{n}C_{2}(\lambda,\mu)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{6}\,3\,(4-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(2-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\,,

(22)

where
Q({\lambda,\mu})
and
Q_{2}({\lambda,\mu})
are the (asymptotic) capacities of the model reported in Eq. (
18
), and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+\frac{4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}\right)\,,

(23)

\displaystyle C_{2}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}\right)\,.

Finally, the lower bound in Eq. (
21
) is trivial if
\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, as the quantum capacity
Q({\lambda,\mu})
vanishes in such a parameter region.

The above theorem demonstrates that
n
uses of an optical fibre with transmissivity
\lambda
and memory parameter
\mu
are sufficient to transmit, up to an error
\varepsilon
, the number of qubits specified on the right-hand side of Eq. (
21
) and the number of ebits (and hence of secret-key bits) specified on the right-hand side of Eq. (
22
). Hence, Theorem
1
provides a method to determine how many uses of an optical fibre of transmissivity
\lambda
and memory parameter
\mu
suffice to transmit a desired number
k
of qubits, ebits, or secret-key bits up to a given error tolerance
\varepsilon
. Indeed, it suffices to impose that the lower bounds in Eq. (
21
) and Eq. (
22
) are larger than
k
, and solve the corresponding inequalities with respect to
n
. The simplicity of these lower bounds makes it possible to solve these inequalities by solving a simple quadratic inequality.

Now, let us briefly give the rough idea behind the proof of the above Thereom
1
. In Ref.
[
42
]
it has been shown that the
n
-mode Gaussian channel
\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}
is unitarily equivalent to a tensor product of
n
pure loss channels, i.e. there exist unitary channels
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
and transmissivities
\{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\ldots,n}
such that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Phi^{(n)}_{\lambda,\mu}=\mathcal{U}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)\circ\mathcal{V}\,.

(24)

Consequently, by exploiting the fact that capacities are invariant under unitary transformations, the asymptotic capacities of optical fibres with memory effects are characterised by the asymptotic behaviour for
n\rightarrow\infty
of the transmissivities
\{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\ldots,n}
. Crucially, as shown Ref.
[
42
]
, these transmissivities can be retrieved by calculating the singular values of a suitable
Toeplitz matrix

[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
. By definition, a Toeplitz matrix is characterised by the property that its element in position
(i,j)
depends only on the difference
i-j
for all
i,j
, meaning that all entries along each diagonal are constant. Notably, the asymptotic behaviour of the singular values of a Toeplitz matrix can be analytically understood thanks to the celebrated
Avram–Parter theorem

[
60
,
61
]
:

Theorem 2

((
Avram–Parter’s theorem

[
60
,
61
]
))
.

Let
\{a_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of real numbers. For all
n\in\mathds{N}
let
T^{(n)}
be the
n\times n
matrix with elements
T^{(n)}_{k,j}\coloneqq a_{k-j}
for all
k,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\{s^{(n)}_{j}\}_{j=1,2,\ldots,n}
be the singular values of the matrix
T^{(n)}
ordered in increasing order in
j
. Assume that the function

s:[0,2\pi]\to\mathds{C}
, defined by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pts(x)\coloneqq\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}a_{l}\,e^{ilx}\,\qquad\forall\,x\in[0,2\pi]\,,

(25)

is bounded. Then for all continuous functions
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded support it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F\!\left(s^{(n)}_{j}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2\pi}F\!\left(|s(x)|\right)\,.

(26)

This theorem was exploited in Ref.
[
42
]
to show that the asymptotic behaviour of the transmissivities
\{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\ldots,n}
is determined by the function
\eta_{\lambda,\mu}
reported in (
19
), i.e. it roughly holds that
\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{n\frac{\theta}{2\pi}}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)
for all
\theta\in(0,2\pi]
. This observation was the main tool of Ref.
[
42
]
to find the closed expressions of the asymptotic capacities reported in (
18
). Instead, in order to obtain easily computable lower bounds on the
n
-shot capacities of optical fibres with memory effects, the Avram–Parter’s theorem alone is insufficient, as it only provides tools for analysing the asymptotic behaviour of Toeplitz matrices (as it inherently regards the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
). Therefore, it becomes necessary to establish explicit error bounds for the Avram–Parter’s theorem. We do this in the following theorem, which is of independent interest.

Theorem 3

((Error bound on the Avram–Parter’s theorem))
.

Let
\{a_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of real numbers. For all
n\in\mathds{N}
let
T^{(n)}
be the
n\times n
matrix with elements
T^{(n)}_{k,j}\coloneqq a_{k-j}
for all
k,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\{s^{(n)}_{j}\}_{j=1,2,\ldots,n}
be the singular values of the matrix
T^{(n)}
ordered in increasing order in
j
. Let us define the function

s:\mathbb{R}\to\mathds{C}
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pts(x)\coloneqq\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}a_{l}\,e^{ilx}\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(27)

Let
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous complex-valued function with bounded support, and assume that
F
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant equal to
L
. Then, for all
n\geq 4
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
k
-th derivative of
s
exists and is continuous, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F\!\left(s_{j}^{(n)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2\pi}F\left(|s(x)|\right)\right|\leq\left(\frac{2^{k+1}\|s^{(k)}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|s\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k}{k+3/2}}}+(2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty})\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k+1/2}{k+3/2}}}\,,

(28)

where
s^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th derivative of
s
,
F^{\prime}
denotes the first derivative of
F
, and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|s^{(k)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x|s^{(k)}(x)|^{2}}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle\|s\|_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x|s(x)|^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x|F^{\prime}(x)|\,,

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|\,.

The proof of the above theorem is provided in Section
C
in the Appendix. Note that this theorem is stronger than the Avram–Parter’s theorem, as the latter can be recovered by taking the limit of Eq. (
28
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This theorem is the crucial tool that we exploit to derive the lower bounds on the
n
-shot capacities of optical fibres with memory effects reported in Theorem
1
. We refer to Section
A
in the Appendix for more details.

IV
Discussion

In this work, we have analysed the performance of non-asymptotic quantum comunication across suitable non-Markovian optical links. In Theorem
1
, we have provided easily computable lower bounds on the non-asymptotic capacities of the model of non-Markovian optical fibre introduced in Ref.
[
42
]
. This result allows one to calculate how many uses of a non-Markovian optical fibre are sufficient in order to transmit
k
qubits, distil
k
ebits, or generate
k
secret-key bits up to a fixed error. In order to derive such lower bounds, in Theorem
3
we introduced an explicit upper bound on the convergence of the celebrated Avram–Parter theorem, which we regard as a tool of independent interest for the field of quantum information theory and matrix analysis.

We leave as an open problem to determine upper bounds on the non-asymptotic capacities of the above model. Moreover, it would be interesting to estimate the non-asymptotic capacities of the above model with the additional presence of thermal noise and amplifier noise.

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pts(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}a_{k}\,e^{ikx}\,\qquad\forall\,x\in[0,2\pi]\,,

(45)

is bounded. Then for all continuous function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded support it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F\!\left(s^{(n)}_{j}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2\pi}F\!\left(|s(x)|\right)\,.

(46)

In
[
42
]
, the Avram–Parter’s theorem was exploited to derive the closed expression of the asymptotic capacities
Q(\lambda,\mu)
,
Q_{2}(\lambda,\mu)
, and
K(\lambda,\mu)
reported in (
33
). More explicitly, in
[
42
]
, the Avram–Parter’s theorem was exploited to prove that the quantum capacity
Q(\lambda,\mu)
is given by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ(\lambda,\mu)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}\right)\,,

(47)

where
\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\cdot)
is defined in (
34
). In (
47
), the role of the function
F
in the statement of the Avram-Parter’s theorem is played by the function
\lambda\mapsto Q(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})
, while the role of the function
s
is played by the function
f_{\lambda,\mu}
defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptf_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{(\lambda,\mu)}_{k}e^{ik\theta}=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{\mu}\exp({i\theta})}}\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi]\,,

(48)

which satisfies
|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\cdot)|^{2}=\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\cdot)
, with
a^{(\lambda,\mu)}_{k}
being defined in (
38
). Additionally, in
[
42
]
, the Avram-Parter’s theorem was exploited to show that the two-way quantum capacity
Q_{2}(\lambda,\mu)
and the secret key capacity
K(\lambda,\mu)
are both given by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ_{2}(\lambda,\mu)=K(\lambda,\mu)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q_{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2}}\right)\,,

(49)

where now the role of the function
F
in the statement of the Avram-Parter’s theorem is played by the function
\lambda\mapsto Q_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})
. While the Avram-Parter’s theorem was crucial to calculate the asymptotic capacities
[
42
]
, it is not enough to derive a lower bound on the
n
-shot capacities (as the Avram-Parter’s inherently regards the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
).

Thanks to (
44
), the
n
-shot capacities can be lower bounded in terms of the sums

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)\,.

(50)

These sums are far from being easily computable, as the transmissivities
\{\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
are related to the singular values of an
n\times n
matrix, and the dimension
n
can be very large. However, the Avram–Parter’s theorem establishes that these two sums, divided by
n
, approach the integrals

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}\right)

(51)

in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, in order to find an easily computable lower bound on the one-shot capacities, it suffices to find explicit upper bounds on the error incurred in the approximation of the sums in (
50
) with the integrals in (
50
) provided by the Avram–Parter’s theorem. That is, we need to upper bound the errors

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}\right)\right|\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}Q_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)}\right)\right|\,.

(52)

To do this, we establish the following bound on the convergence of the Avram-Parter’s theorem, which may be of independent interest for the matrix analysis literature.

Theorem 7

((Error bound on the Avram–Parter’s theorem))
.

Let
\{a_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of real numbers. For all
n\in\mathds{N}
let
T^{(n)}
be the
n\times n
matrix with elements
T^{(n)}_{k,j}\coloneqq a_{k-j}
for all
k,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\{s^{(n)}_{j}\}_{j=1,2,\ldots,n}
be the singular values of the matrix
T^{(n)}
ordered in increasing order in
j
. Let us define the function

s:\mathbb{R}\to\mathds{C}
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pts(x)\coloneqq\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}a_{l}\,e^{ilx}\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(53)

Let
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous complex-valued function with bounded support, and assume that
F
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant equal to
L
. Then, for all
n\geq 4
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
k
-th derivative of
s
exists and is continuous, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F\!\left(s_{j}^{(n)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2\pi}F\left(|s(x)|\right)\right|\leq\left(\frac{2^{k+1}\|s^{(k)}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|s\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k}{k+3/2}}}+(2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty})\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k+1/2}{k+3/2}}}\,,

(54)

where
s^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th derivative of
s
,
F^{\prime}
denotes the first derivative of
F
, and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|s^{(k)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x|s^{(k)}(x)|^{2}}\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\|s\|_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}x|s(x)|^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x|F^{\prime}(x)|\,,

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|\,.

The proof of the above theorem is provided in Theorem
19
in Section
C
. Note that the above theorem is stronger than the Avram–Parter’s theorem, as the latter can be recovered by taking the limit of Eq. (
54
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We will exploit this theorem in Subsection
A.1
to bound the
n
-shot quantum capacity and in Section
A.2
to bound the
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity and the
n
-shot secret-key capacity.

A.1
n
-shot quantum capacity

This subsection is devoted to show an easily computable lower bound the
n
-shot quantum capacity
Q^{\varepsilon,n}(\lambda,\mu)
. Thanks to (
44
),
Q^{\varepsilon,n}(\lambda,\mu)
can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{23}(32-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(16-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{6}}\right)\,,

(56)

and thus it suffices to find an easily computable lower bound on the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)
. We do this in the forthcoming Lemma
8
.

Lemma 8
.

For all
\lambda,\mu\in(0,1)
and all
n\geq 4
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)\geq nQ(\lambda,\mu)-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)\,,

(57)

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}M_{\lambda,\mu}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+\frac{4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}\right)\,.

(58)

Moreover, the lower bound is trivial if
\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, as the quantum capacity
Q({\lambda,\mu})
vanishes in such a parameter region.

Proof.

To simplify the notation, let us introduce the function
\eta\mapsto q(\eta)
and the quantity
M_{\lambda,\mu}
defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptq(\eta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q(\mathcal{E}_{\eta})\,,

(59)

\displaystyle M_{\lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\,.

By exploiting (
33
) together with the fact that
\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)=M_{\lambda,\mu}
, it is simple to observe that
Q(\lambda,\mu)>0
if and only if
M_{\lambda,\mu}>\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, if
M_{\lambda,\mu}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, the inequality in (
57
) is trivial.

Now, let us assume that
\lambda
and
\mu
are such that
M_{\lambda,\mu}>\frac{1}{2}
. By exploiting Theorem
21
in Section
C
with
p=\infty
, we deduce that the largest transmissivity
\eta_{n}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}
can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\eta_{n}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}=\left(s_{n}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)^{2}\leq\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2}=\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}\eta_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)=M_{\lambda,\mu}\,,

(60)

Moreover, note that since it always holds that
M_{\lambda,\mu}<1
, the quantity
q(M_{\lambda,\mu})
is well defined. Hence, we can define a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptF(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}q(x^{2}),&x\leq\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}},\\
q(M_{\lambda,\mu})-L(x-\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}})\,,&\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}\leq x\leq\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}+\frac{q(M_{\lambda,\mu})}{L}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(61)

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptL\coloneqq\frac{2(\log_{2}e)}{\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}(1-M_{\lambda,\mu})}\,.

(62)

By explicitly calculating the derivative
F^{\prime}
, it can be easily shown that the Lipschitz constant of
F
is given by
L
. Moreover, as a consequence of (
60
),
F
satisfies

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptq\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)=F\!\left(s_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)

(63)

and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptq\!\left(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=F\!\left(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|\right)

(64)

for every
i,n
, and
\theta
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}q(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\!\left(s_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}F(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|)\right\rvert\,.

(65)

Hence, since
F
is continuous, all the hypotheses of Theorem
7
are satisfied. We can thus apply Theorem
7
with
k=2
and obtain:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}q(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{8\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{n^{4/7}}+(2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty})\frac{1}{n^{5/7}}\,.

(66)

Hence, it suffices to upper bound the quantities
\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}
,
\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}
,
\|F\|_{\infty}
, and
\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}
. By an explicit, simple calculation, it follows that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f_{\lambda,\mu}|=\sqrt{2\pi M_{\lambda,\mu}}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{M}_{\lambda,\mu}\sqrt{2\pi\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}\,,

and that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|F\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{M_{\lambda,\mu}}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,,

(68)

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}}\!\mathrm{d}\!x\,\partial_{x}[q(x^{2})]+q(M_{\lambda,\mu})=2\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{M_{\lambda,\mu}}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,.

Consequently, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}q(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{8\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}+2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\lambda,\mu)}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}M_{\lambda,\mu}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+\frac{4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{M_{\lambda,\mu}}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,.

(70)

Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}q\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq n\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}q(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)

(71)

\displaystyle=nQ(\lambda,\mu)-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)\,,

where in the last line we exploited
47
.
∎

By combining (
56
) with Lemma
8
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 9
.

For all
n\geq 4
and all
\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the
n
-shot quantum capacity of the model can be lower bounded as:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq nQ({\lambda,\mu})-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{23}(32-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(16-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{6}}\right)\,,

(72)

where
Q({\lambda,\mu})
is the quantum capacity reported in Eq. (
33
), and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+\frac{4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}\right)\,,

(73)

Finally, the lower bound is trivial if
\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, as the quantum capacity
Q({\lambda,\mu})
vanishes in such a parameter region.

A.2
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity and
n
-shot secret-key capacity

This subsection is devoted to show an easily computable lower bound the
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity
Q_{2}^{\varepsilon,n}(\lambda,\mu)
and on the
n
-shot secret-key capacity
K^{\varepsilon,n}(\lambda,\mu)
. Thanks to (
44
), these
n
-shot capacities can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptK^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\geq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}(\lambda,\mu)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{6}\,3\,(4-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(2-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\,,

(74)

and thus it suffices to find an easily computable lower bound on the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)
. We do this in the forthcoming Lemma
10
.

Lemma 10
.

For all
\lambda,\mu\in(0,1)
and all
n\geq 4
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}_{i}}\right)\geq nQ_{2}(\lambda,\mu)-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)\,,

(75)

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}}\right)\,.

(76)

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Lemma
8
and the main technical tool is to employ Theorem
7
. To simplify the notation, let us introduce the function
\eta\mapsto k(\eta)
and the quantity
M_{\lambda,\mu}
defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptk(\eta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\eta})\,,

(77)

\displaystyle M_{\lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda^{\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu}}{1+\sqrt{\mu}}}\,.

As we saw in the proof of Lemma
8
, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\eta_{n}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq M_{\lambda,\mu}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq M_{\lambda,\mu}\,.

Let us define
F:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptF(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}k(x^{2}),&x\leq\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}},\\
k(M_{\lambda,\mu})-L(x-\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}})\,,&\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}\leq x\leq\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}+\frac{k(M_{\lambda,\mu})}{L}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(79)

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptL\coloneqq\frac{2(\log_{2}e)\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\,.

(80)

By evaluating the derivative of
F
, it can be easily shown that the Lipschitz constant of
F
is given by
L
. Moreover, as a consequence of (
78
),
F
satisfies

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptk\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)=F\!\left(s_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)

(81)

and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptk\!\left(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=F\!\left(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|\right)

(82)

for every
i,n
, and
\theta
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}k(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\!\left(s_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}F(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|)\right\rvert\,.

(83)

Hence, since
F
is continuous, all the hypotheses of Theorem
7
are satisfied. We can thus apply Theorem
7
with
k=2
and obtain:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}k(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{8\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{n^{4/7}}+(2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty})\frac{1}{n^{5/7}}\,.

(84)

Hence, it suffices to upper bound the quantities
\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}
,
\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}
,
\|F\|_{\infty}
, and
\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}
. In the proof of Lemma
8
, we proved that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi M_{\lambda,\mu}}\,,

(85)

\displaystyle\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{M}_{\lambda,\mu}\sqrt{2\pi\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}\,.

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\|F\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,,

(86)

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{M_{\lambda,\mu}}}\!\mathrm{d}\!x\,\partial_{x}[k(x^{2})]+k(M_{\lambda,\mu})=2\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,.

Consequently, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}k(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{8\|f_{\lambda,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|f_{\lambda,\mu}\|_{2}+2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\lambda,\mu)}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptC(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{8}M_{\lambda,\mu}\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\frac{1+\sqrt{\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)+\mu}{(1-\sqrt{\mu})^{4}}+4(2\pi)^{3/2}(\log_{2}e)\frac{M_{\lambda,\mu}}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}+8\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-M_{\lambda,\mu}}\right)\,.

(88)

Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\!\left(\eta_{i}^{(n,\lambda,\mu)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq n\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}k(|f_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)|^{2})-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)

(89)

\displaystyle=nK(\lambda,\mu)-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)\,,

where in the last line we exploited
49
.
∎

By combining (
74
) with Lemma
10
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 11
.

For all
n\geq 4
and all
\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the
n
-shot two-way quantum capacity and the
n
-shot secret-key capacity of the model can be lower bounded as:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptK^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,n)}({\lambda,\mu})\geq nQ_{2}({\lambda,\mu})-\sqrt{n}C(\lambda,\mu)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{6}\,3\,(4-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(2-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\,,

(90)

where
Q_{2}({\lambda,\mu})
is the two-way quantum capacity reported in Eq. (
33
), and

B.1
Tensor product of pure loss channels

In order to find an easily computable lower bound on the one-shot capacities of tensor product of pure loss channels, we are going to exploit the

developed in Ref.

Lemma 12

((Lower bound on the one-shot capacities of a tensor product of pure loss channels via entropy variance approach))
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in[0,1]
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. The one-shot quantum capacity, the one-shot two-way quantum capacity, and the one-shot secret-key capacity of the tensor product of pure loss channels
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}}
can be lower bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptQ^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}})-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{23}(32-\varepsilon)^{2}}{(16-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{6}}\right)\,,

(92)

\displaystyle K^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq Q_{2}^{(\varepsilon,1)}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}}\right)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{i}}\right)-\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2^{6}\,3\,(4-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(2-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\,,

where
Q(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda})
and

are reported in (

Before proving this lemma, we need to introduce the following preliminary result, which follows by setting

in the statement of
[
53
, Lemma 55]
(with the latter being a trivial consequence of
[
30
, Proposition 31]

Lemma 13

((Upper bound for the hypothesis testing relative entropy))
.

Let
\rho
be positive definite states and
\sigma
be positive definite operators with bounded trace on a separable Hilbert space. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptD^{\varepsilon}_{h}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq D(\rho\|\sigma)+\sqrt{\frac{{V(\rho\|\sigma)}}{1-\varepsilon}}+\log_{2}6+2\log_{2}\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\,.

(93)

Here,
D^{\varepsilon}_{h}(\rho\|\sigma)
,
D(\rho\|\sigma)
, and
V(\rho\|\sigma)
denote the hypothesis testing relative entropy, the quantum relative entropy, and the relative entropy variance, respectively, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptD^{\varepsilon}_{h}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\log_{2}\inf_{E}\left\{\Tr[E\sigma]\,:\,0\leq E\leq\mathbb{1},\,\Tr[E\rho]\geq 1-\varepsilon\right\}\,,

(94)

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Tr\left[\rho\left(\log_{2}\rho-\log_{2}\sigma\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle V(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Tr\left[\rho\left(\log_{2}\rho-\log_{2}\sigma\right)^{2}\right]-D(\rho\|\sigma)^{2}\,.

This directly implies the following generalisation of
[
53
, Lemma 55]
to tensor product of operators.

Lemma 14

((Upper bound for the hypothesis testing relative entropy for product operators))
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\rho_{1},\ldots\,\rho_{n}
be positive definite states and
\sigma_{1},\ldots\,\sigma_{n}
be positive definite operators with bounded trace on a separable Hilbert space. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptD^{\varepsilon}_{h}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\bigg\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}D(\rho_{i}\|\sigma_{i})+\sqrt{\frac{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(\rho_{i}\|\sigma_{i})}}{1-\varepsilon}}+\log_{2}6+2\log_{2}\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\,,

(95)

where
D^{\varepsilon}_{h}(\rho\|\sigma)
,
D(\rho\|\sigma)
, and
V(\rho\|\sigma)
are defined in (

Proof.

The proof follows by using Lemma

and
\sigma=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}
, and by exploiting the simple-to-prove facts that the relative entropy and the relative entropy variances are additive under tensor product, i.e.

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptD\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\bigg\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D(\rho_{i}\|\sigma_{i})\,,

(96)

\displaystyle V\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\bigg\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(\rho_{i}\|\sigma_{i})\,.

∎

With this lemma at hand, we can prove Lemma

.

The statement is a generalisation of

Appendix C
Ergodic Estimates for Toeplitz Sequences Generated by a Symbol

This section is devoted to the proof of error bound on the Avram-Parter’s theorem stated above in Theorem
7

C.1
Preliminaries

Throughout this section, a matrix-sequence is a sequence of the form
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
, where
A_{n}
is a square matrix and
{\rm size}(A_{n})=n
. Let
C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of continuous complex-valued functions with bounded support defined on
\mathbb{R}
. If
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, the singular values and eigenvalues of
A
are denoted by
\sigma_{1}(A),\ldots,\sigma_{n}(A)
and
\lambda_{1}(A),\dots,\lambda_{n}(A)
, respectively.
We denote by
\mu_{d}
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Throughout this section, “measurable” means “Lebesgue measurable”. Moreover, we denote by
\|g\|_{p}
the
L^{p}
-norm of the function
g
over its domain (which will be clear from the context).

We say that a matrix-sequence
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
admits a singular value symbol (or simply a symbol)
f(x)
when the sampling of
|f(x)|
over a uniform grid in its domain yields an approximation of the singular values of
A_{n}
that improves as
n\to\infty
. The rigorous definition is given below; it is based on an ergodic formula that must hold for every test function
F\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 18

((Asymptotic singular value distribution of a matrix-sequence))
.

Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
be a matrix-sequence with
A_{n}
of size
n
, and let
f:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable with
0<\mu_{d}(\Omega)<\infty
.
We say that
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
has an asymptotic singular value distribution described by
f
if the following ergodic formula holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}F(\sigma_{i}(A_{n}))=\frac{1}{\mu_{d}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}F(|f(x)|)\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\forall\,F\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}).

(117)

In this case,
f
is called the singular value symbol (or simply the symbol) of
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
and we write
\{A_{n}\}_{n}\sim_{\sigma}f
.

For a comprehensive treatment of the topic of symbols,
a tutorial on how to compute them, a showcase of their applications to several problems (mostly coming from discretization of PDEs and iterative numerical methods), the main references are
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
]
.

Among the various matrix-sequences that possess a symbol, Toeplitz sequences generated by
L^{1}
functions stand out for their importance.
A matrix of the form

\left[f_{i-j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{0}&f_{-1}&\ f_{-2}&\ \cdots&\ \ \cdots&f_{-(n-1)}\\
f_{1}&\ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ &\vdots\\
f_{2}&\ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ \ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ \ddots&f_{-2}\\
\vdots&&\ \ddots&\ \ddots&\ \ \ddots&f_{-1}\\
f_{n-1}&\cdots&\ \cdots&\ f_{2}&\ \ f_{1}&f_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(118)

whose
(i,j)
-th entry depends only on the difference
i-j
is called a Toeplitz matrix. In other words, a Toeplitz matrix is a matrix whose entries are constant along each diagonal.
A case of special interest arises when the entries
f_{k}
are the Fourier coefficients of a function
f:[-\pi,\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
in
L^{1}([-\pi,\pi])
, i.e.,

f_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}kx}{\rm d}x,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In this case, the matrix (
118
) is denoted by
T_{n}(f)
and is referred to as the
n
-th Toeplitz matrix generated by
f
. One can also define the associated infinite-dimensional operator
T(f)
and represent it as the infinite Toeplitz matrix
\left[f_{i-j}\right]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
It is not difficult to see that the conjugate transpose of
T_{n}(f)
is given by

T_{n}(f)^{H}=T_{n}(\overline{f})

for every
f\in L^{1}([-\pi,\pi])
and every
n
; see, e.g.,
[
67
, Section 6.2]
. In particular,
T_{n}(f)
is Hermitian whenever
f
is almost everywhere a real-valued function.

The asymptotic singular value distribution of the Toeplitz sequence
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n}
has been deeply investigated over time, starting from Szegö’s first limit theorem
[
73
]
and the Avram–Parter theorem
[
74
,
75
]
, up to the works by Tyrtyshnikov–Zamarashkin
[
76
,
77
,
78
]
and Tilli
[
79
,
80
]
. For more on this subject, see
[
81
, Chapter 5]
and
[
67
, Chapter 6]
and references therein.
Using our notations, the Avram-Parter’s theorem states that
\{T_{n}(f)\}_{n}\sim_{\sigma}f
for any
f\in L^{1}([-\pi,\pi])
. As a consequence, the ergodic formula (
117
) holds with
A_{n}=T_{n}(f)
for any
L^{1}
function
f
.

The analysis of the ergodic formula for Toeplitz sequences generated by real-valued symbols
f
is also linked to the asymptotic study of the eigenvalues and spectral distribution of the matrices
T_{n}(f)
. Several works enquire the existence of an asymptotic expansion of the eigenvalues when we impose certain conditions on
f
.
See
[
82
]
and references therein for the case of (modified) simple loop functions, that are in particular nonnegative
C^{1}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
functions with
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
being a zero of order at most 2 and exactly another extremum
f^{\prime}(\xi)=0
in the open interval
(-\pi,\pi)
.
For the case of (some) smooth even function
f
that is monotonous on
[0,\pi]
see
[
83
,
84
]
.
About the classes of test functions satisfying the ergodic formula (
117
) see for example
[
85
,
86
]
.

In this context, Widom proved a more precise result
[
87
]
. He showed that, for essentially bounded symbols
f
with

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|f\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert k\rvert\lvert f_{k}\rvert^{2}<\infty,

we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}G(\sigma_{j}(T_{n}(f))^{2})-\frac{n}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert^{2})d\theta\right\}=\Tr[G(T(\overline{f})T(f))+G(T(f)T(\overline{f}))-2T(G(\lvert f\rvert^{2}))]

(119)

for any
C^{3}
function
G(x)
defined at least on an interval containing
\sigma_{j}(T_{n}(f))^{2}
and
\sigma_{j}(T(f))^{2}
for all
j,n
. This implies that

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{j}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

thus providing an estimate for the convergence rate of the ergodic formula (
117
) for any function
F(x)\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
that is an extension a function of the form
G(x^{2})
with
G(x)
as specified above.

For concrete applications, we need an explicit bound of the form

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{j}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq c(n)

(120)

holding for any
n\geq n_{0}
with an explicit
n_{0}
. Equation (
119
) shows that, for some choices of
F(x)
and
f(x)
, we can take
c(n)=O(1/n)
, but the dependence of
c(n)
on
f
and
F
is not specified. Moreover, this result holds only for regular enough test functions
F(x)
.

In the main result of this section (Theorem
19
below), we provide an explicit bound
c(n)
in (
120
) for the case of test functions
F(x)
in
C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
that are not necessarily continuously differentiable, but at the cost of requiring that the symbol
f(x)
belongs to
C^{k}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
for some
k\geq 1
, i.e.,
f(-\pi)=f(\pi)
and the
2\pi
-periodic extension of
f(x)
to
\mathbb{R}
is a function in
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})
for some
k\geq 1
. In this scenario, the bound
c(n)
is
O(1/n^{\frac{2k+1}{2k+3}})
and it is derived by approximating
f(x)
with its truncated Fourier series
f_{N}(x)
and the singular values of the banded Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f_{N})
with the ones of the circulant matrices
C_{n}(f_{N})
, which are exactly the sampling of
|f(x)|
over a uniform grid in
[-\pi,\pi]
. Here we denote the set of real-valued Lipschitz functions with Lipschitz constant
L
as
Lip(L)
, i.e. all functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L|x-y|\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us state our main result.

Theorem 19
.

Let
f\in C^{k}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
with
k\geq 1
and let
F\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})\cap Lip(L)
. Then

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{2^{k+1}\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+4\pi L\|f\|_{2}\right)\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k}{k+3/2}}}+(2\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}+4\|F\|_{\infty})\frac{1}{n^{\frac{k+1/2}{k+3/2}}}

for every
n\geq 4
.

C.2
Known results

C.2.1
Matrix norms and Toeplitz matrices

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we denote its
p
-Schatten norms as
\|A\|_{p}
with
\infty\geq p\geq 1
, where
\|A\|_{1}
is the Trace norm,
\|A\|_{2}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm and
\|A\|_{\infty}=\|A\|
is the operator norm. An important result that links together the different norms is given by the Hölder theorem.

Theorem 20

((Hölder))
.

If
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
with
\infty\geq r,p,q\geq 1
, then

\|AB\|_{r}\leq\|A\|_{p}\|B\|_{q}

where by convention,
1/\infty=0
.
In particular, for any
\infty\geq p\geq 1

\|AB\|_{p}\leq\|A\|\|B\|_{p},\qquad\lvert\Tr(AB)\rvert\leq\|AB\|_{1}\leq\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}.

Here we report a classic result linking Toeplitz matrices to their symbols through their
p
-Schatten norm and
p
-norm.

Theorem 21

((Theorem 6.2 of
[
67
]
))
.

Let
f\in L^{p}([-\pi,\pi])
for some
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then

\|T_{n}(f)\|_{p}\leq\left(\frac{n}{2\pi}\right)^{1/p}\|f\|_{p}

where for any matrix
A
,
\|A\|_{p}
is its
p
-Schatten norm.

C.2.2
Lipschitz and BV functions

Definition 22
.

A function
f
is called
Lip(M,\alpha)
with
M\geq 0
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, if

\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\leq M\lvert x-y\rvert^{\alpha}

for every
x,y
in the domain of
f
. Notice that
Lip(M)=Lip(M,1)
.

If
D
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}
and
f\in C^{1}(D)
, then
f\in Lip(\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty})
.
On the other hand, any
f\in Lip(L)
on
D
is also in
BV(D)
with total variation bounded by
L\lvert D\rvert
.
Moreover, the triangular inequality

\lvert\lvert f(x)\rvert-\lvert f(y)\rvert\rvert\leq\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert

is enough to conclude that
f\in Lip(M,\alpha)\implies\lvert f\rvert\in Lip(M,\alpha)
.

Theorem 23
.

(Theorem 4.3 of
[
88
]
)
Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{B_{n}\}_{n}
be two sequences of
d_{n}\times d_{n}
matrices and assume that
F\in Lip(M,\alpha)
is a continuous function. Then

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}F(\sigma_{i}(A_{n}))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}F(\sigma_{i}(B_{n}))\right\rvert\leq M\|A_{n}-B_{n}\|_{p}^{\alpha}d_{n}^{-\alpha/p}

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
p
-Schatten norm for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Lipschitz functions are in particular
Bounded Variation
, so it can be decomposed into the difference of two continuous monotonic increasing functions as showed by the following classical result.

Lemma 24
.

Given
F\in BV([a,b])
there exist
H,G\in C([a,b])
monotonous increasing functions such that

F=G-H,\qquad\|G\|_{\infty}+\|H\|_{\infty}=TV(F)+F(a).

In particular,
G(a)=F(a)
and
H(a)=0
.
If in addition
F\in Lip(L)
, then
F^{\prime}
exists almost everywhere,
TV(F)=\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq L(b-a)
and
H,G
are continuous.

C.2.3
Estimates for the Fourier transform and series

For the main results, we need some estimates on the moments of the Fourier transform of
C^{3}
functions, and on the truncated Fourier series of
C^{k}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
functions.

Lemma 25
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is
2\pi
-periodic and in
C^{k}(\mathbb{R})
. Call
f_{N}
its truncated Fourier series at degree (both negative and positive)
N\geq 0
. Then

\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}}{N^{2k}}

Proof.

If
c_{n}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f
, then the Fourier coefficients of
f^{(k)}
are
(\textnormal{i}n)^{k}c_{n}
, so

\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=2\pi\sum_{\lvert n\rvert>N}\lvert c_{n}\rvert^{2}=2\pi\sum_{\lvert n\rvert>N}\frac{|(\textnormal{i}n)^{k}c_{n}\rvert^{2}}{n^{2k}}\leq\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}}{N^{2k}}.

∎

Lemma 26
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is
2\pi
-periodic and in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then for any
N
,

\|(f_{N})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{4\pi}N^{3/2}\|f\|_{2}\leq 4N^{2}\|f\|_{2}.

Proof.

If
c_{n}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f
, then
\textnormal{i}nc_{n}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f^{\prime}
and by Cauchy-Schwartz,

\|(f_{N})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{|n|\leq N}|nc_{n}|\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{|n|\leq N}|c_{n}|^{2}\sum_{|n|\leq N}n^{2}\leq 4\pi\|f\|_{2}^{2}\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{6}.

Since

N\geq 1\implies 0\leq(N-1)(4N+1)=6N^{2}-(N+1)(2N+1)\implies\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{3}\leq 2N^{3}

then the thesis holds for
N\geq 1
. To conclude, notice that for
N=0
the thesis reads
0\leq 0
.
∎

C.3
Lipschitz Test Functions

This section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
19
. The idea of the proof is to uniformly approximate the periodic symbol
f
with its truncated Fourier series
f_{N}
and consequently to approximate the Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(f)
and
T_{n}(f_{N})
with the related circulant matrix
C_{n}(f_{N})
, defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt(C_{n}(f_{N}))_{i-j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}(T_{n}(f))_{i-j}\,,&|i-j|\leq 2N,\\
(T_{n}(f))_{-n+(i-j)}\,,&|i-j|>2N\cap i>j,\\
(T_{n}(f))_{n+(i-j)},&|i-j|>2N\cap i<j.\end{cases}

(121)

The circulant matrix has singular values
\sigma_{i}\coloneqq\sigma_{i}(C_{n}(f_{N}))=|f_{N}(2\pi i/n)|
, so the average of
F(\sigma_{i})
is a quadrature formula for the function
F(|f_{N}|)/(2\pi)
. The proof can be thus divided into four steps:

1.

using that
f_{N}
is an approximation of
f
, find a bound
c_{1}(n,N)
for

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f_{N})))\right\rvert\leq c_{1}(n,N),

2.

using that the rank of
C_{n}(f_{N})-T_{n}(f_{N})
is
O(N)
for
N<n/2
, find a bound
c_{2}(n,N)
for

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f_{N})))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(C_{n}(f_{N})))\right\rvert\leq c_{2}(n,N),

3.

determine a quadrature bound
c_{3}(n,N)
for

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(C_{n}(f_{N})))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq c_{3}(n,N),

4.

using that
f_{N}
is an approximation of
f
, find a bound
c_{4}(N)
for

\left\lvert\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert)d\theta-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq c_{4}(N).

The sum of the four quantities
c(n,N)\coloneqq c_{1}(n,N)+c_{2}(n,N)+c_{3}(n,N)+c_{4}(N)
is thus a bound for

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq c(n,N)

(122)

and it holds for any
N<n/2
, so the wanted bound will be
\min_{N<n/2}c(n,N)
.

C.3.1
Step 1

Lemma 27
.

Let
f\in C^{k}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
,
F\in Lip(M,\alpha)
and
N\geq 0
.
Then

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f)))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(T_{n}(f_{N})))\right\rvert\leq M\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|^{2}_{2}}{N^{2k}}\right)^{\alpha/2}.

Proof.

Call
\sigma_{i}
the singular values of
T_{n}(f)
and
\sigma_{i}^{(N)}
the singular values of
T_{n}(f_{N})
. From Theorem
23
, Theorem
21
, and Lemma
25
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}^{(N)})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq M\|T_{n}(f-f_{N})\|_{2}^{\alpha}n^{-\alpha/2}

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{\alpha/2}\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|^{2}_{2}}{N^{2k}}\right)^{\alpha/2}

∎

Since in the hypothesis of Theorem
19
,
F\in Lip(L)=Lip(L,1)
, we conclude that for any
N

c_{1}(n,N)=L\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}}{N^{k}}.

(123)

C.3.2
Step 2

Lemma 28
.

Suppose
A,B
are
n\times n
matrices and
F\in Lip(M)\cap C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then

\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(A))-F(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert\leq\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}\frac{\rk(A-B)}{n}.

Proof.

Let
D\coloneqq\rk(A-B)
. By the Interlacing Theorem for singular values
[
67
, Theorem 2.11]
, we know that

\sigma_{i-D}(A)\geq\sigma_{i}(B)\geq\sigma_{i+D}(A)

(124)

for any
n-D\geq i>D
, where the singular values are all sorted in decreasing order. Let
[a,b]
be an interval containing the support of
F
. Thanks to Lemma
24
, we know that
F=G-H
on
[a,b]
with
G,H
continuous, nonnegative (since
G(a)=F(a)=H(a)=0
), monotonously increasing and with
\|G\|_{\infty}+\|H\|_{\infty}=\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}
. Extend
G,H
so that they are constant outside
[a,b]
, while still being continuous.

\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(A))-F(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert+\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(\sigma_{i}(A))-H(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert.

Let us analyse the term with
G
, the other one is analogous. Using (
124
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-\sum_{i=n-D+1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(B))+\sum_{i=1}^{n-D}\Big[G(\sigma_{i+D}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))\Big]

\displaystyle\leq D\|G\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n-D+1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-\sum_{i=1}^{D}G(\sigma_{i}(B))+\sum_{i=D+1}^{n}\Big[G(\sigma_{i-D}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))\Big]

\displaystyle\geq-D\|G\|_{\infty},

thus

\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(\sigma_{i}(A))-G(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert\leq\frac{D\|G\|_{\infty}}{n}

and

\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(A))-F(\sigma_{i}(B))\right\rvert\leq D\frac{\|G\|_{\infty}+\|H\|_{\infty}}{n}=\frac{D\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}}{n}.

∎

Since
\rk(T_{n}(f_{N})-C_{n}(f_{N}))\leq 2N
for
N<n/2
, we can apply the above result and obtain

c_{2}(n,N)=2N\frac{\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}}{n}.

(125)

C.3.3
Step 3

Lemma 29
.

Let
F\in Lip(L)\cap C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and let
g
be a complex trigonometric polynomial of degree
d
. Then for
n>2d

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\sigma_{i}(C_{n}(g)))-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi L}{n}\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+4d\frac{\|F\|_{\infty}}{n}.

Proof.

If
n>2d
, notice that the singular values of
C_{n}(g)
are, up to the order,
g(2\pi i/n)
, so we have to estimate

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\left(\left\lvert g\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)\right\rvert\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert

that can be seen as the error of the rectangle rule applied to the function
F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)
.

The function
g(\theta)
can be expressed as a Laurent polynomial in
z=e^{\textnormal{i}\theta}
of degree
d
, thus it can have at most
2d
zeros. Let
I_{i}\coloneqq[i-1,i]\cdot 2\pi/n
, and notice that if
g(\theta)
is never zero in
I_{i}
, then
\lvert g(\theta)\rvert\in C^{1}(I_{i})
so we can expand by Taylor
\lvert g((i-1)2\pi/n+\theta)\rvert=\lvert g((i-1)2\pi/n)\rvert\pm\theta g^{\prime}(\xi_{\theta})
where
\xi_{\theta}\in I_{i}
depends on
\theta
. Using
F\in Lip(L)
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}F\left(\left\lvert g\left(\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right)\right\rvert\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}}^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}}^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}F\left(\left\lvert g\left(\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right)\right\rvert\right)-F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}}^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}L\left\lvert g(\theta)-g\left(\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right)\right\rvert d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{n}}\theta\lvert g^{\prime}(\xi_{\theta})\rvert d\theta\leq\frac{\pi L}{n^{2}}\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

If instead
g(\theta)
has a zero in
I_{i}
one can use the more immediate bound

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}F\left(\left\lvert g\left(\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}\right)\right\rvert\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{n}}^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq 2\frac{\|F\|_{\infty}}{n}

thus concluding that

\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\left(\left\lvert g\left(\frac{2\pi i}{n}\right)\right\rvert\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert g(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi L}{n}\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+4d\frac{\|F\|_{\infty}}{n}.

∎

Using Lemma
26
on
g=f_{N}
, the result shows that when
N<n/2
and
f\in C^{1}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
,

c_{3}(n,N)=4N\sqrt{N}\frac{\pi L}{n}\|f\|_{2}+4N\frac{\|F\|_{\infty}}{n}.

(126)

C.3.4
Step 4

Lemma 30
.

Let
f\in C^{k}_{per}[-\pi,\pi]
,
F\in Lip(L)
and
N\geq 0
.
Then

\left\lvert\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}}{N^{k}}.

Proof.

By triangular inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)d\theta-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert)d\theta\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\lvert F(\lvert f(\theta)\rvert)-F(\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert)\right\rvert d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\lvert\lvert f(\theta)\rvert-\lvert f_{N}(\theta)\rvert\right\rvert d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\lvert f(\theta)-f_{N}(\theta)\right\rvert d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2\pi}\|f-f_{N}\|_{1}\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}.

Eventually, Lemma
25
shows that

\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}}{N^{k}}

concluding the proof.
∎

The result shows that for any

c_{4}(N)=\frac{L}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\|f^{(k)}\|_{2}}{N^{k}}.
