Quasisymmetric Koebe Uniformization of metric surfaces Thanks: The first author was partially supported by the Simons Foundation Collaboration Grant, (638572, H.H.).
H. Hakobyan and J. Rehmert

Abstract
. We study when a metric surface X X can be mapped quasisymmetrically onto a circle domain D ⊂ ℂ D\subset\mathbb{C} with uniformly relatively separated boundary components. Bonk [ Bon11 ] proved that if X ⊂ ℂ ^ X\subset\hat{\mathbb{C}} and the boundary components of X X are uniformly relatively separated uniform quasicircles then X X is quasisymmetric to a circle domain. Merenkov and Wildrick [ MW11 ] showed that Bonk’s condition is not sufficient in the non-planar case. We prove that under some mild assumptions, a metric surface is quasisymmetric to a circle domain with uniformly relatively separated boundary components if and only if it is 2-TLP. The latter is a version of a condition introduced and studied by Bonk [ Bon11 ] . This answers a question of Merenkov and Wildrick in [ MW11 ] and it is also a natural generalization of Bonk’s result to non-planar metric surfaces.

1.
Introduction

A homeomorphism
f:X\to Y
between metric spaces is said to be a
(weakly) quasisymmetric mapping
if there is a constant
H\geq 1
such that for every triple of distinct points
x,y,z\in X
satisfying
d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{X}(x,z)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{Y}(f(x),f(z))}\leq H.

Two metric spaces
X
and
Y
are said to be quasisymmetrically equivalent, or simply quasisymmetric, if there exists a quasisymmetric map
f:X\to Y
.

One of the central questions of contemporary geometric mapping theory and analysis in metric spaces is the following problem, cf.
[
Bon06
]
:

Quasisymmetric Uniformization Problem.

Suppose
X
is a metric space homeomorphic to some “standard” metric space
Y
. When is
X
quasisymmetrically equivalent to
Y
?

In
[
BA56
]
Beurling and Ahlfors showed that a Jordan curve
J\subset\mathbb{C}
is a quasicircle, that is,
J
is quasisymmetric to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, if and only if it is of
bounded turning
. Recall that
X
is said to be of bounded turning if there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that for all distinct points
x,y\in X
there is a connected subset
\gamma\subset X
containing
x
and
y
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(\gamma)\leq Cd_{X}(x,y).

Tukia and Väisälä obtained a complete characterization of arbitrary, i.e. nonplanar, quasicircles in
[
TV80
]
. They showed that
X
is quasisymmetric to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
if and only if it is of bounded turning and is
(metric) doubling
, that is, there exists a fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that every ball
B
in
X
of radius
0<R\leq\mathrm{diam}X
can be covered by
N
balls of radius
R/2
. Bounded turning and metric doubling are well-known to be quasisymmetrically invariant properties of metric spaces.

In the case of
Y=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, that is, the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the chordal metric, the quasisymmetric uniformization problem has been actively studied since the early 2000s. A fundamental result in this direction is due to Bonk and Kleiner
[
BK02
]
, who showed that if
X
is a metric space homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which is Ahlfors
2
-regular then there is a quasisymmetric mapping
f:X\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
if and only if
X
is linearly locally connected.
We refer to Section
2
for the precise definition of linear local connectivity and only recall here that a metric space
X
is
Ahlfors
2
-regular
if
Hausdorff
2
-measure of every ball
B=B(x,r)
in
X
is comparable to
r^{2}
.

Further important developments in characterizing
\mathbb{S}^{2}
up to quasisymmetric and quasiconformal mappings, as well as new proofs of Bonk and Kleiner’s Theorem have been obtained in
[
Raj17
,
LW20
,
NR23
,
NR24
,
Nta25a
]
. See also the recent survey

[
Nta25b
]
.

1.1.
Quasisymmetric Koebe Uniformization

In this paper we study the quasisymmetric uniformization problem in the case when
Y
is a circle domain in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, meaning that every boundary component of
Y
is either a point or a Euclidean circle. This is often referred to as the
Quasisymmetric Koebe Uniformization
, in analogy with Koebe’s classical theorem which states that every finitely connected domain in the plane is conformal to a circle domain.
We are mostly interested in the case when the circle domain
Y
has no more than countably many boundary components. We say that a metric space
X
is a
countably connected metric surface
if it is homeomorphic to a circle domain
Y
such that
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus Y
has countably many connected components.

Note that if
X
is quasisymmetric to a circle domain
Y\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}

then it has to satisfy certain natural necessary conditions.
For instance, all nontrivial boundary components of
X
would have to be
uniform quasicircles
, i.e. they should satisfy (
1.1
) with the same constant
C\geq 1
.

Even if
X
is a countably connected planar domain with boundary components which are uniform quasicircles, there does not necessarily exist a quasisymmetric map of
X
onto a circle domain. Indeed, let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}Q_{n}
where
Q_{n}
denotes the square
\left[n,n+1-\varepsilon_{n}\right]\times\left[0,1-\varepsilon_{n}\right]
for
n\geq 1
, and
0<\varepsilon_{n}<1/2
approaches
0
as
n\to\infty
. Then it can be easily seen that
X
is not quasisymmetric to a circle domain, see e.g.
[
Bon11
]
. The key obstruction for the existence of a quasisymmetry in this example is the fact that the boundary components are not separated well enough. To make this notion precise we first define
relative distance
between disjoint compact connected subsets
E
and
F
of a metric space
X
as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta(E,F):=\frac{\mathrm{dist}(E,F)}{\min\{\mathrm{diam}E,\mathrm{diam}F\}}.

Unless otherwise stated, we will assume that neither of the subsets
E
and
F
are singletons.

A family
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of compact connected non-point subsets of
X
is said to be
uniformly relatively separated
if there is a constant
c>0
such that for all distinct
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Delta(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j})>c.

In
[
Bon11
]
Bonk proved that assuming uniform relative separation is enough to establish some very strong uniformization results in the planar setting.

Theorem 1.1

(Bonk
[
Bon11
]
)
.

A countably connected domain
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
is quasisymmetric to a circle domain with uniformly relatively separated boundary circles if and only if the boundary components of
X
are uniformly relatively separated uniform quasicircles.

Theorem
1.1
is quantitative in the sense that the quasisymmetry constant
H
of the mapping
f
from
X
onto the circle domain depends only on the constants in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). This fact is crucial, since it allows one to prove the result for finitely connected domains and pass to the limit as the number of boundary components tends to infinity. This also allows one to obtain similar uniformization results for other spaces which can be approximated by finitely connected domains, e.g. the classical Sierpiński carpet.

It is natural to ask if Theorem
1.1
holds if
X
is not assumed to be a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Merenkov and Wildrick showed that this is not the case
[
MW11
, Thm. 1.5]
by constructing a countably connected metric surface
X
satisfying (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) which cannot be embedded into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
quasisymmetrically.

In this paper, we characterize a large class of metric surfaces which are quasisymmetric to circle domains in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with uniformly relatively separated boundary components. In doing so, we also extend Bonk’s Theorem
1.1
to the non-planar setting. In particular, in the context of Ahlfors
2
-regular spaces we prove the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Quasisymmetric Koebe Uniformization)
.

Let
X
be an Ahlfors 2-regular countably connected metric surface. Then
X
is quasisymmetric to a circle domain
Y\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
with uniformly relatively separated boundary circles if and only if

(1)

\overline{X}
is compact,

(2)

X
is of bounded turning,

(3)

X
is 2-transboundary Loewner.

Note that every circle domain
Y\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
clearly satisfies (1) and (2) above. Since these properties are quasisymmetrically invariant,

X
must satisfy them as well. The key novelty of Theorem
1.2
is condition
(3)
, which is defined in terms of Schramm’s transboundary modulus
[
Sch95
]
and which we briefly recall next in the context of Ahlfors
2
-regular spaces. We refer to Section
3
for a more complete and general discussion of transboundary modulus and its properties.

1.2.
Transboundary Loewner Properties

Suppose that
X
is an Ahlfors
2
-regular space which is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,
\partial X
is compact, and the collection of boundary components of
X
, denoted by
\partial_{0}X
, is countable. Denote by
\overline{X}_{\partial_{0}X}
the topological quotient of
\overline{X}
obtained by collapsing each element of
\partial_{0}X
to a point.

Given a family
\Gamma
of curves in
\overline{X}
(or more generally in
\overline{X}_{\partial_{0}X}
),
transboundary modulus
of
\Gamma
is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}_{\partial_{0}X}\Gamma=\inf_{\rho}\left\{\int_{X}\rho^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{2}+\sum_{x\in\partial_{0}X}\rho^{2}(x)\right\},

where the infimum is over all Borel functions
\rho:\overline{X}_{\partial_{0}X}\to[0,\infty)
which are
admissible for
\Gamma
, that is, for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

(1.4)

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}(\gamma):=\int_{\gamma\cap X}\rho ds+\sum_{x\in\gamma\cap\partial_{0}X}\rho(x)\geq 1.

We say
X
satisfies
2
-
transboundary Loewner property
(or is
2
-TLP) if there is a decreasing function
\Psi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that for every pair
E
and
F
of disjoint, non-degenerate continua in
X
and every choice of two distinct boundary components
k_{i}
and
k_{j}
of
X
we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus k_{i}\cup k_{j})\geq\Psi(\Delta(E,F)),

where
\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus k_{i}\cup k_{j})
denotes the family of curves in
\overline{X}
that connect
E
and
F
and do not intersect
k_{i}\cup k_{j}
. The parameter
2
in this definition refers to the fact that the curves in
\overline{X}
that connect
E
and
F
are assumed not to intersect two of the boundary components
k_{i}
and
k_{j}
of
X
.

Remark 1.3
.

Sufficient conditions for the existence of a quasisymmetry from a non-planar countably connected metric surface
X
onto a circle domain
Y\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
were previously obtained in
[
MW11
]
. In particular, it was shown that an Ahlfors
2
-regular metric surface
X
satisfies this if, in addition to some natural necessary conditions, the diameters of the boundary components of
X
satisfy a square-summability condition, see
[
MW11
, Thm. 1.4]
. Since this condition is neither necessary for quasisymmetric uniformization nor is quasisymmetrically invariant, they wondered if one could provide quasisymmetrically invariant conditions characterizing Ahlfors
2
-regular countably connected metric surfaces which can be uniformized by circle domains. Condition (3) in Theorem
1.2
provides such a condition.

Remark 1.4
.

In
[
HL23
]
, the authors introduced a weaker condition than (
1.5
), requiring only lower bounds on
\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X})
. They referred to this as the
transboundary Loewner property (TLP)
and showed that it is a necessary condition for the existence of a quasisymmetric embedding of a finitely connected metric surface into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Moreover, they conjectured in
[
HL23
]
that TLP is also sufficient for
X
to be quasisymmetrically equivalent to a circle domain in certain cases.
Theorem
1.2
shows that this is indeed the case if one assumes a slightly stronger 2-TLP condition (
1.5
).

This paper is organized as follows. Sections
2
and
3
discuss the established theory of quasisymmetric mappings and quasiconformal mappings, as well as modulus and the Loewner property. In Sections
4
,
5
and
6
we define the transboundary Loewner property and study some examples. In Section
7
we consider the modulus estimates in circle domains and Section
8
contains the proof of the main theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

Everywhere below we say a set is countable if it is either countably infinite, finite, or empty. Moreover, unless otherwise stated, everywhere below we will assume that
(X,d,\mu)
is a metric measure space such that
(X,d)
is a separable metric space and
\mu
is a locally finite Borel regular measure.

2.1.
Modulus

An
interval
is a non-empty, connected subset of
\mathbb{R}
(note this includes singletons). Given a metric space
(X,d)
, a curve in
X
is a continuous function
\gamma:I\rightarrow X
where
I
is an interval. We call the curve open, closed, or compact if
I
is open, closed, or compact respectively. If
I^{\prime}\subset I
is an interval, we call the restriction of
\gamma
to
I^{\prime}
a subcurve of
\gamma
. We will often abuse notation and denote
\gamma(I)
by
\gamma
; moreover, we will abuse language and refer to
\gamma(I)
as a curve. If
\gamma
is a singleton, we say the curve is constant.

Given a curve
\gamma
in
X
we will denote by
\ell(\gamma)
its length. We will say a curve
\gamma
is rectifiable if
\ell(\gamma)<\infty
. We say
\gamma
is locally rectifiable if every compact subcurve of
\gamma
is rectifiable.

We recall the definition of modulus of families of curves in quite a general context and refer to
[
Hei01
]
and
[
HKST15
]
for further discussion of the concept and its properties.

Let
\Gamma
be a collection of curves in
X
, and
\rho:X\rightarrow[0,\infty]
a Borel function. We say
{\rho}
is admissible for
{\Gamma}
, denoted
\rho\wedge\Gamma
, if for all locally rectifiable curves
\gamma\in\Gamma
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho\ ds\geq 1,

where integration is with respect to arclength. The
modulus of
{\Gamma}
is defined as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\bmod(\Gamma):=\inf_{\rho\wedge\Gamma}\int_{X}\rho^{2}d\mu.

It will be convenient to define the following short hand notation when the ambient measure space is clear:
\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\rho\ ds
and
A(\rho):=\int_{X}\rho^{2}d\mu.
These quantities will be called the
\rho
-length
of
\gamma
and
\rho
-area
of
X
, respectively.

For
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
families of curves in
X
, we say
\Gamma_{1}

minorizes

\Gamma_{2}
, denoted
\Gamma_{1}<\Gamma_{2}
, if every curve in
\Gamma_{2}
contains a subcurve in
\Gamma_{1}
.

The following properties of modulus are well-known (see
[
HKST15
]
) and will be used repeatedly below.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
(X,d)
separable and
\mu
a locally finite Borel regular measure. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Gamma_{k}
be a collection of curves in
X
.

(i)

\bmod(\emptyset)=0.

(ii)

If
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}
, then
\bmod(\Gamma_{1})\leq\bmod(\Gamma_{2}).
(Monotonicity)

(iii)

If
\Gamma_{1}<\Gamma_{2}
, then
\bmod(\Gamma_{1})\geq\bmod(\Gamma_{2}).
(Overflowing)

(iv)

\bmod(\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{k})\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bmod(\Gamma_{k})
. (Subadditivity)

(v)

If there are pairwise disjoint Borel sets,
B_{k}\subset X
, such that
\gamma_{k}\subset B_{k}
for all
\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma_{k}
, then

\bmod(\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{k})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bmod(\Gamma_{k}).

2.2.
Quasiconformality

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X}),(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be metric measure spaces with
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
being separable metric spaces, and
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
locally finite Borel regular measures. For
K\geq 1
, we say a homeomorphism
f:X\rightarrow Y
is
(geometrically)

K-quasiconformal
if for all families of curves
\Gamma
in
X
, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\bmod(\Gamma)\leq\bmod(f(\Gamma))\leq K\bmod(\Gamma).

There is also another the so-called
analytic definition of quasiconformality
that will be used below. It will appeal to the theory of Newton-Sobolev functions on metric measure spaces. We will not define the Newton-Sobolev class here, as we are not going to use it directly.

Definition 2.2

(
[
Wil12
]
)
.

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})
be a metric measure space with
(X,d_{X})
separable and
\mu_{X}
a locally finite Borel regular measure. Given a metric measure space

(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
and a homeomorphism
f:X\rightarrow Y
, we say a Borel function
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
upper gradient
of
f
if

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}g\ ds\geq d_{Y}(f(\gamma(a)),f(\gamma(b))),

for all rectifiable curves
\gamma
in
X
.
We say
g
is a
weak upper gradient
of
f
if (
2.4
) holds for all rectifiable curves
\gamma
in
X

except for a family of zero modulus
.

A function
g
is said to be a
minimal weak upper gradient
if for every weak upper gradient
h
we have

\displaystyle h\geq g

\mu_{X}
-almost everywhere.

Let
K\geq 1
be given. A homeomorphism
f:X\to Y
is
(analytically) K-quasiconformal
if
f\in N_{\text{loc}}^{1,2}(X:Y)
(the Newton-Sobolev class, see
[
HKST01
]
), and

g_{f}(x)^{2}\leq KJ_{f}(x)

for
\mu_{X}
-almost every
x\in X
,
where
g_{f}
is the minimal weak upper gradient of
f
, and

J_{f}(x):=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{Y}(f(B(x,r)))}{\mu_{X}(B(x,r))}.

The following theorem shows that the analytic and geometric definitions of quasiconformality are equivalent in remarkable generality.

Theorem 2.3

(Williams
[
Wil12
]
)
.

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X}),(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be metric measure spaces with
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
separable and
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
locally finite Borel regular measures. Let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a homeomorphism. Then the following are equivalent with the same constant
K\geq 1
.

•

f\in N_{\text{loc}}^{1,2}(X:Y)
, and for
\mu_{X}
-almost every
x\in X
,

g_{f}(x)^{2}\leq KJ_{f}(x).

•

For every collection of curves,
\Gamma
, in
X
, we have

\bmod(\Gamma)\leq K\bmod(f(\Gamma)).

3.
Transboundary Modulus

Transboundary modulus was first introduced as transboundary extremal length by Schramm
[
Sch95
]
and was used for giving an alternative proof to the countable case of Koebe’s conjecture. He defined it for domains in the sphere; the definition given here will be more general.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
\mu
a locally finite Borel measure on
X
.
Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a finite or countable collection of pairwise disjoint continua in
X
. We denote
K=\cup_{i\in I}K_{i}
and
D=X\setminus K
. Unless otherwise stated, below we will assume that
D
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
.

Given
\mathcal{K}
as above we define an equivalence relation on
X
as follows:

\displaystyle a\sim b\,\Leftrightarrow\,a=b,\mbox{ or }a,b\in K_{i}\mbox{ for some }i\in I.

We will denote by
X_{\mathcal{K}}
the quotient space
X/\sim
equipped with the quotient topology and call
X_{\mathcal{K}}

the
\mathcal{K}
-quotient of
X
. Let
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}:X\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{K}}
be the quotient map. Notice that
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
is injective on
D
; thus,
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}|_{D}
is a homeomorphism. Let
D_{\mathcal{K}}:=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(D)
. For each
i\in I
,
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{i})
is a singleton in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
; define
k_{i}=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{i})
. Let
k=\cup_{i\in I}k_{i}
. Suppose we equip
(X,d)
with a Borel measure
\mu
. We can define a Borel measure
\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
on
X_{\mathcal{K}}
by letting

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{K}}(E)

\displaystyle=\mu(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(D_{\mathcal{K}}\cap E))+\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{k_{i}}(E)

\displaystyle=\mu(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(E\setminus k))+\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{k_{i}}(E\cap k)

for every measurable set
E\subset X_{\mathcal{K}}
. Note that we only need
\mu
to be defined on
D
for
\mu_{\mathcal{K}}
to be well-defined.

As before, by a curve in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
, we mean a continuous function
\gamma:J\rightarrow X_{\mathcal{K}}
where
J\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval. We may also call
\gamma
a transboundary curve. We will often abuse notation and write
\gamma(J)=\gamma
. If
\gamma\subset D_{\mathcal{K}}
, then there is a curve
\gamma^{\prime}\subset D
with
\gamma=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma^{\prime})
. Sometimes we will abuse notation and write
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
if it is clear that the curve is in
D_{\mathcal{K}}
. Since
D_{\mathcal{K}}
is homeomorphic to a domain, it must be open in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
. Hence
\gamma^{-1}(D_{\mathcal{K}})
must be a relatively open subset of
J
; thus, it is a union of relatively open subintervals. Let
\{J_{m}\}_{m\in I_{\gamma}}
be the collection of relatively open subintervals of
J
such that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{m\in I_{\gamma}}J_{m}=\gamma^{-1}(D_{\mathcal{K}}).

Observe that
I_{\gamma}
is empty if and only if
\gamma(t)=k_{i}
for some
i\in I
, that is
\gamma
is a constant curve in
k
. If
I_{\gamma}
has one element, then
\gamma\subset D_{\mathcal{K}}
. For each
m\in I_{\gamma}
, define the subcurve
\gamma_{m}:J_{m}\rightarrow D
of
\gamma
so that
\gamma_{m}(t)=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\gamma(t)).
Note that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{m\in I_{\gamma}}\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma_{m})=\gamma\setminus k.

If
\gamma
is non-constant, then
\gamma_{m}
is non-constant for all
m\in I_{\gamma}
. We say
\gamma
is locally rectifiable relative
\mathcal{K}
if
\gamma_{m}
is locally rectifiable for all
m\in I_{\gamma}
. Given a Borel function
\rho:D\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, we will define the
\rho
-length of
\gamma
relative
\mathcal{K}
by

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma):=\sum_{m\in I_{\gamma}}\ell_{\rho}(\gamma_{m}).

For a curve
\gamma
in
X
, we will often use the following notational shortcuts for
\{\gamma_{m}\}
corresponding to
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)
.

\displaystyle\ell(\gamma\setminus K)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in I_{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)}}\ell(\gamma_{m})

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}(\gamma\setminus K)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in I_{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)}}\ell_{\rho}(\gamma_{m})

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma\setminus K}\rho\ ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in I_{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)}}\int_{\gamma_{m}}\rho\ ds

Let
\Gamma
be a family of curves in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
. A
(transboundary) mass distribution
is a function

\varrho:D\cup\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}\to[0,\infty]

such that
\rho:=\varrho|_{D}
is a non-negative Borel function, and
\rho_{i}\geq 0
for each
i\in I
. We will call
\rho_{i}
the weight corresponding to
K_{i}
.

Alternatively, a mass distribution can be thought of as a function
\varrho:X_{\mathcal{K}}\to[0,\infty]
such that
\rho=\varrho|_{X_{\mathcal{K}}\setminus k}
is a non-negative Borel function, and
\rho_{i}:=\varrho(k_{i})\geq 0,\forall i\in I
.

We say
\varrho
is
admissible for
{\Gamma}
relative
\mathbf{\mathcal{K}}
, denoted
\varrho\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma
, if for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
which are locally rectifiable relative
\mathcal{K}
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\ell_{\varrho}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma):=\ell_{\rho}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)+\sum_{k_{i}\in\gamma}\rho_{i}\geq 1.

Given a mass distribution
\varrho
we define its
mass
by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{\mathcal{K}}(\varrho)&:=\int_{X\setminus K}\rho^{2}d\mu+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\\
&\,=\int_{X_{\mathcal{K}}}\varrho^{2}d\mu_{\mathcal{K}}.\end{split}

Transboundary modulus of
{\Gamma}
is defined as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)&:=\inf_{\varrho\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma}A_{\mathcal{K}}(\varrho)\\
&\,=\inf_{\varrho\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma}\bigg(\int_{X\setminus K}\rho^{2}d\mu+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\bigg).\end{split}

If
\Gamma
is a family of curves in
X
, we write
\varrho\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma
if
\varrho\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma))
, and we define

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma):=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)).

In general, transboundary modulus takes on values in
[0,\infty]
. Observe that if
\mathcal{K}
is empty, or if every
\gamma\in\Gamma
is disjoint from
K
, then this definition coincides with the definition of modulus. Much like modulus, if there are no admissible mass distributions for
\Gamma
, then
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=\infty
. This happens, for example, if
\Gamma
contains a constant curve outside of
K
. Note that if
\Gamma
has no curves which are locally rectifiable relative
\mathcal{K}
, then the zero distribution is admissible for
\Gamma
and
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=0
.

Next we list some of the useful properties of transboundary modulus analogous to those of the classical modulus. We do not present the proofs of these properties since they are well-known and follow the similar arguments for classical modulus, cf.
[
Bon11
,
HL23
,
Mer10
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
\mu
a locally finite Borel regular measure. Fix a countable collection of pairwise disjoint continua
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, let
K=\cup_{i\in I}K_{i}
, and suppose
X\setminus K
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Gamma_{n}
be a collection of curves in
X
.

(i)

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\emptyset)=0.

(ii)

If
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}
, then
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{1})\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{2}).
(Monotonicity)

(iii)

If
\Gamma_{1}<\Gamma_{2}
, then
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{1})\geq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{2}).
(Overflowing)

(iv)

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{n})\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{n})
. (Subadditivity)

(v)

Suppose there are pairwise disjoint Borel sets,
B_{n}\subset X
, such that for all
i\in I
there is at most one
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
K_{i}\cap B_{n}\neq\emptyset
. If, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma_{n}\subset B_{n}
for all
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{n}
, then

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{n})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{n}).

We remark that one can make corresponding statements about families of curves in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
by pulling them back to
X
. For example, if
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
are collections of curves in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
with
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}
, then one can find curve families
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime},\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
in
X
with
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}\subset\Gamma_{2}^{\prime}
and
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{1}^{\prime})=\Gamma_{1}
and
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{2}^{\prime})=\Gamma_{2}
, so that
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{1})\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{2})
.

A key property of transboundary modulus is that, just like classical modulus, it is essentially preserved under quasiconformal mappings. Since our setting is more general than usual we provide the details for the reader’s convenience. Similar lemmas on the quasiconformal quasi-invariance of transboundary modulus were also given in
[
Bon11
]
and
[
HL23
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X}),(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be a metric measure spaces with
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
locally finite Borel regular measures. Pick any countable collection of pairwise disjoint continua,
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
X
and
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1}\}_{i\in I}
in
Y
. Let
k_{i}=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(K_{i})
,
K=\cup_{i\in I}K_{i}
and
j_{i}=\pi_{\mathcal{J}}(J_{i})
,
J=\cup_{i\in I}J_{i}
. Suppose
X\setminus K
and
Y\setminus J
are homeomorphic to domains in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Suppose there is a homeomorphism
f:X_{\mathcal{K}}\rightarrow Y_{\mathcal{J}}
with
f(k_{i})=j_{i}
for all
i\in I
such that
\pi_{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\circ f\circ\pi_{\mathcal{K}}:X\setminus K\rightarrow Y\setminus J
is geometrically
H
-quasiconformal for some
H\geq 1
. Then

(3.4)

\displaystyle H^{-1}\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(f(\Gamma))\leq H\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)

for any family of curves
\Gamma
in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we will mildly abuse notation and write
f
instead of
(\pi_{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\circ f\circ\pi_{\mathcal{K}})|_{X\setminus K}
. By Theorem
2.3
we have that
f
is analytically quasiconformal on
X\setminus K
. Williams
[
Wil12
]
showed that if
\gamma
is any absolutely continuous curve in
X\setminus K
,
f
is absolutely continuous on
\gamma
, and
\rho
is a non-negative Borel function on
Y\setminus J
with
A(\rho)<\infty
, then we have that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}(\rho\circ f)g_{f}\ ds\geq\int_{f(\gamma)}\rho\ ds.

Every compact rectifiable curve has an absolutely continuous arc-length parameterization, see e.g. Proposition 5.1.8 in
[
HKST15
]
. Thus every locally rectifiable
\gamma
in
X\setminus K
satisfies (
3.5
) if
f
is absolutely continuous on every compact subcurve of
\gamma
and
A(\rho)<\infty
. Let
\Gamma_{f}
be the collection of curves in
X\setminus K
on which
f
is not absolutely continuous. Then
\bmod(\Gamma_{f})=0
provided
f\in N^{1,2}_{\text{loc}}(X\setminus K:Y\setminus J)
, see e.g.
[
Sha00
]
. Now, let
\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}}
be the family of curves in
X_{\mathcal{K}}
which contain a subcurve in
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{f})
. By the overflowing property we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})=0
.
Therefore

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})+\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}}),

and we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)
.

Now, pick any
\varrho=(\rho;\{\rho_{i}\}_{i\in I})\wedge_{\mathcal{J}}f(\Gamma)
. Suppose that
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(f(\Gamma))<\infty
. Then we can assume that
A(\rho)<\infty
. Let
\rho^{\prime}=(\rho\circ f)g_{f}
and
\varrho^{\prime}=(\rho^{\prime};\{\rho_{i}\}_{i\in I})
. We would like to sow that
\varrho^{\prime}
is admissible for
\Gamma
relative
\mathcal{K}
.

Pick any locally rectifiable
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}}
. Since
X\setminus K
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, we can construct countably many curves
\gamma_{m}
in
X\setminus K
such that
\ell_{\rho^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)=\sum_{m}\ell_{\rho^{\prime}}(\gamma_{m})
, where each
\gamma_{m}
is locally rectifiable (see the discussion preceding the definition of transboundary modulus). Since
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}}
, we have that
f
is absolutely continuous on any compact subcurve of
\gamma_{m}
for every
m
and therefore
\ell_{\rho^{\prime}}(\gamma_{m})\geq\ell_{\rho}(f(\gamma_{m}))
. From the definition of
\varrho^{\prime}
it follows that

\ell_{\varrho^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)\geq\ell_{\varrho}^{\mathcal{J}}(f(\gamma))\geq 1,
or that
\varrho^{\prime}\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}}
.

Finally, since
f
is analytically quasiconformal we have

\displaystyle\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{f}^{\mathcal{K}})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X\setminus K}(\rho\circ f)^{2}g_{f}^{2}\ d\mu_{X}+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq H\bigg(\int_{X\setminus K}(\rho\circ f)^{2}J_{f}\ d\mu_{X}+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq H\bigg(\int_{Y\setminus J}\rho^{2}\ d\mu_{Y}+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\bigg).

By infimizing over all admissible
\varrho
, we obtain the right hand inequality in (
3.4
). Note that this inequality still holds if
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(f(\Gamma))=\infty
. Applying the same argument to
f^{-1}
we get the other inequality in (
3.4
).
∎

Like modulus, transboundary modulus has nice properties for upper Ahlfors 2-regular spaces.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
\mu
a locally finite Borel regular measure. Fix some
x_{0}\in X
. Suppose there exists constants
C_{0},R_{0}>0
such that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mu(B(x_{0},r))\leq C_{0}r^{2}

for all
0<r<R_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be any countable family of disjoint continua in
X
, none of which contain
x_{0}
, and suppose
X\setminus K
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Then if
\Gamma
is a family of non-constant curves such that every
\gamma\in\Gamma
passes through
x_{0}
then

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{x_{0}\}}(\Gamma)=0.

Proof.

First, notice that there is some
r>0
such that
B(x_{0},r)\cap K=\emptyset
. Notice that every curve in
\Gamma
contains a non-constant subcurve in
B(x_{0},r)
that goes through
x_{0}
, call the family of these subcurves
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Then
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma^{\prime})=0
, by Proposition
3.1
. To show that
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}\cup\{x_{0}\}}(\Gamma)=0
let
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{K}\cup\{x_{0}\}
. Observe that if
\Gamma_{n}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ |\ \gamma\nsubseteq B(x_{0},1/n)\}
then
\Gamma=\cup_{n}\Gamma_{n}
since no curves are constant. Thus, it suffices to show, for sufficiently large
n
, that
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma_{n})=0
. Take
N>n>1/r
. Then every
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
contains a subcurve in
\Gamma_{n,N}:=\Gamma(\overline{B(x_{0},1/N)},X\setminus B(x_{0},1/n);X)
and so
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma_{n})\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma_{n,N})=\bmod(\Gamma_{n,N})
. By the well-known modulus bound for spaces with
\mu
satisfying (
3.6
), see e.g. Proposition 5.3.9. in
[
HKST15
]
, we have
\bmod(\Gamma_{n,N})\leq{C}/{\log(N/n)}
, where
C
depends only on
C_{0}
. Taking
N\rightarrow\infty
shows then that
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma_{n})=0
.
∎

We remark that for modulus, one usually assumes the curves be non-constant because, for
\gamma(t)=x_{0}
,
\bmod(\{\gamma\})=\infty
. However, for transboundary modulus, if
\mathcal{K}
contains
\{x_{0}\}
we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\{\gamma\})=1
as the only admissible distributions give weight 1 to
\{x_{0}\}
. We wrap up our discussion of singletons in the following result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
\mu
locally finite. Suppose
X
is upper Ahlfors 2-regular. Let
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{J}
be countable collections of disjoint continua in
X
with
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{J}
. Suppose
X\setminus K
and
X\setminus J
are homeomorphic to domains in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{J}\setminus\mathcal{K}
consists only of singletons which are all isolated in
J
. Then for any family of non-constant curves
\Gamma
in
X
,

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{J}\setminus\mathcal{K}=\{\{s_{i}\}\}_{i\in I}
. Let
\Gamma_{i}
be the family of non-constant curves in
X
which go through
s_{i}
. Define
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\setminus\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i}
. Since none of the curves in
\Gamma^{\prime}
intersect anything in
\mathcal{J}\setminus\mathcal{K}
, we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma^{\prime})=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma^{\prime}).
Thus it suffices to show that
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma)=\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma^{\prime})
and
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma^{\prime})
. Since
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
, monotonicity gives one direction on both equalities. On the other hand, by subadditivity of tansboundary modulus and by Proposition
3.3
we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in I}\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma_{i})=0.
Since
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}\cup(\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i})
it follows that

\displaystyle\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma^{\prime})+\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i})=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma^{\prime}).

For
\mathcal{J}
, notice that because each
s_{i}
is isolated, we can say that
X\setminus J_{i}
is homeomorphic to a domain where
J_{i}=J\setminus\{s_{i}\}
. By Proposition
3.3
, we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma_{i})=0
and by subadditivity
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\cup_{i\in I}\Gamma_{i})=0
. Therefore, just like for
\mathcal{K}
, we have
\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma)\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma^{\prime}).
As noted above, this completes the proof.
∎

4.
Transboundary Loewner Properties

As usual we assume that
(X,d)
is a separable metric space and
\mu
is a locally finite Borel regular measure on
X
.

We say
X
is a
Loewner space
if there is a decreasing function
\Psi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that for all disjoint continua
E,F\subset X
we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\bmod(\Gamma(E,F;X))\geq\Psi(\Delta(E,F)).

The notion of a Loewner space was introduced by Heinonen and Koskela
[
HK98
]
and has since become one of the most important concepts in analysis on metric spaces. Next we define transboundary analog of the Loewner property. This has appeared implicitly in
[
Bon11
]
and
[
Mer10
]
and more explicitly in
[
HL23
]
. The definition given below differs slightly from these appearances.

Suppose that
X
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,
\partial X
is compact, and
\partial_{0}X
is countable. We say
X
is
transboundary Loewner
if there is a decreasing function
\Psi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}))\geq\Psi(\Delta(E,F)),

whenever
E
and
F
are disjoint and non-degenerate continua in
X
.

From the definitions above it immediately follows that if
X
is Loewner then it is also transboundary Loewner.

In
[
Bon11
]
Bonk studied spherical domains for which neither (
4.1
) nor (
4.2
) holds, however a similar condition still holds if one considers a different conformal invariant. In this paper we take Bonk’s condition as a starting point of our study and give the following definition. We recall that
X
is assumed to be a separable metric space equipped with a locally finite Borel regular measure
\mu
.

Suppose
X
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,
\partial X
is compact, and
\partial_{0}X
is countable. Given an integer
N\geq 0
, we say
X
is
N-transboundary Loewner
if there is a decreasing function
\Psi:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that for all disjoint, non-degenerate continua
E
and
F
in
X
we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus K))\geq\Psi(\Delta(E,F))

whenever
\mathcal{K}\subset\partial_{0}X
with
\#(\mathcal{K})\leq N
.

If
X
is Loewner, then it will be
N
-transboundary Loewner for all
N
:

\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus K))\geq\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;X))=\bmod(\Gamma(E,F;X)).

Conversely, if
\partial_{0}X
is finite and
N\geq\#(\partial_{0}X)
, then
X
being
N
-transboundary Loewner gives
X
is Loewner by selecting
\mathcal{K}=\partial_{0}X
. Notice that being
0
-transboundary Loewner is identical to be transboundary Loewner, as
\overline{X}\setminus K=\overline{X}
. In fact, if
X
is
N
-transboundary Loewner for any
N
, then
X
is transboundary Loewner, as one can always take
\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
. More generally, for all
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq N
, if
X
is
M
-transboundary Loewner, then
X
is
N
-transboundary Loewner.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X}),(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be metric measure spaces with
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
locally finite. Suppose
X
and
Y
are homeomorphic to domains in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,
\partial X,\partial Y
are compact, and
\partial_{0}X,\partial_{0}Y
are countable. Suppose
f:X\rightarrow Y
is quasisymmetric and geometrically quasiconformal. If
X
is
N
-transboundary Loewner, then
Y
is
N
-transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

Pick any non-degenerate, disjoint continua
A,B\subset Y
and any
\mathcal{K}\subset\partial_{0}Y
with
\#(\mathcal{K})\leq N
. Let
A^{\prime}=f^{-1}(A)
and
B^{\prime}=f^{-1}(B)
, noticing that they are disjoint, non-degenerate continua in
X
. Since
f
is quasisymmetric,
f
extends to a homeomorphism of the completions. This means that
f
gives rise to a homeomorphism
f:(\overline{X})_{\partial_{0}X}\rightarrow(\overline{Y})_{\partial_{0}Y}
. Let
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\subset\partial_{0}X
correspond to
\mathcal{K}
, and notice
\#(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})\leq N
. Since
f
is
N
-transboundary Loewner,

\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(A^{\prime},B^{\prime};\overline{X}\setminus K^{\prime}))\geq\Psi^{\prime}(\Delta(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}))

where
\Psi^{\prime}
is the function obtained from the transboundary Loewner property of
X
. Now apply Lemma
3.2
on
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
. Letting
f^{-1}
be
\nu
-quasisymmetric and
f
be
H
-quasiconformal, and using quasi-invariance of relative distance, we conclude

\displaystyle\bmod_{\partial_{0}Y}(\Gamma(A,B;\overline{Y}\setminus K))

\displaystyle\geq H^{-1}\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(A^{\prime},B^{\prime};\overline{X}\setminus K^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geq H^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}(\Delta(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geq H^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}(\nu(2\Delta(A,B))).

The function
\Psi(t):=H^{-1}\ \Psi^{\prime}(\nu(2t))
is decreasing since
\Psi^{\prime}
is decreasing and
\nu
is increasing, so
Y
is
N
-transboundary Loewner.
∎

5.
Examples of Transboundary Loewner Spaces

Because the transboundary Loewner property is a quasisymmetric invariant, it can be used to classify when spaces are quasisymmetrically equivalent. It is, therefore, of interest to generate some examples of model spaces which are transboundary Loewner. Our goal will be to derive conditions on
\mathcal{K}
, a collection of pairwise disjoint continua in some Loewner
\Omega
, which are sufficient to conclude
\Omega\setminus K
is transboundary Loewner. Intuitively, these conditions will prohibit the continua in
\mathcal{K}
from being too “thin”. In particular, we will show that circle domains are transboundary Loewner, which was first shown by Merenkov
[
Mer10
]
(it follows from Proposition 5.3).

Definition 5.1

(Schramm
[
Sch95
]
)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Borel and
1\geq\tau>0
. We say
A
is
{\tau}
-fat
if, for all
x\in A
and
r>0
with
A\nsubseteq B(x,r)
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{2}(A\cap B(x,r))\geq\tau\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x,r)).

It’s not hard to see that disks are
1/4
-fat. Rectangles are
(2\pi c)^{-1}
-fat where
c
is the ratio of the larger side length to the shorter one. Similarly, ellipses are fat with
\tau
depending on the eccentricity.

For a general estimate on transboundary modulus, we will need the continua through which the curves go to be fat. This, however, won’t be enough. We will also need the following property.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\lambda\geq 1
. We say
A
is
{\lambda}
-quasiround
if, there is some
x\in A
and
r>0
with

B(x,r)\subset A\subset B(x,\lambda r).

A set is
\lambda
-quasiround for some
\lambda
if and only if it is bounded and has non-empty interior. What’s important is that we keep track of how thick an annulus containing the boundary must be. Disks are
\lambda
-quasiround for any
\lambda>1
. Like with fatness, rectangles and ellipses are quasiround with constants depending on their eccentricity. However, in general quasiround sets are not fat: take a Jordan domain with an outward pointing cusp; moreover, not all bounded fat sets are uniformly quasiround. That is, for sufficiently small
\tau
and for all
\lambda\geq 1
, there is a bounded
\tau
-fat set which is not
\lambda
-quasiround. Take, for instance,
\mathbb{D}\setminus\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
for small
\alpha
. With more effort, one can come up with a family of Jordan domains which are
\tau
-fat but not uniformly quasiround.

Proposition 5.3

(
[
Bon11
]
,
[
Mer10
]
,
[
HL23
]
)
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a continuum,
\lambda\geq 1
, and
\tau>0
. Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of pairwise disjoint,
\tau
-fat subsets of the plane satisfying
K_{i}\cap E\neq\emptyset
and

\lambda\mathrm{diam}(K_{i})\geq\mathrm{diam}(E)

for all
i\in I
. Then
\#(\mathcal{K})\leq(\lambda^{2}+6\lambda+1)/\tau
.

Proof.

Fix any point
e\in E
. Since
E
is a compact,
\mathrm{diam}(E)<\infty
. Notice
E\subset B[e,\mathrm{diam}(E)]
, and thus
K_{i}\cap B[e,\mathrm{diam}(E)]\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in I
. Define

I_{1}=\{i\in I\ |\ K_{i}\nsubseteq B(e,(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E))\}.

Define
I_{2}=I\setminus I_{1}
. For
i\in I_{1}
, we can say there is some
x_{i}\in\partial B(e,\mathrm{diam}(E))\cap K_{i}
. Notice that

B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))\subset B(e,(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E));

indeed, for
y\in B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))
, we can say

|y-e|\leq|y-x_{i}|+|x_{i}-e|\leq\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)+\mathrm{diam}(E).

Therefore, for
i\in I_{1}
, we can say
B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))
does not contain
K_{i}
. We can use fatness to say

\mathcal{H}^{2}(K_{i}\cap B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))\geq\tau\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))).

Now, for each
i\in I_{1}
, we can say
B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))\subset A[e,(1-\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E),(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E)]
. Since each
K_{i}
is disjoint, we can say

\displaystyle\pi(((1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E))^{2}-

\displaystyle((1-\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E))^{2})=\mathcal{H}^{2}(A[e,(1-\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E),(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E)])

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{H}^{2}(\cup_{i\in I_{1}}B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{H}^{2}(\cup_{i\in I_{1}}K_{i}\cap B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(K_{i}\cap B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle\geq\tau\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\pi(\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))^{2}

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\lambda^{-2}\mathrm{diam}(E)^{2}\#(I_{1}).

Thus

\#(I_{1})\leq\tau^{-1}\lambda^{2}((1+\lambda^{-1})^{2}-(1-\lambda^{-1})^{2})=\tau^{-1}\lambda^{2}(4\lambda^{-1})=\frac{4\lambda}{\tau}.

To estimate
\#(I_{2})
, notice that, by compactness of
K_{i}
there exists
x_{i},y_{i}\in K_{i}
with

d(x_{i},y_{i})=\mathrm{diam}(K_{i})\geq\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E).

Thus,
B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))
does not contain
K_{i}
. Use fatness to say

\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))\cap K_{i})\geq\tau\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))).

For
i\in I_{2}
, we have
K_{i}\subset B(e,(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E))
. Therefore, using disjointness of
K_{i}
,

\displaystyle\pi((1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E))^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(e,(1+\lambda^{-1})\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{H}^{2}(\cup_{i\in I_{2}}K_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(K_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(K_{i}\cap B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle\geq\tau\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(B(x_{i},\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E)))

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\pi(\lambda^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(E))^{2}

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\lambda^{-2}\mathrm{diam}(E)^{2}\#(I_{2}).

Hence,
\#(I_{2})\leq\tau^{-1}\lambda^{2}(1+\lambda^{-1})^{2}=\tau^{-1}(\lambda+1)^{2}.
Thus, we have
\#(\mathcal{K})=\#(I)=\#(I_{1})+\#(I_{2})\leq\tau^{-1}(4\lambda+(\lambda+1)^{2})=\tau^{-1}(\lambda^{2}+6\lambda+1).

∎

The following result, first shown by Bojarski
[
Boj88
]
, is well known. However, it is usually formulated for finite sums, and we’ll need it for infinite sums; we give an argument here which mimics a proof given by Merenkov
[
Mer10
]
.

Lemma 5.4

(Bojarski’s Lemma)
.

Let
\{B(x_{i},r_{i})\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of pairwise disjoint open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a countable collection of non-negative real numbers. Let
\lambda\geq 1
be given. There exists a constant,
c_{\lambda}
, depending only on
\lambda
, such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}\bigg)^{2}dA\leq c_{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg)^{2}dA=c_{\lambda}\pi\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}^{2}r_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Define the uncentered maximal operator of
\phi
by

M(\phi)(x,y)=\sup_{(x,y)\in B(z,r)}\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{B(z,r)}|\phi|\ dA.

Notice that, in particular, for
(x,y)\in B(x_{i},r_{i})
, we have

M(\phi)(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{\pi\lambda^{2}r_{i}^{2}}\int_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi|\ dA.

Hence,

\int_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}M(\phi)\ dA\geq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi|\ dA.

Recall that
M
is a bounded operator (see Duoandikoetxea
[
Duo01
]
Theorem 2.5 and following remarks): there exists a constant,
C
, such that

||M(\phi)||_{2}\leq C||\phi||_{2}

for all
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi|\ dA

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi|\ dA\hskip 90.0pt\text{(Monotone Convergence Theorem)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\int_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi|\ dA

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\lambda^{2}\int_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}M(\phi)\ dA

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}M(\phi)\ dA\hskip 120.0pt\text{(Disjointness)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}||M(\phi)||_{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}\hskip 110.0pt\text{(Cauchy-Schwarz)}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{2}||\phi||_{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}

for all
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. With no loss of generality, suppose
I\subset\mathbb{N}
and let
I_{n}=\{1,...,n\}\cap I
. Let
\phi_{n}=\sum_{i\in I_{n}}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}
. Notice
\phi_{n}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for all
n
and
\phi_{n}\leq\phi_{m}
for
n\leq m
. Suppose
||\phi_{n}||_{2}>0
for sufficiently large
n
(otherwise, the result is trivial). We use the above inequality to say

\displaystyle||\phi_{n}||^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}|\phi_{n}|\ dA\leq C\lambda^{2}||\phi_{n}||_{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}

\displaystyle||\phi_{n}||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}||\phi_{n}||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}.

Once again, we use the monotone convergence theorem to conclude

\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}||\phi_{n}||_{2}\leq C\lambda^{2}\bigg|\bigg|\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mathbb{I}_{B(x_{i},r_{i})}\bigg|\bigg|_{2}.

Squaring both sides gives the desired conclusion.
∎

The following lemma is similar to a lemma of Bonk
[
Bon11
]
, although he assumed uniform relative separation, and there is no such assumption here. It is also similar to a lemma of Merenkov
[
Mer10
]
, though he showed it only for circle domains.

Lemma 5.5

(
[
HL23
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Borel and
\Gamma
a collection of curves in
\Omega
. Fix
\tau>0
and
\lambda\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of pairwise disjoint,
\tau
-fat,
\lambda
-quasiround continua in
\Omega
. Suppose
\Omega\setminus K
is a domain. Then there are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
\lambda
and
\tau
such that

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)\geq\min(c_{1},c_{2}\bmod(\Gamma)).

Proof.

Let
c=(1+12\lambda+4\lambda^{2})/\tau
. Let
c_{1}:=1/(8c^{2})
. Suppose
\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)\leq 1/(8c^{2})
; as otherwise, we have nothing to show. For
\epsilon<1/(8c^{2})
, let
P=(\rho;\{\rho_{i}\}_{i\in I})\wedge_{\mathcal{K}}\Gamma
be such that

A_{\mathcal{K}}(P)\leq\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)+\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{4c^{2}}.

Since each
K_{i}
is
\lambda
-quasiround, we can find
x_{i}\in K_{i}
and
r_{i}>0
such that

B(x_{i},r_{i})\subset K_{i}\subset B(x_{i},\lambda r_{i}).

For brevity, let
B_{i}:=B(x,2\lambda r_{i})
. Define
g:\Omega\rightarrow[0,\infty)
as

g=2\bigg(\rho\ \mathbb{I}_{\Omega\setminus K}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}\cap\Omega}\bigg).

We claim
g\wedge\Gamma
. To see this, pick any
\gamma\in\Gamma
and define

I_{\gamma}:=\{i\in I\ |\ \gamma\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset,\ 2\lambda\mathrm{diam}(K_{i})\geq\mathrm{diam}(\gamma)\}.

Now if
i\in I\setminus I_{\gamma}
, then we either have that
\gamma\cap K_{i}=\emptyset
or

\mathrm{diam}(\gamma)>2\lambda\mathrm{diam}(K_{i})\geq 4\lambda r_{i}=\mathrm{diam}(B_{i});

the latter implies that
\gamma
is not contained in
B_{i}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\ell_{g}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=2\bigg(\int_{\gamma\setminus K}\rho\ ds+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\ell(\gamma\cap B_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 2\bigg(\int_{\gamma\setminus K}\rho\ ds+\sum_{\gamma\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset,i\in I\setminus I_{\gamma}}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\ell(\gamma\cap B_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 2\bigg(\int_{\gamma\setminus K}\rho\ ds+\sum_{\gamma\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset,i\in I\setminus I_{\gamma}}\rho_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle=2\bigg(\ell_{P}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)-\sum_{i\in I_{\gamma}}\rho_{i}\bigg)

Observe that
\rho_{i}\leq\sqrt{A_{\mathcal{K}}(P)}\leq 1/(2c)
; also, we can use Proposition
5.3
to say
\#(I_{\gamma})\leq c
. Hence
\sum_{i\in I_{\gamma}}\rho_{i}\leq\frac{\#(I_{\gamma})}{2c}\leq\frac{1}{2}.
This gives us admissibility:

\ell_{g}(\gamma)\geq 2\bigg(\ell_{P}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)-\sum_{i\in I_{\gamma}}\rho_{i}\bigg)\geq 2\bigg(\ell_{P}^{\mathcal{K}}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\geq 1.

We will now use
g
to estimate the modulus. Since
B(x_{i},r_{i})
are pairwise disjoint, we can use Bojarski’s Lemma (Lemma
5.4
) to say

\displaystyle\bmod(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}g^{2}\ dA=4\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\rho\ \mathbb{I}_{\Omega\setminus K}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}\cap\Omega}\bigg)^{2}dA

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\rho\ \mathbb{I}_{\Omega\setminus K}\bigg)^{2}+\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}\cap\Omega}\bigg)^{2}dA

\displaystyle=8\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\ dA+\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}\cap\Omega}\bigg)^{2}\ dA\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 8\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\ dA+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}}{\lambda r_{i}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{i}}\bigg)^{2}\ dA\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 8\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\ dA+c_{2\lambda}\pi\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}r_{i}^{2}}r_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=8\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\ dA+\frac{c_{2\lambda}\pi}{\lambda^{2}}\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 8\max(1,\frac{c_{2\lambda}\pi}{\lambda^{2}})\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\ dA+\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 8\max(1,\frac{c_{2\lambda}\pi}{\lambda^{2}})(\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma)+\epsilon)

By letting
c_{2}:=(8\max(1,\frac{c_{2\lambda}\pi}{\lambda^{2}}))^{-1}
and taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the result.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Borel set with countably many compact boundary components and
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a countable collection of pairwise disjoint continua in
\Omega
which are
\tau
-fat and
\lambda
-quasiround for some
\tau>0
,
\lambda\geq 1
. Let
K=\cup_{i\in I}K_{i}
, and suppose
\Omega\setminus K
is a domain. If
\Omega
is Loewner then
\Omega\setminus K
is transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be Loewner with decreasing function
\Psi
. Let

\Psi^{\prime}(t)=\min(c_{1},c_{2}\Psi(t)),

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are the constants from Lemma
5.5
. Then for any disjoint, non-degenerate continua
E,F\subset\Omega\setminus K
, by Lemma
5.5
we have

\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;\Omega))\geq\min(c_{1},c_{2}\bmod(\Gamma(E,F;\Omega)))\geq\min(c_{1},c_{2}\Psi(\Delta(E,F)))=\Psi^{\prime}(\Delta(E,F)).

Let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all connected components of
\partial\Omega
. To show
\Omega\setminus K
is transboundary Loewner, we need to show

\bmod_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{\Omega\setminus K}))\geq\Psi^{\prime}(\Delta(E,F)).

Let
K^{\prime}=\partial(\overline{\Omega}\setminus K)
, the boundary of the interior of
K
. Observe that
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(K^{\prime})=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(K)
, and thus
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\Omega)=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}((\Omega\setminus K)\cup K^{\prime})
.

\displaystyle\bmod_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{\Omega\setminus K}))

\displaystyle\geq\bmod_{\mathcal{J}\cup\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;(\Omega\setminus K)\cup K^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;(\Omega\setminus K)\cup K^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\bmod_{\mathcal{K}}(\Gamma(E,F;\Omega))\geq\Psi^{\prime}(\Delta(E,F)).

∎

Example 5.7
.

Countably connected circle domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
are transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

Let
X
be a countably connected circle domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the collection of non-degenerate, bounded complementary components of
X
and
\Omega:=X\cup K
. Then
\mathcal{K}
is a collection of closed disks, which are uniformly fat, uniformly quasiround continua. If
\Omega
is Loewner, then we can apply Corollary
5.6
to say
X
is transboundary Loewner. If none of the boundary components of
X
are points, then
\Omega
is either the plane or a disk, both of which are Loewner. If some of the boundary components are points, then
\Omega
is the plane or the disk with countably many points removed, and thus
\Omega
is Loewner as well.

Now let
X
be a countably connected circle domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To see this, note that the sphere is Loewner. If
X
is the sphere, then
X
is transboundary Loewner. If
X
is the sphere with only countably many points removed, then
X
is Loewner, which implies that
X
is transboundary Loewner. Suppose that
X
has at least one non-trivial complementary component:
D
. Rotate the sphere so that the center of
D
is the north pole; such a rotation is conformal and quasisymmetric. Use stereographic projection to map
X
into the plane. Recall that stereographic projection is conformal and it sends circles not touching the north pole to circles. Thus the image will be a bounded circle domain in the plane. Notice
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus D
is Loewner, and that its image under stereographic projection is bounded and linearly locally connected. Therefore, by a theorem of Heinonen and Koskela, see
[
HK98
]
, stereographic projection is quasisymmetric as a map from
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus D
, and thus its restriction to
X
is quasisymmetric. By quasi-invariance of transboundary modulus, the circle domain in the sphere is transboundary Loewner since the image in the plane is.
∎

One can make similar statements for countably connected square domains. Let us establish a larger class of uniformly fat, uniformly quasiround shapes. A subset of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called an open
\eta
-quasidisk , if it is the quasisymmetric image of
\mathbb{D}
for some
\eta
-quasisymmetry. A closed quasidisk is a quasisymmetric image of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. An
\eta
-quasicircle is an
\eta
-quasisymmetric image of
\partial\mathbb{D}
. We say a collection of quasicircles or quasidisks is uniform if they are all
\eta
-quasisymmetric images of a circle or disk for the same
\eta
. If one has a Jordan curve which is a quasicircle, its interior will be a quasidisk. The converse is true since quasisymmetries extend to the boundary. We will exclude a vast amount of theory of quasicircles; restricting our discussion to the following properties.

Proposition 5.8

(Bonk
[
Bon11
]
(Proposition 4.3))
.

An
\eta
-quasidisk is
\lambda
-quasiround with
\lambda
depending only on
\eta
.

Proposition 5.9

(Schramm
[
Sch95
]
(Corollary 2.3))
.

An
\eta
-quasidisk is
\tau
-fat with
\tau
depending only on
\eta
.

It becomes immediate then, that one can use Corollary
5.6
when
\mathcal{K}
is a collection of uniform quasidisks.

6.
Examples of
N
-Transboundary Loewner Spaces

Let us establish some examples (and non-examples) of
N
-transboundary Loewner spaces.

Example 6.1
.

There are domains which are transboundary Loewner and not 1-transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

We construct a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as follows. Define the following polar rectangle for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 1
:

A_{n}:=\{(r,\theta)\ |\ 1\leq r\leq 2,\ 0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi-\frac{1}{n}\}+(6n,0).

These polar rectangles are pairwise disjoint and uniformly separated. Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
, and notice
\Omega
is a domain. Since the plane is Loewner, and
A_{n}
are uniformly fat and quasiround, we can say, by Corollary
5.6
, that
\Omega
is transboundary Loewner.

To see that it fails to be 1-transboundary Loewner, let
E_{n}=\partial B((6n,0),0.9)
and let
F_{n}=\partial B((6n,0),2.1)
. Notice

\Delta(E_{n},F_{n})=\frac{1.2}{1.8}=\frac{2}{3}.

Now

\Gamma(B[(6n,0),1],\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B((6n,0),2);A[(6n,0),1,2]\setminus A_{n})<\Gamma(E_{n},F_{n};\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus A_{n}).

By the overflowing property and a well-known modulus computation for polar rectangles, we have

\bmod_{\partial_{0}\Omega}(\Gamma(E_{n},F_{n};\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus A_{n}))\leq\bmod(\Gamma(B[(6n,0),1],\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B((6n,0),2);A[(6n,0),1,2]\setminus A_{n}))=\frac{1/n}{\log(2)}.

For any decreasing function,
\Psi
, pick
n>(\Psi(2/3)\log(2))^{-1}
, then

\bmod_{\partial_{0}\Omega}(\Gamma(E_{n},F_{n};\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus A_{n}))<\Psi(2/3)=\Psi(\Delta(E_{n},F_{n})).

Hence it cannot be 1-transboundary Loewner.
∎

-transboundary Loewner spaces. We will need the following result of Bonk to show this.

]

.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be countable collection of closed,
\eta
-quasidisks in

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus K

The 2-transboundary Loewner property will enable upgrading a quasiconformal map to a quasisymmetric one. The following argument is of standard type for such results. In particular, it’s similar to an argument by Bonk

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space with
\mu
locally finite. Suppose
X
is metric doubling,
\overline{X}
is compact, and
\partial_{0}X
is countable. Suppose there is a geometrically

-quasiconformal map

where

is a circle domain, which extends as a homeomorphism
f:\overline{X}_{\partial_{0}X}\rightarrow\overline{\Omega}_{\partial_{0}\Omega}
. If
X
is 2-transboundary Loewner with decreasing function
\Psi
, then
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric where
\eta
depends only on
Q
,
\Psi
,
\mathrm{diam}(X)
, and the doubling constant.

Proof.

Since
\overline{X}
is compact,
\mathrm{diam}(X)<\infty
. There exists
a_{0},a_{\infty}\in X
such that
d(a_{0},a_{\infty})>3\mathrm{diam}(X)/4
. Notice then that
B(a_{0},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4)\cap B(a_{\infty},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4)=\emptyset
. Since
X
is homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,
X
must be connected. Hence
B(a_{0},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4)\cup B(a_{\infty},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4)\neq X
. Let
a_{1}\in X\setminus(B(a_{0},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4)\cup B(a_{\infty},\mathrm{diam}(X)/4))
. Let
\delta=\min(\mathrm{diam}(X)/4,1)
. Notice that
d(a_{0},a_{1}),d(a_{1},a_{\infty}),d(a_{0},a_{\infty})\geq\delta
. Compose
f
with a Möbius transformation so that
f(a_{0})=0
,
f(a_{1})=1
, and
f(a_{\infty})=\infty
. Since Möbius transformations are conformal, the composition is still
Q
-quasiconformal.

We first want to show that this map is weakly quasisymmetric. Suppose, by way of contradiction, that
f
fails to be weakly quasisymmetric. Then for all
H>0
there exists pairwise distinct
a,b,c\in X
such that
d(a,b)\leq d(b,c)
and

where
a^{\prime}=f(a),b^{\prime}=f(b),c^{\prime}=f(c)
.
b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in B(b^{\prime},1.5|b^{\prime}-c^{\prime}|)
. Since
\Omega
is linearly locally connected with
\lambda=1
(Proposition
7.3
), we have that there exists a continua
E^{\prime}\subset\Omega
connecting
b^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
such that

\mathrm{diam}(E^{\prime})\leq 3|b^{\prime}-c^{\prime}|.

Since
\delta\leq 1
we have
B(0,\delta/2),B(1,\delta/2),B(\infty,\delta/2)
are pairwise disjoint:
b^{\prime}
must fail to be in two of them. Moreover,
B(a_{0},\delta/2),B(a_{1},\delta/2),B(a_{\infty},\delta/2)
are pairwise disjoint, so
a
must fail to be in two of them. Thus there exists a
u^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\infty\}
such that
|u^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|\geq\delta/2
and
d(a_{u^{\prime}},a)\geq\delta/2
.

Since
|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|\leq 2
, we have

\frac{\delta}{4}|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta\leq|u^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|.

So
u^{\prime}\notin B(b^{\prime},\delta/4|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|)
. Also, since
\delta\leq 1
,
a^{\prime}\notin B(b^{\prime},\delta/4|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|)
. We can use linear local connectivity (Proposition
7.3

such that

If

\frac{\delta}{4}|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|>6|b^{\prime}-c^{\prime}|.

So we have
E^{\prime}\subset B(b^{\prime},3|b^{\prime}-c^{\prime}|)\subset B(b^{\prime},\delta/4|a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}|)
, and hence

Since we have

Now let

and

Since
\delta\leq\mathrm{diam}(X)
, we have

. Thus

Thus

\Delta(E,F)\leq\frac{2\mathrm{diam}(X)}{\delta}.

Take

7.2

\Omega

\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus J))\geq\Psi(\Delta(E,F))\geq\Psi(2\mathrm{diam}(X)/\delta).

Now use Lemma

\bmod_{\partial_{0}X}(\Gamma(E,F;\overline{X}\setminus J))\leq Q\bmod_{\partial_{0}\Omega}(\Gamma(E^{\prime},F^{\prime};\overline{\Omega}\setminus J^{\prime})),

and the function

, and

,
\Psi
, and
\mathrm{diam}(X)
.

Notice any subset of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}

Theorem 8.2

Uniformization results for metric surfaces with finitely many boundary components given by Rajala-Rasimus
[
RR21
]
and Merenkov-Wildrick
[
MW11
]
have
\eta
depend on the number of boundary components; it’s worth pointing out that this result has no such dependency. However, there is a hidden dependency on the relative distance between boundary components, which is revealed in the following necessary conditions for a metric space to be 2-transboundary Loewner.

Corollary 8.3
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a metric space with
\mathcal{H}^{2}
locally finite. Let
\overline{X}
be compact and
\partial_{0}X
countable. Suppose
X
is metric doubling, bounded turning, homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, and locally reciprocal. If
X
is 2-transboundary Loewner, then
X
is
N
-transboundary Loewner for all
N
and
\partial_{0}X
consists of uniformly relatively separated uniform quasicircles or points.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.2
, we have a quasisymmetric and geometrically quasiconformal map to a circle domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. This will imply that
\partial_{0}X
consists of uniform quasicircles or points. Moreover, by Proposition
4.1
, we have that the circle domain is 2-transboundary Loewner. Use Example
6.6
to say the circle domain is
N
-transboundary Loewner for all
N
and that its bounding circles are uniformly relatively separated. Use Proposition
4.1
and the fact that quasisymmetric mappings preserve uniform relative separation, see e.g.,
[
HL23
, Lemma 2.3]
to deduce that
X
is
N
-transboundary Loewner for all
N
and
\partial_{0}X
is uniformly relatively separated.
∎

Thus, in this context, 2-transboundary Loewner is equivalent to being
N
-transboundary Loewner for all
N
. So if
\partial_{0}X
is finite, we have
X
is 2-transboundary Loewner if and only if it is Loewner, although the function making
X
Loewner will depend on the number of boundary components. This also reduces greatly in the case of domains in the sphere, so that we can say the following.

Corollary 8.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be a countably connected domain. Then
\Omega
is 2-transboundary Loewner if and only if its complementary components are uniformly relatively separated uniform quasidisks or points.

Proof.

Every domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
will have
\mathcal{H}^{2}
locally finite,
\overline{\Omega}
compact, be metric doubling, and locally reciprocal. Also, looking at the discussion following Lemma
7.10
, we can always extend homeomorphisms between spherical domains to a homeomorphism between the quotient spaces, so we don’t need bounded turning. If
\Omega
is 2-transboundary Loewner, by Corollary
8.3
, we have that the complementary components are uniformly relatively separated uniform quasidisks or points. The converse is given by Proposition
6.3
.
∎

Merenkov and Wildrick
[
MW11
]
showed that the boundary components of a metric space being uniformly relatively separated uniform quasicircles is insufficient to conclude quasisymmetric equivalence to a circle domain; despite the fact, which Bonk
[
Bon11
]
showed, that it is sufficient for domains in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. However, the counterexample they gave fails to be transboundary Loewner, and hence it fails to be 2-transboundary Loewner. Thus, the above result shows that the 2-transboundary Loewner property is, in some sense, an appropriate perspective for generalizing Bonk’s sufficient condition to metric spaces.

Corollary 8.5
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a metric space homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathcal{H}^{2}
locally finite and
\partial_{0}X
countable. Suppose
X
is locally reciprocal. Then
X
is quasisymmetric to a circle domain with uniformly relatively separated bounding circles if and only if

•

\overline{X}
is compact,

•

X
is (metric) doubling,

•

X
is bounded turning, and

•

X
is 2-transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

Use Theorem
8.2
and Corollary
8.3
to conclude that any
X
satisfying the assumptions is quasisymmetric to a circle domain with uniformly relatively separated boundary. For the other direction, notice that uniformly relatively separated circle domains in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
have all of the properties listed (see Example

). Using Lemma
7.9
and the fact that all of the listed properties are invariant under quasisymmetric and geometrically quasiconformal maps completes the proof.
∎

The statement here simplifies if we additionally assume Ahlfors regularity.

Corollary 8.6
.

Suppose
(X,d)
is a metric space homeomorphic to a domain in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
with
\partial_{0}X
countable. Suppose

X
is quasisymmetric to a circle domain with uniformly relatively separated bounding circles if and only if

•

\overline{X}
is compact,

•

X
is bounded turning, and

•

X
is 2-transboundary Loewner.

Proof.

Observe that Ahlfors regularity gives
