Understanding barriers and identifying solutions for smoking cessation in primary care: survey results informed by an integrated knowledge translation approach in British Columbia, Canada
Authors: Megan Simmons, Erin Shellington, Tina Afshar, Laura Struik, Femke Hoekstra, Renelle Myers, Milan Khara, Marilyn Gerald, Jim Johnson, Phalgun Joshi, Christopher Carlsten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis quality improvement project was designed to identify perceptions, knowledge and training needs of primary care providers (PCPs) in British Columbia regarding smoking cessation treatment with the intent to improve practices and identify feasible methods to mitigate barriers.MethodsAn integrated knowledge translation approach was used to design, implement and analyse a survey for primary care practitioners in the provincial context of British Columbia. The survey was divided into the four main sections: Demographic Information, Knowledge Assessment, Training Needs and Perception of Barriers. It was distributed through advertisement in e-newsletters for family physicians and nurse practitioners. It is aimed to evaluate and understand PCPs’ perceptions of tobacco use disorder and smoking cessation treatment; confidence when providing tobacco use disorder and smoking cessation support and treatment; educational needs and desired supports and barriers to providing and accessing smoking cessation supports and resources.ResultsThe survey garnered 198 complete responses from 154 family physicians and 44 nurse practitioners. Findings highlight a need for increased training and resources while exploring desired training content and methods of delivery. Time constraints were identified as the primary barrier to providing smoking cessation intervention, alongside lacking referral pathways, clinical services and support, and compensation. Respondents expressed a desire for ‘cheat sheets’ with information on smoking cessation resources and best practices, training and education on counselling techniques and vaping cessation guidance, and integrated clinical workflows.ConclusionThis project emphasises the need for an interdisciplinary approach to smoking cessation centred around integration of resources, training and policies into current workflows (such as electronic medical records or a centralised platform for information). Important gaps have been illuminated in the processes that support smoking cessation in primary care in British Columbia.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This project identifies three overarching themes with implications for research, policy and practice: (1) time and compensation; (2) communication and resources and (3) education and awareness. The findings suggest that enhanced access to training resources on smoking cessation interventions may support primary care practitioners in delivering more effective care. Furthermore, policy and practice revisions, such as adjustments to billing codes or clinical workflows and creating opt-out referral pathways to smoking cessation programmes, could better reflect the practical needs of primary care settings. Finally, integrating multidisciplinary teams into primary care may offer a more comprehensive approach to smoking cessation support.

Introduction

Smoking commercial tobacco remains the leading cause of preventable deaths in Canada. Each year, tobacco-related illnesses account for approximately 46 000 deaths nationally, or approximately 125 deaths per day.1 This daily toll surpassed the combined total of fatalities from traffic collisions, opioid overdoses, suicides, homicides and alcohol-related causes in 2017.1 Smoking significantly shortens life expectancy, both for current smokers and those who have smoked in the past.2 It is estimated that, cumulatively, over 500 000 years of life are lost annually in Canada because of premature death related to smoking.3 Despite overall declines in smoking rates, tobacco use disorder (TUD) remains disproportionately prevalent among equity-seeking groups, particularly Indigenous peoples, individuals with co-occurring substance use disorders, those with mental illnesses and groups with lower socioeconomic status.14

The health and economic benefits of evidence-based smoking cessation treatments are well documented. Successful cessation interventions can lead to significant reductions in healthcare costs, morbidity and mortality associated with smoking-related diseases.57 Furthermore, effective cessation programmes contribute to improved quality of life and increased productivity among former smokers.8 The integration of tobacco use screening, brief counselling and pharmacotherapy, including nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and other approved medications, has been shown to yield the most effective smoking cessation outcomes.9 This evidence-based approach underpins clinical practice guidelines both nationally and internationally.10 A widely recommended strategy for any behaviour modification counselling, such as smoking cessation, is the 5A’s approach. This intervention consists of: Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist and Arrange.11 Ask, Advise and Act (the 3A Approach) an adaptation of the 5A Approach and is widely accepted and practised in Canada.12 In Canada, several provinces offer telephone or online quit lines that provide coaching to help individuals quit tobacco use as well as free access to non-prescription NRT and prescription smoking cessation medications. Despite the availability of best practice guidelines and cessation resources, challenges persist in the delivery of cessation services, particularly within the primary care setting.

Primary care is a key touchpoint in the healthcare continuum for smoking cessation interventions. Studies have highlighted the pivotal role of primary care providers (PCPs) in screening for tobacco use and delivering cessation counselling and treatment.1315 PCPs can offer patients trusted, safe spaces and their encouragement has proven to be a valuable resource in improving both individual and public health.16 However, several barriers hinder PCPs from delivering these services effectively. These include insufficient training in cessation strategies, limited access to clinical supports such as nursing staff, inadequate use of technology for patient identification and treatment optimisation, and constraints related to time and reimbursement for smoking cessation counselling.17

A substantial body of evidence suggests that specialised training of care providers in smoking cessation strategies and available resources can lead to significant reductions in smoking rates.14 A Cochrane review demonstrated that healthcare provider training results in improved smoking cessation outcomes, with trained providers more likely to engage in smoking cessation tasks than their untrained counterparts.18 This underscores the critical role of education and training in enhancing provider competencies. This project aimed to identify gaps and learning needs among PCPs in British Columbia (BC) to inform the development of targeted, evidence-based training strategies and resources. Specifically, it sought to assess and deepen understanding of the perceptions, knowledge and training needs of PCPs, namely family physicians (FPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs), in relation to smoking cessation practices.

Methods

Project conception and design

This project utilised concepts of integrated knowledge translation (iKT)19 by engaging knowledge users throughout the project. The project was originally conceived by members of the BC Tobacco and Vaping Clinical Cessation Community of Practice (CoP). Established by Legacy of Airway Health, the CoP brings together researchers, healthcare providers, government policy analysts, health authority-based tobacco and vapour control leaders, tobacco cessation experts and representatives from not-for-profits and professional associations. The group’s primary focus is knowledge sharing and collaborative learning through projects that address shared interests and priorities to improve smoking and vaping cessation across BC. The CoP initiated a quality improvement project to understand the gaps in knowledge, education needs of healthcare providers and barriers to providing smoking cessation support; specifically, those prescribing smoking cessation medications. Prior to developing the survey, the CoP identified the absence of billing codes, contributing to limited time for addressing cessation, as a primary barrier. It was hypothesised that surveying PCPs could generate data to support the creation of a billing code to help overcome this challenge. The project focused on FPs and NPs due to their frequent patient interactions and significant influence within the healthcare system. Interested members of the CoP participated in the project steering committee and voluntarily contributed to the grant application that funded the project, codeveloped the survey questions, assisted in survey dissemination and participated in data analysis, interpretation and manuscript preparation.

The initial survey was developed based on the previously reported surveys,1417 2027 was refined through steering committee feedback and piloted with two physicians on the research team for clarity and applicability. The committee, comprising CoP volunteers and patient-partners, provided additional feedback before finalisation.

The survey was conducted online between December 2022 and March 2023, using the Qualtrics platform (www.ubc.qualtrics.com) and was designed to be completed in approximately 15 min. The survey included a combination of 34 multiple-choice, Likert scale/matrix responses and rank-order questions, along with two open-ended questions requiring text responses. Questions were divided into four main sections of the following categories: Demographic Information, Knowledge Assessment, Training Needs and Perception of Barriers.

Patient and public involvement

Patient partners, who are members of Legacy for Airway Health’s Community Partner Committee (CPC), were involved in the funding application, design of the survey, review of the manuscript. The Shared Care Committee funding application utilises a patient story related to the challenges faces or gaps that need to be addressed. A member of the CPC shared their difficulties to try to quit smoking and lack of support and access to cessation resources in primary care. Two CPC members were engaged in the survey design and creation as well as final writing review of this manuscript.

Participant recruitment

Survey participants were eligible if they were FPs or NPs currently practising in BC. Participants were recruited through advertisement of the survey in newsletters from the Nurse and Nurse Practitioners of BC (November and December 2022) and the Divisions of Family Practice (January to March 2023). Of the 35 Divisions of Family Practice approached, 30 agreed to participate in survey dissemination. Respondents who completed the survey were compensated with a CA$50 gift card. Respondents’ affiliation, designation, names and emails were collected to prevent survey duplicates and to provide gift cards. Identification information was kept separate from survey responses to maintain anonymity.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected electronically. Multiple choice, Likert scale/matrix questions and rank-order responses were analysed descriptively in Qualtrics to identify key trends and patterns. For the rank-order questions, respondents were asked to rank their top three choices in order of importance, assigning a distinct rank of first (most important), second and third. The results reported below reflect this ranked order, rather than a simple selection of the top three options. Quantitative data were analysed by using descriptive statistics (eg, percentages, mean, median, SD, etc). Open-ended question comments were organised into themes inductively. Responses were compared between FPs and NPs and between urban/suburban and rural/remote/isolated healthcare providers. The survey results were shared with the CoP members and disseminated through recruitment channels to inform knowledge users and guide subsequent project phases.

This project was conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines and aligned with the Tri-Council Policy Statement on Quality Improvement with no involvement of patients and no changes to clinic practice resulting directly from it.28

Perceptions on TUD and smoking cessation

On a 5-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree), 88.7% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that TUD can be overcome (online supplemental 1). Furthermore, 80% of respondents indicated that all patients should be screened and/or treated for TUD (online supplemental 2). In response to a rank-order question, respondents ranked the most effective treatments for TUD as: combination therapy (eg, two NRT products such as patches, gum, oral spray or inhaler or an oral medication plus NRT), oral medications alone, brief smoking cessation counselling (eg, 5A Approach), and NRT alone (figure 1). It should be noted that in practice, these treatments or interventions may be prescribed and/or used in combination. This trend remained consistent across FPs, urban/suburban and rural/remote/isolated cohorts. NP cohort ranked behavioural support provided by QuitNow as second most effective, with 25% of responses.

Responses to the question ‘What do you believe are effective treatments for tobacco use disorder? Rank top three’ (N=195 survey respondents). NRT, nicotine replacement therapy; TUD, tobacco use disorder.

PCP confidence providing support and intervention

On a 5-point Likert scale (1=extremely uncomfortable, 5=extremely comfortable) respondents were asked about their confidence in providing smoking cessation support. Respondents felt most comfortable making follow-up appointments, prescribing NRT/referring to BC Smoking Cessation Program (a provincial programme that provides 12 weeks of NRT coverage annually) and prescribing oral medications. Conversely, respondents were least confident in referring patients to clinical cessation support such as the now-defunct Vancouver General Hospital Smoking Cessation Clinic, which at the time of the survey provided one-on-one, evidence-based support from a tobacco addiction specialist. QuitNow (a provincially funded virtual peer-led coaching programme) and the BC Smoking Cessation Program were the two resources respondents felt were the most effective interventions of the options listed (online supplemental 1).

Barriers identified

In response to a ranked question about the barriers to providing smoking cessation support, respondents most frequently identified time constraints as the primary barrier. Other commonly identified barriers include a lack of referral pathways, clinical services and support, and compensation (figure 2). This trend remained consistent across all cohorts (FPs, NPs, urban/suburban and rural/remote/isolated). Respondents identified failed quit attempts and lack of motivation as the primary barriers their patients face in accessing smoking cessation support. Concurrent mental illness or substance use disorder, a lack of familial or peer support and lack of treatment and support were also all highly ranked barriers. This trend remained consistent across all cohorts (FPs, NPs, urban/suburban and rural/remote/isolated) (online supplemental 3).

All responses to the question ‘What barriers do you face in providing smoking cessation treatment? Rank top three’ (N=198 survey respondents). EMR, electronic medical record.

Educational needs and desired supports

Respondents highlighted ‘cheat sheets’ on TUD, available treatments and free coverage for NRT and oral medications as being the most desirable support to optimise smoking cessation treatment in their practice. Other highly ranked supports include training on smoking cessation counselling and programmes, centralised resources for physicians and patients, clinical support (eg, by respiratory therapist or registered nurse trained in smoking cessation) and embedding screening and referral into electronic medical records (figure 3). This trend remained consistent across all cohorts (FPs, NPs, urban/suburban and rural/remote/isolated).

All responses to the question ‘What are your desired supports for optimising smoking cessation treatments in your practice? Rank top three’ (N=196 survey respondents). NRT, nicotine replacement therapy; PCP, primary care provider; RT, respiratory therapist; RN, registered nurse.

Survey findings revealed a strong demand for education on evidence-based smoking cessation interventions, with counselling techniques, particularly motivational interviewing, ranked as the highest training priority. Respondents also prioritised content on treatment options and their effectiveness, pharmacotherapy coverage (eg, NRT and oral medications), available cessation services in BC and guidance on vaping cessation (figure 4). This preference pattern was consistent across provider cohorts (FPs and NPs) and geographic categories (urban/suburban vs rural/remote/isolated). Notably, NPs ranked information on treating high-risk subpopulations (eg, individuals with concurrent mental illness) as the third most desired topic, with 29% selecting it. Similarly, 19% of respondents from rural/remote/isolated settings ranked this topic as their third priority, closely following treatment options and effectiveness (23%), the most frequently cited third-choice content.

Discussion

The findings from this project echo previous work that PCPs are well positioned to deliver effective smoking cessation training, particularly when adequate support and resources are provided. This aligns with existing literature on barriers to providing smoking cessation interventions in primary care, where system-wide changes are crucial to overcoming implementation obstacles.29 The primary barriers identified in this project may be further mitigated by steering away from the siloed approach to TUD and smoking cessation. BC healthcare considers smoking cessation a population health priority, when TUD is, in fact, a diagnosis under mental illness.30 This division may further contribute to challenges in gaining support from decision-makers and health system views of smoking as a mental illness issue rather than a population health priority, highlighting an ongoing disconnect between policy and practice. This is consistent with previous research suggesting that broader healthcare system priorities may impede the successful delivery of smoking cessation interventions.8 31 Additionally, respondents identified perceived barriers to smoking cessation for patients as failed quit attempts, concurrent mental illness or substance use disorder and lack of supports (both by peers and services). Aligning healthcare practitioners, services and authorities while fostering a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach may help mitigate these barriers.

Interpretation within the wider literature

The project results are broadly in line with the current literature on barriers to smoking cessation interventions in primary care. Previous research highlights that healthcare providers are often constrained by systemic barriers, including insufficient training, time limitations and a lack of integration between primary care and public health services.17 This project underscores those challenges while adding nuance to the conversation around the specific needs of PCPs in this context. Project results highlighted three broad barriers and opportunities for improvement. These fall into the categories: time and compensation; communication and access to resources and education and awareness.

Implications for policy, practice and research

Time and compensation

Insufficient time was ranked, overwhelmingly, by survey respondents, as the number one barrier they face when providing smoking cessation treatment. The qualitative data reaffirmed this notion, with respondents highlighting that lack of time is the biggest obstacle to delivering care. Furthermore, this finding supports the need to establish appropriate billing codes to allow PCPs to engage in smoking cessation treatment, counselling and follow-up sessions. While time was ranked as the greatest barrier, the interconnection between time constraints and inadequate compensation mechanisms for smoking cessation counselling should also be noted. In the absence of dedicated billing codes, PCPs are often required to address cessation alongside other, often more urgent, clinical concerns within a single visit. This limits their ability to allocate sufficient time for comprehensive cessation support, including essential follow-up. The lack of reimbursement for stand-alone cessation appointments represents a critical policy gap that warrants attention, as it directly impacts the feasibility and sustainability of delivering effective tobacco cessation interventions in primary care.

Utilising existing tools like electronic medical records offers the potential to reach a broader audience of care providers and fosters more collaborative, comprehensive care. There is a call to adopt a multidisciplinary approach by leveraging support from allied health providers to enhance the capacity and competency of primary care teams. This team-based approach to smoking cessation has been shown to be effective in the previous literature and may contribute towards a more linear system that can help alleviate time constraints.32 Furthermore, embedding resources, such as referral pathways, into clinical workflows can improve the effectiveness and efficiency of care delivery. This framework may contribute to combat time barriers for PCPs.

Communication and resources

Respondents expressed a strong desire for ‘cheat sheets’ on TUD, available treatments and free coverage. Across all subgroups of FPs and NPs in both urban and rural locations, ‘cheat sheets’ were ranked as the number one most requested content to include in training and resources. Previous work on smoking cessation in primary care looks at the use of ‘flow sheets’, which includes information for providers on prompts and dialogue during consultations, has been shown to contribute to increased quit rates among patients.14 However, the use and effectiveness of ‘cheat sheets’ has not been reported. Implementation of ‘cheat sheets’ would help PCPs become more aware of the materials, services and interventions available to support patients, while also promoting standardised and accurate information across the province. Respondents also requested an easily accessible and centralised resource for all care providers to refer to, and practice guidelines displayed in a one-page document or flowchart. Specifically, there was a recommendation to include information on treatment options and effectiveness, pharmacotherapy coverage and smoking cessation services in BC.

A major finding of this project is the prominent recommendation for using pharmacotherapy as a primary method to quit smoking. This recommendation aligns with best practice evidence that NRT is a critical component to achieving cessation.3336 This also aligns with what end-users want, with a previous project indicating that information on pharmacotherapy was one of the top visited pages on the QuitNow website, BC's free smoking cessation service.37 While BC enables coverage for eligible participants to access NRT for free, there are several limitations that restrict the effectiveness of this programme, including not applying to combination NRT (eg, gum and patch) or supporting individuals beyond 12 weeks in a year.37 38 Our current project reinforces this limitation, with several respondents indicating the need for additional NRT coverage. Considering new tobacco industry-generated tobacco-free products (eg, e-cigs, pouches), it is critical that effective non-tobacco industry-generated treatment options, policies and coverage for these treatment options are not overshadowed by them.

Education and awareness

Despite the overwhelming desire for education and training in smoking cessation that was prominent throughout the survey results, 57% per cent of respondents indicated they have not completed any smoking cessation training; this is a notable area to address and improve on (figure 3). Respondents received over 80% of their ongoing professional training from professional associations and universities, suggesting that this may be a worthy educational gap to be addressed by these institutions. Additionally, counselling techniques, such as motivational interviewing, were suggested to be a valuable resource for PCPs to be trained in. Previous literature stresses the importance of practitioners to feel confident in smoking cessation skills and abilities, this can be done in ways such as provider capacity-building activities, which, ultimately, can lead to better patient outcomes.18 Respondents from this project echoed a need for increased access and awareness of strategies to overcome patient barriers to smoking cessation. There was a strong consensus on the necessity for comprehensive educational programmes tailored to equip healthcare providers with the skills required to tackle complex challenges faced by their patients effectively. Practitioners specifically requested training and support for marginalised groups. As aforementioned, work has addressed, intersectionality highlights the inequities surrounding vulnerable populations, which can add further complexity to providing smoking cessation resources. The need for educational resources on electronic nicotine delivery systems (eg, vaping) and concerns about the use of these devices as harm reduction are evident. There are complexities to consider with any recommendations or counselling given by PCPs, but providing practitioners with education, research and awareness to aid in this may allow for better patient outcomes.29 The multiple requests for information on vaping as harm reduction as well as the vast majority (80%) of respondents reporting that they counsel patients who vape, emphasises the need for continued education and research done in this area. Current research highlights the concerns of electronic nicotine delivery systems (including electronic cigarettes, e-hookahs, vape pens, mods and vaping) on overall health outcomes.39 40 This is a growing area of literature, with recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses providing supportive evidence for e-cigarettes as potential smoking cessation aids41 42; however, both Canadian and global guidance advises against their general use, citing the absence of approved therapeutic products, unresolved questions about long-term safety and possible negative public health implications.43 44 At the same time, e-cigarettes may be considered in situations where individuals have not succeeded with established options or have a strong preference for this approach.44 This highlights an important education gap for practitioners, as uncertainty regarding the role of e-cigarettes was identified as the third most common concern by respondents.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this project include the application of an iKT approach that convened diverse provincial stakeholders around a shared objective. By incorporating the perspectives of end-users, researchers, policy analysts and healthcare providers throughout the design and implementation phases, the project fostered meaningful engagement and positioned its findings for efficient knowledge mobilisation. This collaborative methodology is increasingly recognised as a catalyst for health system improvement.41 Additionally, the integration of a CoP facilitated the identification of practice-relevant issues and supported actionable responses. The CoP model proved effective both as a framework for addressing complex implementation challenges and as a mechanism for sustaining end-user involvement in research and change initiatives. Finally, the project’s provincial focus, targeting PCPs in BC, enhances its relevance for informing context-specific policy and practice change. We have no particular reason to believe that these findings are specific to the BC context, and we suspect that our data have some broad relevance and applicability even if some variability is inevitable based on local regulatory and cultural context.

One limitation of this study is the survey’s reliance on predominantly closed-ended questions, which constrained the depth and nuance of responses from PCPs and may have limited the richness of the data. Moreover, while the rank-order questions provided insight into perceived priority barriers, the response options were all previously established in the literature. As such, the results reflect participants’ views on the relative importance of known barriers rather than the identification of novel challenges. Ideally, comprehensive policy and practice changes would address all of these barriers. Although the project created a valuable forum for exploring innovative health system improvements, the feasibility of the proposed solutions remained uncertain, an issue echoed in other healthcare quality improvement studies.29 This study did not include emerging digital aids, such as mobile applications, for smoking cessation, as many of these tools were not widely available or well-studied within the study context during the 2022–2023 review period; however, such interventions represent an important and evolving area that warrants consideration in future work. Finally, the limited depth of participant input highlights the need for future research to incorporate more direct involvement from frontline providers to capture practical and necessary insights.

Future research

Despite a wealth of research on quality improvement in healthcare, publications focusing specifically on smoking cessation remain sparse. This project contributes to filling that gap by emphasising the need for a systematic and evidence-based approach to health system change. Key hurdles identified, such as the need for sustainable support and better resource alignment, are critical to addressing smoking cessation challenges in a meaningful and lasting way. The next steps should include the development of standardised and accredited smoking cessation training programmes tailored specifically to the needs of PCPs in BC, and lobby for compensation to adequately administer smoking cessation support at the primary care level. Such programmes should be accompanied by adequate funding, policy support and integration with broader health initiatives. Future research should focus on expanding input from PCPs to ensure that training programmes are both practical and feasible. Additionally, further exploration into the role of health system-level changes is necessary to overcome the structural barriers identified in this project.

Conclusion

This project has solidified a demand for changes in payment structure, opportunities and for training and centralised resources for practitioners to support smoking cessation in primary care. It has highlighted the need for aligned funding, structural support and a reimagined approach to public health priorities, rooted in collaborative efforts and end-user feedback. The utilisation of an iKT approach and local context allows for a meaningful and uniquely informed lens to be applied to this work. By addressing these barriers, smoking cessation interventions in primary care can become more effective, ultimately working towards improving population health outcomes.
