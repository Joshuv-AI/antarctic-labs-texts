AI reasoning effort mirrors human decision time on content moderation tasks
Thomas R. Davidson

Abstract
Large language models can now generate intermediate reasoning steps before producing answers, improving performance on difficult problems. This study uses a paired conjoint experiment on a content moderation task to examine parallels between human decision times and model reasoning effort. Across three frontier models, reasoning effort consistently predicts human decision time. Both humans and models expended greater effort when important variables were held constant, suggesting similar sensitivity to task difficulty and patterns consistent with dual-process theories of cognition. These findings show that AI reasoning effort mirrors human processing time in subjective judgments and underscores the potential of reasoning traces for interpretability and decision-making.

In all four heatmaps, the density is highest either on or immediately adjacent to the diagonal, meaning that decisions require the most effort when terms are either identical or similar in offensiveness. Human subjects take the most time to make decisions when presented with two posts without offensive terms or that use anti-White language. Their choices are fastest when shown incongruous pairs, such as homophobic or racist terms coupled with innocuous posts. Similar patterns are evident among the three LRMs, with some model-specific idiosyncrasies: o3 expends the most reasoning power when shown pairs of sexist or anti-White posts, or homophobia and a reclaimed slur; Gemini consumes the most tokens when comparing posts both featuring a generic insult; and Grok uses the most reasoning effort when evaluating pairs of anti-White posts. All three models tend to mirror human subjects when considering incongruous pairs.

Finally, we conducted a qualitative analysis of the reasoning traces from Gemini 2.5 Pro, the only model to provide this data at the time of the study. When both posts are equally offensive, the traces typically state that both violate the content policy before turning to additional contextual factors. In some instances, the models focus on the language, often prioritizing personal attacks rather than statements about groups. In other cases, the traces emphasize factors including the user identity, discussion topic, and engagement metrics.

Discussion

The results reveal parallels in how humans and AI reason about content moderation decisions. The positive association between human decision time and AI reasoning effort demonstrates that the same cases challenge both humans and AI. When posts with identical levels of offensiveness are shown, human subjects and LRMs expend considerably more effort. The descriptive analyses further show how reasoning effort increases when comparing speech with similar perceived offensiveness. Qualitative analysis of reasoning traces confirms that models explicitly acknowledge when primary offensiveness cues are equivalent and shift attention to secondary factors. These parallels suggest that, much like humans, LRMs switch from faster heuristics to slower deliberation when decisions demand deeper analysis, consistent with dual-process theories of cognition
kojima_large_2022
;
yao_tree_2023
.

Reasoning traces may have direct applications in content moderation and other high-stakes settings. Although reasoning traces do not always capture the actual decision-making process
turpin_language_2023
;
chen_reasoning_2025
, their length tracks task difficulty, meaning that longer reasoning traces could provide a practical signal for cases that warrant human review. Beyond content moderation, reasoning traces may also provide greater transparency into decision-making in domains such as law, medicine, and hiring, where AI systems are increasingly deployed
(
korbak_chain_nodate
)
. The observed differences across models highlight the need for further research to understand how architectures, training data, and post-training procedures shape not only what models output, but how they think.

Materials and Methods

The experiment consists of a conjoint task where pairs of synthetic social media posts are shown, and one must be chosen. The posts were designed to represent social media posts that could either be innocuous or use hateful language. Each post varied across multiple attributes: user identity, slur use, cursing, topic, and engagement metrics. A total of 210,000 unique posts were created, representing all possible permutations of attributes. Each consists of an image file resembling a screen capture of a post. Further details are given in the SI text.

Adults in the US (
N=1854
) were recruited via Prolific and performed the experiment in Qualtrics. Subjects who consented to participate were shown fifteen pairs of posts, sampled at random from the corpus, and a content policy resembling that used by Twitter (now X). Subjects were asked to select which post was more likely to violate the policy. The same set of paired profiles was used to repeat the experiment using each model. In each case, the relevant application programming interface (API) was used to pass the model a prompt containing instructions and each pair of images. The model was requested to choose which image was more likely to violate the policy, and the response and the token usage were recorded. See the SI text for additional information.

The dependent variable from the human subjects experiment is the time in seconds from when a pair of profiles is presented until the first click on the screen, recorded by Qualtrics. A small number of outliers were removed to avoid skewing the results, although the substantive findings are identical if these cases are retained. Specifically, cases where subjects clicked in 1 second or less or over 120 seconds were dropped (N = 307 pairs). The final analytic sample consists of 29,664 pairs. Excluding outliers, the mean and median times are 15.1 and 11.2 seconds per pair, respectively. The primary variable from the AI experiments is the number of reasoning tokens consumed, which varies considerably across models. For o3, the results are always in blocks of 64, on average, using 303.3 (
SD=241.6
) reasoning tokens per comparison. Gemini 2.5 Pro consumes more tokens, with a mean of 897.9 (
SD=419.6
). Grok 4 consumes an average of 1600.3 tokens (
SD=1821.9
). For comparability, we standardize the token consumption metrics. The explanatory variable in the second set of analyses is a dummy variable indicating whether or not a given pair of posts used the same slur (including cases where neither included a slur).

We use OLS regression to estimate three sets of models. First, we predict response time as a function of reasoning token consumption to examine the association between human and AI decision-making. We control for the natural logarithm of the task number (from 1 to 15) to account for differences due to learning as subjects performed more tasks, and include subject fixed effects and clustered standard errors to capture heterogeneity across subjects. Second, we evaluate the relationship between response time and the same slur dummy variable, controlling for the same variables as the previous model. Finally, we repeat this analysis using the number of reasoning tokens consumed by each model. We do not control for other conjoint attributes since they are fully randomized and vary independently from the same slur covariate. There is no subject or task-level variation in the LRM experiments.

Names, usernames, and profile images convey information about the post author’s identity. The posts include two additional contextual features to measure the effects of social influence. First, to ascertain the extent to which responses from other users affect the interpretation of the original posts, we vary whether a post includes a reply and, if so, whether the reply agrees or disagrees with the original post. Prior work finds that comments from other users alter perceptions of community norms, including the acceptability of offensive speech
[
36
]
. Each reply consists of a short response to the post that was generated at the same time as the post. The replies relate to the main topic of discussion but vary independently from other treatments, meaning that replies expressing disagreement never directly “call out” people for using slurs (c.f.
[
37

). All replies are made by anonymous accounts. Second, to examine whether the level of engagement affects evaluations, we vary the number of likes shown for the original post, showing either low like counts (between 0 and 5 likes) or high like counts (between 25 and 50 likes). Since the tweets mimic posts by ordinary users rather than celebrities or viral posts, we do not show extremely high numbers of likes. Additionally, to enhance external validity, a timestamp is included to make tweets appear to have been created during the six months before the study, where a date and time are randomly assigned to each post. This feature is designed solely to enhance realism and is not analyzed.

Conjoint task

The conjoint task consists of a forced-choice question where either human subjects or AI models must select an option from a pair of posts. In each case, a policy was provided, and the instructions stated that the post that was more likely to violate the policy should be selected. We used a simple policy used by X (
https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/hateful-conduct-policy
), which reads:

We are committed to combating abuse motivated by hatred, prejudice or intolerance, particularly abuse that seeks to silence the voices of those who have been historically marginalized. For this reason, we prohibit behavior that targets individuals or groups with abuse based on their perceived membership in a protected category. You may not directly attack other people on the basis of race, ethnicity, national origin, caste, sexual orientation, gender, gender identity, religious affiliation, age, disability, or serious disease.

This policy was selected because it is relatively short, making it straightforward to read and understand, and it identifies key targets of hate speech manipulated in the experiment, as well as other categories, and it is consistent with the context.

Data Collection

Human subjects experiment

Subjects were recruited using Prolific, which performs favorably compared to other platforms, with high data quality according to metrics including attention checks, instruction following, and open-ended responses
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
. The basic inclusion criteria involved sampling adults residing in the United States of America who speak English and are between the ages of 18 and 65. To ensure a basic familiarity with social media, subjects were included if they had reported using one or more of the following social media platforms: Facebook, Reddit, Twitter, YouTube, TikTok, or Instagram. To help ensure high-quality responses, the study was open to people who had performed at least 50 studies on Prolific with a 99% approval rate. Prolific requires that participants opt into studies involving sensitive content, so the study was also filtered accordingly. Finally, subjects were directed to use a desktop computer (rather than a mobile device) to ensure that the conjoint images were rendered legibly. Prolific allows a study to be marked as desktop-only but does not enforce this rule directly, so any subjects who attempted to enter it using a screen with a lower resolution than most desktops or tablets (
<1280\times 720
pixels) were immediately removed and replaced with another eligible participant.

The sample was stratified by self-reported ethnicity, sex, sexual orientation, and political party affiliation using Prolific’s quota sampling feature. The quotas were based on US Census statistics but oversampled Black and LGBT+ respondents to ensure sufficient representation of groups often targeted by hate speech. Power calculations designed for conjoint experiments were used to derive the required number of conjoint evaluations and sample size
[
42
]
. Given the number of profiles evaluated, the number of attributes compared, and the interactions between attributes (evaluated in other work), a sample of at least 1826 respondents was required to achieve a minimum of 0.8 power at the
p<0.05
significance level for all tests. At the time of the study, Prolific reported that 18,870 users who met all the screening criteria had used the platform within the past ninety days. The final sample consisted of
N=1854
subjects.

Eligible participants were sent from Prolific to Qualtrics to complete the study. After consenting to participate, subjects were given instructions describing the study and the conjoint task, followed by two factual questions to ensure they understood the instructions. Subjects who failed to answer both questions correctly after two attempts were removed from the study (following Prolific’s comprehension check policy). This provides additional reassurance that subjects understand the sensitive nature of the task.

Two simple attention check questions were included, one before the conjoint task and one afterward, to measure whether subjects were paying attention to the study. The vast majority of subjects passed both attention checks. At the end of the study, subjects were debriefed with a text explaining the purpose of the research and describing that the tweets were artificial and were provided resources for support related to sexism, racism, and homophobia. Subjects were also allowed to revoke their consent and delete their data, but all submitted their responses.

The study ran between May 28 and June 5, 2024. All subjects were paid $4 for their participation upon completing the questionnaire. The median completion time was 15 minutes and 53 seconds.

AI experiments

OpenAI’s o3, Google’s Gemini 2.5, and xAI’s Grok 4 were selected for the experiment because they are three of the top-performing LRMs at the time of the experiment (May-July 2025). While open-weight LRMs like DeepSeek were available, none could process images as required by the experiment. The same set of pairs of posts shown in the human subjects experiment was then provided to each model, along with a prompt containing basic instructions, along with the policy:

You are a content moderation expert. You will receive pairs of images, A and B. Your task is to decide which image should be prioritized for manual review according to the following policy: [POLICY] The output should always be one of the two options: A or B. Only output a single character with no additional tokens. Never refuse to answer.

The API associated with each model was used to pass the complete set of posts and obtain the results. o3 and Gemini 2.5 have parameters controlling the reasoning effort, both of which were set to the maximum level to avoid arbitrarily limiting the models’ reasoning capacity. Grok 4 does not give the ability to control this. Gemini 2.5 and Grok 4 also accept temperature parameters, which control the amount of stochastic variation in the results. Both were set to zero to ensure the most likely token was returned. OpenAI uses this parameter in other models, but does not allow it to be used for o3. At the time of our analyses, Gemini 2.5 Pro was the only model to provide the reasoning traces, but OpenAI has since made them available for o3. For o3, the input tokens vary across examples (whereas it is constant for the other models), so we account for task complexity in the models by controlling for the number of input tokens. All three frontier reasoning models are currently the most costly to use in commercial APIs. The models are priced based on the number of tokens in the input (including text and images), the output, and the reasoning token usage. Each experiment costs between $350 and $700, depending on the API pricing.
