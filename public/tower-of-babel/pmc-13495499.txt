Large-scale genomic analysis reveals the origin and evolution of Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) in plants
Authors: Ning Zhang, Yanan Guo, Pingu Liu, Kemeng Huang, Liang Zhang, Zhixuan Liu, Haoyu Lu, Hongyan Zhang, Lifeng Wang, Ke Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlutathione S-transferases (GSTs) are a crucial gene superfamily for plant stress adaptation. However, their evolutionary trajectories and genomic organizational principles across the plant kingdom remain poorly understood.ResultsThrough a large-scale comparative genomic analysis of 74 plant species, we identified 4,355 GST genes and classified them into 16 subfamilies. Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed massive and lineage-specific expansion of the stress-responsive Tau and Phi subfamilies in land plants, in contrast to the high conservation of ancient subfamilies (e.g., Theta, Zeta). Structural analysis suggested clade-specific motifs in Tau members associated with functional diversification. In polyploid barnyardgrass, genome duplication led to a disproportionate increase in GST copies: Tau and Phi genes showed unbalanced retention and formed selective clusters on homeologous group 1 and 2 chromosomes, while ancient subfamilies maintained dosage stability.ConclusionsOur study elucidates divergent evolutionary dynamics within the GST family. The lineage-specific expansion and selective retention of clustered Tau/Phi genes during polyploidization suggest a potential genomic signature that may be associated with adaptive evolution in plants. This study provides a comprehensive genomic resource and framework for future functional studies of GSTs in plant stress biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13045-7.Keywords: Glutathione S-transferase, Genomics, Gene family evolution, Polyploidy, Stress adaptation

Background

Abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures, frequently occur in combinations in natural environments and pose a critical threat to sustainable agriculture by significantly impairing crop productivity, quality, and adaptive potential [1, 2]. These stresses commonly induce the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), singlet oxygen (¹O₂), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and hydroxyl radicals (OH⁻), within plant tissues [3, 4]. At low concentrations, ROS function as homeostatic signaling molecules; however, beyond a threshold, they become lethal, causing oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules and organelles [5]. To maintain ROS below harmful levels, plants have evolved intricate antioxidative systems comprising both enzymatic components (e.g., superoxide dismutase; SOD, EC1.15.1.1, catalase; CAT, EC1.11.1.6, peroxidases; POD, EC 1.11.1.7) and nonenzymatic antioxidants (e.g., ascorbate; EC 1.11.1.11, glutathione; EC 1.11.1.9, carotenoids, tocopherols) [5, 6]. Within this defense network, the glutathione S-transferase (GST) superfamily plays a pivotal role in oxidative stress tolerance by catalyzing detoxification reactions and contributing to cellular redox homeostasis [7].

GSTs, a multifunctional and structurally conserved gene superfamily, play a key role in plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses [8]. Their function is intimately linked to glutathione (GSH; γ-Glu-Cys-Gly), a tripeptide that serves as a major antioxidant and redox buffer. GSH directly scavenges ROS and is the essential substrate for GSTs [7, 9]. GSTs catalyze the conjugation of GSH to both endogenous and exogenous electrophilic, hydrophobic substrates, forming more polar, hydrophilic compounds for excretion or vacuolar sequestration via ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters [10–13]. Beyond this primary detoxification role, they exhibit versatile catalytic capacity, encompassing GSH-dependent peroxidase and isomerase activities, as well as deglutathionylation reactions [14]. Structurally, functional GST enzymes are dimeric, predominantly homodimers [15]. Each subunit consists of an N-terminal domain containing a highly conserved GSH-binding site (G-site) and a more variable C-terminal domain that binds diverse hydrophobic substrates (H-site) [16–18].

GSTs are ubiquitously expressed across diverse organisms, including microbes, animals, and plants [19]. Based on sequence and structural similarities, plant GST genes are categorized into multiple distinct subfamilies, including Tau, Phi, Lambda, Theta, Zeta, dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR), γ-subunit of the eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1B (EF1Bγ), tetrachlorohydroquinone dehalogenase (TCHQD), as well as metaxin, Ure2p, Hemerythrin, Iota, GSTs with two thioredoxins (GST2N), and microsomal prostaglandin E-synthase type 2 (mPGES2) families [18, 20, 21]. Among these, Tau, Phi, Lambda, and TCHQD GSTs are plant-specific [22]. Of these plant-specific subfamilies, the Tau and Phi have received the most research attention, owing to their prevalence across plant lineages and their established roles in conferring tolerance to diverse abiotic stresses [19, 23].

GST activity was first identified in maize (Zea mays), where it was implicated in defense mechanisms against herbicide injury [24]. Furthermore, GSTs are involved in responses to a wide array of abiotic stresses, such as salt [25], drought [26], low-temperature [27], heavy metal toxicity [28], and UV stressors [29] in transgenic plants. They play a pivotal role in plant stress resistance mechanisms. For example, in oats, AsGSTU12-15 improved stress tolerance under drought and cadmium (Cd) conditions by modulating ROS homeostasis and facilitating GSH-mediated Cd chelation [30]. In tomato, LeGSTU2 has been reported to act as a stress regulator by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD, POD), thereby strengthening ROS scavenging capacity and maintaining ROS homeostasis [31]. Similarly, heterologous expression of the rice genes OsGSTU4 and OsGSTU30 in Arabidopsis thaliana enhanced tolerance to multiple stresses, including salt, oxidative, drought, and heavy metal challenges [32, 33]. Beyond detoxification, GSTs also function as nonenzymatic carriers for intracellular transport [34]. Studies have shown that the plant-specific Phi subfamily may play an important role in the transport of anthocyanin, as it catalyzes the conjugation of anthocyanins to glutathione, facilitating their sequestration into the vacuole via glutathione pumps, and that its loss of function can cause pigment loss [35, 36].

Ongoing advances in sequencing technology have enabled the generation of high-quality plant genome sequences, facilitating comprehensive exploration of the origin and evolution of gene families. Genome-wide identification and analysis of GSTs have been conducted in a variety of plant species, including cotton [37], poplar [38], citrus [39], jujube [40], and beardgrass [41]. These studies have primarily focused on the identification and characterization of GST homologous within specific species, as well as on analyses of their expression profiles across different tissues, developmental stages, and under various treatments. For instance, in tomato, the SlGSTU43 gene has been shown to enhance salt stress tolerance by boosting antioxidant enzyme activity, thereby improving ROS scavenging, and by promoting lignin biosynthesis to facilitate water and nutrient transport, thereby supporting plant growth under salinity [42]. Despite these findings, the genomic architecture of the GSTs across their evolutionary trajectory remains underexplored.

To gain deeper insights into the evolutionary history of GSTs, we performed a comprehensive phylogenomic analysis of GST genes across 74 plant species spanning a broad phylogenetic spectrum from algae to dicots. Our findings elucidate the patterns of origin, expansion, and functional diversification of this gene family across the plant kingdom. Specifically, focusing on Echinochloa crus-galli (L.) Beauv. (barnyardgrass), a polyploid species known for its extreme adaptability, we dissect the GSTs explosion during polyploidization, yielding novel insights into the genomic basis of plant stress adaptation.

Materials and methods

Data retrieval

In this study, genome assemblies and associated annotation files (including GFF, coding sequence (CDS), and protein sequences) for 74 plant species were downloaded from multiple databases, including Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov), National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI- https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html), and other individual genome databases (Table S1). The 74 species were selected to represent the major phylogenetic lineages of plants, covering rhodophytes, chlorophytes, charophytes, bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms (including monocots, core eudicots, and basal angiosperms). Within each lineage, representative species were selected based on the availability of high-quality genome assemblies and annotations, ensuring a comprehensive investigation of GST gene family evolution across green plants. For genes with multiple transcripts, the longest isoform per gene was retained using a custom Perl script that groups sequences by gene ID.

Identification of GST family members

To systematically identify GST members in plants, 61 annotated GST protein sequences from A. thaliana were retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org) and used as query sequences. For each of the 74 species, we used the protein sequences from the downloaded genome annotation files to construct protein databases. These sequences were then used to perform homology searches using BLASTp (v 2.15.0) [43], with an E-value cutoff of 1e− 5.

All protein sequences from the 74 species were then subjected to domain prediction using pfam_scan.pl [44] with default parameters. Candidate GST proteins were identified based on Pfam family identifiers associated with GST domains, including GST_N (PF13409 and PF13417) and GST_C (PF00043, PF13410, and PF22041). Proteins containing at least one GST-related domain were retained. To ensure high confidence, only proteins identified by both BLAST homology searches and HMM-based domain analysis were selected, and redundant sequences were removed.

All candidate GST proteins obtained through this pipeline were further validated using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) [45] and the InterPro database [46]. Proteins lacking GST domains or exhibiting anomalously short lengths (< 100 amino acids (aa)) were excluded. Ultimately, only sequences meeting both the structural criteria and the length threshold (> 100 aa) were retained as high-confidence GSTs for downstream analyses.

Phylogenetic evolutionary analysis of the GST gene family

To infer the phylogenetic relationships among species, single-copy orthologous genes identified by OrthoFinder (v 2.15) [47] were used. Protein sequences of all single-copy orthologous were concatenated and used to construct a species phylogenetic tree with RAxML (v 8.2.12) [48] under the Maximum Likelihood (ML) framework, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The species tree was rooted in iTOL (https://itol.embl.de) using the rhodophyte Pyropia yezoensis as the outgroup, based on its phylogenetic position as one of the earliest-diverging lineages among the sampled species. For phylogenetic analysis of the GSTs, all identified GST protein sequences were aligned using MUSCLE (v 5.1) [49]. An ML phylogenetic tree was then constructed using IQ-TREE (v 3.0.1) [50]. Phylogenetic trees constructed for the subgene family-specific evolutionary analysis of 13 representative species were visualized and annotated with the R packages ggtree (v 3.10.0) [51] and treeio (v 1.26.0) [52].

Conserved motif identification and distribution

To identify conserved motifs within GST proteins, motif analysis was performed using Multiple for Motif Elicitation (MEME - v 5.5.5) [53] Suite with default parameters. The identified motifs were further analyzed in combination with phylogenetic relationships. Visualization of motif distributions along with phylogenetic trees and domain organizations was performed using the R (v 4.3.1) packages ggtree (v 3.10.0) [51], gggenes (v 0.5.1), and ggplot2 (v 3.4.4) (part of the tidyverse suite) [54], with plot assembly facilitated by patchwork (v 1.1.3) [55].

Chromosomal mapping and numbering of GST gene family members

The chromosomal locations of the identified GST genes were mapped based on the genome annotation (GFF) files of the barnyardgrass species. Using TBtools (v 2.390) [56], the genomic coordinates corresponding to the 481 GST genes were extracted and visualized on their respective chromosomes according to genomic sequence order.

Collinearity analysis and identification of gene duplication events

Collinearity analysis of GST genes was conducted using MCScanX [57], implemented within the JCVI toolkit (https://github.com/wyp1125/MCScanX). Gene duplication events, including whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem duplication (TD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD), were subsequently classified using the duplicate gene classifier module in MCScanX.

Selection pressure analysis (dN/dS) and integration with duplication data

To investigate the evolutionary forces underlying GST gene retention and divergence, we performed selection pressure analysis on homeologous gene pairs among the three subgenomes (A, B, C) of hexaploid E. crus-galli. Homeologous gene pairs were identified from syntenic blocks detected by MCScanX. For each pair, coding sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v 7.526) [58] with the auto option, and the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates (Ka/Ks) was calculated using Kaks Calculator 3.0 [59] with the MA method. Pairs with alignment lengths not divisible by three were excluded.

To link duplication types to gene fate, we integrated the duplication classification results with the Ka/Ks data. WGD-derived pairs that were not detected as homeologous pairs were designated as “lost”. Differences in Ka/Ks between duplication types were assessed using the Mann‑Whitney U test, and global differences among subgenome pairs (AH‑BH, AH‑CH, BH‑CH) were evaluated using the Kruskal‑Wallis test. All statistical analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy library [60].

Transcriptome data acquisition, processing, and differential expression analysis

To investigate GST gene expression under different stress conditions, we retrieved RNA-seq datasets from public repositories. Drought stress and pathogen (Pyricularia oryzae) infection data were obtained from the BIG Data Center (accession number PRJCA002334). Herbicide (florpyrauxifen-benzyl) treatment data were downloaded from the NCBI SRA database (BioProject PRJNA938437). Raw reads were processed using fastp (v 1.1.0) [61] for quality control (adapter trimming, quality filtering with Q20 threshold, and length filtering ≥ 50 bp). Clean reads were aligned to the E. crus-galli reference genome (PRJNA414998) using HISAT2 (v 2.2.1) [62]. Alignments were sorted and indexed with SAMtools (v 1.9) [63]. Gene expression was quantified at the gene level using featureCounts (v 2.0.7) [64] with parameters -t gene -g ID. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v 1.42.0) [65] in R, using |log2FC| > 1 and padj < 0.05 as significance thresholds. All analyses were conducted with three biological replicates per condition.

Results

Identification of GST genes in plants

To investigate the distribution of GST genes in plants, we collected genomic data from 74 representative species spanning major plant lineages, including 12 algae, seven bryophytes, five ferns and lycophytes, five gymnosperms, and 45 angiosperms (Table S1). Combined with rigorous homology searches and multi-domain validation, we identified 4,355 GST genes across all examined species, with counts ranging from six in Chondrus crispus to 231 in E. crus-galli (Fig. 1; Table S2), confirming the widespread presence of the GST family in plants.

Genes are categorized by domain architecture: those encoding both N- and C-terminal domains (pink), only the N-terminal domain (blue), and only the C-terminal domain (yellow)

To investigate the evolutionary history of the GST superfamily, we conducted a comprehensive phylogenomic analysis across diverse plant lineages. Our results show that the expansion of the GST gene complement is strongly coupled with increases in organismal complexity and the successful terrestrialization of land plants (Fig. 1). Our findings trace the origin of GST genes to red algae, indicating that this superfamily predates the emergence of land plants (Table S3). In early aquatic algae, the total number of GST genes was generally low, averaging 16.1, and members were predominantly proteins containing only the N-terminal or C-terminal domain. As plants adapted to terrestrial environments, the GST gene family expanded substantially, averaging 32.8 genes in bryophytes and 48.4 in ferns. In angiosperms, the family underwent further remarkable expansion, with the average number of GSTs per species around 75.5, substantially higher than in non-vascular plants, where the average is around 22.3. Additionally, the copy number of GST genes varies considerably across the Poaceae. While foundational diploid grasses such as Oryza sativa L., Z. mays, and Brachypodium distachyon have between 70 and 90 GST genes, the hexaploid weed barnyardgrass (E. crus-galli) encodes a remarkably expanded repertoire of 231 genes.

Furthermore, analysis of protein domain architecture categorized GSTs into three structural forms (Fig. 1): complete types (both N- and C-terminal domains) and truncated types (only the N- or C-terminal domain). Complete-type GSTs are the predominant form in land plants, whereas truncated types are more prevalent in algal lineages. This pattern indicates that the fusion and optimization of functional domains are important evolutionary features in the terrestrial adaptation of the GSTs.

Subfamily composition and evolutionary divergence of GSTs

The diversification of GST subfamilies marks a pivotal aspect of plant functional evolution. To trace the evolutionary origins and lineage-specific expansions of these subfamilies, we constructed an ML phylogenetic tree based on 617 GST protein sequences from 13 species spanning red algae, green algae, liverworts, mosses, lycophytes, and vascular plants (Table S4). The resulting topology provides a high-resolution framework for identifying ancestral GST clades and for tracing the subsequent diversification of terrestrial-specific subfamilies. Based on phylogenetic relationships and conserved domains, the GSTs were classified into 16 distinct subfamilies: Tau, Phi, Theta, Lambda, DHAR, Zeta, mPGES2, TCHQD, EF1Bγ, Omega-like, Hemerythrin, URE2p, Iota, GST_2N, Sigma, and Beta. Additionally, 11 unclassified sequences cannot be assigned to any established class but show similarity to GST genes (Table 1).

The phylogenetic distribution of GST subfamilies reveals a complex history of differential retention, expansion, and loss, reflecting functional adaptation to terrestrial environments (Fig. 2). Among the identified subfamilies, the Tau and Phi expanded most dramatically during the transition to land, especially compared with their sparse presence or absence in algal lineages. In our dataset, these two subfamilies comprise 270 and 96 members, respectively, making them the most dominant GST groups in angiosperms. In contrast, several subfamilies, including Theta (22 members), Lambda (21 members), DHAR (34 members), TCHQD (15 members), EF1Bγ (20 members), and Zeta (23 members), exhibited remarkable evolutionary conservation. Their wide distribution across the plant kingdom suggests highly conserved physiological roles. Notably, the Lambda and TCHQD subfamilies were identified exclusively in land plants, suggesting that neo-functionalization is essential for plant terrestrialization.

Distinct evolutionary patterns emerged when examining lineage-specific distributions. The Sigma (nine members) and Beta (four members) subfamilies were found only in algae, potentially representing ancestral types that were lost through lineage-specific extinction in land plants. Conversely, early land plants (bryophytes and lycophytes) retained several unique subfamilies, including URE2p (13 members), Iota (five members), Hemerythrin (12 members), and GST_2N (eight members). The URE2p subfamily, present in Marchantia polymorpha, Physcomitrella patens, Selaginella moellendorffii, and certain algae, was conspicuously absent in seed plants, a distribution potentially shaped by ancient horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from bacteria [66]. The Hemerythrin clade, characterized by a non-heme iron-binding domain, was notably enriched in P. patens (eight of 12 genes), suggesting a bryophyte-specific expansion following a gene fusion event. Furthermore, the Iota has been identified only in non-vascular plants and algae and appears to have been lost during the evolution of most terrestrial plants. The GST_2N subfamily, characterized by two repeated N-terminal Trx domains and a conserved CPFC motif in one of them, lack the C-terminal domain [67]. They seem to be early structural variants preserved in non-vascular plants but later lost in the evolutionary path leading to higher vascular plants.

Given that the Tau subfamily represents the most significant expansion in terrestrial flora, pinpointing its evolutionary origin is crucial for understanding how plants colonized land. To resolve the ambiguity surrounding their emergence, we performed a detailed domain- and sequence-based analysis of Tau-candidate genes in basal lineages. Consistent with previous studies [21, 68], the two M. polymorpha genes (Mapoly0031s0032 and Mapoly0118s0009) contain complete GST-N (PF13409) and GST-C (PF13410) domains, confirming their classification as canonical Tau-class GSTs. In contrast, the Chara braunii genes GBG62537 and GBG65701 were identified as Omega-like proteins, with BLASTp best hits most similar to Omega (Table S5). This result is consistent with the hypothesis that the Tau subfamily likely emerged with the evolution of the earliest land plants. However, this hypothesis is currently based on the analysis of a single charophyte species (C. braunii); examination of additional charophyte genomes may refine the precise timing of the Tau subfamily emergence.

The structural conservation and diversity in GST subfamilies

Tau and Phi GSTs exhibit GSH-dependent peroxidase activity (GPX), catalyzing the conversion of H₂O₂ to water and organic hydroperoxides to alcohols, with concurrent formation of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) [69, 70]. This antioxidant function is likely a key adaptive trait that has contributed to their expansion and functional diversification in land plants. As the most expanded and functionally diverse GST group in land plants, the Tau subfamily serves as a primary model for elucidating the structural mechanisms underlying plant glutathione transferase diversification. By integrating phylogenetic reconstruction with domain and conserved motif analyses, we resolved the evolutionary patterns within this subfamily into five major clades (I–V) (Fig. 3a), each defined by distinct lineage compositions and clade-specific structural signatures (Fig. 3).

Phylogenetic relationships, domain architecture, and conserved motifs of the plant GST Tau subfamily. a Phylogenetic tree of Tau GSTs derived from a multiple sequence alignment of 13 representative plant species. b Schematic representation of the conserved GST domains across members. c Distribution of conserved protein motifs identified within the Tau subfamily

Structural analysis of the Tau subfamily reveals a clear evolutionary trajectory marked by changes in domain composition (Fig. 3b). The essential GST_N (PF02798) and GST_C (PF00043) / GST_C_2 (PF13410) domain framework defines the family, yet notable variations exist. The most ancestral Clade V, including sequences from the bryophytes M. polymorpha and Tau-like genes from the charophyte C. braunii, often exhibits a simplified configuration. Although not canonical Tau GSTs, the C. braunii sequences share architectural features with basal land plants, suggesting they represent transitional precursors. Notably, some members within this clade lack a canonical C-terminal domain. In contrast, the more derived Clades III and IV, encompassing a broad spectrum of early land plants, and the recently evolved Clades I and II, predominantly composed of angiosperms, possess the complete domain architecture. To further dissect the structural innovations driving the diversification of the Tau subfamily, we performed MEME motif analysis (Fig. 3c) and identified 10 conserved motifs (Fig. S1). Motifs 1–4 constitute the invariant catalytic core shared by all members, likely corresponding to the highly conserved N-terminal GSH-binding site and C-terminal substrate-binding site. Importantly, cross-subfamily comparisons reveal that motif_1 and motif_3 are strictly exclusive to the Tau lineage. These Tau-specific core motifs likely represent critical evolutionary innovations that biochemically define the unique catalytic pocket of this subfamily. In contrast, the remaining motifs (5, 6, 7, 9, 13, and 14) exhibit lineage- or clade-restricted distributions. Notably, motif_5 is exclusively present in the two C. braunii sequences (GBG65701 and GBG62537), which are identified as Omega-like GSTs rather than canonical Tau members. Furthermore, our broader analysis reveals that while motif_5 is widely distributed across various other GST subfamilies, it is conspicuously absent in all true land plant Tau GSTs. This striking absence, coupled with the acquisition of motifs 1 and 3, suggests that the structural reorganization of the catalytic core serves as a defining molecular signature for the canonical Tau lineage, providing robust support for its phylogenetic divergence from other subfamilies. Unique motif acquisitions demarcate different clades: Clades III is primarily characterized by the presence of motif_6, whereas the angiosperm-rich Clade I is distinguished by the absence of motif_14. Importantly, Clades I and II contain numerous orthologous genes with known stress-responsive functions, such as Arabidopsis AtGSTU1 [71] and O. sativa L. OsGSTU4 [72]. This suggests that the emergence of these clade-specific motifs may be evolutionarily linked to the increasing complexity of terrestrial environments and the expanding detoxification repertoire. The precise functional relevance of these specific motifs warrants future experimental validation.

The Phi subfamily exhibits a distinct evolutionary trajectory, marked by an earlier origin and greater structural plasticity (Fig. S2). Domain and motif integration confirmed that while all Phi members maintain a stable core framework typically composed of GST_N (PF02798) and GST_C (PF00043), early-diverging lineages, such as the bryophytes (M. polymorpha and P. patens), exhibit significant subtype differentiation. This is evidenced by the utilization of variant N-terminal domains, including GST_N_2 (PF13409) and GST_N_3 (PF13417). A striking hallmark of Phi evolution is the high frequency of intragenic duplications and multi-module fusions. For instance, certain members, such as Ws005906 (C. praecox), contain tandemly repeated GST modules within a single protein, which is corroborated by the exact duplication of catalytic and substrate-binding motifs (e.g., motifs 2, 4, 6, 9, and 11) across the extended sequence. This profound structural redundancy, together with the extensive genomic tandem duplication clusters observed in A. thaliana and O. sativa L., likely facilitated the rapid expansion of the Phi subfamily, enabling versatile functional diversification and adaptation without losing core detoxification capabilities.

Compared with these dynamically expanded classes, ancient subfamilies, including DHAR, TCHQD, Lambda, EF1Bγ, Theta, and Zeta, reveal deep-rooted functional specializations (Fig. S3-S8). These lineages follow distinct structural pathways rooted in their specific biochemical roles. For instance, Theta-class GSTs demonstrate significant GSH-dependent peroxidase activity, contributing to the reduction of oxidative stress metabolites [73]. DHAR-class GSTs participate in the ascorbate-GSH cycle, regenerating ascorbic acid (AsA) to maintain antioxidant capacity [74]. Against this backdrop of functional specialization, these ancient subfamilies exhibit characteristic evolutionary patterns. The DHAR subfamily maintains remarkable motif stability across all plants, whereas the TCHQD and Theta classes exhibit progressive differentiation through novel domain combinations while preserving the core framework; notably, the Theta lineage is uniquely distinguished by the specific acquisition of motif_10. The Zeta subfamily shows evidence of more pronounced structural innovation, and EF1Bγ members exist as fixed GST-EF1G domain chimeras, a specialized configuration established early in eukaryotic evolution that is strictly defined by the exclusive presence of motif_8 and motif_15. Collectively, these patterns underscore an evolutionary strategy of maintaining a stable catalytic core while leveraging clade-specific structural elaborations to achieve functional diversification.

Disproportionate expansion and subfamily-specific evolutionary trajectories of GSTs during polyploidization

Polyploidization is a key driver of plant evolution, facilitating gene family expansion through WGD and thereby supplying a genetic repertoire for functional innovation and adaptation [75]. Notably, polyploid plants have been proposed to exhibit enhanced environmental adaptability [76, 77]. To explore the impact of polyploidization on the evolution of GST gene families, we selected barnyardgrass, a common polyploid weed in paddy fields with strong environmental adaptability, as the research model [78].

To investigate the expansion patterns of the GSTs under polyploidization and the differential responses among its subfamilies, we conducted a systematic identification and comparative analysis of GST genes across a ploidy series of barnyardgrass, including E. haploclada (2n = 2x = 18), E. oryzicola (2n = 4x = 36), and E. crus-galli (2n = 6x = 54). Consistent with WGD, the total number of GST genes increased with increasing ploidy level. However, this expansion was disproportionate. For instance, the hexaploid E. crus-galli harbored approximately 231 GST genes, which is less than the theoretical threefold increase expected from its diploid progenitor (99 genes) (Table S6), indicating significant gene loss and selective retention after polyploidization. Strikingly, this response was highly subfamily-specific.

To dissect this subfamily-specific response, we compared the expansion dynamics of different GST classes. The large, stress-responsive Tau and Phi subfamilies exhibited an unbalanced expansion, failing to scale proportionally with increasing ploidy. Their copy numbers did not increase in proportion to ploidy, as illustrated by the Tau (diploid: tetraploid: hexaploid = 64:89:131) and Phi (17:31:50) subfamilies. These results showed that the GST gene families exhibit substantial gene loss following WGD in barnyardgrass, which is consistent with previous observations in the whole genome of barnyardgrass [79]. In contrast, the evolutionarily ancient Theta, Zeta, DHAR, EF1Bγ, and TCHQD subfamilies largely maintained the expected proportional increase (approaching a 1:2:3 ratio), demonstrating remarkable dosage stability and gene retention (Table S6). Phylogenetic analysis of barnyardgrass further supports this divergence (Fig. 4a), the Tau and Phi subfamilies, homeologous gene copies in polyploids often showed complex one-to-many relationships or were absent, reflecting dynamic reorganization. In contrast, homeologous gene in the ancient subfamilies predominantly maintained clear one-to-one orthologous relationships across ploidy levels, underscoring their structural conservation.

Phylogenetic relationship and chromosomal distribution. a ML phylogenetic tree of GST proteins from a barnyardgrass ploidy series (E. haploclada, E. colona, and E. crus-galli). b Chromosomal distribution of GST genes in hexaploid barnyardgrass (E. crus-galli)

Next, we conducted comparative chromosomal localization across the diploid, tetraploid, and hexaploid barnyardgrass accessions and revealed a conserved, highly uneven genome-wide distribution of GST genes in barnyardgrass, indicating strong and consistent chromosomal preferences independent of ploidy (Fig. 4b; Fig. S9). In all accessions, the vast majority of GSTs were located on chromosomes 1–4, with a notable paucity or complete absence on smaller chromosomes, such as chr8 and chr9. This conserved pattern was driven primarily by the expansion of the stress-responsive Tau and Phi subfamilies, which were most concentrated on chromosomes 1 and 2 across all ploidy levels. The analysis delineated distinct subfamily preferences: Tau genes were broadly distributed across multiple chromosomes (including: chr1, chr2, chr3, chr5, chr6, and chr7), whereas Phi genes showed more pronounced, localized clustering on chromosomes 1, 2, and 5 (Table S7). Despite polyploidization, the distribution patterns of GST genes on homeologous chromosomes remained highly similar across the subgenomes of polyploids, for instance, among the three subgenomes (A, B, C) of hexaploid E. crus-galli, and likewise between the two subgenomes of tetraploid E. oryzicola. To investigate whether this distribution balance is reflected at the level of selection pressure, we performed Ka/Ks analysis on homeologous gene pairs among the E. crus-galli subgenomes. The results revealed no significant difference in Ka/Ks at the whole‑GST level among subgenome pairs (Kruskal‑Wallis test, p = 0.874), with mean Ka/Ks values of 0.28 (AH‑BH), 0.29 (AH‑CH), and 0.23 (BH‑CH) (Table S8). The comparable Ka/Ks values across subgenomes suggest that, among retained GST homeologous genes, selective constraints are similarly maintained across all three subgenomes. This observation is consistent with (but does not definitively prove) the scenario of balanced retention following WGD inferred from gene counts, where no clear subgenome dominance was observed (Fig. 4b; Fig. S9).

Evolutionary dynamics of GSTs during polyploidization

To delineate the genomic mechanisms underlying the observed expansion and chromosomal distribution patterns, we performed detailed synteny and gene-duplication analyses across the barnyardgrass ploidy series. Synteny analysis confirmed and extended the chromosomal localization findings, demonstrating that the majority of conserved syntenic GST gene blocks were concentrated on chromosomes 1–4, whereas chromosome 8 contained almost no syntenic GST genes (Fig. 5a).

To understand the dynamics behind this architecture, we performed detailed gene-duplication analyses across the ploidy series. Statistical analysis of duplication types revealed shifting expansion modes (Table S9). In E. haploclada, expansion occurred primarily through tandem duplications (TD, 25 pairs) and transposed duplications (TRD, 11 pairs). In E. oryzicola, the total number of events increased, with notable rises in WGD (30 pairs) and TRD (24 pairs). In E. crus-galli, WGD (103 pairs) became the predominant driver, accompanied by TD (10 pairs), TRD (38 pairs), and dispersed duplications (DSD, 32 pairs). Synteny analysis further revealed frequent loss of homologous genes specifically within the Tau and Phi families in polyploids. These findings indicate that as ploidy increases, the GST family expands through multiple duplication modes, with WGD playing an increasingly dominant role, while lineage-specific duplications (TD, TRD) and subsequent selective loss contribute to the final copy number and genomic distribution (Table S10).

Collectively, these selection and duplication analyses indicate that the Tau and Phi subfamilies significantly expanded through recent lineage-specific duplication and divergence. The extensively expanded Tau genes formed syntenic blocks across multiple chromosomes (Fig. S10), whereas the syntenic Phi genes were more spatially restricted to key loci (Fig. S11). The Tau subfamily experienced the most significant expansion, while the Phi subfamily, though expanding to a lesser extent, kept many syntenic genes on primary chromosomes. This retention is probably vital for preserving its functional diversity. These subfamilies, through the formation of local gene clusters and unique chromosome distributions, have mainly driven the expansion of the GST family in barnyardgrass, possibly enhancing adaptive capabilities under selective pressure.

To investigate the candidate function about environmental responsiveness of this evolutionary expansion, we analyzed the expression patterns of GST genes under three distinct stress conditions: drought stress, florpyrauxifen-benzyl herbicide treatment, and Pyricularia oryzae infection (Fig. 5b–d). The analysis revealed that under drought stress, a robust transcriptional response was observed with 17 GST genes being significantly upregulated. The Tau subfamily dominated this response, accounting for 64.71% (11 out of 17) of the upregulated GST genes, followed by the Zeta subfamily (three genes), with single representatives from the DHAR, Phi, and Omega-like subfamilies (Fig. 5b). Under herbicide application, seven GST genes showed significant upregulation. The Tau subfamily remained the primary responder, comprising 57.14% (four out of seven) of the upregulated genes, while one gene each was assigned to the EF1Bγ, Phi, and Zeta subfamilies (Fig. 5c). In response to pathogen infection, a more focused response was observed, with nine GST genes significantly upregulated, of which the Tau subfamily accounted for 88.89% (eight out of nine), and the remaining gene belonged to the Lambda subfamily (Fig. 5d). This subfamily-specific expression pattern aligns remarkably with the evolutionary dynamics described above. The Tau subfamily, which experienced the most significant lineage-specific expansion, is precisely the one that dominates the transcriptional response to multiple abiotic and biotic stresses. In contrast, the ancient subfamilies (Theta, Zeta, DHAR, Omega), which were retained under purifying selection following whole-genome duplication events, showed more limited stress-responsive expression. These expression patterns demonstrate that GST genes, particularly Tau and Phi subfamilies, are transcriptionally responsive to multiple stresses. However, whether this responsiveness directly contributes to enhanced stress tolerance requires further experimental validation (e.g., via transgenic or biochemical assays).

The concordance between the evolutionary expansion patterns and stress-responsive expression profiles suggests a functional specialization of recently expanded GST subfamilies in the stress response of barnyardgrass. These expression patterns further support the evolutionary model proposed above, wherein WGD provided the genomic substrate for GST family expansion.

Discussion

GSTs constitute a large and diverse superfamily in plants, attracting significant interest due to their involvement in various physiological processes. These functions include the detoxification of xenobiotics, including herbicides and environmental pollutants [80], redox homeostasis and oxidative stress tolerance [81], regulation of plant growth and development through hormone metabolism and signal transduction [82], as well as broad-spectrum adaptation to both biotic and abiotic stresses [31, 83]. The specific stress factors (e.g., UV, drought, pathogens, herbicides) and the corresponding GST-mediated detoxification pathways, however, vary dramatically across plant lineages, reflecting their distinct ecological niches and evolutionary histories [84]. Unlike numerous prior studies focused on single species or specific taxonomic groups (e.g., cotton [37], poplar [38], citrus [39]), the macroevolutionary history and diversification mechanisms of this pivotal gene family across the plant kingdom remain poorly understood. Consequently, a comprehensive identification and analysis of the GSTs across the plant kingdom has been lacking.

Our large-scale analysis across 74 plant species, from Rhodophyta to Eudicots, offers a refined perspective on the deep evolutionary history of the GST superfamily. The identification of GSTs in the alga P. yezoensis reinforces and potentially extends the established view of a pre-terrestrial origin for this gene family (Fig. 1), suggesting that its evolutionary roots may trace back to aquatic algal ancestors prior to the divergence of the plant lineage [21]. This finding aligns with the hypothesis that major GST classes diversified in the Proterozoic era, predating the colonization of land [85]. The universal conservation of subfamilies such as Theta, Zeta, DHAR, and EF1Bγ across all examined lineages underscores their fundamental, non-redundant cellular roles and stringent purifying selection [86]. Conversely, the lineage-specific presence of certain subfamilies (e.g., Iota, Ure2p, GST_2N) in early land plants and algae, and their subsequent loss in seed plants, highlights the dynamic nature of gene family evolution [86], where ancient innovations can be selectively pruned during major evolutionary transitions (Fig. 2).

Our findings elucidate distinct evolutionary trajectories for the Tau and Phi subfamilies across land plants. Tau GSTs are plant-specific, and Phi GSTs are predominantly found in plants, but highly similar sequences have also been identified in selected protists, fungi, and bacteria. We find the Phi genes are more prevalent in bryophytes, whereas Tau genes dominate in vascular plants (Table 1), a divergence that mirrors their functional specialization and likely reflects shifting environmental responsiveness. Intriguingly, our Tau phylogeny suggests that bryophytes are paraphyletic, implying that the genetic modules underlying Tau function originated before the evolution of complete terrestrial adaptation (Fig. 3). To validate the classification of ambiguous GST genes, we performed BLASTp searches against the GST collection and UniProt databases (Table S5). Our data support the presence of the Tau subfamily in bryophytes but not in charophytes [21, 68]. This suggests that while the Tau family originated significantly earlier than previously assumed, its definitive emergence as a canonical structure, which is characterized by the loss of ancestral motifs and the acquisition of a Tau-specific catalytic core, is closely tied to the rise of the first land plants (Fig. 3c). It should be noted that our analysis of charophytes is currently limited to a single species, C. braunii. While canonical Tau GSTs were not detected in this species, the possibility remains that other charophyte lineages or additional algal taxa may harbor divergent Tau family members not captured by our current sampling. Future studies incorporating a broader range of charophyte genomes will help further clarify the evolutionary origin and early diversification of the Tau subfamily. Their retention and refinement in early land plants might have been associated with the challenges of early terrestrial environments, although this hypothesis awaits functional testing (e.g., UV [29], drought [26]), ultimately paving the way for the remarkable diversification into five distinct subgroups observed in vascular plants (Fig. 3a), where key stress-responsive functions now reside. This diversification is evident in our identification of five distinct Tau subgroups, within which stress-responsive functions in models such as A. thaliana are often concentrated.

Polyploidy and WGD are widespread in nature and are considered major drivers of speciation and plant evolution [87]. Most duplicated genes arise from WGDs, making the study of duplicate gene fate crucial for understanding plant evolution [88]. To investigate how polyploidy reshapes gene families with potential adaptive functions, we focused on GSTs, a key player in detoxification and stress adaptation. The E. crus-galli serves as an ideal model for this study. It exhibits both recent polyploidization events and pronounced herbicide resistance, a trait often associated with GST expansion and diversification. Our analysis suggests that polyploidy provided the genetic raw material, but the intense selective pressure exerted by modern herbicides likely acted as a powerful filter, driving the rapid retention and neofunctionalization of specific Tau and Phi GST paralogous capable of metabolizing these xenobiotics [89]. This exemplifies how a lineage-specific stressor can potentially influence the evolutionary trajectory of a stress-responsive gene family on a short timescale. Using barnyardgrass as a model, our study reveals a dynamic and complex evolutionary landscape for the GST family during polyploidization. A key finding of our study is that the overall expansion of the GST family in barnyardgrass did not follow the simple expectation of a doubling of gene dosage after polyploidy (Fig. 4). Instead, it underwent intensive selective pruning and subfamily-specific regulation, consistent with the general principle that most duplicated genes are lost following WGD, while a subset is retained due to functional constraints or adaptive opportunities [90]. The Tau and Phi subfamilies were the most sensitive to polyploidy, exhibiting substantial loss of homeologous genes and resulting in a non-proportional expansion pattern. This likely reflects a “dosage balance” effect [91], wherein the genome avoids the potential cost of overexpression for certain stress-responsive genes while simultaneously providing the raw genetic material for subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization, it is a common evolutionary trajectory for retained duplicates [92]. In contrast, ancient subfamilies such as Theta and Zeta were retained in strict proportion to ploidy, suggesting they perform essential, non-redundant basal functions whose stoichiometry must be precisely maintained, a pattern observed in the core cellular machinery of other polyploid systems [93]. Our results in barnyardgrass echo and extend patterns observed in the polyploid evolution of other large gene families, such as transcription factors in wheat [94] or disease resistance genes in cotton [95], where rapid functional diversification post-WGD is often associated with adaptability [88]. Together, these findings suggest that polyploid genomes may facilitate the preferential retention, reshaping, and diversification of gene families associated with stress responses, such as the Tau and Phi subfamilies of GSTs. While these genomic alterations are correlated with the expanded stress-responsive repertoire in barnyardgrass, their direct contribution to environmental adaptability remains a compelling hypothesis that warrants further functional investigation [96]. Further functional studies could provide direct evidence for the adaptive significance of these expansion patterns. Collectively, our study reveals that the macroevolution of the plant GST superfamily has not been a mere neutral expansion but a process continuously shaped by the diverse and changing stress landscapes encountered by different plant lineages from aquatic origins to the colonization of land and the challenges of anthropogenic environments.

Despite the coordinated expansion of Tau and Phi subfamilies and their preferential induction under multiple stresses, suggesting a functional role in stress adaptation, definitive evidence will require experimental validation beyond transcriptomics. Future study should include biochemical characterization of representative GST enzymes, promoter activity assays to elucidate stress-induced regulation, and transgenic approaches in heterologous systems (e.g., Arabidopsis or rice) to directly test the contribution of specific GSTs to stress tolerance. Until such data are obtained, the adaptive significance of GST family expansion in polyploid barnyardgrass remains a compelling hypothesis rather than a conclusively demonstrated mechanism.

Although the present study provides a comprehensive genome-wide identification and evolutionary characterization of GST gene families across major plant lineages, all functional interpretations are derived from computational predictions and comparative genomic inferences. Consequently, while our transcriptomic data demonstrate stress-responsive expression of specific GST subfamilies, we cannot conclude that these expression changes confer adaptive advantages; direct experimental tests (e.g., gain- or loss-of-function assays under controlled stress conditions) are required to establish causality. Future investigations may extend these findings by employing transgenic verification, enzyme activity detection, and stress challenge experiments to experimentally validate the physiological functions and stress response mechanisms of key GST members identified in this study. Such functional assays will help to further elucidate the precise roles of the GST family in plant adaptation to diverse terrestrial environments and stress conditions.

Conclusions

In summary, our study reveals that the GST gene family has an ancient origin in early photosynthetic lineages and has undergone dynamic expansion and diversification throughout plant evolution. The early emergence and subsequent lineage-specific amplification of the Tau subfamily underscore its role in adaptive responses to terrestrial environments, whereas the conserved evolution of other subfamilies reflects essential housekeeping functions. Analyses in polyploid barnyardgrass further demonstrate that GST family expansion is shaped by selective retention and subfamily-specific responses to genome duplication. These results advance our understanding of GST evolution and provide insights into the molecular basis of plant environmental adaptation.
