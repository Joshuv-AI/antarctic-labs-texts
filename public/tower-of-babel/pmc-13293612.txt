Trends and Epidemiological Profile of Climate-Related Disasters in Spain between 1950 and 2024
Authors: Andrea Fernández García, José Antonio Cernuda Martínez, Pedro Arcos González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClimate-related disasters are the result of meteorological, hydrological, and climatological hazards. The increasing frequency of such disasters is closely linked to anthropogenic climate change, alongside other factors such as changes in land use and population exposure. The objective of the present study was to analyze the epidemiological profile of climate-related disasters in Spain and their temporal trends.MethodsA retrospective descriptive study of climate-related disasters in Spain was performed for the period between January 1950 and December 2024. Data were compiled from various international and national sources to ensure comprehensive coverage of events. Both absolute and relative frequencies were analyzed, and mean mortality, injury, and affected person rates per million inhabitants were calculated for each disaster type. Temporal trends were assessed through the Mann-Kendall test, while differences in total mean rates across disaster categories were evaluated using the ANOVA test.ResultsA total of 224 climate-related disasters were documented. Statistically significant increasing trends were identified in the total absolute frequency of disasters (tau = 0.558; p < 0.001), the mean mortality rate (tau = 0.239; p < 0.001), the mean injury rate (tau = 0.144; p = p < 0.001), and the mean rate of affected individuals (tau = 0.481; p = p < 0.001). The mean mortality rate exhibited statistically significant differences according to the type of disaster (F = 4.40; p = 0.013).ConclusionsFrom 1950 to 2024, a statistically significant increasing trend was observed in the number of climate-related disasters and their associated human toll. Data recorded through December 2024 indicate that this upward trajectory continues, particularly regarding mortality and affected individuals.

Introduction

A disaster is defined as a serious disruption in the functioning of a community or society that results in significant loss of human life, property, economic assets, or environmental resources, and exceeds the affected community’s capacity to cope with its own resources [1]. Disasters associated with natural hazards can be generated by a variety of factors, including biological, geological, meteorological, hydrological, and climatological hazards [2, 3]. The latter three categories (meteorological, hydrological, and climatological) constitute the group of climate-related disasters [3–5]. Following the classification of the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED), disaster categories are defined as follows: (i) hydrological events caused by deviations in the normal water cycle or overflow of bodies of water (e.g., floods); (ii) meteorological events caused by short-lived micro- to mesoscale extreme atmospheric conditions (e.g., storms and extreme temperatures); (iii) climatological events caused by long-term atmospheric processes (e.g., droughts and wildfires).

Anthropogenic climate change has been identified as a primary driver of the increasing frequency and severity of these disasters, impacting various aspects of people’s lives, particularly among the most vulnerable communities [6]. Spain is highly vulnerable to the consequences of climate change due to its geographical location and diverse climatic conditions. Since the 1970s, the country has experienced a lengthening of summers, estimated at almost 5 weeks; a reduction in rainfall; and an increase in extreme weather events, along with an increase in temperatures, both maximum and minimum. Heat waves have become more frequent, longer, and more intense [7, 8]. The year 2022 was particularly severe, recording 4,744 deaths, a figure that remains the highest in the recent series, surpassing the 3,009 deaths registered in 2023 [9]. Furthermore, the recent isolated depression at high levels (DANA) in October 2024, particularly affecting the Spanish Levant and the province of Valencia, caused more than 200 deaths [10, 11].

The impact of weather-related disasters on human health is a multifaceted issue. The immediate consequences of these disasters include fatalities and injuries. Moreover, they are associated with the development of respiratory, cardiovascular, and dermatological pathologies [12–14]. Furthermore, the consequences of disasters, such as the overload on hospitals, damage to infrastructure, and the interruption of transport, can impede access to medications and disrupt ongoing chronic treatments. This, in turn, can exacerbate preexisting diseases and hinder an adequate lifestyle [15, 16]. It has been posited that the occurrence of certain disasters may also result in the propagation of communicable diseases [17]. The mental health of individuals exposed to a disaster may be adversely affected, and there is a risk of developing anxiety, depression, suicidal tendencies, or post-traumatic stress disorder, among other conditions [18, 19]. In certain instances, the discharge or mobilization of pollutants resulting from the disaster may also exert deleterious effects on human health in the medium term [20, 21].

Weather-related disasters have been shown to have a considerable economic impact. According to estimates by the World Meteorological Organization [22], economic losses from these disasters between 1970 and 2021 reached USD 4.3 trillion. The drought experienced by Spain in 2023, with an estimated cost of USD 2.4 billion, is positioned ninth in the annual ranking of the costliest weather-related disasters worldwide [23]. The objective of this study was to analyze the epidemiological profile of climate-related disasters in Spain in terms of the morbidity and mortality produced, as well as their temporal trends.

Materials and Methods

An observational study of climate-related disasters in Spain from January 1950 to December 2024 was conducted. The study employed the definition of disaster from the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction [1]: a serious disruption of the functioning of a community or society on any scale due to hazardous phenomena that interact with conditions of exposure, vulnerability, and capacity, causing one or more of the following – human, material, economic, and environmental losses and impacts. To be included in the study, a disaster event had to meet at least one of the following criteria, consistent with the EM-DAT international standards: (i) ten or more people reported dead; (ii) 100 or more people reported affected; (iii) a call for international assistance; or (iv) the declaration of a state of emergency. In our specific analysis for Spain, we also included events meeting a threshold of 50 or more injuries to capture high-morbidity episodes. Explicitly stating these thresholds is crucial for understanding the inherent selection bias: smaller scale events that do not reach these numerical minima are excluded, potentially leading to an underestimation of the total cumulative impact of minor climate-related hazards. The “affected” category includes people requiring immediate assistance for basic survival needs (food, water, shelter, sanitation) or those who have been displaced, as defined by the international databases used.

The study employed a multisource approach to identify and characterize climate-related disasters. EM-DAT [3] served as the primary source for health impact indicators, including mortality, number of injured, and affected persons, ensuring international comparability. To minimize reporting bias and ensure a comprehensive event catalog, data were cross-referenced with other international databases such as Swiss Re [24] and NatCat [25], which provided additional verification of large-scale occurrences. National sources, including reports from the Spanish General Directorate of Civil Protection, the Ministry of the Interior, and the Ministry of Health, were specifically utilized to validate the magnitude of local events and to refine the epidemiological profile of disasters such as the 2024 DANA. Finally, secondary sources such as regional newspapers were used to corroborate historical data from the early decades of the study period.

Population-at-risk figures were obtained from the National Institute of Statistics (INE) [26] for each year and geographic location where the episodes occurred. To ensure demographic comparability across the 75-year study period (1950–2024), health impact indicators were calculated as annual cumulative rates per million inhabitants. For each year, the total number of victims (mortalities, injuries, or affected individuals) was aggregated and divided by the corresponding national population for that specific year. This population-weighted approach accounts for Spain’s demographic growth over time and shifts the focus from individual event impacts to the total annual population burden. By calculating these annualized weighted rates, the analysis mitigates potential biases arising from varying population densities across different historical periods and locations.

This robust data structure is allowed for the assessment of temporal trends using the Mann-Kendall nonparametric test and the evaluation of differences across disaster categories. Specifically, the Mann-Kendall test was employed to assess the presence of temporal trends in the annual cumulative mortality rate, the annual cumulative rate of individuals affected, and the annual cumulative injury rate per million inhabitants for each disaster type throughout the examined period. Furthermore, the ANOVA test was used to determine the presence of significant differences between the mean annual rates of mortality, injuries, and affected persons according to the type of disaster for the entire series. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationship between the annual number of injuries and affected persons at a global level. All calculations were performed using the Stata v. 16.0 statistical package (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Between January 1950 and December 2024, Spain recorded a total of 225 climate-related disasters, comprising 126 climatological (56.0%), 73 hydrological (32.4%), and 26 meteorological (11.6%) events. Table 1 presents the absolute and relative frequency of each disaster type by year. A comprehensive inventory of these 225 recorded disaster events, including their specific group, type, and data source, is provided in Table 2. A statistically significant upward trend was identified in the total annual frequency of climate-related disasters during the 75-year study period (tau = 0.558; p < 0.001), with consistent increasing trends observed across all specific categories. Regarding health impact indicators, the analysis of population-weighted annual cumulative rates, summarized in Table 3, revealed significant temporal growth across all dimensions. The annual cumulative mortality rate showed a robust and statistically significant increasing trend (tau = 0.255; p = 0.003), as illustrated in the temporal progression shown in Figure 1. The annual injury rate per million inhabitants showed a statistically significant upward trajectory over the examined period (tau = 0.218; p = 0.016), with its historical evolution detailed in Figure 2. The strongest correlation with time was observed in the annual rate of affected persons, which exhibited a sharp and highly significant increase (tau = 0.596; p < 0.001), a trend further visualized in Figure 3.

However, these health-related trends varied significantly when disaggregated by disaster category. Climatological disasters were characterized by a significant increase in the affected rate (tau = 0.450; p = 0.002) but a significant decrease in mortality (tau = −0.333; p = 0.008). In contrast, hydrological and meteorological disasters showed generalized increases in affected rates, while their specific mortality and injury trends remained nonsignificant (p > 0.05). Regarding the comparison between disaster types, the ANOVA test performed on the mean annual rates revealed statistically significant differences in mortality (F = 4.40; p = 0.013). Bonferroni post hoc contrasts identified that these differences were specifically located between meteorological and climatological events (t = 2.92; p = 0.023). No significant differences were found between categories for the annual injury (F = 1.24; p = 0.484) or affected rates (F = 0.87; p = 0.687). Finally, an exhaustive correlation analysis was performed between all health impact indicators using annual cumulative rates. No statistically significant correlations were identified between the annual cumulative mortality rate and the rate of affected persons (r = −0.016; p = 0.888), nor between mortality and injuries (r = 0.026; p = 0.821). Similarly, the correlation between the annual rates of affected and injured persons (r = 0.009; p = 0.937) did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that in the Spanish context, the different dimensions of health impacts – lethality, morbidity, and social disruption – operate as independent dynamics, justifying the need for a disaggregated analysis by disaster category.

Discussion

From 1950 to 2023, a statistically significant rise in the frequency of climate-related disasters was documented in Spain, a trend that remains consistent with data recorded through December 2024. The observed rise in disaster occurrences in Spain corresponds with the documented global trends for such events in recent years [4, 27]. According to reports from organizations such as the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, the frequency of disasters of this nature has increased by nearly 35% since the 1990s [27]. In a similar vein, the CRED has documented an escalation in the incidence of these disasters between 2000 and 2019 in comparison with the preceding 2 decades [4]. The trend analysis also reveals an increase in the average rates of mortality, injury, and those affected by weather-related disasters in Spain. The increasing trend in disaster frequency observed in this study must be interpreted with caution. Part of this rise may be attributed to “reporting bias,” resulting from improved institutional capacity, the standardization of international classification systems (such as the EM-DAT criteria established in the late 1980s), and a higher social and media awareness of climate risks. While climatic evidence suggests a real increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme events in the Mediterranean basin, the modernization of monitoring systems and digital databases has likely enhanced the recording of events that, in earlier decades, might have gone undocumented. Therefore, the observed trend likely reflects a combination of both an actual increase in climate-related events and a significant improvement in disaster reporting standards.

The disasters with the highest average mortality rates were weather-related, especially extreme temperature episodes (cold waves and heat waves) [3]. A comprehensive analysis reveals that the planet’s mean temperature has exhibited an overall increase, a phenomenon attributable to climate change. Within the geographical confines of Spain, the average annual temperature has shown an increase of 1.57 degrees since 1961 [28]. The observed rise in global temperatures has been linked to an increase in the frequency and intensity of heat waves, as well as their deleterious impact on human health. This phenomenon has been documented in several studies [29–31] that have revealed a correlation between heat wave events and an increase in morbidity and mortality due to respiratory and circulatory causes in Spain.

Hydrological disasters were responsible for the highest average injury rates in the years studied. Furthermore, floods, a type of hydrological disaster, have been demonstrated to induce additional alterations in affected individuals. These alterations appear to be associated with the emergence of mental health problems, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder [32].

Climatological disasters, including droughts, are the most prevalent disasters identified in this research and those with the highest average number of people affected. Droughts represent a primary climatic hazard that has a profound impact on various sectors within Spain, including agriculture, livestock, water availability, and food supply [33, 34]. Furthermore, droughts have been demonstrated to have adverse effects on human health. These effects include cardiovascular, respiratory, and renal pathologies, malnutrition, vector-borne diseases, and mortality [33]. Salvador et al. [35] discovered an association between droughts and mortality from natural, circulatory, and respiratory causes in several regions of Spain, with the western region demonstrating the highest levels of impact. Interestingly, our exhaustive correlation analysis revealed no statistically significant relationship between annual mortality, injury, and affected rates. This independence suggests that in Spain, the health impacts of climate disasters follow decoupled dynamics; for instance, high-lethality events like heat waves (meteorological) often do not result in massive physical injuries, while large-scale droughts (climatological) affect millions without causing immediate mortality. This justifies the necessity of analyzing each health indicator as an independent variable of climate risk. In this regard, it is important to highlight that the peaks observed in the historical series for injured and affected persons correspond to major national catastrophes. The 1983 outlier is explained by the catastrophic floods in Northern Spain (the Basque Country and Cantabria), which caused a massive number of injuries and displaced persons. Similarly, the 1990 peak reflects the start of one of the most severe and prolonged droughts in Spanish history, which, according to official records, affected over 6 million people due to severe water restrictions and agricultural impact. We have verified these figures against official reports from Civil Protection and historical archives; they are realistic representations of the magnitude of these disasters. While these outliers heavily influence the Mann-Kendall trend analysis – as mortality and morbidity in other years are significantly lower by comparison – they reflect the true catastrophic nature of extreme climate events in Spain. To address the statistical challenge posed by these extreme values, this study utilized the nonparametric Mann-Kendall test. Unlike linear regression models, this rank-based approach is inherently robust against outliers, ensuring that the identified upward trends reflect a consistent temporal progression over 7 decades rather than being a mathematical artifact of these specific catastrophic years.

Furthermore, climate-related disasters have been shown to facilitate the emergence and subsequent spread of communicable diseases. Shifts in temperature and precipitation patterns, in conjunction with the increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, have been shown to modify the distribution and behavior of the vectors responsible for the transmission of certain diseases [36]. The alterations in climatic patterns that are currently occurring in Spain have the potential to result in an increase in the prevalence of diseases that are transmitted by mosquitoes, rodents, ticks, and sandflies [37, 38].

In this context, it is essential to consider that the health indicators analyzed (mortality, injury, and affected) are primarily based on the EM-DAT framework. These metrics are subject to inherent reporting biases; for instance, the definition of “affected” can be inconsistent across different regions and time periods, often underestimating indirect health impacts. Furthermore, data collection in earlier decades relied on manual records, which may have led to an underestimation compared to recent years. These biases, common in large-scale epidemiological disaster studies, require a cautious interpretation of absolute figures, although the observed relative trends remain a robust indicator of the increasing climate-related burden in Spain.

A critical point in historical disaster analysis is whether the observed increase in frequency reflects a true climatic trend or is a result of improved reporting. While “reporting bias” certainly influences the data – especially in the early decades (1950–1970) – the robustness of the upward trend in Spain suggests a genuine increase in extreme events, aligning with IPCC reports. Regarding mortality and morbidity, the increase in these rates also reflects greater population vulnerability and the higher intensity of recent phenomena, such as the 2003 and 2022 heat waves or the 2024 DANA. The inclusion of the finalized 2024 dataset, including the catastrophic October DANA, further reinforces this trend. This event exemplifies how recent hydrological phenomena are surpassing historical records, confirming that the 75-year upward trajectory in disaster frequency is not only persisting but intensifying in the current decade.

Limitations of the Study

The lack of uniform inclusion criteria across databases introduces a reporting bias, meaning the series is more robust for high-intensity events than for small-scale disasters. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of historical records throughout the period (1950–2024) suggests potential underreporting in the early decades; thus, temporal comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

A significant limitation arises from the influence of the aforementioned outliers (e.g., 1983 and 1990) on the statistical distribution. These extreme values significantly weigh the annual means and influence the Mann-Kendall trend analysis. To mitigate the bias inherent in simple arithmetic means, which treat all events equally regardless of the population size, this study employs weighted means for annual rates. This approach ensures that the impact is proportional to the size of the exposed population, providing a more accurate representation of the national health burden. By shifting from simple arithmetic means to annual cumulative rates weighted by the national population, we have corrected for demographic growth over the 75-year period. This methodological refinement ensures that the documented increase in health burden is a genuine epidemiological phenomenon and not a byproduct of Spain’s population increase from 28 to 48 million inhabitants.

In conclusion, this 75-year longitudinal analysis reveals a significant escalation in the frequency and public health impact of climate-related disasters in Spain. By employing population-weighted annual rates, we have demonstrated that the rising burden of mortality, injuries, and affected persons is a genuine epidemiological trend that transcends demographic growth. Meteorological events, particularly extreme temperatures, have emerged as the primary drivers of mortality, while hydrological and climatological hazards continue to cause substantial morbidity and social disruption.

These findings underscore the urgent need for the Spanish public health system to strengthen adaptive strategies, particularly regarding early warning systems and infrastructure resilience against extreme thermal and hydrological events. Furthermore, the challenges encountered in data harmonization highlight a critical need for international standardization of inclusion criteria within disaster databases. Establishing uniform reporting protocols is essential to enhance the comparability of climate risk data and to inform evidence-based global health policies in an era of accelerating climate change.
