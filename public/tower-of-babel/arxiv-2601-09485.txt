On lower bounds for hypergeometric tails

Abstract

1
Introduction, related work and main results

Throughout the text, given a positive integer
n
, we denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Given two random variables
X,Y
, we write
X\sim Y
to denote the fact that
X
and
Y
have the same distribution.
Given positive integers

n,k
such that
n\geq k\geq 1
and a positive integer
i\in[n]
, we denote by
\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
a hypergeometric random variable which counts the number of black marbles in a sample without replacement of size
k
from an urn that contains
i

black and
n-i
white marbles.
Formally, if
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(H=j)=\frac{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}{\binom{n}{k}},\quad\text{ for }\,j\in\{\max\{0,k-(n-i)\},\dots,\min\{k,i\}\}\,.

Recall that
\mathbb{E}(H)=\frac{ik}{n}
and
\text{Var}(H)=k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-k}{n-1}
. Finally,
given a positive integer
n
and a real
p\in[0,1]
, we denote by
\text{Bin}(n,p)
a binomial random variable of parameters
n,p
.

In this article we shall be concerned with lower bounds on the probability that a hypergeometric random variable exceeds its expected value. More precisely, let
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
. What is a sharp lower bound on the “tail”
\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)
?

Such a question arises in several settings.
For instance, suppose that a smuggler has a certain quantity, say
1
, of illicit product that he needs to transport across the border. The smuggler employs
n
agents, which he sends across the border simultaneously, knowing that exactly

k
of the agents will be chosen uniformly at random and seized by the authorities.
The smuggler decides to equally distribute the product over
i\in[n]
agents; thus loading
i
agents with an amount of
1/i
of the product and
n-i
agents with no amount.
The operation remains profitable as long as he manages to keep the amount not seized above the expected amount not seized. Which choice of
i\in[n]
maximizes the probability that the operation remains profitable? What is an upper bound on the probability that the operation remains profitable?

If
Y_{i}
is the amount of the product that makes it across the border, then it holds
Y_{i}=1-\frac{1}{i}\cdot H_{i}
, for
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
, and the expected amount not seized is equal to
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})=1-\frac{1}{i}\cdot\frac{ik}{n}=\frac{n-k}{n}
.
Hence, the operation is profitable with probability

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}>\frac{n-k}{n}\right)=\mathbb{P}(H_{i}<ik/n)=1-\mathbb{P}(H_{i}\geq ik/n)\,.

In this article it is shown that, when
n\geq 8k
, it is optimal for the smuggler to load
n-1
agents with no amount and one agent with the whole amount.

Besides smuggling-versus-customs situations, the problem arises in other settings, the most notable of which being the
Manickham-Miklós-Singhi (MMS) conjecture
, which we briefly discuss here. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a population (a multiset) consisting of
n\geq 2
real numbers whose sum is equal to zero. We sample
k\in[n]
elements from
\mathcal{P}
without replacement, and let
X_{\mathcal{P}}
be the sum of the elements in our sample. The MMS conjecture asserts that, when
n\geq 4k
, it holds
\mathbb{P}(X_{\mathcal{P}}\geq 0)\geq\frac{k}{n}
. The bound is sharp, as can be seen by considering the population
\{1,-\frac{1}{n-1},\ldots,-\frac{1}{n-1}\}
.
It is known (see Pokrovskiy
[
15
]
) that the MMS conjecture holds true when
n\geq 10^{46}k
, and it has been conjectured by Aydinian and Blinovsky (see
[
1
]
) that for every population
\mathcal{P}
as above, and every
1\leq k\leq n
, it holds

\mathbb{P}(X_{\mathcal{P}}\geq 0)\geq\min_{i\in[n-1]}\,\mathbb{P}(H_{i}\geq ik/n)\,,\,\text{ where }\,H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)\,.

Hence, provided that the Aydinian-Blinovsky conjecture holds true, the tails of the hypergeometric distribution appear as extreme instances of the MMS conjecture, where each
H_{i}
corresponds to a sample without replacement from the population
\mathcal{P}_{i}=\{\frac{1}{i},\ldots,\frac{1}{i},\frac{-1}{n-i},\ldots,\frac{-1}{n-i}\}
consisting of
i
values that are equal to
\frac{1}{i}
, and
n-i
values that are equal to
\frac{-1}{n-i}
.

We also investigate the problem of estimating from below the probability that a hypergeometric random variable takes a value that is larger than or equal to its expectation.
A closely related problem is to estimate from below the probability of a binomial random variable being larger than or equal to its expectation; a problem that has attracted considerable attention (see, for example,
[
6
,
8
,
13
,
14
]
, and references therein). For example, it is known (see Theorem
3.2
below) that
\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(k,p)\geq kp)\geq 1/4
, when
kp>1
.
However, the literature regarding the hypergeometric tail appears to be sporadic.

There are, of course, good reasons for this “sporadicness”. By a result of Vatutin and Mikhailov (
[
21
]
, see also
[
9
]
) it is known that a hypergeometric random variable is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables. In other words, the hypergeometric distribution is a Poisson-binomial distribution.
Hence, Berry-Esseen-type theorems for non-identically distributed independent summands apply. In particular, it is known (see
[
12
, Formula (17)]
for a more general result) that, if

H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
, it holds

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{0.5583}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}}\,.

(1)

It follows that
\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq 1/4
, when
\text{Var}(H)\geq(4\cdot 0.5583)^{2}\geq 4.98
.
Note that the inequality (
1
) provides a non-trivial bound when
\text{Var}(H)\geq(2\cdot 0.5583)^{2}\geq 1.24
.
Note also that while inequality (
1
) is simple, its proof is not, and it is natural to wonder whether one can obtain such a lower bound using “first principles” of the hypergeometric law.

Moreover, by combining the above-mentioned result of Vatutin and Mikhailov with upper bounds on the total variation distance between the binomial and a Poisson-binomial distribution,
it has been shown by Ehm (see
[
7
, Theorem 2]
for a more general result) that, when
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
is such that
\text{Var}(X)\geq 1
, it holds

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\,\geq\,\mathbb{P}(X\geq\mathbb{E}(X))-\frac{k-1}{n-1}\,.

(2)

In particular (see Lemma
3.3
below), it holds
\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq k/n
, when

n\geq 8k
and
\text{Var}(X)\geq 1
. As a consequence, if
i
is such that
i(n-i)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{k}
, then loading
n-1
agents with no amount and one agent with the whole amount is a better choice for the smuggler than loading
i
agents with an amount of
1/i
and
n-i
agents with no amount.

In this work, we aim at complementing the above results and at providing evidence that there is space for improvements.
Our first result implies that, when
n\geq 8k
, loading one agent with the whole amount is always a best choice for the smuggler.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n,k
be positive integers such that
n\geq 8k
, and let
i\in[n]
. If

H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H)\right)\,\geq\,\frac{k}{n}\,.

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
we combine (
2
) with certain properties of the hypergeometric distribution that allow us to handle the case where
\text{Var}(X)<1
.
Let us remark that the MMS and Aydinian-Blinovski conjectures mentioned above suggest that the assumption
n\geq 8k
in Theorem
1.1
may be replaced by the assumption
n\geq 4k
, but we are unable to prove it.
Moreover, we obtain the following lower bound.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and let
i,k\in[n]
. Suppose that
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
is a hypergeometric random variable such that
\mathbb{E}(H)\in[1\,,\,\min\{i,k\}-2]
and
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\,\geq\,\frac{e^{-1/8}}{4\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}}{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{n-1}{n-k}\cdot\text{Var}(H)}}\,.

The bound provided by Theorem
1.2
is, of course, weaker than the bound provided by (
1
) but has the advantage that it is obtained using “first principles”, and is applicable on a slightly wider range of the parameters.
In particular, Theorem
1.2
is proven by combining a lower bound on the mean absolute deviation (Theorem
2.6
) with an upper bound on the tail conditional expectation (Theorem
2.10
) of the hypergeometric distribution, which appear to be new and may be of independent interest.

Moreover, Theorem
1.2
occasionally complements and even improves upon the bound provided in (
1
). For instance, it follows from (
1
) that
\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq 0.01
, when
\text{Var}(H)\geq 1.29
.
On the other hand, Theorem
1.2
yields the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 4
and
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
be such that
\mathbb{E}(H)\in[1,\min\{i,k\}-2]
and
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
. Assume further that
\text{Var}(H)\geq 1
. Then

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq 0.049
.

Note that the bound provided by Theorem
1.2
can never be larger than
\frac{e^{-1/8}}{4\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.156
.
One way to strengthen this bound is by improving upon the constant
\frac{e^{-1/8}}{4\sqrt{2}}
.
Our numerical experiments suggest that this constant may be replaced by
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
, but we are unable to provide a proof. Furthermore, our numerical experiments suggest that under the assumptions
\frac{ik}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
it holds

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\,\geq\,\frac{1}{4}
.

2
Auxiliary results

In this section we collect some results regarding hypergeometric and binomial random variables that will be needed in the proofs of our main results.

2.1
Hypergeometric distribution

We begin with a result that provides a “refined Markov inequality” for non-negative integer-valued random variables whose mean is less than or equal to
1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
X
is a random variable, taking values in the set
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, which satisfies
\mathbb{E}(X)\leq 1
. Then

\mathbb{P}(X=0)\geq\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)\,.

Proof.

Observe that

\mathbb{P}(X=0)+\mathbb{P}(X\geq 1)=1\geq\mathbb{E}(X)\geq\mathbb{P}(X\geq 1)+\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)\,.

∎

Now we proceed with establishing some properties of the hypergeometric distribution. Our first lemma is a consequence of Lemma
2.1
, and will be needed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
to analyze the case where the variance is small.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
be such that
\mathbb{E}(H)\leq 1
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(H=1)\geq\mathbb{P}(H\geq 2)\,.

Proof.

Observe that the desired inequality can be equivalently written as

\mathbb{P}(H=0)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(H=1)\geq 1\,.

Now, we may write

\mathbb{P}(H=0)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(H=1)\,=\,\frac{\binom{n-i}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}+\frac{2i\binom{n-i}{k-1}}{\binom{n}{k}}\,=\,\frac{\binom{n-i}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2ki}{n-i-k+1}\right)\,.

We distinguish two cases. Suppose first that
i>1
.
Let
i,k
be fixed, and consider the sequence

q(n):=\frac{\binom{n-i}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2ki}{n-i-k+1}\right)\,,\,\text{ for }\,n\geq ik\,.

It is straightforward to verify that the sequence
\{q(n)\}_{n\geq ik}

is increasing when
n\leq 2ik-i-k
,
and decreasing when
n\geq 2ik-i-k
.
Since in the limit it holds
\lim_{n\to\infty}q(n)=1
, it is enough to show that
q(ik)\geq 1
or, equivalently, that the result holds true when
H\sim\text{Hyp}(ik,i,k)
; i.e., when
\mathbb{E}(H)=1
. In this case, it holds
n=ik
and we compute

\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=1)}{\mathbb{P}(H=0)}=\frac{ik}{ik-i-k+1}=\frac{ik}{(i-1)(k-1)}>1\,.

Now, Lemma
2.1
implies that

\mathbb{P}(H=0)\geq\mathbb{P}(H\geq 2)\,,

and the result follows.
Now suppose that
i=1
. Then

\mathbb{P}(H=0)+2\cdot\mathbb{P}(H=1)=\frac{n-k}{n}+2\cdot\frac{k}{n}\geq 1
,
as desired.
∎

The following result allows to express the tail of a hypergeometric random variable in terms of an “inverse factorial moment”.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
,
and fix an integer
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}\}
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(H\geq m)=\frac{\binom{k}{m}\cdot\binom{i}{m}}{\binom{n}{m}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\binom{Z_{m}+m}{m}}\right)\,,

(3)

where
Z_{m}\sim\text{Hyp}(n-m,i-m,k-m)
.

Proof.

Observe that

Z_{m}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}-m\}
and that for
j\in\{m,m+1,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}\}
we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(H=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}{\binom{n}{k}}\,=\,\frac{\frac{i}{j}\cdot\frac{i-1}{j-1}\cdots\frac{i-m+1}{j-m+1}\cdot\binom{i-m}{j-m}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}{\frac{n}{k}\cdot\frac{n-1}{k-1}\cdots\frac{n-m+1}{k-m+1}\cdot\binom{n-m}{k-m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k}{m}}{\binom{n}{m}}\cdot\frac{\binom{i}{m}}{\binom{j}{m}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{m}=j-m)\,.

Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(H\geq m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=m}^{\min\{i,k\}}\mathbb{P}(H=j)\,=\,\sum_{j=m}^{\min\{i,k\}}\frac{\binom{k}{m}}{\binom{n}{m}}\cdot\frac{\binom{i}{m}}{\binom{j}{m}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{m}=j-m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{k}{m}\cdot\binom{i}{m}}{\binom{n}{m}}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{i,k\}-m}\frac{1}{\binom{j+m}{m}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{m}=j)\,=\,\frac{\binom{k}{m}\cdot\binom{i}{m}}{\binom{n}{m}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\binom{Z_{m}+m}{m}}\right)\,,

as desired.
∎

2.2
Mean absolute deviation

We now proceed with a lower bound on the mean absolute deviation of a hypergeometric random variable. We begin with a result that compares the probabilities of a hypergeometric and a binomial random variable taking the value that is right above their mean. Here and later, given a real number
x
, we let
\lceil x\rceil=\min\{s\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,s\geq x\}
.
The proof employs Robbins’ version of Stirling’s formula (see
[
17
]
), which states that for every positive integer
n
it holds

\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}}<n!<\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}e^{\frac{1}{12n}}\,.

(4)

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
i,k\in[n]
.
Let also
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
,
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
, and
assume further that the following hold true:

1.

\frac{ik}{n}\in(m-1,m]
, for some integer

m\in\{2,3,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}-2\}
.

2.

\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
.

Then

\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=m)}{\mathbb{P}(X=m)}\,\geq\,\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{i(n-i)(n-k)}{(i-m)(n-i-k+m)n}}\,.

Proof.

To simplify notation, set
s:=n-i-k+m
.
Note that the assumption
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
implies that
i,k<n-1
. Also,
note that
s
is an integer such that

s\geq n-i-k+\frac{ik}{n}=\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}
,
and
s=\left\lceil\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}\right\rceil
.
Hence, the assumption
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
implies that
s\geq 2
.
Now we may write

\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=m)}{\mathbb{P}(X=m)}=\frac{\binom{i}{m}\binom{n-i}{k-m}n^{k}}{\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{m}i^{m}(n-i)^{k-m}}=\frac{i!\cdot(n-i)!\cdot(n-k)!\cdot n^{k}}{(i-m)!\cdot s!\cdot n!\cdot i^{m}\cdot(n-i)^{k-m}}\,.

Using (
4
), we estimate

\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=m)}{\mathbb{P}(X=m)}\geq e^{C_{1}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{i(n-i)(n-k)}{(i-m)sn}}\cdot C_{2}\,,

where

C_{1}=\frac{1}{12i+1}+\frac{1}{12(n-i)+1}+\frac{1}{12(n-k)+1}-\frac{1}{12(i-m)}-\frac{1}{12s}-\frac{1}{12n}\,,

and

C_{2}=\frac{i^{i}\cdot(n-i)^{n-i}\cdot(n-k)^{n-k}\cdot n^{k}}{(i-m)^{i-m}\cdot s^{s}\cdot n^{n}\cdot i^{m}\cdot(n-i)^{k-m}}\\
=\left(\frac{i(n-k)}{(i-m)n}\right)^{i-m}\cdot\left(\frac{(n-k)(n-i)}{sn}\right)^{s}\,.

We proceed with estimating
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
from below.
Since
\frac{1}{12i+1}\geq\frac{1}{12n}
and
s\geq 2
, it follows that

C_{1}\geq-\frac{1}{12(i-m)}-\frac{1}{12s}\geq-\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{24}=-\frac{1}{8}
; hence
e^{C_{1}}\geq e^{-1/8}
.

We now show that
C_{2}\geq 1/2
.
Note that if
\frac{ik}{n}
is an integer then
\frac{ik}{n}=m
and it holds
C_{2}=1
; hence we may assume that
m-1<\frac{ik}{n}<m
. Denote
\delta=m-\frac{ik}{n}
. Then
\delta\in(0,1)
, and we may write

C_{2}\,=\,\left(1+\frac{\delta}{\frac{i(n-k)}{n}-\delta}\right)^{\frac{i(n-k)}{n}-\delta}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}+\delta}\right)^{\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}+\delta}\,.

To simplify notation, set
A:=\frac{i(n-k)}{n}
,
B=\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}
and note that
B=n-k-A
. Also, note that the assumption
\frac{ik}{n}\leq i-2
implies that
A\geq 2
.
Now consider the function

f(x)=\left(1+\frac{x}{A-x}\right)^{A-x}\cdot\left(1-\frac{x}{B+x}\right)^{B+x}\,,\,\text{ for }\,x\in[0,1]\,.

Note that it holds
f(\delta)=C_{2}
.
Moreover, straightforward calculations show that

f^{\prime}(x)=\left(1+\frac{x}{A-x}\right)^{A-x}\cdot\left(1-\frac{x}{B+x}\right)^{B+x}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{B(A-x)}{A(B+x)}\right)\,.

Since
A\geq 2
, it follows that
A-x>0
for
x\in[0,1]
. Moreover, it holds
\frac{B(A-x)}{A(B+x)}\leq 1
, and thus
f^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
.
This implies that
f
is decreasing in
[0,1]
; hence we have that

C_{2}\,=\,f(\delta)\,\geq\,f(1)\,=\,\left(1+\frac{1}{A-1}\right)^{A-1}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n-k-A+1}\right)^{n-k-A+1}\,.

Since
A\geq 2
,
n-k-A+1=\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}+1\geq 2
, and the sequences
\{\left(1+\frac{1}{r}\right)^{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
and
\{\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)^{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
are increasing,
we conclude that
f(1)\geq 2\cdot\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{2}
. The result follows.
∎

Our lower bound on the mean absolute deviation of the hypergeometric distribution combines the previous lemma with the corresponding bound for the binomial distribution.

Theorem 2.5

(Berend, Kontorovich
[
2
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a positive integer, and let
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,p)
. If
p\in[\frac{1}{k},1-\frac{1}{k}]
, then

\mathbb{E}(|X-kp|)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\,.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and
i,k\in[n]
.
Suppose that
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
is a hypergeometric random variable which satisfies the following two conditions:

1.

\mathbb{E}(H)\in(1,\min\{i,k\}-2]
.

2.

\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}>1
.

Then

\mathbb{E}(|H-ik/n|)\geq\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}\cdot\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}\,.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{E}(H)\in(1,\min\{i,k\}-2]
, it holds

\frac{ik}{n}\in(m-1,m]
, for some integer

m\in\{2,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}-2\}
.
Let
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
be a binomial random variable having the same mean as
H
, and note that
\frac{1}{k}\leq\frac{i}{n}\leq 1-\frac{1}{k}
.
We will need the following expressions for the mean absolute deviations of
H
and
X
.
It is known (see
[
16
, Formula (3.3), p.554]
) that the mean absolute deviation of
H
can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}(|H-ik/n|)=\frac{2m}{n}\cdot(n-i-k+m)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H=m)\,.

Moreover, it is known (see
[
5
, Formula (1.1)]
) that the mean absolute deviation of
X
can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}(|X-ik/n|)=2m\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=m)\,.

Hence, Lemma
2.4
implies that

\frac{\mathbb{E}(|H-ik/n|)}{\mathbb{E}(|X-ik/n|)}\geq\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2}\cdot\frac{n-i-k+m}{n-i}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{i(n-i)(n-k)}{(i-m)(n-i-k+m)n}}\,,

and therefore, using Theorem
2.5
, we estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|H-ik/n|)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2}\cdot\frac{n-i-k+m}{n-i}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{i(n-i)(n-k)}{(i-m)(n-i-k+m)n}}\cdot\mathbb{E}(|X-ik/n|)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n-i-k+m}}{n-i}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{i(n-i)(n-k)}{(i-m)n}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{ki(n-i)}{n^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-1/8}}{2\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{(n-k)i(n-1)}{n^{2}(i-m)}}\,,

where in the last inequality we have used the fact that
n-i-k+m\geq n-i-k+\frac{ik}{n}=\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}
.
The result follows upon observing that the assumption
\frac{ik}{n}\leq m
implies that
\frac{(n-k)i}{n(i-m)}\geq 1
.
∎

2.3
Tail conditional expectation

In this section we deduce an upper bound on the tail conditional expectation of the hypergeometric distribution.
In order to state our results, we will need some basic facts from the theory of stochastic orders, which we briefly discuss here and refer the reader to

[
18
]
, or
[
11
]
, for further details and references.
A random variable
X
is said to be smaller than the random variable
Y
in the
usual stochastic order
, denoted
X\leq_{st}Y
, if
it holds

\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\geq t)\,,\,\text{ for all }\,t\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Clearly, if
X\leq_{st}Y
then it holds
\mathbb{E}(X)\leq\mathbb{E}(Y)
.
A random variable
X
is said to be smaller than the random variable
Y
in the
likelihood ratio order
, denoted
X\leq_{lr}Y
, if the sequence
a_{m}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=m)}{\mathbb{P}(Y=m)}
is decreasing in
m
over the union of the supports of
X
and
Y
. It is known (see
[
18
, Theorem 1.C.1]
, or
[
11
]
) that

X\leq_{lr}Y
implies
X\leq_{st}Y
.

We begin with an upper bound on the tail conditional expectation of random variables whose mean is also a median.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X
be a random variable whose mean
\mu:=\mathbb{E}(X)
is also a median.
Then

\mathbb{E}(X\mid X\geq\mu)\leq\mu+\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\,.

Proof.

Note that the assumption that
\mu
is a median yields

\mathbb{E}(\max\{0,X-\mu\})=\mathbb{P}(X\geq\mu)\cdot\mathbb{E}(X-\mu\mid X\geq\mu)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}(X-\mu\mid X\geq\mu)\,.

Hence it holds

\mathbb{E}(X-\mu\mid X\geq\mu)\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}(\max\{0,X-\mu\})\,.

Now, set
M^{+}=\max\{0,X-\mu\}
and
M^{-}=\max\{0,\mu-X\}
and note that

M^{+}-M^{-}=X-\mu\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad M^{+}+M^{-}=|X-\mu|\,.

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{E}(M^{+})=\mathbb{E}(M^{-})
as well as
2\cdot\mathbb{E}(M^{+})=\mathbb{E}(|X-\mu|)
, and we conclude that

\mathbb{E}(X-\mu\mid X\geq\mu)\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}(M^{+})=\mathbb{E}(|X-\mu|)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}((X-\mu)^{2})}=\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\,,

as desired.
∎

Our next result asserts that the tail conditional expectation of the hypergeometric distribution is dominated by the tail conditional expectation of the binomial distribution; thus an upper bound on the latter is also an upper bound on the former.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
and
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
.
Let
H^{\ast}
denote the random variable
H
conditional on the event
\{H\geq ik/n\}
. Similarly, let

X^{\ast}
denote the random variable
X
conditional on the event
\{X\geq ik/n\}
.
Then

H^{\ast}\,\leq_{st}\,X^{\ast}\,.

In particular, it holds

\mathbb{E}(H\mid H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\leq\mathbb{E}(X\mid X\geq\mathbb{E}(X))\,.

Proof.

Observe that it is enough to show that
H^{\ast}\leq_{lr}X^{\ast}
, and that it holds

H^{\ast}\in\left\{\left\lceil\frac{ik}{n}\right\rceil,\ldots,\min\{i,k\}\right\}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad X^{\ast}\in\left\{\left\lceil\frac{ik}{n}\right\rceil,\ldots,k\right\}\,.

Let
a_{m}=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(H^{\ast}=m\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\ast}=m\right)}
, for
m\in\{\lceil\frac{ik}{n}\rceil,\ldots,k\}
, and note that
a_{m}=0
when
m>\min\{i,k\}
. Hence, we have to show that the sequence
\{a_{m}\}_{m=\lceil ik/n\rceil}^{\min\{i,k\}}
is decreasing.
Since

a_{m}=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(H^{\ast}=m\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\ast}=m\right)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=m\mid H\geq ik/n)}{\mathbb{P}(X=m\mid X\geq ik/n)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(H=m)\cdot\mathbb{P}(X\geq ik/n)}{\mathbb{P}(X=m)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)}\,,

it is straightforward to verify that it holds

\frac{a_{m}}{a_{m+1}}=\frac{i(n-i-k+m+1)}{(n-i)(i-m)}\,.

The result follows upon observing that
\frac{a_{m}}{a_{m+1}}\geq 1
when
m\geq\frac{i(k-1)}{n}
.
∎

Moreover, we will need the following comparison between the tail conditional expectations of binomial random variables.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, and
p,q\in(0,1)
be such that
p\leq q
. Let
X_{p}\sim\text{Bin}(k,p)
,
X_{q}\sim\text{Bin}(k,q)

and fix a positive integer
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
. Then

\mathbb{E}(X_{p}\mid X_{p}\geq m)\,\leq\,\mathbb{E}(X_{q}\mid X_{q}\geq m)\,.

Proof.

This is a well-known result (see
[
3
]
,
[
11
]
, or
[
13
, Lemma 2.4]
).
∎

We can now state our upper bound on the tail conditional expectation of the hypergeometric distribution. Recall that
\lceil x\rceil=\min\{s\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,s\geq x\}
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
. Then

\mathbb{E}(H\mid H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\,\,\leq\,\,\lceil\mathbb{E}(H)\rceil+\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-k}+1}\,.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.8
we know that

\mathbb{E}(H\mid H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\leq\mathbb{E}(X\mid X\geq\mathbb{E}(X))\,,

(5)

where
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
. Let
m:=\lceil ik/n\rceil
, and consider a random variable

Y\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{m}{k})
. Since
\frac{i}{n}\leq\frac{m}{k}
, it follows from Lemma
2.9
that

\mathbb{E}(X\mid X\geq\mathbb{E}(X))\,\leq\,\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Y\geq\mathbb{E}(X))\,=\,\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Y\geq\mathbb{E}(Y))\,,

(6)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}(Y)=m=\lceil\mathbb{E}(X)\rceil
.
Since
Y
is a binomial random variable whose mean is an integer, it is well-known (see
[
10
]
or
[
19
, Theorem 2.2]
)
that
m
is also a median of
Y
. Hence Lemma
2.7
implies that

\mathbb{E}(Y\mid Y\geq\mathbb{E}(Y))\leq m+\sqrt{\text{Var}(Y)}\,.

(7)

Summarizing the above, it follows from (
5
), (
6
) and (
7
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(H\mid H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m+\sqrt{k\cdot\frac{m}{k}\cdot\frac{k-m}{k}}\,=\,m+\sqrt{m\cdot\frac{k-m}{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m+\sqrt{\left(\frac{ik}{n}+1\right)\cdot\frac{k-ik/n}{k}}\,=\,m+\sqrt{\left(\frac{ik}{n}+1\right)\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m+\sqrt{k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}+1}\,=\,m+\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-k}+1}\,,

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Throughout this section, we assume that

n,k
are positive integers such that
n\geq 8k
, and
i\in[n]
, all the parameters being fixed.
Moreover, we fix a hypergeometric random variable
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
and a binomial random variable
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,\frac{i}{n})
having the same mean as
H
. Our proof employs the following result of Ehm (see
[
7
, Theorem
2
]
).
Recall that the
total variation distance
between
H
and
X
is defined as

d_{TV}(H\,,\,X)=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}}\,|\mathbb{P}(H\in A)-\mathbb{P}(X\in A)|\,.

Theorem 3.1

(Ehm
[
7
]
)
.

Suppose that the parameters
n,i,k
are such that
k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\geq 1
. Then

d_{TV}(H\,,\,X)\leq\frac{k-1}{n-1}\,.

We will also need the following constant lower bound on the probability that a binomial random variable exceeds its mean.

Theorem 3.2

(Greenberg, Mohri
[
8
]
)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
p\in(\frac{1}{k},1)
. If
Y\sim\text{Bin}(k,p)
, then

\mathbb{P}(Y\geq\mathbb{E}(Y))\,\geq\,\frac{1}{4}\,.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we will distinguish four cases.
We begin with the case where
k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\geq 1
, which follows readily from the above mentioned theorems.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
n,i,k
are such that
k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\geq 1
and
n\geq 8k
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)\,\geq\,k/n\,.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.1
we know that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)\geq\mathbb{P}(X\geq ik/n)-\frac{k-1}{n-1}

and is therefore enough to show that

\mathbb{P}(X\geq ik/n)\geq\frac{k}{n}+\frac{k-1}{n-1}
.
Since
k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}\geq 1
, it also holds

k\cdot\frac{i}{n}>1
, and
Theorem
3.2
implies that

\mathbb{P}(X\geq ik/n)\geq\frac{1}{4}
.
Hence it is enough to show that

\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{k}{n}+\frac{k-1}{n-1}
,
which is clearly correct when
n\geq 8k
.
∎

It follows from Lemma
3.3
that we may suppose for the rest of this section that the parameters
n,i,k
satisfy

k\cdot\frac{i}{n}\cdot\frac{n-i}{n}<1\quad\text{ and }\quad n\geq 8k\,.

(8)

This leaves three cases to consider, depending on whether
i
is smaller than, equal to, or larger than
n/2
.
Those three cases above are considered in the following three lemmata, whose proofs complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
We begin with the first case.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that (
8
) holds true, and that
i<n/2
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)\,\geq\,\frac{k}{n}\,.

Proof.

Since
i<n/2
, it follows from (
8
) that
\frac{ik}{n}<\frac{n}{n-i}<2
, and thus
\frac{ik}{n}\in(0,2)
.
We consider two cases.

Suppose first that
\frac{ik}{n}\in(0,1]
.
We may assume that
i\geq 2
; otherwise the result is clearly correct.
Since
\frac{ik}{n}\leq 1
it holds
\mathbb{P}(H\geq ik/n)=\mathbb{P}(H\geq 1)

and therefore it is enough to show that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq 1)\geq\frac{k}{n}\,.

Now, Lemma
2.3
implies that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq 1)=\frac{k}{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{i}{Z_{1}+1}\right)\,,

where
Z_{1}\sim\text{Hyp}(n-1,i-1,k-1)
. The result follows upon observing that
i\geq Z_{1}+1
.

Suppose now that
\frac{ik}{n}\in(1,2)
and
i<\frac{n}{2}
.
Observe that the assumption
n\geq 8k
implies that
i>\frac{n}{k}\geq 8
, and so we may assume that
i\geq 9
.
We may also assume that
k\geq 2
; otherwise the result is clearly true.
Since
1<\frac{ik}{n}<2
it follows that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\frac{ik}{n})=\mathbb{P}(H\geq 2)
.
Moreover, it follows from Lemma
2.3
that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq 2)=\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\cdot i(i-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(Z_{2}+2)(Z_{2}+1)}\right)\,,

where
Z_{2}\sim\text{Hyp}(n-2,i-2,k-2)
.
Jensen’s inequality now implies that

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{(Z_{2}+2)(Z_{2}+1)}\right)\geq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}((Z_{2}+2)(Z_{2}+1))}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(Z_{2}^{2}+3Z_{2}+2)}\,.

Since

\mathbb{E}(Z_{2}^{2}+3Z_{2}+2)=A\cdot\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{(n-2)(n-3)}+A^{2}+3A+2\,,\,\text{ where }\,A:=\frac{(i-2)(k-2)}{n-2}\,.

Hence, in order to prove the desired result, it is enough to prove that

\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\cdot i(i-1)\cdot\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(Z_{2}^{2}+3Z_{2}+2)}\geq\frac{k}{n}\,,

which is equivalent to

A\cdot\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{(n-2)(n-3)}+A^{2}+3A+2\leq i\cdot\frac{(i-1)(k-1)}{n-1}\,.

(9)

Since
\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{(n-2)(n-3)}<1
,
A\leq B:=\frac{(i-1)(k-1)}{n-1}
and
i\geq 9
, we conclude that (

B^{2}+4B+2\leq 9B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B^{2}-5B+2\leq 0\,.

Solving for

, we find that (

) holds true for
B\in\left[\frac{1}{2}(5-\sqrt{17})\,,\,\frac{1}{2}(5+\sqrt{17})\right]

B>\frac{1}{2}(5-\sqrt{17})\approx 0.43844
.
To this end, observe that the assumptions
i\geq 9
and

imply that

\frac{B}{\frac{ik}{n}}=\frac{\frac{(i-1)(k-1)}{n-1}}{\frac{ik}{n}}=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{i}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)>\frac{8}{9}\cdot\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)>\frac{8}{9}\cdot\frac{1}{2}>0.44\,.

Since
\frac{ik}{n}>1

, and the result follows.

\{H\geq k/2\}

\text{Var}(H)\geq 1
, this implies that

\frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}}{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{n-1}{n-k}\cdot\text{Var}(H)}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}}{\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{n-1}{n-k}}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}}\geq\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{n}{n-k}}}=\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}\,.

Since
n\geq 4
, it follows
\frac{n-1}{n}\geq\frac{3}{4}
and we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))\geq\frac{e^{-1/8}}{4\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}\,.

Since the right hand side is decreasing in
\alpha
, the desired result follows upon substituting
\alpha=1/2
.

Now suppose that
k>n/2
. Let
H_{1}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,n-i,n-k)
. Then
n-k<n/2
as well as
1<\frac{(n-i)(n-k)}{n}\leq\min\{n-i,n-k\}-2
, and the result follows upon observing that

\mathbb{P}(H\geq\mathbb{E}(H))=\mathbb{P}(H_{1}\geq\mathbb{E}(H_{1}))
.
∎
