Finiteness properties of asymptotically rigid handlebody groups
Sergio Domingo-Zubiaga

Abstract
. We introduce asymptotically rigid mapping class groups of handlebodies and determine their finiteness properties, which vary depending on the space of ends of the underlying handlebody. As it turns out, in some cases, the homology of these groups coincides with the stable homology of handlebody groups, as studied by Hatcher and Wahl.



Asymptotically rigid mapping class groups
of manifolds have garnered significant attention in recent years (see
[
9
,
8
,
10
,
11
]
) for a variety of reasons. First, they sometimes appear as extensions of
Higman-Thompson groups
by direct limits of mapping class groups of compact manifolds. In certain cases, this implies that their homology coincides with the
stable homology
of the underlying manifold (see
[
1
, Section 10]
). Additionally, in the context of surfaces, these groups form countable subgroups of the so-called
big mapping class groups
, an active area of research at the intersection of geometric group theory, low-dimensional topology, topological groups, and related fields.

A major motivation for studying asymptotically rigid mapping class groups stems from the study of their finiteness properties (see
[
1
,
2
,
11
,
8
,
10
,
3
]
). Recall that a group is said to be of
type
F_{n}
if it admits a classifying space with finite
n
-skeleton, and is of
type
F_{\infty}
if it is
F_{n}
for every
n
. A group
G
is said to be of
type
FP_{n}
if
\mathbb{Z}
, viewed as a
G
-module, has a projective resolution whose first
n
terms are finitely generated.

Notably, the finiteness properties of asymptotically rigid mapping class groups are often determined by the space of ends of the underlying manifold. Specifically, as shown in
[
1
]
, under certain general assumptions on the manifolds involved, if the end space of the underlying manifold is a Cantor set, then the associated asymptotically rigid mapping class group is of type
F_{\infty}
. In stark contrast, when the end space consists of
r
isolated points, the group is of type
F_{r-1}
but not
FP_{r}
, as shown in
[
2
]
.

The objective of this paper is to extend the results of
[
1
,
2
]
to the setting of handlebodies. To state our results, we provide a rough description of the relevant objects, postponing technical details to Section
1
. Let
O
be any handlebody and
Y
a genus-one handlebody, referred to as a
piece
, with
d+1\geq 2
spots. The
tree handlebody

\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is constructed by first performing boundary-connect sums of
r
copies of
Y
to
O
along the spots of
O
, and then inductively boundary-connect summing
d
copies of
Y
to the manifold obtained in the previous step. Notably, the end space of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is a Cantor set if
d\geq 2
, and consists of
r
isolated points if
d=1
. The
asymptotically rigid handlebody group

\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is the group of homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, up to isotopy, that
eventually
map pieces to pieces in a
trivial
manner, see Section
1
for precise definitions.

Our first theorem establishes an analog of
[
1
]
in this setting, specifically for the case where the end space is a Cantor set:

Theorem I
.

Given any handlebody
O
, a genus-one handlebody
Y
,
d\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
, the group
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is of type
F_{\infty}
.

Our second result addresses the case where the end space consists of
r
isolated points, serving as a handlebody analog of the main result of
[
2
]
:

Theorem II
.

Given any handlebody
O
, a genus-one handlebody
Y
, and
r\geq 1
, the group
\mathcal{H}_{1,r}(O,Y)
is of type
F_{r-1}
but not
FP_{r}
.

Following the approach of
[
10
]
, we deduce that in certain cases, the homology of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
coincides with the stable homology of the handlebody groups, as studied by Hatcher and Wahl
[
14
]
.

Corollary III
.

Let
O
be any handlebody, and let
Y
be a genus-one handlebody. Let
M_{g}
be any handlebody of genus
g\geq 2i+4
. Then

H_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}(O,Y))\cong H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(M_{g})).

1
contains the definitions of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
and

\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, along with the base results on handlebodies and handlebody groups we will need. Section
2

4

. Particularly, Section

4

I
, and Section
5

. Section
6

.

1.
Tree handlebodies and asymptotically rigid handlebody groups

This section is dedicated to introducing
tree handlebodies
and their
asymptotically rigid handlebody groups
, following ideas from
[
1
, Section 3]
and
[
2
, Section 2]
.

Let
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
be 3-manifolds with connected boundary. Choose any pair of closed discs
D_{i}\subset\partial N_{i}
for
i=1,2
, an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\mu_{1}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow D_{1}
, and an orientation-reversing homeomorphism
\mu_{2}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow D_{2}
. The
boundary connected sum

N_{1}\natural N_{2}
is the result of identifying
D_{1}
with
D_{2}
via the homeomorphism
\mu_{1}\circ\mu_{2}^{-1}
. Up to homeomorphism, the result of such identification does not depend on the election of the
D_{i}
’s or the
\mu_{i}
’s.

1.1.
Handlebodies and handlebody groups

This subsection gathers the theory of handlebodies that will be used throughout the paper. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
.

A
solid torus

T
is
the product
T:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{D}^{2}
of a circle and a closed disc. A
handlebody
is the boundary connected sum
M=T_{1}\natural...\natural T_{g}\natural\mathbb{D}^{3}
with
g\in\mathbb{N}
, and each
T_{i}
a solid torus. The number
g
, called the
genus
of
M
, is a homeomorphism invariant of the handlebody. We always require the genus of a handlebody to be greater than zero.

A
spotted handlebody

(M,A)
is a handlebody
M
together with a (possibly empty) subset
A\subset\partial M
of
spots
, i.e. pairwise disjoint closed discs. Given a spotted handlebody
(M,A)
, a
spotted subhandlebody

(N,B)
, is a spotted handlebody
N\subset M
such that:

•

\overline{\partial N\cap\textnormal{int}(M)}
is a collection of closed discs,

•

for every spot
D\subset A
either
D\cap\partial N=\emptyset
or
D\subset\partial N
,

•

B=(\overline{\partial N\cap\textnormal{int}(M)})\cup(\partial N\cap A)
, i.e. the subhandlebody inherits spots from both the new discs formed in
\overline{\partial N\cap\textnormal{int}(M)}
and discs in
A
which are in
\partial N
.

Observe that the boundary connected sum of two spotted handlebodies is a spotted handlebody. We require homeomorphisms between spotted handlebodies to send spots to spots.

The
handlebody group

\mathcal{H}(M,A)
of the spotted handlebody
(M,A)
is the group of homeomorphisms of
M
fixing
A
pointwise, up to isotopies fixing
A
pointwise. We will often omit the set
A
from notation for the sake of simplicity.

Given a spotted handlebody
M
with set of spots
A
, fix a set of parametrizations of its spots, i.e. a set of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms
\{\mu_{i}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow D_{i}\}_{D_{i}\subset A}
. The
spot-permuting handlebody group

\mathcal{H}_{o}(M,A)
of the spotted handlebody
(M,A)
is the group of isotopy classes (relative to
A
) of homeomorphisms of
M
that respect these parametrizations, meaning that for any
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{o}(M,A)
, there is a permutation
\sigma
such that
\phi\circ\mu_{i}=\mu_{\sigma(i)}

\forall i
. Note that
\mathcal{H}(M,A)
is a finite index subgroup of
\mathcal{H}_{o}(M,A)
.

Handlebody groups can be studied as subgroups of
mapping class groups
. Given a compact surface
S
we define the mapping class group
\textnormal{Map}(S)
as the group of homeomorphisms of the surface fixing
\partial S
pointwise, up to isotopies fixing
\partial S
pointwise. Write
S_{(M,A)}:=\overline{\partial M\setminus A}
, which is a connected surface with boundary. There is a well-defined, injective map

\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{H}(M,A)\rightarrow\textnormal{Map}(S_{(M,A)})

given by restriction to the boundary (see
[
15
, Section 3]
).

Given
p\in\partial M\setminus A
,
\mathcal{H}(M,A,p)
is the group of homeomorphisms of
M
fixing
A
and the point
p
pointwise, up to isotopies fixing
A
and
p
pointwise. The following exact sequences are the restriction to handlebody groups of the
capping homomorphism
and the
Birman exact sequence
(see
[
7
, Proposition 3.19, Theorem 4.6]
,
[
15
, Section 3]
).

Lemma 1.1
.

Given a spotted handlebody
(M,A)
, choose a spot
D\subset A
and a point
p\in\textnormal{int}{(D)}
, write
A^{\prime}=A\setminus{D}
. There are exact sequences

1\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\textnormal{twist}}\mathcal{H}(M,A)\xrightarrow{\textnormal{cap}}\mathcal{H}(M,A^{\prime},p)\rightarrow 1,

1\rightarrow\pi_{1}(S_{(M,A^{\prime})},p)\xrightarrow{\textnormal{push}}\mathcal{H}(M,A^{\prime},p)\xrightarrow{\textnormal{forget}}\mathcal{H}(M,A^{\prime})\rightarrow 1.

1.2.
Tree handlebodies

Following
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
, in this subsection we introduce
tree handlebodies
which, loosely speaking, are the result of gluing handlebodies via boundary connected sum in a tree-like manner.

For
d\geq 1
let
Y^{d}
be a genus-one spotted handlebody with
d+1
spots. We enumerate the spots in
Y^{d}
as
A_{0},A_{1},...,A_{d}
and fix an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\mu_{0}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow A_{0}
, and orientation-reversing homeomorphisms
\mu_{i}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow A_{i}
for
i\geq 1
.
Let
O
be a spotted handlebody with spots
B:=\{B_{1},...B_{r}\}
. Fix orientation-reversing homeomorphisms
\nu_{i}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow B_{i}
for each spot. We set
O_{0}=O
, and define a sequence
\{O_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of spotted handlebodies in the following way:

(1)

O_{1}
is the spotted handlebody that results from gluing
r
copies of
Y^{d}
to
O_{0}
, via boundary connected sum of each
Y^{d}
with
O_{0}
using the maps
\mu_{0}\circ\nu_{i}^{-1}
for the gluing.

(2)

For
k\geq 2
each
O_{k}
is the spotted handlebody that results from gluing a copy of
Y^{d}
to each spot in
O_{k-1}
, via boundary connected sum of each
Y^{d}
with
O_{k-1}
using the maps
\mu_{0}\circ\mu_{i}^{-1}
for the gluing.

Figure 1.
The handlebody
O_{4}
, for
O
a genus 3 handlebody,
d=2
, and
r=3
.

With respect to the above terminology we define the
tree handlebody

\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}O_{k}
. Each of the connected components of
\overline{O_{k}\setminus O_{k-1}}
for
k\geq 1
is called a
piece
. By construction, each piece is homeomorphic to
Y^{d}
. A spotted subhandlebody
M\subset\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is a
suited handlebody
if it is connected and is the boundary connected sum of
O_{0}
and finitely many pieces.

Figure 2.
In blue, a suited handlebody of
\mathcal{T}_{2,4}(O,Y)
, with
O
a solid torus. In red, a spotted subhandlebody which is not suited.

1.3.
Two types of tree handlebodies

Tree handlebodies
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
with
d\geq 2
will be referred as
Cantor handlebodies
, as their
space of ends
of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is a Cantor set. We would like to remark that the boundary of a Cantor handlebody (as a manifold with boundary) is a Cantor surface in the terminology of
[
1
, Definition 3.1]
.

On the other hand, tree handlebodies
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
with
d=1
will be referred as
star handlebodies
. The space of ends of the star handlebody is a finite set of
r
points.

Figure 3.
A Cantor handlebody (left) and a star handlebody (right).

1.4.
Asymptotically rigid handlebody groups

We now adapt the definition of rigid structure of
[
1
, Definition 3.8]
to the setting of handlebodies. For each piece
Z
of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, choose a homeomorphism
i_{Z}:Z\rightarrow Y^{d}
that agrees with the
\mu_{i}
’s used for the boundary connected sums.

Definition 1.2
.

(Preferred rigid structure). The set
\{i_{Z}\>:\>Z\text{ is a piece}\}
is called the
preferred rigid structure
on
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
.

Definition 1.3
.

(Asymptotically rigid homeomorphism).
Let
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
be a tree handlebody equipped with its preferred rigid structure. A homeomorphism
f:\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is
asymptotically rigid
if there exists a suited handlebody
M\subset\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
such that

(1)

f(M)
is suited,

(2)

f
is
rigid outside of
M
, that is, for every piece
Z
outside of
M
,
f(Z)
is also a piece and
i_{f(Z)}\circ f_{|Z}\circ i^{-1}_{Z}=\textnormal{id}_{Y^{d}}
.

The suited handlebody
M
is called a
defining handlebody
of
f
. Observe that any suited handlebody containing
M
will be as well a defining handlebody for
f
. The composition
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
of two asymptotically rigid self-homeomorphisms
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is always defined up to enlarging the defining handlebody of
f_{2}
so that it contains the image of the defining handlebody of
f_{1}
. This justifies the following definition:

Definition 1.4
.

(Asymptotically rigid handlebody group)
The
asymptotically rigid handlebody group

\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is the set of isotopy classes of asymptotically rigid self-homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, with the composition as its operation.

To simplify notation, we will often blur the difference between elements of the asymptotically rigid handlebody groups and their representatives.

Remark 1.5
.

An asymptotically rigid homeomorphism is determined by its action on any defining handlebody, hence one may be tempted to consider asymptotically rigid homeomorphisms up to
proper isotopies
, i.e. isotopies supported in suited handlebodies. However, if two asymptotically rigid self-homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}
are isotopic then they are also properly isotopic by a combination of
[
1
, Proposition 3.11]
and
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
, so isotopy and proper isotopy can be used interchangeably. We use isotopy over proper isotopy in Definition
1.4
to stress that
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
is a subgroup of the mapping class group
\textnormal{Map}(\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y))
.

1.5.
Relation with mapping class groups

Asymptotically rigid handlebody groups are the analogs, for handlebodies, of the
asymptotic mapping class groups

\mathcal{B}_{d,r}(O,Y)
in
[
1
]
(when
d\geq 2
), and of the
surface Houghton groups

\mathcal{B}_{n}
in
[
2
]
(when
d=1
). The restriction of elements of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
to the boundary surface of the tree handlebody gives elements of
\mathcal{B}_{d,r}(O,Y)
when
d\geq 2
; and elements of
\mathcal{B}_{r}
when
d=1
and
O
has
g=0
, hence there are maps

\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{d,r}(O,Y),\;d\geq 2,

\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{r},\;d=1,

defined by restriction to the boundary, which are well defined, as two representatives of the same element of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
define isotopic elements of the mapping class group of the surface
\partial\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
. The map
\mathfrak{R}
is a monomorphism because of
[
15
, Lemma 3.1, Section 3]
.

However,
\mathfrak{R}
cannot be used to deduce the finiteness properties of asymptotically rigid handlebody groups, as the group
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
has infinite index as a subgroup of
\mathcal{B}_{d,r}(O,Y)
or
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, which can be deduced from
[
15
, Corollary 5.4]
.

1.6.
Compactly supported handlebody group

The
compactly supported handlebody group

\mathcal{H}_{d,r}^{c}(O,Y)
is the subgroup of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
of elements with compact support, meaning that they are the identity outside of a compact subhandlebody. Recall the handlebodies
O_{k}
in the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
. Using the fact that every inclusion
O_{k}\subset O_{k+1}
induces a homomorphism
j_{O_{k},O_{k+1}}:\mathcal{H}(O_{k})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(O_{k+1})
, we can equivalently define the group as a direct limit:

\mathcal{H}^{c}_{d,r}(O,Y):=\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}\,\mathcal{H}(O_{k}).

1.7.
Relation with Higman-Thompson groups

Analogously to
[
1
, Subsection 4.1]
, there is a sequence relating asymptotically rigid handlebody groups of Cantor handlebodies with
Higman-Thompson groups
(
[
17
]
,
[
6
, Section 4]
), denoted by
V_{d,r}
with
d\geq 2

r\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
be any tree handlebody with
d\geq 2
. The action of an element of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
on the space of ends of the tree induces a homeomorphism of the Cantor set, which as in
[
1
, Proposition 4.3]
gives rise to a short exact sequence:

1\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{d,r}^{c}(O,Y)\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)\xrightarrow{}V_{d,r}\rightarrow 1.

2.
Cube complex

The key ingredient to proving Theorem
I
and Theorem
II
is a classical criterion due to Brown
[
6
]
. The language here used is the one of
discrete Morse theory
as written in
[
5
]
. We give a brief summary, and refer the reader to
[
5
]
,
[
1
, Appendix 1]
for further details.

2.1.
Brown’s criterion

Let
G
be a group acting cellularly and cellwise isometrically on a piecewise euclidean
CW
-complex
\mathcal{K}
(i.e. a
CW
-complex where each closed cell is isometric to a convex polyhedron embedded in Euclidean space), equipped with a
G
-invariant
Morse function
, that is, a
G
-invariant map
h:V(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined on the vertices
V(\mathcal{K})
of
\mathcal{K}
, with a unique maximum per cell, which we denote the
top vertex
. The Morse function can be extended linearly to a map
h:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined on the whole of
\mathcal{K}
. Given
v\in V(\mathcal{K})
, a
descending cell
of
v
is a cell with
v
as top vertex. We refer to
h(v)
as the
height
of
v
. Images of vertices of
\mathcal{K}
under
h
are denoted
critical values
. The map
h
is said to be
discrete
if the set of critical values is discrete. Denote by
\mathcal{K}^{\leq s}
the subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
spanned by vertices of height at most
s
.

Let
c
be a coface of
v
, i.e. a cell with
v\in c
. The set of
directions
based at
v
and pointing into
c
is the quotient of the set of straight line segments with
v
as an endpoint and its other endpoint in
c
under the following equivalence relation: two segments are equivalent if one is an initial segment of the other. The set of directions from
v
into
c
forms an spherical polyhedron
\textnormal{lk}(v,c)
. The
link

\textnormal{lk}(v,\mathcal{K})
is the result of identifying the links
\textnormal{lk}(v,c)
of every coface
c\in\mathcal{K}
of
v
along their intersections. The
descending link

\textnormal{lk}^{\downarrow}(v,\mathcal{K})
of a vertex
v
is the link of
v
in the union of all its descending cells.

We say a group
G
is of type
F_{n}
if it acts properly discontinuously, freely and cocompactly on a CW-complex with vanishing homotopy groups
\pi_{0},...,\pi_{n-1}
. A group is of type
F_{\infty}
if it is of type
F_{n}
for all
n>0
. The group
G
is said to be of type
FP_{n}
if the
G
-module
\mathbb{Z}
admits a projective resolution whose first
n
terms are finitely generated. Being of type
F_{n}
implies being of type
FP_{n}
(see
[
12
, Section 8.2]
).

The version of the criterion we introduce can be deduced from
[
2
, Subsection 3.1]
and its proof. We split it into two results as in this way, Theorem
2.1
will apply to Cantor handlebodies and Theorem
2.2
to star handlebodies.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Brown’s criterion I) Let
G
be a group acting cellwise by isometries on a piecewise euclidean
CW
-complex
\mathcal{K}
. Assume
\mathcal{K}
is equipped with a discrete
G
-invariant Morse function
h:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that:

(1)

\mathcal{K}
is contractible.

(2)

The quotient of
\mathcal{K}^{\leq s}
by
G
is finite for all critical values s.

(3)

Every cell stabilizer is of type
F_{\infty}
.

(4)

There exists
l\geq 1
such that, for a sufficiently large critical value
s
and for every vertex
v\in\mathcal{K}
with
h(v)\geq s
,
\textnormal{lk}^{\downarrow}(v,\mathcal{K})
is
(l-1)
-connected.

Then
G
is of type
F_{l}
.

Particularly, if condition
(4)
is satisfied for any
l
, the group is of type
F_{\infty}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Brown’s criterion II) Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{K}
be a
CW
-complex satisfying every hypothesis in Theorem
2.1
. If, additionally, the following two hypotheses are satisfied:

(5)

There exists a critical value
s
such that for every vertex
v\in\mathcal{K}
with
h(v)\geq s
,
\textnormal{lk}^{\downarrow}(v,\mathcal{K})
is of dimension
l
.

(6)

For each critical value
s
there exists a vertex
v
with
h(v)\geq s
such that
\textnormal{lk}^{\downarrow}(v,\mathcal{K})
is non-contractible.

Then
G
is op type
F_{l}
but not of type
FP_{l+1}
.

In the next subsection, we will introduce a cube complex
\mathfrak{X}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
acts cellwise by isometries. The proofs of Theorems
I
and
II
then boil down to checking a series of premises depending on whether we are in the Cantor handlebody case (with
d\geq 2
), or in the star handlebody case (with
d=1
):

Cantor case (Theorem
I
):
Condition (1) and (2) in Theorem
2.1
are proved in Corollary
2.5

2.2.
Stein-Farley cube complex

The goal of this section is to introduce the complex

Remark 2.3
.

As in
[
1
]
, a key element in the construction of the complex
\mathfrak{X}
is the fact that handlebodies satisfy the inclusion, intersection, and cancellation properties, as shown in Appendix
A
. The places where these properties are used will be highlighted in the text.

Consider all ordered pairs
(M,f)
where
M
is a suited handlebody of
\mathcal{T}
and

. We deem two such pairs
(M_{1},f_{1})
and

to be equivalent, and write

, if and only if there are representing homeomorphisms (abusing notation)

and

such that

maps

onto

and is rigid outside of

. We denote by

the equivalence class of the pair

with respect to this relation, and write

for the set of equivalence classes. Observe that

acts on

by left multiplication, namely

Consider a pair

. Since

is a suited handlebody, it is the union of finitely many pieces and

. We define the

of the pair

as the number of pieces in

We introduce a relation

on the elements of

by declaring

if and only if

and

for suited handlebodies

such that

Define

such that

can be used to construct a cube complex with

as its 0-skeleton and every

as the

associated to the tree handlebody

.

Observe that
\mathcal{H}
acts on

respecting the cubical structure, so the action on

is cellular. In addition, the (discrete) function

can be linearly extended over cubes to a Morse function over

. A direct translation of the reasoning in
[

and
[

results in the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

is contractible, and the action of

on

Corollary 2.5

The complex

satisfy conditions (1) and (2) in Theorem

3.1.
Connectivity tools

Given a topological space

(-
1

if

if

[

-connected, and the link of every

(n-d-2)
-connected.

(A) Barycentric subcomplexes:
Given a simplicial complex

, consider its barycentric subdivision
X^{\prime}
. The complex

is the subcomplex of

spanned by the vertices corresponding to simplices of dimension at least

.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
14
, Lemma 3.8]
If

is wCM of dimension

then

is
(n-m)
-connected.

(B) Quillen’s Fiber Theorem:
The following theorem is stated in
[
1
, Proposition A.17]
, and is a specification in terms of simplicial complexes of a more general result due to Quillen
[
21
, Proposition 7.6]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be simplicial complexes,
p:Y\rightarrow X
a simplicial map and assume that the preimage of each closed simplex is
n
-connected. Then
X
is
n
-connected if and only if
Y
is
n
-connected.

We will also need one more version of Quillen’s Fiber Theorem, which can be deduced from
[
21
, Proposition 1.6]
and
[
13
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
p:Y\rightarrow X
be a simplicial map. Define
p_{m}:Y_{m}\rightarrow X_{m}
to be the maps induced by

. Suppose

is contractible for each vertex

. Then

is a homotopy equivalence.

(C) Retraction into a subcomplex:
The theorem we now introduce is a slight variation of
[
13
, Lemma 2.9]
. Let
Y
be a subcomplex of a simplicial complex
X
, with a function
c:V(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
taking positive values outside of
Y
, and value 0 on
Y
. Define the complexity of a simplex
\sigma\in X
as
c(\sigma):=\underset{v\in\sigma}{\sum}c(v)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under the conditions above, suppose that to each vertex
v\in X\setminus Y
we associate a vertex
\Delta v\in\textnormal{lk}(v,X)
, and to each simplex
\sigma\in X
with
\sigma\notin Y
we associate one of the vertices
v_{\sigma}\in\sigma
so that:

(1)

The join
\sigma*\Delta v_{\sigma}
is a simplex of
X
.

(2)

Let
\Delta\sigma=\textnormal{lk}(v_{\sigma},\sigma)*\Delta v_{\sigma}
and
\Delta^{k}\sigma=\underbrace{\Delta\Delta...\Delta}_{\text{k times}}\sigma
.Then if
\sigma\notin Y
there exists
k>0
such that
c(\Delta^{k}\sigma)<c(\sigma)
.

(3)

If
\tau
is a face of
\sigma
which contains
v_{\sigma}
then
v_{\tau}=v_{\sigma}
.

Then
Y
is a deformation retraction of
X
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

For each
\sigma=\langle v_{\sigma},v_{1},...,v_{k}\rangle\notin Y
, we define a flow on
\sigma*\Delta v_{\sigma}
. In barycentric coordinates, given a point in
\sigma*\Delta v_{\sigma}
, we shift the weight from
v_{\sigma}
to
\Delta v_{\sigma}
while keeping the rest of the weights of the vertices invariant. If
\Delta v_{\sigma}\in\sigma
then the flow sends any point in
\sigma
to the face
\langle v_{1},...,\Delta v_{\sigma},...,v_{k}\rangle
. If
\Delta v_{\sigma}\notin\sigma
, points in
\sigma
travel to
\langle\Delta v_{\sigma},v_{1},...,v_{k}\rangle
by a flow of lines in
\sigma*\Delta v_{\sigma}
(see Figure
4
).

Figure 4.
A representation of the flow lines.

Condition (3) guarantees that the flow is continuous at every point, i.e. that the flows in different simplices agree in their common faces. Condition (2) guarantees that any point in
X
reaches
Y
after flowing through finitely many simplices, as the complexity of the simplices always decreases after a finite number of steps. So, if we make every point travel through the flow as to reach
Y
in time 1, we get a continuous retraction from
X
into
Y
.

∎

(D) Bad simplex argument:
These concepts and results are introduced in
[
13
, Section 2.1]
. Let
X
be a simplicial complex. For each simplex
\sigma
we select a subset of vertices
\bar{\sigma}\subset\sigma
, designated as

, such that:

(1)

For
\sigma\subset\tau
, we have
\bar{\sigma}\subset\bar{\tau}
. (Monoticity)

(2)

For any simplex,
\bar{\bar{\sigma}}=\bar{\sigma}
. (Idempotence)

A simplex
\sigma
is
good
if
\bar{\sigma}
is empty, and it is
bad
if
\bar{\sigma}=\sigma
. Any face of a good simplex is also good because of monoticity, so the set of good simplices form a subcomplex

denoted the

. The
good link

of a simplex
\sigma
consists of proper cofaces
\tau
of
\sigma
such that
\bar{\sigma}=\bar{\tau}
. Because of monoticity, we deduce that

is a subcomplex of the link of
\sigma
. The following theorem is introduced in
[
13
, Section 2.1]
. Here, we phrase it as in
[

, Proposition A.7]
, where a proof of the theorem can be found.

Theorem 3.5
.

(Bad simplex argument) Suppose there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all bad simplices
\sigma
, the good link

is
(m-\dim(\sigma))
-connected. Then, the inclusion
X^{\text{good}}\xhookrightarrow{}X
induces an isomorphism in homotopy groups
\pi_{d}
for
d\leq m
, and a epimorphism for
d=m+1
.

(E) Join complex:
The concept of

is introduced by Hatcher and Wahl in
[
14

.

Definition 3.6
.

A

over a simplicial complex

is a simplicial complex together with a simplicial map

satisfying the following properties:

(1)

is surjective,

(2)

is injective on individual simplices,

(3)

for each

The sets

and

are not necessarily equal. When the inclusions

.

The following theorem is a combination of
[
1

and

Theorem 3.7
.

If

•

-connected.

F(\cdot)&\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{D}^{k+1}\setminus\mathbb{D}^{k+1}_{1/2}}\\
\end{cases}.

This defines a continuous extension of

. So for

, every

-sphere in
\textnormal{DT}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A,I)
is null homotopic. Therefore,
\textnormal{DT}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A,I)
is
\lfloor(g-3)/2\rfloor
-connected.

∎

The tether-curve-disc complex.

In
[

Hatcher and Vogtmann prove connectivity results of different complexes of curves and arcs. Their work serves as a guide for the following subsections. Analogously to
[

, Subsection 5.3]
, we define the

\textnormal{TC}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A,I)

Let
(M_{g},A)
be a spotted handlebody. A

. We say an curve is

Let

•

•

•

k

k+1

{\alpha\cup c}

\partial M_{g}

\partial M_{g}

[

.

The complex
\textnormal{TC}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A,I)

\lfloor(g-3)/2\rfloor
-connected.

3.16
.

3.5
to prove that

∎

, but before, we need some notation. Every curve

•

•

[
13
, Proposition 5.6]
. We offer an outline of the proof.

Theorem 3.17
.

The complex
\textnormal{C}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A)
is
\lfloor(g-3)/2\rfloor
-connected.

Outline of the proof of Theorem
3.17
.

Given
\sigma\in\textnormal{C}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A)
the fiber

is an arc complex, with
n
-simplices corresponding to collections of
n+1
pairwise disjoint isotopy classes of arcs with one endpoint in
I
, and the other in one of the curve-discs in

, attached to the positive side of the curve (see Figure

can then be proven to be contractible building a flow by using Theorem
3.4
and a surgery technique similar to the proof of Lemma

.

Hence, according to Theorem
3.2
, it suffices to check the connectivity of
\textnormal{TC}\mathcal{D}^{+}(M_{g},A,I)
. Here a bad simplex argument is used. We have a map
\textnormal{TC}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A,I)\hookrightarrow\textnormal{TC}\mathcal{D}^{+}(M_{g},A,I)

We define the set of bad vertices of a simplex

as the set of tether-curve-discs whose curve-discs coincide with at least one other vertex in

∎

3.9.
The handle complex.

The rest of this section introduces the last group of simplicial complexes in Diagram
3
, and the corresponding results that will be used to prove the connectivity bounds for the descending links. In this last part of the section, we closely follow the reasoning in
[
1
, Section 9]
.

A
handle
in
M_{g}
is a genus one subhandlebody with exactly one spot. The
handle complex

\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A)
, is the complex whose
k
-simplices correspond to collections of
(k+1)
isotopy classes of handles with a set of pairwise disjoint representatives. Observe that a regular neighborhood of a curve-disc is a handle, hence we have a map

p:\textnormal{C}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A)\rightarrow\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A),

which is surjective, as a preimage can always be found by choosing an embedded disc, and an oriented curve (see Figure
18
).

Figure 18.
A possible choice of preimages for a handle.

Lemma 3.18
.

With respect to the map
p
, the complex
\textnormal{C}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A)
is a complete join over
\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A)
. Hence
\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A)
is
\lfloor(g-3)/2\rfloor
-connected.

Proof of Lemma
3.18
.

The second part of the lemma is immediate once the first is proven, because of Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
3.17
. We now prove the first part. The map
p
is simplicial, as disjointness is preserved by taking regular neighborhoods, and inyective on simplices, as disjoint curve-discs never share an image.

For any given handle, the disc in the preimage is unique up to isotopy, as there is exactly one disc up to isotopy in a genus one one-spotted handlebody (see Figure
18
). On the other hand, the curve is unique up to Dehn twisting around the disc, and choosing an orientation. For any simplex
\sigma=[x_{0},...,x_{p}]\in\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A)
, each
\hat{x_{i}}\in p^{-1}(x_{i})
is a curve-disc entirely contained in the handle
x_{i}
. This implies that each set of preimages
\{\hat{x_{0}},...,\hat{x_{P}}\}
have a set of pairwise disjoint representatives, hence
p^{-1}(\sigma)=p^{-1}(x_{0})*p^{-1}(x_{1})*...*p^{-1}(x_{p})
. According to Definition
3.6
,
\textnormal{C}\mathcal{D}(M_{g},A)
is a complete join over
\textnormal{H}(M_{g},A)
.

∎

A

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
m=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{g-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor\right\}

Outline of the proof of Theorem
3.22

The main idea is to apply Theorem
3.2
in order to deduce a connectivity bound for
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}^{+}(M_{g},A,B)
from Lemma
3.20

3.13.
The handle-tether-disc complex.

With Theorem
3.22
to hand, we can now prove the connectivity of the

\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)
, a complex with the same set of vertices as
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}^{+}(M_{g},A,B)
but where
k
-simplices are collections of
k+1
isotopy classes of handle-tether-discs with a set of pairwise disjoint representatives. As in other subsections,

can be seen as a subcomplex of its extended version, which gives rise to an inclusion

i:\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)\hookrightarrow\textnormal{HTD}_{d}^{+}(M_{g},A,B)

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
m=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{g-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor,|B|-2\right\}
. Then
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)
is
m
-connected.

Proof of Theorem
3.23
.

We will prove the result by induction in the complexity
c:=\min\left\{dg,|B|\right\}
of the triad
(M_{g},A,B)
. We take triads with
c<2d
, i.e. either satisfying
g=1
, or
d\leq|B|\leq 2d
, as base cases. The theorem holds true as
m=-1
in these cases, and the complex is nonempty. We now proceed with the induction step.
Given
(M_{g},A,B)
of complexity
c
, suppose the theorem holds for any triad of smaller complexity than
c
. We apply a bad simplex argument (Theorem
3.5
):

Consider the complex
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}^{+}(M_{g},A,B)
, and take as the set of bad vertices of a simplex
\sigma
those who share their outer disc with some other vertex in the simplex. This set satisfies the conditions of the bad simplex argument, and the good simplex for this criteria is precisely
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)
. So it only remains to check that the good link of a bad

-simplex is
(m-k)
-connected.

The good link of a bad
k
-simplex
\sigma
is isomorphic to the handle-tether-disc complex
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g-k-1},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, where
M_{g-k-1}
is the handlebody obtained by cutting off the handles in the handle-tether-discs of
\sigma
;
A^{\prime}
is the set of discs not in an outer disc of
\sigma
, plus discs corresponding to regular neighborhoods in
\partial M_{g-k-1}
of each of the handle-tether-discs (notice that the neighborhoods can overlap, see Figure

); and

. The triad
(M_{g-k-1},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
has smaller complexity than
(M_{g},A,B)
, hence by the induction hypothesis
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g-k-1},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is

-connected with

m^{\prime}=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{(g-k-1)-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor,|B^{\prime}|-2\right\}.

Following the next set of inequalities:

(1)

\left\lfloor{\frac{(g-k-1)-3}{2}}\right\rfloor\overset{*}{\geq}\left\lfloor{\frac{g-2k-3}{2}}\right\rfloor\ =\left\lfloor{\frac{g-3}{2}}\right\rfloor-k
,

(2)

\left\lfloor{\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor\overset{**}{\geq}\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|-kd+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor-\frac{kd}{2d-1}\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor-k
,

(3)

\text{If }\left\lfloor\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor\geq|B^{\prime}|-2\text{, then }d=1\text{ and }|B^{\prime}|-2\overset{**}{\geq}|B|-kd-2=(|B|-2)-k
,

*
:
k\geq 1
, as a single vertex cannot be bad,

**
:
|B^{\prime}|\geq|B|-kd
, as each outer disc contains
d
discs in
B
, but at least two vertices share outer disc,

we deduce that
m^{\prime}\geq m-k
, hence the connectivity of
\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g-k-1},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is at least

for
m=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{g-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor,|B|-2\right\}
.

According to Theorem
3.5
, the inclusion
i
induces an isomorphism in

for every

. Together with Theorem
3.22
, this finishes the proof.

3.14.
Connectivity of the piece complex

We are ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.9
.

Define a map

p:\textnormal{HTD}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)

given by taking a regular neighborhood in
M_{g}
of each handle-tether-disc. The map is simplicial, as disjointness is preserved. We now examine the possible choices of preimages for a given vertex. Each piece contains a unique handle up to isotopy. Similarly, there exists a single eligible outer disc up to isotopy, and the tether is also uniquely determined up to isotopy. Consequently, each piece has exactly one corresponding preimage, so the map is bijective. It follows that the map
p
is an isomorphism, and because of Theorem
3.23
, the complex
\mathcal{P}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)
is
m
-connected, with
m=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{g-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor,|B|-2\right\}
.

It remains to check that the link of any
k-
simplex is
m-k-1
connected. But the link of any
k-
simplex
\sigma
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{d}(M_{g^{\prime}},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, where
M_{g^{\prime}}
is obtained from
M_{g}
by cutting off a set of disjoint representatives of the pieces in
\sigma
,
A^{\prime}
are the spots of
M_{g^{\prime}}
(the ones from
A
plus the new ones from the surgery), and
B^{\prime}=B\cap A^{\prime}
. The complex
\mathcal{P}_{d}(M_{g^{\prime}},A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is
m^{\prime}-
connected, with
m^{\prime}=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{g^{\prime}-3}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor,|B^{\prime}|-2\right\}
. Checking the following calculations:

(1)

\left\lfloor{\frac{g^{\prime}-3}{2}}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{(g-k-1)-3}{2}}\right\rfloor\ \geq\left\lfloor{\frac{g-2k-2-3}{2}}\right\rfloor\ \geq\left\lfloor{\frac{g-3}{2}}\right\rfloor-k-1
,

(2)

\left\lfloor{\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor\overset{*}{\geq}\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|-kd-d+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor-\frac{kd+d}{2d-1}\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{|B|+1}{2d-1}-2}\right\rfloor-k-1
,

(3)

\text{If }\left\lfloor\frac{|B^{\prime}|+1}{2d-1}-2\right\rfloor\geq|B^{\prime}|-2\text{, then }d=1\text{ and }|B^{\prime}|-2\overset{*}{\geq}|B|-kd-d-2=(|B|-2)-k-1
,

*
:
|B^{\prime}|\geq|B|-(k+1)d
, as each piece contains
d
discs in
B
,

we can see that
m^{\prime}\geq m-k-1
, hence
\mathcal{P}_{d}(M_{g},A,B)
is wCM of dimension
m+1
. For
A=B
, we get the bound in the statement of the theorem.
∎

We are now in position to prove Theorem

. As in the previous section, let

a spotted subhandlebody of genus

If

and

\textnormal{lk}^{\downarrow}(v,\mathfrak{X})

for

for

. We now check that the action satisfies Condition (5) in Theorem

Lemma 5.2
.

The action of
\mathcal{H}_{1,r}(O,Y)
on
\mathcal{T}_{1,r}(O,Y)
satisfies Condition (5) in Theorem

for

For sufficiently large

∎

Before checking Condition (6) in Theorem

Lemma 5.3

For every vertex

The action of

6.
Homology of the asymptotically rigid handlebody group

This section is dedicated to studying the homology of
\mathcal{H}_{d,r}(O,Y)
, particularly in the case where
r=1
and
d=2
. We focus on this case because the related Higman-Thompson group is the classical Thompson group
V=V_{2,1}
, which is known to be simple
[
18
]
and acyclic
[
24
]
, a crucial fact for our argument. This section follows the ideas in the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
, with similar arguments employed in
[
1
,
3
]
.

In Subsection
1.6
, we defined
\mathcal{H}^{c}_{d,r}(O,Y)
as the direct limit

\mathcal{H}^{c}_{d,r}(O,Y):=\underset{\longrightarrow}{\lim}\,\mathcal{H}(O_{k}),

were the
O_{k}
are the manifolds involved in the construction of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
. Since the inclusion property holds for handlebodies (see Appendix
A
), the embedding
O_{k_{1}}\hookrightarrow O_{k_{2}}
induces a monomorphism
j:\mathcal{H}(O_{k_{1}})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}(O_{k_{2}})
, which in turn induces a homomorphism on homology
j_{i*}:H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(O_{k_{1}}))\rightarrow H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(O_{k_{2}}))
for each
i
. A key result by Hatcher and Wahl
[
14
, Theorem 1.8]
establishes the homological stabilization of handlebody groups. We restate this result in a form suited to our context below.

Theorem 6.1
.

Given any handlebody
O
, and a genus-one handlebody
Y
, if the genus of
O_{k}
is greater or equal than
2i+4
, then

H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(O_{k}))\cong H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(M_{g})),

where
M_{g}
is any handlebody of genus
g\geq 2i+4
.

As
\mathcal{H}^{c}_{2,1}(O,Y)
is the direct limit of the groups
\mathcal{H}(O_{k})
, and the maps in homology stabilize, we get:

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
O
be any handlebody, and let
Y
be a genus-one handlebody. Let
M_{g}
be any handlebody of genus
g\geq 2i+4
. Then

H_{i}(\mathcal{H}^{c}_{2,1}(O,Y))\cong H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(M_{g})).

In particular, since each
\mathcal{H}(M_{g})
is of type
F_{\infty}
(by
[
20
, Theorem 1.1 a, Theorem 6.1]
) every homology group
H_{i}(\mathcal{H}(M_{g}))
is finitely generated. Thus we obtain:

Corollary 6.3
.

The group
H_{i}(\mathcal{H}^{c}_{2,1}(O,Y))
is finitely generated for every
i\geq 0
.

The connection between the homological stability results for handlebody groups, and the homology of the asymptotically rigid handlebody becomes apparent in the following result. Its proof closely follows the proof of
[
1
, Proposition 10.5]
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
O
be any handlebody, and let
Y
be a genus-one handlebody. For every
i\geq 0
,

H_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}(O,Y))\cong H_{i}(\mathcal{H}^{c}_{2,1}(O,Y)).

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

As we saw in Subsection
1.7
, there is a short exact sequence:

1\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{c}_{2,1}(O,Y)\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}_{2,1}(O,Y)\xrightarrow{}V\rightarrow 1.

We apply the Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence, getting

E^{2}_{p,q}=H_{p}(V,H_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}^{c}(O,Y),\mathbb{Z}))\Rightarrow H_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}(O,Y),\mathbb{Z}).

For convenience, we write
A_{q}=H_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}^{c}(O,Y),\mathbb{Z})
. Because of Corollary
6.3
, we know
A_{q}
is finitely generated and abelian, particularly it is residually finite. By
[
4
, Theorem 1]
,
\textnormal{Aut}(A_{q})
is residually finite. On the other hand, the group
V
is simple
[
18
]
. These two fact combined imply that any homomorphism
V\rightarrow A_{q}
is trivial, and consequently, any action of
V
in
A_{q}
must be trivial. Since
V
is acyclic
[
24
]
, every
H_{p}(V,A_{q})
for
p\geq 1
must be trivial and
H_{0}(V,A_{q})\cong A_{q}
. Thus the spectral sequence collapses, and
H_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}(O,Y),\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{i}(\mathcal{H}_{2,1}^{c}(O,Y),\mathbb{Z})
for all
i
.

∎

A combination of Theorem
6.4
and Corollary
6.2
yields Corollary
III
, which states what is the homology of the asymptotically rigid handlebody group.

Appendix A
Inclusion, intersection, and cancellation properties

In this appendix we introduce the inclusion, intersection, and cancellation properties, and prove that tree handlebodies satisfy them. We will deduce these properties from the fact that they are satisfied for surfaces, which is established in
[
1
, Apendix B1]
.

Definition A.1
.

(Inclusion property). We say that
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
has the
inclusion property
if the following holds: Let
M
be a suited handlebody, and
N\subset M
a connected spotted subhandlebody that is either suited or the complement of a suited handlebody with at least two spots. Then the homomorphism
\mathcal{H}(N)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(M)
induced by the inclusion map
N\hookrightarrow M
is injective.

Lemma A.2
.

For all
d,r\geq 1
,
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
satisfies the inclusion property.

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Recall the map
\mathfrak{R}
in Subsection
1.5
. Consider the following diagram,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(N)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textnormal{Map}(S_{M})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}

The diagram commutes, as given
f\in\mathcal{H}(N)
and a representative
f_{1}
of
f
, restricting
f_{1}
to
S_{N}
and then extending it by the identity to
S_{M}
, yields the same homeomorphism as extending
f_{1}
to
M
and then restricting it to
S_{M}
. Let
g
and
f
be distinct elements of
\mathcal{H}(N)
. Restriction to the boundary is injective because of
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
so
\mathfrak{R}(g)\neq\mathfrak{R}(f)
. Because of the inclusion property for surfaces (see
[
7
, Section 3.6]
),
i(\mathfrak{R}(g))\neq i(\mathfrak{R}(f))
. Since the diagram commutes, it follows that
\mathfrak{R}(i(g))\neq\mathfrak{R}(i(f))
. Restriction to the boundary is well defined, so
i(g)\neq i(f)
.

∎

Definition A.3
.

(Intersection property). Let
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
be a tree handlebody with the inclusion property. We say that
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
has the
intersection property
if, for every suited handlebody
M
, the following holds: Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be disjoint spotted subhandlebodies, each homeomorphic to
Y^{d}
, such that all but one of the spots in
L_{i}
are spots in
M
. Write
M_{i}
for the complement of
L_{i}
in
M
, and set
N=M_{1}\cap M_{2}
. Then
\mathcal{H}(N)=\mathcal{H}(M_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}(M_{2})
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
N\subset M
be two spotted subhandlebodies of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
. Then,
\mathcal{H}(N)=\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})\cap\mathcal{H}(M)
as subgroups of
\textnormal{Map}(S_{M})
.

Proof of Lemma
A.4
.

The inclusion from left to right is immediate. For the other inclusion, let
f\in\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})\cap\mathcal{H}(M)\subset\textnormal{Map}(S_{M})
. As
f\in\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})
, we can take
f_{1}
a representative fixing
S_{M}\setminus S_{N}
. Because
f\in\mathcal{H}(M)
, we can take
f_{2}
a representative which extends to a self-homeomorphism
g_{2}
of
M
.

Select a regular neighborhood
R_{M}
of
S_{M}
in
M
, and parametrize
R_{M}
as
S_{M}\times[0,1]
(with
S_{M}\times\{1\}
being
S_{M}
itself). Take an isotopy
\phi_{t}
from
f_{2}
to
f_{1}
, such that
\phi_{0}=f_{2}
and
\phi_{1}=f_{1}
. We use
\phi_{t}
to define a map
\Phi(p,x):=(\phi_{x}(p),x)
in
S_{M}\times[0,1]
, which agrees with
f_{1}
in
S_{M}\times\{1\}=S_{M}
, and with
f_{2}
in
S_{M}\times\{0\}=\partial\overline{M\setminus R_{M}}
.

The handlebody
\overline{M\setminus R_{M}}
is homeomorphic to
M
. Let
h:\overline{M\setminus R_{M}}\rightarrow M
be a homeomorphism. We define a homeomorphism

g_{3}:=\begin{cases}h^{-1}\circ g_{2}\circ h&\quad\text{in}\quad\overline{M\setminus R_{M}},\\
\Phi(p,x)&\quad\text{in}\quad R_{M},\\
\end{cases}

which fixes
S_{M}\setminus S_{N}
, although not necessarily
M\setminus N
. However, it sends the spots of
N
to discs with the same curve as boundary, and according to Lemma 2.3 in
[
15
]
, any two discs sharing their boundaries are isotopic. Composing the isotopies between discs with
g_{3}
, we can construct a homeomorphism whose restriction to
S_{M}
is
f_{1}
, and that fixes
M\setminus N
(it fixes
\partial(M\setminus N)
so it also fixes the interior, because of
[
15
, Lemma 3.1]
), hence
f\in\mathcal{H}(N)
.

∎

Lemma A.5
.

For all
d,r\geq 1
,
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
satisfies the intersection property.

Proof of Lemma
A.5
.

Take the equality
\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})=\textnormal{Map}(S_{M_{1}})\cap\textnormal{Map}(S_{M_{2}})
, which is true because of the intersection property for Cantor surfaces (a direct consequence of the Alexander method
[
7
, Section 2.3]
). Set
M=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
. Intersection at both sides of the equality
\textnormal{Map}(S_{N})=\textnormal{Map}(S_{M_{1}})\cap\textnormal{Map}(S_{M_{2}})
by
\mathcal{H}(M)
and use of Lemma
A.4
, yields the equality
\mathcal{H}(N)=\mathcal{H}(M_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}(M_{2})
.

∎

Definition A.6
.

(Cancellation property). We say that
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
satisfies the cancellation property if the following holds: Let
M
be a suited handlebody with at least one piece, and
D
a separating disc that cuts off a spotted subhandlebody homeomorphic to
Y^{d}
. Then the remaining component
N\subset M
is homeomorphic to some suited handlebody of
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
.

Lemma A.7
.

For all
d,r\geq 1
,
\mathcal{T}_{d,r}(O,Y)
satisfies the cancellation property.

Proof of Lemma
A.7
.

There is exactly one handlebody up to homeomorphism for each genus (see
[
19
, Proposition 9.2.19]
). If there is a homeomorphism between two spotted handlebodies, we can always take such homeomorphism to take spots to spots as long as the number of them agree. Choose any suited handlebody
N^{\prime}
of the same genus as
N
and containing
O_{0}
, we can find a homeomorphism taking
N^{\prime}
to
N
.

∎
