Heart ventricle regeneration in the lizard Eublepharis macularius, the leopard gecko
Authors: Kathy Jacyniak, Catherine J. A. Williams, Hugues Beaufrère, Jeremy Simpson, Guo N. Huang, Xiaoxin Chen, Matthew K. Vickaryous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Among mammals, injury to the heart typically results in scar formation and diminished cardiovascular function. In contrast, some teleost fish and salamanders can replace damaged heart tissue and restore overall function. For most species, however, less is known. Here, we investigate cardiac self-repair in an amniote capable of multi-tissue regeneration, the leopard gecko (Eublepharis macularius). To create a heart lesion, we placed a liquid nitrogen-cooled metal probe directly onto the ventricle. The result was a cryoinjury to ~20% of the ventricle. Cardiac cryoinjury induced localized cardiac cell death, followed by an increase in cell proliferation by injury-adjacent cardiomyocytes and non-cardiomyocytes. By 100 days, the histology of the ventricular myocardium was nearly completely restored. Echocardiography and invasive hemodynamic monitoring demonstrated that global cardiac function is restored within this timeframe, verifying functional replacement of the myocardium. To explore the molecular basis, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of the injury-adjacent tissue and found that many of the molecular mechanisms common to other cardiac regenerating species, including genes involved in heart development, glycolysis, and extracellular matrix deposition, are also conserved in geckos. Taken together, this work expands the comparative framework of heart regeneration to include reptiles and indicates that the ability to replace missing or damaged cardiomyocytes is shared across >50 million years of evolution.

Introduction

Among most adult mammals, injuries to the heart result in the irreversible loss of cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) and the formation of a permanent non-contractile fibrotic scar1–3. Combined, cardiomyocyte loss and scar formation lead to long-term consequences in global cardiac function, including a reduction in systolic (contraction) and diastolic (relaxation) function, and, in some cases death4–8. However, scarring is not the default response to heart injury in all species. It is now well-understood that some species of teleost fish, amphibians, fetal and neonatal mammals, and even adult spiny mice can spontaneously regenerate functional cardiomyocytes9–27. This cell-specific replacement leads to either improved function (e.g., spiny mice) or even complete restoration (zebrafish, axolotls).

Following injury, regrowth of ventricular tissue involves three synchronized yet overlapping phases: inflammation, repair, and regeneration. Inflammation begins as damaged cardiomyocytes undergo cell death and mononuclear immune cells begin to infiltrate the site of the injury1,28–30. As tissue debris is removed from the wound site, the repair phase begins with the activation of fibroblasts and deposition of a collagen-rich, extracellular matrix (ECM)3,31,32. Unique to regeneration-competent species, fibrous reparation is transient and overlaps with the proliferation of cardiomyocytes from uninjured tissues adjacent to the wound track33–35. During the final regenerative phase, the once scar-like repair is replaced by a fully functional (contractile) myocardium9,12,36–38. While adult spiny mice have a higher tolerance for cardiac ischemia and show improved reparative responses compared with other mammals (including laboratory mice), they do not fully regenerate the myocardium17,26,27.

Among amniotes (reptiles, birds, mammals), lizards are recognized as some of the most proficient regenerators39. Although best known for their ability to spontaneously regrow a lost tail40–42, some lizard species can also regenerate portions of their spinal cord43,44, optic nerve45, skin46–48, teeth49, and brain50,51. While less is known about the reparative capacity of lizard heart tissue, there are reports that mechanical and cautery injuries to the ventricle induce a surge in cardiomyocyte proliferation52–54. To the best of our knowledge, investigations of ventricular function in lizards following injury have yet to be performed.

Here, we investigate changes in the structure, function, and transcriptional profile of the heart during ventricular regeneration in the lizard species Eublepharis macularius (Blyth, 1854), the leopard gecko (hereafter ‘gecko’). Geckos are commercially bred and readily available in the pet trade55, with well-established husbandry methods56 and an impressive repertoire of regenerative abilities39. Geckos readily tolerate puncture injuries to the heart57,58 and their cardiomyocytes share several hallmark attributes in common with other cardiac regenerating species: they are predominantly diploid, relatively small in size, and capable of re-entering the cell cycle59,60. To initiate the reparative response, we employed a cryoinjury ( = cryocauterization). In zebrafish and other species with limited coronary artery networks, cryoinjury has emerged as one of the most reproducible approaches for inducing in situ myocardial necrosis9,12,13,19. The resulting loss of cardiomyocytes mimics the damage that occurs following a coronary ligation in species that depend on coronary circulation (e.g., mammals) and resembles the pathophysiology characteristic of myocardial infarction12,61–65. Our data reveal that geckos spontaneously regenerate the myocardium following a cryoinjury and that cardiomyocyte replacement correlates with the restoration of global function. Using RNA-sequencing (RNAseq), we show that many of the key biological processes and differentially expressed genes (DEG) previously identified in zebrafish, axolotls, and other salamanders are also characteristic of geckos. Taken together, we show that ventricular myocardial regeneration involves highly conserved morphogenetic and transcriptional programs, shared among species that diverged hundreds of millions of years ago. Further, our findings indicate that spontaneous myocardial replacement may be more taxonomically widespread than previously recognized.

Results

Cryoinjury induces a transient lesion that is largely regenerated

To test the regenerative capacity of the gecko heart, we surgically accessed the ventricle, created a tear in the pericardium, and applied a liquid nitrogen-cooled metal probe to induce the cryoinjury (Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Fig. 1). The site of cryoinjury demonstrated temporary blanching (Fig. 1c), confirming creation of the lesion. At 3-dpci, there was an obvious TUNEL+ transmural infarct devoid of MHC expression (Fig. 2e). While cardiomyocytes were absent, Vim-immunostaining revealed various non-contractile cells, likely mononucleated immune cells (Fig. 2f), and collagen deposition had begun (Fig. 2g, h). While the epicardium was damaged, the fibrous pericardium, which was initially torn to access the ventricle, was regenerated and indistinguishable from the surrounding uninjured tissue. By 14-dpci the number of TUNEL-expressing cells had decreased (Fig. 2i), while the wound continued to be devoid of MHC and immunoreactive for Vim (Fig. 2j). A disorganized, fibrous matrix thus formed within the infarct (Fig. 2k). Further, the epicardium re-developed across the cortex and was notably thickened at the lesion as compared to areas distant to the infarct (Fig. 2l). Within 30-dpci collected individuals there were no longer any TUNEL+ cells within the infarct. Instead, the site of cryoinjury was characterized by a heterogeneous combination of MHC-expressing cardiomyocytes and Vimentin-expressing non-cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2m, n). The fibrous matrix within the cortex was now more organized, with abundant collagen fibers running in a parallel organization interspersed amongst few cells (Fig. 2o, p). By 100-dpci, the myocardium was virtually restored (Fig. 2q), with only a small, fibrous remnant of the infarct localized along the border of the ventricular cortex (Fig. 2r–t).

To estimate infarct size, we quantified the maximal cross-sectional area of damage using Masson’s Trichrome staining (Supplementary Fig. 2). At 3-dpci, the cryoinjured lesion occupied 21.5% ( + /− 8.1%, n = 3) of the cross-sectional area of the ventricle and included myocardial tissues from both the trabeculated and compact compartments. By 100-dpci the myocardium was almost restored, with damaged tissue occupying 1.96% ( + /− 0.88%, n = 5) of the cross-sectional area of the ventricle, primarily along the compact cortex (Fig. 2r–t). Taken together, cardiac cryoinjury in geckos results in the formation of a necrotic lesion and ECM formation that over time becomes gradually replaced with myocardial tissue.

Gecko cardiomyocytes proliferate in response to cryoinjury

To determine if ventricular cardiomyocytes actively proliferate in response to cryoinjury, we co-localized MHC with three different proliferation markers (Fig. 3): the thymidine analogue BrdU (Supplementary Fig. 3), the DNA synthesis marker PCNA, and the mitotic marker pHH3 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared with shams (Fig. 3a–d), each of the 3-, 14-, and 30-dpci hearts showed an increase in the number of proliferating cardiac cells, both cardiomyocytes and non-cardiomyocytes, especially when visualized using BrdU and PCNA (Fig. 3e–p). Next, we used the same quantitative approach to investigate the spatial distribution of proliferating non-cardiomyocytes (MHC−/BrdU + ) across the ventricle. We found that the pattern of distribution for proliferating non-cardiomyocytes closely matched that of cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3x; Table 1). When injury-adjacent regions were compared with injury-distant regions we found: no significant differences in non-cardiomyocyte proliferation within control and sham ventricles; a 4.1-fold increase (p < 0.0001, general linear mixed model) in the injury-adjacent region at 3-dpci; a 4.5-fold increase (p < 0.0001, general linear mixed model) at 14-dpci; ~3.2-fold increase (p = 0.0061, general linear mixed model) at 30-dpci; but no significant difference by 100-dpci (Fig. 3x; Table 1). We also found that when the number of proliferating non-cardiomyocytes in the injury-distant region was compared to the sham ventricle across groups, only the 3-dpci were significantly different (p = 0.0025, general linear mixed model) (Fig. 3y; Table 1).

To determine whether there was an overall increase in the proportion of proliferating cardiomyocytes following injury in the cryoinjured ventricle, we quantified the proliferative response of MHC + /BrdU+ cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3z; Table 3). Between control and sham ventricles, we found no significant differences in the number of MHC + /BrdU+ cardiomyocytes (p = 0.1969, general linear mixed model). We then used the same quantitative approach to investigate MHC−/BrdU+ non-cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3za; Table 3). Comparing control and sham ventricles, we found no significant differences in the number of labelled non-cardiomyocytes (p = 0.6878, general linear mixed model). However, at subsequent timepoints the number of MHC−/BrdU+ non-cardiomyocytes was not significantly different (30-dpci, p = 0.2074; 100-dpci, p = 0.9210, general linear mixed model). Overall, these data demonstrate that cardiomyocytes (and non-cardiomyocytes) are proliferatively activated in response to cryoinjury, and that this response is primarily driven by injury-adjacent (local) populations.

Overall, we found that various genes associated with heart repair and regeneration in other species are also differentially expressed in geckos (Fig. 5e). For example, there is an early upregulation of genes associated with cell proliferation (fgf1), wound healing (has2), cardioprotection (slit2), muscle membrane repair (trim72), and pro-regenerative ECM deposition (eln, dpt)67–73. Heart regenerating species also commonly demonstrate a metabolic shift to glycolysis10,74–77. In support of a similar switch in geckos, we identified an early upregulation of hk2, cyb5a, and pgam1, followed at later time points by an upregulation of egfr, pdk2, sorbs1, and pdk3. Another characteristic of cardiac regeneration and repair is the deposition and organization of ECM within the wound site78,79. As in zebrafish, gecko heart regeneration involves an early upregulation of multiple ECM genes, including col1a1, col1a2, col5a1, col5a2, col12a1, mmp2 and mmp27. At later stages, there is a significant upregulation of col8a2 and downregulation of both col9a2 and col18a110,15,77,80–85.

Next, we conducted a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using Metascape.org, which incorporates a core set of default ontologies including GO processes, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, Reactome gene sets, canonical pathways, and CORUM complexes86. As the gecko genome is not fully annotated, we selected the M. musculus database for comparative annotations. Of the 2991 DEG, 2110 ( ~ 70.5%) were annotated and thus considered for analysis. As expected, many of the top 100 enriched GO terms are associated with heart regeneration (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Data 1). We then used clusterProfiler to analyze the GO terms focusing on Biological Processes (Fig. 6; Supplementary Figs. 6–10). By 3 dpci, many of the top enriched GO terms related to ECM deposition and organization, and muscle cell morphogenesis (Fig. 6a, b). As indicated by the upregulation of genes related to collagen metabolic processes, ECM remodeling begins by 14 dpci (Fig. 6c, d). At 30 dpci, there is transcriptional evidence of cardiomyocyte differentiation and epithelial cell proliferation, coinciding with restoration of the damaged myocardium (Fig. 6e, f). Discussion

Here, we provide the first evidence of structural and functional heart regeneration in an adult amniote, the gecko E. macularius. We determined that following a cryoinjury, the gecko ventricle spontaneously regenerates most of the damaged tissue and restores global function. We also show that many (but not all) of the key cellular and molecular events characteristic of gecko ventricular regeneration appear to be conserved with zebrafish and salamanders, species separated from lizards by more than 350 million years of evolution87. Although a broader explanation as to why adult geckos are able to regenerate the heart remains elusive, it should be recognized that leopard geckos in particular are renowned as proficient regenerators of many different tissues and organs, including the tail, spinal cord, skin, and forebrain39–41,43,44,47,51,88, hinting at systemic factors that go beyond individual organs and organ systems.

At the start of the experiment, all the animals were sexually immature. Previous research on geckos has demonstrated that, under normal (i.e., uninjured) homeostatic conditions, the rate of cardiomyocyte proliferation declines with increasing age and sexual maturity60. Whether this ontogenetic reduction in cardiomyocyte cell cycling impacts the regenerative capacity of the gecko heart (as it does in rodents and humans89–91) remains unclear. However, it is worth noting that while the rate of proliferation decreases, cardiomyocytes from sexually mature/adult-sized geckos remain diploid and are not significantly different in size compared to those of immature geckos60, similar to the situation in zebrafish92. Related to this, the increasing chronological age does not appear to impact ventricular regeneration in zebrafish92.

Cryoinjuries represent a highly reproducible model for generating myocardial infarcts and have been successfully employed to study heart repair in a variety of species of teleost fish and salamanders, as well as rodents and pigs9,13,19,61–65. In adapting this strategy for small lizards, we also developed a new pre-, peri- and post-operative pain management approach for reptile surgery. Although the appropriate use of anesthetics (including species-specific ventilation and temperature requirements) and analgesics (including opioids such as hydromorphone) in clinical and experimental reptile surgery remains a subject of current study93,94, the high (100%) survival rate of geckos in our experiments underscores the value of proper drug and peri-anaesthetic protocol selection and use.

Myocardial regeneration in geckos is characterized by an early and robust surge in proliferation. Proliferative activation of resident cardiomyocytes is a hallmark process of heart regeneration across most species9,13,20,23,25,33,35,78,95,96,97. Similar to zebrafish and salamanders, the proliferative response of geckos includes both injury-adjacent ( = local, epimorphic) and injury-distant ( = global, compensatory) activation12,35,96,98. However, whereas the injury-distant response appears to be short-lived (less than two weeks), the injury-adjacent proliferative response peaks early (3-dpci) and does not return to baseline until 100 dpci. In contrast, among zebrafish the global proliferative response appears to facilitate regenerative myocardial outgrowth35. Although our findings strongly imply a proliferative surge by pre-exiting cardiomyocytes, we acknowledge that our inability to co-localize a nuclear marker of mature cardiomyocytes (such as mef2) with our proliferation markers as a technical limitation and that genetic fate-mapping is necessary to determine the cellular source of the regenerated myocardium. Whether the newly generated cardiomyocytes demonstrated evidence of polyploidy or endoreplication also remains uncertain.

Matching the proliferative response of cardiomyocytes following cryoinjury, we also observed a similar spatiotemporal pattern of non-cardiomyocyte proliferation. Although the identity of these non-cardiomyocyte populations in geckos remains unclear, evidence from other vertebrates suggests that it might be a heterogeneous population of cardiac cells, including immature cardiomyocytes (i.e., not expressing contractile proteins), immune cells, endothelial cells, and cardiac (myo)fibroblasts11,92,99. Additional support from our transcriptomic data reveals a dynamic interplay of enriched biological processes involved in ECM deposition and organization, and differential expression of genes associated with matrix assembly. Taken as a whole, the findings highlight the contributions and dynamic interplay of multiple cell types (cardiomyocytes and non-cardiomyocytes) to the regenerating gecko myocardium.

Cryoinjuries have a significant but transient impact on global cardiac function. Using 2D B-mode echocardiography to measure FAC, we show that cryo-generated infarcts have a significant impact on systolic function as early as 3-dpci, with a peak functional impairment at 7-dpci. Over time however, there is a gradual normalization of systolic function, corresponding with structural restoration of the myocardium, up until 100-dpci when FAC of injured geckos no longer differs from that of sham controls. A comparable temporary functional decline is characteristic of other species capable of heart regeneration12,23,36,38,62,100 and stands in stark contrast with species that fail to regenerate4,5,7,8. Although 2D echocardiography has previously been employed to clinically evaluate reptiles66,101,102, to the best of our knowledge this study is the first to track reptilian ventricular function following an experimental injury. In particular, the use of B-mode, with the ventricle in long axis view, provided good image quality and reproducible tracing of the epicardial (rather than the endocardial) edge. However, two important limitations should be acknowledged. First, most common echocardiographic ventricular measurements (e.g., fractional volume shortening, ejection fraction, stroke volume, and cardiac output) employ assumptions about ventricular shape, and end diastolic blood volume that have yet to be defined for reptiles103, requiring direct measurement of ventricular mass to give ventricular myocardial volume and therefore a requirement for euthanasia to accurately ascertain e.g., ejection fraction (Williams et al., 2019). Second, gecko ventricles are trabeculated (spongy) and located deep to the ossified pectoral apparatus. As a result, M-mode ultrasound (which is generally viewed as providing increased spatial resolution compared with B-mode104) was unable to reliably resolve the ventricle. Similarly, Doppler blood flow measurements within the ventricle and in the great vessels were rarely attainable and at best, inconsistent with repeated measurements (unpublished data).

Invasive hemodynamics verified that most indices of systolic and diastolic function were restored by 100-dpci. One exception was maximal rate of ventricular pressure development (dP/dtmax). Combined, these findings suggest that while preload, afterload, and heart rate are unaltered in regenerated hearts, contractile capacity increases (even with the presence of a fibrous remnant). Further research is necessary to understand whether this post-regenerative alteration to produce somewhat enhanced contraction relates to changes in reptilian features of ventricular morphology (e.g., partially septated and trabeculated) and/or physiology (viz., behavioural thermoregulation).

Paralleling the structural and functional data, our transcriptomic analyses reveal that many of the molecular mechanisms common to other regeneration-competent species are conserved in geckos. For example, many of the top biological processes include terms associated with cardiomyocyte proliferation and muscle cell differentiation, and processes involved in cardiac ventricle development and trabecula morphogenesis. It is worth noting however that while some genes implicated in successful cardiac regeneration are upregulated (e.g., trim72, eln, and Ankrd1), others are not (NFE2L1 and Nrf1)10,14,15,68,69,105. Another notable difference between geckos and other heart regenerating species relates to the expression of the key cardiogenic transcription factors gata4, hand2, and tbx5. All are known to be upregulated during ventricular regeneration in zebrafish99,106–108, and their forced expression (along with mef2c) has been used to reprogram mammalian cardiac fibroblasts into induced cardiomyocytes109–111. Nevertheless, at the time points sampled during our study of geckos, expression of gata4, hand2 and tbx5 was not significantly altered at any of the timepoints investigated, and mef2c was downregulated. Whether these findings speak to inconsistencies in spatiotemporal sampling, or an inability to detect modest (yet biologically impactful) changes in these transcription factors, remains to be determined. However, we speculate that differences in transcription factor expression may represent evidence of a regenerative response that has evolved. A classic example of the evolution of regeneration comes from studies on muscle regeneration in salamanders, which have revealed two taxon-specific sources of myoblasts: resident stem cells in axolotls and dedifferentiated myoblasts in newts112. We hypothesize that the molecular mechanisms involved in gecko ventricular regeneration have been subject to selection pressures distinct from those of other heart regenerating species. Future investigations of other reptiles may help resolve whether our findings are characteristic of a broader taxonomic grouping of species (members of Gekkota, or lizards in general), or unique to the leopard gecko.

Although ventricular repair involves a period of ECM deposition, this fibrotic infill is largely (although not entirely) replaced. Similar to zebrafish, the upregulation in DEGs related to fibrosis, including ECM deposition and organization, and the promotion of collagen (e.g., col1a1, col1a2, col4a1, col5a1, col5a2 and col12a1) is transient10,15,80,82,84,85,113. By 100 dpci, the only evidence of the infarct is a small ( < 2% of the ventricular cross-sectional area) fibrous remnant along the outer margin of the myocardium. Importantly, this has no impact on global systemic function, suggesting that complete structural fidelity is unnecessary to restore ventricular function. Similarly functional restoration in the absence of structural exactness has been reported for zebrafish, including ventricular rounding and myocardial thickening36 and persistent fibrosis38. Other evidence from zebrafish indicates that the presence of remnant fibrotic tissues also has no impact on the ability of the ventricle to regenerate from subsequent cryoinjuries114.

Taken together, our investigation expands the comparative framework of ventricular regeneration to include reptiles, a group of species separated from zebrafish and axolotls by more than 350 million years of evolution. Although numerous questions related to how geckos rebuild their hearts remain, this work reveals that the ability of replace missing or damaged cardiomyocytes is likely more broadly conserved across vertebrates than previously recognized and thus may provide an opportunity for a deeper understanding of the evolution of heart regeneration.

Methods

Animal care

The University of Guelph Animal Care Committee reviewed and approved the Animal Utilization Protocol (protocol #4226) in accordance with the procedures of the Canadian Council on Animal Care and Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiment (ARRIVE) guidelines115. Captive bred leopard geckos (Eublepharis macularius, ‘geckos’) were acquired from a commercial breeder (Jaymz Kay Reptiles, Beamsville, Ontario, Canada). At the start of the experiment, all were under one year of age and were sexually immature with no obvious external secondary sexual characteristics (average body mass: 13.1–19.7 g; snout-vent length: 8.5–10.2 cm). Growth was monitored weekly by measuring mass, and snout-vent length was collected at the start and at each collection time point throughout the experiment. Geckos were housed in a secure, temperature-controlled environmental chamber with an average ambient temperature of ~28°C and a 12-hour light: 12-hour dark photoperiod41. Each animal was housed individually in 5-gallon polycarbonate enclosures, with a subsurface heat cable placed on one side to establish a heat gradient (Hagen Inc., Baie d’Urfe, Quebec, Canada). Each enclosure included two hide boxes and a water bowl. Geckos were fed gut-loaded larval Tenebrio spp. (mealworms) or Zophobas spp. (superworms) dusted with powdered vitamin D (cholecalciferol) and calcium carbonate supplementation (Zoo Med Laboratories Inc.; A34-8; San Luis Obispo, CA, USA) or were offered Repashy Grub Pie meal replacement gel (Repashy Ventures, Oceanside, CA, USA) if they were uninterested in mealworms. At the end of the experiment, we determined that all but two geckos involved in the study were female (one male was a sham, one a 100 days post-cryoinjury (dpci) gecko; see below). Therefore, sex-based analyses were not performed.

Experimental approach

Geckos (n = 38) were randomly assigned into one of six experimental groups: controls (naïve animals); shams (surgery but no cryoinjury); 3-dpci; 14-dpci; 30-dpci; and 100-dpci (n = 6 for all groups, except n = 8 for 100-dpci). Geckos were collected at their respective time point or at day 100 (including all controls and shams).

Celiotomy and cardiac cryoinjury procedure

Geckos were acclimated for three months prior to the start of the experiment. All cryoinjury and sham surgeries were performed during the day between 9:00am and 4:00 pm, and geckos were fasted for 24 hours prior to surgery. All injections were administered with sterile 0.5cc insulin syringes (Abbott Laboratories, Saint-Laurent, Quebec, Canada). On Experimental Day 1, all cryoinjury and sham control geckos were perioperatively anesthetized using Alfaxan® (30 mg/kg alfaxalone; Jurox Inc., North Kansas City, MO, USA) administered intramuscularly into the dorsal epaxial muscles of the cervical region, followed by hydromorphone (0.5 mg/kg; McKesson Canada Corporation, Brampton, ON, Canada) diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride (Baxter, Deerfield, IL, USA) administered into the same area contralaterally. Once the righting reflex was lost and the geckos were non-responsive to nociceptive stimulus (toe pinch with non-rat tooth tissue forceps), they were placed into dorsal recumbency and lidocaine (maximum total dose of 10 mg/kg; lidocaine hydrochloride; Teligent; Mississauga, ON, Canada) diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride was administered at the surgical site. Lidocaine was also topically administered over the glottis to facilitate intubation. Geckos were intubated using an IV catheter (BD precisionGlide, 18–24 g) connected to a SAV-04 Small Animal Ventilator (Vetronic Services, Abbotskerswell, UK) using room air via manual flow with the following settings: 4-6 breaths per minute; max airway pressure 4 cm H2O; flow 40 ml/min. All geckos were monitored for respiratory movements throughout the surgery; if movements stopped, intermittent positive pressure ventilation was implemented. Geckos were placed on an electric cloth heat pad covered in sterile surgical draping to maintain a constant body temperature of ~27.6 °C and were constantly monitored with a thermal probe inserted in the cloaca. The surgical area was then aseptically prepared with povidone-iodine concentrate (Betadine solution), and Press’n Seal® Cling Film (Glad) was placed over the gecko as a sterile barrier to maintain the surgical field.

Following anesthetic induction, a single skin incision was made parallel to the ventral ribs (and hence in a craniomedial-caudolateral direction). This was followed by incisions through the subcutaneous tissue and then the intercostal musculature directly beneath the skin incision. A retractor was then inserted into the intercostal incision to reveal the underlying heart in its pericardial sac. Sterile cotton swabs were used to remove any excess blood. The pericardial sac of cryoinjured geckos was torn using fine forceps (Fine Science Tools, Foster City, CA, USA) to expose the underlying ventricle. For the cryoinjury, a cryoprobe was then placed in liquid nitrogen for 30 seconds, then held at room temperature for 20 seconds prior to being applied directly to the left side of the ventricle (approximate 2 mm from the apex) for 10 seconds (area of contact approximately 7 mm × 1 mm). To avoid rupturing the ventricular wall, removal of the probe was facilitated by the application of several drops of sterile saline 0.9% sodium chloride (Baxter, Deerfield, IL, USA). Cryoinjury-induced damage was immediately evident as tissue blanching, followed by a partial return to colour. Sham geckos received a similar celiotomy surgery and rupture of the pericardial sac, but the applied probe was at room temperature; no blanching of the ventricular tissue was observed. Following surgery, the ribs were aligned, and the thoracic wall was sutured using PDS II 5.0 polydioxanone monofilament absorbable sutures (Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson MedTech, Markham, ON, Canada) (3-4 stitches), applied in a horizontal mattress stitch. The skin was then aligned and sutured closed using the same material (2–3 stitches), applied in a cruciate appositional suture pattern.

After closure of the surgical site, geckos were given an additional intramuscular dose of hydromorphone (0.5 mg/kg) prior to being placed in a clean, heated (27.6 ˚°C) enclosure for recovery. Once geckos regained consciousness, tramadol (5 mg/kg, Ontario Veterinary College, Guelph, ON, Canada) was administered orally (via a sterile micropipette) for post-operative analgesia and mealworms were presented within two hours of waking. Tramadol (5 mg/kg) was also administered 48 hours and 96 hours following surgery. Gecko were monitored twice daily for the first two weeks, then daily for the duration of the experiment for signs of pain or infection. At the time points of interest (3-, 14-, 30-, and 100-dpci) geckos were euthanized by alfaxalone overdose (150 mg/kg IP, confirmed via lack of nociceptive withdrawal and corneal reflex), and their hearts were collected for histological and transcriptomic investigations, while chemical fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) of the head and body provided permanent central nervous disruption.

2D Transthoracic echocardiography

Cardiac function of all geckos was assessed weekly and immediately before each tissue collection time point using 2D transthoracic echocardiography. Briefly, geckos were fasted for 24 hours prior to echocardiography and sedated using an intramuscular injection of Alfaxan® (30 mg/kg alfaxalone) into the dorsal epaxial muscles of the cervical region. Once the righting reflex was absent, geckos were placed in right lateral recumbency onto an electric heat pad and maintained at ~27.6°C, monitored using a thermal probe (TH-5 rectal probe, Physiotemp Instruments) inserted in the cloaca. The electric heat pad was integrated with a Vevo 2100 imaging system (VisualSonics Inc., Canada) and equipped with a 40-MHz ultrasound transducer (MS550D, VisualSonics Inc., Canada). As gecko skin is hydrophobic, a thin layer of food grade mineral oil (Equate, Vi-Jon, LLC, St. Louis, MO, USA) was first applied, followed by a thin layer of acoustic ultrasound gel (Transonic). To locate the ventricle, the ultrasound probe was positioned in the left axillary region using a micromanipulator to obtain a long access view of the heart. Once the entire length of the ventricle (apex to base) was visualized, the probe was slightly elevated to minimize thoracic compression during scanning. A total of three image sequences, each capturing two full cardiac cycles, were acquired and saved for off-line analysis. Once the scans were completed, excess coupling gel and oil were removed, and the geckos were either returned to their home enclosures to recover from anesthesia, euthanized (if a collection time point) or subjected invasive hemodynamics (see below). Following the completion of the experiments, stored echocardiology videos were examined by a blinded observer (K.J.) to acquire ventricular area (mm2) and ventricular longitudinal length (mm) using the cardiac package (Visualsonics) LV-trace function. Both measurements were obtained by tracing the outer surface of the ventricle at the epicardium at the maximal and minimal points in the cardiac cycle, corresponding to end-diastole and end-systole respectively. Each measurement was taken in triplicate and averaged to yield a single value. We calculated Fractional Area Change (FAC, reported as a percentage) using the following formula: (end-diastolic area - end-systolic area)/end-diastolic area × 100, as for previous studies on species with a trabeculated myocardium36,66,101.

Invasive hemodynamics

To assess long-term cardiac function, sham (n = 6), control (n = 6), and 100 dpci (n = 8) geckos additionally underwent invasive hemodynamic assessment at the study endpoint on experimental day 100, using an approach adapted from previous work116. Once echocardiography scans were completed, geckos received an additional 30 mg/kg dose of alfaxalone, as well as an intramuscular injection of 0.5 mg/kg hydromorphone into the dorsal epaxial muscles of the cervical region. If the procedure lasted longer than 30 minutes, supplemental 5–10 mg/kg doses of alfaxalone (intramuscular into the forelimb) were administered. As described for the celiotomy procedure, once righting reflex was abolished, lidocaine was topically administered over the glottis and geckos were intubated, then connected to a SAV-04 Small Animal Ventilator (room air via manual flow, 4–6 breaths per minute; max airway pressure 4 cm H2O; flow 40 ml/min). All geckos were monitored for respiratory movements. Lidocaine ( < 10 mg/kg, diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride) was then administered to the ventro-caudal portion of the throat and cranial portion of the pectoral girdle. Once nociceptive responses were abolished, a 3 cm transverse incision cranial to the forelimbs was made through the skin and underlying musculature to expose the right internal carotid artery. The carotid artery was then isolated, and a 1.2-Fr pressure catheter (Transonic Systems) was inserted and advanced through the right aortic arch to reach the ventricle. Body temperature was maintained at ~27.6°C using a heat pad and lamp, according to a TH-5 rectal probe thermometer inserted into the cloaca. Once stabilized, pressure signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 2 kHz. Hemodynamic readings including end-diastolic pressure, ventricular peak pressure, dP/dt at V10, heart rate, and maximal rates of ventricular pressure development (dP/dtmax) and decay (dp/dtmin) were obtained and analyzed using Spike2 (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK) and custom script analysis116. Once hemodynamic readings were recorded, geckos were euthanized with a 150 mg/kg intramuscular injection of alfaxalone to the dorsal epaxial muscles, followed by decapitation, fixation of the head, and removal of the heart.

5-bromo-2’-deoxyuridine (BrdU) injections

Two days prior to each collection time point, geckos underwent a series of BrdU injections to document cardiac cell proliferation following cryoinjury. BrdU is a thymidine analog that is incorporated into the DNA synthesis phase of the cell cycle; therefore, any cell that cycles during its administration will incorporate the analog, which can then be subsequently detected using immunofluorescence (Kiel et al., 2007; Kubikova et al., 2020; Solius et al., 2021). All BrdU experiments were conducted using the protocol of59. Briefly, a 50 mg/mL BrdU stock solution was prepared by diluting 50 mg of BrdU powder (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) in 1 mL dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). A 5 mg/mL working solution of BrdU was then prepared by diluting the stock solution with 9 mL of sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Ontario, Canada). Each gecko received a 50 mg/kg dose of the working BrdU solution, administered into the peritoneal cavity at 12-hour intervals (8:30 am and 8:30 pm), alternating the left and right side for each injection interval. Additionally, sham (n = 6), control (n = 6), and 100 dpci (n = 8) geckos received BrdU on experimental day 98 prior to being collected on experimental day 100.

Tissue collection

Geckos were euthanized with an overdose of Alfaxan® (150 mg/kg alfaxalone) administered intramuscularly into the dorsal epaxial muscles of the cervical region. Once the righting reflex and spontaneous ventilation were lost animals were decapitated, and the hearts removed.

Tissue preparation for histochemistry

Prior to processing for histology (n = 3 for all timepoints except 100 dpci with an n = 5), in situ images of the heart in ventral view were taken using a Keyence VHX digital microscope. Hearts were then dissected out and rinsed in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Ontario, Canada), prior to being placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for ~22 hours for immersion fixation. Hearts were then transferred and stored in 70% ethanol prior to automated tissue processing (Fischer Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Tissues were dehydrated in 100% isopropanol, followed by clearing in xylene and embedding in molten paraffin wax using an automated processor (Fischer Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). A rotary microtome (HM 355S Automatic Microtome; Thermo Scientific; Ottawa, Canada) was used to section the hearts at 5 µm. Tissues were mounted on charged slides and baked in the 37°C oven overnight, then for 30 mins in the 60°C oven the following day.

Masson’s trichrome

Masson’s Trichrome was used to characterize the myocardial architecture and visualize fibrous connective tissue, adapted from an established protocol (Allwood et al., 2024; Ogilvie et al., 2021). Briefly, slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a series of xylenes (3××/2 minutes), followed by a series of 100% isopropanol (3×/2 minutes), once though 70% isopropanol (2 mins), and once in deionized water (2 minutes). The slides were placed in Bouin’s solution heated to 56°C for 15 minutes, rinsed in deionized water, then counter stained in Mayer’s Hematoxylin for 10 minutes (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, ON, Canada) followed by another deionized water rinse. Slide-mounted tissues were then counterstained in ammonia water (10 seconds) and rinsed again before being placed in acid fuschin/ponceau xylidine (3 minutes). Slides were rinsed once more, stained in phosphomolybdic acid (1.5 minutes), after which they were rinsed in deionized water. Next, slides were stained in working light green (1.5 minutes) and rinsed. Finally, slides were passed through 95% isopropanol (2 minutes), 3 changes of 100% isopropanol (2 minutes each) and 3 changes of xylene (2 minutes each). All slides were coverslipped using Cytoseal (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) and were left to dry overnight in the fume hood.

Maximal infarct size (%) was measured from slide-mounted transverse sections of ventricle, stained with Masson’s trichrome, by quantifying the area of damage within the middle of cryolesion divided by the total area of section using the ×200 objective on a Keyence digital microscope (VH-Z100R; Keyence Canada Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada). Quantifications were performed using the Axiovision KS3000 software (Zeiss).

Picrosirius red

To better understand the organization of fibrous connective tissue at the wound site, select slides were stained with picrosirius red using a Leica S5010 Autostainer XL (Leica Microsystems; Concord, ON, Canada). Slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated with xylene (3x/2 minutes), 100% isopropanol (3x/2 minutes), 70% isopropanol (2 minutes), and finally water (10 minutes). Tissues then underwent an incubation in picrosirius red (0.5 g Sirius red (Sigma Aldrich; 365548; Oakville, ON, Canada) in 500 mL of picric acid (Ricca Chemical; 586016; Arlington, TX, USA) (90 minutes), followed by two rinses with acidified water (1 minute each). Tissues were then dehydrated with 70% isopropanol (45 seconds), 100% isopropanol (3x/2 minutes), and cleared in xylene (3x/2 minutes). Slide-mounted tissues were then coverslipped using the Leica CV5030 Fully Automated Glass Coverslipper (Leica Microsystems; Concord, ON, Canada). Picrosirius red stained slides were imaged using differential interference contrast (DIC) imaging at ×20 using an Axio Imager D1 Microscope (Carl Zeiss Canada Ltd, Toronto, ON) with an AxioCam305 camera (Carl Zeiss Canada Ltd, Toronto, ON).

Immunofluorescence-standard protocol

A standard immunofluorescence protocol was performed to visualize the contractile protein Myosin Heavy Chain (MHC; RRID: AB_476811) and the intermediate filament and fibroblast marker Vimentin (Vim; RRID: AB_528506), each with the mitosis phase marker phosphorylated histone H3 (pHH3; RRID: AB_1549592), the DNA synthesis phase marker Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA; RRID: AB_2160375), and the analog BrdU (see Supplementary Table 4 for more antibody details). Briefly, tissue-mounted slides were rehydrated to water (see above), then incubated in 1× PBS (5 minutes) before undergoing a citrate buffer retrieval step (12 minutes at 95˚C, followed by 20 minutes to cool at room temperature). Slides were rinsed with 1× PBS (2 minutes), incubated with 0.1% trypsin (Type II-S, from porcine pancreas; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO)(20 minutes at 37˚C), rinsed again with 1× PBS (2 minutes), then blocked using 5% normal goat serum diluted in diluent (1% bovine serum albumin [BSA; VWR, Radnor, PA], 0.5% Tween20 [Sigma Aldrich St. Louis, MO], and 0.1% sodium azide [Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA])(30 minutes at 37˚C). Blocking solution was then removed, and one section/slide was incubated with primary antibodies in diluent (see Table 4.1 for details), while the other served as an omission control with diluent only (overnight at 4˚C). The following day, slides were rinsed with 1× PBS (3×/2 minutes) then incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in sterile 1× PBS (Cy3 labeled Goat anti-Rabbit and Alexa Fluor-488 labeled Goat anti-Mouse) (1 hour at room temperature) followed by another round of rinses with 1× PBS (3×/2 minutes). Slides were then incubated in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) diluted 1:5000 with sterile 1× PBS (2 minutes), underwent a final round of rinses with 1× PBS (3×/2 minutes), then were coverslipped and left to dry overnight in the dark.

Immunofluorescence- abbreviated protocol

Slide-mounted tissues were deparaffinized and rehydrated to deionized water (as above), rinsed with 1× PBS (3×/2 minutes) followed by a blocking step with 3% normal goat serum diluted in sterile 1× PBS (1 hour at room temperature). The blocking solution was tipped off, and slides incubated with the primary antibodies diluted in sterile 1× PBS (see Supplementary Table 4), with the second tissue on each section serving as the omission control with sterile 1× PBS only (overnight at 4˚C). The following day underwent the same steps as the standard protocol.

Immunofluorescence- Terminal deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick-End Labelling (TUNEL) Assay

To document cell death, representative tissue sections from the wound site were stained using the TUNEL assay. Tissue-mounted slides were rehydrated to deionized water (as above) and placed in 1× PBS (2 minutes). A TUNEL reaction mix was prepared (In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), and slides were incubated (1 hour at 37°C). For the TUNEL reaction mix, 45 µL of the label solution (tetra-methyl-rhodamine-dUTP solution) and 5 µL of the blue solution (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase solution) were combined and applied to one tissue, with the second tissue on the slide serving as the omission control with 50 µL of the label solution (tetra-methyl-rhodamine-dUTP solution) only. Slides were then rinsed in 1× PBS (3×/2 minutes), and the standard immunofluorescence protocol was subsequently resumed at the citrate buffer retrieval step.

Quantification of proliferating BrdU+ cardiac cells

To quantify the proportion of proliferating cardiomyocytes (and non-cardiomyocytes), serial sections immunostained for MHC and BrdU from three geckos per time point were used (except n = 5 for 100 dpci). From each, three non-overlapping, transverse sections (at least 80 µm apart) located within the wound site in the ventricle (or equivalent location for control geckos) were selected using random.org (3 geckos/timepoint × 6 timepoints × 3 slides/gecko + 6 slides for 2 extra geckos at 100 dpci= 60 sections total). To determine whether there are differences in cell proliferation across the ventricle, two regions of interest were investigated: an injury-adjacent region, within 200 µm of the infarct; and an injury-distant region, >1000 µm from the infarct. Each region of interest included the outer compact and/or inner trabeculated compartments. Regions of interest were imaged at ×40 using an Axio Imager D1 Microscope (Carl Zeiss Canada Ltd, Toronto, ON) with an AxioCam305 camera (Carl Zeiss Canada Ltd, Toronto, ON). Four non-overlapping fields of view (FOV) were taken from each section, with two FOV representing each region. Total number of nuclei (indicated by nuclear counterstain DAPI) was counted using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health), with the “Analyzer particles” plug-in. Proliferating cardiomyocytes (MHC + /BrdU + ) and non-cardiomyocyte (MHC−/BrdU + ) were counted manually. Changes in the proportion of BrdU+ cells (MHC+ and MHC−) at each timepoint were used as an indicator of cell proliferation.

Tissue preparation for bulk RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing samples, ventricular tissue from the injury-adjacent region (within 200 μm of the infarct; the border zone) was dissected out and placed in liquid nitrogen (n = 3 for each timepoint, including sham and uninjured control geckos). Total RNA was extracted individually from each animal with TRIzolTM reagent (Invitrogen, #15596026) and RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene Inc. Briefly, poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads were used to purify messenger RNA from total RNA. Once fragmented, random hexamer primers were used to synthesize the first strand cDNA followed by the second strand cDNA synthesis. After end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, amplification, and purification, the library was ready for transcriptome assembly and annotation.

RNA sequencing based transcriptome assembly and annotation

RNA sequencing analysis was performed with reference to the method described by ref. 117. Briefly, the RNA sequencing reads were initially processed and cleaned using trimgalore (version 0.6.7) to trim low-quality base calls and adapter sequences (threshold: Phred quality score <20). Hisat2 (2.2.0) was used to align the clean reads to the corresponding leopard gecko reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_028583425.1/) with parameters set to “-dta -very-sensitive’117,118 and then StringTie (v2.2.1)117,119 was used to conduct de novo transcriptome assembly for each sample. At each time point, reference-based assemblies were merged using the command ‘stringtie -merge” with the parameter “gap between transcripts to merge together” that was set to 50 bp, and low expression levels were filtered by setting a threshold of “TPM > 0.5”. Using CD-HIT (v4.8.1), removal of redundant transcripts was performed using a sequence identity threshold of 95% (-c 0.95)117,120. Nucleotide BLAST (v2.13.0)121 using the Gencode vM29 mouse gene annotation122 was used to annotate the transcript sequences that were obtained from the de novo transcriptome assembly using the parameters “-qcov_hsp_perc 10; -evalue 1e-30”. If a transcript sequence blasted to multiple transcript sequences in the Gencode database, the annotation for the transcript used was the one with the highest “bit score”. Salmon (version 1.8.0) was used to quantify gene expression. Transcript fasta files were indexed using the command “Salmon index” with default parameters. Clean RNA sequencing reads were then aligned to the indexed transcripts using parameters (-useVBOpt -seqBias -gcBias) to correct for sequence-specific bias and fragment GC bias. Read counts were then normalized by the median of ratios method123 and the mean of M-values (TMM)117,124. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot and volcano plots generated using R (version 4.4.2) using the plotPCA function of the package DEseq2 (1.46.0) and package ggplot2 (version 3.5.2). Heatmap of log2foldchange generated using GraphPad Prism. Additional Gene Ontology (GO) pathways analyses were performed in metascape.com, using annotations for Mus musculus. The GO enrichment analysis for the dotplot was generated using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.14.6), with a focus on Biological Processes. Sample clustering was performed using the R package pheatmap (v1.0.13). Venn diagram for distribution of differentially expressed genes across time points was generated using https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html.

Statistics

All analyses except those for invasive hemodynamics were made using ProcMIX software (SAS 9.4, Cary, NC) and the results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. Graphs were subsequently generated using Prism9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). For echocardiographic analysis, comparisons included a repeated measure, split-plot design between sham and cryoinjured geckos, and sham geckos across the study, and additional comparisons were made between experimental day 100 geckos (shams, control, days 100). All data are reported as the mean (expressed as a percentage) with 95% CI. Main and group effects were considered, and simple effects (sham geckos at each time point, sham and infarct geckos at each time point, and experimental day 100 geckos) were analyzed. Residual analyses were performed to assess normality and homogeneity for ANOVA, including the Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Cramer–von Mises, and Anderson–Darling analyses, and appeared fundamentally normal. Additionally, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to choose amongst error structures and came up with a Toeplitz analysis method. Overall, the analysis appeared fundamentally normal, and the ANOVA assumptions were adequately met. One formal outlier was identified for each analysis, but they were inconsequential to the data set and were therefore included in the final analysis. To determine whether a statistically significant difference exists across sham gecko, between sham and experimental geckos, and at experimental day 100, a general linear mixed model was used. Sham geckos were not sampled for echocardiography on day 3 to avoid interference of the effects of the anesthesia. To determine whether a statistically significant difference exists between experimental day 3 geckos and sham geckos sampled at baseline, residual analyses were performed to assess normality and homogeneity. The data appeared fundamentally normal, and a parametric t-test was used for analysis.

For the proliferating cardiomyocyte (BrdU + /MHC + ) and non-cardiomyocyte (BrdU + /MHC−) analysis, a two-factor CRD split plot design was employed, comparing injured against sham geckos at each timepoint, and considering the region sampled (injury adjacent: within 20 μm from infarct, and injury-distant: over 1000 μm from infarct) considered in the analysis. All data are reported as the mean (as a proportion, expressed as a percentage) with 95% CI. Main and group effects were considered, and simple effects (sham and infarct geckos at each time point, and the region sampled at each time point) were analyzed while complex effect were disregarded. The data was considered binary because a cell was either proliferating (BrdU + ) or it was not (BrdU−). Residual analyses were performed to assess normality and homogeneity variance for an analysis of variance, including the Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Cramer–von Mises, and Anderson–Darling analyses, and appeared fundamentally normal. To determine whether a statistically significant difference exists across sham gecko, between sham and experimental geckos at each timepoint, and across all three groups at experimental day 100 (control, sham, and experimental day 100), a general linear mixed model was used.

For invasive hemodynamics, statistics and graphs were conducted in Prism9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. Once data were assessed for normality, parametric unpaired t-tests were performed to analyze the data.
