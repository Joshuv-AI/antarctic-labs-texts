Dipanshu Panda † , Jayden Koshy Joe † , Harshith M R † , Swathi Narashiman, Pranay Mathur, Anish Veerakumar, Aniruddh Krishna, Keerthiharan A

Abstract
We present a method for fine-grained control over music generation through inference-time interventions on an autoregressive generative music transformer called MusicGen. Our approach enables timbre transfer, style transfer, and genre fusion by steering the residual stream using weights of linear probes trained on it, or by steering the attention layer activations in a similar manner. We observe that modelling this as a regression task provides improved performance, hypothesizing that the mean-squared-error better preserve meaningful directional information in the activation space. Combined with the global conditioning offered by text prompts in MusicGen, our method provides both global and local control over music generation. Audio samples illustrating our method are available at our demo page .

Related Work

Our contributions extend several prior approaches used to enable style transfer and genre fusion.
Timbretron
[
12

In comparison, our approach only requires learning a light-weight linear representation of the concept of interest.
Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) suggests an efficient method in comparison to full fine-tuning, yet requires several orders of magnitude more parameters than learning linear representations. Further, while methods like fine-tuning are advantageous to learn representations for new concepts, they could hinder performance on other tasks while we can choose to apply inference-time-interventions only when necessary.

III
Problem set-up

Our goal is to identify the layer at which a particular concept of interest is best linearly represented. To do this we apply k-means clustering to the activations and observe the layer-wise external clustering indices - Adjusted Rand Index and Normalized Mutual Information. The Jaccard similarity matrix is then plotted for the layer with the highest Adjusted Rand Index, and we observe significant overlap between the ground-truth labels and clusters obtained, confirming the presence of musical concepts in the MusicGen model.

To observe whether these relationships are linear, we employ linear residual stream probes, further aiming to disentagle concepts rather than learning new relationships.
A linear mapping helps us pool in information about the required genre from the residual stream, and get a good idea of how well the concept in represented in that layer.

Residual Stream Probe:

Given the residual stream activations for each layer at a specified token position
\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}
by running the model on pairs of datasets labelled
D_{0}
having label 0 and
D_{1}
having label 1, the task of the probe is to learn a linear relation
\hat{y}=W^{T}\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}+b
to learn the predictor
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow{0,1}
:

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}})=\begin{cases}1&if\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}\in M(D_{1})\\
0&if\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}\in M(D_{0})\end{cases}

We hypothesize that the layer providing the highest accuracy has the best linear representation of the concept being probed using samples from dataset
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
. We define probes trained on the attention layer activations in a similar manner.

IV
Method

IV-A

Background

We adopt a decoder-only transformer as our generative backbone. Formally, given a vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
and an input token sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{V}^{N}
, the model predicts a distribution over the next token at each position. Concretely, the model outputs a tensor
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times|\mathcal{V}|}
of logits (or probabilities) for each of the
N
output positions. At each layer
\ell
, the model maintains a “residual stream” vector
\mathbf{{h}^{\ell}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(where
d
is the hidden dimensionality). Each transformer layer adds contributions from attention and MLP layers to the residual stream as follows:

\tilde{{h}^{l}_{i}}={h}^{l}_{i}+\texttt{Attn}^{l}({h}^{l}_{1:i})

{h}^{l+1}_{i}=\tilde{h}^{l}_{i}+\texttt{MLP}^{l}(\tilde{h^{l}_{i}})

After the final transformer layer, it is passed through an unembedding matrix which is transformed to a probability distribution over the vocabulary using a softmax activation.

IV-B

Datasets and Models

IV-B
1
Notes, instruments and chord progressions

We utilize the SynTheory dataset, a synthetic dataset of rhythmic and tonal music theory concepts. (
[
15
]
)

We pass audio samples from the notes subset and obtain residual stream activations. This amounts to samples consisting of 92 instruments for the 12 notes of the chromatic scale over 9 octaves. This is used to identify whether pitch classes are represented in the model activations. We use audio samples from the chords subset for a particular root note to observe whether tonal variations like major and minor chords are represented. We use chord progressions belonging to a particular key to determine whether harmonic motifs and cadences are represented. Lastly we use samples from the chord subset of a particular root note and type to determine whether instruments are well distinguished.

IV-B
2
Genre probing

The GTZAN audio dataset contains 1000 tracks, across 10 genres each containing 100 track. Each track is 30 seconds long. We divide each track into 10 second non-overlapping segments to form a dataset with 300 samples per class. The Free Music Archive provides over 100,000 tracks along with rich meta-data across 161 genres. We also utilize further samples from
[
16
]
. We sample 10 second segments of a particular genre to form a dataset of 900 samples per class after combining with samples from GTZAN.
Since our objective involves classification, we attempt training on the following variations:

•

Music belonging to one genre with label as 1 and a uniform sampling of other genres set to 0

•

Music belonging to one genre with label 1 and another genre as 0 to enable steering between these genres

•

A collection of n genres that are one-hot encoded and trained for multi-label classification

In call variations the dataset as balanced with equal number of samples in each class.

IV-C

Extracting Feature Vectors

To identify the steering vector to control generation, we utilize three methods:

IV-C
1
Difference-in-means

In this technique
[
18
]
, we compute the difference between the means of the residual stream activations when run on music belonging to two different classes as described in the previous section. This helps isolate key feature directions that differentiate the two input classes and has been widely used in various contexts. (
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
14
]
7)
Formally:

\mathbf{v_{0}}=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{s\in D_{0}}\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}(x)}

\mathbf{v_{1}}=\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{s\in D_{1}}\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}(x)}

\mathbf{r_{10}}=\mathbf{v_{1}}-\mathbf{v_{0}}

where
N_{0}
is the number of samples in dataset
D_{0}
with label 0 and
N_{1}
are the number of samples in dataset
D_{1}
with label 1, with
a
being a certain pre-determined token position. We choose to save activations of the last token generated.

IV-C
2
Weights of linear probes

Inspired by literature on interpreting neural networks with the use of probes, we train linear classifiers on the residual stream activations to predict the target label. Specifically, for each residual stream vector at a particular layer
\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}
, we predict the label associated with each audio sample using:

\hat{y^{l}_{a}}=W_{l}^{T}\mathbf{h^{l}_{a}}+b^{l}_{a}

We train the probe while keeping the weights of the transformer frozen, using either binary cross-entropy or mean-squared error loss. In effect, the weights of this probe learns a linear direction that best separates the two classes, and hence, we use it to steer generation.

IV-D

Model Intervention

Given the steering vector
\mathbf{r^{l}}
of unit magnitude, we modify the residual stream activations by adding a scaled version to the residual stream prior to the layer that has best linear representations of that concept and scaling the sum such that the norm is maintained.

\mathbf{h^{l}_{i}}\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf{h^{l}_{i}}+\alpha\mathbf{r^{l}}}{1+\alpha}

\alpha
is a scaling factor that determines the strength of the intervention. We provide a comparison by applying the methods described earlier on the activations from the attention heads.

IV-E

Experiments

IV-E
1
Set-up

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method for fusing two music genres, we propose an evaluation framework utilizing the genre understanding capabilities of the
clap-htsat-unfused
model. We utilized the zero-shot audio classification pipeline available via HuggingFace

Our approach involved two steering methods:

•

Residual Steering:
Intervention on the residual streams of each decoder layer of the MusicGen-Melody transformer.

•

Attention Output Steering:
Intervention on the attention outputs for each decoder layer before these are passed into the Position-wise Feedforward Neural Network.

To evaluate the success of genre fusion:

1.

Let
x_{\text{orig}}
be the original audio generated using a prompt from the
source genre
.

2.

Let
x_{\text{steered}}
be the audio obtained after steering the model toward the
target genre
.

3.

We employ the CLAP model in a zero-shot classification setting with the set of genre labels
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}
that includes both the source and target genres.

4.

For each audio sample
x
, we compute:

\text{CLAP}(x,g)\in[0,1]

which denotes the similarity score between the audio and the genre label
g
as interpreted by the CLAP model.

5.

The
target genre score improvement
is then defined as the improvement in the predicted class probability for the target genre for the steered audio.

6.

This indicates that the steered audio is more representative of the target genre than the original and we were able to incorporate elements of the target genre with steering while also retaining elements of the original genre thus, achieving fusion.

This evaluation metric is suitable for several reasons:

•

The CLAP model provides a robust and generalizable embedding space for audio and text.

•

By comparing the class probabilities of the generated audio with the textual genre labels before and after steering, we quantify the semantic shift induced by our intervention.

•

An increase in the target genre score while preserving the source genre score reflects the successful genre fusion rather than full style transfer.

•

This method is much more standardized and robust as compared to qualitative evaluation.

Results

Our experiments show the presence of musical concepts within the MusicGen model due to the significant overlap observed in the clusters obtained and the ground-truth labels through external validation indices. Presence of these concepts motivated approach, which is able to consistently increase the presence of the target class in the music generated. It is observed that linear probes trained using mean-squared error loss instead of cross-entropy show higher performance. This corroborates with results from other previous work (
[
21
]
,
[
9
]
), which suggests that such an intervention is equivalent to one gradient step towards maximizing that the target class is predicted. This could be further justified by the fact that a lack of a non-linearity while dealing with a mean-squared-error loss preserves the latent direction corresponding to the concept of interest as the probe weights better are than when cross-entropy is used.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we achieve fine-grained control over genre fusion and instrument transfer with a lightweight intervention method. We extended the application of techinques from Activation Engineering on Large Language Models and apply it to music, to a scenario where the features do not have clear contrasting samples.
Directions for future work could include identifying reliable methods to determine the steering strength, which in our case was obtained using a hyper-parameter search around the component of the activations along the steering vector. Further insights into phenomena like superposition in the attention layer activations and in the residual stream will aid achieving more generalizable intervention schemas with wider applicability.
