Association of proton pump inhibitor use with clinical outcomes in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Han Ma, Xiaoxia Lyu, Shichun Lu, Xiaolin Wang, Chao Sun, Yusheng Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become an important treatment option for patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, the association between proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use and clinical outcomes remains uncertain, particularly in the setting of short-term peri-treatment exposure. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the association between PPI use and clinical outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICI therapy.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 229 patients with advanced NSCLC who received first-line treatment with either ICI monotherapy or ICI combined with chemotherapy between January 1 and December 31, 2023. Follow-up was censored on December 31, 2024. Clinical data were collected, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, PD-L1 expression, treatment regimen, and PPI use. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were evaluated.ResultsAmong the 229 patients, 131 (57.2%) received PPI during the peri-treatment period. In patients without PPI use, no significant differences in PFS or OS were observed between ICI monotherapy and ICI combined with chemotherapy. However, among patients with PPI exposure, those treated with ICI plus chemotherapy had significantly longer PFS and OS compared with those receiving ICI monotherapy (both P<0.01). In addition, within the ICI monotherapy subgroup, PPI use was associated with significantly shorter PFS and OS. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis demonstrated that this association remained significant after adjustment for baseline confounders. Duration-based analysis further showed that even short-term PPI exposure (≤1 day) was associated with poorer outcomes.ConclusionsPPI use was associated with poorer outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC, particularly among those receiving ICI monotherapy. This association appeared attenuated in patients treated with ICI combined with chemotherapy, suggesting a treatment-dependent interaction rather than a uniform drug effect.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI), proton pump inhibitor (PPI), advanced pulmonary carcinoma

Highlight box

Key findings

• Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was associated with poorer progression-free survival and overall survival, particularly among those receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) monotherapy. This association appeared attenuated in patients treated with ICI combined with chemotherapy, suggesting a treatment-dependent interaction.

What is known and what is new?

• The efficacy of ICIs may be influenced by concomitant medications. PPIs have been proposed to affect immunotherapy outcomes; however, existing evidence remains inconsistent, especially in the context of short-term exposure.

• This real-world study demonstrates that the association between PPI use and survival outcomes differs according to treatment strategy. Specifically, PPI use was associated with worse outcomes in patients receiving ICI monotherapy, whereas this association was not evident in those treated with ICI combined with chemotherapy.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that peri-treatment PPI use may serve as a clinical marker of poorer outcomes, particularly in patients receiving ICI monotherapy. Clinicians should carefully assess the necessity of PPI use during immunotherapy, while recognizing that the observed associations do not establish causality.

• Further prospective, multicenter studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to guide optimal management of concomitant medications during immunotherapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies worldwide and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality (1), accounting for nearly 2 million new cases and approximately 1.76 million deaths annually. Surgical resection remains the primary curative treatment for patients with early-stage disease. However, more than half of patients are diagnosed at locally advanced or metastatic stages, where surgery alone is insufficient (2,3). In addition to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as an important therapeutic option for patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The efficacy of ICIs can be influenced by multiple factors, among which concomitant medications have attracted increasing attention. During cancer treatment, additional drugs are often prescribed to manage adverse effects or comorbidities. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), widely used for acid suppression and gastrointestinal symptoms, have been proposed to affect immunotherapy outcomes in several studies (4-7). However, existing evidence remains inconsistent, and the impact of PPI use, particularly in the setting of short-term peri-treatment exposure, is not fully understood. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the association between PPI use and clinical outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICI therapy, with particular attention to differences according to treatment strategy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0341/rc).

Methods

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital (approval No. 2025164) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Eligible patients received either ICI monotherapy or ICI combined with chemotherapy as first-line treatment between January 1 and December 31, 2023. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between PPI use and clinical outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICI-based therapy. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, histological subtype, tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS), ICI type, treatment regimen, and survival outcomes. PPI use was defined as administration during the peri-treatment period of ICI therapy. In this cohort, most PPI exposures were short-term and primarily prescribed for gastrointestinal symptom management or prophylactic purposes.

Treatment response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from initiation of first-line therapy to disease progression or death from any cause. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. Follow-up was censored on December 31, 2024, with a median follow-up duration of 15 months.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between clinical variables and survival outcomes, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To further address potential confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values <0.1 considered acceptable. In addition, duration-based subgroup analysis was performed to explore the association between short-term PPI exposure and survival outcomes.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 229 patients with advanced NSCLC were included in this study, of whom 131 (57.2%) received PPIs during the peri-immunotherapy period and 98 (42.8%) did not. Baseline characteristics stratified by PPI use are shown in Table 1. The two groups were generally well balanced with respect to age, gender, pathology, ECOG PS, and treatment regimen. The mean age was 67.4±8.7 years, and the majority of patients were male (84.3%). Adenocarcinoma was the predominant histologic subtype (51.5%), and approximately half of the patients received ICI monotherapy (51.5%). Notably, patients in the PPI group had a significantly higher proportion of ever/current smokers compared with non-users (79.4% vs. 59.2%, P=0.001). Corticosteroid use was numerically higher in the PPI group (20.6% vs. 14.3%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.29). Antibiotic use was also comparable between the two groups (17.6% vs. 11.2%, P=0.25). Regarding PPI exposure characteristics, the mean duration of PPI use was 1.22±0.59 days. Among PPI users, the majority (83.2%, n=109) received only a single day of PPI, while 16.8% (n=22) received multiple-day PPI (≥2 days). The most common PPI indications were prophylactic use (78.6%), with therapeutic use accounting for 21.4% of prescriptions.

Multivariable Cox regression analysis

Multivariable Cox regression analysis for the ICI monotherapy group (Table 2) revealed that PPI use remained independently associated with significantly worse PFS (adjusted HR =3.20; 95% CI: 2.00–5.11; P<0.001) and worse OS (adjusted HR =2.52; 95% CI: 1.60–3.97; P<0.001) after adjusting for age, gender, ECOG PS, smoking history, corticosteroid use, and antibiotic use.

Among other covariates, increasing age was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR =1.02 per year; 95% CI: 1.01–1.02; P<0.001) but not with OS (HR =1.01; 95% CI: 0.99–1.03; P=0.35). IPTW-weighted analysis

To further minimize potential confounding by indication, we performed IPTW analysis (Table 3). Before weighting, several covariates showed imbalance between PPI users and non-users, with SMDs exceeding 0.1 for age (SMD =−0.249), ECOG group (SMD =0.170), smoking (SMD =0.153), steroid use (SMD =0.143), and PD‑L1 expression (SMD =0.319). After IPTW weighting, all covariates achieved excellent balance, with SMD values below 0.1 for all variables, indicating successful reduction of confounding. The weighted Cox regression model demonstrated that PPI use remained significantly associated with worse PFS (HR =3.24; 95% CI: 2.00–5.25; P<0.001) and worse OS (HR =2.45; 95% CI: 1.48–4.04; P<0.001), consistent with the primary multivariable analysis. The effective sample sizes after weighting were 39.02 for the non-PPI group and 57.65 for the PPI group.

Dose-response relationship by PPI duration

Given that the majority of PPI users (83.2%) received only a single day of PPI, we stratified PPI use by duration to explore whether the association varied according to exposure length (Table 4). Compared with non‑users, single-day PPI users had significantly worse PFS (median: 5.0 vs. 8.0 months; HR =2.71; 95% CI: 1.76–4.17; P<0.001) and worse OS (median: 11.8 vs. 16.5 months; HR =2.11; 95% CI: 1.34–3.31; P=0.001). Multiple‑day users (≥2 days) showed even stronger associations with PFS (median: 3.0 vs. 8.0 months; HR =4.16; 95% CI: 1.96–8.80; P<0.001) and a numerically but not statistically significant association with OS (HR =2.18; 95% CI: 0.96–4.96; P=0.06), likely due to the small sample size in this subgroup (n=9).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses (Table 5) demonstrated that the negative association between PPI use and PFS was consistently observed across all examined subgroups. The association was significant in both younger and older patients (HR =2.81 and 2.99, respectively), in both males and females (HR =2.87 and 3.05), and in both ever-smokers and never-smokers (HR =2.64 and 4.44). Among patients with ECOG PS 0–1, PPI use was associated with significantly worse PFS (HR =3.40; 95% CI: 2.12–5.46; P<0.001). Similarly, significant associations were observed in patients not receiving corticosteroids (HR =2.79) or antibiotics (HR =3.32). Tests for interaction were not statistically significant for any subgroup (all P>0.05), indicating no evidence of effect modification. Subgroups with small sample sizes (ECOG ≥2, steroid users, antibiotic users; n=14–17) were underpowered for reliable HR estimation.

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our findings (Table 6). The association between PPI use and worse PFS remained significant across all sensitivity analyses. Under an alternative PPI definition (≥2 days as users), the association remained significant (HR =2.43; 95% CI: 1.16–5.11; P=0.01). After excluding patients with early events (PFS <3 months), the HR was 3.76 (95% CI: 2.27–6.22; P<0.001). The association also remained significant after excluding corticosteroid users (HR =3.35; 95% CI: 2.07–5.41; P<0.001) and after excluding antibiotic users (HR =4.16; 95% CI: 2.42–7.15; P<0.001).The E-value for the main analysis was 4.28 (95% CI lower bound: 2.94), indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both PPI use and PFS by a risk ratio exceeding 4.28 to fully explain the observed effect, suggesting that unmeasured confounding is unlikely to account for the findings.

Impact of PPI use on survival outcomes in patients receiving ICI therapy

In the ICI monotherapy cohort, patients receiving PPIs exhibited significantly inferior PFS compared to those without PPI exposure (Figure 1). The Kaplan-Meier curves showed an early and sustained separation between the two groups, with PPI users experiencing rapid disease progression. The median PFS was markedly shorter in the PPI group, and the difference was highly significant (log-rank P<0.001). To further evaluate the interaction between PPI use and treatment strategy, patients were stratified into four groups: no PPI + ICI, no PPI + ICI + chemotherapy, PPI + ICI, and PPI + ICI + chemotherapy. As shown in Figure 1B, patients treated with PPI + ICI demonstrated the worst PFS among all groups, with a steep decline in survival probability within the first 10 months. In contrast, the no PPI + ICI + chemotherapy group achieved the most favorable outcomes, suggesting a potential mitigating effect of chemotherapy on the negative impact of PPI use. Overall, these findings indicate that PPI exposure is associated with reduced efficacy of ICI therapy, particularly in the absence of combination treatment.

Discussion

ICIs have transformed the treatment landscape for advanced NSCLC, yet the overall response rate remains approximately 30% (8-10). Identifying modifiable factors that influence ICI efficacy has therefore become a research priority. Concomitant medications represent one such factor, and PPIs have attracted particular attention due to their potential to modulate the gut microbiome (11-13). However, existing evidence remains inconsistent, and the clinical significance of short-term peri-treatment PPI exposure—a common but understudied real-world scenario—has not been fully elucidated. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the association between short-term PPI use and ICI outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC.

The most striking observation of this study is that even a single day of PPI exposure was associated with significantly worse clinical outcomes. From a pharmacological perspective, such minimal exposure is highly unlikely to exert a sustained biological effect on systemic anti-tumor immunity (14,15). This challenges a direct causal interpretation and instead supports an alternative explanation: PPI use in this setting should be interpreted as a surrogate marker of underlying clinical vulnerability rather than a causal factor. Patients requiring peri-treatment PPI support, regardless of duration, are more likely to have higher symptom burden, more frequent corticosteroid use, greater comorbidity burden, or poorer overall functional status—all well-established determinants of inferior outcomes in patients receiving ICIs (16-18). Therefore, PPI prescription may function as a clinically observable proxy reflecting a more fragile physiological and immunological state that is not fully captured by conventional variables.

A major concern in observational studies is residual confounding, particularly confounding by indication. Although we incorporated key variables such as corticosteroid use, smoking history, and ECOG PS into multivariable models and IPTW analyses, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded (19). Importantly, these routinely collected variables may not fully capture the complexity of patients’ clinical condition, including systemic inflammation, nutritional status, or occult disease burden. Therefore, the observed association between PPI use and worse outcomes is likely influenced, at least in part, by unmeasured dimensions of clinical vulnerability (20).

To address this concern, we applied a multi-layered analytical strategy. Multivariable Cox regression and IPTW yielded consistent results, and covariate balance was substantially improved after weighting. In addition, multiple sensitivity analyses—including alternative exposure definitions and exclusion of early events, corticosteroid users, and antibiotic users—produced stable and directionally consistent estimates. The calculated E-value further indicated that an unmeasured confounder would need to have a strong association with both PPI use and survival outcomes to fully explain the observed effect. Together, these findings suggest that the association is unlikely to be entirely attributable to measured or unmeasured confounding (21-23).

An important finding of this study is the treatment-dependent nature of the association. The negative association between PPI use and survival outcomes was more pronounced in patients receiving ICI monotherapy and attenuated in those receiving ICI combined with chemotherapy. This difference may be explained by the distinct mechanisms of action of these treatment strategies. ICI monotherapy relies primarily on immune system activation to mediate tumor control and is therefore more sensitive to underlying immune competence. In contrast, combination chemotherapy exerts anti-tumor effects through both cytotoxic mechanisms and immunogenic cell death, which may partially compensate for impaired immune function. As a result, the prognostic signal associated with PPI use may be masked or attenuated in the context of combination therapy (24-27).

Notably, in the ICI plus chemotherapy group, PPI use did not appear to be associated with worse outcomes and may even show a seemingly protective effect. This finding should be interpreted with caution and is unlikely to reflect a true beneficial effect of PPIs. Instead, it may represent treatment selection bias or the dominant impact of chemotherapy overriding underlying differences in patient vulnerability. This observation further supports the interpretation that PPI use acts as a marker of baseline clinical condition rather than a direct determinant of treatment efficacy.

The observed dose-response relationship, with progressively increasing HRs from no use to single-day and multi-day PPI exposure, further supports the robustness of the association. However, rather than indicating a direct biological gradient, this pattern is more consistent with increasing degrees of underlying clinical vulnerability captured by PPI exposure. Even a single prescription may reflect subtle but clinically meaningful differences in patient status that are not otherwise measurable.

Our findings are consistent with several prior studies reporting an association between PPI use and reduced efficacy of ICIs, but extend existing knowledge in important ways. First, this study specifically focuses on short-term peri-treatment PPI exposure, a clinically common but previously underexplored scenario. Second, it highlights the treatment-dependent nature of this association, providing insight into how different therapeutic strategies may interact with patient-related factors. Third, the incorporation of E-value analysis provides a quantitative assessment of robustness to unmeasured confounding, strengthening the credibility of the findings.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that PPI use should be interpreted not merely as a concomitant medication but as a potential clinical signal of increased vulnerability. When prescribing PPIs in patients undergoing ICI therapy, clinicians should be aware that this may reflect a higher-risk patient population requiring closer monitoring and more comprehensive supportive care. However, these results do not support withholding PPIs when they are clearly indicated. Rather, the emphasis should be placed on appropriate use, careful documentation of indication and duration, and heightened clinical vigilance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study and is therefore subject to inherent selection bias. Second, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Third, the extremely short duration of PPI exposure limits the biological plausibility of causal inference, and thus the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Fourth, PD-L1 expression data were incomplete, which may introduce additional confounding. Finally, subgroup analyses were limited by sample size and should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

Short-term PPI use is associated with poorer outcomes in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICI therapy, particularly in those treated with ICI monotherapy. However, this association is unlikely to reflect a direct causal effect. Instead, PPI use appears to serve as a clinically accessible marker of underlying patient vulnerability. Recognizing this signal may help identify high-risk patients who require closer monitoring and more individualized management. Future prospective studies are needed to further clarify the relationship between concomitant medications, patient condition, and immunotherapy outcomes.
