Functional valorisation of forest derived lignocellulosic waste with high sorption rate and pH stability
Authors: Anna Przybylska-Balcerek, Przemysław Bartczak, Joanna Zembrzuska, Adam Piasecki, Jakub Frankowski, Kinga Stuper-Szablewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLignocellulosic residues derived from forest and agricultural systems represent an abundant and underutilised biomass stream with potential applications in water treatment. In this study, a comparative screening of selected lignocellulosic materials (oak sawdust, glans peduncles, barley straw, camelina straw, and flax/hemp dust) was performed for the removal of bisphenol A (BPA) from aqueous solutions, with activated carbon used as a reference. All investigated materials exhibited rapid adsorption, with equilibrium uptake of 90–95% achieved within 3 min and equilibrium approached within ~ 30 min under the studied conditions. Adsorption kinetics were well described by the pseudo-second-order model (R² = 0.996–0.999), although this is interpreted as a descriptive fit rather than direct evidence of chemisorption. Oak sawdust and glans peduncles showed the most stable performance across the tested pH range (3–11). The study has a screening character and was conducted at elevated BPA concentrations (20–500 mg/L) to enable kinetic analysis. Therefore, the results provide a comparative and mechanistic basis for further investigation rather than direct evidence of environmental or industrial applicability. The pHpzc analysis suggests that sorption is not controlled solely by electrostatic interactions; instead, specific interactions related to lignin-rich domains—such as π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects—play a substantial role. The results highlight the importance of structural and chemical characteristics of woody biomass in determining functional performance under aqueous exposure and emphasise the potential of oak-derived residues as stable, sustainable materials contributing to the valorisation of forest resources.Keywords: Bisphenol A, Pollution, Water, Adsorption, Environment, Lignocellulosic adsorbent waste materialSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Water pollution constitutes a significant environmental challenge, particularly due to the presence of persistent synthetic contaminants. One of the most concerning compounds is Bisphenol A (BPA; 2,2-bis(4-hydroxyphenyl)propane), produced globally at approximately 3.5 million tons per year and widely used in the manufacture of polycarbonates and epoxy resins. BPA enters aquatic environments during production processes, through leaching from plastic products, and as a result of insufficient wastewater treatment1–4. Owing to its structure containing two phenolic rings, BPA exhibits estrogen-like activity and disrupts endocrine function even at low concentrations. Biomonitoring studies confirm widespread human exposure and its frequent detection in various environmental compartments5–8.

In response to increasing water contamination, adsorption has emerged as a promising removal technology due to its high efficiency, operational simplicity, and limited formation of harmful by-products9,10. The process involves electrostatic and non-electrostatic interactions between the adsorbate and the sorbent surface11–14. In recent years, natural lignocellulosic materials—often derived as by-products of agricultural and industrial processes—have attracted considerable attention as low-cost, biodegradable, and widely available adsorbents15–18. Their appeal stems not only from their abundance and renewability, but also from their complex chemical structure, which consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These components contain numerous functional groups (such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phenolic groups) that can interact with pollutant molecules through various mechanisms, including hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions17,18. Moreover, the inherently porous structure of many lignocellulosic materials contributes to a relatively high surface area, enhancing their adsorption capacity. Commonly studied materials include sawdust, rice husk, straw, coconut fibers, and various wood-derived residues19,20. In many cases, these materials are used both in their raw form and after physical or chemical modifications (e.g., thermal activation, acid or alkaline treatment), which aim to increase the number of available active sites and improve adsorption performance. The effectiveness of such adsorbents for BPA removal has been evaluated under a wide range of experimental conditions. This variability makes direct comparison between studies difficult and limits the ability to reliably assess the relative performance of different materials.

Consequently, a significant research gap still exists in the form of a lack of systematic comparative studies conducted under identical and well-controlled experimental conditions. In particular, there is a notable scarcity of consistent data on adsorption kinetics for different lignocellulosic materials evaluated in parallel. Most studies focus primarily on maximum adsorption capacity, while the rate of adsorption—critical from a practical application perspective—remains insufficiently explored in a comparable framework. As a result, it is still unclear which low-cost biomaterials are capable of the most rapid removal of BPA under comparable conditions.

Literature reports consistently indicate that BPA adsorption efficiency is strongly influenced by several process parameters. Among them, solution pH plays a critical role, as it affects both the ionization state of BPA and the surface charge of the adsorbent; most studies are conducted within a pH range of 3–921–25. Contact time is another important factor, determining the attainment of adsorption equilibrium, and may vary from several minutes to several hours depending on the material properties. Adsorbent dosage directly influences the availability of active sites, while the initial pollutant concentration governs the driving force of mass transfer. Therefore, careful consideration and control of these parameters are essential for the proper evaluation and comparison of lignocellulosic materials in BPA removal applications.

In this context, the present study aims to address this gap by performing a controlled screening of selected, previously untested or underexplored lignocellulosic biomaterials of regional origin. The investigated sorbents include low-cost agricultural and industrial residues such as oak sawdust, acorn peduncles, straw from Camelina sativa and barley, as well as by-products from flax and hemp processing. These materials represent locally available, niche waste streams whose adsorption potential toward BPA has not yet been systematically evaluated.

The objective of this work is to conduct a process-oriented comparative analysis of these materials under uniform experimental conditions, with particular emphasis on the influence of key operational parameters (contact time, pH, adsorbent dose) and the identification of materials exhibiting the fastest adsorption performance. Rather than providing a full adsorption characterization (e.g., isotherm modeling), this study focuses on a practical screening approach aimed at identifying efficient, low-cost sorbents with high potential for rapid BPA removal from aqueous systems.

This study does not aim to provide full adsorption characterisation (e.g., isotherm modelling), but rather a process-oriented comparison.

In the first phase of the adsorption experiments, a series of model BPA solutions were prepared at varying concentrations (20, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, and 500 mg/L). Each solution was transferred into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask, and a fixed quantity of adsorbent (200 mg) was added. The flasks were then agitated using a laboratory shaker for selected time intervals (1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min). After shaking, the mixtures were filtered under vacuum. The resulting filtrates were analyzed chromatographically to assess the adsorption performance. The applied BPA concentration range (20–500 mg/L) was selected as a model system to ensure sufficient kinetic resolution and reliable comparison of adsorption rate parameters among the investigated sorbents. Higher initial concentrations allow clearer determination of qt and qe values over time and reduce the relative impact of analytical uncertainty on kinetic modeling. Although these concentrations exceed typical environmental levels, the results provide a mechanistic and comparative basis for future studies conducted at lower, environmentally relevant concentrations.

In the second set of experiments, the focus was on evaluating the effect of adsorbent dosage. A model BPA solution with a fixed concentration of 25 mg/L was used. The solutions, placed in 250 mL flasks, were treated with varying adsorbent amounts (50, 100, 300, and 500 mg) and shaken for 3 min. The remaining steps, including filtration and analysis, followed the same procedure as in the first phase.

For the pH optimization trials, the model BPA solution was adjusted to specific pH levels by adding either sodium hydroxide or hydrochloric acid. A pH meter was used to monitor and adjust the pH precisely. Once the desired pH was achieved, 200 mg of adsorbent was introduced into a 25 mg/L BPA solution. The subsequent steps of mixing, filtration, and chromatographic analysis were conducted as previously described. The contact time of 3 min was selected based on preliminary kinetic studies, which demonstrated that for all investigated adsorbents more than 90–95% of the equilibrium adsorption capacity (observed at 30 min) was achieved within this period. This indicates that after 3 min the system was already very close to the adsorption plateau, and further extension of contact time did not result in significant changes in the amount of BPA removed. Therefore, the application of a short contact time allowed for reliable comparison of the effects of adsorbent dose and pH under near-equilibrium conditions, while also reflecting the potential for rapid BPA removal in practical treatment systems. The selected pH values (3,7, and 11) were chosen to represent acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions that may occur in industrial effluents, treated wastewater, and technological process waters. Evaluating adsorption performance across this broad pH range allows assessment of sorbent stability and removal efficiency under variable operational conditions, as well as examination of the influence of surface charge and BPA speciation on the adsorption mechanism.

The use of a short contact time was based on preliminary kinetic experiments, which showed that 90–95% of equilibrium adsorption (reached at 30 min) occurred within the first 3 min for all adsorbents. Therefore: the system operated under near-equilibrium conditions; short contact time enabled comparative evaluation of rapid adsorption performance; the approach reflects realistic conditions for fast treatment systems. Importantly, this study focuses on initial adsorption kinetics and process efficiency, not equilibrium thermodynamics.

All adsorption experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD).

Determination of pHpzc

The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of the investigated sorbents was determined using the pH-drift method. This method is based on the assumption that after introducing the sorbent into an electrolyte solution with a defined initial pH (pH₀), protonation/deprotonation of functional groups on the material surface occurs, resulting in a change of pH to a final value (pHf). The pHpzc value was defined as the pH at which no change in the solution pH is observed (ΔpH = 0), i.e., pHf = pH₀.

A 0.01 M NaCl solution was used as the background electrolyte to maintain constant ionic strength. A volume of 50 mL of NaCl solution was added to a series of flasks, and the initial pH (pH₀) was adjusted within the range of 2–12 using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions. Subsequently, 0.10–0.20 g of sorbent was added, the vessels were tightly sealed, and the suspensions were shaken for 24 h at 20 ± 1 °C.

After equilibrium was reached, the final pH (pHf) was measured using a calibrated pH meter. The pH difference was calculated according to the equation: ΔpH = pHf − pH₀, and a plot of ΔpH versus pH₀ was constructed. The pHpzc value was determined as the point of intersection of the curve with the line ΔpH = 0.

Chromatographic analysis

Analytical measurements were conducted using an Acquity H-Class UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector. Separation of analytes was achieved on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Waters, Dublin, Ireland). A gradient elution method was applied, employing two mobile phases: (A) acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid, and (B) an aqueous solution of 1% formic acid (pH = 2). Quantification of phenolic compounds was performed using an internal standard approach. Detection was carried out at wavelengths of 280 nm and 320 nm. Final results were expressed in milligrams per gram (mg/L). Compound identification was confirmed by comparing retention times with those of reference standards, followed by spiking the samples with known quantities of the standards and reanalyzing them. The method’s detection limit was 1 µg/L26–28.

Adsorption–desorption cycles and structural stability assessment (SEM-coupled regeneration study)

The reusability, regeneration efficiency, and structural stability of the investigated adsorbents were evaluated through consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles combined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis. The adsorption step was carried out under optimized conditions (initial BPA concentration: 25 mg/L; adsorbent mass: 200 mg; solution volume: 50 mL; pH 7; temperature: 20 °C; contact time: 30 min). After adsorption, the suspensions were vacuum-filtered, and the loaded sorbents were gently rinsed with distilled methanol and water to remove unbound BPA.

Desorption was performed using 20 mL of methanol as the regeneration solvent. The BPA-loaded sorbent was agitated for 30 min at 20 °C. After filtration, the desorption solution was analyzed chromatographically to quantify the released BPA. Desorption efficiency (%) was calculated as the ratio of the desorbed amount to the previously adsorbed amount of BPA.

Following desorption, the materials were washed with distilled water to remove residual solvent and dried at 60 °C to constant mass. The regenerated sorbents were reused in subsequent adsorption cycles under identical experimental conditions. The adsorption–desorption procedure was repeated for three consecutive cycles to evaluate the stability of adsorption performance.

To assess potential morphological changes and structural degradation associated with repeated use, representative samples of each sorbent were subjected to SEM analysis before the first adsorption cycle and after the third regeneration cycle. The samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer and examined using a scanning electron microscope. SEM observations focused on surface morphology, pore structure integrity, fiber fragmentation, and possible pore blockage. Correlation of morphological changes with variations in adsorption capacity allowed evaluation of whether performance loss, if observed, was associated with structural deterioration, surface site modification, or pore accessibility changes.

Regeneration efficiency (%) after each cycle was calculated as the ratio of adsorption capacity in a given cycle to that of the fresh material.

Statistical differences among the tested adsorbents and experimental conditions (adsorbent mass and pH) were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3.

Results and discussions

It should be noted that the present Results and Discussion section focuses on process-oriented observations and comparative analysis under controlled conditions. Due to the absence of equilibrium isotherm modeling, the interpretation of adsorption performance is inherently limited, and conclusions regarding maximum capacity or surface heterogeneity cannot be drawn.

The aim of this work was to equilibrate screening of waste biomaterials with high BPA removal and identify process parameters (time/dose/pH) that enable rapid purification. Therefore, the focus was on kinetics and the effect of pH/dose, but not on the general set of equilibria required for isotherm configuration (e.g., a C0 series with confirmed equilibrium for each point, a wide range of Ce).

This study introduces a novel approach to water purification that uses natural sorbents derived from lignocellulosic waste materials to sorb bisphenol A (BPA) from water. The research aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of these sorbents in removing BPA. Laboratory experiments were conducted to assess the effects of pH, contact time, and BPA concentration on sorption efficiency. A review of the existing literature indicates that modified adsorbents and composite materials have shown promising results in BPA removal from water. Activated carbon was used as a reference material in the experiments. The findings revealed that at BPA concentrations of 20, 31.25, and 62.5 mg/L, the adsorption efficiency reached ≥ 99.5% (bordering on LOQ) throughout the testing period. When the BPA concentration was increased to 125 mg/L, the highest adsorption efficiency (approximately 99%) was observed between 1 and 5 min, with equilibrium being reached after 10 min and remaining stable at 98.5% for up to 30 min.

Effect of contact time

For all investigated adsorbents, a rapid adsorption phase was observed at the initial stage of the process. A sharp increase in adsorption capacity (qt) occurred within the first 5–20 min, followed by a gradual decrease in the adsorption rate until equilibrium was reached. In most cases, adsorption equilibrium was established within approximately 30 min, and extending the contact time to 180 min did not result in significant changes in qe values.

This behavior indicates that, at the beginning of the process, adsorption is governed primarily by the occupation of readily available active sites located on the external surface of the adsorbents. As these sites become progressively saturated, the adsorption rate decreases, and the process approaches equilibrium. The relatively short equilibrium time suggests that intraparticle diffusion is not the dominant rate-limiting step under the studied conditions.

Effect of initial BPA concentration

An increase in the initial BPA concentration resulted in a proportional increase in the equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe) for all investigated materials. This trend reflects the higher driving force for mass transfer at elevated concentrations, which enhances the probability of interaction between BPA molecules and available surface sites.

However, it should be emphasized that the observed qe values do not represent the maximum adsorption capacity of the materials, as equilibrium isotherm analysis was not performed. Therefore, the reported values should be interpreted as apparent adsorption capacities under the specific experimental conditions rather than intrinsic material properties.

Similar concentration-dependent trends have been widely reported for BPA adsorption on lignocellulosic and carbon-based materials, where increasing C₀ enhances sorption until saturation effects become dominant.

Comparison of adsorbents

All investigated lignocellulosic materials exhibited comparable adsorption performance under the studied conditions. However, this comparison should be interpreted with caution, as it is based on single-point equilibrium data rather than full isotherm characterization. The present comparison reflects relative adsorption efficiency within the applied concentration range rather than a definitive ranking of material performance. The similarity in adsorption behavior suggests that common structural features of lignocellulosic biomass, such as lignin and oxygen-containing functional groups, may govern BPA uptake under the tested conditions.

Possible adsorption mechanism

The molecular structure of BPA, containing aromatic rings and hydroxyl groups, facilitates interactions with lignocellulosic surfaces. The adsorption mechanism may involve π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of BPA and lignin structures, hydrogen bonding with hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups, as well as hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces.

The rapid attainment of equilibrium and the shape of the kinetic curves suggest that the process is predominantly governed by surface adsorption with a significant contribution of physical interactions.

A comparison of the correlation coefficients (R²) indicates that the PSO model provides a substantially better fit to the experimental data than the PFO model. For the PFO model, R² values ranged from 0.648 to 0.982, and significant discrepancies were observed between the calculated and experimental equilibrium adsorption capacities. These results suggest that the PFO model is not suitable for describing BPA adsorption onto the investigated materials under the studied conditions.

In contrast, the PSO model yielded consistently high R² values (0.996–0.999), with calculated equilibrium capacities closely matching the experimental data. This indicates that the PSO model provides an accurate mathematical description of the adsorption kinetics.

However, it should be emphasized that high correlation coefficients alone do not provide direct evidence of the adsorption mechanism. The pseudo-second-order model is known to successfully describe a wide range of adsorption systems, irrespective of whether the process is governed by physical or chemical interactions. Therefore, in the present study, the PSO model should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive kinetic model rather than a mechanistic one.

The rate constants (k₂) obtained from the PSO model decreased with increasing initial BPA concentration, which may be attributed to progressive occupation of available adsorption sites and changes in apparent rate parameters under higher surface coverage conditions. The calculated initial adsorption rate (h) increased with increasing concentration, confirming that the driving force of mass transfer plays a key role in the early stages of adsorption.

Overall, the kinetic analysis indicates that BPA adsorption onto the investigated lignocellulosic materials is rapid and strongly dependent on the availability of surface sites. Nevertheless, without complementary spectroscopic or calorimetric evidence, no definitive conclusions regarding the nature of the adsorption mechanism (e.g., chemisorption vs. physisorption) can be drawn.

Rate constants and initial adsorption rate

The rate constants for the PFO model (k₁) varied considerably (0.039–0.498 min⁻¹), reflecting differences among adsorbents and experimental conditions. However, due to the relatively poor fit of this model, these parameters have limited mechanistic significance.

For the PSO model, the rate constants (k₂) ranged from 0.002 to 0.052 g/mg·min. A general trend of decreasing k₂ values with increasing initial BPA concentration was observed. This behavior may be attributed to higher surface coverage at elevated concentrations, resulting in a relative decrease in the apparent rate constant due to partial saturation of active sites.

The initial adsorption rate (h = k2qe2) was markedly higher at 500 mg/L than at 250 mg/L for all adsorbents, confirming that an increase in the initial concentration enhances the driving force of the process. Among the studied materials, barley straw and activated carbon exhibited particularly high h values, indicating rapid BPA uptake during the initial stage of adsorption.

Comparison of adsorbent performance

All lignocellulosic materials exhibited kinetic behavior comparable to that of activated carbon. Very high R² values (≥ 0.997) for oak sawdust, camelina straw, barley straw, and glans peduncles confirm the suitability of the PSO model for describing BPA adsorption onto these biosorbents.

Although the pseudo-first-order model provided weaker fits, especially for linen and hemp dust, the PSO model consistently showed excellent agreement with the experimental data (R² ≥ 0.996). This may indicate a more heterogeneous surface structure in some materials, yet without significantly affecting the overall adsorption performance.

The similarity in equilibrium adsorption capacities and kinetic parameters between agricultural by-products and activated carbon highlights the strong potential of low-cost lignocellulosic materials as effective alternatives to conventional commercial adsorbents.

Mechanistic considerations

Although the pseudo-second-order model is often associated with chemisorption in the literature, such an interpretation should be treated with caution. In the present system, the adsorption of BPA is more likely governed by a combination of non-covalent interactions, including π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects.

The rapid attainment of equilibrium and weak dependence on pH further support the contribution of non-electrostatic interactions. However, these conclusions remain indirect, as no direct spectroscopic evidence of specific surface interactions was obtained. Therefore, the proposed mechanism should be considered hypothetical and requires further validation.

In contrast, the pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetics model provided significantly better results. The adsorption rate constant (k2) and equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe) were determined from the t/qt vs. time relationship (Fig. 3). The PSO model yielded an excellent fit of the experimental data to the theoretical values, as evidenced by high correlation coefficients (r²) ranging from 0.996 to 0.999, which were close to unity for all tested adsorbents (Table 4). Notably, the experimentally measured BPA adsorption quantities were consistent with those calculated using the PSO model. Additionally, a trend was observed where, as the concentration of the adsorbate increased, the value of the rate constant k2 decreased. This suggests the gradual saturation of the active sites on the surface of the adsorbents.

In addition, the initial adsorption rate constant (h) was calculated. The highest values of h were observed for barley straw (122.54-129.58 mg/g∙min), regardless of the adsorbate concentration. This result directly indicates that, among the tested adsorbents, barley straw exhibited the fastest adsorption process.

The experimental data for BPA adsorption on the selected adsorbents were best described by the pseudo-second-order kinetics model. PSO better describes the data, suggesting that the rate of the process depends on the availability of active sites; the mechanism may involve both physical and specific interactions (H-bonding/π–π). Similar findings have been reported by other researchers conducting BPA adsorption studies (Table 5).

Correlation between SEM analysis and PSO kinetic modeling

The morphological observations obtained by SEM are in strong agreement with the kinetic modeling results of BPA adsorption (Table 6). For all investigated adsorbents, the pseudo-second-order (PSO) model provided the best fit to the experimental data, with very high coefficients of determination (R² = 0.996–0.999). The excellent agreement with the PSO model indicates that the adsorption rate is primarily governed by the availability of active surface sites and suggests that surface interactions play a dominant role in the rate-limiting step.

In the case of activated carbon, SEM images revealed a highly developed hierarchical porous structure, consisting of interconnected macro- and mesopores. Such morphology facilitates rapid mass transfer and explains the high initial adsorption rate (h) obtained from the PSO model. However, partial pore blockage and slight structural degradation observed after successive regeneration cycles may reduce the effective surface area and contribute to changes in the apparent rate constant (k₂).

For lignocellulosic materials, particularly oak sawdust, SEM analysis demonstrated a well-preserved cellular architecture with clearly defined vascular channels and intercellular voids. These structural features likely promote the diffusion of BPA molecules into the internal structure of the material, ensuring effective utilization of adsorption sites. Importantly, the structural stability of oak sawdust after three regeneration cycles correlates with consistently high qe values and excellent PSO fitting parameters, indicating sustained accessibility of active sites and structural integrity.

In contrast, camelina and barley straw exhibited progressive structural degradation and fiber fragmentation after repeated regeneration. Such morphological changes may influence the distribution and accessibility of adsorption sites, as well as local diffusion resistance. Nevertheless, the consistently high R² values for the PSO model suggest that, under the studied conditions, the adsorption process remains predominantly controlled by surface reactions rather than intraparticle diffusion.

Although the PSO model is often associated with chemisorption mechanisms, the present system likely involves a combination of physical and specific interactions. ππ.

The adsorption of BPA onto lignocellulosic materials may involve several types of interactions, including π–π interactions with lignin domains, hydrogen bonding with hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, and hydrophobic effects. These interactions are consistent with the chemical structure of both BPA and lignocellulosic biomass.

However, it should be clearly emphasized that these mechanisms are proposed as plausible hypotheses rather than confirmed pathways. No direct spectroscopic or surface characterization techniques (e.g., FTIR, BET, XPS) were employed in this study to verify the nature of these interactions. Therefore, the mechanistic interpretation remains indirect and should be treated with caution.

The weak dependence of adsorption efficiency on pH suggests that non-electrostatic interactions may contribute significantly to the overall adsorption process. Nevertheless, this observation alone is not sufficient to unambiguously identify the dominant mechanism.

SEM analysis was used to evaluate the morphological characteristics and structural stability of the investigated materials before and after adsorption–desorption cycles. The obtained images provide qualitative information on surface structure, pore accessibility, and possible structural degradation.

It should be noted that SEM does not provide direct information on adsorption mechanisms or rate-limiting steps. Therefore, the SEM observations are not used here as evidence of specific adsorption mechanisms or kinetic control.

Instead, SEM results are interpreted in a complementary manner, providing structural context for the observed adsorption performance. For example, the presence of accessible channels and preserved morphology may facilitate mass transfer, while structural degradation may contribute to reduced adsorption efficiency upon reuse. However, these relationships should be considered indicative rather than conclusive.

The decrease in adsorption efficiency observed over successive adsorption–desorption cycles was closely associated with morphological changes in the sorbent surfaces revealed by SEM analysis. In the case of materials exhibiting high performance stability (e.g., oak sawdust and glans peduncles), SEM images obtained after the third regeneration cycle did not indicate significant degradation of the cellular structure or noticeable pore collapse. The continuity of conductive channels and the accessibility of the specific surface area were preserved, suggesting that the active sites remained largely available for BPA molecules.

In contrast, for sorbents that showed a more pronounced decline in adsorption capacity (e.g., barley straw and flax/hemp dust), partial fiber fragmentation, flattening of the porous structure, and local pore blockage were observed. These effects were likely caused by incomplete BPA desorption or reorganization of the lignocellulosic structure under cyclic wetting and drying conditions. Such phenomena may have led to reduced accessibility of active sites and increased diffusion resistance in subsequent cycles.

The obtained results indicate that maintaining high sorption efficiency is directly linked to the morphological stability of the material and the resistance of its structure to regeneration processes, confirming the complementarity of kinetic and microscopic analyses in evaluating the application potential of the investigated biosorbents.

The Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion model revealed a multi-stage adsorption process. The initial linear region corresponds to rapid surface adsorption and boundary layer diffusion, followed by a second stage associated with slower mass transfer processes.

Importantly, none of the plots passed through the origin (C ≠ 0), indicating that intraparticle diffusion was not the sole rate-controlling step. In several cases, negative intercept values were observed. Such behavior has been reported in systems characterized by very rapid adsorption and may reflect deviations from ideal model assumptions, including limited applicability of the linear approximation at short contact times.

Therefore, the Weber–Morris model was used here primarily as a qualitative tool to identify different stages of mass transfer rather than to derive physically rigorous parameters.

The highest and most stable adsorption efficiency, independent of environmental pH, was observed for oak sawdust and glans peduncles, for which BPA removal reached ≥ 99.5% (bordering on LOQ) across the entire investigated pH range. This result correlates with their pHpzc values (6.5 ± 0.3 and 4.5 ± 0.4, respectively), indicating that under neutral and alkaline conditions the surface of these materials is negatively charged, yet this does not limit sorption efficiency. This may suggest a dominant contribution of non-electrostatic interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of BPA and the lignin structure of the biomass.

High, although slightly lower, efficiency (approximately 80%) was observed for camelina straw and activated carbon, which showed greater sensitivity to pH changes. A different trend was found for barley straw and flax/hemp dust (pHpzc 4.8 ± 0.4 and 3.7 ± 0.3, respectively). Under neutral and alkaline conditions, these materials achieved approximately 70% removal efficiency, whereas under acidic conditions the adsorption efficiency decreased significantly to about 10%. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) indicated statistically significant differences between pH levels for these sorbents (p < 0.05), which was confirmed by Tukey’s post hoc test—the efficiency at pH 3 was significantly lower than at pH 7 and 11 (p < 0.05). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed for oak sawdust and glans peduncles within the tested pH range (p > 0.05), confirming their stable sorption properties.

The obtained results should be interpreted in the context of the acid–base properties of BPA (pKa ≈ 9.56). Under acidic and neutral conditions, BPA predominantly exists in its non-dissociated form, whereas above pH 8–9 it undergoes deprotonation, forming mono- or divalent anions. The decrease in adsorption efficiency under alkaline conditions reported in numerous literature sources is commonly attributed to electrostatic repulsion Chang between the negatively charged sorbent surface and the anionic form of BPA45–47. Wang and Xiao48 and Chang et al.49 demonstrated reduced BPA adsorption under alkaline conditions for activated carbons, while Bohdziewicz and Liszczyk50 observed a marked decrease in efficiency above pH 10. In contrast, Zheng et al.51 reported optimal adsorption in the pH range 4–7 for organosilicates, associating this with the predominance of the molecular form of BPA.

In the present study, the absence of a clear decline in efficiency under alkaline conditions for most materials suggests that, in addition to electrostatic interactions, specific mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions play a significant role. The particularly high and stable performance of oak sawdust and glans peduncles indicates their considerable application potential in water treatment processes, especially under variable environmental conditions where resistance to pH fluctuations is an important technological parameter.

Comparative analysis of material properties and adsorption performance

A more in-depth comparison of the investigated lignocellulosic materials indicates that their similar adsorption performance towards BPA is likely governed by shared structural and chemical characteristics. All studied sorbents are composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which determine both the surface chemistry and the interaction mechanisms with organic pollutants52–54.

Despite differences in origin, the quantitative composition of the materials shows a relatively comparable lignin content (approximately 14–25%), with the highest value observed for oak sawdust. Given the aromatic nature of lignin, this component plays a crucial role in BPA adsorption through π–π interactions between the phenolic rings of BPA and the aromatic domains of the sorbent. Therefore, the comparable lignin fraction across the materials may explain the similar adsorption capacities observed.

In addition, all materials possess a similar set of surface functional groups, primarily hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), and phenolic groups. These functionalities enable hydrogen bonding and contribute to non-electrostatic interactions, consistent with the observed weak pH dependence of adsorption efficiency. This suggests that specific interactions, rather than purely electrostatic effects, dominate the adsorption mechanism55–57.

Morphological observations further support this interpretation. SEM analysis revealed that the investigated materials exhibit well-developed surface structures with accessible channels and voids that facilitate rapid diffusion of BPA molecules to active sites. Although detailed textural parameters (e.g., BET surface area or pore size distribution) were not determined, the observed structural similarities suggest comparable accessibility of adsorption sites.

Overall, the similar adsorption performance of the tested lignocellulosic materials can be attributed to three main shared features: the presence of lignin, which enables π–π interactions; a common set of functional groups responsible for hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions; and a porous structure that ensures efficient mass transfer. Differences in performance stability, particularly the superior behavior of oak-derived materials, are likely related to their higher lignin content and greater structural integrity under experimental conditions. The comparison of the investigated materials demonstrated that all lignocellulosic biosorbents exhibited similarly high BPA adsorption efficiency and very rapid kinetics, reaching near-equilibrium within a short time. However, oak sawdust and glans peduncles clearly stood out, as they combined high removal efficiency with the greatest stability across a wide pH range. In contrast, barley straw showed the highest initial adsorption rate, indicating its advantage in applications requiring very rapid pollutant removal. Considering all evaluated criteria—efficiency, kinetics, and stability—oak sawdust can be identified as the material with the most favorable and well-balanced overall performance among the tested sorbents.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that lignocellulosic waste materials can rapidly remove BPA from aqueous solutions under controlled laboratory conditions. Among the tested materials, oak sawdust and glans peduncles showed the most stable performance across varying pH conditions.

However, due to the screening nature of the study and the use of elevated BPA concentrations, the results should be interpreted as preliminary. The findings provide a basis for further investigation rather than a complete evaluation of practical applicability.
