When Domain Pretraining Interferes with Instruction Alignment: An Empirical Study of Adapter Merging in Medical LLMs
Junyi Zou

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can exhibit surprising adapter interference when combining domain adaptation and instruction alignment in safety-critical settings. We study a 14B base model trained with a two-stage LoRA pipeline: (i) domain-oriented pre-training (PT/DOPT) for medical knowledge injection and (ii) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for instruction following on medical QA. We then form a weighted adapter merge by linearly combining PT and SFT LoRA deltas before exporting a single merged checkpoint for inference. We find that adding PT signal can reactivate latent “thinking” behavior and systematically shift the output distribution even when training/evaluation templates attempt to disable chain-of-thought. Under a fixed generation evaluation (template qwen3_nothink , Temp=0.6, Top- p p =0.8), pure SFT achieves BLEU-4=17.84 on our validation set, while the merged model (PT=0.3, SFT=0.7) drops to BLEU-4=6.50. Meanwhile multiple-choice accuracy remains comparable (avg 0.777 vs 0.778) and MedQA improves from 0.664 to 0.681. We further show that small pipeline mistakes (e.g., loading the wrong adapter, export-directory overwrite, or template mismatch) can spuriously attribute SFT-only behavior to merged models. We provide a lightweight merge-verification routine that numerically checks merged weights against the intended linear combination, along with full logs for reproducibility.



1
Introduction

Medical question answering requires both domain knowledge (e.g., guideline-consistent phrasing and terminology) and safe instruction following (e.g., acknowledging uncertainty, recommending clinical confirmation). A common approach is supervised fine-tuning on medical instruction data. However, single-stage SFT may (i) under-inject broad domain knowledge when training data is limited, and (ii) distort the base model’s generalization through overfitting or catastrophic forgetting. We explicitly conduct a data leakage audit due to the known risks in medical corpora.

This report describes a practical pipeline for building a medical LLM using widely available tooling (LLaMA-Factory + PEFT LoRA), emphasizing reproducibility and training stability. Our contribution is not proposing a new merging algorithm, but demonstrating and characterizing a concrete interference pattern between alignment-oriented and knowledge-oriented adapters in safety-critical domains, where surface metrics and reasoning accuracy diverge. Our specific contributions are:

•

Two-stage LoRA training
that separates domain knowledge injection (PT) from instruction alignment (SFT).

•

Weighted Adapter Merging
that combines PT and SFT deltas into a single merged checkpoint for safety-critical domains.

•

Metric misalignment insight
: characterizing how surface metrics diverge from reasoning accuracy in medical tasks.

•

Safety/refusal correlation finding
: observing that mixed models correlate with increased refusal behavior in our probe set.

Related Work

2.1
Domain-Adaptive Pre-training (DAPT)

Continued pre-training on in-domain corpora is a widely used strategy to improve domain vocabulary coverage, style, and factual recall, especially when downstream supervised data is limited. Gururangan et al. show that domain-adaptive pre-training (DAPT) and task-adaptive pre-training can yield large downstream gains across domains
[
2
]
. In our setting, PT acts as a knowledge injection stage, shifting the model’s distribution toward medical discourse before instruction alignment.

2.2
Instruction Tuning and Alignment

Instruction tuning finetunes a base model to follow natural language instructions and generalize to new tasks
[
8
]
. Public pipelines such as Self-Instruct synthesize instruction-following data at scale and demonstrate that instruction tuning can significantly improve helpfulness without requiring proprietary user logs
[
7
]
. While RLHF-style alignment (e.g., InstructGPT) is effective, it requires additional preference data and training stages beyond SFT
[
6
]
. We focus on SFT-only alignment and safety-aware response behavior.

2.3
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

Adapter-based methods provide parameter-efficient transfer by keeping the base model frozen and learning small task-specific modules
[
3
]
. LoRA introduces low-rank updates for attention and MLP projections, enabling efficient finetuning with minimal additional parameters
[
4
]
. QLoRA further enables memory-efficient training by backpropagating through a frozen 4-bit quantized backbone into LoRA adapters
[
1
]
. Our experiments adopt LoRA for both PT and SFT.

2.4
Model Merging and Adapter Composition

Merging weight deltas is a simple way to combine capabilities from multiple finetuned checkpoints. Model soups average weights of finetuned models to improve robustness without additional inference cost
[
9
]
. Task arithmetic formalizes fine-tuning deltas as task vectors that can be added or scaled to steer behavior
[
5
]
. Our Weighted Adapter Merging can be viewed as a two-vector arithmetic operation specialized to PT and SFT adapters, with a fixed mixture weight chosen to prioritize instruction-following while retaining domain knowledge.

4.1
Qualitative Analysis: The ”Thinking” Mode Artifact

The quantitative drop in the Mixed model warrants deeper inspection. A qualitative review reveals that the introduction of PT weights re-activates or distorts the latent ”Thinking” (Chain-of-Thought) patterns of the Qwen3 base model.

•

Pure SFT
: Outputs are concise and direct, closely matching the reference gold standard which typically lacks reasoning traces.

•

Mixed (0.3/0.7)
: The model tends to produce verbose outputs containing reasoning steps enclosed in
<think>
tags. Since these thought traces are not present in the reference answers, they are penalized by BLEU and ROUGE.

•

Safety Benefits
: Despite the metric penalty, the ”Thinking” mode offers tangible benefits. For unsafe queries (e.g., ”How to make poison”), the Mixed model explicitly reasons about the malicious intent in its thought process and generates a firm refusal.

5
Conclusion

We presented a case study for adapter interference in safety-critical domains using a 14B-parameter model built via a two-stage LoRA pipeline. Our investigation into Weighted Adapter Merging demonstrates that a calibrated mixture (e.g., 30% PT, 70% SFT) offers a viable trade-off between rigid metric performance and safety/interpretability. While Pure SFT yields the highest BLEU scores, the Mixed model’s activation of ”Thinking” capabilities correlates with increased refusal behavior in our probe set and reasoning that automated metrics fail to capture.
