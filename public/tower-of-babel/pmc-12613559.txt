CMV infection in active systemic lupus erythematosus: clinical characteristics, prognosis and treatment outcomes
Authors: Kittiwan Sumethkul, Tassanee Kitumnuaypong, Pannipa Bupparenoo, Sungchai Angthararak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify factors associated with, and treatment outcomes of Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection in patients with active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).MethodsSLE patients treated with glucocorticoids, with/without immunosuppressive drugs who still had persistently active disease, were identified. All patients who were suspected of having CMV infection and tested for CMV PCR were enrolled. Clinical and laboratory characteristics, SLE disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K) and treatment outcomes were collected. One-year mortality was compared between CMV-infected and non-infected patients.Results103 SLE patients were included. Thirty-seven (35.9%) had CMV infection (DNAemia); CMV disease (group 1) n = 6, symptomatic CMV infection (group 2) n = 8 and CMV infection coexisting with active SLE (group 3) n = 23. Significant characteristics of patients with CMV infection included higher SLEDAI-2 K, proteinuria, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, AIHA, alopecia, pleuritis, low C3, higher prednisolone dose, treatment with mycophenolate mofetil and cyclosporine. Multivariable analysis revealed that proteinuria (OR6.95; 95%CI 1.29–37.59; p = 0.024), low C3 (OR5.30; 95%CI 1.09–25.76; p = 0.039) and 30-day-average prednisolone dose > 20 mg/day (OR8.01; 95%CI 1.54–41.63; p = 0.013) were associated with CMV infection. Among non-surviving patients, 8/9(88.9%) had CMV infection. CMV was the direct cause of death in 2 patients and indirect cause in 6 (bacterial sepsis and active disease). Despite ganciclovir treatment, mortality rate was higher in patients in group 1 (66%) compared to group 2 and group 3 (12%). However, fever, leucopenia, nephrotic syndrome, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and lung hemorrhage mimic lupus flare, can be reversed after antiviral treatment.ConclusionsDifferent clinical presentations, prognosis and clinical outcomes were identified in SLE patients with CMV infection. After treatment with antiviral therapy, symptomatic CMV infection was associated with the best prognosis. CMV disease was associated with worst prognosis. CMV infection coexisting with active SLE was the most common type of CMV infection in active SLE. This group was associated with variable prognosis according to the clinical context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-025-00592-x.

Introduction

Despite improvements in standard of care for patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), the all-cause mortality rate among SLE patients remains high compared to the last decade, primarily due to renal disease, cardiovascular disease, and infections [1]. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is one of the most common opportunistic infections that causes morbidity and mortality not only in patients with HIV, solid organ transplant (SOT), and hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) recipients, but also in other immunocompromised individuals especially in patients with autoimmune diseases [2–4]. CMV reactivation can frequently occur when the host immune system is compromised. CMV virus can be detected by various methods such as CMV pp65 antigenemia, conventional cell culture for CMV virus, CMV antigen and cytopathic changes detection in tissue specimens or detection of serologic response (CMV IgG or IgM antibodies) to CMV infection [5]. At present, CMV quantitative nucleic acid amplification testing (PCR for CMV DNAemia or CMV QNAT) is recommended as the most sensitive and widely used method for diagnosis and monitoring response to therapy [6].

The prevalence of CMV infection in SLE from previous studies ranged between 59 and 74% according to different methods of CMV virus detection [7–11]. CMV reactivation can cause significant clinical illness and is difficult to distinguish from SLE flare [12]. CMV infection has strong impact on the outcome for SLE treatment as was shown by a mortality rate as high as 42.9% in one study [13]. Screening for CMV infection in patients with SLE varies according to different clinical practices. There has been limited information of CMV infection in SLE patients who have persistently active disease despite receiving intensive immunosuppressive therapies. Since several clinical manifestations can be either from CMV infection or active lupus, the present study aimed to determine the clinical characteristics, prognosis, and clinical outcomes associated with different clinical presentations of CMV infection in active SLE patients who had been treated with glucocorticoids with or without immunosuppressive therapies. Furthermore, the consequences of CMV infection were determined by comparing the one-year mortality rate after detection of CMV DNAemia between SLE patients with CMV infection (Presence of CMV DNAemia) and CMV non-infected patients (Negative for CMV DNAemia).

Patients and methods

A retrospective cohort study over an 8 year-period (January 2014 to January 2022) was conducted at a single tertiary medical institute. All SLE patients who fulfilled the 1997 American College of Rheumatology revised criteria for the classification of SLE [14] or the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinic classification criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus (2012 SLICC Criteria) [15] were identified from hospital electronic medical records. The inclusion criteria were as follows: individuals aged 18 years and older, currently receiving a glucocorticoids, with or without concomitant immunosuppressive therapy, who had persistently active SLE, defined as failure to achieve DORIS remission despite treatment [16]. Additionally, patients who were suspected to have CMV infection based on clinical findings such as fever, myocarditis, or organ involvement (e.g., pneumonia, hepatitis, retinitis, encephalitis) and/or laboratory criteria, including leukopenia (WBC < 3,500 cells/µL), neutropenia (PMN < 1,500 cells/µL), thrombocytopenia (Platelet < 100,000 cells/µL), or elevated hepatic aminotransferases and received at least one test result for CMV quantitative nucleic acid amplification testing (CMV QNAT) were enrolled. If CMV QNAT was measured more than once, the one with highest copy numbers was used for analysis. Patients with overlapping disease (specifically rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis/polymyositis, primary Sjogren’ syndrome, mixed connective tissue disease and systemic vasculitis), HIV infection, cancer and/or solid organ or bone marrow transplant recipients were excluded. The clinical, laboratory investigations concerning the characteristics of SLE and CMV infection during the same period of CMV QNAT testing were collected. SLE disease activity within 4 weeks before CMV QNAT testing was calculated according to the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K). SLE Disease activity was classified as follows: mild activity (SLEDAI-2 K score 3–5), moderate activity (SLEDAI-2 K score 6–10), high activity (SLEDAI-2 K score 11–19), and very high activity (SLEDAI-2 K score ≥ 20) [17]. The mean daily glucocorticoids dose was estimated using the average 30-day prednisolone equivalent dose prior to CMV QNAT. Medications for the treatment of SLE and antiviral therapy were also reviewed.

CMV DNA was extracted from plasma and analyzed by using Real-time PCR technology (COBAS@ 6800). Patients were considered to have CMV infection if plasma CMV QNAT detected ≥ 34.5 IU/ml (DNAemia) which denoted the activity of CMV replication [18]. Clinical manifestations that might be related to CMV infection were collected including pneumonia, upper and lower gastrointestinal symptoms, hepatitis, retinitis, encephalitis and ventriculitis, nephritis, cystitis, myocarditis, pancreatitis, fever, new or increased malaise, leucopenia (WBC < 3500cells/µL) or neutropenia (PMN < 1,500 cells), thrombocytopenia (platelet < 100,000cells/µL, and elevated level of hepatic aminotransferases (alanine aminotransferase) [19]. CMV-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) and G (IgG) results, measured by using enzyme-linked fluorescent immunoassay were recorded. Histopathological results and CMV PCR from body fluid or tissue of involved organ were also collected in some patients according to the clinical indication. CMV from BAL was determined by CMV ELITe MGB® Kit which is a real-time PCR assay, designed for the detection and quantification of Cytomegalovirus DNA (Linear Range BAL:890 − 50,000 copies/ml. CMV from CSF was determined by using BIOFIRE® FILMARRAY® Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel with overall performance 94.2% sensitivity and 99.8% specificity for the lowest detection limit of 4.3 × 103 copies/ml.

The patients with CMV infection were classified into 3 groups as described. Group 1: Tissue-invasive CMV disease (CMV disease) was defined as patients who had appropriate clinical symptoms and/or signs of CMV infection and documentation of CMV in tissue or body fluid from the involved organ with or without positive CMV DNAemia [19]. Patients in this group was further classified as definite CMV disease (for a positive CMV test by histopathology from the body tissue) or probable CMV disease (for a positive CMV DNA test from the body fluid) of the involved organs. CMV retinitis required typical ophthalmological finding confirmed by an ophthalmologist. Group 2: Symptomatic CMV infection / CMV syndrome. CMV syndrome was defined by the presence of CMV DNAemia plus at least two of the following: fever > 38 °C, new onset or increase malaise / fatigue, leucopenia/thrombocytopenia or > 2 times elevated hepatic aminotransferases [19] Symptomatic CMV infection was defined as patients with positive CMV DNAemia who had clinical symptoms or signs suggestive of CMV illness but did not fulfill the criteria for the diagnosis of CMV disease or syndrome. Patients in group 2 must have both the clinical and laboratory results which showed improvement after being treated with intravenous ganciclovir [6, 19, 20]. The doses of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive drug could be reduced after being treated with antiviral therapy. Group 3: CMV infection coexisting with active SLE was defined as patients with positive CMV DNAemia who did not have fulfilled criteria for the diagnosis of CMV disease or CMV syndrome. Some of the patients in this latter subgroup were treated with intravenous ganciclovir but had no improvement of the clinical manifestations and laboratory results after antiviral treatment. Antiviral treatment for CMV was prescribed to all patients in group 1 and 2. For patients in group 3, initiation of ganciclovir was considered by the amount of CMV DNAemia and subject to the attending physician’s judgment. The treatment outcomes were then recorded. One-year mortality rate after CMV QNAT blood testing was determined. The procedures used in this study adhere to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the hospital committee for Ethics in Human Research (20th March 2023, approval number 66039).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SDs) or median and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentage. Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney’s U test was used for comparing means between two groups as appropriate. Chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used for comparing proportions between different groups as appropriate. Association between clinical characteristics and the occurrence of CMV infection were determined by univariable analysis and multivariable logistic regression. Variables with P value < 0.10 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. P value < 0.05 was considered statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version. 22 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

103 SLE patients suspected of having CMV infection were included in the study. The mean ± SD age of the patients in the cohort was 35.15 ± 12.22 years (range 18–70), and 98 (95.1%) were female. The median (range) of SLE disease duration was 6 (1–32) months. Mean SLEDAI-2 K score of all patients in the cohort was 7.41 ± 6.10. Fifty-two patients (50.5%) had mild disease activity, 46 patients (44.6%) had moderate to high disease activity and 5 patients (4.9%) had very high disease activity. At the time of CMV QNAT test, the most common clinical manifestations were hematologic manifestations (leucopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia or autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA); 58.95%), mucocutaneous involvement (47.4%), and renal involvement (46.3%). The 30-day average prednisolone dose was 24.51 ± 18.54 mg/day and the mean current prednisolone dose was 44.3 ± 136.36 mg/day. Immunosuppressive drugs were used in 73 (70.9%) of patients. Diagnosis and clinical manifestations of CMV infection

The serological prevalence of remote or reactivated CMV infection documented by anti-CMV IgG and anti-CMV IgM seropositivity were found in 94 (91.3%) and 20 (19.4%) of the studied patients, respectively. The prevalence of CMV infection (DNAemia) in SLE patients determined by positive CMV QNAT was 35.9% (37/103). All patients with CMV infection had seropositivity for anti-CMV IgG, but only 29.7% (11/37) had seropositivity for anti-CMV IgM. The mean SLEDAI-2 K score of the CMV infection group was significantly higher than patients in the non-CMV infection group (11.38 ± 5.75 versus 5.18 ± 5.11, p value < 0.001). CMV infection was more prevalent in SLE patients with moderate to high disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K ≥ 6) compared to those with mild disease activity (RR 1.73, 95% CI 1.23–8.44; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis was done to compare patients with symptomatic CMV infection (group 2) with those having CMV infection coexisting with active SLE (group 3), in order to explore differences in clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and medication usage between the two conditions. Patients with symptomatic CMV infection were treated with higher dose of MMF (2,187.5 ± 1,099.9 versus 521.7 ± 959.2 mg/day) p < 0.001, higher average 3 month-prednisolone dose 58.9 ± 23.0 versus 34.5 ± 15.9 mg/day) p = 0.006, and proportion of immunosuppressive drug (100% versus 60.9%) p = 0.036, when compared to patients with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE. In addition, patients with symptomatic CMV infection had more frequent hematologic involvement (87.5% versus 39.1%) p = 0.018 and higher incidence of fever (37.5% versus 8.7%) P = 0.049 when compared to patients with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE.

Associations between CMV infection, antiviral treatment and clinical outcomes

Nine out of the 103 (8.7%) SLE patients in the cohort died within 1 year after undergoing blood testing for CMV QNAT; eight of those patients had CMV infection (4 patients with CMV disease and 4 patients with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE). All 8 former patients had moderate to very high disease activity, with SLEDAI-2 K scores ranging from 6 to 20. Mortality rate was significantly increased in the SLE patients with CMV infection (8/37; 21.6%) compared with the non-CMV infected group (1/66; 1.5%) with RR 6.22; 95%CI 1.01–39.69; p value = 0.001. The cause of death of one single patient without CMV infection (SLADAI-2 K score 12) was pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP).

Therapeutic dose intravenous ganciclovir was administered to 18 CMV-infected patients (all individuals in group 1 and 2 and four patients from group 3). After treatment, 5/18 patients did not survive, while 11/18 patients were able to reduce their glucocorticoid dose and temporarily or completely withhold immunosuppressive drugs. However, 2/18 patients required an increase in glucocorticoids dose and could not reduce the dose of immunosuppressive drugs.

Five patients who received ganciclovir did not survive. CMV infection was the direct cause of death of 2 patients (both of them had CMV disease). The other 3 patients (2 patients with CMV disease and 1 patient with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE) died from the indirect effect of CMV infection which triggered lupus flare and increased susceptibility to bacterial co-infection (Supplement Table 1). Three additional patients who died had CMV infection coexisting with active SLE and did not receive ganciclovir treatment. All of them had low titers of CMV DNAemia (CMV viral load 927, 342, 170, IU/ml). Causes of death of these three CMV infected with coexisting with active SLE patients were active disease (including abdominal pseudo-obstruction, AIHA, thrombocytopenia, active lupus nephritis) (n = 2) and Klebsiella pneumoniae septicemia (n = 1).

Discussion

In SLE patients with persistently active disease activity, indolent infection frequently occurs. CMV infection is one of the most common viral infections in humans determined by the worldwide positive CMV IgG seroprevalence of 83% [21]. After primary infection, CMV virus remains lifelong in latent stage, but reactivation can commonly occur when the host immunity is suppressed. In the previous study by Gardiner BJ et al., CMV infection was demonstrated in patients with autoimmune diseases such as SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic vasculitis, particularly in those who had received glucocorticoids. The study emphasized the severe outcomes of CMV reactivation and the difficulty in distinguishing it from disease flare-ups [22]. Sebastiani et al., reported CMV infection in 4 SLE patients who were undergoing treatment with immunosuppressive therapies that were associated with devastating outcome [23]. The prevalence of CMV infection in SLE from the previous studies ranged between 59 and 74% according to the different methods of CMV virus detection [7–11, 24]. The present cohort documented 35.9% of SLE patients with persistently active disease who had CMV infection detected by CMV QNAT. Serological response to CMV infection especially anti-CMV IgM was used to determine CMV reactivation in many previous studies, but this study demonstrated that anti-CMV IgM was not sensitive enough for detecting CMV infection (Only 29.7% of patients with detectable CMV DNAemia). The explanation might be due to CMV replication occurred before the seroconversion phase of anti-CMV IgM. The present study suggests that if CMV reactivation is highly suspected, CMV QNAT is necessary to be tested, even if anti-CMV IgM was negative.

The clinical manifestations of CMV infection in SLE patients are varied. CMV is known as an immunogenic virus that can activate the host immune system and can manifest as a mimic of SLE flare due to CMV antigens can increase CD4+/CD8 + T-cell ratios in SLE PBMCs and their IL-4 and IFN-γ production, driving the development of a large pool of memory T-cells which could facilitate flaring of autoimmune disease [25]. Ramos-Casals et al., demonstrated that SLE patients with CMV infection occurred predominantly in severely immunosuppressed SLE patients who may mimic a lupus flare or present with specific organ involvement, such as gastrointestinal bleeding and pulmonary infiltration [26]. A recent study in 16 SLE patients with CMV infection by Contreras-Valero JF et al., found the high proportion of patients with renal involvement, gastrointestinal damage and receiving steroids before CMV infection [27]. This study showed that the most common clinical manifestations of SLE patients with coexisting CMV infection were hematologic manifestations (leukopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia and AIHA) which presented in 58.9% of the CMV infected patients. CMV infection was more commonly found in SLE patients with moderate to high SLE disease activity (SLEDAI-2 K ≥ 6). This study has suggested that CMV infection should be suspected in SLE patients with persistent moderate to high disease activity after being treated with moderate dose of corticosteroid (prednisolone ≥ 20 mg/day). This notion should be particularly suspected in patients who present with hematologic manifestation, proteinuria and low C3.

Most of the CMV infected SLE patients 23/37(62.2%) in this study presented with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE. This study has demonstrated that there are difficulties to differentiate between lupus flare and manifestations of CMV infection. The present study classified CMV infection in SLE patients into 3 groups as noted above. Interestingly, patients with CMV infection who presented with hematologic manifestations, received high dose of corticosteroid or MMF, and fever were more likely to develop symptomatic CMV infection than CMV infection coexisting with active SLE (see subgroup analysis). Since hematologic manifestations and fever are also common presentations of active lupus, the presence of these clinical characteristics makes the diagnosis of CMV infection more difficult. It is important to note that CMV disease has been associated with very high mortality rate when compared with symptomatic and CMV infections coexisting with active SLE. In addition, this study has shown that several clinical manifestations mimicking a lupus flare in the symptomatic CMV infection group (fever, leucopenia, proteinuria, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and lung hemorrhage) could be reversed after antiviral treatment (see Table 3). These findings may not be directly associated with the effect of ganciclovir but related with the effect in suppressing lupus flare after CMV elimination.

CMV reactivation in SLE patients may be precipitated by impaired immune function due to SLE disease itself or may be due to steroid and immunosuppressive drugs used to control disease. This study found that moderate to high dose glucocorticoids had stronger impact than the use of other immunosuppressive drugs in the association with CMV infection. The result from this study asserts the need for glucocorticoids dose minimization during the chronic maintenance phase of SLE treatment to prevent infectious complications [28].

From this study, the one-year mortality rate of SLE patients with CMV infection after CMV DNAemia detection was significantly high (8/37; 21.6%) compared with SLE patients without CMV infection (1/66; 1.5%). Ganciclovir treatment was associated with a survival rate of 72% in the treated subgroup, while the survival rate for patients with CMV infection who were not treated with ganciclovir was 84%. This apparently disappointing effect of ganciclovir treatment could be attributed to the higher proportion of patients with CMV disease in the ganciclovir-treated group (6/18, 33%) compared to the non-ganciclovir treated- group (0/19, 0%). The mortality rate of CMV disease was very high (66.7%) even for those who were treated with intravenous ganciclovir. All patients with symptomatic CMV infection who were treated with therapeutic dose ganciclovir survived. The mortality rate of patients with CMV infection coexisting with active SLE was 17.4%. The causes of death in this subgroup were active SLE disease together with infection. The result of this study emphasizes that the negative impact of CMV infection in SLE patients can be from the direct effect of CMV infection and also the indirect effect of CMV that causes lupus flare and /or susceptibility to bacterial co-infection. CMV infection could be considered as one of the poor prognostic factors even with a low viral load. This was consistent with the study of Lee et al., which showed that 26.9% of CMV infected non-survivors had subclinical CMV infection [29]. Maksimowicz-McKinnon K et al. also found that CMV viremia was associated with longer ICU and hospital stays, as well as a higher incidence of nosocomial infections. These findings suggested that subclinical CMV viremia may worsen outcomes in SLE patients [24]. Vigorous efforts have to be carried out to find any other possible pathogens in all SLE patients who present with fever and sepsis.

The present study had some limitations other than the retrospective design. First, most of the patients in the study were tested for CMV DNAemia only once. However, this study was able to demonstrate that the presence of CMV DNAemia in active SLE patients was related to poor clinical outcome. Second, ganciclovir was prescribed to only 18 patients with CMV DNAemia, based on the physician’s judgment, primarily for those with CMV DNAemia levels greater than 2,000 IU/ml. This can lead to limitation of the ability to truly assess the efficacy of ganciclovir treatment. However, it is important to note that patients with CMV disease were associated with poor clinical outcome even with antiviral therapy. At present, there is no guideline for initiating ganciclovir preemptive treatment in SLE patients with CMV infection. Most information has come from solid organ transplant model which indicated that CMV DNAemia level of 2000–5000 copies/ml correlated with increased risk for CMV disease [30]. In addition, this study was able to demonstrate that three patients with CMV DNAemia < 2,000 IU/ml did not survive due to bacterial sepsis and active disease. One interesting patient with CMV disease presented with acute CMV encephalitis (positive CMV PCR in CSF) and a very low titer CMV DNAemia (324 IU/ml). (Supplement Table 1). This suggested that the decision to prescribe antiviral therapy should consider both the level of CMV DNAemia and presence of CMV end organ involvement. Third, hematologic manifestations such as leukopenia, thrombocytopenia and anemia could be the manifestations of CMV infection/syndrome or lupus flare. Hence, this study has used the term CMV infection coexisting with active SLE instead of subclinical CMV infection as it is described elsewhere [24, 29]. Fourth, given that all enrolled participants had persistently active SLE and suspected CMV infection while undergoing treatment with corticosteroids and/or immunosuppressive agents, the results cannot be generalized to the entire SLE population. Finally, we are unable to identify any serological results or other clinical parameters to distinguish between the symptoms of CMV infection and lupus flare. Further studies of specific indicators are required.

In conclusion, CMV infection (DNAemia) is common in active SLE patients who are treated with glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive therapies. SLE patients with moderate to high disease activity, were at high risk of CMV infection. Factors associated with CMV infection in active SLE patients were proteinuria, low C3 and 30-day average prednisolone dose more than 20 mg per day. The mortality rate of SLE patients with CMV infection was higher than non-CMV infected SLE patients. CMV infection coexisting with active SLE was found to be the most common type of CMV infection in our cohort. Symptomatic CMV infection was associated with reversible clinical outcomes that mimic lupus flare after receiving antiviral therapy. The mortality rate was very high in SLE patients with tissue invasive CMV disease. Bacterial co-infection and sepsis were the causes of death in the majority of SLE patients with CMV infection. Further studies are required to determine the prognosis of CMV infection coexisting with active SLE, symptomatic CMV infection and tissue invasive CMV disease in SLE patients. Biomarkers associated with these different types of CMV infection may be clinically useful.
