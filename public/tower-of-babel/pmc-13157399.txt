Factors influencing information overload among breast cancer patients in the information explosion era: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiahao Wei, Enqing Liu, Jing Xie, Pengfei Wang, Ziye Bai, Honglang Jiang, Yue Xiong, Liu Bai, Jin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the digital age, the proliferation of information frequently leads to Cancer Information Overload (CIO) among breast cancer (BC) patients. CIO results in difficulties with decision-making and compromises patients’ quality of life. Previous studies have primarily focused on the adverse outcomes of CIO, but research on its antecedents remains limited. Therefore, this study investigates the factors influencing CIO among BC patients.MethodsA single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted from February to August 2025. A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit 598 patients with BC from a tertiary traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) hospital in Guangdong Province. Participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire, the Cancer Information Overload Scale (CIOS), the Digital Health Literacy Scale (DHLS), an asthenopia questionnaire, and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21). Data were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression analysis in SPSS.ResultsUnivariate analysis identified several significant influencing factors, including occupation, location, education level, caregiver, disease course, treatment, and comorbidities. Bivariate analysis revealed positive correlations between CIO and both asthenopia and depression. Multiple linear regression analysis further demonstrated that asthenopia, digital health literacy, education level, comorbidities, disease course, depression, and type of caregiver were significant predictors. Collectively, these variables explained 74.9% of the total variance in CIO.ConclusionCIO is highly prevalent among BC patients. Tailored intervention strategies based on the identified predictive factors are essential. These include optimizing health information delivery channels, incorporating multi-subject social resources, and delivering psychocognitive interventions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is one of the most common malignancies among women and the leading cause of cancer-related death, posing significant health implications and public health challenges [1]. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease data, over 2.3 million new BC cases were diagnosed globally in 2022, accounting for 11.6% of all new cancer cases [2]. Recent statistics from the National Cancer Center of China indicated that 357,200 new BC cases were reported in China in 2022, representing 19.9% of new cancer diagnoses among women and making BC the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in females [3]. Despite advances in early screening and treatment modalities, enabling most BC patients to achieve long-term survival [4], survivors still face complications such as disease recurrence, metastasis, and psychosocial dysfunction.

With the high incidence of BC and continued improvements in early detection and treatment, the number of cancer survivors has steadily increased. This increase is accompanied by a parallel explosion in cancer-related information [5]. BC patients often undergo diverse and prolonged treatment regimens, leading to complex and increasing health information needs. This information explosion has produced overwhelming quantities of information, and misinformation, on the internet. When patients cannot effectively filter or process this information, it can result in information overload and suboptimal health outcomes [6]. Cancer Information Overload (CIO) is defined as feelings of confusion and being overwhelmed experienced by individuals facing excessive cancer-related information [7]. BC patients, who require substantial information regarding disease knowledge, treatment options, and prognosis, are particularly susceptible to CIO [8]. CIO can lead to information avoidance and decreased treatment adherence [9]. Moreover, CIO contributes to negative emotional experiences, such as fatigue, anxiety, and depression [10]. When the quality, complexity, and ambiguity of information exceed an individual’s processing capacity, decision-making quality may also be impaired [11].

Studies have shown that over 90% of cancer patients access information from the internet to inform their medical decisions [12]. Digital-based interventions can efficiently deliver information and supportive care, helping patients acquire health-related knowledge [11]. However, when confronted with large amounts of information of varying quality, BC patients become vulnerable to low-quality health information [13]. Digital health literacy (DHL) refers to the ability to seek, find, understand, appraise, and apply health information from electronic resources to address health-related issues [14]. High DHL enables patients to engage effectively in health-related decision-making and to access accurate, reliable online health information. In contrast, BC patients with insufficient digital literacy have poorer abilities to access and utilize online health information, negatively impacting their interpretation and trust in online resources [15, 16].

Furthermore, the role of physical symptoms, particularly asthenopia, warrants closer clinical attention in oncology. BC survivors frequently experience ocular surface symptoms and visual fatigue, exacerbated by long-term endocrine therapies or intensive screen-based health-seeking behaviors [17]. From a cognitive perspective, asthenopia can be a significant physiological barrier to effective information processing. When visual function is compromised, the cognitive effort needed to parse and filter digital data increases, potentially accelerating the onset of CIO [18]. Therefore, incorporating asthenopia into CIO analyses provides a more holistic understanding of patients’ informational burden.

In the current era of rapid digital technology development, internet platforms, such as social media and short-form videos, have become important health information sources. However, the gap between the quantity of information available and the ability to evaluate its quality is widening [19]. BC survivors have diverse and evolving emotional and informational needs. Amid vast yet uneven online information quality, patients often struggle to assess its reliability and relevance, increasing the risk of CIO. Therefore, understanding factors contributing to CIO during health information acquisition is crucial for reducing associated risks, developing effective information strategies, and assisting patients in comprehending treatment-related information [16].

While previous research has extensively documented the adverse outcomes of CIO, including decision-making regret and reduced quality of life, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding multifaceted predictors of CIO in BC patients. Specifically, existing studies have primarily focused on static sociodemographic characteristics, leaving the interplay between physiological strain, psychological distress, and digital competencies largely unexplored. Identifying these factors can inform tailored interventions aimed at mitigating CIO, alleviating negative emotional distress, and enhancing both decision-making quality and treatment adherence. Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of CIO among BC patients, analyze influencing factors, and explore the relationships among CIO, DHL, asthenopia, and depressive symptoms to provide evidence-based insights for clinical interventions.

Methods

Study design

In this study, a cross-sectional design was employed to investigate the prevalence of information overload and its psychosocial influencing factors among BC patients, in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [20].

Sample size

In accordance with Kendall’s guideline, the recommended sample size is 5—10 times the number of variables examined [21]. This study included 18 variables (14 from demographic and social characteristic questionnaires and 4 from other scales), yielding a minimum required sample size of 90—180. Considering a 20% potential invalid response rate, the target sample size was increased to approximately 113. A total of 620 questionnaires were distributed, of which 598 were valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 96.5%.

Data collection and participants

This study was conducted in the Oncology Department of a tertiary traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) hospital in Guangzhou, China, between February and August 2025. Using a consecutive sampling method, a total of 598 patients with primary BC who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were recruited from the outpatient clinic and oncology ward. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment in the study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) newly diagnosed primary BC, including de novo stage IV cases. Exclusion criteria included: (1) recurrent BC (patients with a prior history of BC treatment who experienced a new oncological event); (2) pre-existing psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment that prevented cooperation.

To ensure the stability and reliability of the study, a pilot test was conducted with 20 participants prior to the formal survey, and the study protocol was refined based on its outcomes. The pilot study demonstrated that the instruments had high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.85 for all scales) and good feasibility, with an average completion time of 15—20 min. All participants reported that the items were clear and easy to understand. The formal investigation was conducted during hospitalisation. Questionnaires were administered following routine health education sessions. Researchers provided a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, significance, and questionnaire procedures. All questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site, and missing responses were checked immediately to ensure data completeness and reliability.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Integrated Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Southern Medical University, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No. 202308-KYSB-HLXH-001, dated January 17, 2024). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Instruments

Demographic characteristics

Demographic and clinical data relevant to the study objectives included age, religion, occupation, TNM stage, and disease duration.

Measurement of CIO

The CIOS is an 8-item, unidimensional scale [7]. The Chinese version, validated by Sun et al. [22], contains eight items within a single dimension. In previous studies involving Chinese cancer patients, the CIOS demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.857) and strong construct validity. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from “never” (1) to “always” (4). Total scores range from 8 to 32, with higher scores indicating greater information overload. In this study, the scale showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.930.

Measurement of DHL

The Digital Health Literacy Scale (DHLS) was originally developed by van der Vaart and Drossaert [23] and adapted into Chinese by Liu et al. [24]. This instrument comprises 15 items across three dimensions: information acquisition and evaluation ability, interaction ability, and application ability. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 15 to 75. Higher scores indicate higher levels of DHL. The Chinese version has been widely used in chronic disease research and has demonstrated high reliability and predictive validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale in the present study was 0.881.

Measurement of asthenopia

The severity of asthenopia in BC patients was measured using an 11-item asthenopia questionnaire [25]. This instrument comprises two dimensions: Eye Symptoms, and Visual Function and General Symptoms. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4 (0 = None, 1 = Mild, 2 = Moderate, 3 = Relatively severe, 4 = Extremely severe). Total scores range from 0 to 44, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Following the criteria established by Deng et al. [25], a total score greater than 8 indicates the presence of asthenopia. The questionnaire’s validity and reliability have been established in the Chinese population. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in the present study was 0.943.

Measurement of depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the depression subscale of the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) [26]. The simplified Chinese version of the DASS-21 was translated and validated by Gong et al. [27], demonstrating high internal consistency and strong construct validity in Chinese populations. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (does not apply) to 3 (applies very much or always). The scale demonstrated strong reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.916 for depression.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Efforts were made to contact participants to minimize missing data. Cases with more than 20% missing values for any variable were excluded from analyses involving that variable. Sociodemographic count data were reported as frequencies and percentages. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were summarised as median and interquartile range. For univariate analyses, independent-samples t-tests were used to compare two groups with normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, and one-way ANOVA was applied for multi-group comparisons. Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used for variables not meeting normality assumptions.

For two-factor analysis, Spearman correlation was used to examine relationships between CIO and DHL, asthenopia, and depression, given non-normal data distribution. In multivariate analysis, multiple linear regression was deemed appropriate, as the dependent variable (CIO) was continuous and met assumptions of linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, and normality. Stepwise multiple linear regression was performed with CIO as the dependent variable and statistically significant indicators from univariate and two-factor analyses as independent variables. Prior to model construction, fundamental assumptions for linear regression were rigorously verified. The normality of residuals was assessed using standardized residual histograms and normal P-P plots. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values; a VIF < 5 and tolerance > 0.2 indicated no significant multicollinearity. Independence of residuals was confirmed by the Durbin-Watson test (values close to 2.0), and homoscedasticity was examined using scatter plots of standardized residuals. Only statistically significant predictors were retained in the final model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Univariate analyses of factors associated with CIO

The univariate analysis showed that several factors, including occupation, location, education level, caregiver, disease course, treatment, and comorbidities, had significant associations with CIO scores (P < 0.001). In contrast, no statistically significant associations were found between CIO scores and age, religion, marital status, income level, TNM stage, or lymph node metastasis (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Correlations among CIO, DHL, asthenopia, and depression

The Spearman correlation results are shown in Table 2. Information overload was significantly negatively correlated with digital health literacy (r = −0.666, P < 0.01) and positively correlated with asthenopia (r = 0.673, P < 0.01). A weak positive correlation was also observed between information overload and depression (r = 0.255, P < 0.01). Digital health literacy was negatively correlated with asthenopia (r = −0.585, P < 0.01) and weakly negatively correlated with depression (r = −0.161, P < 0.01). Additionally, asthenopia showed a weak positive correlation with depression (r = 0.209, P < 0.01). Multiple linear regression analysis

Multiple linear regression analysis identified ten predictors of CIO levels in BC patients, including occupation, location, education level, caregiver, disease course, treatment, comorbidities, DHL, asthenopia, and depression (Table 3). The model explained 74.9% of the variance in CIO (adjusted R2 = 0.749, F = 179.452, P < 0.001). Significant predictors included asthenopia (β = 0.475, P < 0.001), DHL (β = −0.219, P < 0.001), education level (β = 0.169, P < 0.001), comorbidities (β = −0.148, P < 0.001), disease course (β = 0.050, P = 0.023), depression (β = 0.050, P = 0.022), and caregiver (β = 0.044, P = 0.049). Asthenopia emerged as the strongest predictor, accounting for a substantial proportion of the variance. No multicollinearity was detected (all VIF < 3). Discussion

Overall CIO level among BC patients

CIO refers to feelings of being overwhelmed by the excessive volume of cancer- and treatment-related information in the current era of information explosion, leading to psychological stress responses [28]. This study examined demographic and social factors influencing CIO in BC patients, focusing particularly on relationships between DHL, asthenopia, depression, and CIO. The findings revealed high overall CIO levels among BC patients, with a mean score of 21.31 ± 6.86. This result is notably higher than scores reported in surveys involving patients with cardiovascular diseases [29], liver cancer [30], and head and neck cancer [31], and among adults without cancer histories [32]. Several factors may explain this phenomenon: (1) BC treatment regimens are often complex, and the high survival rate necessitates prolonged patient engagement with disease-related information [33]; (2) mastectomy is closely linked to female gender identity, potentially triggering emotional disturbances such as anxiety and depression [34]. Furthermore, the increasing trend toward younger BC onset may lead to role conflicts and exposure to diverse information sources [35]. Additionally, within the CIO scale, the sub-item “decision-making dilemma” received the highest score (2.82 ± 1.01). Previous research indicated that the quality of BC-related information on short-video platforms is generally poor [36, 37]. Patients frequently experience psychological distress and decision-making paralysis due to ambiguous information and poorly explained treatment trade-offs. These observations suggest that the informational burden on BC patients significantly exceeds that experienced by patients with most other chronic diseases, emerging as a critical psychosocial issue. Identifying potential CIO predictors is essential for developing targeted, multifaceted interventions.

Influencing factors of CIO in BC patients

Education level

This study identified a significant association between education level and CIO among BC patients (P < 0.001). Specifically, patients with higher education levels (junior college, undergraduate, or above) exhibited significantly higher CIO scores compared to those with lower educational attainment. This finding aligns with results reported by Chae et al. [38] and may largely relate to differences in cognitive structures between these groups. Research indicates that highly educated patients have a stronger desire to participate in medical decision-making and tend to seek detailed information about treatment benefits and risks [39]. However, this engagement may increase cognitive load. Moreover, these individuals actively seek information through professional websites and academic literature, exposing themselves to a larger volume of content [40]. The highly variable quality of such information further increases the burden of evaluation and filtering [41]. Therefore, healthcare professionals should consider educational differences among BC patients and incorporate psychological counseling to mitigate excessive, anxiety-driven information-seeking behavior.

Caregiver

The study also found that patients receiving external social support had significantly lower CIO scores than those caring for themselves (P < 0.05). This difference may be attributed to family and social support reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms through emotional counseling and practical assistance. Such support frees cognitive resources, allowing patients to better comprehend and integrate medical information [42]. Previous studies have indicated that BC patients lacking social support experience greater depressive symptoms and poorer quality of life, directly impairing their ability to process complex medical information [43]. Consequently, clinical nursing practices should emphasize integrating family and social resources and establishing an information collaboration mechanism among hospitals, families, and communities to mitigate CIO and enhance patient adaptation.

Course of disease

Notably, CIO levels were significantly higher among patients at initial BC diagnosis and those with a disease duration exceeding one year, compared to patients in the intermediate phase (P < 0.05). This difference may be explained by newly diagnosed patients encountering diverse treatment options and uncertainties [44]. Additionally, emerging evidence indicates that long-term survivors maintain heightened vigilance toward disease-related information, potentially sustaining their cognitive burden [45].

Comorbidities

Our findings indicate that patients with comorbidities (e.g., chronic physical conditions alongside BC) exhibit significantly elevated CIO levels (P < 0.001). Patients managing multiple chronic conditions face dual informational challenges: they must process complex oncological data while simultaneously addressing self-management requirements associated with their pre-existing illnesses [46], particularly within the first five years after diagnosis. Therefore, healthcare providers should develop tailored communication strategies and implement long-term follow-up plans to reduce information overload and enhance adaptive coping.

Digital health literacy

The study found that DHL served as a significant negative predictor of CIO (P < 0.001). Specifically, BC patients with higher DHL reported a lower perceived information burden, consistent with Zhao et al. [47] Research indicates that greater DHL is associated with increased satisfaction with the information obtained, thereby reducing overload perceptions. DHL reflects patients’ abilities to acquire, comprehend, and critically evaluate health information, enhancing their capacity to filter and process extensive health-related content and maintain psychological adaptability [48]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that patients with higher DHL are more likely to access and benefit from peer support resources, indirectly contributing to reduced CIO [49]. Beyond mere information acquisition, DHL functions as a metacognitive regulatory mechanism controlling the threshold of information absorption. Our findings suggest that patients with high DHL possess superior “stop rules” during digital information-seeking, enabling them to discern when additional information yields diminishing returns [50]. Furthermore, DHL facilitates the transition from passive reception to active filtration, allowing patients to construct a coherent mental model of their treatment trajectory, effectively buffering the “noise” from conflicting online narratives. Therefore, healthcare providers should aim to optimize health information delivery systems and offer personalized guidance to improve patients’ DHL.

Asthenopia

Notably, this study found asthenopia to be the strongest predictor of CIO levels (β = 0.475, P < 0.001), suggesting that visual strain significantly contributes to cognitive overload in BC patients. This result aligns with previous research on short-video addiction, indicating that severe asthenopia correlates with a reduced capacity to process medical information [51]. Previous studies have shown that BC patients receiving long-term treatment with aromatase inhibitors and other medications often develop lacrimal gland and meibomian gland dysfunction, leading to dry eye disease and visual impairment [52]. From a Cognitive Load Theory perspective, asthenopia directly impairs information processing efficiency by exhausting limited cognitive resources. Prolonged digital browsing reduces blink frequency and destabilizes tear film integrity [53]. Somatic discomfort caused by asthenopia consumes finite attentional resources, causing frequent task switching during medical information processing. Sustaining visual focus requires active engagement of the prefrontal cortex. Therefore, visual fatigue forces the brain to redirect significant metabolic energy toward basic perceptual functions, depleting executive resources [54]. Furthermore, asthenopia-induced fatigue can trigger social avoidance, limiting the buffering effect of social support on informational pressure [55]. These results highlight the urgent need for eye-friendly information delivery in clinical oncology. We recommend that healthcare providers: (1) diversify information formats by incorporating auditory tools (e.g., voice-guided education) to bypass visual fatigue; (2) optimize the visual design of patient manuals through high-contrast, large-font infographics; and (3) implement structured rest intervals, advising patients to follow the “20—20—20” rule during information-seeking sessions to preserve cognitive capacity for shared decision-making.

Depression

The study revealed that BC patients with higher levels of depression experienced significantly greater CIO (P < 0.05), consistent with Wang et al. [56] Research indicates that BC patients are susceptible to depression due to disease-related stress, altered body image, and loss of social roles. Impaired cognitive function associated with depression further compromises their ability to process information [57]. From a biofeedback perspective, chronic depression is often associated with persistently elevated cortisol levels. Under these conditions, hippocampal function is suppressed, leading to impaired memory consolidation [58]. Moreover, a systematic review confirmed that both insufficient and excessive information provision could diminish quality of life and exacerbate depressive symptoms [59]. Therefore, we recommend screening patients for high-risk depressive symptoms and implementing cognitive-behavioral interventions to enhance their information-processing abilities.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected via convenience sampling from a single TCM hospital, introducing potential selection bias and limiting generalizability. Additionally, reliance on self-reported questionnaires poses a risk of response bias, including social desirability effects and recall inaccuracies. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships, as it only provides a snapshot of CIO without capturing dynamic changes over time. Third, this study focused on newly diagnosed BC patients, excluding recurrent cases. While this ensured sample homogeneity during the initial treatment phase, it may limit external validity. Patients experiencing recurrence often face more complex decisions and heightened uncertainty, potentially leading to more severe CIO. Future research should encompass a broader spectrum of the cancer trajectory, including recurrent populations, to enhance generalizability.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the roles of multiple factors influencing CIO. Educational level, type of caregiver, disease course, comorbidities, DHL, asthenopia, and depression significantly influenced CIO variation. Although the findings are subject to limitations from a single-center design and convenience sampling, they provide a foundation for targeted clinical interventions. To effectively mitigate CIO in this population, the following strategies are proposed: First, personalized cognitive and psychological interventions should be implemented based on patients’ educational background and depression severity, enhancing their ability to integrate information. Second, visual aids and optimized information-delivery methods should be adopted, accompanied by long-term follow-up plans. Finally, multi-source social support systems should be integrated to strengthen patients’ social resources and buffering capacity.
