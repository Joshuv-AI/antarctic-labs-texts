When Transplant Tourism Returns to the United States: Clinical and Ethical Challenges
Authors: Veena Ganesan, Jacob M Appel, Dinushika Mohottige, Susan Lerner, Samira S Farouk
Published: 2026

Transplant tourism, according to the World Health Organization,1 describes a process in which patients cross national borders to obtain organ transplants outside of their country of residence. Transplant tourism encompasses a spectrum of practices, ranging from ethically regulated cross-border transplantation to organ commercialization with unclear donor protections. Factors influencing transplant tourism are multifactorial: they include limited availability of transplant resources (organs, transplant centers/care providers) as well as complex hurdles such as evaluation and waitlist time which are associated with transplant in resource-rich settings such as the United States. Amid these constraints, prolonged wait times and structural barriers can motivate patients to seek alternatives abroad.

Often, the context or circumstances in which organs are procured during transplant tourism may be unclear and mired in ethical complexities regarding commercialization.2 These collective dilemmas and the growth of transplant tourism have prompted increased regulatory and ethical guidance regarding organ transplantation, including the 2008 Declaration of Istanbul,3 which outlines the goal of fostering safe, accountable practices that mitigate harms including potential exploitation of vulnerable donors. Whether patients receive kidney transplants abroad via regulated or unregulated systems in other countries, their return to the United States has the potential to stress both health care providers and the health care system.

Numerous ethical, legal, and clinical concerns have been raised in relation to transplant tourism and organ commercialization, yet few have explored the complexities of posttransplant care. This gap in understanding is particularly relevant to transplant tourism, where fragmentation of care across international settings may directly affect clinical outcomes after return to the United States.

To date, no consensus exists regarding the delivery of high quality, evidence-based posttransplant care for individuals who receive an organ through transplant tourism. Many health care professionals may struggle to reconcile the discordance between United Network for Organ Sharing guidance suggesting that although there is no ‘duty’ to provide care outside of a nonemergent situation, immediate posttransplant care for these individuals often ultimately requires the expertise of a transplant care team. Posttransplant care for these individuals can highlight tensions between clinicians’ ethical duty to “do no harm”4 and engage in compassionate patient-centered care and broader health system-level obligations to promote equity, transparency, and integrity in care processes. These dilemmas are further intensified when clinicians must manage complications with limited clinical information or uncertain perioperative practices. In addition, although the diversion of health care resources toward managing complications of transplant tourism has been proposed, this remains largely suggested and difficult to quantify. However, emerging evidence suggests that transplant tourism may be associated with increased downstream health care utilization and costs, particularly due to higher rates of complications and rehospitalization.

In this Editorial, we present a representative case and discuss the intersecting clinical, ethical, and professional challenges faced by US transplant centers and those who may provide care for patients who receive kidney transplants abroad.

Case: A 64-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease, thought to be due to diabetic kidney disease, has been receiving hemodialysis for 3 months via a tunneled dialysis catheter and presents for initial kidney transplant evaluation. At his initial evaluation visit, he learns that the average wait time for a deceased donor kidney transplant is 5 to 8 years. He says he “cannot” wait that long. His blood type is B, and he has no living donors in the United States. Five months after this initial visit, he completes all testing required for listing and is registered on the waitlist by this center.

One month later, he presents to a general nephrologist, whom he had last seen before initiation of hemodialysis. He reports that 2 months ago, he received a living donor kidney transplant from his nephew in another country for which he paid $15,000. He does not have records from the surgery, posttransplant course, or any additional information regarding the donor. On physical examination, he appears ill. A right-lower quadrant incision appears erythematous, and staples are in place. An allograft ultrasound reveals that a ureteral stent is in place. The general nephrologist reaches out to the nearest transplant center for assistance in the management of this new kidney transplant recipient. A local transplant nephrologist agrees to see the patient, and the patient is overbooked to be seen next week. He is asked to bring all available records and medications.

Clinical and Logistical Challenges

Posttransplant care for this patient presents numerous clinical and logistical challenges not only for the patient, but also for general nephrologists, transplant care teams, and health systems. Acceptance of a new patient by a transplant team has the potential to increase the burden of work for all members of the care team, which includes surgeons, nephrologists, coordinators, dieticians, and social workers. When patients present for care soon after transplantation, they are often at higher risk for complications and often may require more frequent visits and follow-up.

Moreover, several additional challenges may impede care processes and affect patients and care teams. First, lack of access to peritransplant medical records may jeopardize care quality and increase time requirements and administrative burdens. Knowledge of the organ characteristics, immunological testing, intraoperative events, induction and maintenance of immunosuppression regimens, and posttransplant course are critical elements of optimal posttransplant care. Although some patients may be able to convey this information verbally, others may have limited health literacy, which may limit precise information transfer and communication. In instances in which paper records are available, lack of interpretability due to use of non-English language or non-US metrics and tests may further impede accurate information gathering.

Second, patients may present with only their pills and packaging—often listing medications with names or doses not used in the United States. Clinical teams must first decipher the medication list and verify drug–drug interactions and check laboratory tests before prescribing often essential transplant medications. Medication reconciliation, even without added challenges posed by transplant tourism, is a time consuming and often flawed process that has been associated with adverse events when not done accurately.5 Finally, questions may remain regarding insurance coverage and copayments for posttransplant medications, which may require additional system-level support from financial and social work teams.

In the United States, the annual cost of chronic dialysis has been quoted around $90,000 per year6 (the majority paid by Medicare), compared with $25,000 per year for immunosuppressive therapy for a kidney transplant recipient—after around $133,000 for the surgery itself. When a transplant is performed in the United States, Medicare pays the transplant center a fixed payment, which can range from $19,000 to $27,000.7 For posttransplant follow-up visits, Medicare pays less than $200 per visit,8 even with the highest complexity outpatient billing code. Although end-stage renal disease-specific Medicare costs are potentially saved by individuals engaged in transplant tourism, the amount of reimbursement to transplant centers and teams for this care is minimal and likely disproportionate to the time and complexity required to effectively deliver posttransplant care, and no current guidance from the United Network for Organ Sharing exists regarding legal obligations to provide care, nor does a modified reimbursement structure to appropriately compensate for additional time/costs. Data are lacking regarding the economic impact of transplant tourism on the US health care system, Posttransplant management, particularly in the early months, can be quite challenging without a comprehensive understanding of a patient’s history. The diagnostic process to identify potential concerns can become unwieldy because the patient’s risk for certain conditions cannot be assessed effectively with limited information. For example, the ability to stratify rejection and infection risk may be hindered when critical data are missing. In summary, a 20-minute posttransplant visit for a patient returning after transplant tourism can feel like practicing medicine in the dark—an uncomfortable and potentially dangerous challenge for both the patient and clinician.

Ethical Challenges

Transplant tourism poses a range of ethical challenges for clinicians. Through the lens of the core principles of biomedical ethics—autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice—many of these tensions become more apparent. With growing kidney transplant volume9 and a known transplant nephrology workforce shortage,10 how and where to allocate limited personnel resources is a complex issue that is compounded by patients who have not pursued transplant through legally authorized and regulated channels. To a degree, these regulations impose standards (eg, eligibility based upon comprehensive medical and surgical evaluations, psychosocial evaluation) and try to ensure that scarce personnel resources are not squandered on patients with poor forecasts for either survival or organ stewardship. Those who participate in transplant tourism circumvent these safeguards. In short, while these patients do not “jump the line” by poaching scarce organs, organs themselves are not the sole scarce resources in transplantation medicine.

From the perspective of justice, this distortion in staffing implicates health care equity in 2 distinct ways. First, patients who have the means to travel abroad for organs (eg, finances, social capital) may receive staff support at the expense of those without such underlying resources. In other words, if transplant tourists receive more transplant care, underresourced patients potentially risk receiving less posttransplant care. Second, because many of these transplant tourists may be at higher risk of complications or even graft failure, they may then impose additional health care costs on the system that divert resources from others.

Beyond these specific burdens on clinical providers are the larger ethical implications of condoning such a practice. The principle of nonmaleficence raises concerns regarding harms to both recipients and donors. The vetting of donors abroad can be poor, and evidence exists that some donors may face overt coercion in the process; a matter of such concern has been the subject of Congressional hearings.11 Despite these concerns, physicians remain obligated to care for patients who return after transplantation abroad. As Camus observes The Plague, “The only means of fighting a plague is—common decency.” In the clinical setting, that decency is reflected in a physician’s commitment to care for the sick, even when the circumstances giving rise to their need for treatment is ethically troubling. The principle of beneficence strongly supports providing posttransplant care to transplant tourists as part of this professional obligation.

Doing so in light of the origins of these organs may also cause moral distress. Clinicians may experience such distress when their duty to care for transplant tourists’ conflicts with concerns regarding how the transplanted organ was procured. Some physicians may also experience negative countertransference toward patients perceived to have circumvented established regulatory systems, viewing such conduct as unethical or antisocial. After all, such conduct may not be victimless in that it undermines respect for the rule of law and fundamental fairness. A first step toward addressing these concerns is awareness of one’s own attitudes, values and reactions. Open discussion with colleagues or even ethics consultants may prove beneficial. Keeping in mind that many transplant tourists are motivated by desperation and prolonged suffering and often have an incomplete understanding of the medical and ethical implications of undergoing transplantation abroad may help foster nonjudgmental regard and patient-centered care. Compassion for such patients need not imply endorsement of transplant tourism itself.

Respect for autonomy requires acknowledging that many patients who pursue transplantation abroad are motivated less by a desire to circumvent established systems than by a determination to escape the burdens of dialysis and prolonged waiting times. At the same time, these decisions are often made without full appreciation of the medical risks and ethical uncertainties that may accompany transplantation outside regulated frameworks. Providers who believe transplant tourists were aware of, and relatively indifferent to, potential human rights violations in the procurement of their organs may experience negative countertransference that can then impede clinical care. At the same time, many transplant tourists are unaware of this troubling context. They may also lack a full understanding of the risks to themselves that transplant tourism imposes and thus are often victims in these transactions as well. For many physicians, the ethical tensions created by transplant tourism resist easy resolution. The obligation to care for patients seeking relief from illness exists alongside concerns for the welfare of donors and the equitable stewardship of a health care system with finite resources.

Potential Solutions

Whether in the setting of organ commercialization or regulated cross-border transplantation, the development of policies and consensus guidelines for US clinicians and health systems for the care of individuals engaged in transplant tourism may be beneficial. In the current model, transplant centers and clinicians may not feel they are adequately reimbursed for providing care for transplant recipients who are “not theirs,” while they may feel morally obligated to care for these sometimes complex patients. Expansion of reimbursement and recognition of the care of these patients may mitigate some of the ethical challenges described above. Similar to allograft survival and average wait times, publicly available data capturing the number of transplant tourists being cared for at a particular transplant center may be worthwhile.

Within the United States, continued efforts to expand timely access to both deceased donor and living donor kidney transplantation are needed. Financial reimbursement expansion of policies to promote access and to and opportunities for living donor transplantation including financial neutrality and optimized federal living donor protections (eg, universal lifetime health care coverage) may deter transplant tourism.

Conclusions

In summary, several clinical, logistical, financial, and ethical challenges may emerge after an individual returns to the United States after engaging in transplant tourism. Given ongoing mismatches between organ availability and need, transplant tourism may be perceived by some patients as a more expedited opportunity to liberate themselves from dialysis dependence, despite the inherent medical, financial, and ethical complexities associated with the practice. Although transplant tourism has often been described as a conflict driven by organ shortages, which positions patient autonomy against ethical concerns regarding coercion, transplant tourism additionally raises complex concerns for transplant centers and teams, which may include moral distress, questions regarding reimbursement, and the long-term well-being and care transition for these patients. Ultimately, a practical and patient-centered approach coupled with increased policy support and guidance is needed. This approach should acknowledge both the ethical and logistical complexities of transplant tourism coupled with the obligation of providing appropriate care. Further investigation is needed to better characterize the clinical and health system implications of transplant tourism and to inform guidance for clinicians navigating these challenging scenarios.
