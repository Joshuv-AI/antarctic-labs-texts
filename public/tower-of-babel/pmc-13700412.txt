Continuous glucose monitoring reveals thyroid feedback-related residual glycemic variability and hypoglycemia during inpatient glucose optimization in euthyroid adults with type 2 diabetes: a CGM-based observational study
Authors: Miriayi Alimujiang, Lei Zhang, Wenwen Chen, Shuai Ma, Jiejie Zhao, Qiyu Chen, Dilidaer Muhanhali, Peng Yu, Yan Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous glucose monitoring (CGM) captures short-term glucose excursions and hypoglycemia beyond what HbA1c reflects, but endocrine traits that shape inpatient glycemic variability (GV) during therapy intensification are poorly defined. We evaluated whether thyroid feedback indices that proxy central thyroid hormone sensitivity are associated with CGM-derived residual GV and hypoglycemia in euthyroid inpatients with type 2 diabetes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 224 euthyroid adults with type 2 diabetes admitted for structured glucose optimization who wore blinded CGM for at least 6 consecutive days. Baseline FT3, FT4 and TSH were used to derive thyroid feedback indices (TFQI, TSHi and TT4RI). Daily CGM metrics were computed; Day 6 was prespecified as the primary later-inpatient analysis day at the cohort level. High GV was defined as Day 6 MAGE ≥ 3.9 mmol/L, and hypoglycemia as ≥ 2 consecutive readings < 3.9 mmol/L. Associations were tested using linear regression (including day-specific models) and multivariable logistic regression with sequential adjustment, including mean glucose and clinical covariates; post hoc sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for available discharge medication classes.ResultsOverall CGM profiles improved from Day 2 to Day 6 (TIR: 62 ± 25% to 84 ± 15%; MAGE: 6.34 ± 2.73 to 4.78 ± 2.04 mmol/L; both p < 0.001), while hypoglycemia still occurred in a subset. The TFQI–MAGE association increased in magnitude across inpatient days and was significant on Day 6 (β = 1.38, p < 0.001). In fully adjusted models, higher TFQI was associated with high GV (OR 3.11, 95% CI 1.27–7.60) and hypoglycemia (OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.56–14.63).ConclusionsIn euthyroid adults with type 2 diabetes undergoing inpatient titration, higher thyroid feedback indices, particularly TFQI, were linked to greater residual CGM variability and a higher risk of hypoglycemia on the later inpatient day. Background

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides a near-continuous record of interstitial glucose and complements HbA1c and intermittent capillary testing. CGM-based metrics, including time in range (TIR) and measures of glycemic variability (GV), summarize both glycemic exposure and excursions over hours to days. Greater GV has been associated with vascular dysfunction and adverse outcomes, making it a clinically relevant target in diabetes care [1–5].

Hospital admissions for type 2 diabetes frequently involve initiating, switching, or escalating glucose-lowering therapies. CGM can depict glycemic instability during this dynamic titration period; however, in the first days after admission, rapid regimen changes, stress responses, and dietary transitions may dominate GV and mask links between baseline physiological traits and CGM variability.

Thyroid hormones influence hepatic glucose production, pancreatic insulin secretion, and peripheral substrate utilization. Beyond absolute FT3, FT4, and TSH values, composite indices such as the thyroid feedback quantile-based index (TFQI), thyrotroph TSH index (TSHi), and thyrotroph T4 resistance index (TT4RI) have been proposed to capture the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid feedback setpoint (central sensitivity) within the euthyroid range [6, 7].

Impaired thyroid hormone sensitivity has been related to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular risk [8–17]. Whether thyroid feedback indices track short-term CGM-derived GV and hypoglycemia risk—especially in hospitalized patients undergoing ongoing titration—remains unclear.

We therefore linked baseline thyroid feedback indices to CGM-derived GV at a prespecified later inpatient time point (Day 6) and examined how associations evolved across days. We hypothesized that higher central thyroid hormone resistance would be associated with greater residual GV and a higher likelihood of hypoglycemia, informing CGM-guided inpatient monitoring strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline. We retrospectively reviewed adults with type 2 diabetes admitted for structured inpatient glucose optimization to the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism, Shanghai Geriatric Medical Center (Shanghai, China) between June 2023 and June 2024. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years; a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes; blinded CGM worn for ≥6 consecutive days with adequate data completeness; and euthyroid thyroid function (FT3, FT4, and TSH within local reference intervals).

We excluded patients with overt thyroid dysfunction or use of thyroid hormone/antithyroid drugs; severe insulin deficiency (fasting C-peptide < 1 ng/mL); eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2; insufficient CGM data (<6 days); HbA1c ≥ 11% at admission (to reduce extreme dysglycemia and heterogeneity); or missing key baseline variables.

Admissions were typically for diabetes education and therapy initiation in newly diagnosed disease or for escalation of therapy in established diabetes with persistent hyperglycemia despite outpatient treatment.

Inpatient glycemic optimization and definition of the later inpatient analysis window

During CGM monitoring, patients were managed using our routine inpatient diabetes pathway, including standardized diabetes meals provided by the ward, bedside glucose checks, structured education, and daily adjustment of insulin and/or oral antihyperglycemic agents by the endocrinology team toward guideline-based targets. Although meals were standardized at the ward level, individual caloric intake, meal completion and meal timing were not captured in a time-stamped analyzable format.

To reduce the influence of the most dynamic early titration phase at the cohort level, we prespecified Day 6 as the primary (later inpatient) analysis day. Day 6 showed higher TIR and lower MAGE than earlier days across the cohort, while hypoglycemia still occurred in some individuals; therefore, Day 6 represents a later inpatient phase rather than universal glycemic stability.

Covariates

Demographics, diabetes duration, anthropometrics, comorbidities, and glucose-lowering medication classes at admission and discharge were extracted from the electronic medical record. Complete medication-class data were available for 194 participants. Exact insulin doses, injection timing, daily titration increments, and individual oral or injectable drug doses were not available in the structured dataset. Baseline laboratory data included fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, lipid profile, serum creatinine, and fasting C-peptide [9]. Thyroid function tests (FT3, FT4, and TSH) were obtained as part of routine admission evaluation within 24 hours of admission and CGM initiation, typically in the early morning after an overnight fast when feasible. Measurements were performed in the central laboratory using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays. HbA1c was measured in the central laboratory and reported as percentage (%), as previously reported [9].

We characterized central thyroid hormone sensitivity using published indices. TFQI was computed from the empirical cumulative distributions of FT4 and TSH within the study population, while TSHi and TT4RI were derived from FT4 and TSH using established equations; the FT3/FT4 ratio was also calculated. We followed the original descriptions and subsequent applications of these indices [10, 16, 18].

Continuous glucose monitoring and GV metrics

All participants wore blinded CGM (FreeStyle Libre Pro; Abbott Diabetes Care) for at least 6 consecutive days. Sensors were applied to the upper arm and recorded interstitial glucose every 15 minutes (96 readings/day). Data were masked to patients and clinical staff and downloaded for retrospective analysis [8].

Daily CGM metrics included TIR (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), MAGE, LAGE, SD, and CV, calculated using standard definitions. Hypoglycemia was defined as ≥ 2 consecutive sensor readings < 3.9 mmol/L, consistent with prior CGM literature and reference ranges [4, 5, 19, 20].

Primary endpoints were Day 6 MAGE and CV (continuous) and a binary high-GV outcome defined as Day 6 MAGE ≥ 3.9 mmol/L. This cut point was chosen based on published CGM reference values in Chinese populations [19, 20].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean±SD or median (IQR) depending on distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test), and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons used Student’s t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Day-to-day changes in CGM metrics (Day 2 to Day 6) were evaluated using repeated-measures ANOVA for approximately normal variables and Friedman tests otherwise. All analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 MP (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

We evaluated associations between baseline thyroid parameters/indices and Day 6 GV outcomes (MAGE and CV) using linear regression. Clinically high GV and hypoglycemia were examined using logistic regression with sequential covariate adjustment, including mean glucose. Because inpatient medication changes could confound CGM-derived variability, we performed post hoc sensitivity analyses limited to participants with complete medication data (n = 194) by adding discharge medication classes (insulin, metformin, sulfonylureas, meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists) to the fully adjusted models. Diet could not be modeled at the patient level because individualized intake data were unavailable.

Analyses were complete-case (no imputation). As a robustness analysis, we repeated linear regression models for MAGE separately for each inpatient day (Day 2–6). We also performed exploratory descriptive checks of FT3 in relation to BMI, diabetes duration, insulin use and Day 6 MAGE to address whether FT3 might reflect broader metabolic turnover. No prespecified subgroup or interaction analyses were conducted; p < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this multi-day blinded CGM study of euthyroid inpatients with type 2 diabetes undergoing inpatient glucose optimization, higher baseline thyroid feedback indices—particularly TFQI—were associated with greater residual GV on Day 6 and with increased odds of both clinically high GV and hypoglycemia.

These findings support the concept that the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid feedback setpoint, rather than thyroid hormone concentrations alone, may capture subtle endocrine phenotypes relevant to short-term glucose excursions. Thyroid hormone signaling affects hepatic glucose output, insulin secretion, adrenergic tone, and substrate utilization; reduced central sensitivity reflected by higher TFQI may therefore mark metabolic inflexibility, neuroendocrine stress, or impaired counterregulatory reserve during inpatient titration rather than directly causing GV [7, 8, 10–13, 16, 17, 21]. The FT3 findings should be interpreted in this context: FT3 may reflect peripheral metabolic turnover in some patients, but FT3 was not a significant predictor of Day 6 MAGE and showed only weak descriptive relationships with BMI, diabetes duration and insulin use in this cohort.

A key interpretive issue for inpatient CGM studies is confounding from rapid changes in therapy, nutrition and stress physiology after admission. We attempted to mitigate early titration noise by prespecifying a later analysis day, examining day-by-day patterns, summarizing admission-to-discharge medication changes, and adding a post hoc sensitivity model adjusted for available discharge medication classes. Nevertheless, exact insulin dose, dose timing, titration rate, oral/injectable drug dose, meal completion, and patient-level caloric intake were unavailable, so residual confounding remains possible.

From a diabetes technology viewpoint, our results illustrate how CGM can quantify both improvement and persistent glycemic fragility during hospitalization. TFQI is derivable from routine thyroid tests and may provide complementary information for deciding who might benefit from closer CGM review, greater hypoglycemia vigilance, and a more conservative titration pace. These findings do not imply that TFQI should be used as a standalone insulin-titration rule or as an indication for thyroid-directed therapy.

Because thyroid feedback indices are low-cost and routinely available, they could be incorporated into inpatient CGM workflows as a pragmatic risk-stratification marker once externally validated. For example, patients with higher TFQI might warrant closer review of overnight glucose trends, more cautious insulin escalation after early CGM improvement, reinforcement of meal consistency, and earlier attention to hypoglycemia risk. Future studies should test whether integrating endocrine feedback markers with CGM analytics improves safety outcomes.

Strengths include the use of multi-day blinded CGM in a relatively well-characterized euthyroid inpatient cohort and evaluation of multiple thyroid sensitivity indices. Limitations include the single-center observational design and the inability to model exact insulin doses, dose timing, titration rate, oral/injectable drug doses, individualized dietary intake, inflammatory markers, or stress-related hormones (e.g., cortisol) during CGM, all of which could influence GV. Although medication-class sensitivity analyses supported the main findings, they cannot replace prospective models with time-stamped treatment and nutrition data. Prospective studies with repeated thyroid testing, detailed titration records and standardized dietary capture are needed. We also did not report TAR/TBR metrics or evaluate parametric TFQI, which may further refine CGM-based phenotyping [22].

Conclusions

In hospitalized euthyroid adults with type 2 diabetes, higher thyroid feedback indices, especially TFQI, were associated with greater residual CGM-derived glycemic variability and a higher risk of hypoglycemia during later inpatient titration. These findings suggest that thyroid feedback markers may complement CGM metrics for inpatient risk stratification, while treatment decisions should continue to rely on integrated clinical assessment, medication exposure, diet and real-time glucose patterns.
