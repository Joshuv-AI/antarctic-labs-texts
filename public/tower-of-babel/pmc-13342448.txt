Modifiable lifestyle factors and their association with depression and quality of life in community-dwelling older adults: a multicriteria index analysis
Authors: Juliana Nogueira Pontes Nobre, Luana A. Soares, Leonardo A. C. Teixeira, Bernat Viñola Prat, José Cláudio Luiz Nobre, Maria Fernanda S. Mourão, Maria Clara M. Oliveira, Bárbara R. Barbosa, Geisy K. A. Silva, Ana Flávia V. Trindade, Letícia G. Amarante, Diana C. Cuellar, Ronaldo L. Thomassini, Vanessa A. Mendonça, Ana Cristina Rodrigues Lacerda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Life habits can influence the aging process. However, there remains a gap in how these factors can associate frailty, independence in activities of daily living, depression and quality of life in older adults. (1) To develop an index to synthesize modifiable lifestyle factors of older adults of middle-income countries; (2) to check the relationship and the association of this index with frailty, independence in daily activities, depression and quality of life. This exploratory, cross-sectional, quantitative study included 192 community-dwelling older adults aged 60 to 96 years (M = 70.66, SD = 7.71), of whom 124 (64.6%) were women. The criteria established for the index, based on the literature, were: (1) smoking, (2) Alcohol consumption, (3) Nutrition, (4) Physical Activity, and (5) Sleep quality. Multi-attribute utility theory was applied to determine the Multicriteria Index (MI) of modifiable lifestyle factors. Pearson’s correlation, simple linear regression, and multivariate analyses were conducted to examine the association between the index and clinical outcomes. The index was correlated and associated with frailty, depression, independence in daily activities and quality of life (p < 0.05). The multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated that modifiable lifestyle factors associated with a healthy lifestyle were linked to 22% of the variation in higher quality-of-life scores and lower depression scores (p < 0.05). Modifiable lifestyle factors for a healthy lifestyle have a positive factor in contributing to overall quality of life in the aging process and healthy lifestyle habits can promote the health of older adults.

Background

Aging is determined by functional alterations in cellular and organ-related mechanisms that contribute to the progressive decline of individual physiological reserve1,2. These biological changes interact with psychosocial and environmental determinants across the life course, resulting in heterogeneous aging trajectories that influence functional capacity and autonomy in later life.

These alterations converge into a complex condition in which the individual experiences a decline in physical and psychological abilities, leading to increased vulnerability and a higher risk of adverse health outcomes across multiple pathologies2. Within this broader framework, quality of life in older age emerges from the dynamic interaction between intrinsic capacity and modifiable lifestyle domains, including tobacco and alcohol use, diet, physical activity, and sleep.

Healthy lifestyle habits have been encouraged in international guidelines3 to reduce the impact of the aging process and promote healthy aging, which depends on cumulative exposure to behavioral, social, and environmental factors throughout life. Recent evidence further emphasizes that integrated lifestyle patterns are strongly associated with reduced mortality, functional decline, and frailty among older adults4.

For example, smoking is classified as a global epidemic5,6. Active smoking leads to intravascular thrombosis and cardiovascular events7, coronary events and strokes8, cancer9, fibrosis and chronic respiratory diseases10, and diabetes11. In older adults, tobacco exposure compounds age-related vascular and pulmonary decline, thereby accelerating functional impairment and frailty. Alcohol consumption is linked to experiences of pleasure, sociability, and respite from stressful life circumstances12. There is evidence that alcohol use can cause disorders3, and even small amounts may be problematic for older adults, as they may metabolize and excrete alcohol more slowly13. Alcohol can accelerate and exacerbate the onset of age-associated conditions such as falls14,15. The use of alcohol alongside other medications can also result in adverse interactions, such as increased blood alcohol levels, altered drug metabolism, and reduced medication efficacy16.

Evidence shows that healthy eating practices include the consumption of fresh foods and a low intake of ultra-processed foods17. Older adults are vulnerable to malnutrition due to age-related physiological decline18, reduced access to nutritious food, comorbidities19, and frailty1. Therefore, eating well and ensuring dietary quality contribute to healthy lifestyle habits and the maintenance of health2,20. Nutritional adequacy is also closely linked to the preservation of muscle mass, immune competence, and cognitive function, all of which are central components of quality of life in older age.

The relevance of physical activity for maintaining habitual activities, muscle strength, lean mass, and overall quality of life is well recognized. Low levels of physical activity are among the main risk factors for sarcopenia21. Physical activity and nutrition are the most extensively studied strategies to prevent sarcopenia20 and to minimize the effects of aging. Regular engagement in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity is also associated with improved mental health, functional independence, and reduced healthcare utilization in older populations3,4.

Sleeping well is closely related to adequate rest and health maintenance. Poor sleep quality is associated with depression22. With increasing age, significant changes in sleep architecture occur23,24. The sleep quality of older adults is positively correlated with overall health status22,24. Sleep disturbances may further exacerbate inflammatory processes, cognitive decline, and mood disorders, reinforcing the interdependence between sleep and other lifestyle domains22.

Although evidence shows that modifiable lifestyle factors can promote healthy aging25, important gaps remain regarding the combined and cumulative impact of these factors on depression, frailty, and quality of life in older adults4. This underscores the relevance of studies that develop practical tools that can be incorporated into clinical practice, especially in primary health care, to support the health of community-dwelling older adults. In line with the agenda of the World Health Organization and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in promoting well-being for all6, this study aims to summarize modifiable lifestyle factors through a synthetic measure, known as the Multicriteria Index (MI). To validate the results, this study seeks to determine whether the index can predict outcomes that positively impact the quality of life of community-dwelling older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a quantitative, exploratory, cross-sectional study incorporating a Multi-Attribute Utility Theory (MAUT) analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal dos Vales do Jequitinhonha e Mucuri (UFVJM) under Protocol CAAE 64132122.0.0000.5108. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki26. Reporting followed the recommendations of the STROBE Initiative (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology – STROBE) statement27. Data collection was carried out between January 2023 and December 2024.

Participants

Community-dwelling older adults registered at primary health care units in Diamantina, Minas Gerais, Brazil were recruited through home visits and invited to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) registration in a primary health care unit; and (3) ability and willingness to provide informed consent, regardless of sex, race, or socioeconomic status. The exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to attend the research center for in-person assessments; and (2) classification as severely frail (scores of 7–9) on the Clinical Frailty Scale28, which could impede travel to the research center and the completion of study assessments.

Sample size was estimated using OpenEpi software (version 3.01), based on parameters adopted in previous studies with similar designs29,30. Considering a finite population of 6,139 older adults aged 60 years or older in the municipality, a target precision of 10%, a confidence level of 85%, and an effect size of 131, and anticipating a 10% attrition rate, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 192 older adults.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1). For the present sample, the study demonstrated a statistical power of 0.99, assuming an effect size of 0.29 (interpreted as small to moderate according to Cohen’s conventions) and a type I error rate (α) of 0.0532.

Instruments

A standardized and previously structured questionary was administered to collect sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, education level) and self-reported health history.

MI – Lifestyle domains

To compose the MI, lifestyle habits were assessed, including current and former smoking status, alcohol consumption (yes/no), and frequency of alcohol use.

Dietary patterns were evaluated using the Dietary Practices Scale aligned with the recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines for the Brazilian Population (GABP). This instrument demonstrates evidence of validity and reliability for the Brazilian population33. The scale includes questions on eating habits, with scores assigned according to response categories.

Physical activity levels were assessed using the Active Australia Questionnaire (AAQ), which consists of four self-reported questions that quantify time spent in physical activity during the week preceding assessment. The instrument captures: (1) walking continuously for at least 10 min; (2) moderate-intensity activities; and (3) vigorous-intensity activities that require substantial effort and lead to dyspnea or increased respiratory rate. The AAQ allows for the estimation of the total number of sessions, total weekly duration, and the classification of participants according to physical activity level, categorizing individuals as sedentary, insufficiently active, or sufficiently active. The questionary has undergone cross-cultural adaptation for older Brazilian adults and demonstrates high reliability (intra-class correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.97; inter-rater ICC = 0.82)35.

Sleep and rest habits were assessed through self-reported sleep quality, measured on a five-point Likert scale (very poor, poor, fair, good, very good).

Health and functional outcomes

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), proposed by Yesavage, validated for the Brazilian population35. Scores were categorized as follows: <5 points (no depressive symptoms), 5–10 points (moderate depressive symptoms), and ≥ 11 points (severe depressive symptoms)36.

Functional capacity was evaluated using the Lawton and Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale, validated for Brazil37. This instrument assesses independence in activities such as using transportation, shopping, meal preparation, housekeeping, and medication management.

Frailty status was determined using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), which summarizes overall fitness or frailty after clinical evaluation38. The CFS is a 9-point ordinal scale ranging from 1 (“very fit”) to 9 (“terminally ill”)28.

Fear of falling was assessed using the Falls Efficacy Scale – International (FES-I), validated for Brazilian older adults39. The FES-I includes 16 items that assess concern about falling during daily activities, with responses scored from 1 (no concern) to 4 (extreme concern). Higher scores indicate greater fear of falling.

Quality of life was assessed using the World Health Organization Quality of Life – Bref (WHOQOL-bref) questionary, validated for Brazil40. The instrument comprises 26 items: two addressing overall quality of life and general health, and 24 distributed across four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Responses are provided on Likert-type scales reflecting intensity, capacity, frequency, or evaluation. Domain scores are transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better perceived quality of life.

Data analysis

MAUT, known as Multicriteria Decision Support, was used. MAUT is a tool used in the context of the connection and existence of multiple factors in the evaluation process, such as aging, making it possible to identify, characterize and combine different variables41, also presented in other studies with similar themes29,30. The phases of MAUT are as follows:

Phase 1: selection of criteria

First, the selected criteria were required to faithfully represent the construct under evaluation and were defined based on the existing literature42. Accordingly, the modifiable lifestyle factors considered in this study were selected a priori and included: (1) smoking3,6,43,44; (2) alcohol consumption14,45; (3) nutrition19; (4) physical activity20,21; and (5) sleep quality22,24.

Phase 2: establishment of a utility scale for scoring each criterion

After selecting the criteria, the subsequent phase aims to place the scores of the selected criteria on the same ordinal scale. In MAUT, it may happen that some selected criteria have different measurement units quantified through attributes42. In this study, the selected criteria have answers quantified by attributes described in the fourth column of Table 1. In this phase, the answers were converted into numerical variables using an ordinal scale. For each answer, a positive value was attributed when the practice was considered favorable and null if the criterion does not characterize healthy lifestyle habits.

The first criterion, “1) Smoking. In the study, it scored 0 for older adults who are smokers5,6, 0.5 for those who smoked but stopped smoking43,44,46 and 1 for those who never smoked6. This scoring approach reflects evidence indicating that current smoking is associated with a higher risk of adverse health outcomes, while smoking cessation is linked to partial recovery of health risks compared with individuals who have never smoked5,6,43.

The second criterion “Alcohol consumption”. Thus, the individual who uses alcoholic beverages scored 0 and, as a result of the reduction in use12, there was a proportional increase in the score so that the individual who does not use alcoholic beverages scored 13,45,47. This scoring approach was based on evidence suggesting that reducing or avoiding alcohol consumption is associated with better health outcomes and a healthier lifestyle profile12,45,47. The detailed scoring categories for alcohol consumption are presented in Table 1.

The third criterion was nutrition. In this study, we considered the nutritional score (based on food quality)2,20. The total score was classified as undernutrition, risk of undernutrition, and adequate nutrition according to the recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines for the Brazilian Population33. The total score was divided by 60.

The fourth criterion was Physical Activity. In the present study, the scoring was based on the physical activity recommendations established by the World Health Organization (WHO)5. Older adults classified as sedentary received a score of 0.33, those classified as insufficiently active received a score of 0.66, and those considered sufficiently active received a score of 15,20. The detailed scoring criteria are presented in Table 1.

The fifth criterion was sleep. In the present study, the score ranged from 0.2 for older adults who rated their sleep as very poor to 1 for those who rated their sleep as very good22,24(Table 1). Phase 3: determination of weight for each multicriteria

The numerical measure that measures the importance of each criterion is the weight. It is possible to assign different weights if the decision-maker understands that there is a different relevance between the criteria (supported in the literature or in the opinion of experts on the subject)42. In the present study, the method of proportions was used to assign weights48. First, a prior analysis of the selected criteria was carried out and, following scores were assigned to each of the selected criteria: Smoking: 1; Alcohol consumption: 1; Physical Activity: 3; Nutrition: 3; Sleep: 4. Subsequently, the proportions were calculated for the assigned weights, being: Smoking: 0.0833; Alcohol consumption:0.0833, Physical Activity: 0.2500, Nutrition: 0.2500 and sleep:0.2500. The scores were considered based on epidemiological evidence that identifies these behaviors as essential determinants of healthy aging and mortality. Previous studies have used similar lifestyle scores, combining these factors, to assess health outcomes in population cohorts49.

Phase 4: calculation of the MI

In the present study, different weights described in phase 3 were considered for each criterion, and, for MI calculation, an average of the evaluations of all criteria was made for each participating older adults. The MI represented the weighted sum of the evaluations of the different evaluated criteria. Phase 5: validation of results

At this moment, it is verified whether the performed multicriteria analysis meets the objective42,50. In this study, it was intended to verify if a higher MI was related to the better lower depression score, lower risk of falls, lower frailty index and higher quality of life scores.

First, the Excel Program (version-2010) was used to formulate the multicriteria model; then, for the validation step, the data were transferred to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version-22.0). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation and chi-square tests were performed to examine the association between the main dependent variable (MI) and the outcome variables. With the outcome variables that presented a value of p < 0.05 in the correlation analysis, simple linear regression was performed with the main dependent variable (MI) to verify how much the created index could explain the results in the depression, risk of falls, frailty, independence in daily activities and quality of life. Subsequently, multivariate analysis was carried out between the main dependent variable (MI) and the outcome variables whose p < 0.05.

Results

192 community-dwelling older adults, aged between 60 and 96 years old, participated in this study (X = 70.66, SD 7.71). Of these, 124 (64.6%) are female. Of the participants, the majority had few years of education (120/62.8% either did not study or studied until elementary school), 167 (87%) did not present depressive symptoms and 102 (54.3%) presented moderate or high concern for the risk of falling. The mean multicriteria index (MI) of the participants was 0.76 (SD 0.96). The MI was inversely correlated with the classification of frailty and depression and positively correlated with independence in daily activities and quality of life (p < 0.05). The MI was included as the main dependent variable in the regression models and was significantly inversely associated with frailty classification and depressive symptoms. Specifically, higher MI scores, reflecting a greater accumulation of positive (modifiable) health-related factors, were associated with lower frailty levels and reduced depression scores. In other words, were more frequently classified as robust and less likely to present clinically relevant frailty.

Similarly, each incremental increase in the MI was associated with a decrease in depressive symptom scores, indicating a protective relationship between healthier lifestyle profiles and mental health outcomes.

Additionally, the MI showed a positive and direct association with Independence in daily activities and quality of life. Higher MI scores were significantly associated with higher IADL scores, indicating greater functional independence, as well as with higher WHOQOL-brief domain scores, reflecting better perceived quality of life.

The results demonstrated that the MI, the dependent variable in the regression models, showed a significant association with quality of life and an inverse association with geriatric depression. It was also associated with Independence in daily activities and inversely associated with Frailty, although these associations were not significant. Specifically, higher MI scores were positively associated with quality of life outcomes and negatively associated with geriatric depression.

Thus, the accumulation of healthy lifestyle behaviors, namely non-smoking, abstaining from alcohol consumption, adherence to healthy dietary practices, sufficient physical activity, and restorative sleep, operationalized as a composite MI score, was associated with better quality of life scores and reduced depressive symptom scores. The regression model explained 22% of the variance in the dependent outcome (R² = 0.22), with statistical significance (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study sought to synthesize modifiable factors related to healthy lifestyle habits through the development of a composite measure, the MI. To validate the results, the study proposed examining the correlations and associations between the MI and depression, risk of falls, frailty, instrumental activities of daily living, and quality of life in older adults. These findings may contribute to both research and clinical practice by supporting the development of preventive and assessment strategies for older adults, based on easily measurable modifiable lifestyle factors.

Evidence indicates that depression is associated with an increased risk of suicide, hospitalizations, greater utilization of health services, and increased family burden51,52. In the present study, a higher accumulation of modifiable healthy lifestyle habits was associated with lower depression scores (R² = 0.13, p < 0.001), suggesting a protective effect of lifestyle behaviors on mental health and healthy aging53. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that lifestyle factors significantly influence depressive symptoms54.

We hypothesize that older adults who maintain a healthy lifestyle—particularly those who are physically active, as assessed within the MI55—benefit from the cumulative effect of multiple protective factors against depression. There is substantial evidence that more frequent participation in physical activity is associated with better mental health outcomes, including fewer depressive symptoms56,57. Both positive psychosocial mechanisms (e.g., increased self-esteem, enhanced self-efficacy, and greater social support) and biological adaptations (e.g., reduced inflammation and improved neuroplasticity) have been proposed to explain the association between physical activity and lower levels of depression58.

Furthermore, evidence suggests that depression may also increase the risk of falls in older adults59, highlighting the interconnected relationship between mental health, functional decline, and overall vulnerability in aging.

In older adults, falls impose major health, economic, and societal burdens59. Falls are the leading cause of injury among older adults60. Moreover, previous studies have reported that a history of falls is one of the strongest predictors of future falls61. Consequently, older adults may enter a negative cycle in which they reduce their activity levels due to fear of falling, leading to declines in functionality and overall health59,61. A serious fall may also result in decreased independence and increased frailty60. Therefore, the inverse association observed between the MI and fall-related outcomes is clinically relevant. In this context, higher MI scores were associated with greater robustness, functional independence, and higher levels of activity, which may contribute to a positive protective cycle, potentially reducing the risk of falls and frailty2.

Although frailty and functional independence (Lawton scale) remained in the final multivariate regression model, their p-values were greater than 0.05, indicating that these variables did not retain statistical significance after adjustment. Nevertheless, their presence in the model suggests that lifestyle behaviors may still be related to these domains of aging, even if the association was weaker in this sample. Several clinical guidelines for frailty management emphasize the importance of identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors—such as unhealthy dietary patterns and insufficient physical activity—at early stages62. Within the conceptual framework adopted in this study, the integration of multiple positive lifestyle factors may generate a protective or “positive” spiral that promotes healthy aging63, potentially contributing to better physical resilience and maintenance of independence in older adults2,64.

In contrast, quality of life and depressive symptoms were the variables that remained significantly associated with the MI in the multiple linear regression analysis. Higher MI scores were positively associated with higher quality-of-life scores and inversely associated with depressive symptoms. Lifestyle behaviors are known to play an important role in psychological well-being and perceived quality of life in older populations. Healthier lifestyle patterns—including balanced diet, regular physical activity, adequate sleep, and avoidance of smoking and alcohol—have consistently been linked to better mental health outcomes and improved overall well-being.

Reduced quality of life and depressive symptoms are common concerns in aging populations and are often interconnected. Evidence suggests that depression can negatively affect multiple dimensions of quality of life, including physical functioning, social participation, and emotional well-being65. Conversely, lower perceived quality of life may contribute to the development or worsening of depressive symptoms over time, suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship between these constructs66.

The findings of the present study reinforce this relationship, indicating that a healthier lifestyle profile—as captured by higher MI scores—was associated with better quality of life and lower levels of depressive symptoms. Specifically, the combination of healthy behaviors—non-smoking, abstaining from alcohol consumption, adherence to a healthy diet, regular physical activity, and restorative sleep—operationalized through the MI, was associated with higher quality-of-life scores and lower depression scores (R² = 0.22, p = 0.01). These results highlight the potential importance of multidimensional lifestyle interventions aimed at improving mental well-being and perceived quality of life among older adults49.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self-report questionnaires may introduce recall and social desirability biases. The criteria that make up the Multicriteria Index (smoking and alcohol consumption) do not account for the duration of consumption, in addition to the nature of the weighting. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design does not allow for inferences regarding cause-and-effect relationships. The modest sample size highlights the need for replication in nationally representative samples. Moreover, because the participants were older adults from primary care or community settings, the results cannot be generalized to older adults individuals in other contexts, such as institutionalized or hospitalized populations. However, it is important to emphasize that validated and culturally adapted instruments were used to ensure the reliability of the collected data. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate multiple modifiable lifestyle factors into a single composite index summarizing healthy behaviors among older adults in a Brazilian urban context. Additionally, we employed Multi-Attribute Utility Theory (MAUT), a robust multi-criteria decision analysis approach widely used in health research to jointly assess multiple domains29,30. Given the multidimensional and dynamic nature of aging, the use of multifactorial analytical approaches is essential for a better understanding of the complex relationships between lifestyle behaviors, depression, and quality of life34,66.

Conclusion

The present MI demonstrates potential clinical and research applicability, as it summarizes modifiable lifestyle habits among older adults and provides an overall indicator related to important functional domains. A positive and statistically significant association was observed, indicating that higher MI scores were associated with lower depressive symptom scores and better quality of life outcomes. These findings suggest that the MI may serve as a practical tool for identifying lifestyle patterns that are linked to mental well-being and perceived quality of life in older populations. In clinical and public health settings, the index may help guide health promotion strategies by highlighting modifiable behaviors that could be targeted through interventions aimed at improving psychological health and overall well-being among older adults.
