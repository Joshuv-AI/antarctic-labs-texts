An intelligent electronic-textile system with adaptive sweat-regulation for continuous exercise-induced fatigue monitoring
Authors: Jing Wu, Jingying Yang, Qisijing Liu, Pixian Zhang, Bowen Zheng, Linyuan Liu, Tingtao An, Hao Wang, Fupei Xu, Yudi Shen, Shuo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCharacterizing exercise-induced fatigue is challenging due to difficulties in monitoring biochemical and electrophysiological signals consistently. While wearable sensors able to monitor both signals are available, they struggle to maintain stable signal acquisition across exercise-rest scenarios because of significant sweat variation. Here we describe the development of a system, FatigueVisual, combining an adaptive sweat-regulating hybrid electronic textile (e-ASRHT) patch with artificial intelligence (AI) analytics to improve fatigue management. The key innovation lies in the precise regulation of the breakthrough pressure of micropores in fabric electrodes and sweat-excreting areas, allowing for accurate sweat management. This design enables efficient enrichment at an ultralow sweat rate (5.0×10−4 ml cm−2 min−1) while rapidly transporting excess sweat at a rate 2533 times the human physiological limit, ensuring stable chemical-electrophysiological sensing from exercise to rest. A cloud-connected AI model, trained on over 200,000 time-resolved observations collected from 10 participants, recognizes six fatigue-related states and provides personalized exercise management via a mobile app. Validation in three participants showed 87.3% agreement with clinical assessments. The system reduced exercise fatigue by 51.8% and accelerated recovery by 48 h compared with the control group. In practice, FatigueVisual may enable intelligent sports health management.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Biotechnology

Introduction

As exercise gains recognition for improving healthy aging and preventing chronic diseases1,2, the health risks of improper or excessive exercise, especially exercise-induced fatigue, are becoming increasingly prominent. Exercise-induced fatigue, a multidimensional physiological response to intense or prolonged exercise, stems from central nervous system (CNS), autonomic nervous system (ANS), and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation, leading to reduced muscle endurance, metabolic/hydration disruptions, metabolite accumulation, and hormonal imbalance (Fig. 1a)3–5. Without intervention, it may lead to muscle damage, immune disorders, and organ damage. Serious (potentially life-threatening) medical complications have been reported in more than 0.05% of participants in distance-running events, where fatigue factors, including hypohydration and electrolyte disturbances, contribute substantially to mortality6,7. For oncology patients and postsurgical individuals with compromised metabolic adaptability, the cumulative impact of postponed exercise fatigue manifestations may significantly exacerbate secondary complications. Among 80 advanced breast cancer patients enrolled in an exercise-based intervention trial, the most prevalent adverse effect was pain (51.3%), followed by dizziness (12.5%), exercise-associated muscle soreness/cramping (11.3%), and self-reported fatigue (8.9%)8. Accumulating evidence confirms that sophisticated exercise health surveillance systems enable the proactive identification of fatigue risks in athletic populations through biomarker tracking, and adaptive resistance training in clinical settings that reduces fatigue symptoms9,10. These fatigue monitoring approaches are indispensable for both athletic performance preservation and therapeutic risk management.

The primary challenge in current exercise-induced fatigue monitoring is the difficulty of continuously tracking multidimensional physio-biochemical signals. Traditional methods (e.g., Borg scale, blood lactate/creatine kinase) fail to capture real-time fatigue dynamics and lack metabolic risk warnings for special populations (e.g., cancer patients)11. Wearable devices (e.g., electrocardiograph (ECG), electromyography (EMG) monitors) provide subclinical alerts but cannot synchronize biochemical and physiological signals, resulting in fragmented fatigue analysis12,13. Flexible electronic skin-based wearable sensors, while capable of concurrently monitoring sweat biochemical signals and skin electrical responses, also face constraints: exercise-induced sweat accumulation (due to poor breathability) disrupts skin electrophysiology, whereas at rest, low sweat flow (even with iontophoresis (IP)) limits continuous biochemical monitoring14–16. Although 3D directional diversion structures enable high-speed sweat expulsion to stabilize electrophysiological signals, they struggle with low-flow sweat because of capillary limitations, leading to localized fluid film formation on micropillar surfaces and uneven transport to biochemical sensing zones, which compromise monitoring accuracy at rest17,18. Microfluidic/bioinspired designs improve trace sweat collection but fail under high sweat flow19–21. The fundamental problem is contradictory sweat dynamics: excess sweat destabilizes signals during exercise, whereas insufficient sweat disrupts engagement at rest, preventing omni-scenario real-time monitoring. Moreover, although machine learning (ML) algorithms can interpret wearable sensor readouts to identify and classify diseases22–24, a comprehensive assessment of exercise-induced fatigue for precise personalized health management is currently unavailable, mainly due to the difficulty in simultaneously recognizing its complex and dynamic multidimensional physiological states.

To address the obvious deficiencies of current wearable sensing patches in dynamic sweat management and the lack of a comprehensive intelligent decision-making module, which restrict their stability, applicability, and practical application (Supplementary Table 1), our study proposes an adaptive sweat-regulating hybrid electronic textile (e-ASRHT) patch that integrates sweat accumulation and rapid sweat renewal. The e-ASRHT patch comprises a flexible fabric with dual-function Janus pores for sensing and sweat drainage, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) layer with gradient micropillars, and double absorbent layers (Fig. 1b–e). It should be clearly noted that the concept of liquid directional transport proposed in this study is derived from the pioneering work on 3D liquid diodes conducted by Zhang et al.17. Compared with the 3D liquid diode research (Supplementary Table 2), the core innovation of this work lies in the precise control of the breakthrough pressure (BTP) of micropores in the fabric electrodes and sweat-excreting areas, enabling accurate sweat management under different conditions (rest/exercise) (Fig. 1f). Under resting micro-sweat conditions, lower micropore BTP enriches sweat in the electrodes, ensuring precise monitoring of physiological and biochemical signals. Under heavy sweating conditions during exercise, increased pressure activates sweat-excreting micropores for efficient sweat management, ensuring consistent sensor performance. Additionally, capillary-driven flow-based micropillars direct excess sweat to the absorbent layer, thereby ensuring sweat renewal. Moreover, combined with an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven intelligent decision-making module, this patch constructs a complete “sensing-analysis-guidance intervention” closed-loop system, facilitating precise and real-time fatigue assessment across various scenarios.

Results

Adaptive sweat-managed e-textile patch

The fabric with Janus micropores (JFM) was created in three steps: first, hydrophilic cotton was modified with perfluorosilane-functionalized TiO₂ nanoparticles to make it superhydrophobic (contact angle (CA) of 156°, Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2); second, gallium-indium alloy liquid metal (LM) was 3D-printed and stretched to form a conductive film on the fibers (electrode diameter of 1.0 mm) (Supplementary Fig. 3); third, a patterned mask was used for selective plasma treatment to create micropore arrays with varying CAs between exposed drainage spot (DP)/sensing spot (SP) and unexposed nonspot (NP) areas (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3). Unmeasurable CAs were recorded as zero because, with longer plasma treatment, water droplets rapidly moved from the back to the top spot areas. Compared with hydrophobic fabrics like polyester, the cotton with natural porous structure and hydrophilicity showed stronger adhesion of TiO₂ and maintained higher hydrophobicity (CA > 145°) after long-term simulated sweating exposure (Supplementary Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Table 4). We further assessed the liquid transport performance of both SP and DP. At a sweat flow rate of 0.5 ml min−1, both SP and DP showed antigravity transport. DP penetrated evenly in 5 s, while SP enriched sweat on the electrode in 3 s, demonstrating greater enrichment capacity and enabling synergistic function (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Movies 1, 2). Functional modifications to the SPs did not significantly affect their antigravity transport capacity (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). The adaptive sweat management patch was designed with 0.8 mm diameter DPs and 1.6 mm diameter SPs, balancing exposed area and treatment duration to improve its antigravity transport capacity (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 5). At a low flow rate (5.0 × 10−4 ml cm−2 min−1), the SPs preferentially enriched sweat compared with the DPs, with a consistently higher sweat transport rate through SPs than DPs. At a high flow rate (5.0 × 10−2 ml cm−2 min−1), the DPs and SPs were simultaneously activated, effectively preventing skin fluid accumulation (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Movies 3, 4). With this optimal design, the JFM achieved a high sweat-transport rate of 7.22 ml cm−2 min−1 (Supplementary Fig. 11), and its liquid management capability was 2533 times the physiological human sweat secretion rate. The specific values of the latter can be found in the benchmark data reported by Zhang et al. and Smith et al.17,25. The JFM can dynamically regulate the sweat flow rate, thereby enabling the dual functions of microsweat enrichment and macrosweat drainage. BTP was adopted as the core indicator for evaluating the directionality of liquid transport, and the definition and test method of this indicator were based on the report by Lao et al.26. The BTPs of both the DPs and SPs increased with increasing sweat flow rate (Fig. 2f), and the BTP ratio (DP/SP) decreased systematically from ~2.0 at low flow rate to ~1.0 at high flow rate. This dynamic pressure difference is proposed as the key mechanism enabling adaptive sweat management (Supplementary Fig. 12). To verify fluid transport characteristics, we used a capillary bundle model based on the Hagen-Poiseuille principle27, along with computational fluid dynamics simulations, to simulate fluid flow rates (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 6). Despite geometric simplifications and ignoring deformation, which may affect permeability and pressure estimates, these assumptions (detailed in the “Methods” section) are necessary and standard for practical applications in fabrics. Theoretical calculations under these assumptions showed that the transport rate ratio aligned with the pressure ratio trend, matching experimental data and supporting our proposal.

The electronic fabric also excelled in breathability, mechanical stability, batch-to-batch consistency, extreme-condition durability, and biocompatibility (Supplementary Figs. 14–18). Though the fabric’s sweat transport rate decreased by 1.3% after 72 h (7.1 ml cm−2 min−1) and 38.2% after 30 days (4.5 ml cm−2 min−1), it remained higher than the maximum human sweating rate (2.85 × 10−3 ml cm−2 min−1)25, ensuring stable performance over 30 days (Supplementary Fig. 19). BTP measurements, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that alterations in surface energy, wettability, sweat fouling, and microstructural degradation were responsible for the gradual decline in transport performance of the JFM (Supplementary Figs. 20, 21 and Supplementary Table 7). To counteract this, we applied a hydrophilic coating (ZCP0030B, Sysmyk) to enhance sweat transport. The treated fabric showed a higher sweat transport rate and slower performance decline than the untreated control group over 30 days (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Sweat self-renewal can be achieved by integrating hydrophilically modified PDMS gradient micropillars with double-layer absorbent pads (Supplementary Figs. 23–27). The gradient micropillars can directionally guide the central droplet to the outlet within 1.0 second and exhibit a liquid transport rate 2.2 times that of micropillars with equal height but unequal spacing (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Figs. 28, 29, and Supplementary Movie 5). The double-layer absorbent pads were constructed using annular and circular PET fabrics (Supplementary Fig. 23). The annular PET (pore size 50 μm, thickness 550 μm, and width 5.0 mm) was positioned between the PDMS micropillar layer and the JFM, while the circular PET (pore size 20 μm, thickness 300 μm, and diameter 2.8 cm) was placed on top of the PDMS. Both fabrics were adhered to PDMS micropillars with silicone adhesive. This setup realized sweat renewal for 1.0 h at a flow rate of 0.1 ml min−1 and maintained stable performance even after replacement of the circular PET fabric (Supplementary Fig. 30), meeting long-term wear requirements.

The e-ASRHT achieved preferential sweat accumulation at the electrode area within 3.0 min, followed by sweat expulsion through peripheral pores at ~10.0 min (Fig. 2i). In contrast, the JFM formed obstructive liquid films over time, whereas the Janus fabric (JF) without micropores showed delayed sweat delivery (after approximately 8.0 min) and an unstable supply because of complex pathways. Infrared images revealed that, after exercising for 30 min, the average skin temperature increased by 0.6 °C beneath the e-ASRHT, whereas the commercial electrodes and PDMS caused an average temperature increase of nearly 1.65 °C (Fig. 2j). The e-ASRHT exhibited excellent biochemical and physiological sensing performance under both resting and exercise conditions, with high responsiveness and stability. After 30 min of exercise, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the e-ASRHT EMG signals decreased the least (5.74%) compared with JF (16.0%) and JFM (11.1%), improving the sensing stability by 4.53 times that of commercial electrodes (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Fig. 31). At rest, compared with the other sensors, the patch quickly detected glucose signals within 3.0 min of IP sweat induction and responded 8.0 min faster (Fig. 2l). Moreover, even under extremely low sweat flow rates (<4.0 × 10−4 ml cm−2 min−1), it maintained stable sensing for 60 min, extending the sensing duration by 55 min compared with the JF sensor, demonstrating outstanding sensing durability and sensitivity. The glucose sensor also demonstrated extremely high stability under different sweat flow rates (average relative standard deviation (RSD), 2.02%) (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 32). The breathability of the e-ASRHT was also on par with that of other fabric-based electrodes reported in the literature17,28. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of the e-ASRHT was 149.0 g m−2 h−1, which was comparable to that of the original fabric (158.0 g m−2 h−1) and the hydrophobically treated fabric (151.3 g m−2 h−1, P > 0.05, ns) (Supplementary Fig. 14). After 72 h of continuous wear of the e-ASRHT, the skin remained dry and the temperature stayed stable during exercise, confirming the excellent air permeability and sweat-wicking capability that avoid sweat accumulation and sensor problems (Supplementary Figs. 33, 34). Moreover, the SNR of EMG signal dropped by just 2.7%, while glucose sensing remained stable, indicating minimal interference with sensor performance (Supplementary Fig. 35).

Multimodal sensing based on e-ASRHT patches

To enable continuous and stable multimodal sensing, the electronic fabric patch must be electromechanically stable and durable, in addition to exhibiting good skin conformability, breathability, and moisture permeability. We developed a printable elastic conductive ink (ECI) using LM, silver nanoflakes (AgFKs), and an elastomer (styrene-isoprene-styrene/ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymer, SIS/EVA) to produce a flexible fabric patch with excellent electromechanical stability and durability (Fig. 3a, b). The ECI exhibited shear-thinning behavior with low viscosity at <100 Pa·s and a loss modulus exceeding the storage modulus above 55 Pa, achieving printability comparable to that in a previous report (Supplementary Fig. 36)29. With a maximum adhesion strength of 0.55 MPa, it enabled uniform patterning on diverse substrates and the formation of a laterally porous, longitudinally folded conductive film (Fig. 3c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 37). An optimized LM: AgFKs ratio of 2:1 resulted in a fabric circuit with 100% stretch stability and conductivity (3323.5 S cm−1) (Supplementary Fig. 38). The ECI formed hydrogen bonds with lubricants on AgFKs, promoting a uniform distribution of the conductive components and increasing network stability and stretch resistance (Supplementary Fig. 39). The printed ink demonstrated excellent adhesion stability, with only a minor decrease after 48 h in a high-temperature and high-humidity environment (50 °C, 85% RH) (Supplementary Fig. 40), meeting sensor requirements at normal temperatures and during intense sweating. SEM analysis showed the ink remained firmly attached to the fabric even after repeated stretching and bending (Supplementary Fig. 41). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) results indicated that hydrogen bonds formed between the ink’s ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymer and the cotton fabric’s cellulose (Supplementary Fig. 42). Additionally, the methyl groups in the ink’s SIS block copolymer physically adhered to the fabric’s rough surface, enhancing initial adhesion and ensuring stability. The patterned electrodes demonstrated comparable electromechanical stability (bending 10,000 cycles, crumpling 100 cycles) and wash durability (10 cycles) to those of previously reported permeable fabric electronics (ΔR/R0, 17.2–45.5%, Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 43)30,31. This high-performance conductive system supports the reliable, real-time monitoring of fatigue-related physiological signals throughout exercise.

The e-textile was further integrated with sensors for muscle activity (EMG), energy metabolism (glucose, urea, NH4+), hydration (Na+ and K+), metabolic byproducts (lactate), and stress hormones (cortisol and testosterone). All the biochemical sensors were built on a three-electrode system (Ag/AgCl reference, counter, and working electrodes): Na+, K+, and NH4+ were measured via ion-selective electrode (ISE) potentiometry (Fig. 3f–h and Supplementary Figs. 44–47)32. Urea, glucose, and lactate were quantified using enzyme-based potentiometry or amperometry (Fig. 3i–k, Supplementary Figs. 48–50)33. To prevent Prussian blue (PB) degradation in enzymatic electrodes (especially for glucose and lactate) under H2O2 exposure, a Nafion membrane was coated onto the electrode surface. This membrane blocks H2O2 and oxidants, slowing PB reduction and maintaining enzyme activity and electrochemical stability34. In simulated sweat tests, the Nafion coating reduced the PB peak potential shift by a factor of 11.2 relative to that of unprotected electrodes and reduced peak current attenuation from 31.7% to 11.6% (Supplementary Fig. 51). SEM analysis showed that Nafion acted as a physical barrier against PB degradation (Supplementary Fig. 52). After 72 h of H2O2 exposure, the Nafion-coated electrode retained 93.8% (glucose) and 96.0% (lactate) sensitivity, compared with 39.7% and 35.8% for unprotected electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Tables 8, 9). Real-time monitoring showed that electrodes with Nafion had much lower drift rates (0.17 nA−1 h for glucose and 0.15 nA−1 h for lactate) compared with unprotected electrodes (1.5 nA h−1 for glucose and 1.2 nA h−1 for lactate) (Supplementary Fig. 54). Finally, the Nafion-coated enzymatic electrodes were applied for glucose and lactate detection (Supplementary Figs. 55, 56). Testosterone and cortisol were continuously monitored using molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)-based amperometry (Fig. 3l, m and Supplementary Figs. 57–60)35. Electrochemical cleaning was performed on the MIP sensors between cycles. After 20 cycles, sensitivities for cortisol and testosterone decreased by just 5.2% and 6.1%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 61). Baseline responses remained stable with RSD below 5.0%, showing effective regeneration and stable imprinting sites (Supplementary Fig. 62). To address the impact of ambient temperature and sweat ionic strength on biosensors for analytes such as glucose and lactate, we employed real-time monitoring data from temperature and sodium ion sensors to establish a multiple regression model, enabling dynamic calibration of the target analyte measurements (Supplementary Figs. 63, 64).

For 72-h monitoring, we implemented a maintenance strategy to ensure the stability and accuracy of ISE, enzyme, and MIP sensors. ISE and enzyme sensors self-clean with sweat, maintaining stability for over 72 h, making them suitable for extended use. The MIP sensor regenerates via in situ potential stimulation, maintaining sensitivity and selectivity for 20 cycles. We set regeneration intervals based on needs to control the MIP sensor’s continuous usage duration. The EMG sensor featured a stretchable, serpentine mesh design with differential electrodes, delivering high signal fidelity and performance comparable to those of commercial devices (Supplementary Fig. 65). The raw EMG signals were preprocessed in Python (including baseline removal, demodulation, smoothing, and Fourier transform) to extract the mean frequency (MEF) and median frequency (MDF) for analysis.

The patch also incorporated a low-current (50 μA) IP electrode and a highly adhesive conductive hydrogel for efficient sweat stimulation and stable skin attachment (Supplementary Figs. 66–69)36,37. After IP stimulation, the e-ASRHT patch enabled rapid sweat collection, allowing the simultaneous, selective, and interference-free detection of multiple biomarkers (Supplementary Fig. 70). The calibration curves for these biomarkers covered the normal sweat concentrations found in healthy individuals during exercise (Supplementary Table 10). For example, NH₄⁺, which indicates muscle energy supply during intense exercise and typically ranges from 0.5 to 8.0 mM, was assigned a calibration range of 0.05 to 10 mM to ensure detection accuracy. Although testosterone studies in sweat are limited, we designed a calibration range of (0.00347−1.74 )× 10⁻⁶ mM, considering differences between sweat and blood levels, to cover exercise-related variations. It should be noted that our sensor primarily detects exercise-induced physiological and biochemical stress, rather than extreme pathological conditions. Correlation plots for validation against gold standards demonstrated a strong positive linear association (R2 > 0.91) between wearable sensor and laboratory gold-standard measurements (Supplementary Figs. 71, 72). These results indicated that the wearable sensor can accurately capture changes in biochemical markers in sweat and reliably reflect physiological states related to fatigue. A compact custom electronic module enabled IP control, real-time data acquisition, and wireless transmission via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), along with power management (Supplementary Figs. 73, 74, and Supplementary Table 11). Metal pogo pin electrodes and perforated adhesive securely attached rigid components to textiles, offering a stable, comfortable, and flexible interface for long-term wearable sensors (Supplementary Fig. 75 and Supplementary Table 12). Finally, the integrated e-ASRHT device successfully monitored multimodal data continuously for 24 h during daily activities (Fig. 3n).

ML-powered comprehensive fatigue assessment model

An ML-based model was developed for real-time, personalized fatigue assessment (Fig. 4a). Approximately 260,000 time-resolved physio-biochemical observations were collected from 10 participants (6 males and 4 females) across the full exercise cycle (Supplementary Figs. 76–85 and Supplementary Table 13). This cycle comprised a 30-min pre-exercise baseline, a 120-min of exercise session, and 30-min of postexercise assessments at 0-, 24-, 48-, and 72-h. This ethically approved study ensured data anonymity and informed consent. The measured parameters included eight sweat biomarkers (glucose, lactate, Na+, K+, urea, NH4+, testosterone, and cortisol), physiological signals (MEF and MDF of EMG), and basic demographics (body weight and sex). The sampling frequencies were optimized for sensor response: 6.0 s for glucose, lactate, Na+, K+, urea, and NH4+, 1.0 min for MEF and MDF, and 10 min for cortisol and testosterone, with missing data interpolated linearly (optimization in Supplementary Fig. 86). These results revealed that during exercise, the glucose level, testosterone/cortisol (T/C) ratio, and MEF decreased, reaching their lowest values at 0.5 h postexercise, whereas the lactate level, Na+/K+ ratio, and urea level increased, peaking simultaneously before returning to baseline after 72 h. This pattern reflected energy depletion, metabolic byproduct accumulation, and muscle fatigue, which recovered with rest (Fig. 4b). Although the overall trends were consistent, significant interindividual differences were observed in biomarker response timing and magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 87). For example, the lactate concentration of participants 3 and 9 increased by more than 56.9% at 1.0 h during exercise, whereas that of participant 1 peaked earlier, at 0.5 h (53.49%). After exercise, participant 4 exhibited smaller changes and faster recovery, whereas participant 3 showed sustained high fluctuations, with some markers still elevated at 72 h. Subjective fatigue (SF) scores indicated that most participants felt fatigued after 1 h, experienced peak fatigue at two h, and largely recovered by 0.5 h postexercise (Fig. 4c). These variations underscore the need for multimodal, individualized, and real-time monitoring to accurately assess fatigue dynamics and guide effective exercise interventions.

In this study, fatigue was categorized into six potential factors: SF, blood glucose supply (BG), blood lactate accumulation (BL), hydration status, muscle fatigue (MF), and protein level. These potential factors of fatigue were labeled by questionnaires, blood glucose and lactate levels, urine specific gravity (USG), the salivary T/C ratio, and salivary urea nitrogen (SUN), respectively, for further ML-based model construction. The USG, T/C ratio, and SUN were reported to be associated with hydration status, MF, and protein level, respectively38–40. Fatigue-related biomarkers and physiological indicators, including SF score, BG, BL, hydration, protein metabolism markers, and MF index, were classified into mild/no fatigue (0), moderate fatigue (1), and severe fatigue (2). SF was evaluated using the Borg Category-Ratio 10 scale (CR-10)41. BL, protein metabolism, and MF were assessed through changes in biomarkers like ΔLactate%, ΔSUN%, and ΔT/C% in blood or saliva. Classification thresholds were based on the study cohort’s empirical distribution (Supplementary Figs. 88, 89, and Supplementary Table 14). Hydration status was classified using USG thresholds from exercise physiology literature: USG < 1.010 for well-hydrated (label 0), USG 1.010–1.020 for mild dehydration (label 1), and USG > 1.020 for significant dehydration (label 2). BG dynamics were classified by glucose decrease rate during exercise, linked to fatigue levels. Three ranges of glucose change rates were defined to indicate increasing fatigue severity. To ensure timely biomarker detection and feasible experimental design, we used retrospective windows for aligning fatigue labels with continuous sensor data. Alignment rules are shown in Supplementary Fig. 90. Most participants developed moderate fatigue symptoms—glucose decline, lactate rise, and muscle fatigue—after 40 min of exercise, with protein insufficiency emerging by 90 min, aligning with typical exercise physiology (Supplementary Fig. 91)42. Among individuals reporting no or mild SF (43.1%), 10.9% had severe glucose deficiency. Moderate to severe SF was typically accompanied by notable BG insufficiency and abnormalities in hydration, BL, MF, and protein levels (Fig. 4d). These findings demonstrate that fatigue feature thresholds can serve as reliable indicators for accurate fatigue assessment. The most common grade of fatigue-related factors was Grade 0 (ranging from 40.2–62.3%), followed by Grade 1 (21.1–38.3%) and Grade 2 (11.8–21.7%), with a mild imbalance in the ratio of Grade 0 to the combined Grades 1 and 2 (0.67–1.65), making it suitable for ML (Supplementary Fig. 92). Label pairs showed moderate to strong positive correlations (Pearson’s r = 0.43–0.76) (Supplementary Fig. 93), highlighting their joint role in indicating exercise-induced fatigue. These shared physiological drivers allow the model to efficiently learn common features, thereby boosting its predictive performance and robustness. To address high collinearity between monitored biomarkers (MEF & MDF: r = 0.99), MEF was excluded from further analysis (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 94).

Prior to model training, the data for each subject were normalized to their respective baseline (initial) values to reduce inter-subject variability and improve population-level generalization. Twelve classification algorithms, including simple linear models (LR and SGD Classifier), traditional non-linear models (SVM, KNN, and GNB), ensemble tree models (DT, RF, ET, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and LightGBM) and a deep-learning model (CNN) were used to model fatigue features. These algorithms excelled in 5-fold cross-validation (accuracy > 0.9) and leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation (accuracy > 0.7) (Supplementary Figs. 95–100). Analysis of 10 rounds of LOSO showed that all models had AUC values above 0.66 for the evaluation of various fatigue labels, indicating good stability and generalizability across individuals. The combined learning curve and LOSO validation results indicate that tree-based models outperform both baseline and complex deep learning models for intelligent fatigue assessment in this study. As sample size grew, baseline models’ accuracy (such as LR and GNB) converged to around 0.7, while moderate complexity models like RF and ET reached an accuracy of 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. 101). The performance scores (derived from the weighted sum of 5-fold CV and LOSO validation metrics) of most tree-based models were higher than that of CNN. Moreover, increasing the parameters of CNN failed to significantly improve its performance; instead, it performed even worse than the simpler CNN model (Supplementary Table 15). These findings suggest that increased model complexity does not guarantee better performance and is dependent on dataset characteristics. The suboptimal performance of CNN may likely stem from a fundamental mismatch between its internal decision-making mechanism and the structure of tabular data. While tree-based models are inherently well-suited to the logical structure of tabular data characterized by small sample sizes, short time series, and low dimensions. Thus, for this study’s fatigue assessment tasks, choosing a moderately complex model is more practical. Finally, the best models for SF (XGBoost), BG (ET), hydration (ET), BL (KNN), MF (RF), and protein supply (ET) were selected based on the combined performance scores from 5-fold cross-validation and LOSO cross-validation (Fig. 4f–h, Supplementary Figs. 102–107, and Supplementary Tables 16, 17). Using the selected models, we developed a comprehensive fatigue index (fatigue index = (ScoreSF + ScoreBG + ScoreBL + ScoreProtein + ScoreHydration + ScoreMF)/6) to quantitatively assess fatigue levels.

To identify key features for multidimensional fatigue assessment, we used the SHAP method, combining 5-fold and LOSO cross-validation results, to quantify feature importance (Fig. 4i–k and Supplementary Table 18). Cortisol and MDF consistently showed high SHAP values across all fatigue labels (Supplementary Figs 108 and 109). Additionally, ablation experiments showed that removing Cortisol and/or MDF significantly decreased model accuracy, highlighting their critical role in fatigue assessment (Supplementary Fig. 110). High Spearman's rank correlations further confirmed their stability (Supplementary Figs. 111 and 112). Cortisol relates to CNS stress, and MDF measures muscle fatigue43,44, highlighting fatigue management by both central and peripheral systems. Additionally, feature importance analysis revealed varying indicator contributions at different fatigue levels (Fig. 4j), emphasizing the need for a multidimensional approach to enhance real-time fatigue monitoring accuracy.

Monitoring exercise-rest fatigue using the intelligent FatigueVisual system

We developed a FatigueVisual intelligent decision-making and real-time monitoring system by deploying optimized fatigue assessment models on a cloud server and integrating them with a mobile app for visualization (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Movie 6). To evaluate system efficacy, three participants underwent three self-controlled conditions (no intervention [C], real-time monitoring intervention [MI], and self-awareness intervention [SI]) in a balanced Latin square order (Supplementary Table 19). This design counter-balanced condition sequences across participants to control for potential learning/fatigue effects and across-condition interference. A 1-week washout period separated sessions to minimize carryover effects. To standardize exercise intensity across different conditions, bicycle resistance and pedaling cadence were adjusted to maintain heart rate at 70–80% of maximum during cycling. The MI group received real-time system-guided adjustments to carbohydrate (CHO), electrolyte water (EW), and protein intake, along with exercise intensity modifications (details of the supplementation plan were described in the “Methods” section) (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 113). Compared with SI and C groups, the MI condition for all three participants demonstrated consistently lower fatigue levels (Fig. 5d). The fatigue relief rates of the MI groups, calculated as (Cfatigue index − MIfatigue index)/Cfatigue index × 100%, ranged from 43.28% to 51.82%, which was substantially higher (1.82-fold) than that of the SI group (22.18–31.35%) (Fig. 5e). At 2-h postexercise, the MI group’s fatigue relief rate (48.11%) was 1.98 times that of the SI group (24.34%). By 24-h postexercise, it reached 97.94%, compared with 75.48% in the SI group (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, the obtained wearable data and physiological marker levels such as BG, BL, SF score, USG, and SUN demonstrated more rapid and pronounced improvements following MI interventions, further validating the effectiveness and physiological consistency of the system (Fig. 5g, h, and Supplementary Figs. 114 and 115). In the MI group, timely EW supplementation for Participant 1 at the 60-min mark led to a maximum increase in the Na+/K+ ratio of only 12.53% during exercise, which was much lower than the 25.23% increase in the SI group and the 53.75% increase in the C group (Supplementary Fig. 116). The USG level in the MI group decreased by 1.1% after EW supplementation, with the reduction being 1.90 times that in the SI group (0.58%) (Supplementary Fig. 117). After CHO intake, the sweat glucose level in the MI group increased by 28.56% at 30 min, which was 5.18 times that in the C group (5.51%) and 2.27 times higher than that in the SI group (12.56%) (Supplementary Fig. 118). BG in the MI group decreased by 17.39% at 30 min postexercise, which was less than that in the C (26.47%) and SI (33.78%) groups (Supplementary Fig. 119). SUN in the MI group was slightly above baseline 30 min postexercise (17.74 ± 1.11%) but nearly returned to the baseline (-0.28 ± 0.58%) after 24 h, while that in the C and SI groups remained elevated (C, 15.71 ± 0.92%; SI: 7.41 ± 1.31%) (Supplementary Fig. 120). The system fatigue assessment outcomes also agreed strongly with those of traditional fatigue scoring methods, with an accuracy of 87.27 ± 15.12% and a misjudgment rate of 6.15 ± 10.36% (Fig. 5i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 121). These findings suggest that the dynamic monitoring–real-time detection–immediate intervention framework effectively maintains a balance in electrolytes and energy, speeds up waste clearance, reduces negative physiological changes, prevents chronic fatigue buildup, and aids in quicker recovery. However, the findings are limited to a small, relatively homogeneous population and cannot be extrapolated to diverse populations (older adults, patients with chronic diseases, etc.) without further study. Further studies with larger groups, longer monitoring, and personalized calibration are needed to confirm its broader applicability and long-term effectiveness.

Discussion

We have successfully developed the FatigueVisual system to address critical challenges in continuous, multidimensional fatigue monitoring and management across exercise–rest scenarios. By leveraging flexible electronic fabrics with dynamic BTP regulation in the JFM and 3D gradient micropillars as a sweat management device, the e-ASRHT achieves sensitive sensing under ultralow sweat flows and reduces sensing noise during high-intensity activity, overcoming the limitations of conventional wearables that cannot operate effectively under dynamic sweat conditions. Integrated with physio-biochemical sensors and an AI-powered framework, the system helped subjects significantly reduce fatigue and accelerate recovery. However, the system has not yet undergone validation in large scale cohorts, long-term (weeks-long) wear stability, or adaptability to real-world athletic scenarios. Future work will focus on expanding the dataset, incorporating additional exercise types, and refining personalized intervention logic to further enhance its generalizability and practical utility.

Fabrication and assembly of the e-ASRHT

Preparation of superhydrophobic fabric

Fabrics used for the experiments were woven cotton, treated with conventional desizing, boiling, and bleaching processes prior to use. The superhydrophobic coatings were prepared using the following protocol: 2.0 g of PFOTES was dissolved in 198 g of ethanol and stirred vigorously for 2.0 h. Next, 10 g of P25 TiO2 nanoparticles were added to the mixture and stirred continuously for 30 min to form the homogeneous suspension. Cotton was immersed in the coating suspension for 5 min and dried at room temperature for 10 min to obtain double-sided superhydrophobic cotton fabrics.

Fabrication of the ECI and printing of the e-ASRHT

The ECI was composed of liquid metal microspheres (LMMS), AgFKs, and a polymer matrix of SIS-EVA. LMMS were first prepared by mixing 4.0 g of LM with 20 ml of ethanol and sonicating the mixture for 5 min in an ice bath by an ultrasonic cell-breaking system (Biosafer 650-92, Nanjing Biosafer Co., Ltd.). After standing overnight, the upper solvent layer was decanted and 30 wt% of SIS-EVA (SIS/EVA = 1:1) toluene solution was added to the LMMS vials and vortex-mixed (Scientific Industries Vortex-genie 2) for 10 min, and AgFKs (10 nm) was added and vortexed for a further 20 min to obtain a homogeneous ECI. The structures of physiological and biochemical sensing electrodes were designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software (AutoCAD, Autodesk). Flexible e-ASRHT patches were fabricated via high-precision, semi-solid extrusion 3D printing, employing a specially formulated, highly conductive and permeable ink. The working, counter, reference, iontophoresis, and EMG electrodes were printed onto superhydrophobic cotton fabric using the ECI via a 3D bioprinter. In addition, the temperature electrode was printed using a flexible graphene-based ink (Voltera, Canada). The sensing area of the working electrode was circular (diameter: 1 mm), and the distance between adjacent electrode sites was 3.0 mm. Subsequently, the printed electrodes were cured in an oven at 100 °C for 30 min. The print line width was tuned by adjusting the pressure and extrusion speed of the printer.

Plasma treatment of the e-ASRHT

One side (noted as the top) of the superhydrophobic-finished fabric printed with biochemical sensing electrodes was tightly covered with a PET tape mask with a laser-cut pore pattern (pore sizes ranging from 0.4 to 2.0 mm, with 3.0 mm spacing between adjacent pores). The biochemical sensing electrode points were precisely aligned with the centers of the pores in the tape mask. On the other side (designated as the back) of the fabric, a PET tape mask without any pore pattern was applied. The mask-covered e-fabric was placed in an oxygen plasma etcher (PE100RIE, Plasma Etch Inc.) and processed for a specified duration with an O2 flow rate of 50 cm3 min−1 and a power of 300 W. Owing to the patterned mask, only the spotted regions were exposed to the plasma environment.

Fabrication of PDMS micropillars and assembly of e-ASRHT patches

The layout of the PDMS gradient micropillars was designed using AutoCAD, and PDMS templates were fabricated via photolithography. The template was treated with OTS for vapor-phase silanization to facilitate the release of PDMS micropillars. PDMS (a 10:1 ratio of base to curing agent, Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) was poured into the mold, degassed under vacuum for 10 min, and baked at 80 °C for 30 min. A total of 0.1 g of SiO2 and 0.05 g of SDS were dispersed in 10 ml of 1.0 wt% PVA solution and sonicated for 10 min to form a hydrophilic coating. A long-lasting superhydrophilic layer was formed by spraying the solution onto the plasma-treated PDMS, followed by heating at 110 °C for 10 min in an oven. Subsequently, both the PET fabric and the absorbent paper were laser-cut into specific structures to serve as the absorbent pad. Finally, the JFM, PET fabric, PDMS micropillars, and absorbent paper were assembled in sequence and bonded using Sil-Poxy silicone adhesive.

Preparation of iontophoresis hydrogels

First, 4 ml of AA was added to 7.0 ml of a 10% (w/v) glucose aqueous solution. Subsequently, 0.3 g of CS (deacetylation degree: 93.5%, molecular weight: 66,000 g·mol−1) was added and stirred to form a homogeneous dispersion (Solution A). Solution B was prepared as follows: 0.1 g of BA was dissolved in 3.0 ml of deionized water. Next, 0.005 g of MBAA and Solution B were added to Solution A and ultrasonicated for 10 min. Subsequently, 0.015 g of APS was added and thoroughly mixed under rapid stirring. Afterward, 3.0 wt% carbachol was added, and the mixture was poured into a mold and heated at 55 °C for 3.0 h to form a carbachol anode hydrogel. The cathode hydrogel was fabricated using the same procedure, with the only modification being the replacement of carbachol with NaCl.

Fluid simulation

The outlet flow rates of SP and DP were simulated using FLUENT in ANSYS Workbench 2024 R1. Key details of the two-phase flow simulation are summarized as follows: To accelerate computation and conserve memory, a simplified 3D asymmetric turbulent two-phase flow model was adopted as the multiphysics model. The turbulence was modeled using the standard k–ε model with standard wall functions. A geometric model was first constructed, with dimensions closely matching those of the actual membrane and pores. Material properties were then assigned, and the laminar flow and phase field modules were activated. A two-phase flow system consisting of “Air” and “Water, liquid” was defined, with water set as Fluid 1 and air as Fluid 2. The density and viscosity of water were set to 1000 kg·m−3 and 0.89 × 10−3 Pa·s, respectively. Additional parameters included a temperature of 293.15 K, reference pressure of 1.0 atm, and liquid-gas surface tension of 72.8 mN·m−1.

The following simplified assumptions were adopted for the fluid flow process in this study to simplify the simulation calculations and focus on the core transport mechanism: First, the flow is assumed to be incompressible, meaning that the fluid density remains constant when pressure changes; second, for both fluid phases, the influence of gravity is incorporated, and a linear function is used to characterize the wettability gradient on the membrane pore surface; third, the complex and interlaced pore network is simplified into a bundle of parallel, non-intersecting, cylindrical capillaries, ignoring the irregularity of pore shape, cross-sectional changes, and tortuosity of flow paths; the fabric is assumed to be a rigid, non-deformable solid, whose pore size and porosity do not change with the pressure difference. Finally, the mesh was manually refined, and a relatively fine meshing scheme was selected for the simulation.

Biosensor preparation and characterization

Reference electrode preparation

The shared Ag/AgCl reference electrode was prepared by drop-casting 2.5 μl of 0.1 M FeCl3 onto the surface of the 3D-printed electrode for 30 s, and then rinsed with deionized water. Then 1.0 μl of PVB reference mixture (prepared by dissolving 79.1 mg of PVB and 50 mg of NaCl in 1 ml of methanol) was drop-coated onto the Ag/AgCl surface and then dried overnight.

ISE preparation

The Na+ selective permeable membrane (ISM) solution was prepared as follows: 1 mg sodium ionophore X, 0.55 mg NaTFPB, 33 mg PVC, and 65.45 mg DOS were dissolved in 660 μl of THF, followed by continuous stirring to ensure thorough mixing. The K+ ISM solution was prepared as follows: 2 mg Val, 0.5 mg NaTFPB, 32.7 mg PVC, and 64.7 mg DOS were dissolved in 660 μl of THF. The NH4+ ISM solution was prepared as follows: 1.0 mg nonactin, 0.14 mg KTpClPB, 30 mg PVC, and 65 mg DOS were dissolved in 660 μl of THF. 1.0 μl of Na+ ISM solution, 1.0 μl of K+ ISM solution, and 1.0 μl of the NH4+ ISM were drop-cast onto the working electrode and dried overnight.

Enzyme sensor preparation

Enzyme biosensors were prepared using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E, CH Instruments). AuNPs were deposited on the surface of the working electrode by cyclic voltammetry in 1.0 mM HAuCl4 (in 0.1 M KCl) at a voltage range of 0.2–1.0 V, with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 for 10 cycles to increase the surface area and improve the sensitivity. A Prussian blue (PB) transducer layer was deposited by applying cyclic voltammetry for 10 cycles for glucose and 20 cycles for lactate in a fresh solution containing 2.5 mM FeCl3, 2.5 mM K₃[Fe(CN)6], 100 mM KCl, and 100 mM HCl. The enzyme mixture solutions were prepared by adding 10 μl of 50% GA and 10 μl of the enzyme solution (1000 U ml−1) to 1.0 ml of the 1% BSA solution (w/v). Then 0.5 μl of the enzyme mixture was dropped onto the surface of each enzyme sensor electrode and dried at 4 °C overnight. To improve the linear response range and stability of the lactate sensors, 1.0 μl of PU (20 mg ml−1 in THF) was applied as a diffusion-limiting membrane. To prepare the urea potentiometric sensor, 0.5 μl of the enzyme mixture solution containing urease (100 U ml−1) was added dropwise to the surface of the NH4+ potentiometric sensor prepared above and left at 4 °C overnight. Finally, Nafion solution (2.0% v/v) was added dropwise to its surface to improve detection stability.

MIP sensor preparation

Electropolymerization of cortisol MIP membranes was carried out by cyclic voltammetry in 1× PBS (pH = 7.4) deposition solution containing 2.5 mM FeCl3, 5 mM K3[Fe(CN)6], 5 mM KCl, 100 mM HCl, and 6 mM cortisol template in the range from −0.2 to 0.9 V at a scanning rate of 50 mV s−1 (10 cycles). After electropolymerization, the cortisol template was removed from the MIP matrix by overoxidation (15 cycles) in 1× PBS solution at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 in the range of −0.2 to 0.8 V. Testosterone MIP membranes were similarly synthesized by replacing the cortisol template with a testosterone template. As a control, a non-imprinted polymer (NIP) was prepared and performed using the same procedure, but the template molecule was not included in the deposition solution.

In vitro sensor characterization

All in vitro biosensor characterizations were performed using a multichannel electrochemical workstation (CHI 1430, CH Instruments) with open-circuit potentiometry and amperometry. For in vitro ISE sensor characterization, analyte solutions were prepared in deionized water in the range of 0.1–500 mM for NaCl, 0.5–20 mM for KCl, and 0.05–10 mM for NH4Cl. For in vitro enzyme sensor characterization, analyte solutions were prepared in PBS in the range of 5–250 μM for glucose, 1 to 50 mM for lactate, and 0.05–50 mM for urea. For the in vitro MIP sensors, cortisol concentrations ranged from 0 to 1000 μg l−1 and testosterone concentrations ranged from 0 to 500 ng l−1. The ISE sensors were characterized by open-circuit potentiometry, while the enzyme and MIP sensors were evaluated by chronoamperometry at an applied potential of 0 V.

Both potentiometric and chronoamperometric responses were recorded at 1 s sampling intervals, except for long-term monitoring, where the sampling interval was set to 6 s to minimize data overload. Temperature effect characterization was performed on a ceramic hot plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and EMG signals were obtained and verified via an Arduino module (AD8232, AD8226).

Rigid components description

The device uses a 3.7 V, 80 mAh lithium battery, providing a theoretical runtime of about 13 h (Runtime = 80 mAh/6.16 mA). With an online charging module, it supports 72-h monitoring. For data handling, the system follows a ‘collection-storage-transmission’ approach. It samples data at 0.167 Hz, storing it temporarily in local memory. This design conserves power by using Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to transmit accumulated data in bursts at specific intervals.

On-body evaluation of e-ASRHT for exercise fatigue monitoring

Subject recruitment

The verification and evaluation of the e-ASRHT were conducted on healthy human subjects in accordance with protocols approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Nankai University (NKUIRB2024174). Participating subjects were recruited from the campus and neighboring communities through posted advertisements. This study included 10 healthy participants (6 males and 4 females; age 23–30 years, BMI, 18.9–23.5). Detailed information is provided in Table S13. Participants were in good health and had no underlying metabolic diseases or depression disorders. All subjects gave written informed consent before participating in the study. Additional written informed consent for publication of photographs was obtained from the individual shown in Figs. 1e and 5b. Facial features were blurred to further protect participant’s privacy.

On-body monitoring by e-ASRHT

The skin was cleaned with alcohol pads before the e-ASRHT applied. First, the physiological (temperature, EMG signals) and biochemical data (glucose, lactate, cortisol, testosterone, Na+, K+, NH4+ and urea) were collected at rest after IP sweat induction. After 30 min of resting-state data collection, subjects performed moderate-to-high intensity exercise with room temperature maintained at 21–32 °C and relative humidity at 29–35%. Cycling experiments were conducted using an electronically braked leg cycle ergometer (Keep K0102C exercise bike) to maintain a heart rate of 70–80% of the maximum heart rate throughout the ride by varying the ergometer’s resistance and the subject’s pedaling frequency. In exercise fatigue intervention experiments, three participants were recruited to complete the group of exercise fatigue monitoring experiments (C, MI, and SI). This study employed a balanced order design (Latin square design) to arrange three experimental groups. The three subjects were exposed to the three conditions in different orders. Subject 1: C-SI-MI; Subject 2: SI-MI-C; Subject 3: MI-C-SI. Each subject received the three conditions in a unique sequence, thereby achieving overall balance and randomization of the order and avoiding potential learning effects, fatigue effects, or cross-interference between interventions caused by a fixed order. A 1-week interval was maintained between each experimental session to minimize the carryover effects on subsequent conditions. To ensure consistency in exercise load across different conditions, the bicycle resistance and participants’ pedaling frequency were adjusted to keep their heart rate consistently within 70–80% of the maximum heart rate during cycling. The C group received no intervention or supplementation. The SI group received tailored nutritional supplements based on their individual physiological requirements, while the MI group was provided with targeted supplements (including EW, CHO, or protein) as recommended by the e-ASRHT system. Supplements include three types: electrolyte water (Powerade ZeroTM, 48 mg sodium, 68 mg potassium, 44 mg calcium and 11.8 mg magnesium), CHO-calorie gels (GuTM; 23 g CHO, 0 g protein, 100 calories per pack) and impact whey protein-calorie drinks (MYPROTEINTM; 20 g protein, 0.1 g fat, 0.7 g CHO, 85 calories per bottle)45.

Collection and in vitro analysis of body fluid samples

Resting sweat was induced and collected using a Macroduct 3700 Sweat Collection and Analysis System with a pilocarpine hydrogel. For saliva collection, participants were asked to avoid food, drinks, and gum for 30 min beforehand. After rinsing the mouth with water, saliva samples were collected using Salivette collection tubes. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 2.0 min, the supernatant was collected, and cortisol and testosterone concentrations were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (H094-1-2, H090-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). For blood glucose and lactate measurements, the fingertip was disinfected with alcohol, and a blood sample was collected from the side of the finger using a lancet. Glucose and lactate concentrations were immediately analyzed with an Accu-Chek blood glucose meter (Roche, China) and a Lactate Scout 4 analyzer (EKF, Germany), respectively. For human hydration analysis, urine samples were collected and analyzed for USG using a refractometer. When collecting samples for multiple reference simultaneously, priority was given to collecting subjective fatigue scores due to their timeliness, followed by blood lactate, blood glucose, saliva, and urine samples.

Validation against gold standards

A cohort of five healthy subjects was recruited to participate in a standardized 2-h exercise protocol (20 samples randomly selected from 4 time points). Simultaneously, the e-ASRHT sensing device was worn by each participant for the duration of the exercise to continuously monitor dynamic changes in target biomarkers. The sweat sample collection strictly followed the procedures: samples were collected from the area adjacent to the wearable sensor patch to avoid introducing bias due to differences in sweat gland distribution. Sterile, specialized sweat-absorbing pads were used to collect sweat every 10 min at fixed intervals, with the start and end times of each collection accurately recorded. Immediately after collection, the sweat-absorbing pads were transferred into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 3050 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to extract the sweat. The biomarker concentrations were then quantitatively analyzed using established laboratory gold-standard methods. The concentrations of Na+, K+, and NH4+ were determined using ion chromatography technology (Thermo Fisher-ICS-1100). Urea, glucose, and lactate concentrations were measured using enzyme-based colorimetric assay kits (S0574M, S0201M, S0227S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Cortisol and testosterone were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (H094-1-2, H090-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). To eliminate the effects of sweat secretion delay and collection time discrepancies, we paired the average sensor readings during the corresponding sweat collection time period with the laboratory measurements from the same sweat sample. This approach ensured that both the sensor data and the laboratory results reflected the biochemical state of the same physiological period.

Questionnaire for exercise fatigue evaluation

The Borg CR10 Scale was used to assess subjects’ self-perceived fatigue levels before and after the exercise. The scale is a numerical 10-level category scale: 0 indicates no perceived fatigue and 10 is the maximum perceived fatigue leading to the end of the exercise. Assessments were conducted verbally before and after the exercise, as well as at 10-min intervals throughout the protocol.

ML pipeline for fatigue assessment

Model selection for fatigue feature classification

(1) Data preprocessing The dataset comprised sweat biochemical parameters (glucose, lactate, Na+, K+, urea, NH4+, cortisol and testosterone), physiological signals (EMG, MEF/MDF), and basic participant information (weight, sex). To account for variations in sensor response times, different sampling intervals were applied: 6 s for rapid-response indicators such as glucose, 1 min for EMG signals, and 10 min for cortisol and testosterone. Raw EMG signals were preprocessed in Python (v.3.8), involving baseline correction, demodulation, smoothing, and Fourier transform, before extracting MEF and MDF for subsequent analysis. To synchronize with physiological signals, biochemical sensor data were manually aligned by applying a 10 min delay, compensating for natural sweat latency. Various methods for handling missing data—including mean, median, mode, forward fill, backward fill, linear interpolation, polynomial interpolation, spline interpolation, and KNN imputation-were systematically compared in Python (v.3.8). The optimal imputation method was selected based on a comprehensive evaluation of three key metrics (completion rate, average mean change, and variance change). To reduce inter-subject variability and improve population-level generalization, the data for each subject were normalized to their respective baseline (initial) values prior to model training. (2) Machine learning selection A total of 12 machine learning algorithms were employed for training, including Random Forest, Extra Trees, LightGBM, XGBoost, Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbors, Multi-layer Perceptron, AdaBoost, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and CNN. All models were developed in Python using the previous normalized data. To comprehensively evaluate model performance in terms of accuracy, stability, and generalizability, 5-fold cross-validation and subject-level leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation were implemented. The optimal model was selected based on a composite score derived from both 5-fold cross-validation and LOSO evaluations, considering key metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC. (3) SHAP analysis Following model training, SHAP values were computed to interpret the contribution of individual features to prediction outcomes. Additionally, the feature importance patterns derived from five rounds of 5-fold cross-validation and ten folds of LOSO validation were analyzed to assess the robustness of the optimal model.

Construction of the FatigueVisual system

A FatigueVisual system was developed by integrating of six optimal predicative models for different fatigue labels to monitor real-time multimodal data and provide timely fatigue warnings during physical exercise. This integrated framework involves the deployment of data preprocessing and optimal ML modules on a cloud server. Physio-biochemical data gathered in real time via Bluetooth are uploaded to the cloud, where they undergo preprocessing and are subsequently analyzed by the selected models to determine fatigue levels and characteristics. With the transmission, processing, and analysis of data, the system outputs in real time the predicted results for each fatigue labels as well as the weighted average score of the six fatigue label predictions. Results were relayed to a smartphone application for graphical visualization.

Statistical analysis

OriginPro 2025b (OriginLab Software, USA), GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) and the online data analysis platform (CNSknowall) were used to process the experimental data. The experimental data were presented as mean ± s.d. Statistical differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired one-tailed Welch’s t-test. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used to analyze difference among groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001.
