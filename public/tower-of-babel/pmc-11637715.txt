Imatinib is effective in some PDGFRA/B‐negative hypereosinophilic syndromes: A step closer to unveiling underlying mechanisms
Authors: Giuseppe G Loscocco, Grzegorz Helbig
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) comprise different clonal, reactive, or idiopathic disorders characterized by elevated eosinophil levels and subsequent organ damage. Kim et al. in a multicentre, single‐arm, prospective phase II study, treated 32 patients with PDGFRA/B‐negative HES with imatinib at the dose of 100–400 mg daily. Respective overall and complete haematological response rates were 46.9% and 18.8%, and the median time to response was 1.5 months. The molecular basis of responses was identified by using whole‐exome and whole‐transcriptome sequencing in 11 patients. STAT5B::RARA, PAK2::PIGX, and FIP1L1::CHIC2 fusions were identified in responders, whereas RNF130::BRAF and WNK1::KDM5A were identified in non‐responders. Imatinib could be a therapeutic option for some, possibly clonal, PDGFRA/B‐negative HES.Commentary on: Kim et al. Phase II trial of imatinib mesylate in patients with PDGFRA/B‐negative hypereosinophilic syndrome. Br J Haematol 2024; 205:2305‐2314.Keywords: eosinophilia, hypereosinophilic syndrome, imatinib, PDGFRA, PDGFRB

The current classification of eosinophilia‐associated disorders (although eosinophilia is not universally present) proposed by the 2022 World Health Organization classification of myeloid neoplasms 1 and International Consensus Classification, 2 although requiring clinical, laboratory, and histopathological findings, is largely based on the results of molecular studies and includes myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (MLN‐eo‐TK). 3 Depending on the presence of specific molecular alterations, patients with MLN‐eo‐TK can be divided into two groups: imatinib (IM)‐ responsive (e.g. PDGFRA/B‐rearranged patients) and IM‐unresponsive (e.g. FGFR1 or JAK2‐rearranged patients). Idiopathic hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) still remains a diagnosis of exclusion. 1 , 2

Kim et al. 4 in a multicentre, single‐arm, prospective phase II study, treated 32 patients with PDGFRA/B‐negative HES with IM starting from the dose of 100 mg daily for the first week, up to 400 mg daily at the investigator's discretion, if no haematological response was observed. Prior to study enrollment all patients were negative for BCR::ABL1, and also for canonical PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and FGFR1 rearrangements. Only steroid administration was allowed during and within 4 weeks before starting study treatment at the daily dose ≤15 mg/day of prednisolone.

The primary end‐point was the overall haematological response (OHR) rate, defined as the sum of the complete haematological response (CHR) and partial haematological response (PHR) rates. As a secondary end‐point, the author investigated the genetic characteristics associated with the haematological response to IM through whole‐exome (WES) and whole‐transcriptome sequencing (WTS), available for 11 patients. The median age of enrolled patients was 50 years and 20 patients (62.5%) were male. CHR rate was 18.8% (6/32) whereas the PHR rate was 28.1% (9/32), resulting in an OHR rate of 46.9%, with the median time to response of 1.5 months. Among the six patients who achieved CHR, the median time to CHR was 1.75 months (range, 1–11); 4/6 patients increased IM to 400 mg/day and 5/6 sustained CHR up to the 24th cycle (around 2 years).

Among many somatic mutations identified, PCDHB13 and SIGLEC16 variants were detected in 4/6 non‐responders, whereas mutations in both DSPP and HRNR were detected in 2/5 responders. Of note, no mutations in myeloid neoplasms‐associated genes have been documented by WES. Moreover, transcriptome analysis led to identify some fusion transcripts; STAT5B::RARA, PAK2::PIGX and FIP1L1::CHIC2 fusions in patients with haematological responses whereas RNF130::BRAF and WNK1::KDM5A in non‐responders.

Some major limits of the current research are acknowledged by the authors. First, the small sample size (11/32 patients) used in WES and WTS analysis, limited the possibility to detect other gene fusion transcripts including atypical PDGFRA/B rearrangements, and capability to discriminate the gene expression profile and mutations between responders and non‐responders. Moreover, functional studies of these rare variants and rearrangements as well as before and after IM comparisons between responders and non‐responders were not performed. Second, pathological confirmation through tissue biopsy was not performed in the assessment of haematological response. Other limitations are related to the possibility that a concomitant dose ≤15 mg/day of prednisolone may confound responses to IM and a failure to diagnose a lymphocytic variant of HES through immunophenotyping or clonal analysis of the T‐cell receptor gene rearrangement. Despite the above limitations, this phase II trial demonstrates that IM is highly effective in around 20% of PDGFRA/B‐negative HES patients, controlling eosinophil counts and symptoms, while other patients are refractory. The question raised by the study is: which forms of HES are idiopathic and which are not? Although functional studies are lacking, it is possible that the responses observed in these patients are the result of the presence of alternate tyrosine kinases sensitive to IM to varying degrees. Accordingly, some of these forms may represent clonal HES, whereas non‐responders are true idiopathic HES.

The benefits of IM at 100–400 mg daily in patients with PDGFRA/PDGFRB rearranged HES have been demonstrated in numerous studies. 5 , 6 , 7 It was shown that haematological and molecular response rates for these patients were over 90%–95%, and what is more, it is possible to effectively discontinue the drug and achieve a long‐term molecular eradication in a significant proportion of patients. 8 , 9

For PDGFRA/B‐negative HES, IM could be offered as a steroid‐sparing agent or subsequent line of treatment for those who failed previous therapeutic options. Of note is that in a daily clinical practice, the use of this drug is often random without clinical and biological justification. In order to identify responsive patients, it was suggested to apply a short course of IM at 100 mg daily without a need to escalate IM dose. 10

It is true, however, that in contrast to PDGFRA/B‐rearranged patients such spectacular effects of IM treatment have not been demonstrated for PDGFRA/B‐ negative HES patients. Response rates varied from 25% to 40% and were short‐lived in most patients. 11 , 12 Nevertheless, some of these patients responded quickly to treatment, which may indicate the presence of previously unknown genetic alterations particularly sensitive to IM, but such in‐depth research was not performed. Moreover, it was shown that IM may be especially effective in producing a long‐lasting response in those presenting myeloproliferative/myelodysplastic features. The presence of such features as dysplastic eosinophils, anaemia and thrombocytopenia, an elevated vitamin B12 and tryptase levels as well as increased bone marrow cellularity and reticulin fibrosis were associated with better response to IM. 13 However, these findings were not validated. Of note, the best responses to IM were also demonstrated in patients without myeloproliferative criteria. 14 It would undoubtedly be beneficial if the response to IM could be predicted by clinical data only. Unfortunately, this is not the case.

In the light of the above‐mentioned findings, the question of who is a candidate for IM treatment, outside of PDGFRA/PDGFRB rearranged HES, remains unanswered. It also seems that PDGFRA/B‐negative patients may need a higher dose of IM to achieve and maintain a response; however, treatment escalation may result in increased toxicity. Moreover, concerning this patient population, we could ask: (i) how long should they be treated before the treatment is considered ineffective? (ii) should we consider IM dose tapering or even discontinuation in those who achieved long‐lasting response? (iii) in the light of the recent approval of anti‐IL‐5 mepolizumab, is IM a therapeutic option in the subset of mepolizumab refractory idiopathic HES?

It cannot be ruled out that some of the IM‐responsive patients have other point mutations or rearrangements that are IM‐sensitive. To date, at least 7 other than FIP1L1 (4q12) and 31 other than ETV6 (12p13) gene partners have been identified for PDGFRA and PDGFRB, respectively. 15 The heterogeneous nature of genomic aberrations and the promiscuity of fusion partners may limit the diagnostic accuracy of current cytogenetic approaches, requiring high‐throughput next‐generation sequencing methods. Further comprehensive investigations with larger sample sizes are required to validate the predictive genetic biomarkers for IM in PDGFRA/B‐negative HES, overcoming the limits of the current research.
