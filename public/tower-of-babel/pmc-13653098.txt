Just transition, double bind or both? Climate change action by North American community land trusts
Authors: Jason Simpson Spicer, Ruoniu (Vince) Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCan socially or community-owned enterprise models systematically enable equitable climate action, as has been previously suggested by case study research? To empirically measure and test this proposition, we systematically surveyed all North American community land trusts (CLTs), which primarily serve lower-income households. We found that a majority of CLTs engage in climate action, with mitigation efforts more prevalent than adaptation efforts. Statistical analyses confirm CLTs’ rate of action cannot solely be explained by their climate hazards exposure. This suggests they can concurrently address economic precarity whilst enabling climate action, thereby enabling a “just transition”. Nonetheless, CLTs fail to engage in climate action as often as they would like, reflecting financial constraints shaped by policy choices. This suggests the simultaneous existence of a climate “double bind”, whereby CLTs must, to a degree, choose between economic security and climate goals. We conclude by identifying potential policies to address this double bind.

Introduction

Nobel prize-winning economists (Tirole, 2017; Stiglitz, 2009; Holmstrom, 1999; Ostrom, 1990) and leading social scientists (Mazzucato, 2023a, 2023b; Henderson, 2020; Collier, 2018; Dahl, 1986; Galbraith, 1952) have long highlighted the potential benefits of social and community ownership models. These models have also been studied by urban and regional scholars, who have specifically examined how they can advance equitable and just outcomes in cities (Healey, 2015; Amin, 2009; Moulaert and Ailenei, 2005). In particular, urbanists have suggested that democratically controlled “collective housing tenure” (Shelby, 2025) models, which remove residential space and urban land from speculative markets, can simultaneously address economic precarity while also remediating environmental and climate vulnerabilities (Lamb et al., 2023a, 2023b; Veronesi et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2018; Skerratt, 2013), thereby helping to realise a “just transition” to a sustainable economy (Newell and Mulvaney, 2013; Shi and Fitzgerald, 2023; Mundaca et al., 2018). Research on climate action by organisations operating with such ownership models, however, has largely consisted of small-N case studies. This begs the question: can these models comprehensively and systematically prioritise climate change alongside economic goals, to move towards a “just transition” as posited? Or is this merely wishful thinking? Might they instead need to prioritise their economic goals — such as tenure security for lower-income households — over climate action? If this latter proposition is true, might it affirm the existence of a climate justice “double bind”, where one goal must be set aside for the other (Eriksen and Visentin, 2024 ; Leichenko and O’Brien, 2019, 2008; Donald and Gray, 2019)?

To examine this question, we surveyed all known community land trusts (CLTs; N = 355) in the US and Canada. CLTs are a collective housing tenure model that can decommodify urban land and maintain housing and commercial space affordability; their primary beneficiaries in both US and Canadian cities are low-income, marginalised populations (Spicer and Trana, 2025). Our bi-national surveys included the first known climate-related questions asked of an entire class of socially and/or community-owned entities in either country. We supplement our survey results with interview data from CLT participants in major cities in both countries, to yield four findings.

First, North American CLTs not only systematically engage in climate action, but they also do so at a greater rate than expected based on their exposure to location-specific climate hazards. Second, they are most likely to engage in climate mitigation actions, which directly result in financial savings with shorter payback periods. These include energy efficiency measures, renewable energy installations and green infrastructure investments. Relatedly, they are least likely to engage in adaptation actions to prepare for or address climate-related disasters, in part due to their cost, suggesting a foregrounding of economic concerns even when acting with respect to climate. Third, CLTs in both countries fail to engage in climate action as much as they would like to, and consistently identified financial obstacles as the key barrier to further action; policy in turn appeared to be a key factor in shaping these financial obstacles. Finally, Canadian CLT climate action is slightly stronger, though not at statistically significant levels, than US action. This is consistent with scholarship on “small differences that matter” in the social welfare and housing policy frameworks between the two countries (Suttor, 2011, 2016).

CLTs’ climate efforts ultimately suggest an urban “just transition” may be partially realised through the actions of non-state, non-market actors, such as those operating with social and community ownership models, including collective housing tenure models. But their actions may also be limited, to a degree, by a climate justice “double bind”. We therefore find evidence in support of both a just transition, and a climate double bind, with respect to CLTs, which we suggest may apply more broadly to other social and community enterprise models, as well. We conclude by identifying and reviewing actions to address this bind, such as enhanced social-public partnerships and tax expenditure reform.

Literature review: connecting climate action to social and community enterprise

We situate our analysis in two key literatures: on the possibilities of achieving a just transition against the limits of the climate justice “double bind”; and on the potential role of social or community enterprise models, such as CLTs, to realise more transformative and just urban and regional economies, including with respect to urban climate action.

Equitably addressing climate change: just transition, double bind or both?

As the world’s city-regions and nations seek to transition to a post/low-carbon and green economy, extensive literature has examined the challenges associated with doing so in an equitable and just manner. This is often referred to as a “just transition”, i.e., one which does not disproportionately burden low-income and marginalised groups within and across societies (Newell and Mulvaney, 2013; Newell et al., 2021; Wang and Lo, 2021; Shi and Fitzgerald, 2023; Fitzgerald, 2022).

To realise such a transition, community organisations and entities representing the interests of low-income and marginalised populations must often simultaneously balance climate concerns with limitations relating to economic precarity (Mundaca et al., 2018). This can result in varying trade-offs. For example, when “dirty” facilities are replaced by “clean” ones (Sovacool et al., 2017), lower-income residents may benefit from reduced pollution and environmental externalities in their neighbourhoods, but their jobs at such facilities may not be replaced with comparable employment opportunities. Similarly, climate disasters and risks disproportionately cause destruction and displacement in low-income “frontline” communities, which are often racialised. This is due to the fact that these groups are more likely to be located on the least desirable land, which is most prone to climate and environmental challenges (Pulido, 2000). Groups living on such land are also more likely to face housing insecurity (Tagtachian and Balk, 2023), and are subject to greater damage in the face of climate change-related disasters (Peacock et al., 2014). Due to their comparative lack of resources, these communities may also require more time to build back after climate disaster (Lee and Van Zandt, 2019; Van Zandt et al., 2012), if they are not displaced entirely (ibid). They may also be displaced through “green gentrification”, which can occur when climate upgrades drive housing values higher, especially in market-rate housing stock (Niu et al., 2025). These dynamics may be exacerbated by national governments’ climate policies, which may systematically fail to channel resources to local groups, who may be best situated to address such challenges in contextually appropriate ways (Colenbrander et al., 2018).

These dynamics are indicative of both a climate “dual vulnerability” (Fakhoury et al., 2025) and a troubling climate justice “double bind” (Eriksen and Visentin, 2024; Leichenko and O’Brien, 2019, 2008; Donald and Gray, 2019). Specifically, the very groups most subject to climate injustice and climate vulnerabilities often face even more pressing economic challenges; this means that they may face pressures to set aside one goal to accomplish the other (ibid). Relatedly, scholars have argued that such dynamics are likely to be endemic in any neoliberal1 political-economic order. This is because such orders rely on profit-maximising market mechanisms to advance “green growth”, resulting in technocratic-corporatist urban sustainability strategies (Teelucksingh et al., 2016) to move to a low-carbon economy (Hickel and Kallis, 2020; Hickel and Sullivan, 2023). Investors’ “dual mandates” (George et al., 2022), in which climate action2 is tied to profits, may not be achievable in less-resourced communities, given the greater cost associated with their heightened climate change exposure, and their reduced resource base from which to extract profits.

Social and community ownership models: towards more just and sustainable urban economies?

Separately, scholars have studied the role that social and community ownership models can play in realising a more sustainable, equitable and just economy, one built on a different institutional logic than the neoliberal political-economic order. We review three points from this literature — with respect to the definitional scope, theoretical mechanisms of relevance and real or imagined benefits of these ownership forms — before discussing its specific relevance to city and regionally scaled climate action through CLTs, and to the existence of a just transition vs. a double bind in cities.

Definitional scope and origins

“Middle way” (Childs, 1936) enterprise models, rooted neither in central state ownership, nor in the profit-maximising logic of arms’ length investor-ownership, are typically embedded in civil society or community bonds, and are often cast as part of the “third sector”, as distinguished from the first (public) and second (private, profit-maximising) (Corry, 2010). The naming convention for this group of models varies across academic traditions, and there can be substantive epistemological and normative differences across them. Radical geographers, for example, historically referenced “alternative economies”, but today prefer “diverse and community economies” to describe these models and their associated liberatory practices (Gibson-Graham and Dombroski, 2020). This naming reflects a desire to move away from centring capitalism as the default to which all others are “alternatives”, as to move towards constructing an economy with a different centre, one with community at its heart. This is consistent with the epistemology of the “social and solidarity economy” term(s), as well (Amin, 2009; Miller, 2010; Spicer, 2023).

In contrast, business and management scholars variably refer to hybrid, alternative, social or community entrepreneurship and enterprise (Tracey et al., 2005; Haugh and Pardy, 1999; Battilana et al., 2017; Davis, 2016). In so doing, they also adopt a normative stance more consistent with Nobel prize-winning economists (Stiglitz 2009; Holmstrom 1999; Tirole 2017; Ostrom 1990), mainstream sociologists (Rothschild 1979) and political scientists (Dahl 1986) who study these enterprise models. These scholars often analyse the potential of social and community enterprise models to exist alongside and within capitalism, as “prefigurative” components (Yates, 2015) of a pluralistic economy. Henderson (2020), for example, called such enterprises “a crucial part of a reimagined capitalism”, a point echoed by Mazzucato (2023a, 2023b). A generation ago, leading US political scientist Dahl (1986), drawing on Canadian political science’s older economic democracy tradition (MacPherson, 1942), concluded that the uneasy marriage between liberal capitalism and democracy was unlikely to be sustained without the sufficient presence of economic democracy, as might be realised specifically through social or community ownership models (which are generally conceived of as a distinct subset of social and community enterprise models). Relatedly, Galbraith (1952) argued that both government and social ownership models, such as co-operatives, could serve as a “countervailing power” against capital, with the latter acting as a competitive threat that could regulate capitalists’ behaviour.

Despite their differences, all these scholars largely agree as to the origins of these models. Though indigenous societies often had their own social and community ownership traditions (Spicer and Kay, 2022), today’s institutionally replicable enterprise models are largely products of nineteenth-century utopian socialism, which incubated these models as a countervision to capitalist industrialisation and urbanisation (ibid). Accordingly, these models featured prominently in early modernist city planning, such as Howard’s Garden City (Hall, 1988), Buckingham’s plan for Victoria (Rockey, 1983) and Fourierian communities (Lewis, 2017 ). Specific social and community ownership models, often incubated by these utopian-social movements directly in city plans and urban policy initiatives, varied by factor of production (land, labour and capital), as is still the case today (Moulaert and Ailenei, 2005), particularly in federal countries like the US and Canada (Spicer and Zhong, 2022). Typically, these models have included various forms of co-operatives and mutual enterprises; community, purpose and/or “civic” trusts (Healey, 2015); and non-profit or profit-limited industrial foundation ownership models. These models involve majority control by actors other than either the state or capital. Accordingly, their collective owners often have fundamentally different goals for these enterprises and organisations than traditional investors, giving rise to different “institutional logics” (Battilana et al., 2017), as detailed below.

As applied to land/housing, the most well-known models include CLTs (Wegmann et al., 2017), in which a tri-partite governance board typically owns and controls the land underneath price- or income-limited buildings (Wang and Spicer, 2024). Other examples include limited-equity or zero-equity housing co-operatives, where residents democratically own shares in the co-operative that directly owns the income- or price-restricted building. Resident-owned communities (ROCs) combine elements of both these models, but apply them to land beneath manufactured housing communities (i.e. trailer or caravan parks; Lamb et al., 2023a, 2023b). Related models with similar traits include mutual housing associations (Stone, 2003), community investment trusts and shared equity models (Ehlenz and Taylor, 2019). While these “collective housing tenure” or “collective land tenure” (Shelby, 2025; Shi et al., 2018) models vary in how they bundle property rights and responsibilities, they all formally foreground community stakeholders in ownership and governance. By design and policy, they are also typically limited to low- and moderate-income households (ibid).

Theoretical bases and mechanisms of action

Deploying Marxian and Weberian theory in complementary fashion, scholars have identified the mechanisms through which such enterprises enable a more equitable and sustainable economy. Through a Weberian institutionalist lens, by embedding ownership in society and community bonds, these models enable the foregrounding of substantive value rationality (wertrational) over instrumental rationality (zweckrational; cf. Kalberg, 1980), giving rise to a distinctive collectivist-democratic organisational rationality and logic (Rothschild, 1979; Spicer and Kay, 2022). From a Marxian perspective, they enable action which supports use-value in place of exchange value (ibid). This enables substantively rational, use-value concerns to be considered in lieu of solely instrumental ones like profit maximisation. Such substantively rational actions may involve paying workers a living wage, or voluntarily embedding environmental sustainability and resilience costs into production. Ultimately, these entities voluntarily choose to embed additional costs, both by internalising the cost of negative externalities (such as the cost of community or employee welfare benefits that other types of entities might shirk) and generating positive externalities (such as a sense of employee and/or stakeholder well-being) in their operation. Notably, study of these entities’ ability to embed climate-specific externalities, negative or positive, has not been empirically well measured across any class of such entities, as discussed below.

General posited benefits and specific importance to urban climate action

Reflecting this theoretical basis, economists and social scientists have long considered the potential benefits of these entities, empirically measuring their effects on workers and communities with respect to retained wealth, improved job satisfaction, better health, and enhanced worker participation and economic democracy (Jackall and Levin, 1984; Rothschild and Whitt, 1986; Schneiberg et al., 2008; Dahl 1986; Wright 2013; Michie et al., 2017; Pérotin, 2016; Schlachter and Ársælsson, 2024).

Among the posited benefits are their ability to internalise environmental costs and consider environmental or climate-related sustainability and resilience actions into their operations (Michie, 2015; Bennett, 2025 ). US Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, for example, argued such models would be critical to delivering a green new deal (White, 2020), and the global co-operative movement was the first worldwide organisation to adopt the UN’s sustainable development goals (ILO, 2015). Though such claims linking ownership to environmental and climate action have been repeatedly made (Case and Taylor, 1979; Upright, 2020; Song, 2016; Spicer and Casper-Futterman, 2023), there has been limited widespread empirical measurement of such action (see Lee et al., 2025, for a notable exception, as applied to the case of employee ownership).

This gap in empirical evidence with respect to direct climate action is particularly pronounced in the case of land/building-related applications of social and community ownership models. Here, the lack of broader evidence is doubly important. Unlike social and community ownership models operating across other factors of production, land/building-related third-sector models are often formally restricted to those with lower or moderate incomes. This restriction may exacerbate the double bind: it creates a selection effect with respect to who participates. If membership is formally limited to the least resourced, participants may be even more likely to prioritise economic concerns over climate-related ones.

Evidence with respect to how these land/building models enable climate action has largely involved qualitative or small-N academic case studies, or from grey literature and news accounts. These sources suggest that collective land/housing tenure models, through their democratic foregrounding of substantive value rationality and use value, can both directly and indirectly enable the realisation of climate-related benefits. They can use democratic ownership and governance to choose to advance equity in adaptation and migration efforts, by directing such action to specifically benefit low- and moderate-income frontline communities (US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), 2022; Veronesi et al., 2022). By retaining resources that might otherwise be extracted by profit-seeking investors, they can specifically adaptively upgrade or rebuild disaster-affected housing for lower-income residents, thereby avoiding disaster-related displacement effects of disaster capitalism, and associated “green gentrification”-related redevelopment (Lamb et al. 2023a, 2023b; Applegate, 2022; Leon, 2019; Grannis 2019, 2021). They can also, when appropriate and desired, help relocate disaster-affected communities (ibid). Overall, Grannis (2021) reviewed cases to show that CLTs can enhance housing resilience and sustainability, provide green space, deploy green infrastructure, support renewable energy and help resettle communities to “higher ground”, all representing forms of direct climate action (consistent with earlier such arguments and qualitative analysis by Leckie, 2013), while indirectly enabling action by providing housing security, providing other community amenities and enhancing economic resilience against financial crisis and shocks.

A HUD report (2022), for example, highlighted the role of CLTs as a tool to adapt to and mitigate climate change in a socially equitable way, highlighting a net-zero price/income-restricted development by a CLT in Tukwila, Washington. In Portland, Oregon, a CLT Executive Director building community resiliency infrastructure articulated how and why CLTs are able to take such action: “CLTs are able to build innovative and sustainable housing because they are driven by their public mission, not profit … The land trust could play a piece of the puzzle for recovery and resilience and push the envelope on energy-efficient homes … (but) developers and builders, if they can’t make money on it, it’s not going to happen. But we’re not about profit. We’re about affordability and stewardship” (ibid). Similar accounts document CLTs adapting to and mitigating climate change in Puerto Rico and Florida (Leon, 2019).

As noted above, Grannis (2019, 2021) reviewed a range of CLT climate actions across different regions and political contexts in the US, identifying specific ways CLTs can partner with local governments to enhance climate action. These actions include seeking improved access to city-owned/controlled land (prioritised for CLTs over profit-maximising investors) and gaining better access to public financing tools and technical assistance. For example, in Texas, a local government provided “financing to the Guadalupe Community Land Trust to help the land trust incorporate green design features into affordable housing projects, such as solar panels and energy efficiency upgrades, to help the land trust build ‘net zero’ CLT homes for lower-income residents” (Grannis, 2019, 741). In Houston, Texas, after Hurricane Harvey caused widespread damage that disproportionately affected housing-insecure populations, the city partnered with a local CLT to fund the development of climate-resilient and permanently affordable housing (Applegate, 2022). In stating its purpose, the Houston CLT’s director stated, “developers build cheap housing in dangerous areas, which risks displacing vulnerable people who can’t afford to rebuild if their house is destroyed” (ibid).

Despite these qualitative cases suggesting that social and community ownership of land and buildings can advance urban and regional climate action, and despite studies showing that these models may expand communities’ overall capacity and well-being,3 thereby indirectly improving their ability to take climate action, empirical studies of the prevalence of such climate action remain limited. How widespread is climate action by specific classes of collective tenure models like CLTs? Do they show systematic evidence of foregrounding environmental and climate action, whilst also simultaneously securing affordable tenure and preventing the displacement of lower-income populations — thereby realising a just transition? If they do exhibit high rates of action, might this merely reflect elevated exposure rates to climate risks, as the literature suggests? Like other efforts to secure a just transition, might CLTs, particularly given selection effects associated with their low/moderate-income focus, be subject to a double bind, in which they must prioritise economic security over climate action? Or might the increase in economic security generate indirect benefits which can enhance their capacity to act to address climate concerns? Either way, might CLTs be more likely to prioritise adaptation (i.e. engage in actions to make hazards-prone properties more resilient to the effects of climate change already affecting them) over mitigation (i.e. actions to make their properties more sustainable and contribute less to and/or prevent climate change), reflecting a need to address immediate economic and physical risks? Conversely, might adaptation be so costly that CLTs instead prioritise lower-cost mitigation strategies? Both possibilities appear plausible based on existing literature, but neither has been empirically examined. Our study is the first known effort to investigate these questions across an entire class of such organisations.

Given the scientifically well-established role of the built environment and cities in generating carbon emissions, pollution and other negative climate externalities (Kamal and Robert, 2009; IPCC, 2022), these gaps in the literature matter. Might collective tenure models, given that they may enable climate action without yielding such displacement, be a “crucial part of a reimagined” greening of the city, to paraphrase Henderson (2020)? Might they enable climate upgrades to housing without generating “green gentrification” (Niu et al., 2025).

Research methods and approach

To begin to address this gap in the literature, we systematically surveyed the entire population of a major class of collective housing tenure models in North America (CLTs) about their climate change-related efforts. First developed by Black civil rights organisers in the US South in the 1960s (Wang and Spicer, 2024), CLTs have since spread across the world, and are a flexible community ownership model for buildings with diverse tenure structures (e.g. multi- and single-family). The surveys were completed in the US and Canada in 2022–2023. The US survey was the first comprehensive effort to generate primary data from the entire population of CLTs in that country in over a decade; no prior US CLT survey had ever asked about climate change or related environmental actions or plans. The Canadian survey was the first national CLT survey of its kind. Both were conducted and developed in partnership with the main national CLT field-building organisations: Grounded Solutions Network (GSN) in the US, and the Canadian Network of CLTs (CNCLT). The authors accordingly developed a series of Likert-scale questions in which respondents were asked to identify their organisation’s interest in addressing and responding to the effects of climate change and environmental degradation, exposure to climate hazards, specific actions they have or are planning to take and barriers experienced in taking or planning such action. The questions were developed based on existing, broader population surveys related to climate action, including those of the Yale Program on Climate Change and the Pew Research Center. The US surveys were conducted approximately 6 months before the Canadian survey. The US questions were therefore subsequently slightly modified for the Canadian context, in consultation with CNCLT. The questions were part of a longer instrument, which inquired about other dimensions of CLTs unrelated to climate action. The climate change-related questions, however, were asked as part of a distinct module within the longer survey. Respondents to the survey were typically either paid or volunteer CLT staff, or volunteer board members. Whilst some respondents may have been CLT residents, in general, residents’ views were not systematically obtained, and are not the surveys’ focus. Rather, the CLT organisation is the primary unit of analysis. Full technical specifications for the complete surveys are publicly available, and includes details on the processes of identifying and contacting all CLTs in both countries (Wang et al., 2023; Trana et al., 2023). A total of 355 organisations were identified, and were found across all regions of both countries, and across most major metropolitan areas (ibid). Overall, the US survey results for the climate change module questions were obtained for 51.2% of the US’s entire population of CLTs (N = 161 of 314); in Canada, results were obtained for 75% of CLTs (N = 33 of 41).

The quantitative survey results were supplemented by qualitative interviews, which were conducted in both countries to “ground truth” (Glaser and Strauss, 1967) the survey results. With respect to climate change specifically, interviewees (N = 8) were selected to assure coverage across both countries, as well as diverse political (right/left) and environmental (urban/rural; significant known climate hazards) contexts in both countries. These semi-structured interviews included qualitative feedback from CLTs to contextualise and situate their survey responses, such as the role of policy, finance and organisational capacity in explaining their actions.

To analyse results of the climate change-related questions, a two-step process was deployed. First, we produced aggregate, descriptive statistics, which detail the level of interest, types of actions and hazards, and barriers experienced by CLTs in both countries. Based on these questions and on our review of existing literature, we then transformed the data, as to enable us to conduct a range of statistical tests to answer the proposed research questions. This second step involved converting many of the Likert-scale survey results into dichotomous, categorical/binary results, to which a series of χ2 one-way (goodness-of-fit) and two-way (independence) tests were applied. The nature of these tests and their relationship to the research questions are further elaborated below.

Results and findings

Below, we present four key findings. First, an outright majority of CLTs in both countries are systematically taking or planning action to address climate change at rates not readily explained by their climate hazards exposure alone. Second, climate mitigation (i.e. sustainability) efforts appear more widespread than adaptation (i.e. resilience) actions, reflecting the relative perceived cost-benefit of each, which are in turn shaped by policy. Third, CLTs would like to act to address climate risks at a greater rate than they currently are, with financial considerations acting as the leading barrier to such action. This is particularly the case for those CLTs which are already engaged in or planning action. Finally, within North America, Canadian CLTs appear to be engaging in action at a slightly stronger rate than US counterparts. We also conclude that CLTs appear to be engaging in widespread climate action to benefit low-income persons, which suggests they can systematically advance climate justice. But they are also, to a degree, subject to a climate double bind, with financial concerns limiting their climate action, particularly with regards to adaptation efforts.

Climate action undertaken by majority of North American CLTs, at rate greater than expected

North American CLTs not only systematically desire to engage in climate action, but they also actually act at a greater rate than expected based solely on their exposure to location-specific climate hazards.

As reported in Figure 1, approximately 70% of respondent CLTs in both countries state their organisations identified and/or referenced climate change as an issue for their organisation to address. In addition, approximately 56% of US CLTs and 61% of Canadian CLTs (a χ2 one-way goodness-of-fit test of statistical significance between these two rates was not significant, p = 0.71) are either currently acting or in the planning stages of action to address climate change-related environmental impacts.

Climate change reference and action rates among CLTs. Bar chart comparing US (n = 161) and Canadian (n = 33) CLTs on two measures: climate change referenced by CLT Board of Directors (70% for both US and Canada) and planning or taking action to address climate change (56% US, 61% Canada).

Ideally, to test how strong or significant this reported level of action was as compared to exposure risks, we would compare these results to surveys of synthetically matched (i.e. propensity score matching or coarsened exact matching) non-CLT homeowners’ associations and/or properties held by such associations. Alternatively, we might regress CLTs’ specific properties against known climate risks. Neither type of data, however, currently exists. Instead, we tested the organisational-level rates of climate action against their self-reported climate risk exposures. Because we also asked survey respondents about their exposure to climate hazards, we can exactly match actions to exposure, and can test associations between these variables for statistical significance.

Specifically, we first asked respondents to identify the existence of six major climate threats that were relevant to their properties (windstorms/hurricanes, temperature extremes, flooding, extreme participation, drought and wildfire). These results are represented in Figure 2; survey respondents were free to select as many hazards as they had experienced. We then calculated the proportion of the combined bi-national population of CLTs which reported exposure to at least one of these hazards, as compared with those that reported no such exposure(s). Overall, a slight majority of CLTs (56%) reported not having any experience with major climate change-related hazards or threats. US respondents, however, were consistently more likely to report multiple exposures, and therefore had a higher exposure rate across all major categories of climate threat, with windstorms/hurricanes the most common US threat (p̂ = 20%), followed by temperature extremes and floodings (p̂ = 18%), with the latter two the most common (p̂ = 12%) in Canada.

Weather events affecting CLTs by country. Grouped bar chart showing weather events reported by CLTs in the US and Canada, including wildfire, droughts, extreme precipitation, flooding, temperature extremes and windstorms or hurricanes.

To enable statistical testing of action vs. exposure rates, we then consolidated climate hazard exposures into a binary variable, coding CLTs which were exposed to a climate hazard as 1 (p̂ = 44%). We then cross-tabulated the shares of CLTs which were exposed to any hazard, against whether they had indicated they were planning/taking action (p̂ = 57%, as noted earlier). We then performed two different χ2 tests for independence (i.e. two-way tests) on this cross-tabulated data. First, we tested for differences in action rates by exposure rates, to see if climate hazards-exposed CLTs were acting a greater rate than those that were not. We find that climate hazards-exposed CLTs acted at a rate of 71%, while those unexposed act at a rate of 48%, a difference that is statistically significant (p = 0.001). This result, however, does not address whether these rates of action are higher or lower than expected, based on hazards-exposure rates. We therefore retested these proportions against a different expected frequency, which is the overall exposure rate. The intuition guiding this hypothesis is as follows: given that CLTs serve low/moderate-income persons with limited resources who may be more likely to be located on hazard-prone land, we might therefore expect them to only respond at their rate of exposure, i.e. 44%. Here, we perform two different one-way or goodness-of-fit tests, and we find that the rate of action by hazards-exposed CLT of 71% is higher than the overall exposure rate of 44% (p < 0.001). Interestingly, there is no discernible difference between the action rate of non-exposed CLTs of 48%, and the overall exposure rate of all CLTs of 44% (p = 0.41). We interpret this result to mean that, if we assume that CLTs merely act out of self-interest to address to problems on their hazard-prone land, we cannot readily explain the action level of non-exposed CLTs, which are acting at a frequency as if they, too, were hazards-exposed.

Overall, these results are consistent with the “climate justice” proposition that CLTs’ distinct institutional logic, which enables the foregrounding of substantive value rationality over solely economic concerns, enables their ability to take climate action.

CLTs prioritise mitigation over adaptation, regardless of hazards exposure

Second, we find that North American CLTs are more likely to engage in climate mitigation actions than in adaptation efforts. Analysis of these results, taken in conjunction with qualitative interview data, suggests this reflects the structure and nature of policy incentives, which are geared towards improving the cost-benefit payback period of mitigation actions, and less effective in addressing comparatively costly adaptation efforts to address serious climate hazards. This is consistent with a foregrounding of economic concerns, and suggests the existence of a climate “double bind”.

Specifically, we asked respondents to indicate which actions they had undertaken from a list of 13 possible property and land-related actions (developed based on existing literature and in conjunction with the CLT field-building organisations) as reported in Figure 3. Overall, the most common actions taken in both countries were related to energy efficiency, including implementing passive design, net zero and LEED standards, with more than half (p̂ = 51%) of US and approximately one-third (p̂ = 33%) of Canadian CLTs affirming action on this front.

We then coded these actions as either primarily mitigative (i.e., sustainability-focused, including preventive action such as energy efficiency) or adaptive (i.e., resiliency-focused, such as administering disaster recovery funds or relocating buildings) to produce summary statistics on their prevalence. Overall, 57% of CLTs across the two countries engaged in at least one mitigative strategy, whilst just 23% engaged in adaptive efforts.4 Beyond those referenced above, examples of mitigative efforts included installing community septic systems, microgrid systems, green roof systems, among others. Beyond relocating buildings or administering disaster recovery funds, other primarily adaptive actions we identified included actions such a flood, wind or fireproofing buildings, and opening disaster preparedness centres and resilience centres, allocating savings for disaster preparedness, or increasing the number of units in response to climate-related migration.

We then conducted two different two-way χ2 independence tests. The first two tests addressed whether the proportion of CLTs engaging in mitigative action (vs. those that did not) or adaptive action (vs. those that did not) statistically differed by whether they were exposed to a climate-related hazard.5 We might expect CLTs with climate hazards-exposure to prioritise adaptative over mitigative efforts. In a second test, we examined whether adaptive action rates by exposure group are statistically higher or lower than the overall hazards-exposure rate for all CLTs. These analyses are represented in Table 2. We find that hazards-exposed CLTs are, at a statistically significant rate, more likely to engage in mitigative action than non-exposed CLTs, but they are more likely to engage in adaptive action, as well. We also find that both hazards exposed and non-exposed groups are less likely to engage in adaptive efforts at rates suggested by their exposure rates.

These findings are somewhat unexpected: they suggest that mitigative efforts predominate across all sub-groups of CLTs, but that the CLTs most likely to engage in both mitigative and adaptive efforts are those which are hazards exposed. Our interview data helped explain these results. Interviewees suggested that CLTs prioritise mitigation actions — such as energy efficiency and renewable energy installations, or green infrastructure investments — because they result directly in financial savings, with shorter payback periods. As one interviewees stated, when asked about how and why they focused on certain climate actions over others, “I can’t say it’s entirely driven by cost, but cost-benefit is a big part of it, more so than any sense of what’s most important for us or for the planet …. if we can figure out how make a change in a way that’s cost-neutral or even accretive, we start there …” Affirming Colenbrander et al.’s (2018) analysis of the ways central governments may shape local climate action, the respondent went on to articulate specific state and local policy mechanisms, in part funded with US federal money from the Inflation Reduction Act, which enabled a range of mitigation efforts to be undertaken at virtually no cost, which would also yield financial savings (with respect to lower property “running costs”) fairly quickly.

Other CLTs interviewed in both countries identified how these policy-linked financial dynamics explain their inability to engage specifically in adaptation actions at a greater rate, further consistent with a foregrounding of economic concerns. As another interviewee stated, “We know we are at a significant risk long-term for flooding … but there’s no (policy) money we’ve been able to identify for the necessary retrofits … at least not until we’ve had a serious flood … it’s only a matter of time.”

Action rate is lower than desired, primarily due to financial constraints

Third, CLTs in both countries fail to engage in climate action as much as they would like to. They consistently identified financial obstacles as the key barrier to further action, more so than political considerations or organisational capacity.

Specifically, as noted in results reported in the first results sub-section above, while prevalence of action is widespread (57% in the two countries combined), it is lower than these organisations’ stated interest levels (70%). We conducted a two-way χ2 test on the difference in these response rates; they are statistically significant (p < 0.001).

What explains the gap between the desire to act when compared to the actual rates of action? We asked survey respondents to name their top three barriers and obstacles to taking action, providing a range of options which identified specific types and sub-types of financial, political or organisational capacity barriers; the list was developed in consultation with both countries’ field-building organisations. These results are reported in Figure 4. Overall, two of the three listed funding-related options were key barriers in both countries: funding for implementation and funding for planning.

Based on the outsized role of funding as a key variable in these results, we then further consolidated our response codes into a nominal binary categorical variable, with two possible codes: one for financial and one for “other”. Was the barrier primarily financial (there were three distinct finance-related barrier categories, see Figure 4)? Or was it some other barrier type (including both political and organisational-capacity related barriers, such as staff capacity and leadership, resident/board awareness and support, or political support)? Overall, of those organisations which named specific types of barriers, 88% named at least one financial barrier, whilst just under 40% named any other type of barrier.

Interview data affirmed, however, that even if political factors were deemed less important, financial barriers were nonetheless partly a function of policy. As another respondent stated, “market-rate housing gets all kinds of tax breaks, including for climate upgrades, but we don’t always qualify.” The respondent then went on to identify ways in which some of their residents don’t have enough income to benefit from certain tax policies, and how the CLT organisation, as a non-profit (US) or charity (CA), may also not be eligible or not qualify for policies which use tax expenditures (i.e. tax exemptions, deductions and credits) to incentivise climate action.

Canadian action CLT slightly stronger than in the US

Finally, we examined whether there might be differences between Canada and the US which might be consistent with insights from comparative housing market and political economy-related social welfare frameworks. Such comparativist institutional frameworks have long argued that while the US and Canada belong to similar “families” of capitalist economies with associated similarly social policy, welfare and non-profit/voluntary sector regimes (Hall and Soskice 2001 ; Esping-Andersen, 1990), Canada’s welfare state and approach to social policy, both with respect to direct and partnership-based government intervention, is slightly more robust than in the US (Salamon and Anheier, 1998). With respect to housing and to social housing specifically, this may be one of the “small differences that matter” between the two countries (Suttor, 2011, 2016).

Although US and Canadian CLT action and interest rates in Figure 1 appear almost identical, we further analysed overall action rates by hazards exposure by country. Our null hypotheses, that there might be a small, but possibly not statistically significant difference between countries, was supported by the analysis. Overall, Canadian CLTs experiencing climate hazards act at slightly greater rates than their US counterparts, as do those not experiencing such hazards. As the sample size grows larger over time with the growth of the CLT sector, the difference in climate action rates may develop to reach statistical significance, and may warrant follow-up study.

Conclusion and discussion

Despite longstanding claims that social and community ownership models can foreground values such as those advancing climate resiliency and sustainability action, to date there had been scant empirical evidence in support of it, particularly in relation to land and housing. Our evidence suggests CLTs are systematically attempting to engage in climate action, but not to the degree they would like, and that financial barriers are a key reason why, particularly with respect to adaptation. We suggest, therefore, that whilst CLTs can be a tool to achieve climate justice in cities by enabling frontline communities to direct and marshal resources towards securing climate-resilient housing, such efforts are to a degree also impeded by the existence of a climate double bind. These findings are limited by the fact that we were unable to test our results against a true control group of non-CLT properties serving lower-income populations, as to conduct more robust causal statistical analysis. Future studies, however, might seek to generate data as to enable such comparison.

Ultimately, given the dual selection effects that may characterise CLTs (i.e. they serve populations with fewer resources to address climate change, on land in greater need of climate action), this double bind is not surprising. It suggests that only through more systematic public-social or public-community partnerships (Fainstein, 1990; Spicer et al., 2024) to enable third-sector entities to scale up (Healey, 2015) in ways which are responsive to contextually variable needs, can CLTs achieve their potential to realise a just transition in the built environment. Interviewees highlighted the importance of systematic partnership with local governments, and with values-aligned private/social capital sources, to obtain support in acquiring land and accessing financial tools and technical support, particularly with respect to adaptive efforts. This affirms existing literature about the role of local government and alternative finance in enabling climate action (Colenbrander et al., 2018; Knox-Hayes, 2017).

A key question which remains is how to pay for such policy interventions. We note that while high-income democratic countries exhibit considerable variation with respect to how they tax housing, both the US and Canada systematically offer comparatively high levels of tax expenditures (e.g. income and capital returns which are exempt from tax) to unrestricted, market-rate housing (Wexler, 1996). In the US, for example, the tax expenditure policy on housing capital gains costs over $46 billion annually (US Congress, 2024). This annual tax expenditure equals the production cost of nearly 200,000 affordable housing units (Government Accountability Office, 2023), nearly four times the entire stock of CLT housing units in the two countries combined today (Spicer and Trana, 2025). Greater deployment of policies which restrict such tax expenditures and redirect associated increases in marginal tax revenue to funding climate action through low and moderate income-targeted collective housing models like CLTs might be an appropriate place to start.
