Mechanically-driven expandable patch promotes tracheal defect reconstruction via synergistic microcurrent and mechanical force cascades
Authors: Zhenyu Zhao, Jing Liao, Long Wang, Jijun Sun, Yujie Xie, Yu Chen, Chang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracheal defects constitute severe respiratory disorders arising from multifactorial etiologies, with current therapeutic modalities significantly constrained by anatomical limitations, compromised vascularization at anastomotic sites, and frequent graft rejection. These challenges are further compounded by the inability to deliver continuous and quantitatively controlled regenerative signals, thereby limiting the efficacy of tissue regeneration and vascular reconstruction. Herein, we designed a mechanically driven, 3D-printed piezoelectric system (PFT) capable of transducing mechanical energy into therapeutic cues upon exogenous ultrasound activation. By leveraging the synergistic interplay between ultrasound-induced micro-expansion of the patch for effective defect occlusion and the simultaneous electromechanical transduction of regenerative signals, this system enables coordinated enhancement of both structural sealing and vascular reconstruction, thereby facilitating efficient and integrated tracheal defect repair. As a sustainable external modulation method, the mechanically-driven expandable patch system can serve as a broadly applicable strategy to enhance regenerative therapy for various systemic organs dependent on vascularization.Subject terms: Biomaterials, Tissue engineering, Implants

Introduction

Tracheal defects, resulting from tumor resection, stenosis, infection, trauma, or congenital malformations, represent a severe and potentially life-threatening respiratory condition1. These defects can lead to progressive airway obstruction, respiratory distress, or fatal asphyxiation, with reported mortality rates ranging from 5-9% and exceeding 50% when complicated by fistula formation2–5. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, current clinical interventions, including tracheal anastomosis, autologous tissue transplantation, and artificial tracheal grafts, remain inadequate for addressing large or complex defects. Tracheal anastomosis is constrained by the deep location and limited extensibility of the thoracic trachea, which reduces the repairable segment length and increases the risk of postoperative stenosis6,7. Additionally, the absence of rapid vascular integration at the anastomotic site contributes to poor long-term outcomes. Autologous tissue transplantation, although biologically compatible, involves extensive donor-site trauma and presents high complication rates, such as ischemic necrosis and granuloma formation, frequently culminating in reconstructive stenosis8,9. Artificial tracheal transplantation, on the other hand, suffers from poor vascularization, high infection susceptibility (particularly in contaminated surgical fields), and immune rejection, which can result in severe complications such as mediastinitis or erosive vascular hemorrhage4,10. These limitations collectively underscore the urgent need for next-generation biomaterials that can effectively bridge tracheal defects while promoting long-term tissue integration.

Tissue engineering has emerged as a promising alternative; however, current strategies are hindered by an inability to simultaneously replicate the native trachea’s biomechanical and biological complexity. Repair materials must exhibit sufficient tensile strength, appropriate elastic modulus, and compliance to withstand dynamic respiratory cycles involving airflow, pressure fluctuations, and mechanical strain11–13. Yet most engineered scaffolds fail to mimic these properties, leading to structural failure such as collapse, fracture, or dislocation during physiological activities like coughing or swallowing14–16. Furthermore, the success of tracheal regeneration profoundly relies on vascular reconstruction at the implant site: insufficient angiogenic stimulation at the implantation site frequently results in ischemic necrosis and suboptimal tissue regeneration17–19. Currently, most tracheal repair materials primarily rely on inert materials for mechanical support or utilize chemical and physical methods combined with bioactive substances to promote tracheal regeneration7,8,11,20–22. Conventional biomaterials deliver pro-regenerative cues mainly through pre-immobilized growth factors or surface-tethered ligands, providing static, spatially confined signals with only short-lived efficacy. Furthermore, inert scaffolds preserve structural integrity yet lack dynamic signaling capacity, whereas chemically or physically modified matrices depend on an initial burst release that rapidly depletes their bioactive payload. Therefore, these materials fail to provide sustained, spatially and temporally regulated delivery of pro-regenerative cues, severely limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, while achieving effective in situ sealing of tracheal defects, how to sustainably modulate vascular regeneration and tissue repair has emerged as a critical challenge urgently requiring resolution.

Recent studies have demonstrated that piezoelectric biomaterials can convert mechanical inputs into bioelectrical cues, and ultrasound has been used as a non-invasive trigger to enhance such electromechanical stimulation in several regenerative settings23,24. However, most of these platforms primarily focus on electrical stimulation for tissue regeneration and do not address the airway-specific requirement of immediate and robust defect sealing under dynamic respiratory loading. To address these multifaceted challenges, we engineered a mechanically driven expandable patch that provides sustained mechanical reinforcement, adaptive defect closure, and programmable regenerative stimulation. This patch enables dynamic modulation of its biomechanical and bioactive functions, thereby adapting to the evolving stages of tracheal repair (Fig. 1). This system couples a 3D-printed piezoelectric scaffold (PLLA-FF-TA, PFT) with a hydrogen nanobubble-loaded hydrogel Nanobubble (NB) (GelMA-TA@NB, GN) that undergoes ultrasound-responsive expansion. Under exogenous ultrasound, the patch exhibits controlled micro-expansion to seal the defect while converting mechanical input into localized electrical cues to activate angiogenic pathways. Using ultrasound as a non-invasive trigger, the system provides a sustained and tunable regenerative microenvironment during tissue repair. Specifically, the piezoelectric scaffold promotes vascular regeneration via Piezo1-Notch signaling, and ultrasound-triggered cavitation in the GN hydrogel enables localized, on-demand H₂ release to further enhance endothelial activation. In vitro and in vivo results demonstrate strong adhesion, effective tracheal closure, and improved tissue regeneration. Programmable ultrasound enables sustained, precisely timed regulation of output voltage (0–2 V) to dynamically modulate Piezo1-Notch activation and angiogenesis. In both goat and rabbit models, four weeks of sequential stimulation achieved 100% tracheal closure and patency, with a 24.65% increase in tissue regeneration efficiency. As a scalable, patch-based approach, this system represents a sustainable and controllable external modulation strategy applicable to vascular-dependent organ regeneration.

Results

Design and fabrication of mechanically-driven expandable patch

The design of the mechanically driven expandable patch was informed by a hierarchical engineering strategy, formulated to meet the dual and interdependent demands of tracheal defect repair, namely the provision of immediate and robust mechanical sealing, alongside the facilitation of long-term, biologically driven tissue regeneration. To achieve these concurrent goals, we engineered a composite system that integrates two synergistic components: a piezoelectric 3D-printed scaffold capable of transducing mechanical stimuli into localized bioelectrical signals, and an ultrasound-responsive hydrogel matrix embedded with hydrogen nanobubbles designed to undergo controlled cavitation-induced expansion and gas release (Fig. 2A, B). This composite design was developed through a stepwise fabrication process to simultaneously provide mechanical support, dynamic defect sealing, and pro-regenerative stimulation. Initially, biocompatible poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) scaffolds were fabricated using extrusion-based 3D printing, chosen for their favorable mechanical robustness, biodegradability, and compatibility for respiratory tissue interfaces25. We then functionalized these scaffolds with a piezoelectric hybrid coating by immersing them in a solution of L-diphenylalanine (FF) and tannic acid (TA), allowing a self-assembled peptide-polyphenol nanolayer to form on the polymer surface. This process yielded a PLLA-FF-TA (PFT) scaffold matrix aiming to transduce ultrasound-induced mechanical perturbations into localized electrical signals (Supplementary Fig. 1)26. Process parameters for scaffold fabrication were detailed in Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 2. Morphological analysis (Fig. 2D) revealed homogeneous lamellar structures in the 3D-printed scaffolds, featuring 0.4 mm interlayer spacing and controlled pore diameters ( ~ 200 μm). Following TA modification, the PFT scaffolds exhibited evident chromatic darkening, suggestive of successful phenolic interaction at the polymer interface (Fig. 2D). Further spectroscopy corroborated the presence of the FF/TA coating, revealing a characteristic absorption band around 2100 cm−1 indicative of hydrogen-bonding interactions (Supplementary Fig. 3). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the modified scaffold surface revealed nanotubular structural formations, collectively affirming the successful incorporation of TA and FF on the PLLA scaffold matrix. Optical microscopy confirmed the preservation of the scaffold’s geometric regularity, while revealing distinct surface topography in the PFT group relative to unmodified PLLA controls (Fig. 2E). At higher magnification, SEM micrographs revealed that the surface of the PFT group featured self-assembled nanotube structures, supporting the morphological and chemical fidelity of the piezoelectric surface engineering (Fig. 2E).

Subsequently, the GelMA-TA@NB (GN) hydrogel expandable system loaded with H₂ nanobubbles was constructed, wherein ultrasound-triggered cavitation of the nanobubbles aims to induce in situ hydrogel expansion, achieving effective defect sealing (Fig. 2B). In addition, placement of the hydrogel precursor within the grid followed by ultrasound triggering of GN hydrogel formation resulted in a slight volumetric expansion (Fig. 2F). Morphological analyses further demonstrated that the hydrogel matrix exhibited a porous architecture with uniformly dispersed nanobubbles visible on the surface, as validated by SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), which confirmed the presence of phosphorus (P) elements associated with NB membrane composition (Fig. 2G, H). TEM (Transmission Electron Microscope) can be utilized for observation of the behavior of nanobubbles in both pure water and protein solutions27,28.TEM confirmed the uniform morphology of nanobubbles in the hydrogel precursor solution (Fig. 2G)29. Furthermore, NB was fluorescently labeled and observed using confocal microscopy, which further proved successful NB encapsulation within the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 2I). Hydrogen release kinetics, assessed via cumulative release profiling (Supplementary Fig. 4), indicated a markedly elevated release rate in the ultrasound-triggered group (PFT@GN + US), peaking approximately 5 h post-stimulation. In contrast, minimal and delayed H₂ liberation was observed in the absence of ultrasound activation, confirming the critical role of exogenous triggering for efficient gas delivery. Moreover, the degradation curve of the composite patch (in PBS) revealed an accelerated degradation rate within the first 20 days ( > 50% mass loss), attributed to rapid hydrogel resorption, followed by a gradual decline reflecting the slow degradation of the PLLA based scaffold (Supplementary Fig. 5A). This biphasic degradation behavior supported the temporal coordination of short-term defect sealing with long-term scaffold support, aligning with the regenerative objectives of tracheal reconstruction. Similar two-stage profiles were observed in collagenase and in vivo, with rapid early mass loss over 10–15 days and a subsequent slower phase extending to 85 days (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Although in vivo degradation was slightly faster and more variable initially, the overall kinetics were consistent, enabling early support followed by gradual degradation.

Following a comprehensive characterization of the hydrogel components and validation of their ultrasound-responsive expansion behavior, the gel precursors were mixed directly onto the surface of the PFT scaffold and crosslinked in situ under ultraviolet irradiation, resulting in the formation of the final composite construct, designated as PLLA-FF-TA@Gel-NB (PFT@GN). PFT@GN patch preparation process is as follows: Briefly, PLLA scaffolds were first fabricated using extrusion-based 3D printing technology, followed by the preparation of PFT hybrid polymer substrates through immersion and self-assembly methods. The piezoelectric properties of PFT amplify ultrasound-induced mechanical effects, synergistically activating endothelial cell pro-angiogenic pathways. Subsequently, the GN hydrogel expandable system loaded with H₂ nanobubbles was constructed, wherein ultrasound-triggered cavitation of the nanobubbles induces in situ hydrogel expansion, achieving effective defect sealing. Finally, the fusion of PFT with the GN expandable system formed the mechanically driven expandable patch, ultimately enhancing tracheal defect repair. This integration strategy yielded a multifunctional patch engineered to provide both biomechanical stability and microenvironmental modulation during tracheal repair. Upon ultrasound activation, the hydrogel exhibited robust volumetric expansion within the scaffold, forming serrated boundaries consistent with asymmetric cavitation-induced stress (Fig. 2J). Orthogonal-view imaging confirmed rapid permeation of the hydrogel precursor into the porous PFT framework under static conditions, followed by immediate gelation and ultrasound-induced structural expansion (Fig. 2J). The core mechanism of the piezoelectric effect involves ultrasonic mechanical waves inducing directional alignment of peptide bond dipole moments in FF, enabling efficient electromechanical transduction through non-centrosymmetric nanotube/nanofiber structures. Recent progress leveraging the piezoelectric effect of FF has yielded notable results30–32.

Piezoelectric activation and structural functionality of the mechanically driven patch

Scatter plot analyses based on linear regression elucidate the interrelations among key functional parameters, including nanobubble destruction efficiency, hydrogel porosity and fluorescence signal retention. Results demonstrated a near-unity R2 value with an intercept approaching zero, indicating a strong proportional relationship between nanobubble disruption rate and hydrogel porosity, alongside an inverse correlation with residual fluorescence intensity. These results further validated that ultrasonic activation effectively disrupted the nanobubbles, concurrently reducing the intensity of fluorescence-labeled nanobubbles and inducing hydrogel volumetric expansion through increased porosity (Supplementary Fig. 6). Systematic modulation of nanobubble rupture and ultrasound parameters enabled precise tuning of piezoelectric signal intensity, as shown in Fig. 3A. Compared with that before ultrasound treatment, the NB diameter was reduced after ultrasound treatment (P < 0.0001), and the particle size distribution density curve exhibited an overall leftward shift, indicating that ultrasound exposure caused the NB population to shift toward a smaller size scale (Fig. 3B, C). DLS (Dynamic Light Scattering) analysis showed a progressive decrease in the mean nanobubble diameter with increasing ultrasound exposure time (Fig. 3D), accompanied by a slight shift in zeta potential (Supplementary Fig. 5C). The marked size reduction after ultrasound likely results from nanobubble rupture, gas release, and lipid reassembly into smaller structures, further highlighting the critical effect of ultrasound.

Quantification of piezoelectric signal generation under various mechanical and acoustic stimuli was conducted using oscilloscope-based measurements. Under a compressive load of 30 N, the PFT@GN composite patch exhibited a peak piezoelectric output of approximately 1.76 V, surpassing all control groups (P < 0.05), thereby confirming the piezoelectric enhancement conferred by the hybrid scaffold-hydrogel architecture (Fig. 3E). To further assess load-dependent behavior, the piezoelectric charge output was measured across sequential mechanical stimuli of varying intensities. Application of weak (10 N), moderate (30 N), and strong (50 N) revealed a consistent positive correlation between load magnitude and voltage output, with statistical differences observed across all conditions in PFT@GN + US (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3F). In parallel, ultrasound-triggered piezoelectric responses were systematically investigated across a range of acoustic intensities while maintaining a constant stimulation duration of 15 s. Under these conditions, the PFT@GN group produced a voltage output of approximately 1.82 V upon stimulation with 0.98 W/cm2, exceeding that of the comparator groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3G). Progressive enhancement of output voltage was observed with escalating ultrasonic intensities from 0.3 W/cm2 to 0.98 W/cm2 and 1.5 W/cm2, with each escalation producing significant gains in signal amplitude (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3H). These findings collectively validate the capacity of the composite patch to transduce both mechanical and acoustic inputs into controllable bioelectric signals, establishing a robust platform for ultrasound-mediated, on-demand piezoelectric signals. As shown in Fig. 3I, J, Supplementary Fig. 7A, B, the output voltage was quantified and subjected to statistical analysis. Under different conditions, including material groups, stimulation intensity, power, and frequency, differences in output voltage were observed.

To evaluate the stability of piezoelectric output and assess the contribution of the FF-TA layer, we monitored changes in the patch’s open-circuit voltage under ultrasound stimulation during long-term incubation in serum-containing medium. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7C, PFT@GN + US maintained a relatively high voltage output over 8 weeks, decreasing from a mean of 1.9 V at week 0 to a mean of 1.75 V at week 2, and then remaining stable from weeks 4 to 8. In contrast, the PLLA control group consistently exhibited a lower voltage of 1.1 to 1.3 V with no obvious time-dependent trend. The persistence of piezoelectric activity was temporally matched to the regenerative process, further demonstrating that TA/FF surface modification of PLLA confers sustained piezoelectric functionality. To enable comparison with standard piezoelectric polymers beyond open-circuit voltage output, we further quantified the intrinsic electromechanical parameters of the materials. Frequency-dependent impedance spectra Z-f was recorded for PLLA, FF, and PFT@GN (Fig. 3K). The intrinsic piezoelectric coefficient d33 measurements showed that PFT@GN exhibited a higher d33 than pristine PLLA and FF controls (Fig. 3L). Based on the impedance spectra, PFT@GN showed the best piezoelectric performance, exhibiting the deepest impedance minimum in the 103 Hz resonance region and a pronounced peak in the subsequent 104 Hz antiresonance region, indicating stronger electromechanical coupling and a more sufficient resonance response. In comparison, PLLA, black, also showed clear resonance and antiresonance features, whereas FF exhibited the shallowest resonance minimum and a blunter peak shape, with resonance characteristics that were less prominent than those of the other two.

To assess the impact of ultrasound-triggered expansion on structural integrity, the mechanical properties of the patch were systematically evaluated before and after activation. Load-unload testing revealed energy dissipation behavior, as evidenced by characteristic hysteresis loops in the stress-strain profiles (Fig. 3M and Supplementary Fig. 8A), indicative of efficient crack propagation resistance and viscoelastic damping. A slight strength reduction (5%) was observed after the first loading cycle, but subsequent cycles demonstrated stable fatigue resistance, underscoring the material’s durability under cyclic mechanical loading. The patch achieved a resilience of 56.59%, outperforming conventional crosslinked systems such as alginate hydrogels (30–40%) and freeze-thawed PVA (Polyvinyl Alcohol) hydrogels (30%)33,34. Further compression endurance testing over multiple cycles confirmed the long-term mechanical stability of the patch, validating its robustness under physiologically relevant mechanical stress (Fig. 3M). Stress-strain analysis showed no alteration in ultimate strength between pre- and post-ultrasonication (Fig. 3N, O). Following ultrasound exposure, the compressive modulus of the patch increased from 1.85 MPa to 2.18 MPa post-ultrasonic activation (Fig. 3P). Nevertheless, both values substantially exceeded those of typical biological soft tissues, such as skeletal muscle (0.01-0.1 MPa) and articular cartilage (0.45–0.8 MPa), highlighting the suitability of the patch for load-bearing tracheal applications35.

We constructed a lumen pressurization setup to gradually apply intraluminal pressure to the defect interface covered by the patch and recorded the burst or leak pressure when obvious leakage occurred (Fig. 3Q). The PFT@GN + US group exhibited higher burst pressure than the non-triggered and control groups, while showing no significant difference from fibrin glue or a PEG (Polyethylene glycol)-based sealant, indicating reliable airtight sealing under pressure gradients relevant to tracheal repair. Lap shear testing conducted according to ASTM F2255 showed that the shear strength of PFT@GN + US was comparable to that of fibrin glue and a PEG-based sealant, indicating clinically relevant tissue adhesion under shear loading (Fig. 3R, S). Furthermore, we performed controlled tensile testing using a calibrated digital force gauge to quantify the maximum tensile debonding force at the patch-tissue interface (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). The maximum tensile force of PFT@GN + US was comparable to that of clinical control materials such as fibrin glue and a PEG-based sealant.

The biocompatibility, in vitro vascularization, and the potential molecular mechanisms of the mechanically-driven expandable patch

To assess the cytocompatibility of the engineered scaffold, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were co-cultured with PLLA and PFT scaffolds. Confocal microscopy results (Fig. 4A) revealed favorable cellular growth states on all scaffold surfaces, which were further corroborated by 3D visualization and SEM (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Polar coordinate analysis of surface-adherent cells orientation (Fig. 4C) demonstrated that HUVECs cultured on the PFT surface exhibited elongated morphology with 87% cellular alignment along scaffold fibers, whereas the PLLA group showed no significant orientation preference (67% alignment, Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). This anisotropic cellular alignment is likely attributed to the FF-induced nanotubular structures aligned along the scaffold axis, as well as local piezoelectric polarization effects that may influence cytoskeletal organization36,37. The tube formation assay was performed in a Transwell system, with the upper insert containing the composite biomaterial (3D-printed scaffold/hydrogel) and the lower well coated with basement membrane matrix. The upper chamber provided biomaterial-derived stimulation, whereas the lower matrix layer supported endothelial cell growth and tubulogenesis. Tube formation was then assessed by fluorescence microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Compared with the PBS control group, all ultrasound-activated material groups promoted endothelial cells to form denser and more continuous tubular networks. Among them, the PFT@GN + US group exhibited the most abundant branch connections and a more complete closed mesh structure in both fluorescence imaging and skeletonization results. The total mesh area, length, and number of junctions reached the highest levels in the PFT@GN + US group, higher than those in PBS and the other controls. In contrast, PFT@Gel+US and PT@GN + US produced only moderate increases, indicating that PFT@GN + US elicits the strongest pro-angiogenic effect through synergistic piezoelectric and H2-mediated activity (Fig. 4D–F and Supplementary Fig. 11C).

Live/dead staining further confirmed good biocompatibility, with cell viability exceeding 95% across all groups, negligible red-stained dead cells observed, and no statistically significant differences in viability between groups (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. 11A). We further established an H2O2 induced oxidative stress model and quantified intracellular ROS (Reactive oxygen species) using DCFH-DA. The results showed that H2O2 treatment increased the ROS signal. Under the same oxidative stress conditions, the PFT@GN + US group showed reduced ROS fluorescence intensity, indicating effective intracellular ROS scavenging under ultrasound-triggered H2 release. Meanwhile, the material itself did not cause an increase in ROS (Supplementary Fig. 12). To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pro-angiogenic effects observed with the composite patch, transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed on HUVEC co-cultured with the PFT@GN + US. The workflow comprised standardized co-culture protocols, high-quality RNA extraction, strand-specific library preparation, paired-end sequencing via the Illumina platform, and multi-dimensional bioinformatics analysis encompassing differential gene expression and functional annotation (Fig. 4H)38,39. A cross-omics differentially expressed genes (DEGs) screening strategy was employed to define gene sets with statistical significance (P-value < 0.05) and biological effect size (expression fold change> 2 or < 0.5). Volcano plot visualization was used to illustrate global changes in the gene expression profile, where the horizontal and vertical axes represented log2-transformed fold changes and -log10-transformed significance levels, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13A). Heatmaps revealed the expression patterns of DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 13B), showing that the PFT@GN + US group exhibited unique DEGs compared to the PBS control group, suggesting that the vascularization-promoting effect of this composite patch might be associated with these DEGs. A total of 265 DEGs were identified, including 189 downregulated and 76 upregulated genes. These DEGs were further screened, and heatmap analysis was conducted specifically for those related to vascular regeneration (Fig. 4I).

To clarify the molecular basis by which PFT@GN + US promotes tracheal repair, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes and compared the results with those of the PBS group. (Fig. 4J). GO analysis showed that, within the biological process category, the enriched terms were primarily associated with vascular regeneration and endothelial activity, including endothelial cell chemotaxis, cell migration involved in sprouting angiogenesis, and positive regulation of VEGF (Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor) production and cellular response to VEGF. These findings indicate that PFT@GN + US reshapes the local repair microenvironment toward endothelial recruitment, migration, and angiogenic sprouting, consistent with the vascular reconstruction observed by CD31 staining and Doppler perfusion. Terms related to response to bacterium and response to lipopolysaccharide were also enriched, suggesting concomitant activation of innate immune and inflammatory regulatory programs relevant to the open-airway environment. Within the cellular component category, enrichment was concentrated in the extracellular region, extracellular space, the external side of the plasma membrane, and endosomal compartments, indicating remodeling of the extracellular milieu together with altered membrane trafficking and endocytic activity. In the molecular function category, enrichment of epidermal growth factor receptor binding, oxidoreductase activity, glutathione binding, and transcription-related functions suggested coordinated activation of growth factor signaling, redox homeostasis, and regeneration-associated transcriptional programs.

KEGG analysis further supported a mechanotransduction-centered repair response (Supplementary Fig. 13C). In particular, enrichment of the Piezo1 signaling pathway implicated mechanosensitive ion-channel signaling, in line with the ultrasound-triggered, mechanically responsive design of PFT@GN + US. Classical repair-associated pathways, including Notch and MAPK signaling, were also enriched, indicating activation of molecular programs governing cell migration, proliferation, and matrix remodeling. In parallel, enrichment of immune-related pathways, such as neutrophil extracellular trap formation and C-type lectin receptor signaling, suggested restructuring of local innate immune responses, consistent with the observed changes in inflammatory infiltration and CD68-related findings. Metabolic pathways, including arachidonic acid metabolism and arginine and proline metabolism, further indicated engagement of inflammatory mediator production and regeneration-associated metabolic adaptation. Additional GO and KEGG analyses of angiogenesis-related differentially expressed genes yielded similar trends (Supplementary Fig. 13D, E). Together, these transcriptomic results aligned with the histological and in vitro vascularization data, supporting the conclusion that PFT@GN + US promotes repair by coordinately activating Piezo1-Notch-associated angiogenesis, immune regulation, and tissue-remodeling pathways. In addition, this study still has certain limitations. Tracheal repair involves not only endothelial cells, but also multiple other cell populations, including epithelial cells, fibroblastic/stromal cells, cartilage-associated cells, and immune cells. Therefore, the RNA-seq results in this study should be interpreted as a mechanistic readout centered on endothelial responses, rather than a comprehensive description of the overall molecular landscape of regenerating tracheal tissue. Although our in vivo histological and immunofluorescence analyses have already demonstrated several repair-related features, including epithelial regeneration, stromal remodeling, cartilage formation, and regulation of macrophage infiltration, these processes have not yet been systematically resolved at the transcriptomic level. Future studies integrating in vivo single-cell transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomics will be needed to further elucidate the multicellular interaction mechanisms among the vascular, epithelial, stromal, cartilaginous, and immune microenvironments.

Notably, the pro-angiogenic effect of the composite patch appeared to be mediated by activation of the Piezo1-Notch axis (Fig. 4K). To verify this mechanotransduction pathway after ultrasound stimulation, we performed western blot analysis of repaired tissues (n = 3) (Fig. 4L and Supplementary Fig. 14). In addition, the Western blot bands from three biological replicates on the same membrane have been fully presented in the Source Data file, allowing readers to further assess the consistency among independent replicates and the reproducibility of the results. Compared with PBS, the PFT@GN + US group showed increased Piezo1, DLL4 and Notch1 expression, together with elevated c-Fos. p-PI3K/PI3K and p-Akt/Akt levels were also increased, indicating activation of PI3K-Akt signaling. Importantly, blockade with the mechanosensitive ion-channel inhibitor GsMTx4 reduced Notch1, p-PI3K/PI3K and p-Akt/Akt, supporting a functional link between Piezo1 activity and downstream Notch-PI3K-Akt signaling. By contrast, Piezo2, KCNK2 and TRPM were unchanged, suggesting that this effect is predominantly Piezo1-dependent. Together, these results support a model in which ultrasound-triggered activation of the composite patch engages the Piezo1-DLL4-Notch1 cascade and PI3K-Akt signaling to promote endothelial migration and vascular lumen formation40. To our knowledge, this work provides a trachea-oriented demonstration of an externally programmable electroexpansion patch, in which ultrasound synchronizes defect-sealing micro-expansion with piezoelectric stimulation to initiate the Piezo1-associated angiogenic signaling cascade at the material-tissue interface. We note that ultrasound-activated piezoelectric platforms have been explored in other regenerative contexts; here, we extend the strategy to the uniquely demanding airway environment and integrate it with a sealing function required for tracheal defect repair. This multigenic cooperative action provides a molecular basis for the patch’s pro-angiogenic efficacy: piezoelectric effects mediate mechanochemical signaling via Piezo1, while the DLL4/Notch1-PI3K axis integrates biochemical signals to guide angiogenesis. In addition, c-Fos is a canonical immediate-early transcription factor and a core component of the AP-1 complex that can be induced by Ca2⁺-dependent signaling downstream of mechanosensitive channels. Its induction provides a mechanistic bridge between the acute Piezo1-mediated Ca2⁺ influx and a sustained transcriptional program associated with endothelial activation and angiogenesis. Consistent with prior evidence that c-Fos/AP-1 participates in pro-angiogenic gene regulation and endothelial migration/proliferation, we included c-Fos as a representative downstream transcriptional effector for validation together with Piezo1, DLL4/Notch1 and PI3K41 (Fig. 4K).

Ex vivo adhesive performance and skin repair efficacy of the mechanically-driven expandable patches

To assess the versatility of the patch’s adhesive performance, ex vivo tissue adhesion tests were performed on porcine skin and muscle using the PFT@GN patch (Fig. 5A). The composite patch exhibited strong interfacial adhesion across both tissue types and retained mechanical integrity under pressurized hydraulic conditions, highlighting its potential applicability across diverse soft tissue environments (Fig. 5B). To assess the therapeutic efficacy of the composite patch in tracheal defect repair, we conducted a series of in vivo surgical interventions in rabbits, complemented by ex vivo adhesion assessments on porcine tissues. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 5C, the surgical procedure involved the pre-placement of a lyophilized PFT scaffold at the site of a surgically induced tracheal defect. Subsequently, the Gel-NB hydrogel precursor was injected into the scaffold matrix and crosslinked via UV irradiation. Controlled ultrasound exposure was then applied to activate in situ hydrogel swelling, enabling real-time monitoring of patch expansion dynamics. A potential concern for ultrasound-triggered activation in the trachea is acoustic impedance mismatch at the air-tissue interface, which can attenuate intraluminal ultrasound propagation. In our design, however, the patch is implanted on the external tracheal surface and activated by transcutaneous ultrasound delivered through soft tissues with coupling gel, minimizing dependence on sound transmission across the air-filled lumen and enabling sufficient mechanical strain for electromechanical signaling. New Zealand rabbits were randomly assigned to six experimental groups: Sham + US (sham surgery with ultrasound stimulation), PLLA-TA@Gel+US (PT@Gel + US) (adhesive-only patch lacking piezoelectric and NB-triggered expansion functions), PFT@Gel + US (piezoelectric patch without NB-triggered micro-expansion/H₂ release), PFT@GN (composite patch without repeated ultrasound stimulation), PT@GN + US (non-piezoelectric patch containing the GN nanobubble module to assess the NB/H₂-related contribution), and PFT@GN + US (full functional patch integrating both piezoelectric signaling and NB-triggered expansion/H₂ release).

Mechanical testing using hemostatic forceps confirmed the adhesive integrity of the patch, which withstood directional mechanical displacement and induced measurable deflection of the adjacent tracheal tissue (Fig. 5D). Complementary in vivo ultrasound imaging revealed that the patch effectively maintained airway patency, offering robust mechanical reinforcement and mitigating tracheal collapse under physiological loading (Fig. 5E). Postoperative evaluations demonstrated that the PFT@GN scaffold achieved effective sealing at the tracheal defect site, with the patch system exhibiting favorable swelling performance post-ultrasound irradiation (Fig. 5F). Intraoperative and postoperative observations showed that the patch closely conformed to the surrounding tissues, with a continuous interface and no obvious edge lifting or delamination. We irradiated tissues with ultrasound at 0.98 W/cm2 and 1 MHz, using an intermittent mode with 1 min on followed by 1 min off. During ultrasound irradiation, temperature changes were measured using a thermal imaging camera. The temperature in the irradiated region increased slowly and reached a maximum of 42.1 °C after 10 min of continuous exposure. This temperature range is consistent with mild hyperthermia and does not cause thermal ablation leading to coagulative necrosis42(Fig. 5G).

Survival rate over a four-week postoperative period revealed a 100% survival rate in the PFT@GN + US group. In contrast, the PT@Gel + US group experienced a 43% mortality rate, with 3 out of 7 animals succumbing to complications. Postmortem examination identified two early-stage deaths (at 1.42 and 1.94 weeks post-surgery) due to inadequate patch sealing, leading to aspiration, respiratory distress progressing to asphyxia. A third mortality occurred at 2.8 weeks post-surgery due to secondary fistula formation resulting from poor defect healing. These findings underscored the critical, non-redundant contributions of both hydrogel expansion and piezoelectric signaling in ensuring successful tracheal reconstruction (Fig. 5H). Specifically, the absence of ultrasound-induced hydrogel expansion impaired immediate mechanical sealing, predisposing to early failure, whereas inadequate piezoelectric stimulation compromised long-term regenerative outcomes. To verify the scalability of this ultrasound triggered mechanical electrical coupling patch strategy for tissue repair beyond the trachea, we further performed a proof-of-concept study in a full-thickness skin wound model (Supplementary Fig. 15A–C). In addition, the body weight change trends were similar among groups during the observation period, with no obvious abnormal fluctuations, indicating good overall tolerability under the applied ultrasound conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15D). Compared with the control group (Ctrl+US), the PFT@GN + US group exhibited a faster wound-closure trajectory during follow-up, with a more pronounced reduction in wound area over time. By postoperative day 10, the relative residual wound area in the PFT@GN + US group was lower than that in the other groups (Supplementary Fig. 15E), (P = 0.0349 and 0.0295). To further evaluate the efficacy of skin repair, we performed H&E staining of wound tissues and quantitatively analyzed epithelial gap width (Supplementary Fig. 16A). Histological results showed that the wound regions in the control group and some control material groups still exhibited relatively obvious epithelial defects and incompletely continuous epidermal coverage. In contrast, the PFT@GN + US group presented more continuous epidermal coverage and a smaller epithelial gap. Quantitative results further confirmed that epithelial gap width in the PFT@GN + US group was lower than that in the control group and the other treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 16B; P = 0.0098 and 0.0456), suggesting that this system can effectively promote the re-epithelialization process, thereby accelerating skin wound healing.

Therapeutic efficacy of mechanically-driven expandable patches for tracheal repair in a rabbit model

Bronchoscopic and gross examinations of the reconstructed trachea at 8 weeks post-surgery revealed that the bioengineered trachea-like structures closely resembled native rabbit tracheae in both shape and luminal architecture (Fig. 6A). The PFT@GN + US group maintained a stable, continuous tubular structure at the repair site, with smooth, unobstructed luminal surfaces and an absence of fibrotic stenosis (Fig. 6A). In contrast, control groups predominantly exhibited pathological alterations such as abnormal hyperplasia (e.g., excessive granulation tissue formation) or structural collapse. The dashed-line demarcated regions in the figures clearly demonstrated effective tissue integration at the repair interface, where neotissues showed robust extracellular matrix deposition and active epithelialization processes (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 17). The repaired tracheae achieved an inner diameter of 6.24 mm (vs. 6.56 mm in autologous controls) and a tissue thickness of 2.54 mm (vs. 2.78 mm in autologous controls), with key morphological parameters approximating native tracheal anatomy, thereby validating the system’s capacity to guide physiological tissue regeneration and restore functional luminal structures (Supplementary Fig. 17)43,44. Dynamic ultrasound imaging was further used to monitor the patch region over the respiratory cycle. Throughout a complete respiratory cycle from 0 to 2.5 s, the patch contour deformed synchronously with tracheal motion and continuously maintained contact with the tissue interface. Color doppler ultrasound also revealed stable blood flow signals around the patch. These results indicate that although the patch exhibits strong anti-collapse supporting capability in axial compression tests, its thin sheet-like structure and interfacial layer design can still provide good dynamic compliance and stable conformal attachment under physiological respiratory loading (Fig. 6B). In addition, representative gross photographs show abundant neovascular trajectories and branches around the patch-covered area, with visible fine vascular networks and signs of congestive perfusion. Vessels gradually extended from the surrounding tissues toward the repaired region and covered the patch surface and edges, indicating active vascular reconstruction and reperfusion in the repaired region and providing potential blood supply support for subsequent tissue regeneration (Fig. 6C).

Histopathological evaluation using sagittal sections and H&E staining further demonstrated the formation of a continuous stratified epithelial barrier at the repair margins, in stark contrast to the sparse monolayer epithelial coverage observed in control groups (Fig. 6D, F). Cross-sectional observations revealed that neocartilage in the experimental group exhibited characteristic lacunar-like structures, homogeneous extracellular matrix deposition, and an alternating lamellar arrangement of cartilage-fibrous tissue that closely mimicked native tracheal architecture45. Quantitative morphometric analysis showed a substantial increase in the regenerated area of the experimental group compared to controls (P < 0.05, Fig. 6F and Supplementary Fig. 18). Safranine O staining further confirmed the presence of dense collagen fiber networks (blue-stained regions), enhanced microvascular density (red-stained regions), and well-defined cartilage-fibrous tissue interfaces in the regenerated zones (Fig. 6E, G, Supplementary Fig. 18). Functional assessments verified that the regenerated tissues in the PFT@GN + US group possessed native-like mechanical properties and vascularization characteristics, with luminal compliance sufficient to maintain stable ventilation. Respiratory rates and patterns of experimental animals remained within physiological ranges, with no observed compensatory signs such as coughing or wheezing. Statistical analysis demonstrated that the cartilage regeneration area ratio in the PFT@GN + US group was higher than in other treatment groups (P < 0.05), validating the system’s superiority in promoting structural regeneration (Fig. 6G). Overall, these results indicate that the final material PFT@GN + US, through combined electromechanical effects and H2 related actions, can achieve stronger angiogenesis and coordinated cartilage and tissue regeneration, thereby providing the best restoration of airway structure and function, whereas groups with only a single module, either pure piezoelectricity or H2 alone, show relatively limited repair outcomes. In addition, H&E examination revealed no typical morphology of coagulative necrosis or heat fixation like changes (Fig. 6D). Cellular outlines were preserved, and no nuclear pyknosis, karyorrhexis, or karyolysis, or increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia was observed. The tissue architecture showed no hardening or homogenization, and no transition zone surrounding a necrotic core was present. Masson trichrome staining was used to assess collagen, fibrosis, and tissue remodeling, and no findings of a post-thermal necrosis repair phase or scar formation were detected (Fig. 6E). Therefore, histological staining evaluation excluded the possibility of thermal necrosis. Taken together with the thermal imaging analysis, these results support that the applied ultrasound regimen can be effectively activated within a safe thermal window.

Histological analysis of vascularization patterns in regenerated tissue zones demonstrated that neovascularization exhibited tissue-region specificity, with spatial distribution highly colocalized with fibrous tissue remodeling areas, while cartilage regions maintained physiological avascular characteristics46. This spatial dichotomy served as a critical biological marker validating the structural heterogeneity between cartilage and fibrous tissue domains47. Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis revealed that CD31+ vascular endothelial cells predominantly accumulated at the periphery of fibrous tissues, forming a nutrient-transporting microcirculatory network surrounding cartilage while strictly preserving avascular homeostasis in the cartilage matrix (Supplementary Fig. 19A). Quantitative evaluation demonstrated statistical increase of CD31 fluorescence signal intensity in the PFT@GN + US composite patch group compared to control groups (P < 0.05), confirming its pro-angiogenic efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 19B). To evaluate vascular reconstruction in the repaired region, we interpreted vessel like structures based on H&E and CD31 staining and used PDGFRα immunofluorescence to indicate the involvement of vessel associated fibroblasts, followed by quantification of the number of vessels per unit area (Fig. 6H, L). The results showed that vascularization was more pronounced in the PFT@GN + US group. PDGFRα signal intensity was higher than that in PFT@Gel+US and PT@GN + US (P = 0.0436 and 0.0175), and vessel density, defined as the number of vessels per unit area, was concomitantly increased (P = 0.0014 and 0.0214) (Fig. 6I, L). Overall, these findings indicate that PFT@GN + US promotes more sufficient neovascularization and surrounding cellular remodeling, forming a higher density vascular network that provides nutritional support for subsequent regeneration.

Immunofluorescence staining for Aggrecan, Sox9 and Collagen II was performed in the repaired region. Relative to the Sham + US, PT@GN + US and PFT@Gel + US groups, the PFT@GN + US group showed stronger Aggrecan and Collagen II signals, together with markedly increased Sox9 expression. Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed higher mean intensities of Aggrecan, Sox9 and Collagen II in the PFT@GN + US group, indicating more complete activation of the chondrogenic program and greater deposition of hyaline cartilage-like extracellular matrix (Fig. 6J, M–O). We further provided histological evidence related to tracheal epithelium and stromal remodeling (Fig. 6K, P). Immunofluorescence co staining showed that the PFT@GN + US group exhibited a more continuous distribution of CK14 positive epithelial cells, accompanied by enhanced Ac-Tub, acetylated tubulin, signals, indicating epithelial maturation and recovery of cilia related structures. Quantification of epithelial thickness further showed that the PFT@GN + US group exceeded both the control and the other treatment groups. Consistent with these findings, SEM analysis demonstrated more distinct cilia-like ultrastructures on the mucosal surface in the PFT@GN + US group, whereas such features were comparatively sparse in the control and material-only groups (Supplementary Fig. 20). To evaluate local inflammatory responses, CD68 immunofluorescence staining was performed to characterize macrophage infiltration in the repaired region (Supplementary Fig. 21). CD68-positive signals were detected in all groups, indicating postoperative immune activation; however, the overall signal intensity was lower in the PFT@GN + US group and more pronounced in the PFT@Gel+US and PT@GN + US groups. Quantitative analysis confirmed that CD68 fluorescence intensity was lower in the PFT@GN + US group than in these two comparison groups (P = 0.0257 and 0.0251), suggesting attenuated macrophage infiltration and a more regeneration-permissive local microenvironment.

To validate the translational potential of the composite patch in a clinically relevant setting, we employed a large-animal model of tracheal defect reconstruction using a goat model (Fig. 7A). A standardized full-thickness defect (1 cm × 1 cm) was created by resecting three consecutive tracheal cartilage rings from the ventral wall, followed by application of the composite repair patch. Postoperative evaluations included serial bronchoscopic examinations at 4 and 8 weeks to monitor patch-tissue integration and luminal patency. After defect modeling, the defect site was reconstructed by pre-applying the lyophilized PFT scaffold, followed by injection of the Gel-NB hydrogel precursor, UV-mediated crosslinking, and subsequent ultrasound irradiation to induce in situ expansion and patch formation (Fig. 7C). Macroscopic morphological observations further demonstrated that neotissues matched the native trachea in color, texture, and structural dimensions. In contrast, the PT@Gel + US group displayed pronounced inflammatory responses in the tracheal wall, characterized by irregular structural contours. Tracheal ultrasonography, as a non-invasive, real-time, high-resolution imaging modality, demonstrated clinical utility in airway assessment, intubation guidance, lesion diagnosis, and dynamic monitoring. We employed a large-animal ultrasound system to evaluate tracheal regeneration in experimental goat (Fig. 7E). In the PFT@GN + US group, the mucosal layer exhibited a hyperechoic linear structure (thickness < 0.5 mm) with surface mucus layers generating “twinkling artifacts.” The submucosal layer presented a hypoechoic band (1-2 mm thick) containing loose connective tissue, vascular plexuses, and mucous glands, while color doppler flow imaging revealed submucosal microvascular networks (flow velocity < 12 cm/s, resistance index 0.5-0.6). Cartilaginous rings displayed either a beaded or homogeneous configuration, appearing as arc-shaped hyperechoic structures with posterior acoustic shadowing and no calcification. In the PT@Gel+US group, variability in the thickness and continuity of regenerated tissue was observed among individuals, and some samples still showed relatively incomplete structural organization. By contrast, the PFT@GN + US group displayed more consistent tissue bridging and defect closure, with airway wall architecture more closely recapitulating native morphology. Quantitative analysis based on histological sections showed that the regeneration area in the PFT@GN + US group was higher than that in the PT@Gel+US group (P = 0.0306), indicating a stronger capacity to promote effective regenerative coverage of the defect region.

Consistent with these findings, collagen-related immunofluorescence staining (Col II/DAPI) showed stronger and more spatially extensive Col II signal within the repaired region of the PFT@GN + US group (Fig. 7G, J). Further statistical analysis of fluorescence intensity demonstrated an increase in Col II fluorescence intensity in this group (P = 0.0175), suggesting enhanced extracellular matrix deposition and tissue reconstruction. At 8 weeks post-implantation, tracheal specimens from the experimental goat were harvested, subjected to transverse axial sectioning, and processed for CD31/Piezo1 co-immunostaining (Fig. 7H, K, L). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed continuous, smooth linear distribution of CD31 and Piezo1 co-positive signals along vascular endothelial membranes in the PFT@GN + US group, with signal intensification particularly at intercellular junctions (tight junctions and adherens junctions). Microvessels exhibited typical CD31+/Piezo1+ colocalization within uniform luminal structures (15–50 μm diameter), arranged as a reticulated, branching vascular network devoid of aberrant connections. Non-vascular regions (cartilage and interstitium) showed complete absence of CD31 signals, while DAPI counterstaining confirmed homogeneous nuclear distribution. In contrast, the PT@Gel+US group exhibited reduced CD31/Piezo1 co-expression (P < 0.05 vs. the PFT@GN + US group) in fluorescence intensity analysis. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the composite patch effectively promoted tracheal defect regeneration through mechanisms tightly associated with Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction signaling pathways.

Discussion

Tracheal defects represent critical and life-threatening conditions in the respiratory system48. To address this clinical challenge, we developed a mechanically-triggered electroexpansion patch system, which demonstrates distinct advantages over existing strategies: (1) The 3D-printed hydrogel composite patch achieves efficient expansive sealing and robust tissue adhesion at tracheal defect sites, effectively overcoming the limitations of current tracheal tissue engineering approaches; (2) Upon ultrasound activation, the PFT@GN + US scaffold enables high-efficiency mechanoelectrical conversion while enhancing endothelial cell vascularization and tracheal tissue regeneration. Concurrently, ultrasound-triggered nanobubble cavitation facilitates precise H2 release, which synergizes with piezoelectric effects to activate the Piezo1-Notch signaling pathway in endothelial cells, thereby accelerating tracheal defect repair. Collectively, our established mechanically-triggered electroexpansion patch system provides a therapeutic strategy for achieving both effective tracheal sealing and functional tissue regeneration.

In the field of human tissue and airway defect repair, the development of biomaterials with reliable sealing capacity holds significant clinical value. For congenital or acquired myocardial defects, repair materials must withstand sustained hemodynamic loads, imposing stringent requirements on their mechanical properties49. Current research predominantly focuses on biodegradable polymer systems such as polylactic acid (PLA) and polycaprolactone (PCL), with functional optimization achieved through dual-disc configurations (exemplified by the ReAces occluder) or zero-edge eccentric structures50. The dual-disc design creates a “sandwich-like” clamping effect at defect sites, utilizing cardiac pulsation-induced fluid pressure to achieve dynamic adaptation with defect margins, thereby effectively minimizing mechanical interference with cardiac valves and conduction systems. These material systems undergo gradual degradation within 6-24 months post-implantation, ultimately achieving the therapeutic goal of “implant residue-free” repair. However, the sophisticated manufacturing processes required for such layered-structure devices substantially hinder clinical translation. Moreover, their relatively high mechanical stiffness hinders the ability to achieve the airtight sealing required to conform to the compliance of tracheal cartilage and surrounding soft tissues. In contrast, composite materials for hard/soft tissue repair-including fibrin matrices, gelatin-PCL composite membranes, and eggshell membrane-inorganic nanoparticle scaffolds-demonstrate unique advantages. These systems feature controllable hierarchical porous architectures and bioactive components, enabling rapid tissue adhesion through electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding with moist tissue surfaces, thus achieving suture-free fixation51,52. While capable of sustained growth factor release to promote tissue regeneration, these materials exhibit inadequate elastic modulus regulation and insufficient tissue adhesion when adapted for airway defect repair.

In the field of biomaterials, piezoelectric materials have gained attention in tissue regeneration engineering due to their unique electromechanical conversion properties (encompassing both direct and converse piezoelectric effects)53,54. The underlying mechanism involves mimicking the intrinsic electrophysiological microenvironment of biological tissues, where regulation of cellular membrane potential activates critical signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin pathway), thereby accelerating tissue repair processes. Recently, Zuo’s research group developed 3D-printed polyurethane-based composite piezoelectric scaffolds. This system generated an open-circuit voltage of 197 mV under low-intensity pulsed ultrasound stimulation, with experimental data demonstrating enhancement of osteoblast differentiation efficiency in rat calvarial defect models55. Notably, barium titanate-functionalized implants can continuously release bioelectrical signals through cyclic mechanical stress generated by daily patient activities, with such dynamic electrical stimulation accelerating osseointegration at bone-implant interfaces. Furthermore, cutting-edge research integrated piezoelectric materials with magnetic nanoparticles to achieve remotely controllable electrical stimulation via external alternating magnetic fields. This synergistic approach has been demonstrated to enhance vascular network formation and hydroxyapatite crystal deposition in bone defect regions56,57. However, when applied to airway defect repair, these systems still face technical challenges regarding insufficient interfacial adhesion strength. In the field of neural regeneration, polyvinylidene fluoride-based conduits or polypyrrole conduits can induce directional axonal growth through piezoelectric effects generated by limb movement, enabling functional reconstruction of peripheral nerve defects58. Nevertheless, current piezoelectric material systems predominantly rely on passive mechanical energy harvesting from body movements for tissue regeneration, exhibiting critical limitations in regulation precision and spatiotemporal specificity. A representative comparison of ultrasound-activated piezoelectric scaffolds/films/hydrogels is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

In addressing the two principal therapeutic bottlenecks inherent to tracheal defect repair: namely, the inability to achieve satisfactory mechanical properties and adhesion of repair materials, which results in incomplete defect sealing and places patients at elevated risk of suffocation, and the delayed vascularization of grafts, which leads to central necrosis and poor long-term outcomes due to insufficient pro-angiogenic stimulation, we developed a mechanically driven expandable patch designed to overcome these limitations. Specifically, ultrasound-induced micro-expansion of the patch enables effective and conformal defect sealing, while the integrated 3D-printed scaffold confers robust mechanical support and structural stability (Figs. 2 and 5). The PLLA based porous scaffold has numerous advantages over the solid PLLA sheet, including dynamic conformal attachment, rapid closure, controlled release, and synergistic degradation and regeneration. We also provided a comparative description in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally, ultrasound-triggered activation of the patch facilitates precise and sustained release of bioactive agents, which, in conjunction with its intrinsic piezoelectric properties, synergistically activates the Piezo1-Notch signaling pathway in endothelial cells, thereby enhancing vascularization and promoting comprehensive tracheal tissue regeneration (Figs. 4, 6, and 7). By harnessing this coordinated interplay between ultrasound-mediated mechanical expansion and synchronized mechanoelectrical transduction, the system achieves simultaneous reinforcement of structural sealing and stimulation of regenerative angiogenesis, thus providing an integrated and effective solution for tracheal defect repair. While current strategies in tracheal reconstruction predominantly focus on integrating tissue engineering, 3D bioprinting, and biomaterial design to produce bioactive substitutes with biomimetic architectures (e.g., vasculature networks and cartilaginous frameworks)59–61, our mechanically triggered electroexpansion patch advances this paradigm by combining mechanical robustness, adhesive sealing, and pro-angiogenic capacity within a single platform. Notably, this approach synergistically combines mechanical expansion-mediated defect sealing with mechano-piezoelectric vascular reconstruction to promote tracheal tissue regeneration. However, translating these promising preclinical findings into clinical applications will necessitate multidisciplinary collaboration to overcome challenges related to manufacturing scalability, regulatory approval, and optimization of biological performance for human use.

The patch developed here is an implantable airway repair device, and its eventual regulatory classification will need to be defined in the context of jurisdiction-specific frameworks, indications and claims. Nevertheless, because it is deployed in a life-sustaining airway and exerts active therapeutic effects, it would likely be regulated as a high-risk implantable therapeutic device in many settings, with the external ultrasound generator considered either an accessory or part of the integrated system. For clinical translation, reproducibility and lot-to-lot consistency will require a well-defined quality-control strategy and release criteria. Encouragingly, the present platform is built on a modular workflow that is inherently compatible with scale-up: scaffold geometry is tightly prescribed, supporting reproducible manufacturing, whereas FF and TA post-functionalization are solution-based steps that can be parallelized without materially increasing process complexity. The system is also amenable to a clinically practical sterile-kit format with intraoperative in situ assembly. The dry scaffold could be terminally sterilized by ethylene oxide or irradiation and supplied in a validated stable form, whereas the hydrogel precursor could be prepared and filled aseptically, with final injection and photocrosslinking performed in the sterile surgical field. Future system-level validation should therefore encompass sterility assurance, packaging integrity, residual and material-compatibility testing and confirmation that ultrasound responsiveness is retained after sterilization, thereby establishing a realistic pathway toward a manufacturable, registrable and clinically deployable ultrasound-activated implant system.

Fabrication process and parameters of composite 3D-printed scaffolds

The PLLA (2500HP, NatureWorks) raw pellets were first pre-dried in a vacuum oven at 60–80 °C for 4–6 hs to effectively reduce moisture content, thereby preventing pore formation or thermal degradation during subsequent processing. The material was then subjected to melt blending using single/double-screw extrusion equipment, extruded at 185 °C to form uniform-diameter printing filaments. After water-cooling, shaping, and winding, the filaments underwent secondary drying until achieving a moisture content < 0.1% prior to use. For digital modeling, a biomimetic structural model with 60% porosity, 200 μm characteristic pore size, and optimized interconnectivity was designed using 3D modeling software to meet the dual requirements of mechanical performance and cellular infiltration in tissue engineering. Critical printing parameters included: nozzle temperature of 182 °C, constant-temperature forming platform at 60–80 °C (enhancing interlayer adhesion), layer resolution of 0.1–0.3 mm, printing speed of 25 mm/s, and 80% grid infill density. Post-printing, the constructs were annealed at 100 °C for 1-2 hs to improve crystallinity and relieve residual stress, followed by ultrasonic cleaning with ethanol and deionized water to remove surface impurities. The sterilized three-dimensional porous scaffolds were obtained through these procedures. Parameter optimization enabled the construction of highly oriented, ordered microstructures, with each PLLA scaffold sample dimensioned at approximately 15 mm × 15 mm × 2 mm and an inter-filament spacing of 0.4 mm. To confer piezoelectric properties, freeze-dried PLLA scaffolds were immersed in an 8 mg/mL FF peptide solution (Aladdin) for 6 h, followed by re-freeze-drying. This immersion-freeze-drying cycle was repeated three times to prepare PF scaffolds. Subsequently, the PF scaffolds were immersed in a 10 ml 1 wt% TA solution (Aladdin) at 10 °C for 48 h to fabricate the final PFT composite scaffolds.

Preparation of nanobubbles and hydrogel precursor solution

GelMA-based precursor solutions were prepared using GelMA (degree of methacrylation 59.2%, Engineering For Life, China). The photoinitiator was added to the precursor at a final concentration of 0.25% (w/v). Unless otherwise stated, the same GelMA batch and photoinitiator concentration were used across all experiments to minimize variability in crosslinking density, stiffness, and ultrasound-responsive expansion. To effectively fabricate H2-loaded NBs, DPPC, DSPE-PEG, propylene glycol, and glycerol components were precisely weighed in an EP tube and subjected to thermal melting in a 65°C constant-temperature water bath. The homogenized system was subsequently purged with H2 to evacuate residual air, followed by immediate transfer to a specialized mechanical oscillator (Ag and Hg mixer) for 90-s high-frequency agitation. After phase separation, the bottom colloidal dispersion was collected and diluted stepwise with PBS. Phase separation was ultimately achieved through low-speed centrifugation (300 rpm, 5 min), selectively harvesting the NB-enriched supernatant. For hydrogel precursor preparation, 5% freeze-dried GelMA-TA was rehydrated in a 1 ml solution containing a photoinitiator (0.25 wt% LAP), which was then blended with 100 μL of the aforementioned NBs to obtain the Gel-NB hydrogel precursor. In addition, NBs were conjugated with CY5 fluorescent dye to facilitate observation of their spatial distribution within the hydrogel matrix.

Fabrication of mechanically-triggered electroexpansion patch

To prepare PFT@GN + US composite scaffolds, 100 μL of Gel-NB hydrogel precursor was injected into freeze-dried PFT scaffolds, followed by UV crosslinking for 15 min. Finally, ultrasound irradiation (1 MHz, 0.98 W/cm2, applied for 1 minute, stopped for 1 minute) was applied to observe the hydrogel swelling behavior, yielding the final composite scaffolds. For H2 release characterization under ultrasound triggering, MB dye reduction assays were employed. A 5 μM MB working solution (pH 7.4 PBS) containing 0.1 mg/mL platinum nanoparticle catalyst was prepared and deoxygenated by nitrogen purging for 30 min in sealed quartz cuvettes. A linear standard curve (R² > 0.99) correlating ΔA664 with H2 molar quantity was established using known H2 injections (0–100 μL). Test samples were then introduced, with A664 values recorded every 30 s under magnetic stirring until a plateau. Absorbance changes were converted to cumulative H2 release via the standard curve, generating time-release profiles fitted with a first-order kinetic model. All experiments were conducted under controlled conditions: temperature 25 ± 0.5°C, dissolved oxygen < 0.1 mg/L, and light protection.

Characterization of composite patch morphology and mechanical properties

The physicochemical properties of PFT@GN + US composites were systematically characterized. Phase-contrast optical microscopy (Nikon) was employed to observe the morphology of each group. Surface topography of freeze-dried patches was analyzed using SEM (FEI) and laser scanning confocal microscopy (Zeiss). Elemental composition of composite microspheres was determined by EDS (Thermo Scientific). Mechanical properties of hydrogels were evaluated via a universal testing machine (Tinius Olsen), with stress-strain curves recorded to extract fracture strength and elastic modulus. For ISO-standardized mechanical testing, the following protocol was implemented: First, specimens were precision-machined into standardized cylindrical/cubic geometries with defect-free surfaces. During electromechanical universal testing machine operation (compression mode), specimens were vertically aligned at the geometric center between the platens. The crosshead distance was meticulously adjusted to the specimen’s original height, with optical alignment performed to eliminate eccentric loading effects. Pre-test configurations included: quasi-static strain rate (5 mm/min), data acquisition frequency ( ≥ 10 Hz), and termination criteria (structural failure or deformation exceeding safety thresholds). Dual-channel calibration (force-displacement) was executed, complemented by strain gauge deployment for localized deformation monitoring via resistance signal acquisition. Upon loading initiation, axial compressive force was applied at a constant rate while full-range load-displacement curves and distributed strain data were synchronously recorded. The TRAPEZIUM V analysis platform dynamically generated stress-strain profiles, where engineering stress was calculated based on initial cross-sectional area, and true strain was derived from laser displacement meter/strain gauge signals. Young’s modulus was determined through linear regression of elastic deformation data (0.25% strain range). Post-test, curve parameters were reanalyzed to verify data reproducibility and error tolerance, particularly focusing on yield points and fracture thresholds.

To quantitatively evaluate the sealing capability of the patch for luminal defects, a burst pressure test setup was constructed. Briefly, a tube with a standardized aperture defect was fixed in a clamp to form a sealed chamber, and the tested material was placed to cover the defect region. Fluid was then injected into the chamber using a syringe at a rate of 1 mL/min to gradually increase the intraluminal pressure, while a pressure sensor was used to record pressure changes in real time. When continuous fluid leakage was observed or an abrupt pressure drop occurred, the pressure at that moment was recorded as the critical burst pressure. At least n = 5 samples were tested for each group. In addition, tissue lap shear adhesion strength testing was performed according to ASTM F2255. Briefly, fresh porcine skin was cut into rectangular specimens, and an overlap region was defined at one end of each specimen with an overlap area of 10 mm × 10 mm = 100 mm². The composite patch PFT@GN or other control materials were placed within the overlap region between two tissue pieces and triggered according to the assigned groups. Tensile shear loading was applied using a universal testing machine (Germany, Zwick Roell). The free ends of the tissues were clamped at both sides such that the loading direction was parallel to the overlap plane to induce shear failure. The loading rate was set to 10 mm/min until failure. The sample size for each group was n = 5.

To quantitatively evaluate the tensile debonding resistance of the patch-tissue interface, a calibrated digital force gauge (digital force gauge) was used for vertical tensile testing. The composite patch was attached to the surface of an ex vivo tracheal tissue model with the attachment area controlled at 1 cm2, and a consistent preload of 1 N was applied for 5 min to achieve full contact; for the ultrasound-triggered group, ultrasound treatment was applied after attachment, with parameters identical to those used in the animal experiments. The hook of the force gauge was then connected to the free end of the patch, and vertical loading was applied at a constant tensile rate of 1 mm/mi until interfacial failure, with the peak force recorded as the maximum tensile debonding force (tensile force). Each group included at least n = 5 independent samples. To better mimic enzymatic microenvironments beyond PBS hydrolysis, enzymatic degradation was conducted in parallel and quantitatively correlated with in vivo residual mass. Briefly, samples (n = 3 per group) were freeze-dried to constant weight and the initial dry mass was recorded as W0. For collagenase-mediated degradation, samples were incubated at 37 °C in collagenase solution (30 U mL−1 collagenase from Clostridium histolyticum) prepared in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2. At predetermined time points (0–85 day), samples were retrieved, gently rinsed with deionized water, blotted to remove surface liquid, freeze-dried again to constant weight, and weighed as Wt; the remaining mass (%) was calculated as Wt/W0 × 100.

Investigation of piezoelectric properties and functional relationships in composite patches

To quantify the direct piezoelectric effect under mechanical stimulation, specimens with effective areas of 1–3 cm2 were selected. Controlled mechanical stimulation was applied via ultrasound parameters (central frequency: 1–10 MHz, power density: 0.3–1.5 W/cm2) to the samples. A dual-channel detection system was established using an electrochemical analyzer and a high-precision oscilloscope (Chinstruments), where gold-plated probes formed closed-loop circuits with the sample electrodes to capture transient voltage signals. For ultrasound stimulation, composite patches were immobilized in a deionized water-immersed acoustic coupling device. Open-circuit voltage waveforms were monitored in real time via a high-impedance probe (input impedance ≥10 MΩ) connected to an oscilloscope (bandwidth ≥ 100 MHz). For quasi-static mechanical loading, a multi-range universal testing machine (preloaded at 10/30/50 N gradients) equipped with a customized spherical indenter delivered normal forces, while synchronized acquisition of stress waveforms (via a dynamic force sensor: 0–100 N range, ± 0.5% FS accuracy) and time-domain charge signals (from a charge amplifier) was performed. Pre-experimental calibration involved sensitivity verification using standard quartz piezoelectric references. Intrinsic resonant frequencies of specimens were determined via a broadband impedance analyzer (20 Hz-10 MHz) to optimize stimulation parameters. Raw signals were preprocessed through a 500 kHz anti-aliasing filter and digitized at 1 MS/s sampling rate. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) spectral analysis extracted characteristic frequency components, and electromechanical coupling efficiency was calculated based on the quadratic relationship between RMS output voltage and input mechanical work. All experiments were conducted in an electromagnetic shielding chamber with nano-silver conductive adhesive and multi-contact spring probes, ensuring low-impedance electrical contacts. Ambient parameters were strictly controlled at 23 ± 2°C and relative humidity < 30% to eliminate electrostatic noise.

The intrinsic piezoelectric coefficient (d33) was measured using a d33 meter (JKZC Science and Technology) in accordance with the instrument’s standard procedure. For each group (PLLA, FF and PFT@GN), at least 5 independent specimens were measured. Frequency-dependent impedance spectra were recorded using an impedance analyzer (Agilent) with a two-electrode configuration. Samples were sandwiched between conductive electrodes to form a capacitor-like structure, and the impedance magnitude Z was collected over (1 Hz-100 KHz). All measurements were conducted under identical conditions for direct comparison among PLLA, FF and PFT@GN. In addition, samples measuring 1 × 1 cm were incubated in 10% serum containing medium at 37 °C for up to 8 weeks. At predetermined time points, 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks, specimens were gently rinsed with PBS, equilibrated in fresh medium, and the open circuit voltage under ultrasound stimulation was recorded using an oscilloscope, Tektronix MDO series. For each group, n = 3.

DLS was employed to analyze nanobubble size distribution based on the correlation between Brownian motion and light intensity fluctuations. Laser irradiation was applied to the bubble suspension, and time-dependent nanobubble size profiles were derived from autocorrelation function analysis of the captured light intensity fluctuation signals induced by random motion. Furthermore, numerical fitting was performed to explore functional relationships among ultrasound intensity, nanobubble disruption rate, and piezoelectric output. Specifically, gradient ultrasound irradiation (1-5 W) was applied to the piezoelectric composite system containing nanobubbles using a controlled experimental platform. Ultrasound intensity, nanobubble disruption rates (statistically quantified via high-speed microscopic imaging based on bubble count/volume reduction ratios), and piezoelectric outputs (acquired via charge amplifiers and oscilloscopes) were synchronously recorded. Data were normalized, and outliers (e.g., voltage spikes caused by cavitation noise interference) were excluded. Nonlinear least-squares fitting was implemented to model experimental data, while parameter sensitivity was evaluated via Monte Carlo simulations ( ± 10% perturbation). Combined with theoretical frameworks (e.g., cavitation dynamics equations and piezoelectric constitutive relationships), dominant factors were identified. Solution temperature ( ± 1 °C), ionic strength, and initial bubble concentration were rigorously controlled to minimize variable interference. To evaluate NB stability under physiological ionic strength and protein adsorption, NB were dispersed in physiological saline and serum-containing medium and incubated at 37 °C. At predetermined time points from 0 to 10 min, aliquots were collected for hydrodynamic size and zeta potential measurements using a Zetasizer Nano (Malvern) under identical settings. For cyclic ultrasound tests in serum, NB suspensions were exposed to ultrasound at 1 MHz and 0.98 W/cm2 at room temperature or 37 °C. After each cycle at 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 min, size and zeta potential were measured to quantify acoustic persistence and interfacial changes.

Biocompatibility evaluation of mechanically-triggered electroexpansion patch

The 3D-printed scaffolds were first subjected to UV sterilization pretreatment. Endothelial cells were obtained from the human umbilical vein endothelial cell line PUMC-HUVEC-T1 (No. C01510C, Procell). HUVECs were prepared as single-cell suspensions (1×106/mL) via trypsin digestion, pre-adapted at 37 °C, and uniformly seeded onto scaffold surfaces. After 2 h of static culture in a 5% CO₂ incubator to promote cell adhesion, complete medium replacement was performed for 7-day in vitro culture. For biocompatibility assessment, F-actin cytoskeletons were specifically labeled using phalloidin fluorescent probes (Beyotime), with cell spreading morphology observed via confocal CLSM. Cell-scaffold complexes were freeze-dried and characterized by SEM to reveal cellular spatial distribution within 3D architectures. Quantitative analysis of cell orientation was conducted through a spatial quadrant partitioning method: hydrogel cross-sections were equally divided into four regions, where the polar angle distribution frequency of endothelial cells in each quadrant was statistically analyzed and visualized via polar coordinate mapping to elucidate radial/angular positioning patterns. Additionally, material extracts were co-cultured with HUVECs. Cell viability and morphological parameters were systematically evaluated using live/dead cell double-staining kits (Beyotime) according to manufacturer protocols, comprehensively assessing the composite’s biosafety profile. Intracellular reactive oxygen species were quantitatively measured using the DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, China). Cells were incubated with DCFH-DA at 10 µmol/L for 25 min, followed by washing, and then treated with 100 µM H2O2 to induce oxidative stress. Subsequently, ultrasound or no ultrasound treatment was applied. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ, and results were reported as the mean fluorescence intensity per field of view.

In vitro pro-angiogenic potential evaluation of composite patch

Twenty-four-well culture plates were uniformly coated with growth factor-reduced Matrigel matrix (8.9 mg/mL, BD Biosciences). HUVECs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 4 h. Pre-treated patch materials were subsequently transferred to Transwell migration chambers (8 μm pore, Corning) and suspended above the Matrigel-coated surface. Following 24 h incubation under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2), angiogenic parameters, including tube length, branch points, and network complexity, were quantified using ImageJ software with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis and validation of composite patch-activated endothelial cells

Following molecular biology protocols, the RNA sequencing workflow was executed as follows: Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa). Lysates were mixed with chloroform at specified ratios, vortexed to achieve complete emulsification, and phase-separated via high-speed centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min) at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was combined with isopropanol for nucleic acid precipitation, followed by centrifugal enrichment and gradient washing with 75% ethanol. Purified RNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water, and RNA integrity was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Santa Clara, CA) to ensure compliance with library construction standards. A strand-specific mRNA library was prepared using the TruSeq kit (Illumina) through polyA-tailed mRNA capture with magnetic beads, fragmentation, and dual-index adapter ligation. Sequencing was performed on a Novaseq 6000 platform (OE Biotech) in 150 bp paired-end mode to generate raw data. DEGs were identified via the DESeq2 algorithm, with significance thresholds set at P < 0.05 and expression fold-change criteria of |log2FC | >1. GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package based on hypergeometric testing, with significant terms (q < 0.05) selected for biological process interpretation. A protein-protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING database (v11.5), and topological analysis in Cytoscape (v3.9.1) identified hub genes.

Mechanistically, protein expression levels of Piezo1 (ab203271, Abcam), DLL4 (ab7280, Abcam), Notch1 (ab52627, Abcam), PI3K (ab191606, Abcam), P-PI3K (#4228, Cell Signaling Technology), p-Akt (#4060, Cell Signaling Technology), Piezo2 (Proteintech 26205-1-AP), KCNK2 (Proteintech 26807-1-AP), TRPM (Proteintech 55251-1-AP) and c-Fos (ab208942, Abcam) were validated by Western blotting under standardized electrophoresis conditions (10% SDS-PAGE, 100 V, 90 min). For phosphorylated proteins, pathway activation was evaluated by normalizing the signal intensity of the phosphorylated form to that of the corresponding total protein, and the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-Akt/Akt ratios were used for statistical analysis. To further evaluate the involvement of the Piezo1-related mechanotransduction pathway, cells were pretreated with 5 μM GsMTx4 for 30 min before stimulation. The cells were then subjected to material co-culture and ultrasound stimulation under the same conditions, after which samples were collected for Western blot analysis. Except for the GsMTx4 treatment, all other experimental conditions were kept identical to those of the corresponding control group.

In vitro tissue evaluation

Porcine muscle and skin tissues were harvested to evaluate the in vitro adhesion performance of composite patches. Uniform 1 cm × 1 cm defects were surgically created in the tissues and repaired using the composite patches. The operational workflow was systematically documented during patch application. Tissue adhesion stability was assessed through repeated hydrodynamic rinsing cycles under controlled pressure (10-15 kPa), with adhesion integrity monitored at each procedural stage. To evaluate the local thermal effects during ultrasound triggering, infrared thermographic monitoring was performed on the surface of ex vivo porcine skin. After trimming, the porcine skin samples were flattened and fixed on a nonconductive tray. After equilibration at room temperature, a small amount of ultrasound coupling gel was applied between the probe and the skin to ensure acoustic energy coupling. An ultrasound probe was used to apply intermittent stimulation to the porcine skin surface at 1 MHz and 0.98 W/cm2, with a cyclic mode of 1 min on and 1 min off, and the total observation time was 10 min. In parallel, an infrared thermal imaging camera was used to continuously record the surface temperature of the probe's exposed region, and thermographic images and temperature values were extracted at 1, 3, 6, and 10 min. Temperature readings were taken as the peak temperature within the exposed region.

Mouse model evaluation of the skin repair effect of the patch

Mice were housed under standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, an ambient temperature of 20–24 °C, and relative humidity of 50–60%, with ad libitum access to food and water (BALB/c mouse, female, 8 weeks old). A BALB/c mouse model of full-thickness dorsal skin defects was established. After anesthesia, dorsal hair was removed, and the area was routinely disinfected. A sterile biopsy punch was used to create circular full-thickness skin defects with a diameter of 8 mm on the dorsum, removing the epidermis and dermis down to the surface of the subcutaneous tissue, and two wounds were created in each animal. The animals were then randomly divided into four groups: Ctrl + US, PT@GN + US, PFT@Gel + US, and PFT@GN + US. Immediately after wound creation, the corresponding material was placed on the wound surface and fixed with sterile dressings. Ultrasound parameters were set as follows: frequency 1 MHz, acoustic intensity or power density 1 W per square centimeter, 10 min per session with ultrasound applied for 1 min followed by 1 min off, with a treatment frequency of once daily. Treatment started on postoperative day 1 and continued until day 10. To reduce contraction bias, the wound margins can be fixed with silicone rings. Standardized photographs of the wounds were taken on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 10. During imaging, a fixed distance and consistent lighting conditions were maintained, and a ruler was placed within the field of view for scale calibration. ImageJ was used to trace the wound boundaries and calculate the wound area. The wound area on day 1 for each animal was normalized to 100 percent, and the relative wound area percentage at each time point was calculated. The relative wound area on day 10 was used for intergroup comparisons. On postoperative day 10, animals were euthanized, and the wound tissue, together with surrounding normal skin, was harvested and fixed in 4 percent paraformaldehyde, followed by routine dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. After H&E staining, panoramic and locally magnified images were acquired under a light microscope. Epithelial gap width was defined as the distance between the edges of the newly formed epidermis on both sides of the wound, and three sections per animal were used for statistical analysis.

Rabbit model evaluation of patch repair performance

Experimental rabbits (New Zealand rabbit, female, 8 weeks old) were secured in dorsal recumbency, and anesthesia was induced via intramuscular xylazine injection. The cervical region was shaved and anatomically landmarked by tracheal palpation. A full-thickness rectangular defect (0.3 cm × 0.6 cm) was surgically created in the anterior tracheal wall using microsurgical instruments. The composite repair patch was precisely applied to the defect site. Postoperatively, localized ultrasound therapy (1 MHz, 0.98 W/cm2, applied for 1 min, stopped for 1 min) was administered every other day. Daily monitoring included body weight changes and respiratory clinical parameters: cough frequency, sputum production, stridor intensity, and dyspnea index.

During the respiratory cycle, ultrasound imaging was performed at 8 weeks after patch implantation in the rabbit model. B-mode and color Doppler imaging were acquired for the patch region. The probe was kept in light contact on the tracheal surface, and coupling gel was applied to reduce compression artifacts. Without changing the probe position, dynamic images were continuously collected for at least five respiratory cycles. To illustrate the deformation process across the respiratory cycle, representative time points, 0 s, 1 s, 1.5 s, and 2.5 s, were selected from the continuous frames for display and analysis. The patch location was annotated based on its echogenic features and intraoperative positioning, and the tracheal contour was delineated using dashed lines. Color flow signals within a unified region of interest were recorded to assess perfusion around the patch. After 8 weeks, euthanasia was performed to harvest tracheal specimens for regeneration assessment. Excised tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hs, followed by paraffin sectioning for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, Safranine O staining and CD31/VEGF immunofluorescence co-localization analysis. All animal procedures were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee (approval No. K25-252Y) and were conducted in accordance with relevant animal welfare guidelines. Anesthesia, peri-operative analgesia, and antibiotic prophylaxis were provided as described. Animals were assigned to experimental groups using randomization. Because the surgical procedures and patch handling were inherently different between groups, surgeons were not blinded during implantation; however, outcome assessments were performed blinded whenever feasible. Bronchoscopic images and histological/immunofluorescence samples were coded prior to analysis, and quantitative measurements were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation. All image-based quantifications (e.g., CD31 intensity/density, cartilage-related metrics and collagen alignment) were performed on coded images by assessors blinded to group identity.

Goat model evaluation of repair efficacy

A compound anesthesia protocol was administered to an age-and weight-qualified experimental goat (goat, female, 1 year old), involving intramuscular injection of a ketamine-xylazine cocktail combined with atropine sulfate pretreatment to suppress glandular secretion. Goats were randomly assigned to two groups: PFT@GN + US (n = 3) and PT@Gel + US (n = 3). Following the establishment of an artificial airway, mechanical ventilation was maintained using an anesthetic machine with a nitrous oxide-halothane-oxygen gas mixture. Goats were anesthetized with a ketamine-xylazine-based protocol with atropine premedication, followed by establishment of an artificial airway and mechanical ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained using a nitrous oxide-halothane-oxygen mixture, and vital signs were continuously monitored throughout surgery. A standardized full-thickness tracheal defect (1 cm × 1 cm) was created in the ventral midportion of three consecutive tracheal cartilage rings and immediately repaired with the ultrasound-responsive electroactive patch. Patch reconstruction was completed by placement of the scaffold, injection of the hydrogel precursor, UV-mediated crosslinking, and subsequent ultrasound irradiation to induce in situ expansion and patch formation. Postoperative monitoring included daily endoscopic evaluations using a fiberoptic bronchoscopy system (Karl Storz) to assess patch-tissue integration and airway patency index, with particular emphasis on long-term respiratory compensation and quality-of-life indicators in large animals. Concurrent safety assessments involved blood biochemical profiling for multi-organ functional monitoring, periodic quantification of serum inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulin levels to evaluate systemic toxicity, and dynamic tracking of cartilage regeneration through a large-animal ultrasound imaging system (DAWEI) to characterize neotissue morphology. After an 8-week experimental period, euthanasia was performed to harvest tracheal specimens for comprehensive evaluation of tissue regeneration efficacy and structural reconstruction integrity.

Histopathological evaluation of animal tissues

Fresh tracheal specimens (with intact cartilage structures) were immediately immersed in pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde fixative (pH 7.4 buffered system) and continuously fixed for 48 hs at 4 °C to preserve the three-dimensional cartilage matrix architecture. Following thorough PBS rinsing, calcified cartilage regions were decalcified in 10% EDTA solution (37°C constant-temperature shaker for 5 consecutive days, with daily renewal of decalcification reagent). A graded ethanol dehydration protocol (70% → 80% → 90% → 100% ethanol, 2 h immersion per concentration) was implemented, followed by xylene clearing and paraffin embedding in 58 °C molten paraffin. Embedding orientation strictly maintained the tracheal longitudinal axis to preserve the sagittal alignment of cartilage rings. Paraffin blocks were pre-cooled to − 20 °C on a freezing stage, and 4–6 μm transverse serial sections were cut using a precision rotary microtome. Structurally intact sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides and thermally cured at 60°C for 12 hs on a slide warmer to enhance tissue-slide adhesion. In addition, to observe the ciliary surface ultrastructure, mucosal tissues from the repaired region were prepared for scanning electron microscopy. Samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, dehydrated through a graded series, critical point dried, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged under SEM.

Angiogenic regeneration was quantified via CD31 immunofluorescence (ab28364, Abcam; 1:200 dilution, 4°C overnight) using a confocal microscope (LSM 980, Zeiss) with Z-stack imaging. In addition, primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence included anti-PDGFRα (Abcam, ab203491, 1:200), anti-Aggrecan (ABclonal, A11691, 1:100), anti-CD68 (Agilent, M063, 1:50), anti-SOX9 (Santa Cruz, sc-166505, 1:200), anti-Collagen II (DSHB, II-II6B3, 2–5 µg/mL), anti-CK14 (Abcam, ab7800, 0.1–1 µg/mL), and anti-acetyl-α-tubulin (Lys40) (Invitrogen, 32-2700, 2 µg/mL). Secondary antibodies corresponding to the antigen-antibody species were used, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Concurrently, Safranine O staining (Servicebio) was performed to analyze fibrotic tissue deposition and chondrogenesis, with quantitative collagen fiber alignment assessed via ImageJ’s OrientationJ plugin (v3.0.4). For goat specimens, dual immunofluorescence co-staining of Piezo1 (ab203271), anti-Collagen II (Merck Millipore, MAB8887) and CD31 (ab28364) was conducted under standardized protocols (4% PFA fixation, 0.1% Triton X-100 permeabilization), followed by 3D reconstruction using Imaris software (v9.7.1, Bitplane) to map mechanotransduction-vasculogenesis spatial correlations.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (9.0, USA) was used to evaluate the statistical significance of all comparative studies in this article. All parametric analyses presumed data conformity to the Gaussian distribution without formal normality verification. For multi-group comparisons, statistical evaluation was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc correction for pairwise contrasts. Inter-group differences between the two datasets were assessed via a two-tailed Student’s t test with the assumption of equal variance.

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD, and error bars represent SD. Sample sizes (n) and the specific statistical tests used are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (ns, not significant; P- values are annotated in the figure).

Statistics and reproducibility

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicate experiments. For image-based assays, including optical microscopy, confocal microscopy, SEM, TEM, and histological/immunofluorescence analyses, representative images are shown, and similar results were obtained across independent repeats. No data were excluded unless stated otherwise.
