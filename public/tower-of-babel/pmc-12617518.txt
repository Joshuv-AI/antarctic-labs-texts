Severe floods significantly reduce global rice yields
Authors: Zhi Li, Lorenzo Rosa, Steven Gorelick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rice serves as a staple food for over half of the world’s population. Although the reduction of rice yields by droughts is well known, when flooding fully submerges rice plants for over 1 week, crops cannot survive. Considering rice growth stages, using a flood dynamics model and difference-in-differences, we assessed the causal impact of rice crop submergence on yield losses from 1980 to 2015. Globally, these floods reduce annual rice yield by 4.3%, with China’s East Coast experiencing 14% losses. Since 2000, yield losses have increased due to more frequent extreme floods, a trend anticipated to continue. Our findings highlight the need for flood-resistant rice cultivars to mitigate risks and support global food security, and implement adaptation strategies against both flood and drought rice yield losses.

INTRODUCTION

Rice serves as a staple food for over half of the global population (1). In China, it sustains more than 65% of the population and contributes ~40% of the nation’s caloric intake (2). However, globally, the increasing frequency of climate-related disasters, such as droughts and floods, has placed substantial pressure on rice yields, historically leading to famine, supply shortages, and regional price volatility (3, 4). Research suggests that as climate change accelerates, intensifying extreme weather events, the stability of future rice production is at considerable risk (5, 6).

Although rice is a semi-aquatic crop that benefits from controlled inundation—such as irrigation or shallow flooding during early growth stages—uncontrolled or severe flood events can substantially reduce yields. The 2011 Southeast Asia flood destroyed more than 1 million hectares of rice in Thailand, 0.4 million hectares in Cambodia and the Philippines, and 0.3 million ha in Myanmar (7). During the growing phase, flooding can limit the amount of oxygen and sunlight available to plants, thereby inhibiting photosynthesis and physically displacing rice plants through high-velocity flows, particularly during flash floods (8). At the harvest stage, floodwater complicates field access, making harvesting particularly challenging. Despite these well-studied impacts, large-scale assessments of flood-related rice crop loss remain underexplored, especially compared to drought impacts (9). We hypothesize three reasons for this research gap. First, floods are typically restricted to rice fields, driven by topographic variations, making correlations between local flood occurrences and national yield data less apparent. Second, rice, as a semi-aquatic crop, exhibits some flood resilience and tolerance of submergence for 3 to 4 days (10)—a tolerance it lacks under drought conditions. Third, no clear criteria exist to define a “rice-killing” flood, limiting consistency across studies.

Past research often uses extreme rainfall as a proxy for flooding in yield analyses (11, 12). However, this approach lacks specificity for identifying the causal effects of flooding (13). Other studies have attempted to develop universal flood depth-duration-damage curves for rice yields (14, 15). Nonetheless, because of variations among rice cultivars in flood vulnerability and complex interactions between flood timing and rice phenology (16), these damage functions lack broad applicability. Considering these limitations, we propose “rice-killing flood” as an event that devastates rice crops based on flood dynamics and rice phenology.

Here, we define rice-killing events as periods when flood depth equals or exceeds crop height, resulting in complete submergence for at least 7 days. This definition is supported by extensive research demonstrating that rice crops are severely affected when fully submerged for more than 7 days (17–19). For instance, the percentage of rice yield loss increases to ~50% (17, 19). To identify these events, we used the hydrodynamic model CaMa-Flood (20), running simulations from 1980 to 2015 at a spatial resolution of 0.25° (roughly 25 km at the Equator) to generate spatiotemporal flood depths. Then, we use the difference-in-differences (DiD) model (21), a statistical approach for estimating the causal effect of a treatment by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between a treatment group and a control group. This model is used to identify the causal relationship between rice-killing flood events (a binary variable based on thresholding flood depth) and yield losses (22) and is applied across all major rice-growing regions globally. Details on the model setup and implementation are provided in Materials and Methods. In doing so, we quantify the effects of rice-killing floods on rice yields in major rice-growing regions and identify vulnerable regions whose rice productions are susceptible to floods. The results of this study can inform stakeholders and decision-makers’ plans to mitigate flood losses and adapt agriculture to climate extremes.

RESULTS

Historical rice-killing floods

We curated a series of historical rice-killing flood events worldwide from government records, news reports, and published literature. A comparison between the reported flood-affected rice-growing areas and our simulated results reveals a strong alignment, indicating consistency between the reported data and the simulated flood extents (Fig. 1). In India, the world’s second-largest rice producer worldwide [196 million tonnes in 2022 (23)], our flood simulations closely aligned with reported data (Fig. 1). The 2008 India floods resulted in 1 million ha of damaged rice paddy from June to September (24). Our model estimated 1.03 million ha, with a difference margin of 3%. Another important event, the 1987 India flood, damaged ~0.7 million hectares of rice, with our model estimating 0.8 million ha (Fig. 1).

The 2011 Southeast Asia flood, one of the most devastating events, affected seven countries, causing 1302 deaths and affecting 9 million people (7, 25). Our model fits the trend shown in Fig. 1 but generally predicts lower affected rice areas than reported in Thailand (0.7 million ha versus 1.3 million ha) but aligned closely in Cambodia (0.4 million ha versus 0.4 million ha) and the Philippines (0.6 million ha versus 0.6 million ha). Conversely, the model predicts higher impacts in Vietnam (0.2 million ha versus 0.1 million ha), Laos (0.07 million ha versus 0.06 million hectares), and Myanmar (0.4 million ha versus 0.3 million ha) (Fig. 1). These discrepancies likely arose from model parameters and unique regional practices, such as fallowing land in the Mekong River basin (26).

For the most severe flood event in China, the world’s largest rice producer [210 million tonnes in 2022 (23)], our model correctly identifies these destructive events but predicts slightly higher flood-affected areas than was reported. China’s devastating 1998 flood occurred in July and August, coinciding with the rice harvest, affecting ~3 million ha of rice fields (Fig. 1). Our model estimated 3.3 million ha—10% higher than reported. Moreover, China’s 1991 flood, its second largest, affected 2.8 million ha of farmland, including Anhui, Jiangsu, and Hunan Provinces, the nation’s key rice-growing areas (27). The reported flood-affected rice-growing area was 2 million ha, whereas our model identified 2.5 million ha, a 27% overestimate.

Attributes of global rice-killing floods

We applied our model globally to characterize the key features of global rice-killing floods across major rice-producing basins (annual rice production greater than 1 million tonnes), including their duration, seasonality, and the seasonality strength. Strong seasonality means a rice-killing flood tends to occur in a particular month (Fig. 2). The Yangtze River basin has experienced these most destructive events, with, on average, 1 to 3 per year from 1981 to 2015 (Fig. 2A). As China’s primary rice-growing region, known as the “land of fish and rice,” the Yangtze Basin produces more than two-thirds of the nation’s rice (>114 million tonnes; fig. S1). The high frequency of these destructive flood events here poses substantial risks to national rice security and caloric intake for the population (28).

In the 35 years period studied, flood events are frequent in the Amazon Basin (98 events/year), Mekong Basin (97 events/year), Ganges Basin (94 events/year), Irrawaddy Basin (72 events/year), Niger Basin (43 events/year), and Madagascar (39 events/year) (Fig. 2). Note that each grid cell is treated independently in our analysis; thus, a widespread flood event affecting multiple grid cells is recorded as multiple rice-killing flood events if the flood conditions meet the defined criteria locally. In 2000, the Amazon Basin produced around 3 million tonnes of rice, primarily from Brazil (fig. S1), while the Mekong Basin ranked fourth globally in rice production with more than 27 million tonnes per year. The Ganges Basin is another rice-killing flood hotspot, producing more than 60 million tonnes per year of rice for India. Collectively, these four basins produce over half of the global rice supply, sustaining ~1.7 billion people (23).

Global rice-killing floods have an average duration of 15.4 days, with important regional variability (Fig. 2B). Among major global river basins, the Sabarmati Basin exhibits the longest rice-killing floods, exceeding 30 days. Notably, regions with prolonged flood durations do not necessarily coincide with areas of frequent flooding or high rice production. Basins with extended-duration rice-killing floods are typically characterized by flat floodplain topography, which slows the recession of floodwaters compared to steeper basins where drainage occurs more rapidly (29).

The seasonal timing of these flood events largely overlaps with rice growth and harvest periods (fig. S2). The rice-killing floods typically occur in late Spring (e.g., Mississippi River, Yangtze River, and Euphrates) and Summer (e.g., Amazon River and India). Distinct weather patterns drive the seasonality of these floods in various regions (Fig. 2C). For example, the Meiyu front (30) in late spring triggers devastating floods in the Yangtze River Basin; this quasi-stationary front brings warm, moist air from the south against cooler, drier air from the north. In India, these major floods coincide with the Indian monsoon peak from July to September, while in the Amazon Basin, these floods generally occur in late austral summer (January), driven by the South American monsoon system. During normal years, this monsoonal rainfall provides necessary water for rice growth, but excessive rain in a wet year can be devastating (31). In the Mississippi River basin, localized convective systems often cause these major floods (32). This seasonality pattern aligns closely with general flood timing, as the primary rice-growing season often coincides with peak flood season, particularly in rain-fed systems (33).

The strength of this seasonality (Fig. 2B) coincides with the variability of these major flood dates. Basins in a Mediterranean climate (e.g., West Coast of the United States and Spain) and Monsoonal climates in India exhibit strong seasonality, where major floods reliably occur in specific months. High-latitude basins, such as Po Valley, (>60°) also show strong seasonality, where floods are mainly driven by snow or ice melt. Conversely, these floods in the Amazon Basin and Indonesia display weak seasonality, as extreme rainfall events in these regions are sporadic. The Yangtze River basin’s flood seasonality is similarly weak, influenced by flow regulation from cascading dams.

Global rice-killing floods aggregated by major river basins

Using a DiD model, we estimate that, on average, grid cells experiencing rice-killing floods exhibit a 4.3% reduction in rice yield, corresponding to an aggregate global loss of ~18 million tonnes of rice per year over the period 1981–2015. To capture the effects across various scales, we aggregated the data at both basin and country/region levels. Basin-level statistics provide insight into the heterogeneity in hydroclimatic conditions driving these destructive events, while country/region-level data account for variability in economic development and improved agricultural management practices. Basin-level or region-level yield losses are computed as the average impact across all grid cells within each basin or region—regardless of flood exposure—based on regional fixed effects, thereby capturing the integrated influence of hydroclimatic variability and land use characteristics within the basin boundary.

At the basin level, yield losses reach as high as 14.0% in the China Coast basin and 15.9% in the Huang He Basin (Fig. 3A). Globally, other basins show substantial impacts as well, such as a 7.4% loss in the India West Coast Basin. The yield loss in other major rice-growing basins generally ranges from 3 to 4% per event.

On the larger country/region level (Fig. 3B), North Korea exhibits the highest yield loss at 15%, followed by East China at 8% (6 million tonnes of rice per year), and India’s states of West Bengal and Bihar at 7% (3 million tonnes of rice per year). Interannual variability in yield loss due to floods

We analyzed yield changes caused by rice-killing floods using a 5-year moving window to assess interannual variability (Fig. 4). The long-term trends and seasonal patterns in flood-induced rice yield losses depicted in Fig. 4 may partly reflect the modulating influence of large-scale climate modes such as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), which affects flood frequency and intensity in major rice-producing regions such as Southeast Asia (34–36). The major historical impacts (1980–2015) of ENSO are implicitly captured in the historical precipitation and temperature data. The results indicate that North Korea, Indonesia, China, the Philippines, and Nepal have experienced greater yield loss per rice-killing floods in recent decades. This suggests that the magnitude and/or duration of these floods in these countries are intensifying, posing substantial risks to regional food security.

DISCUSSION

This study focuses on the devastation of rice-killing floods, quantifying their causal effects on rice yield. These events involving total crop submergence for 7 day or more are most prevalent in the Yangtze River Basin, Amazon Basin, and Mekong Basin, with strong seasonality aligned with rice growing stages. Globally, these devastating floods reduce annual rice yield by an average of 4.3% (Fig. 3). In major rice-producing countries of China and India, yield losses are even more pronounced, at 5.3 and 6.4%, respectively, corresponding to production losses of 10 million and 5 million tonnes or 5 and 4% of national production. In specific regions, such as the China East Coast, yield losses can reach up to 14% (Fig. 3A). Over recent decades, yield losses per rice-killing flood have increased, likely driven by more frequent and intense extreme weather events. Cultivating flood-resistant rice varieties emerges as a promising mitigation and adaptation strategy to safeguard rice yields in the face of climate change.

Using the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT) data (38), we colocate rice yield losses caused by these major floods with documented economic losses from these events (fig. S4). The analysis reveals a significant negative correlation (r = −0.5, P < 0.05), indicating that reductions in rice yield are strongly associated with increased economic losses. Specifically considering all global data, a 1% decrease in rice yield corresponds to a $4.6 million marginal economic losses. Applying this statistic, we estimate that India and China, on average, incur $29 million and $24 million in losses per a rice-killing flood event, respectively, for each rice-killing flood. However, for Northeast India and East China where the yield losses reach up to 7 and 14%, respectively, the economic losses can increase to $32 million and $57 million per rice-killing flood.

There are some uncertainties in our quantification of yield loss that warrant further exploration. First, there are other definitions from field experiments that might be considered rice-killing flood events (39, 40). To rigorously assess the robustness of our causal findings, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses by reestimating the DiD model under varying thresholds for flood depth and duration. Specifically, we systematically reran the DiD model with flood depth thresholds ranging from 0.5 m below to 0.5 m above crop height and flood duration thresholds incrementally adjusted from 3 to 14 days of submergence (fig. S5). Results show that yield loss increases as the flood depth approaches the crop height, from −0.4 m (flood depth 0.4 m below crop height) to 0 m (equal to crop height), but levels off at a minimum once the crop is fully submerged (fig. S5A). This indicates that flooding beyond complete submergence does not substantially exacerbate yield loss during the vegetative stage, suggesting that our method captures the underlying yield loss process. Conversely, flood duration exhibits a continuous relationship with yield loss, increasing from day 3 (3.8%) to day 14 (4.7%) over the globe (fig. S5B). This suggests that prolonged submergence poses even greater risks of crop loss. Second, we cannot account for postflood replantation. In cases when there was time to replant rice crops after complete flood damage, and if there were no additional severe floods during the growing season, reported annual yield data may not fully reflect prereplanting flood impacts on yield. Last, our study focuses solely on physiological damage to rice crops, such as crop oxygen deficit caused by flooding. The impacts of floods on yields would be even greater if there were mechanistic damage, particularly during flash floods (41). These floods are characterized by high flow velocities, which are especially destructive in mountainous regions where rice is cultivated on terraces. These floods can physically uproot or damage rice crops. The resolution of our simulation, which cannot capture events shorter than 6 hours or flooded areas smaller than 1 km (42), limits its ability to fully represent these additional losses due to flash floods. However, our simulation does incorporate damage caused by coincidental occurrences of flash floods and long-duration floods.

Although our results demonstrate that rice-killing floods overwhelmingly reduce rice yields across most global river basins (Fig. 3) where rice is grown, there are notable exceptions where floods appear to enhance rice yields. For instance, in the Penner Basin in India (fig. S6), rice yields improve following high rainfall events. A plausible explanation for this phenomenon is the high evaporative demand in these regions. Stagnant floodwater in these areas likely evaporates quickly, reducing the duration of submergence and minimizing the oxygen deficit for rice plants. Evidence for this positive impact on rice yields in these basins is that they are water-stressed, hot, high evaporation regions (fig. S7). This interplay of hydrology and climate underscores the importance of regional water stress conditions on improving yields or at least modulating the yield losses of these destructive floods.

With advances in agricultural technology, flood-resilient rice cultivars have been developed in Southeast Asia (8). Two types of genetically engineered flood-resilient rice cultivars have been developed to address the losses of flooding (43). The first type, flash flood-resilient rice varieties, can survive submergence for 10 to 14 days by entering a dormant state, a strategy referred to as “holding its breath,” which allows the plant to conserve energy by halting growth temporarily. The second type, long-duration flood-resilient rice varieties, is designed to withstand submergence lasting more than 14 days by rapidly elongating their internodes, enabling the plant to grow above the water’s surface and maintain access to sunlight and air.

To evaluate the extent to which these cultivars minimize yield loss, we conducted experiments adjusting flood thresholds specific to flood-resilient rice. Based on Xu and Mackill (44), which showed that the sub1 flood-resistant rice variety can survive submergence for up to 2 weeks, we extended the flood duration (>14 days) to simulate conditions for flood-resistant crops. In addition, to account for the faster growth and tolerance of these cultivars, we raised the flood depth to half a meter above the crop height continuously during its growth cycle, thereby fully submerging the rice crop. The results indicate that flood-resilient rice yields diminish, but yield losses under rice-killing flood conditions are significantly reduced when compared to normal rice varieties (fig. S8). On a global scale, yield losses are reduced by an average of 70% when using flood-resilient rice. Regionally, these cultivars mitigate yield losses by 75% in India and 12% in China. We find similar results in field experiments in India. For instance, Ismail et al. (40) noted that the sub1 flood-resistant variety reduces the yield losses by 77%. This finding underscores the effectiveness of flood-resistant rice in mitigating the losses of these destructive floods. However, the current global distribution and adoption rates of flood-resilient rice remain low, and the proportion of flood-resilient rice cultivars relative to normal rice varieties is yet to be quantified.

Although not the primary focus of this study, we provide a preliminary estimate of future changes in rice-killing floods using extreme rainfall as a proxy. The use of extreme rainfall to project floods is justifiable, as shown in prior studies (45). Here, we define extreme rainfall as the annual maximum 7-day basin-average rainfall and calculate the relative change between the historical period (1980–2014) and the projected future period (2033–2067). Figure S9 presents the projected changes in extreme rainfall for major rice-growing basins globally based on a multimodel ensemble mean. For example, the Lake Chad basin, which currently experiences the seventh-longest flood duration, is projected to see 38% increases in extreme rainfall. Similarly, the China Coast basin, a region with one of the highest yield losses from rice-killing floods (14% per event), is projected to experience a 14% increase in extreme rainfall. On average, major rice-producing basins in regions such as China and India are likely to experience a 13% increase in extreme rainfall. While these projections do not directly translate into changes in the frequency or intensity of rice-killing floods or associated yield losses, they indicate a likely increase in the occurrence and severity of these events due to the intensification of extreme rainfall. Note that these estimates represent only a first-order approximation of future climate extremes.

Both excessive water (floods) and insufficient water (droughts) can cause substantial damage to rice crops. However, rice is generally less tolerant to drought, which tends to occur on a larger spatial scale compared to the more localized nature of flood. Zhang et al. (46), in a meta-analysis of drought impacts on rice, reported an average yield reduction of 25.4% during droughts. To compare these effects, we conducted an additional experiment substituting floods with droughts in the DiD model. Using Global Drought and Flood Catalog (47), we simulated soil moisture data for the same period, we defined an agricultural drought event as relative soil moisture falling below its 20th percentile (meaning that the probability of occurrence is 0.2 each year) and persisting for over 2 weeks. Our analysis revealed that droughts cause an 8.1% decrease in rice yield, almost double the 4.3% yield reduction observed for floods. Although this estimate does not align directly with the meta-analysis findings—likely due to differences in defining rice-killing droughts—it highlights the severe losses of both droughts and floods on rice production, posing significant threats to global food security. Moreover, the compound effects of flood-to-drought and drought-to-flood sequenes result in nearly twice the yield loss compared to individual flood or drought events alone (11). These findings emphasize the importance of a comprehensive evaluation of climate extremes, including floods, droughts, heatwaves, and cold stress, as well as the order in which these events occur or co-occur. How these combined effects can be mitigated remains a major challenge.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This section outlines the datasets and methods used in the study. The data include rice yield and harvest information, as well as ancillary data for assessing economic losses. Subsequently, we detail the methodology for defining a rice-killing flood, configuring the flood model, and applying the DiD model to identify the causal effects of rice-killing floods on yield and production.

Rice yield and harvest data

The global annual rice yield data (GDHY) is available at 0.5° resolution for the period 1981–2016, spanning 36 years (22). GDHY integrates agricultural census statistics with satellite remote sensing to estimate grid-level yield values. For this study, we utilized the latest version, v1.2 + v1.3. Grid-cell yield data from GDHY was matched to model simulations using the nearest-neighbor method to establish statistical relationships.

Given technological advances over time, rice yields exhibit a positive trend at the grid level. We applied a 5-year central moving average to detrend the yield time series, following Han et al. (48), as this method effectively removes both long-term technological trends and residual seasonality, enabling consistent calculation of relative yield anomalies across time and basins (49–51). The percent change in yield after detrending is computed asY^i,t=Yi,t−∑t−2t+2Yi,t/5∑t−2t+2Yi,t/5×100%(1)where Y^i,t is the yield percent change after detrending and Yi,t is the rice yield at grid cell i and year t.

To compute rice production, we used the global dataset of monthly irrigated and rainfed crop areas from the year 2000 (MIRCA2000) (52). MIRCA2000 includes 26 crop types, distinguishing between rainfed and irrigated crops at the same spatial resolution as the yield data. In our analysis, we assumed a constant rice-growing area over time. Global rice harvest data are used to calculate rice production. This assumption is justifiable because the global rice harvested area in major rice-producing countries (e.g., China, India, Indonesia, and South Korea) remains relatively constant after 2000 [data from Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) (23); fig. S10]. The exception is South Korea, where it shows a decrease in rice harvested area after 2000. This exception has been attributed to “the decrease in rice consumption and the growing preference for imported non-rice grains.” (53)

Ancillary data

To correlate yield losses per rice-killing flood with total economic losses caused by floods, we used the EM-DAT (38), which has recorded disaster impacts since 1900. EM-DAT compiles data from various sources, including the United Nations, governmental and nongovernmental agencies, insurance companies, research institutes, and press agencies. For a disaster to be included in the database, it must meet at least one of the following criteria: (i) at least 10 fatalities (including deaths and missing persons), (ii) at least 100 people affected, or (iii) a call for international assistance or an official declaration of emergency. This ensures that disasters logged in the database reflect significant humanitarian and economic impacts, with a primary focus on safeguarding human lives.

Definition of rice-killing floods

We introduce the concept of rice-killing floods, defined based on the interplay between flood dynamics and rice phenology. While previous studies have examined “rice floods” using rainfall as a proxy for flooding (54), such an approach overlooks critical land surface hydrological processes, including soil infiltration and alternative flood types (e.g., groundwater flooding). Given that rice is a flood-irrigated crop, the determination of a rice-killing flood cannot rely solely on binary flood inundation states (i.e., flooded or nonflooded) or soil saturation levels (55).

Studies and field surveys (14, 19, 56) indicate that rice damage is a function of flood height, duration, and the specific rice growth stage (e.g., vegetative, reproductive, or ripening). In this study, rather than applying a continuous damage function, we define a rice-killing flood as an event that causes yield loss. Specifically, this category of flood is defined as one that fully submerges rice plants for at least 7 days.

Flood height is simulated using a hydrodynamic model, while rice crop height is determined on the basis of phenological stages, calculated as a function of the time difference between planting and flood occurrence. The rice height control points are set as follows: 40 cm at 20 days, 50 cm at 40 days, 100 cm at 65 days, 130 cm at 95 days, and 150 cm at 135 days postplanting (19). Heights between these control points are interpolated linearly. In addition, we use a minimum flood depth threshold of 0.5 m, as this is the water depth when rice damage begins to occur.

To account for different rice cultivars, which demonstrate greater tolerance to flooding compared to conventional rice, we adjusted the model by increasing the rice crop height by 0.5 m and extending the inundation duration threshold to more than 14 days. This adjustment reflects reports indicating that certain flood-resilient rice varieties can survive submergence for up to 14 days by using a “hold its breath” strategy, allowing them to endure prolonged flooding conditions (40).

Flood simulation and validation

This study determines rice-killing floods based on flood dynamics—specifically, flood depth and duration—different from previous approaches that rely solely on flood extent. To achieve this, we use the global hydrodynamic model CaMa-Flood (20), which simulates flood dynamics at a daily temporal resolution and 0.25° spatial resolution. Unlike satellite observations, which cannot directly measure flood depth and often fail to accurately capture flood duration due to infrequent temporal coverage, CaMa-Flood provides a robust alternative. Notably, CaMa-Flood incorporates subgrid heterogeneity to enhance the resolution of floodplain inundation dynamics within its coarse grid cells (0.25° resolution). Each grid cell is discretized into a unit catchment that includes subgrid topographic parameters derived from higher-resolution datasets, such as the Multi-Error Removed Improved (MERIT) DEM and MERIT Hydro, describing the river channel and floodplain. Flood fraction—the proportion of the grid cell that is inundated—is dynamically computed based on total surface-water storage and local elevation profiles using pregenerated lookup tables that relate water levels to wetted areas, volumes, and inundation extents. This approach allows the model to resolve fractional inundation and associated impacts at subgrid scales, providing more accurate estimates even for affected areas smaller than the full grid resolution. The model has been extensively applied in global flood simulation studies, including assessment of climate change impacts on flood dynamics (57, 58), economic loss estimation (59, 60), and evaluations of global crop production changes due to flooding (13).

We used CaMa-Flood version 4.21 to produce global flood depth, velocity, and fraction at a daily and 0.25° resolution for the period 1980–2015, spanning 35 years. The first year was used as a model spin-up period to establish in-channel river stages. The model uses land surface runoff as input, which is routed through prescribed river networks and parameterized floodplains. For this study, we used the processed Earth2Observe European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecast runoff product, which has been extensively validated in prior research (61, 62). While our analysis uses static land use and land cover (LULC) fields in the underlying land surface models, as is standard in global hydrological reanalyses (62), this approach may introduce uncertainty in regions experiencing rapid landscape changes over the 1980–2015 period, such as urban expansion in East and Southeast Asia. However, by focusing on grid cells with consistent nonzero rice yields throughout the study period, we inherently exclude areas undergoing significant LULC transformations (e.g., from forest to agriculture or to urban areas). In addition, our analysis found more than 90% rice growing regions contain 0 to 1% urban built-up, and any effect is limited to the period 2000–2015. That said, urban expansion is unlikely to affect the results of this study.

The model also incorporates reservoir and lake effects using the Funato-Yamazaki reservoir scheme. Reservoir outflow is calculated on the basis of reservoir storage, with three distinct operational controls beyond normal conditions. Under emergency conditions, where dam failure is imminent due to persistent high inflows, floodgates are fully opened, equating outflow to inflow. In flood control conditions, outflow is significantly increased compared to normal operations. Last, a constant minimum outflow is maintained to ensure downstream environmental flow.

To validate the simulated flood extent, we compared it with satellite-observed flood fraction data over global rice-growing fields. For this, we used the Global Inundation Estimation from Multiple Satellites (GIEMS-2), a monthly flood fraction product with a spatial resolution (~25-km grid spacing) matching that of our model configuration. GIEMS-2 covers the period 1992–2015 (63). To align temporal resolutions, we aggregated the simulated daily results to a monthly time scale using an averaging method. The validation results (fig. S11) show a global average correlation coefficient of 0.5 between simulations and observations. Notably, correlation coefficients for major rice-growing countries, such as China and India, exceed the global average, affirming the model’s effectiveness in simulating floods in key rice production regions.

DiD model for flood impact on rice yield

The DiD model (21) is a widely used econometric approach for estimating causal effects in observational studies, particularly suited for analyzing the impact of interventions or treatments in a quasi-experimental setting. In this study, the DiD model is used to examine the impact of flooding events on rice yields across various river basins over multiple years, controlling for both temporal and spatial heterogeneity. Specifically, this approach compares the change in rice yields in flood-affected (treated) basins to those in unaffected (control) basins before and after flood events, thereby isolating the yield variations directly attributable to flooding from broader trends in yield changes that may be due to other temporal or regional factors. The model assumes parallel trends between the treated and control basins in the absence of flooding, allowing for the interpretation of the difference in yield changes across these groups as the causal impact of flooding on rice production. This design captures both time-invariant characteristics unique to each river basin (e.g., soil properties and hydrologic patterns) and broader temporal trends, providing a rigorous framework to assess flood-induced yield loss while accounting for both spatial and temporal autocorrelation in yield dynamics across river basins.

At each rice growing grid cell, we generate a time series of occurrences (yes/no) of rice-killing floods and rice yield and production in that year or panel data. To conduct regional aggregation, we concatenated all grid cells into a panel dataset and assigned region-level codes (e.g., basin or country), applying geographic fixed effects; standard errors are clustered at the corresponding spatial unit level to account for within-region correlation. We then use the DiD model to infer the percent changes in rice yield/production for each such devastating flood. The DiD model is a statistical technique to infer causal relationships. It compares the changes in outcomes over space and time between a group that is exposed to a treatment (the treatment group) and a group that is not (the control group). Readers are referred to fig. S12 for a schematic diagram of the model design. The DiD model has been widely applied in economics and social sciences to examine the effect of human interventions. For example, it has been used to investigate the impact of flood hazard areas on the US housing market (64, 65). The spatial difference compares regions experiencing floods to regions without floods in the same year, and the temporal difference compares the same region in years experiencing floods to years without floods. The basic setup of the model is as followsYit=α+δXit+γPt+θ(Xit×Pt)+μi+λt+ϵit(2)where Yit represents the yield anomaly from the GDHY dataset; Xit represents the occurrence of floods in binary form from model simulation, and its coefficient δ represents the effect of a flood event on yield change; Pt is a dummy variable to determine which period the rice yield change belongs to (1 if after the flood event, zero otherwise) and its coefficient γ is used to identify the actual impact of the event. The interaction term of the two variables Xit×Pt is the DiD estimator, and its coefficient θ captures the causal effect of flooding on rice yield; μi represents river basin–fixed effects, capturing time-invariant characteristics specific to each basin, such as hydroclimatic conditions; λt represents the time effects, accounting for year-specific shocks affecting all basins, such as technological improvement or overall climatic conditions; ϵit is the error term. In practice, μi and λt are implemented as categorical fixed effects for each basin and year, respectively. These absorb unobserved, time-invariant spatial heterogeneity and year-specific global shocks—thereby isolating the causal effect of flooding on rice yield anomalies.
